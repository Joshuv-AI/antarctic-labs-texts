MicroRNA-132/212 negatively modulates opioid reward by targeting dopamine transporter in the ventral tegmental area
Authors: Jie Meng, Zhonghao Li, Yi Zhang, Dajun Zhang, Haiting Liu, Xiangyang Zang, Yaqiong Zhao, Jing Wen, Wei Shu, Xiaoke Nan, Xianchan Li, Yan-Xue Xue, Xiaojian Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAddictive drugs, notably opioid drugs, have significant societal implications, yet the cellular and molecular mechanisms underpinning rewarding effects remain largely elusive. Noncoding transcripts, including the microRNAs (miRNAs), are pivotal regulators of diverse biological functions. Notably, the microRNAs miR-132 and miR-212, arising from a shared noncoding transcript, have links to several psychiatric conditions, including cocaine addiction. However, their contribution to opiate rewarding remains speculative. In this study, we discovered that repeated morphine administration decreases the expression of miR-132/212 in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and induces a concurrent upregulation of the dopamine transporter (DAT). Using a luciferase reporter assay, we found that the DAT coding gene, SLC6A3 mRNA 3’UTR, is a direct target of miR-132/212. These miRNAs negatively regulated both mRNA expression and protein levels of DAT in vitro. This was corroborated by in vivo studies which revealed that behavioral experiments using a morphine-induced conditioned place preference (CPP) paradigm showed that suppression of miR-132/212 in the VTA facilitated the formation of morphine-induced CPP. Conversely, overexpression of miR-132 attenuated morphine preference in male and female adult rats, as well as adolescents. In sum, our findings uncover a regulatory mechanism wherein miR-132/212 modulates morphine induced reward behavior by fine-tuning DAT expression at the posttranscriptional level, providing a potential therapeutic target of rewarding effects. Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Addiction

Introduction

Drug abuse has emerged as a global social illness, posing a significant economic burden. The World Drug Report in 2022, published by the United Nations Office for Drug Control and Crime Prevention shows that there was a total of 284 million illicit drug users worldwide in 2022, with a year-on-year growth of 3 million [1]. An estimated 4% of individuals aged 15–64 worldwide engage in illegal drugs, and more than 1/10 of drug users, approximately 29 million people, suffer from drug abuse disorders or drug addiction. However, only 1/6 of individuals with drug dependence receive drug-dependence treatment. The prevention, treatment and care of patients with drug dependence is a heavy burden on the public health system [2]. Understanding the mechanisms of drug dependence and the development of effective treatments have great clinical value and social significance [3–5].

miRNAs, a class of noncoding RNAs, regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. They bind to target messenger RNA transcripts primarily in the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR), either causing their degradation or repressing their translation [6]. Interestingly, psychoactive substances have the potential to perturb miRNA biogenesis and maturation through modulating transcription factors or enzymatic activities [7]. In a reciprocal manner, miRNAs can influence molecular pathways, such as DNA methylation, intracellular signaling events, or synaptic modifications, which are hypothesized to underpin the emergence of preference in response to drug exposure [8–10]. miR-132 and miR-212, both members of the evolutionarily conserved miR-132 (MIPF0000065) family, are two multifunctional microRNAs with sequences that are nearly identical [11, 12]. Acute exposure to cocaine increased miR-132 levels in the striatum of mice [13]. Cocaine-induced conditioned place preference (CPP) led to diminished miR-212 levels [14]. Furthermore, miR-212 appears to modulate cocaine seeking behavior by influencing synaptic plasticity within the nucleus accumbens (NAc), a process governed by key proteins such as CREB, TORC1, MeCP2 and BDNF [15–18]. Recently, miR-132/212 are shown to modulate opiate-associated addictive behaviors. Targeted overexpression of miR-132 in the dentate gyrus (DG) of hippocampus enhanced morphine reward seeking in rats [19]. In contrast, only miR-132 3p (but not 5p) is involved in morphine dependence by reversing structural plasticity in rat DG neurons [20]. Morphine appears to regulate miR-132/212 expression through pathways mediated by mu-opioid receptor, encompassing MAP kinase pathway, CaMKII-CaMKIV and PKA-CREB [21, 22]. However, investigations into the target genes through which miR-132/212 regulates the development of opioid reward are conspicuously lacking.

Dopamine is a quintessential neurotransmitter intricately linked to the reward to various drugs [23, 24]. Dopamine and dopamine transporter (DAT) play important roles in the action mechanisms of opioid drugs. Such drugs indirectly influence dopamine levels in the synaptic cleft and act on opioid receptors, which blocks the inhibitory effects of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) neurons on dopaminergic neurons, causing large amounts of dopamine to be released from the presynaptic membrane into the synaptic cleft [25, 26]. DAT, a member of the Na+/Cl- dependent transporter gene family, is a membrane protein located in the central dopaminergic neuronal terminal [27–29]. DAT regulates dopamine neurotransmission and is critically implicated in neuropsychiatric disorders such as reward [30]. Opioid drugs increase the expression of DAT in the mesolimbic system, including brain areas like ventral tegmental area (VTA), NAc and hippocampus [31–33]. However, the mechanisms underlying the regulatory role of DAT in the effect of opiate remain elusive. The SLC6A3 gene, which encodes DAT, resides on chromosome 5p15.32. The SLC6A3 3’UTR is 1945 bp in length, and intriguingly, houses a miR-132 (hsa-miR-132-3p, MIMAT0000426)/miR-212 (hsa-miR-212-3p, MIMAT0000269) binding site at 153-159 bp. Therefore, this investigation sought to decipher whether DAT might be a target of miR132/212 in regulating the rewarding effect of opioid drugs.

Results

Chronic morphine exposure diminished miR-132/212 expression in the VTA

To elucidate the effect of chronic use of morphine on the expressions of miR132/212 and DAT in vivo, we centered our investigation on VTA, a region implicated in morphine rewarding effect. We allocated the rats into two groups: the morphine-CPP conditioning group and the saline CPP conditioning (Fig. 1A). After 8 days of training, rats in the morphine-CPP group spend more time in the morphine-paired chambers (F (1, 32) = 49.58, P < 0.0001, Fig. 1B). Twenty-four hours after CPP test, we remove their brains for detection of the expression of 132/212 and DAT. RT‒qPCR analysis revealed that miR-132/212 of VTA were significantly downregulated in the morphine-CPP group (t = 11.26, P < 0.001, miR-132; t = 10.70, P < 0.001, miR-212. Figure 1C), concomitant with an increase in mRNA and protein level of DAT within the VTA (t = 13.18, P < 0.001, mRNA level; t = 3.01, P < 0.01, protein level, Fig. 1D) compared to the control group. To determine whether the observed modulation in miR132/212 and DAT protein within the VTA were attributable to morphine administration or CPP conditioning, another two cohorts of animals were subjected to daily morphine or saline administration over 8 consecutive days. Then we remove their brains for detection of the expression of 132/212 and DAT on day 9 (Fig. 1A). The alteration in expression of miR-132/212 (t = 28.77, P < 0.0001, miR-132; t = 16.19, P < 0.0001, miR-212, Fig. 1E), the mRNA and protein level of DAT (t = 14.39, P < 0.001, mRNA level; t = 2.21, P < 0.05, protein level, Fig.1F) within the VTA post chronic exposure paralleled the results observed in the morphine-CPP training. Morphine also increases the mRNA level of DAT in NAc (t = 2.43, P < 0.05, Fig. 1G；t = 0.81, P > 0.05, miR-212. Figure 1H). Collectively, these results indicate that chronic morphine exposure decreased miR-132/212 levels while increased the expression of DAT in the VTA.

miR-132/212 suppressed DAT expression in vitro

We then examine whether miR-132/212 affects the DAT expression in nerve cells. We conducted transient transfections of SK-N-SH nerve cells using miR-132/212 mimics and inhibitors. After 48 h, we measured the expression level of DAT mRNA and DAT protein. Our findings revealed significant alterations in the expression of DAT mRNA within SK-N-SH cells. Specifically, the introduction of miR-132 mimics led to a decrease in DAT mRNA levels, while the use of miR-132 inhibitors resulted in an increase (F(2, 6) = 86.38, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2A). These changes in DAT mRNA levels corresponded with similar fluctuations in DAT protein expression levels after treatment with miR-132 mimics and anti-miR-132 (F(2, 6) = 90.50, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2B). Similar trends were observed in the case of miR-212 and anti-miR-212, which demonstrated the ability to decrease or increase the expression levels of both DAT mRNA (F(2, 6) = 102.10, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2C) and DAT protein (F(2, 6) = 18.44, P = 0.0027, Fig. 2D). These results indicate that miR-132/212 possess the capacity to influence the expression of DAT mRNA and protein in SK-N-SH cells.

miR-132/212 targeted SLC6A3 3’UTR

miRNA posttranscriptional gene silencing, particularly through 3’UTR targeting, has been implicated in neurogenesis and brain function. miR-132 and miR-212 both belong to the miR-132 family (MIPF0000065), exhibiting sequence homogeneity, as they share identical seed region sequences and most mature microRNA sites [34]. Computational analysis results from TargetScan [35] (www.targetscan.org) and miRanda [36] (www.microrna.org) have identified a binding site for miR-132/212, characterized as 7mer-m8 (an exact match to positions 2-8 of the mature miRNA, the seed + position 8) [35], located at 153–159 bp of the SLC6A3 3’UTR with a length of 1945 bp. This sequence remains highly conserved across primates, rodents and other mammals (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, the seed region sequences of miR-132/212 exhibit substantial conservation in the SLC6A3 3’UTR across various mammalian species (Fig. 2F).

To elucidate the posttranscriptional regulation dynamics, a fragment of the SLC6A3 3’UTR was inserted downstream of the psiCHECK2 vector Renilla luciferase reporter gene, constructing a luciferase reporter gene expression vector. Post transient transfection of the reporter plasmid and microRNA into SK-N-SH cells, Renilla luciferase activity was assessed following a 24 h incubation. The results confirmed that miR-132 and miR-212 significantly reduced the luciferase expression activity of SK-N-SH cells by 58.3 and 68.7%, respectively, while their inhibitors increased luciferase expression activity by 22.0 and 9.7%, respectively (F (4, 10) = 102.80, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2G).

To confirm the effect of miR-132/212 on DAT expression in SK-N-SH cells, we conducted a point mutation on the predicted seed zone of miR-132/212 utilizing the overlapping PCR method (Fig. 2E), resulting in the incorporation of the mutant SLC6A3 3’UTR of the psiCHECK2 reporter gene, yielding a mutant plasmid. The results showed that the regulatory effect of miR-132/212 on the expression activity of Renilla luciferase disappeared after point mutation of the seed zone (F (4, 10) = 1.31, P > 0.05, Fig. 2H).

In addition, we inserted the DAT gene protein coding sequence and 3’UTR downstream of the GFP gene to construct a GFP-DAT fusion expression plasmid. Since the expression level of DAT in HepG2 cells is relatively low, we chose HepG2 cells to overexpress the GFP-DAT fusion protein. The fusion expression plasmid and miR-132/212 were co-transfected into HepG2 cells, and after a 48 h incubation, the results showed that miR-132/212 significantly reduced the expression of GFP-DAT fusion protein (Fig. 2I).

These results indicate that miR-132/212 can target the form of 7mer-m8 at 153-159 bp of SLC6A3 3’UTR.

miR-132/212 modulated dopamine levels via DAT regulation

To investigate the regulation of dopamine uptake by miR-132/212 in neuronal cells, we used an ELISA-based assay to assess the effects of miR-132/212 on dopamine uptake in SK-N-SH cells [37–39]. SK-N-SH cells were transfected with miR-132/212 mimics or inhibitors, and following thorough washing to remove extracellular dopamine, intracellular dopamine levels were measured. Subsequent data showed that miR-132/212 reduced the uptake of dopamine in SK-N-SH nerve cells; their inhibitors significantly increased dopamine uptake in nerve cells (F (4, 10) = 27.21, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3A).

To further verify whether miR-132/212’s modulation of dopamine transport function in SK-N-SH nerve cells is enacted through DAT mediation, we designed a series of gene overexpression and silencing experiments (Fig. 3B-E). When the pcDNA3.1-SLC6A3 plasmid was translated into SK-N-SH cells to induce DAT overexpression, the level of intracellular dopamine uptake was significantly increased by 218.6% (t = 19.56, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3B). miR-132/212 significantly reduced the effects of DAT overexpression on the cellular function of dopamine uptake; they significantly reduced the intracellular dopamine levels (F (4, 10) = 110.20, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3C). After siRNA was transiently transfected into SK-N-SH cells, DAT mRNA was significantly reduced, and the level of cellular dopamine uptake was significantly decreased by 72.8% (t = 34.55, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3D). The regulatory effects of miR-132 on dopamine uptake in SK-N-SH nerve cells disappeared after DAT knockdown (F (4, 10) = 1.30, P > 0.05, Fig. 3E).

The norepinephrine transporter (NET), which is expressed in SK-N-SH cells, also contributes to the uptake of dopamine (DA). To counteract the confounding influence of NET, we employed desipramine (20 nM), a selective NET inhibitor, effectively neutralizing its activity. This intervention further substantiated the role of miR-132/212 in regulating DA reuptake through DAT (F (2, 9) = 49.85, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3F). Conversely, in the presence of the DAT selective inhibitor nomifensine, miR-132/212 has no effect on the levels of dopamine (F (2, 9) = 0.51, P > 0.05, Fig. 3G). These results revealed that miR-132/212 regulates DA levels by modulating DAT. To further investigate changes of extracellular DA levels, we employed microdialysis after overexpression of miR-132 in the VTA. In vivo microdialysis experiment also indicated that, miR-132 overexpression in the VTA could increase extracellular DA levels in the NAc (t = 6.00, P < 0.01) and VTA (t = 4.46, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3H-J). These findings demonstrate that alterations in DAT expression levels impact nerve cells’ dopamine uptake capacity, further suggesting that miR-132/212 regulates dopamine uptake in nerve cells via modulation of DAT expression.

miR-132/212 inhibition augments morphine’s rewarding effect via DAT upregulation

Then, we examined whether miR-132 overexpression within the VTA could affect morphine rewarding effect and DAT expression. The miRNA sponge has been proven to be effective in inactivating specific miRNAs [40]. We injected miR-132/212-specific sponge or control virus into VTA before training for morphine CPP (Fig. 4A). Knockdown of miR-132 or miR-212 significantly decreased the expression of each respective microRNA without observable cross-regulatory effects (F (2, 6) = 15.34, P < 0.01, miR-132；F (2, 6) = 9.32, P < 0.05, miR-212, Fig. 4B). The behavioral data revealed both miR-132 and miR-212 sponges enhanced the establishment of morphine CPP (F (1, 34) = 6.71, P < 0.05, Fig. 4C; F (1, 32) = 5.95, P < 0.05, Fig. 4D). Subsequent assessments revealed that injecting miR-132 (t = 4.81, P < 0.01, Fig. 4E left) or miR-212 (t = 3.69, P < 0.05, Fig. 4F left) construction virus into the VTA significantly increased DAT expression in the VTA. In the VTA, knockdown of miR-132 or miR-212 elevated DAT mRNA (t = 3.37, P < 0.05 for miR-132, Fig. 4E center; t = 3.28, P < 0.05 for miR-212, Fig. 4F center) and protein (t = 11.48, P < 0.0001 for miR-132, Fig. 4E right; t = 5.11, P < 0.001 for miR-212, Fig. 4F right) levels. These results show that the combination of miR-132/212 and SLC6A3 3’UTR was sufficient to downregulate DAT protein in vivo. This indicates that inhibition of miR-132/212 in the VTA can increase the expression of DAT, thereby increasing morphine preference.

miR-132 overexpression disrupted morphine’s rewarding effect

Next, we investigated whether directly introducing active miR-132 into the VTA could potentially decrease DAT protein expression and reduce the rewarding effect of morphine. We generated a construct in which miR-132 was expressed under the control of the U6 promoter and stereotaxically microinjected it into the VTA before CPP training (Fig. 5A). Post miR-132 microinjection into the VTA, there was a reduction in the CPP score of the miR-132-OE group in male rats (F (1, 60) = 2.90, P = 0.09, Fig. 5B) compared to the control group. Further evaluations confirmed that the overexpression of miR-132 elevated the intracellular abundance of miR-132 in the rat VTA (t = 5.20, P < 0.01, Fig. 5C), and decreased the mRNA (t = 6.87, P < 0.01, Fig. 5C) and protein levels (t = 2.75, P < 0.05, Fig. 5C) of DAT in the VTA. We confirmed that found that miR-132 overexpression did not affect the locomotion, as measured by the open field test (P > 0.05, Fig. 5D), or natural reward consumption, as assessed by sucrose preference test (P > 0.05, Fig. 5E). To directly test the role of DAT in the VTA in morphine reward, we microinjected the selective DAT inhibitor GBR12909 into the VTA 20 min prior to each morphine administration during the CPP conditioning phase. We found that DAT inhibitor GBR12909 significantly reduced the CPP score for morphine compared to vehicle controls (F (1, 28) = 9.11, P < 0.01, Fig. 5F-G). This result demonstrates that direct manipulation of DAT in the VTA attenuates opioid reward.

To examine the specificity of miR132 overexpression in VTA, we microinfused miR-132-OE virus into substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc). This intervention did not affect CPP scores (F (1, 34) = 1.87, P > 0.05, Fig. 5H), indicating the region-specific effect of miR-132 on morphine reward. To further explore the general regulatory role of miR132 in morphine reward, we microinjected miR-132-OE into the VTA of female and adolescent rats, and found that miR-132 overexpression in VTA also significantly reduced the CPP for morphine in female (F (1, 32) = 13.71, P < 0.001, F (1, 32) = 29.57, P < 0.001, Fig. 5I) and adolescent rats (F (1, 32) = 6.54, P < 0.05, F (1, 32) = 27.30, P < 0.05, Fig. 5J). These findings indicate that the miR-132 overexpression in the VTA can inhibit the expression of DAT in the VTA, leading to a diminished rewarding effect of morphine.

Discussion

Our study revealed that DAT serves as a regulatory target of miR-132/212 in the modulation of morphine reward. We confirmed the binding site of miR-132/212 on the DAT 3’UTR with constructed wild-type DAT 3’UTR and the miR-132/212 binding site mutant luciferase reporter gene plasmid, and the results revealed that a negative regulation of DAT mRNA and protein expression by miR-132/212 in SK-N-SH and nerve cells. This modulation was further confirmed by animal models of morphine rewarding effect. We observed a concurrent decrease in miR-132/212 in the VTA and increase in DAT protein levels within VTA. Finally, we found overexpression of 132 in the VTA reduced the expression of DAT in the VTA and specifically attenuated morphine preference. While inhibition of 132/212 to the target gene in the VTA augmented the expression of DAT in the VTA and promoted morphine preference.

Previous studies have posited the implication of miR-132 and miR-212 in reward [34]. Hollander et al. suggested that the miR-212 level in the dorsal striatum of rats was significantly upregulated after cocaine was given. Overexpression of miR-212 in the striatum reversed the track of rat cocaine intake over the long term [16]. Studies by Sadakierska-Chudy et al. indicated that self-administration of cocaine in rats increased the expression levels of striatal miR-132 and miR-212 [41]. By enhancing the activity of RAF1 (Raf-1 proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase), striatal miR-212 increased the sensitization of adenylate cyclase, increased the expression of cAMP responsive element binding protein (CREB)-regulated transcriptional coactivator (TORC), significantly increased the stimulatory effect of cocaine on the CREB signaling pathway, and reduced the response of rats to the incentive properties of cocaine [15]. Homeostatic interactions between methyl CpG binding protein 2 (MeCP2) and miR-212 in the dorsal striatum may be important in regulating vulnerability to cocaine reward. MeCP2 regulates cocaine intake through homeostatic interactions with miR-212 to control the effects of cocaine on striatal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels [16, 42, 43]. These studies have expounded the role of miR-132/212 in governing cocaine reward-associated behaviors. Recently, in the zebrafish it has been observed morphine decreases the expression of miR-21 and increases the expression of miR-132, BDNF and tyrosine receptor kinase B. After knockdown of miR-132/212 and OPRM1, the expression of BDNF was downregulated. MiR-132/212 combined with the seed sequence of MeCP2 mRNA inhibited the expression of MeCP2, ultimately increasing the expression of BDNF [22]. In our current investigation, we discerned that exposure to morphine precipitated a reduction of miR-132/212 in the VTA and that overexpression of miR-132/212 within the VTA could mitigate the conditioned place preference for morphine in rats. The regulatory role of miR-132/212 appears to be general, as overexpression of miR132 in VTA consistently reduced morphine CPP across male, female and adolescent rats. Importantly, the effects of miR-132 were specific, as miR-132 overexpression in VTA did not influence locomotor activity or natural reward preference, as evidenced by the lack of changes in the open field test and sucrose preference test. In addition, miR-132 overexpression in SNc did not change morphine reward, further highlighting the regional specificity of miR-132’s role in modulating morphine reward. Notably, chronic exposure to morphine led to an increase in miR132/212 in DG of the hippocampus, However, overexpression of miR-132 in DG reduced morphine reward self-administration and morphine withdraw symptoms [19, 20]. Synthesizing these insights with prior research, it is proposed that miR-132/212 might orchestrate diverse drug-associated behaviors through disparate neural mechanisms. Several signaling pathways, including CREB, BDNF and meCP2, may potentially be involved in regulating miR132/212 expressions following chronic morphine exposure. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying these require further exploration in future study.

While cocaine primarily exerts its rewarding effects by directly inhibiting the DAT, thereby blocking dopamine reuptake and causing rapid extracellular accumulation in the mesolimbic pathway [44], opioids like morphine act indirectly by activating mu-opioid receptors on GABAergic interneurons in the ventral tegmental area, leading to disinhibition and increased firing of dopaminergic neurons without primary DAT involvement [45, 46]. This mechanistic divergence questions the generalizability of DAT inhibitors from psychostimulants to opioids [47]. We found that the DAT inhibitor GBR12909 significantly reduced morphine CPP scores compared to vehicle controls, indicating that VTA DAT manipulation attenuates opioid reward. However, chronic opioid exposure may induce adaptive DAT upregulation or functional changes in reward circuits, potentially modulated by miR-132/212, highlighting the need for opioid-specific investigations. Opioid drugs may exert their influence on dopamine levels by enhancing the expression of DAT in the VTA and NAc [31, 48].This mechanism can be considered a common molecular pathway contributing to reward, at least to some extent [49, 50]. The role of DAT in the rewarding of psychostimulants has been extensively studied [51–54]. High dose of cocaine administration induced significant spontaneous activity in wild-type rats, which was related to the transport of DAT [55]. In contrast, the excessive spontaneous movements induced by cocaine were reduced in DAT KO mice [56]. Future studies using VTA-specific DAT overexpression will further validate this novel regulatory mechanism.

In the present study we have discovered that miR132/212 can negatively regulate the DAT expression and morphine rewarding effect through binding to the DAT 3’UTR. Following morphine administration, DAT expressions were elevated, concomitant with a decrease in miR-132/212 expression level. Furthermore, we designed a miR-132/212 sponge with AAV expression that could specifically adsorb miR-132/212 and found that miR-132/212 knockdown resulted in an increase in DAT expression and an enhanced place preference for morphine in rats. Conversely, when miR-132 was overexpressed, DAT expression decreased, accompanied by a reduction in morphine-induced CPP. To further validate the role of miR-132/212 in modulating dopamine through the regulation of DAT, we designed a DAT gene knockdown and overexpression experiment. The experimental results showed that when DAT was overexpressed, miR-132/212-induced low levels of dopamine transport were elevated. Conversely, when the DAT was knocked down, the dopamine transport was reduced, and the ability of miR-132/212 in regulating dopamine transport in neurons disappeared. Using microdialysis, we showed that miR-132 overexpression in the VTA increased extracellular DA levels in both the NAc and VTA, demonstrating that DAT alterations affect neuronal dopamine uptake and confirming miR-132/212’s regulatory role. Our findings demonstrate that miR-132 overexpression in the VTA suppresses morphine reward by downregulating DAT expression, an effect observed consistently across male, female, and adolescent rats, suggesting a potential age- and sex-independent mechanism for miR-132 in modulating opioid reward. We observed that chronic morphine administration downregulates miR-132/212 expression in the VTA, potentially leading to opioid receptor activation via an indirect mechanism. While mu-opioid receptors are predominantly expressed on GABAergic interneurons rather than dopaminergic neurons in the VTA, morphine primarily acts on these GABAergic neurons to inhibit their activity, leading to disinhibition and heightened firing of dopaminergic neurons [57–59]. This increased DA neuronal activity may subsequently alter intracellular signaling pathways, such as CREB-mediated transcription or BDNF signaling, which are critical regulators of miR-132/212 expression in dopaminergic neurons [9]. Beyond changes in DAT expression, these signaling adaptations in dopaminergic neurons could independently contribute to morphine-induced CPP by enhancing synaptic plasticity and reward consolidation in the mesolimbic pathway. Future studies should systematically investigate the brain-region-specific expression of these miRNAs and their upstream regulators and downstream molecular networks in substance use models to inform precise miRNA-based gene therapies. Future studies should directly investigate the role of miR-132/212 in morphine relapse to validate these findings in behaviorally relevant paradigms.

In summary, we have demonstrated that DAT is a direct target of miR-132/212, which can affect the expression of DAT at the cellular level and affect the dopamine selective uptake of DAT. Furthermore, the rat CPP experiment revealed that an overexpression of miR-132/212 in the VTA led to a reduction in DAT expression and concurrent decreased morphine preference in the rats. These findings hold promise for the development of effective anti-morphine dependence treatments by targeting the miR-132/212 signaling pathway in the future. We expect that miR-132/212 and its mimics will emerge as potent tools in the arsenal against opioid reward, offering new avenues for addiction therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The dopaminergic SK-N-SH neuroblastoma cell line (ATCC HTB-11) was maintained in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% antibiotic penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) and 0.1 g/L sodium pyruvate in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2/95% air at 37 °C. Retinoic acid (10 μmol/l; all-trans-retinoic acid, ATRA; Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was used to induce SK-N-SH cell differentiation.

Human liver cancer HepG2 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s minimal essential medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic P/S at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

All cell lines were purchased from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All media ingredients were obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Animals

Adult Sprague-Dawley rats (260 to 280 g, Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd) were habituated to housing conditions 2 weeks before testing. Animals were housed in groups of five per cage after arrival and individually housed after surgery with appropriate temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (50 ± 5%), as well as freely accessible water and food. Light duration was controlled on a 12 h light/dark cycle. All behavioral experiments were performed during the animal’s dark period. Animal care and experimentation were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee for Animal Use and Protection of Peking University.

Drugs, miRNAs and viruses

Morphine sulfate powder was provided by Qinhai Pharmaceutical (Xining, China). Morphine sulfate powder was dissolved in sterile saline and administered at 5 mg/kg (hypodermic injection, i.p.). All drugs were freshly prepared before the experiments.

AAV-CMV-GFP-miR132-3P sponge, AAV-CMV-GFP-miR212-3P sponge, AAV-CMV-GFP-miRNA sponge, AAV-U6-miR132-3P-CMV-RFP, and AAV-U6-mir132-3P-CMV-RFP were designed by webio (Jinan, China). miRNA mimics, anti-miRNAs and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were obtained from Ribobio (Guangzhou, China). The virus control, microRNAs, siRNAs, siRNA-sponge, and virus-sponge were dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Luciferase reporter assays

The SLC6A3 3’UTR was amplified from the SK-N-SH cellular genomic DNA, and the multiple cloning sites upstream of the synthetic Renilla luciferase gene (hRluc) was inserted into the psiCHECK2 vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at the Xho I and Not I sites, resulting in the psiCHECK2-SLC6A3 3’UTR vector. The mutation of the predicted seed regions of miR-132/212 within the human SLC6A3 3’UTR was generated by overlap extension PCR and then cloned into the psiCHECK2 vector. All of the constructs were confirmed by sequencing (Invitrogen, USA).

Each purified reporter plasmid (0.2 μg) was transfected into SK-N-SH cells at 70% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) for 4 h before introduction of miRNA mimics, anti-miRNAs or negative control duplexes (RiboBio or QiaGen, Hilden, Germany) for another 48 h. The luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Catalog Number E1910) according to the manufacturer’s and detected by a Victor X5 multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Renilla luciferase activity was normalized to the corresponding firefly luciferase activity.

Cell transfection with small RNAs

Transfection of miRNA mimics, anti-miRNAs and siRNAs was carried out using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The miRNA mimics (double-stranded RNA oligonucleotides), anti-miRNA (2’-O-methyl antisense oligonucleotides against the target miRNAs) and negative control duplexes were applied at a final concentration of 100 nM. After incubation of the small RNAs for 24–72 h, the cells were harvested for further analyses.

Fluorescence microscopy

The CDS-3’UTR fragment (coding sequence and 3’-untranslated region) of human SLC6A3 mRNA was generated by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) from total RNA extracted from SK-N-SH cells and fused downstream of the green fluorescent protein (GFP) coding sequence in the pcDNA3.1/NT-GFP-TOPO (Invitrogen, USA) vector at the PCR insert site, resulting in a pcDNA3.1-GFP-SLC6A3CDS-3’UTR fusion vector.

The purified reporter plasmid (0.2 μg) was transfected into HepG2 cells at 70% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) for 4 h before the introduction of miRNA mimics, anti-miRNAs or negative control duplexes for another 72 h [60, 61]. DAT-overexpressing HepG2 cells were then incubated at 37 °C with 10 ng/ml Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min. Finally, HepG2 cells were visualized and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a 10 × objective.

With a deep anesthesia by 1% pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, i.p.), rats were subjected to the intracardial perfusion with 100 ml PBS, pH 7.4, and 60 ml 4% PFA. Subsequently, the brains were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 6–8 h, followed by being kept in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h. Coronal brain sections (40 μm thick) were prepared by a frozen section microtome (VT1000S, Leica).

Analysis of mRNA and microRNA expression levels

cDNA synthesized from RNA extracted from rat brain tissue and the human neuroblastoma cell lines SK-N-SH under different conditions was used for qRT-PCR by the SV Total RNA Isolation System (Promega) and GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [60, 61]. Primer sequences used for amplification are described in Supplementary table 1. After the resultant cDNA was diluted 10-fold, real-time quantitative PCR was carried out in the CFX96 Real-time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (TAKARA, Dalian, China).

SLC6A3 data were analyzed using the Ct value. This was normalized to the Ct value of the endogenous control GAPDH to obtain the ΔCt. Normalizing the ΔCt of the sample of interest with that of the calibrator, we obtained the ΔΔCt. Finally, to determine the relative concentration of the target gene in the sample of interest, we applied the 2 − ΔΔCt formula.

Purification of small RNAs from human cells and animal tissues was performed using the miRNeasy Mini Kit, reverse transcription for quantitative Real-Time PCR using the miScript II RT Kit and Real-Time PCR for mature miRNA expression profiling using the miScript SYBR Green PCR Kit according to their manufacturer’s handbooks [60, 61]. All three kits were purchased from QiaGen. Data analysis was also performed using the ΔΔCt method of relative quantification for mature miRNA expression levels. U6 small nuclear RNA (RNU6) served as the endogenous control for the miRNA expression levels.

Analysis of dopamine levels

SK-N-SH and cells were plated in 24-well culture dishes (20,000 cells/well) and transfected with siRNA or miRNA mimics, as well as their control miRNAs, for 48 or 72 h. Cells were incubated with 5 μg/ml dopamine hydrochloride (Wuhan Pharmaceutical, Wuhan, China) for 5 h. During this process, desipramine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) or nomifensine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) can be optionally added to the cell culture medium to block NET or DAT, respectively. Cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and incubated with RIPA lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, pH 7.6), which contained a protease inhibitor cocktail completely free of EDTA (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Then, the cells were lysed by ultrasonication for 3 min. The levels of intracellular dopamine were measured using the Dopamine ELISA Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China, Catalog Number E-EL-0046c; or Rocky Mountain Diagnostics, Colorado Springs, CO, USA, Catalog Number BA-E-5300). The tests were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the plates were analyzed using a Microplate Reader (Bio-Rad).

Brain microdialysis

AAV-U6-miR132-3P-CMV-RFP (6.0×1013 TU/ml, 500 nl) and AAV-U6-CMV-RFP (6.0×1013 TU/ml, 500 nl) were bilaterally infused into the VTA, and the microdialysis catheter was ipsilaterally placed in the VTA (AP, −6.2 mm; ML, ±0.5 mm; DV, −8.0 mm) and NAc (AP, +2.4 mm; ML, ±1.7 mm; DV, −6.8 mm) in 2% isoflurane anesthetized rats followed by microdialysis experiments after 3 weeks recovery. The dialysis probes with a 2.0 mm membrane length were used (CMA 12, Sweden) to detect the concentration of DA in the VTA and NAc. The dialysis probe was continuously infused with filtered ddH2O and artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) for 20 min respectively ex vivo at a constant rate of 2 ul/min (Harvard Apparatus, MA, USA). The probe was aimed to the VTA or NAc through microdialysis catheter and required 2 h equilibrium duration with constantly pumping ACSF in vivo. Every sample was collected totally 30 ul with fixed rate of 2 ul/min. The standards and dialysate samples (20 μl) were injected into the high-performance liquid chromatography–electrochemical detector (HPLC-ECD) apparatus (Sykam, Germany; DECADE Lite, Netherlands) equipped with a chromatographic column (C18, 3 μm particle size, 100 × 2.1 mm; Thermo Scientific, USA). The mobile phase of HPLC was composed of methanol and buffer salt solution (3:37 (v/v)) and the pH was adjusted to 3. The flow rate was 0.2 ml/min and the detection duration was 20 min. The peak values were first normalized against an internal standard curve based on the current signal (y, nA × min). Subsequently, quantification was performed by comparison with an external standard curve using an Antec-ALEXYS On-Line Analysis System (Antec®, Netherlands). The probe recovery values ex vivo was approximately 14%.

Analysis of protein expression levels

Human neuroblastoma cell lines (SK-N-SH) [60, 61] and the brain tissues [62–65] of rats were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer. The lysate was centrifuged at 15 000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used as whole cell extracts. The protein extracts (50–100 μg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA, IPVH00010), and incubated with each primary antibody (anti-DAT or β-actin, 1/1000-1/2000) overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the PVDF membrane was incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies. Immunoreactivity was detected using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The bands underwent grayscale scanning and processing analysis with the imaging software ImageJ 1.50b (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). DAT antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Cat# ab111468, RRID: AB_11155293), and β-actin antibody was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology Inc. (Cat# 58169, RRID: AB_2750839).

Surgery and Intracranial Injection

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% for induction and 2% for maintenance, O2 flow rate 0.5 l/min) and placed in a stereotaxic apparatus. Injections were performed with Hamilton syringes, which were connected to a microinjection pump (World Precision Instruments, Inc. Sarasota, FL, USA).

AAV-CMV-GFP-miR132-3P sponge (3.0 × 1013 TU/ml, 500 nl), AAV-CMV-GFP-miR212-3P sponge (3.0 × 1013 TU/ml, 500 nl), AAV-CMV-GFP-miRNA sponge (3.0 × 1013 TU/ml, 500 nl). AAV-U6-miR132-3P-CMV-RFP (6.0 × 1013 TU/ml, 500 nl), and AAV-U6-CMV-RFP (6.0 × 1013 TU/ml, 500 nl) were bilaterally infused into the VTA (AP, -6.2 mm; ML, ±0.5 mm; DV, −8.0 mm) [66]. After injection, the pipette was left in the injection site for an additional 10 min to allow diffusion of the viral particles. The rats were allowed to recover from surgery for 3 weeks. The data from rats with missed target brain regions were excluded from analysis.

Morphine-induced Conditioned place preference and morphine chronic exposure

Morphine-induced CPP was performed using an unbiased, counterbalanced protocol [62, 64, 65, 67]. The CPP apparatus consisted of five identical three-chamber polyvinylchloride (PVC) boxes. The CPP apparatus contained two distinct chambers (27.9 cm long × 21.0 cm wide × 20.9 cm high) separated by a removable partition (12.1 cm long × 21.0 cm wide × 20.9 cm high with a smooth PVC floor). One chamber had a grid floor with black walls, and the other had an expanded metal (mesh) floor with black and white stripes on the walls. Three distinct chambers were separated by manual guillotine doors. The two larger chambers were equipped with four photobeams to record time and activity automatically and provided distinct contexts that were paired with morphine or saline injections. On preconditioning and test days, the partition had an opening to allow the rat access to both chambers. On conditioning days, this was replaced by a solid partition. Baseline preference was assessed by placing the rats in the center compartment of the CPP apparatus and allowing ad libitum access to all compartments for 15 min (preconditioning test). Rats were then assigned a chamber to be paired with morphine in an unbiased counterbalanced design. The rats were trained for 8 consecutive days with alternating injections of morphine (5 mg/ml/kg, i.p.) or saline (1 ml/kg, i.p.) in both compartments and immediately confined to one chamber for 45 min. Then, the rats were returned to their home cages. For morphine chronic exposure [31], the rats were daily injected for 8 days with morphine (5 mg/ml/kg, i.p.) or saline (1 ml/kg, i.p.).Twenty-four hours after conditioning or morphine exposure, the rats were tested for morphine preference by allowing access to both chambers for 15 min in a drug-free state. The experimental conditions during testing were the same as during the initial baseline preference assessment. The CPP score was defined as the time (in seconds) spent in the morphine-paired chamber minus the time spent in the saline-paired chamber during CPP testing.

Cannula implantation and microinjection

Rats were implanted with bilateral guide cannula (62501, 62502, RWD, shenzhen) targeting the VTA under anesthesia. Following a 7-day recovery period, microinjections were performed during the CPP conditioning phase. Twenty minutes before each morphine injection, the DAT inhibitor GBR12909 (1 μg/0.5 μl/side, HY-13217A, MedChemExpress) or vehicle was infused bilaterally into the VTA over 3 min, with the injection cannula left in place for an additional minute to ensure diffusion. Cannula placements were verified histologically post-hoc, and only data from animals with correct bilateral placements were included in the analysis.

Tissue sample preparation

Tissue sample preparation was based on previous reports [62–65, 67]. The rats were decapitated without anesthesia 2 h after the end of the 15 min CPP test. The brains were then quickly removed, frozen in −60 °C N-hexane, and transferred to a −80 °C freezer. We used a freezing cryostat (−20 °C) to make 1 mm thick coronal sections located approximately 2.1–2.6 mm from bregma. Bilateral tissue punches (12 or 16 gauge) of the NAc core or shell were then taken and pooled together. The above procedures were performed at 4 °C. The mRNA and protein concentrations of all samples were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fish Scientific, Waltham, MA USA). Samples were further diluted in the appropriate solvent to equalize the concentrations.

Open field test

The open field was a white Plexiglas box (40 × 40 × 40 cm), and the rats were allowed to move freely in the box for 5 min. The total distance traveled by the rat was recorded and scored by Ethovision software.

Sucrose preference test

Rats were individually habituated to a bottle containing 2% sucrose and a second bottle containing water on either side of the cage, and the placement of the bottles was changed every day. During the first 3 days, baseline sucrose and water intake were recorded. After deprivation of both food and water for 24 h, the mice were exposed to a bottle containing sucrose and a bottle containing water, but not food, for 12 h. Sucrose preference was expressed as (sucrose/(sucrose + water)) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of at least three independent experiments performed in duplicate. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using simple randomization. Behavioral testing and data analysis were conducted by experimenters blinded to group allocation. One-way analysis of variance was performed to determine the significance among groups using SPSS Statistics 19.0 (IBM, USA). All post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test. Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For clarity, the post hoc analyses are indicated by asterisks in the figures but are not described in the results section.
