Comparative Prognostic Analysis of Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss With Vertigo in Pediatric and Adult Patients
Authors: Changshuo Shan, Guohui Chen, Jin Li, Feifei Guo, Yun Gao, Jiao Zhang, Jing Guan, Dayong Wang, Qiuju Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare the association of vertigo with rates of frequency‐specific hearing recovery in pediatric and adult patients with unilateral idiopathic sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL).MethodsA total of 80 pediatric and 444 adult patients hospitalized with SSNHL were included in this study. Clinical and audiological data were collected, and frequency‐specific hearing recovery percentages before and after treatment were compared between pediatric and adult SSNHL patients with and without vertigo.ResultsIn pediatric SSNHL patients with vertigo, the median recovery rates at 250–500 Hz, 1000–2000 Hz, and 4000–8000 Hz were 62.5%, 28.2%, and 21.1%, respectively, whereas those without vertigo showed higher median recovery rates of 90.3%, 82.1%, and 56.5%. Similarly, adult SSNHL patients with vertigo exhibited median recovery rates of 53.2%, 33.7%, and 24.6% across the same frequency ranges, compared to 88.9%, 76.6%, and 53.3% in adults without vertigo. In both pediatric and adult cohorts, hearing recovery decreased progressively with increasing frequency. Patients with vertigo exhibited consistently lower recovery across all frequency bands compared to those without vertigo. However, no statistically significant differences in frequency‐specific recovery were observed between pediatric and adult SSNHL cohorts.ConclusionThe incidence of vertigo increases with the severity of hearing loss in SSNHL ranging from moderately severe to total deafness. Vertigo is a prognostic factor associated with poorer hearing recovery across all frequencies, underscoring its importance as a critical clinical indicator in the management of SSNHL.Level of Evidence3

Introduction

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is defined as an unexplained sensorineural hearing loss of ≥ 30 dB HL across three consecutive frequencies occurring within 72 h, which can develop at any life stage. According to clinical practice guidelines, its annual incidence ranges from 5 to 27 cases per 100,000 individuals, with a peak onset between 43 and 53 years and predominantly unilateral involvement [1]. Pediatric SSNHL is relatively uncommon, accounting for only 6.6% of all SSNHL cases [2]. The underlying pathogenesis remains unclear, although proposed mechanisms include viral infections, vascular compromise, and autoimmune reactions [1, 3]. While cochlear dysfunction is the primary manifestation, vestibular involvement may occur in a subset of patients [4, 5].

Vertigo is a common comorbidity in SSNHL, with clinical vestibular assessments typically including caloric testing, the video head impulse test (vHIT), cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (cVEMP), and ocular vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (oVEMP). Epidemiological studies report that 28%–57% of SSNHL cases experience vertigo [6, 7]. Most existing studies focus on adult populations, showing that SSNHL accompanied by vertigo is associated with more severe hearing loss and poorer prognosis, potentially due to dysfunction of the posterior semicircular canal [8, 9]. In contrast, pediatric vestibular testing remains challenging due to longer test durations and procedure‐related discomfort, limiting its clinical applicability and contributing to the scarcity of pediatric data.

Although several studies suggest that vertigo is not an independent prognostic factor in pediatric SSNHL [10, 11, 12, 13, 14], significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the comparative prognostic influence of vertigo between pediatric and adult cohorts, particularly in terms of frequency‐specific hearing recovery patterns in cases with and without vertigo.

This study aimed to systematically compare frequency‐specific hearing recovery patterns between SSNHL patients with and without vertigo in both pediatric and adult SSNHL cohorts. By analyzing therapeutic outcomes across different frequency bands, we sought to elucidate the prognostic role of vertigo in distinct age groups and provide insights to guide clinical evaluation and management of SSNHL.

Materials and Methods

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed the clinical data of patients admitted to the Department of Otolaryngology at our institution between October 2008 and June 2023. A total of 80 pediatric and 444 adult patients with unilateral SSNHL were included. Inclusion criteria: (1) Sensorineural hearing loss exceeding 30 dB HL at three consecutive frequencies within 72 h; (2) Age between 6 and 59 years; (3) Treatment initiated within 14 days after onset. Exclusion criteria: (1) Bilateral SSNHL or pre‐existing contralateral hearing impairment; (2) Recurrent or fluctuating hearing loss; (3) History of vertigo or concurrent vestibular disorders, such as Ménière's disease; (4) Other causes including middle ear pathology, retrocochlear lesions, or hereditary hearing loss. Clinical data were collected including age, sex, time from onset to treatment, and accompanying symptoms (tinnitus, vertigo, ear fullness). The presence of vertigo was defined based on descriptions in the admission records of each patient, as reported by the patient or their family members, and was characterized as a sensation of spinning. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Approval No: S2017‐024‐01). As a retrospective analysis, the requirement of informed consent was waived by the committee.

Audiometric Assessment

Comprehensive audiological evaluations were performed using the independently developed Medical Center of Clinical Audiology Information System (MCCAIS, Registration No: 2009SR06436). Pure‐tone audiometry was conducted with a MADSEN Astera clinical audiometer in a standard soundproof booth, following the testing protocol outlined in the national standard GB/T 16296.1–2018. Test frequencies ranged from 250 to 8000 Hz, with corresponding maximum output levels of 100, 115, 120, 120, 120, and 105 dB HL, respectively. When no response was detected at the maximum volume output level, the hearing threshold was recorded as the maximum output plus 5 dB. Objective audiological examinations, including auditory brainstem response (ABR) and distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE), were integrated to exclude functional hearing loss and ensure result accuracy. All audiometric equipment was routinely calibrated according to standard procedures. The initial hearing status was defined as the audiometric results obtained at admission, while the final hearing status referred to those measured either upon complete recovery or at least 1 month post‐treatment, with a maximum follow‐up period of 6 months.

The degree of hearing loss was classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) 2021 criteria, calculated as the four‐frequency pure‐tone average (4F PTA) of air conduction thresholds at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz. Based on the admission 4F PTA values, hearing loss severity was categorized as: 50 ≤ 4F PTA < 65 dB HL (moderately severe); 65 ≤ 4F PTA < 80 dB HL (severe); 80 ≤ 4F PTA < 95 dB HL (profound); and 4F PTA ≥ 95 dB HL (total deafness) [15].

Treatment and Outcome Assessment

The treatment protocol is based on the Chinese Guideline for Diagnosis and Treatment of Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss (2015) [16]. All participants were managed according to a standardized basic treatment regimen. The corticosteroid regimen for adults was as follows: intravenous infusion of dexamethasone sodium phosphate injection was administered for a total cumulative dose of 45 mg (10 mg/day for 3 days, followed by 5 mg/day for 3 days). If corticosteroids had been previously administered in outpatient or other settings, the corresponding dose was subtracted. After systemic administration, the treatment was switched to retroauricular injections of methylprednisolone sodium succinate (40 mg per injection), administered every other day for a total of 4–5 injections. For pediatric patients with sudden hearing loss, the specific dosage was adjusted based on the patient's body weight and overall clinical condition. Hemorrheologic medication: Ginkgo biloba extract injection (87.5 mg) was administered once daily for 7 days in children and 10 days in adults. Neurotrophic medication: Mecobalamin injection (0.5 mg) was administered once daily for 7 days in children and 10 days in adults. For patients presenting with vertigo, betahistine mesylate injection and alprostadil injection were also administered. For patients who responded poorly to initial treatment, interventions such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy and acupuncture were employed as salvage measures [17].

Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated by calculating the percentage improvement of the two‐frequency pure‐tone average (2F PTA). Hearing improvement percentages were determined according to the 2011 Spanish Consensus on SSNHL [18]. Frequency‐specific analyses were conducted across three bands—250–500 Hz, 1000–2000 Hz, and 4000–8000 Hz—and mean hearing improvement percentages were computed for each subgroup. The formula used for calculating hearing improvement percentages is provided below. Percentage recovery=2FPTAinitial−2FPTAfinal2FPTAinitial−2FPTAinitial contralateral×100%

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and differences between groups were analyzed using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, with modified significance thresholds applied for multiple testing. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t‐test. Non‐normally distributed variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. All tests were two‐tailed, and p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 524 patients with unilateral SSNHL were included, comprising 80 pediatric and 444 adult SSNHL cases. The pediatric group had a median age of 12 years (IQR 10–14.75), while the adult group had a median age of 39 years (IQR 32–49). The overall age range was 6–59 years. In the pediatric cohort, the median time from symptom onset to treatment was 7 days (IQR 4–10.75). There were 38 males (47.5%) and 42 females (52.5%), with vertigo reported in 55 cases (68.8%) and ear fullness in 21 cases (26.3%). Among adults, the median time to presentation was 6 days (IQR 3–9), with 236 males (53.2%) and 208 females (46.8%). Vertigo occurred in 246 cases (55.4%), and ear fullness in 214 cases (48.2%). Statistically significant intergroup differences were observed in treatment onset time, vertigo incidence, and ear fullness prevalence in children and adults (p = 0.005, p = 0.026, and p < 0.001 respectively; Table 1).

Hearing Recovery at Various Frequencies Before and After Treatment

In both pediatric and adult SSNHL patients, recovery before and after treatment was compared between the with‐vertigo and without‐vertigo subgroup. Significant post‐treatment hearing improvement across all frequencies was observed in both groups (p < 0.05; Figure 1). Patients with vertigo exhibited greater improvement at low frequencies compared to high frequencies. Additionally, patients without vertigo demonstrated consistently higher recovery rates across all frequencies than those with vertigo.

Frequency‐Specific Recovery Percentages in Pediatric and Adult Cohorts With and Without Vertigo

Among Pediatric SSNHL patients with vertigo, the median recovery rates were 62.5% (29.6–91.7), 28.2% (14.8–54.3), and 21.1% (7.7–31.4) at 250–500 Hz, 1000–2000 Hz, and 4000–8000 Hz, respectively. In contrast, pediatric patients without vertigo achieved significantly higher recovery rates of 90.3% (62.0–95.2), 82.1% (33.3–92.4), and 56.5% (24.6–89.7) at corresponding frequencies (p = 0.025, p = 0.002, p < 0.001; Figure 2A).

In the adult SSNHL cohort, patients with vertigo showed median hearing recovery rates (IQR) of 53.2% (34.9–86.7), 33.7% (21.5–47.8), and 24.6% (11.3–38.4) across the same frequency ranges. Adults without vertigo exhibited markedly higher recovery rates of 88.9% (59.2–100.0), 76.6% (36.3–93.6), and 53.3% (22.2–86.7) at corresponding frequencies (p < 0.001 for all comparisons; Figure 2B).

Comparative analysis of hearing recovery percentages between pediatric and adult patients with vertigo revealed no statistically significant differences across 250–500 Hz, 1000–2000 Hz, and 4000–8000 Hz frequency bands (Figure 3A). Likewise, no significant intergroup differences were observed in hearing recovery percentages between pediatric and adult cohorts without vertigo (Figure 3B).

In pediatric patients with vertigo, significant recovery discrepancies were observed among all frequency pairs: 250–500 Hz vs. 1000–2000 Hz (p = 0.001), 250–500 Hz vs. 4000–8000 Hz (p < 0.001), and 1000–2000 Hz vs. 4000–8000 Hz (p = 0.01). However, pediatric patients without vertigo showed statistically significant differences only between 250–500 Hz and 4000–8000 Hz (p = 0.037), with no significant variations among other frequency pairs.

In adult SSNHL patients with vertigo, statistically significant inter‐frequency differences in therapeutic recovery percentages were observed across all pairwise comparisons: 250–500 Hz vs. 1000–2000 Hz (p < 0.001), 250–500 Hz vs. 4000–8000 Hz (p < 0.001), and 1000–2000 Hz vs. 4000–8000 Hz (p < 0.001). Similarly, adult patients without vertigo also demonstrated significant inter‐frequency variations: 250–500 Hz vs. 1000–2000 Hz (p < 0.001), 250–500 Hz vs. 4000–8000 Hz (p < 0.001), and 1000–2000 Hz vs. 4000–8000 Hz (p = 0.002).

Discussion

SSNHL constitutes an otologic emergency, with vertigo being a frequent comorbid symptom. While most prior research has focused on adult populations, pediatric investigations remain limited. In the present study, we demonstrated a positive correlation between the degree of hearing loss and vertigo prevalence across both pediatric and adult SSNHL cohorts. Notably, cases with vertigo exhibited significantly poorer hearing recovery compared to those without vertigo, with similar patterns observed in children and adults. These findings underscore vertigo as a clinically meaningful prognostic indicator in pediatric SSNHL management.

The pediatric cohort comprised subjects with a median age of 12 years (IQR 10–14.75), with a minimum inclusion threshold of 6 years. This age criterion was established based on developmental considerations: children under 6 often demonstrate limited perceptual and communicative abilities necessary for accurate symptom reporting, whereas those ≥ 6 years generally possess sufficient cognitive maturity for reliable clinical evaluation [12]. We focused on cases with moderately severe or greater hearing loss (≥ 50 dB HL) for two primary reasons. First, this criterion mitigates the confounding effects of spontaneous recovery, which occurs in approximately 35%–39% of SSNHL cases and is inversely correlated with the degree of hearing loss [19]. Second, including SSNHL patients with moderately severe or greater hearing loss helps exclude vestibular‐related comorbidities, such as Ménière's disease, which can initially present as sudden low‐frequency hearing loss resembling early‐stage SSNHL [20].

Prior studies have predominantly suggested that pediatric patients often present with a greater degree of hearing loss compared to adults [12]. However, our analysis revealed no significant differences between pediatric and adult cohorts within the moderately severe to total deafness categories. Our retrospective statistical analysis demonstrated a lower prevalence of mild hearing loss in pediatric cohorts compared to adults, a finding potentially attributable to the subtle impact of mild auditory deficits on daily communication, which may evade detection by both children and their caregivers. In contrast, moderately severe or greater hearing loss, which substantially impairs speech communication abilities and sound localization, is more readily detectable in clinical settings due to its overt functional consequences.

Multiple prognostic factors influence SSNHL outcomes, including age, concomitant symptoms, degree of hearing loss, audiogram curve types, and treatment onset time [1, 13, 21]. Both vertigo and ear fullness constitute subjective discomforts in SSNHL. In this study, vertigo was reported in 68.8% of pediatric patients versus 55.4% of adults, while ear fullness occurred in 26.3% of children versus 48.2% of adults. Lu et al. demonstrated no significant differences in the prevalence of vertigo and ear fullness between pediatric and adult populations, whereas a meta‐analysis conducted by Wood et al. reported lower rates of those symptoms in pediatric SSNHL compared to the current findings [13]. These discrepancies may reflect the exclusive inclusion of hospitalized patients with moderately severe to total deafness hearing loss, a younger age of the pediatric cohorts, and potential discrepancies in symptom perception and reporting.

The prognostic relevance of ear fullness remains controversial. Wang et al. reported superior hearing recovery outcomes in patients with ear fullness compared to those without [22], whereas Zhou et al. found that intensified subjective grading of ear fullness correlated with a greater degree of hearing loss in SSNHL patients, though no direct association with hearing prognosis was established [23]. These conflicting findings highlight the need for further investigation into the relationship between ear fullness and SSNHL outcomes. Consistent with previous studies, our data confirmed strong correlations between the degree of hearing loss and vertigo occurrence. Pediatric and adult SSNHL patients with moderately severe to total deafness exhibited vertigo rates ranging from 37.5% to 88.6% and 20.9% to 79.6%, respectively. Nonparametric statistical analysis revealed that a statistically significant difference between the two groups was observed only in the severe hearing loss category, which may be attributed to the small sample size.

Prior research on pediatric SSNHL has primarily concentrated on clinical characteristics and prognostic factors while studies examining vestibular function remain limited due to the challenges of performing comprehensive vestibular assessments in young children. Most investigations reported no significant association between vertigo and pediatric SSNHL prognosis [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]; however, contradictory evidence exists. Li et al. and You et al. identified vertigo as a risk factor for poorer outcomes [24, 25], whereas Kim et al. reported it as an adverse prognostic marker without definitive clinical significance [26]. In our comparative analysis, no statistically significant differences were observed in recovery percentages across 250–500 Hz, 1000–2000 Hz, and 4000–8000 Hz frequency bands between pediatric and adult SSNHL cases with vertigo, indicating analogous recovery patterns. Similarly, recovery percentages across these frequency bands did not differ between pediatric and adult SSNHL patients without vertigo. Collectively, these findings support vertigo as a clinically relevant prognostic indicator in pediatric SSNHL.

SSNHL frequently involves concurrent vestibular dysfunction in addition to cochlear damage. Multiple factors, including ischemia, inflammation, and immune mechanisms, have been implicated in SSNHL pathogenesis. The complex interplay of anatomical proximity, neural innervation, and vascular supply underlies patterns of vestibular impairment in vertigo‐associated SSNHL, with vestibular damage potentially extending from cochlear lesions to peri‐cochlear vestibular structures before progressing distally [6, 9, 27]. Saccular and utricular dysfunction predominates over semicircular canal involvement in SSNHL patients with vertigo [6, 27], with the posterior and horizontal semicircular canals being more susceptible than the superior counterparts [28, 29, 30]. Cluster analyses have identified posterior semicircular canal impairment as a poor prognostic indicator [9, 28], potentially explained by shared vascular supply from the common cochlear artery to both cochlea and posterior semicircular canal, where ischemic compromise may limit recovery [28]. Our data demonstrate a progressive decline in hearing recovery percentages with increasing frequency in patients with vertigo, with significantly poorer restoration compared to those without vertigo, suggesting that vertigo predominantly affects mid‐ to high‐frequency rehabilitation. Previous studies employing Siegel's criteria or the American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery (AAO‐HNS) guidelines, which focus on 500–2000 Hz and 500–4000 Hz ranges, may have overlooked critical high‐frequency recovery above 4000 Hz, potentially explaining inconsistent prognostic correlations. Our comprehensive evaluation across 250–500 Hz, 1000–2000 Hz, and 4000–8000 Hz bands provides enhanced resolution for assessing frequency‐specific effects of vertigo.

Even in SSNHL patients without vertigo, potential damage to the vestibular organs may still exist despite the absence of subjective vertigo symptoms [31]. Qian et al. demonstrated vestibular dysfunction in 80.4% of such cases [7], while Hong et al. reported vestibular dysfunction rates ranging from 1.3% to 20.3% across different vestibular structures [28], potentially reflecting differences in lesion severity, individual sensory thresholds, and compensatory neuroplasticity [9, 27]. Previous studies have also found posterior semicircular canal injury and high‐frequency hearing loss ≥ 30 dB as independent risk factors for incomplete hearing recovery [30]. Neuroanatomically, SSNHL without vertigo primarily affects inferior vestibular nerve pathways while sparing superior counterparts [32]. Consistently, our findings demonstrate significantly poorer recovery at 4000–8000 Hz compared to 250–500 Hz in both pediatric and adult cohorts without vertigo. However, vestibular dysfunction may have been present, potentially accounting for the poorer high‐frequency recovery observed in some SSNHL patients without overt vertigo.

This study has two primary limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size of the pediatric cohort (n = 80) precluded stratified analysis of vestibular impairment severity and its differential prognostic impact across varying degrees of hearing loss. Secondly, vestibular dysfunction patterns were not directly investigated in pediatric SSNHL. Future studies should prioritize larger pediatric populations to elucidate the relationships among vertigo manifestations, vestibular functional status, and frequency‐specific hearing recovery across severity‐stratified subgroups.

Conclusions

The severity of hearing loss in SSNHL is positively associated with the incidence of vertigo. Vertigo is a prognostic factor associated with poorer hearing recovery across all frequencies. As a critical clinical marker in pediatric SSNHL, vertigo assessment holds substantial prognostic significance for therapeutic outcome evaluation.
