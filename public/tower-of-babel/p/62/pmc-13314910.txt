Unraveling the economics of pulmonary embolism care in a 23-year resource utilization study
Authors: Pablo Alberto Cuartas, Claire Ferguson, Danylo Orlov, Dewi Sihaloho, Ron Shemtov, Solomon Seckler, Sherene E. Sharath, Panos Kougias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe systematic assessment of resource utilization in cost-intensive diseases such as pulmonary embolism (PE) is important to optimize health system efficiency. We examined the association between treatment modality, cost, and length of stay among inpatients diagnosed with PE.MethodsUsing data from the National Inpatient Sample (1998-2021), we performed a retrospective trend analysis. Inpatient charges were converted to costs using the cost-to-charge ratio. Average cost differences between catheter-directed thrombolysis, anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed embolectomy, and surgical embolectomy were assessed using survey-weighted linear regressions, with hospital clustering. Differences in median length of stay, by treatment modality, were modeled using quantile regression.ResultsThe sample included 1,375,419 admissions; 1,338,332 (97.3%) received anticoagulation, 22,934 (1.7%) underwent systemic thrombolysis, 8363 (0.6%) underwent catheter-directed thrombolysis, 3453 (0.3%) underwent catheter-directed embolectomy, and 2337 (0.2%) underwent surgical embolectomy. Compared with catheter-directed thrombolysis, surgical embolectomy, catheter-directed embolectomy, and systemic thrombolysis were associated with higher average costs (surgical embolectomy, +$46,156; catheter-directed embolectomy, +$9042; systemic thrombolysis, +$3094, respectively; all P < .001); anticoagulation was less expensive ($9707 less expensive; P < .001). The median length of stay was longer after anticoagulation (+0.2 day), systemic thrombolysis (+1.4 days), and surgical embolectomy (+5.6 days; all P < .001). Catheter-directed embolectomy was associated with a shorter median length of stay (−0.6 day; P < .001).ConclusionsSubstantial cost disparities exist among PE treatment modalities, spanning thousands of dollars. Although catheter-directed embolectomy was associated with the shortest median length of stay, the least expensive treatment modality was anticoagulation and the most expensive was surgical embolectomy. These insights offer guidance for optimizing resource allocation strategies in managing this prevalent postoperative complication.

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a highly prevalent disease and the third most common cause of cardiovascular death.1 In addition to significant adverse physiological effects, the economic burden of PE is notable. PE-related admissions cost the United States >$1 billion each year.2 The average length of hospital stay has been cited as between 3 and 4 days,3 which is cost-intensive for hospitals to manage and portends significant loss of productivity. For inpatients admitted to the hospital for other causes, iatrogenic PE prolongs the length of stay and strains limited hospital resources.

In recent years, the armamentarium of approaches to manage PE has broadened.4 Correspondingly, there is an increased demand for longitudinal high-quality analyses of outcomes, cost differences, and effectiveness comparing new and existing treatment modalities. Depending on PE severity, patient characteristics, and surgeon preferences, standard treatment options include anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed thrombolysis, catheter-directed embolectomy, or surgical embolectomy.5 However, studies have reported conflicting evidence on the cost variations and lack a national perspective, particularly in the setting of minimally invasive therapies.6,7

The systematic, time-dependent assessment of resource utilization in cost-intensive diseases such as PE is critical to optimize health system efficiency. Therefore, we sought to determine the associations between PE treatment modality, cost, and length of stay among inpatients using a national, multiyear dataset.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study is a longitudinal trend analysis of resource use, represented by cost and length of stay, of PE treatment types, using data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) from 1998 to 2021. As the largest publicly available resource for inpatient data related to outcomes, costs, and lengths of stay,8 the NIS allows for analysis pooled by region, income, comorbid conditions, and other important covariates. Importantly, the data approximate 35 million hospitalizations nationally from approximately 7 million observed hospitalizations annually. Compared with other electronic health record-sourced databases, the NIS has a similar data distribution pattern across all elements,9 making it a valuable resource for the analysis of costs and lengths of stay. To estimate costs, the NIS collects data on the total charges billed for each hospitalization. The Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project provides hospital-level cost-to-charge ratio files for each year, which allows for converting the charges into costs.10

Analytic sample

Our sample was obtained from a cohort of all NIS patients diagnosed with PE between 1998 and 2021 (n = 1,381,082). At the time of this analysis, the latest available data were from 2021. We selected this study period to ensure that ≥5 years' worth of data were available since the introduction of the latest therapeutic device, catheter-directed embolectomy. We excluded those patients who were <18 years of age at admission (n = 5663), which resulted in a final sample size of 1,375,419 (Fig 1). For catheter-directed embolectomy, data were only available starting in 2015 and through 2021. Thus, the analysis of catheter-directed embolectomy cost and length of stay covered 2015 to 2021. All information regarding specific diagnoses was extracted from the NIS database using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM), and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. Outcome

The primary outcome was the inflation-adjusted cost associated with each PE case. Inpatient charges for each hospitalization, provided by the NIS, were converted to costs using the HCPU's cost-to-charge ratio files, to produce a single composite unit of indirect and direct costs. The Federal Reserve's consumer price index was used to inflation-adjust costs to reflect values in 2024, the year data were extracted. A secondary outcome was length of stay, defined as the number of days between the admission date and the discharge date.11 For both outcomes, the results were stratified by treatment modality.

Predictors

The primary predictor was treatment modality, including catheter-directed thrombolysis (reference), anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed embolectomy, and surgical embolectomy. Catheter-directed thrombolysis, systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed embolectomy, and surgical embolectomy were identified based on their respective ICD-9 and ICD-10 procedure codes, in accordance with previously published work12, 13, 14 (Supplementary Tables I and II, online only). Patients who underwent multiple treatment types were classified according to the most invasive procedure received. Because there is no procedure code for anticoagulation in the NIS, patients diagnosed with PE who did not receive any other treatment type were assumed to have been treated with anticoagulation.

Other variables considered as potential predictors included age, sex, race/ethnicity, primary insurance payer, income quartile, and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index. We also examined institution-level indicators such as hospital region (Northeast. Midwest, South, West), Medicaid burden quintile, and household income quartile. These data were obtained based on information collected in the NIS database. Hospital Medicaid burden was calculated based on the proportion of patients with Medicaid insurance at each facility, categorized into quintiles. Facilities with a low proportion of patients with Medicaid insurance were defined as the lowest quintile, with ascending quintiles representing ascending proportion of patients with Medicaid insurance.15 The Elixhauser Comorbidity Index was included to adjust for a composite of 38 different pre-existing conditions16 based on secondary diagnoses derived from hospital administrative data.17

Statistical analyses

Data management and analyses were performed using Stata SE 18.0 (StataCorp) using a significance level of an α 0.05 for all analyses. Figures were created with Stata SE 18.0 (StataCorp) and R (v4.5.1; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The mean adjusted cost of hospitalization for patients undergoing each procedure type was plotted by year. Average cost differences were assessed using survey-weighted linear regressions, with hospital clustering. Length of stay and inflation-adjusted costs were studied across different treatment modalities, creating a predictive model. Differences in the median length of stay were modeled using quantile regression. For both outcomes, catheter-directed thrombolysis was the reference category.

Results

Patient demographics

The analysis sample included 1,375,419 adults diagnosed with PE between 1998 and 2021. Among them, 1,338,332 (97.3%) underwent anticoagulation, 22,934 (1.7%) systemic thrombolysis, 8363 (0.6%) catheter-directed thrombolysis, 3453 (0.3%) catheter-directed embolectomy, and 2337 (0.2%) surgical embolectomy (Table I). Most of the sample was female (729,853 [53%]), in alignment with the sex proportion of the largest group, anticoagulation, which comprised 53% (n = 711,752) of the sample. The other four treatment groups reported higher proportions of males (systemic thrombolysis, 11,501 [50%]; catheter-directed thrombolysis, 4439 [53%]; catheter-directed embolectomy, 1837 [53%]; and surgical embolectomy, 1203 [52%]). The mean patient age was oldest in the anticoagulation group (62.9 ± 17.2 years; P < .001) and youngest in the surgical embolectomy group (55.52 ± 16.24 years; P < .001), likely reflecting a preference to avoid invasive treatment modalities in patients of advanced age. Similarly, all treatment groups that underwent invasive procedures had a younger mean age than anticoagulation (systemic thrombolysis, 57.8 ± 16.6 years [P < .001]; catheter-directed thrombolysis, Annual trends in cost by treatment modality

After adjusting for inflation, hospitalization for surgical embolectomy, systemic thrombolysis, and anticoagulation increased in cost, whereas catheter-directed thrombolysis and catheter-directed embolectomy became less costly overall. Of the modalities that have become more expensive over the time frame herein considered, systemic thrombolysis represents the greatest percentage increase in cost. The mean annual inflation-adjusted cost of systemic thrombolysis increased by 38% (Fig 2). Surgical embolectomy saw the second greatest percent increase in cost over time, increasing by 32%. The mean annual inflation-adjusted cost of anticoagulation increased as well, by 24%. Of the treatment methodologies that have become less costly over the time period considered in this study, catheter-directed embolectomy saw the greatest percentage of cost decrease at 25%. Catheter-directed thrombolysis decreased by 12%.Fig 2Annual inflation-adjusted hospitalization costs by pulmonary embolism (PE) treatment type.

Although the annual inflation-adjusted cost of hospitalization for catheter-directed embolectomy was initially more expensive than systemic thrombolysis from 2015 to 2020, this relationship reversed in 2021, after which systemic thrombolysis has been more expensive than catheter-directed embolectomy. Although the annual cost of catheter-directed thrombolysis was greater than systemic thrombolysis, that relationship briefly reversed from 2008 to 2009 and systemic thrombolysis was more costly for that 1 year only; the cost of catheter-directed thrombolysis was again higher than that of systemic thrombolysis thereafter. The cost of catheter-directed thrombolysis then remained higher than that of systemic thrombolysis until 2013, when systemic thrombolysis became more costly than catheter-directed thrombolysis, which has been the case through the end of this study period.

Independent predictors of hospitalization cost by treatment modality

The treatment modality implemented was an independent predictor of the cost of hospitalization. Compared with catheter-directed thrombolysis, surgical embolectomy added $46,156 (95% confidence interval [CI], $41,680-$50,632; P < .001), catheter-directed embolectomy $9042 (95% CI, $7142-$10,942; P < .001), and systemic thrombolysis $3094 (95% CI, $2200-$3989; P < .001) to costs, while anticoagulation was $9707 less expensive (95% CI, −$10,364 to −$9051; P < .001) (Table II). Female sex was associated with lower costs by $3019 (95% CI, −$3183 to −$2855; P < .001). Each additional year of age decreased cost by $124 (95% CI, −$133 to −$115; P < .001). Analysis of zip code, income quartile, and cost of hospitalization revealed an association with socioeconomic status. Hospitalizations for the highest income quartile were more costly by $2710 (95% CI, $2286-$3134; P < .001), whereas hospitalizations for patients in the second highest quartile were $1071 more (95% CI, $787-$1355; P < .001). Independent predictors of length of stay

Compared with catheter-directed thrombolysis, all other treatment modalities were associated with statistically significant, albeit not always clinically meaningful, differences in lengths of stay. Multivariable regression analysis showed that anticoagulation was associated with an increase in length of stay by an average of 0.24 day (95% CI, 0.11-0.36 day; P < .001), systemic thrombolysis by an average of 1.37 days (95% CI, 1.22-1.52 days; P < .001), and surgical embolectomy by 5.61 days (95% CI, 5.33-5.89 days) (Table III). Conversely, catheter-directed embolectomy was associated with a shorter length of stay, on average 0.57 fewer days (95% CI, −0.796 to –0.340 day). Each additional decade of age was associated with a 0.10-day longer length of stay (P < .001), likely reflecting disposition-related issues that are more prevalent in older patients. Additionally, an increased comorbidity burden increased length of stay by 0.60 day for each unit of incremental increase of the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (95% CI, 0.592 to 0.610 day; P < .001). Patients from the highest income quartile experienced a 0.22-day decrease in length of stay (95% CI, −0.26 to −0.19 day; P < .001). A high Medicaid burden increased length of stay by 0.11 day (95% CI, 0.68-0.15 day). Discussion

In this nationally representative analysis of cases of PE, we observed several important findings regarding treatment cost trends, relative expenditures across modalities, and predictors of hospitalization cost and length of stay. First, inflation-adjusted hospitalization costs for patients treated with surgical embolectomy, systemic thrombolysis, and anticoagulation increased over time, whereas costs decreased for patients treated with catheter-directed thrombolysis and catheter-directed embolectomy. Second, relative to catheter-directed thrombolysis, surgical embolectomy predicted a substantially increased cost of hospitalization, catheter-directed embolectomy and systemic thrombolysis predicted a moderately increased cost, and anticoagulation predicted a decreased cost. Third, catheter-directed embolectomy predicted a shorter length of stay compared with catheter-directed thrombolysis, whereas anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, and surgical embolectomy predicted longer admissions (Fig 3). Prior investigations of cost in PE management have been limited by smaller cohorts, restricted comparisons across treatment types, and a lack of longitudinal cost evaluations. As expected, our study found the least invasive treatment, therapeutic anticoagulation, to be associated with the lowest costs, which likely reflects its isolated use in patients with milder disease and a lower level of inpatient care. The finding that systemic thrombolysis predicts increased hospital cost aligns with prior work, including a multicenter European study identifying systemic thrombolysis as an independent predictor of hospital cost.18 Our study extends these findings by suggesting that hospitalization costs associated with systemic thrombolysis are increasing nationally over time, and that it remains an escalator relative to anticoagulation and catheter-directed thrombolysis, even after adjusting for patient and hospital characteristics. The association of systemic thrombolysis with intracranial bleeding and other potential catastrophic complications may explain the association with increased costs.19

In examining the cost of catheter-based interventions, the finding that catheter-directed embolectomy was a relative cost escalator to catheter-directed thrombolysis is novel compared with the existing literature. A previous multicenter study did not find a significant hospitalization cost difference between the two modalities,6 but national longitudinal data in this study may capture broader variation in contemporary practice. Additionally, the procedural cost of catheter-directed embolectomy has been reported to be ≤$10,000 more than catheter-directed thrombolysis, independent of length of stay, which may contribute to the relatively increased cost of hospitalization.7 Furthermore, we found surgical embolectomy to be associated with the highest costs compared with the other treatment modalities. This finding is consistent with another study that found surgical embolectomy to be significantly more costly than systemic thrombolysis or catheter-directed thrombolysis.20 The higher costs associated with surgical embolectomy may be due to its invasiveness, which result in a higher level of inpatient care and longer admissions. These findings underscore that PE treatment modality is a significant predictor of total hospitalization cost for patients diagnosed with PE, and, for patients requiring more than anticoagulation, catheter-directed thrombolysis might be the most cost-effective option.

In addition to analyzing costs, our study analyzed length of stay, because length of stay may reflect illness severity and treatment effectiveness. Catheter-directed embolectomy was predictive of the shortest length of stay, followed by catheter-directed thrombolysis, anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, and pulmonary embolectomy. However, aside from surgical embolectomy, the length of stay for the other treatments differed by no more than 2 days. The relatively increased length of stay for patients treated with systemic thrombolysis and surgical embolectomy compared with catheter-directed thrombolysis is consistent with a prior study of relative lengths of stay between catheter-directed therapy, systemic thrombolysis, and surgical embolectomy.20 In addition, we found anticoagulation to be associated with a slightly increased length of stay, whereas catheter-directed embolectomy was associated with hospitalizations that were one-half of a day shorter. This finding is consistent with a study by Khazi et al21 that found that catheter-directed embolectomy was associated with a shorter length of stay compared with conservative therapy.

Importantly, our study identified important sociodemographic disparities in PE resource utilization. Minority race and greater facility Medicaid burden predicted both increased costs and increased length of stay. These results reinforce prior reports of disproportionate resource utilization and longer admissions in racial minorities and patients without private insurance, who were more likely to have longer admissions.22 These results suggest persistent inequities in PE treatment resource utilization, necessitating further research into population-level disparities.

Study limitations

Limitations of this study include data collection in an administrative database, which is at risk for coding misclassifications and missing data. Additionally, the available data did not allow for a determination of the severity of the PE. Although the analyses controlled for baseline comorbidities, we could not adjust for illness severity at the time of patient presentation and the subsequent effect on appropriate treatment modalities and hospital costs and lengths of stay. Additionally, changes in costs and lengths of stay over the last two decades are likely multifactorial, possibly being influenced by the changing frequencies with which different modalities are implemented, but also by factors independent of the treatment modality implemented. The aim of this study was to analyze cost and length of stay over the whole period; thus, a multivariate analysis by time was not conducted. Despite these limitations, the NIS is best suited for a national analysis, which allows for a broader understanding of relative economic comparisons between treatment modalities at a population-representative scale.

Conclusions

Few studies offer a predictive model of the costs associated with PE classified by treatment modality. Our results demonstrate that systemic thrombolysis and surgical embolectomy were cost escalators relative to catheter-directed thrombolysis, and catheter-directed embolectomy was the only treatment modality that reduced hospitalization length of stay. Although disparities in PE economics are influenced by sociodemographic factors such as race, income, insurance type, and facility resources, we demonstrated that the modality used also plays a significant role in determining hospital cost and length of stay. Further research should aim to optimize resource allocation and mitigate disparities in the management of PE.
