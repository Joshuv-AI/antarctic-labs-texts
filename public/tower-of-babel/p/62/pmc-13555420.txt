Use of Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation for Management of Hemostasis After Complex Cardiac Surgery
Authors: Dagmawi Demessie, Yara Menassa, Aabha Divya, Jamil Borgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntractable bleeding during cardiac surgery is a rare but life-threatening complication. We describe a novel management approach in which the patient was supported with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation until hemostasis could be achieved. This approach serves as an unloading technique for the heart while allowing compression of bleeding sites and safe transfusion without hemodynamic instability or myocardial dysfunction.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has become a crucial life-supporting intervention since the 1970s, with a continued rise in its use.1 The growing body of literature reflects ECMO’s evolving role as a vital rescue therapy for critically ill patients in cardiorespiratory failure, transplant bridging, and severe trauma and as a salvage strategy for postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock across various cardiac surgeries.2 Intraoperatively, ECMO has been shown to play a role in lung transplantation or in patients who cannot be weaned off cardiopulmonary bypass at the end of the procedure.3,4 To date, there are limited reports, to our knowledge, of ECMO use primarily intended for hemostatic rather than for circulatory support, particularly in the cardiac surgery setting. In this report, we describe the deliberate initiation of venoarterial (VA) ECMO primarily to facilitate hemostasis in the setting of diffuse, unlocalized intraoperative bleeding in a patient who had not yet had frank hemodynamic collapse.

The patient is a 56-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and kidney transplantation 18 years earlier. She presented with heart failure symptoms including fatigability and dyspnea on exertion worsening during a period of 2 weeks. She was hemodynamically stable with a significant finding of a holosystolic murmur and mild troponin (1246 ng/L) and significant B-type natriuretic peptide elevation (2340 pg/mL). Echocardiography showed an ejection fraction of 25%, regional wall motion abnormalities, severe mitral regurgitation, and moderate aortic stenosis. Angiography showed multivessel coronary artery disease with a 70% right coronary artery stenosis and 90% mid–left anterior descending artery stenosis. Notably, her creatinine concentration, at baseline between 2.0 and 2.2 mg/dL, peaked at 4.0 mg/dL.

The cardiothoracic surgery team evaluated her and recommended high-risk cardiac surgery that entails 2-vessel bypass and aortic and mitral valve replacement. As no exact risk calculator existed for this operation, we used coronary artery bypass grafting and aortic valve replacement as an approximation and indicated a predicted mortality of 26.3%, mostly driven by her renal disease and previous transplant and steroid use.

During surgery, there was a moderate amount of pericardial adhesion from prior uremic pericarditis. It was immediately noted that her tissues were remarkably friable. Extra care was taken at every step to execute this operation that included mitral valve replacement with a 25-mm bioprosthetic valve, aortic valve replacement with a 19-mm bioprosthetic valve, and 2 vessel bypasses with a left internal mammary artery graft to left anterior descending artery and a saphenous vein graft to the posterior descending artery. Given the friability of the aortic tissues and concern for excessive bleeding, aortic root enlargement was not pursued to minimize operative complexity and bleeding risk. The cardiopulmonary bypass time and cross-clamp time were particularly long, attributed to the difficulty in handling the friable tissue.

After separation from cardiopulmonary bypass, heparinization was reversed with protamine, and multiple units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and platelets were administered with no improvement in hemostasis. Absence of surgical bleeding was confirmed multiple times by checking all anastomotic sites and the atriotomy and aortotomy sites. Bleeding was noted to be due to diffuse tissue oozing, particularly the pericardial fat. We attempted packing the mediastinum multiple times, with no significant improvement. A total of 13 units of packed red blood cells, 10 units of FFP, 4 units of cryoprecipitate, 3 units of platelets, and 1 dose of prothrombin complex concentrate (Kcentra; CSL Behring) were given.

More than 3 hours were spent attempting to achieve reasonable hemostasis, to no avail. At that point, the heart started showing early signs of swelling and some dysfunction requiring increasing doses of inotropic support.

A decision was made to place the patient on VA ECMO, which was performed by using the previously placed aortic cannula for return and the left femoral vein for drainage. At the time of VA ECMO initiation, the patient’s hemoglobin level was 8.4 g/dL and platelet count was 98 × 109/L. Other coagulation parameters, including international normalized ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, and thromboelastography, were not obtained. Once on ECMO, the heart was packed tightly, and reversal of coagulopathy was achieved by continuous administration of blood products (Figure).

Reasonable hemostasis was achieved with that approach after a period of 1 hour. The patient’s chest was left open, packed with Kerlix gauze, and covered by a black foam vacuum-assisted closure sponge. A tape was applied over the sponge, and the patient was then transferred to the surgical intensive care unit in a critical condition on VA ECMO.

In the intensive care unit, the patient was hemodynamically stable. During the first 24 hours postoperatively, chest tube output was 2096 mL, and the patient received 3 units of packed red blood cells, 3 units of FFP, and 1 unit of platelets. VA ECMO flows were maintained at approximately 2.5 L/min without difficulty.

The patient was returned to the operating room 3 days later and was successfully decannulated off ECMO under transesophageal echocardiography monitoring, showing good biventricular function. The patient was extubated 1 day later and gradually regained function during the following 10 days before being discharged.

Comment

Whereas significant bleeding is a known risk of cardiothoracic surgery, intractable intraoperative bleeding is uncommon, with little evidence addressing it. Severe hemorrhage is generally regarded as a relative contraindication to ECMO because profound hypovolemia may prevent adequate flow and higher perfusion pressures can aggravate bleeding. ECMO is more commonly used intraoperatively to support cardiac function during cardiac compression or manipulation while a discrete bleeding source is repaired, serving primarily as circulatory support rather than a hemostatic intervention.2 To our knowledge, reports of VA ECMO initiated primarily to facilitate hemostasis in diffuse, unlocalized bleeding during cardiac surgery, without frank hemodynamic collapse, are rare. Studies describe ECMO use in obstetric hemorrhage,5 and several reports describe its use during pulmonary endarterectomy or catheter-induced pulmonary hemorrhage, using venovenous or VA ECMO without systemic anticoagulation.6, 7, 8

In our case, VA ECMO was initiated not for hemodynamic collapse or cardiac manipulation but as a strategic intervention to manage intractable diffuse bleeding that persisted despite exhaustive hemostatic efforts. The advantages of this technique are multifold. First, it allows unloading of the heart by draining blood return and reducing venous pressure at bleeding surfaces. It also controls aortic pressure, limiting high systolic peaks and providing a steadier mean pressure. Second, it enables sustained mechanical compression of bleeding areas through packing without hemodynamic compromise. Third, it allows liberal administration of blood products without significant heart swelling or dysfunction. The decision to proceed and the timing of ECMO initiation in similar scenarios should be carefully considered and weighed against potential complications.
