Global genomic survey of Salmonella Kentucky: discovery of a chromosomeborne blaNDM-5 and the emergence of ST314, an MDR clone mediated by the IncR plasmid
Authors: Kaifeng Chen, Junhao Peng, Zhong Peng, Renhang Xiao, Nanwei Wang, Yuhui Deng, Canji Wu, Zexin Zheng, Jin Guan, Yixin Tang, Qijie Lin, Chenggang Xu, Xuebin Xu, Ming Liao, Jianmin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Salmonella enterica serotype Kentucky (S. Kentucky) is a global challenge, with increasing resistance to cephalosporins, ciprofloxacin, and carbapenems significantly limiting treatment strategies, yet its worldwide dissemination patterns and AMR profiles remain unclear. Therefore, this study monitored the current trends in global spatiotemporal distribution and AMR of 9,557 S. Kentucky from 47 countries through large-scale genome-based molecular epidemiology. We revealed that S. Kentucky demonstrated a broad geographic distribution, with ST314 emerging as a novel epidemic sequence type, particularly in China. Additionally, we found chicken supply chain represents the main source for the transmission of S. Kentucky ST314 to humans. Particularly, ST314 represents an antimicrobial-resistant clone and exhibited resistance mechanisms distinct from the multidrug-resistant clone ST198. Notably, we made a groundbreaking discovery of the chromosome-borne blaNDM-5 in S. Kentucky, and the IncR plasmid was identified as a crucial mediator of AMR in Chinese ST314. Machine learning indicated that the IncR plasmid was involved in the localized evolution of ST314 within China. Based on the analyzed sequence data, we showed that ST314 was originated from the United Kingdom, and formed a unique antimicrobial-resistant clone specific to China with subsequent spread to other countries. Our analysis highlights the expanding geographic distribution and increasingly complex AMR of S. Kentucky, emphasizing the emergence of ST314 as a new global risk for the spread of antibiotic-resistant S. Kentucky.KEYWORDS: S. Kentucky, ST314, antimicrobial resistance, spatial and temporal distribution, machine learning

Background

Salmonella ranks as the third leading cause of death among diarrheal diseases in humans worldwide [1], responsible for approximately 1.35 million infections, 26,500 hospitalizations, and 420 deaths annually [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified fluoroquinolone-resistant Salmonella, as well as third-generation cephalosporin-resistant and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, including Salmonella, as priority pathogens posing the greatest threat to human health [3]. Among the over 2,600 serotypes assigned to Salmonella, Kentucky has emerged as one of the most highly antimicrobial-resistant serotypes, significantly limiting clinical treatment options [4]. Notably, S. Kentucky has consistently ranked among the top 20 serotypes causing human clinical cases of salmonellosis from 2018 to 2020 [5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for surveillance of the epidemiology and antimicrobial resistance patterns of S. Kentucky.

The global prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. Kentucky strains has raised serious concerns worldwide due to their increasing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [4,6,7]. For instance, ciprofloxacin has been recommended as the first-line treatment for severe infections caused by Salmonella [8]. However, S. Kentucky has more than doubled the proportion of ciprofloxacin resistance over the past decade in France, garnering significant attention [4]. Previous studies have identified sequence type (ST) 198 as a concerning population for antimicrobial-resistant S. Kentucky [7,9]. Numerous studies have concentrated on the prevalence, development of antibiotic resistance, and potential risks associated with S. Kentucky ST198 in recent years [4,6,7,10,11]. However, our previous study has revealed that S. Kentucky ST314 also represents a prevalent antimicrobial-resistant clone, particularly exhibiting phenotypes of ciprofloxacin resistance and MDR [12]. This suggests that novel epidemiological patterns may emerge, warranting further comprehensive evidence. In this study, we conducted a retrospective investigation on Salmonella strains obtained from various host species across China between 1994 and 2021. This investigation involved wholegenome sequencing (WGS) and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). By integrating the genome sequences of S. Kentucky from diverse regions worldwide available in public databases, we illustrated the global spatial and temporal prevalence patterns of antimicrobial-resistant S. Kentucky. Our analysis identified S. Kentucky ST314 as an emerging epidemic clone with a distinct resistance mechanism compared to ST198.

Methods

Salmonella isolates collected in this study

This study conducted a parallel retrospective investigation of Salmonella in China – a major global producer and consumer of meat products – where conditions may be conducive to the spread of S. Kentucky. In total, we analyzed 10,136 serotyped Salmonella strains isolated from humans and/or food animals between 1994 and 2021.

Among these, Salmonella isolates from food animals were mainly collected from community samples obtained through long-term monitoring and sampling efforts led by South China Agricultural University (Guangzhou), Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan), and Northwest A&F University (Yangling). These three institutions are located in Southern, Central, and Northern China, respectively, and their sampling regions are representative of the country’s geographic diversity.

Additionally, human-origin Salmonella isolates were derived from routine public health samples collected through regular surveillance by the Shanghai Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Shanghai CDC). These isolates were obtained from various regions across China – including East China, South China, Northwest China, Southwest China, North China, Central China, and Northeast China – via routine hospital testing of human feces, blood, urine, and other clinical specimens. The database established by Shanghai CDC has been widely used for epidemiological surveillance of Salmonella in China [13].

The sampling method was implemented in accordance with ISO-6579 (International Organization for Standardization) [14]. Samples from food animals were collected through long-term random sampling from farms, slaughterhouses, markets, and the environment, whereas the human samples primarily consisted of feces, blood, and urine were obtained from routine hospital examinations. Briefly, collected samples were transferred to sterile sampling bags containing buffered peptone water, vigorously shaken for 3 min, and then incubated at 36 ± 1℃ for 8–18 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of the suspension was added to 10 mL of Tetrathionate (TT) broth (Huankai Biology, Guangdong, China) and 1 mL was added to 10 mL of Rappaport-Vassiliadis (RV) soya broth (Huankai Biology, Guangdong, China), with both incubated at 42 °C for 18–24 h, respectively. Following selective enrichment, the suspension was inoculated onto xylose lysine tergitol 4 agar plates (Huankai Biology, Guangdong, China) and Salmonella Chromogenic Agar (CHROMagar™, France), then incubated at 36 ± 1 °C for 18–24 h. Isolates exhibiting typical Salmonella morphology were further confirmed using the API 20E test strip (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France). All Salmonella serotyping was performed via slide agglutination tests following the White-KauffmannLe Minor scheme, as previously described [15]. Among these Salmonella isolates, 223 isolates were identified as S. Kentucky, originating from humans (n = 102), chickens (n = 107), ducks (n = 6), the environment (n = 4), pork (n = 2), vegetables (n = 1), and pigeons (n = 1). All of these strains were included in this study (Supplementary Data 2). Additionally, whole-genome sequences of 9,334 S. Kentucky samples from various regions worldwide (including 56 samples from China) were obtained from the NCBI Genome database for bioinformatic analysis (Supplementary Data 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antibiotics against 223 S. Kentucky strains were determined using the agar dilution method, following the protocol outlined in the document published by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute of the United States (CLSI document M100-ED35 and CLSI VET01S-2024) [16,17]. The antibiotics tested were procured from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) and included a range of classes: penicillins (ampicillin), cephalosporins (cefotaxime, cefepime), carbapenems (imipenem), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, nalidixic acid, ofloxacin), aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin), phenicols (florfenicol, chloramphenicol), polymyxins (polymyxin B), sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole), tetracyclines (tigecycline, tetracycline), and macrolides (azithromycin). The interpretation of results was done based on CLSI breakpoints, with E. coli ATCC 25922 serving as the quality control strain.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The draft genome sequences of 223 S. Kentucky strains isolated in this study were obtained through Illumina sequencing. Briefly, bacterial genomic DNAs were extracted using a commercial bacterial DNA extraction kit (Omega, Norcross, USA). The quality and quantity of the DNAs were assessed through electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel and Qubit 3.0 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using an NEBNext UltraTM II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) using the paired-end 350 bp sequencing protocol. The quality of the generated paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp) was verified and trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.36 [18]. Low-quality raw reads (Q < 20) were filtered out. These high-quality reads were de novo assembled using SPAdes (version 3.9.0) [19] to generate contigs. The quality of the assembled genomes was evaluated using Quast Tools 5.0.2 [20]. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted using MLST v2.11 (https://github.com/tsee mann/mlst). The presence of ARGs, insertion sequences (ISs), and plasmids was identified using Resfinder [21], ISfinder ((http://www-is.biotoul.fr/)http://www- (http://www-is.biotoul.fr/)is.biotoul.fr (http://www-is.biotoul.fr/). (http://www-is.biotoul.fr/)), and PlasmidFinder [22], respectively.

To elucidate the structure of the IncR plasmid, the complete genome sequence of an IncR plasmid (pSH176-IncR) carried by S. Kentucky strain H176 was generated using Oxford Nanopore sequencing (Oxford Nanopore Technologies [ONT], Oxford, UK) in combination with Illumina sequencing. Following the assessment of the quality and quantity of the extracted plasmid DNA using the aforementioned-method, the SQKLSK109 ligation sequencing kit (ONT, Oxford, UK) was utilized to prepare sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on the PromethION platform (ONT, Oxford, UK). Simultaneously, the extracted plasmid DNA underwent Illumina sequencing as described above. Raw data obtained from Illumina sequencing were processed using Trimmomatic v0.36 to generate clean data, whereas those from Nanopore sequencing were evaluated using the FastQC package (version 0.11.2), followed by assembly using Canu v1.3 [23].

Subsequently, the assembled data was corrected using clean data from Illumina sequencing, with gaps filled using GapFiller v1.11 [24]. Finally, the data underwent a thorough validation using PrInSeS-G v1.0.0. The obtained plasmid sequences were aligned and visualized through genome sequence alignments using the BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG v0.95). Plasmid sequences identified in K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and Salmonella downloaded from GenBank were also included in the analysis.

Phylogenetics and phylodynamics

To comprehend the population structure of S. Kentucky prevalent in China, the genome sequences of 279 S. Kentucky samples from China were aligned with the complete genome sequence of S. Kentucky strain 201001922 (GenBank accession number: CP028357), and Snippy v4.6.037 was employed to identify SNPs from core gene alignments, according to previously reported methods [11,25]. Genomic regions associated with indels, adjacent mismatches, transposases (annotated by Prokka), ARGs (identified by AMRFinder), prophages (predicted by PHASTER), and recombination events (detected by Gubbins v1.4.5) were masked prior to downstream analyses [26]. The resulting Clean.core.fasta alignment was input into RAxML v8.2.4 to reconstruct the maximum likelihood phylogeny under the GTRGAMMA model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL. Clustering analysis of the phylogenetic tree using the rhierBAPs v1.1.3.

Timed phylogenies were also established through Bayesian phylogenetic inference to explore the evolutionary processes and geographical distribution of 726 S. Kentucky strains from China (275 strains) and other countries (451 strains; see Supplementary Data 7). Briefly, we used TempEst v1.5.1 to calculate the linear correlation between the root-to-tip distances of the resulting phylogeny and the sampling time of the isolates (in months/years), thereby validating the strength of the temporal signal. The results showed that the linear correlation for the global branch of ST198 was R² = 0.4178, and the respective correlation for the global branch of ST314 was R² = 0.2905, both of which support the use of Bayesian methods for time calibration. Subsequently, Bayesian phylogenetic inference was performed using BEAST v1.10.4 to estimate the substitution rate and the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of the clonal expansion of S. Kentucky. Specifically, model combination validation in BEAST supported the use of a GTR+Γ4 substitution model, an uncorrelated log-normal relaxed clock, and an exponential growth coalescent prior. For each analysis, three independent MCMC chains of 100 million generations were run, with sampling conducted every 10,000 iterations. Convergence was confirmed in Tracer v1.7 (ESS > 200 for all parameters). Log files and tree sets were combined using LogCombiner v1.10.4 after discarding 20% of the burn-in, and maximum clade credibility (MCC) trees were summarized in TreeAnnotator v1.10.4. The resulting trees and posterior estimates were visualized in spreaD3.

Extraction, electroporation, and fitness cost detection of IncR plasmids

The S. Kentucky strain H176, harboring the IncR plasmid, was incubated at 37°C overnight. Following this, the bacterial plasmid was extracted according to the instructions outlined in the plasmid extraction kit (Omega, Norcross, USA). Subsequently, the extracted plasmid was introduced into S. Kentucky strains lacking the IncR plasmid via electroporation (200 ohms, 25 uF, 2 kV). LB agar plates supplemented with 2 μg/mL ciprofloxacin were employed to select for strains that have successfully acquired the IncR plasmid.

Conjugation assay

Conjugation assays involving imipenem-resistant S. Kentucky (donor), IncR plasmid-positive S. Kentucky (donor), and E. coli C600 (recipient) were carried out following established protocols [27]. The donor and recipient bacteria were grown to mid-log phase (OD600nm = 0.5 - 0.6), mixed at a 1:4 ratio, and then allowed to culture overnight at 37 °C. The bacterial mixture was then spread onto Cormagar chromogenic mediu supplemented with streptomycin (3,000 μg/ml) along with imipenem (4 μg/mL) or ciprofloxacin (2 μg/mL) and incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C. Blue individual colonies on the plates were selected, and positive transconjugants were confirmed through PCR amplification of resistance genes or plasmid replicons [28,29], as well as through drug susceptibility testing.

Construction of machine learning models

Pan-genomic analysis was conducted using Roary [30]. Six distinct machine learning models were utilized for training: logistic regression, linear support vector machine, support vector machine with RBF kernel, random forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost. Outer cross-validation (n_splits=5) and inner cross-validation (n_splits=3) were employed to optimize hyperparameters within a specified hyperparameter space for each model. The developed model was then evaluated on the test set, and a confusion matrix was constructed. The model performance was evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity and specificity values, and Cohen's κ statistic.

The complete machine learning process for multi-class classification using the Random Forest classifier, including processing the imbalanced dataset, evaluating model performance, performing feature importance analysis, and plotting the ROC curve. When dealing with the unbalanced problem of rational categories, data were oversampled using the synthetic minority oversampling technique. These data were randomly divided into training and test sets using the train_test_split function, with the test set comprising 20% of the total data. Immediately after that, the model was trained using SHAP to visualize the top ten most important features of each class. Finally, the nucleic acids from the top ten pan-genome-specific genes were extracted, and the corresponding protein sequences were aligned with protein names and functional information obtained from the UniProt knowledge base [31].

Data visualization and statistical analysis

The data were visualized using GraphPad Prism v8.0, Origin, R v4.4.2, RStudio 1.1.383, and TBtools. All statistical analysis were performed in GraphPad Prism v8.0. The Kruskal–Wallis test (with Dunn’s test) was used to assess the significance of pairwise differences between multiple groups. The Mann–Whitney U test and Chisquare test were employed to evaluate the statistical significance of the difference between two groups. Statistical difference was set as P ≤ 0.05 (*), P ≤ 0.01 (**), P ≤ 0.001 (***), while “ns” indicates no significant difference.

Results

S. Kentucky exhibits a wide geographic distribution, with ST314 emerging as a new epidemic sequence type

As of March 8, 2023, we investigated all 9,334 genome sequences of S. Kentucky samples publicly available in NCBI (Supplementary Data 1) and observed that these samples were collected between 1972 and 2023, from 47 countries and regions across 6 continents globally (Figure 1a). The majority of these strains originated from the United States (82.80%, 7,729/9,334), followed by the United Kingdom (10.41%, 972/9,334), and Switzerland (1.35%, 126/9,334) (Figure 1b). Although China was among the top six countries globally in terms of S. Kentucky origins, only 56 S. Kentucky genome sequences had been published (Figure 1b).

To assess the prevalence of S. Kentucky in China, we conducted a retrospective study on 10,136 Salmonella strains isolated from 23 Chinese provinces between 1994 and 2021. Bacterial serotyping revealed that 223 strains were S. Kentucky (2.20%), ranking it as the ninth most prevalent serotype (Figure S1, Supplementary Data 2). Interestingly, the prevalence of S. Kentucky showed an increasing trend over time (Figure S1). By combining the 56 S. Kentucky samples originating from China in NCBI, we observed that a total of 279 S. Kentucky strains were prevalent in 17 Chinese provinces (Figure 1c). Furthermore, the number of S. Kentucky strains surged notably after 2014, indicating a clear increasing prevalence trend over time (Figure 1d).

The multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis of 9,557 S. Kentucky strains (9334 from NCBI and 223 generated in this study) revealed 65 STs. Among these, ST152 as the dominant ST type, accounting for 76.16% (7,279/9,557) of the strains, followed by ST198 (17.16%, 1,640/9,557) and ST314 (2.61%, 249/9,557) (Figure 1a). In China, ST198 (63.80%, 178/279) and ST314 (34.77%, 97/279) were the predominant S. Kentucky STs, in contrast to ST152 (0.36%, 1/279) (Figure 1a). Particularly, the ST314 clone was collected from 17 countries, with notable occurrences in the United Kingdom (38.55%, 96/249) and China (38.96%, 97/249) (Figure 1a). In China, ST314 displayed a prevalence trend similar to ST198 between 2011 and 2021, being widespread in seven provinces (Figure 1e, f). Notably, in certain years and/or provinces, the isolation rates of ST314 surpassed those of ST198 (Figure 1e, f).

Chicken supply chain represents the main source for the transmission of S. Kentucky ST314 to humans

The 9,557 S. Kentucky strains were sourced from 46 origins, with a significant portion originating from chickens (75.44%, 7,210/9,557) and humans (14.17%, 1,354/9,557) (Supplementary Data 1, 2, Figure 2a). Subsequently, we examined the population structure of these 9,557 strains using core-genome MLST (cgMLST) data following a previously established protocol [32]. The analysis revealed that these strains formed tight clusters within three major lineages (Figure S2a). Specifically, chicken and human isolates were predominantly clustered within the ST198 and ST314 lineages, while the ST152 lineage primarily comprised chicken isolates (Figure 2c, S2b). Calculations of pairwise allelic differences (PADs) within the three prominent STs (ST152, ST198, and ST314) indicated the smallest intra-source genomic diversity, with a median PADs of 3.2 between chicken and human isolates within ST314, compared to median PADs ranging from 5.09 to 8.39 for isolates within ST198 and ST152 (Figure 2d).

Given that the ST314 strains are predominantly from China (38.96%, 97/249; Figure 1a), and S. Kentucky in China was also mainly isolated from chicken (53.76%, 150/279) and human (38.71%, 108/279) (Figure 2b), we conducted single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis among the Chinese ST314 strains (n = 97) from different host species. The results demonstrated that chicken isolates exhibited fewer SNPs (average SNPs = 14.28) when compared to human isolates in contrast to isolates from other hosts (average SNPs = 15.06–48.91), suggesting a closer genetic relationship between ST314 chicken isolates and humans isolates than isolates from other sources (Figure 2e, S2c). The 97 ST314 strains from China included 55 chicken isolates from the chicken supply chain (one isolate from a chicken farm [strain NW1149], 12 isolates from slaughterhouses, and 42 isolates from terminal markets) and 32 human isolates (Figure 2f). Chicken isolates were isolated from Guangdong, Guangxi, Shanghai, and Zhejiang between 2014 and 2018, and 2020, whereas human isolates were obtained from Guangxi, Shanghai, and Xinjiang from 2013 to 2019 and in 2021. BAPS analysis demonstrated intermixing between human and chicken isolates within groups BAPS6, BAPS8, and BAPS9, including intraregional transmission events between chickens and humans in Guangxi and Shanghai. These strains – isolated between 2013 and 2019 and in 2021 – differed by 3 to 23 SNPs. Additionally, interregional transmission events between chickens and humans were detected. Human and chicken isolates in BAPS6, BAPS8, and BAPS9 were sourced from 4 and 3 cities (BAPS6), 2 and 2 distinct cities (BAPS8), and 3 and 2 cities (BAPS9), respectively, with all three BAPS groups spanning multiple years (Figure S2c). These findings demonstrate a close relationship between chicken-derived and human-derived S. Kentucky ST314, irrespective of whether they originate from the same or different geographical locations or time periods. Notably, 91.67% (11/12) of isolates from chicken slaughterhouses, 95.24% (40/42) from terminal markets, and 78.13% (25/32) from humans shared SNPs ≤18 with the strain isolated from chicken farm. Furthermore, 72.73% of isolates from slaughterhouses shared SNPs ≤18 with all 40 isolates from terminal markets, and all terminal market-derived isolates also shared SNPs ≤18 with at least 22 of the human isolates (Figure 2f). These results suggest a transmission pathway of S. Kentucky ST314 along the chicken supply chain.

S. Kentucky ST314 represents an antimicrobial resistant clone and it exhibits a different resistance mechanism from ST198

To comprehend the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profile of S. Kentucky, we assessed the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 16 antimicrobial agents belonging to 10 classes on the 223 strains isolated from China between 2009 and 2021. The findings revealed varying proportions of resistance among the strains to all tested different classes of antimicrobials, including penicillins (ampicillin), cephalosporins (cefotaxime, cefepime), carbapenems (imipenem), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, nalidixic acid, ofloxacin), aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin), phenicols (florfenicol, chloramphenicol), polymyxins (polymyxin B), sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole), tetracyclines (tigecycline, tetracycline), and macrolides (azithromycin). The resistance rates were 59.19%, 47.53%, 0.45%, 89.69%, 51.57%, 60.54%, 0.45%, 97.31%, 90.58%, and 21.97%, respectively (Figure 3a). The S. Kentucky ST314 exhibited resistance to seven out of the ten tested antimicrobial classes, with 80.60% of the ST314 strains demonstrating MDR phenotypes (Figure 3b). Over 50% of the ST314 strains displayed resistance to fluoroquinolones (70.15%), phenicols (79.10%), sulfonamides (95.52%), and tetracyclines (83.58%) (Figure 3a). Notably, the resistance rates of ST314 strains against florfenicol and chloramphenicol surpassed those of the ST198 strains (Figure 3c). Additionally, a subset of the ST314 strains exhibited resistance to the key therapeutic options such as ciprofloxacin (68.66%), cefotaxime (4.48%), and the last-resort agent tigecycline (34.44%) (Figure 3c). Remarkably, the resistance rate of ST314 against ciprofloxacin escalated from 50.00% in 2014 to 100% in 2016, fluctuated thereafter, and reached 100% again by 2021 (Figure 3d). Two S. Kentucky ST314 strains were identified as resistant to both ciprofloxacin and cefotaxime concurrently.

We conducted a comprehensive screening of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) utilizing WGS data from the 9,557 S. Kentucky samples. This analysis unveiled a total of 35,594 genes associated with resistance, distributed across 12 various antimicrobial classes: aminoglycosides (n = 24,828), β-lactams (n = 1,848; inclusive of carbapenemase coding genes [n = 16] and extended spectrum β-lactamase [ESBL] coding genes [n = 1,400]), quinolones (n = 282), macrolides (n = 216), trimethoprims (n = 307), tetracyclines (n = 5,784), sulfonamides (n = 1,591), phenicols (n = 372), fosfomycin (n = 107), rifampicin (n = 157), lincosamides (n = 93), and colistin (n = 9) (Supplementary Data 3, Figure S3). Significantly, a robust correlation was observed between the AMR phenotypes and the presence of ARGs. For instance, our antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) identified a single strain resistant to imipenem (MIC = 4 μg/mL), and this particular strain was found to harbor blaNDM-5. This is the first discovery of blaNDM-5 in S. Kentucky. Consequently, we sequenced the complete genome of this strain using Nanopore sequencing. Subsequent analysis delineated that blaNDM-5 resided on the chromosome within a distinct genetic structure flanked by insertion elements (Figure 3e). This genetic element shared a genetic framework strikingly similar to that found in a Salmonella strain isolated from chickens (GenBank accession no. CP083731; 99.94% nucleotide identity) and a plasmid carried by an Escherichia coli strain (GenBank accession no. CP049111; 99.98% nucleotide identity) (Figure 3e).

Globally, it was observed that ST198 strains (average ARGs, 6.06) exhibited the highest average number of ARGs, followed by ST314 strains (average ARGs, 4.43) and ST152 strains (average ARGs, 3.17) (Figure 3f). Moreover, ST314 strains originating from China (average ARGs, 8.94) were noted to harbor a greater average number of ARGs compared to ST314 strains from other countries (average ARGs, 1∼6.5) (Figure 3g, S3). Subsequent analysis suggested that ST314 might employ a distinct antimicrobial resistance mechanism as opposed to ST198. Over recent years, ST198 strains displayed a rising trend in both the diversity and quantity of ARGs, while ST314 strains exhibited genetic stability concerning ARGs (Figure 3h). Specifically, ST314 strains predominantly utilized four types of plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) mechanisms (qnrB6, 73.20%; qnrS1, 12.37%; qnrVC5, 1.03%; aac(6’)-Ib-cr, 76.29%) to confer fluoroquinolone resistance, and did not exhibit any mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining region (QRDR). Whereas ST198 strains primarily relied on the QRDR mutations, approximately 98.31% of ST198 strains exhibited at least one mutation, including five specific mutations in gyrA (S83, D87) and parC (S80). (Figure S4). A similar trend was observed in the analysis of global S. Kentucky, where ST314 strains containing PMQR genes were predominantly identified in China (Figure S4, S3).

IncR plasmid was identified as a key mediator of antimicrobial resistance in S. Kentucky ST314 from China

The investigation involved screening the plasmid replicons carried by S. Kentucky, revealing the identification of 75 distinct known plasmids in the 9,557 strains (Supplementary Data 4, Figure. S5). Among the strains from China, a total of 31 distinct known plasmids were identified, and the Col(pHAD28) type plasmid (36.56%, 102/279) emerged as the most prevalent, followed by IncR (26.16%, 73/279), Col156 (24.37%, 68/279), and ColRNAI (19.71%, 55/279) (Figure 4a, Supplementary Data 5). Notably, further analysis unveiled that the IncR plasmid was predominantly found in ST314 strains (75.26%, 73/97) isolated from China, with minimal occurrences in ST198 (0.55%, 9/1640) or non-Chinese ST314 strains (0.66%, 1/152) (Figure 4a, b). Subsequently, the complete sequence of an IncR plasmid in an S. Kentucky ST314 strain H176 was generated, designated as pIncR-H176 (GenBank accession no. CP173280). Sequence alignments demonstrated that pIncR-H176 exhibited high homology to an IncR plasmid (GenBank accession no. CP072498) carried by a Klebsiella pneumoniae strain isolated from a patient in Guangdong Province (99.99% nucleotide identity). Structurally, pIncR-H176 harbored 11 ARGs in addition to the plasmid backbone, conferring resistance to phenicols (floR), tetracyclines (tet(A)), macrolides (mphA), aminoglycosides (aadA16, aph(6)-Id), sulfonamides (sul1), fluoroquinolones (qnrB6, aac(6’)-Ib-cr), trimethoprims (dfrA27), rifampicin (arr-3), and a small multidrug resistance transporter (emrE) (Figure 4c). Remarkably, subsequent plasmid transformation assays successfully transformed pIncR-H176 into ST314 strains but failed to do so in ST198 strains, suggesting a potential ST-preference for this plasmid (Figure 4d, S6). The transfer of pIncR-H176 resulted in a 7-fold increase in resistance to ciprofloxacin, a 3-fold increase in resistance to chloramphenicol and gentamicin, a 127-fold increase in resistance to tetracycline, and a marginal increase in resistance to azithromycin for the recipient strain (Figure 4e, Supplementary Data 6). Furthermore, bacterial passage assays indicated that pIncR-H176 remained stable within the recipient strain, even after multiple passages in antibiotic-free medium, without imposing any fitness cost on the ST314 strains (Figure 4f, S6).

Machine learning highlights plasmid IncR mediated localized evolution of S. Kentucky ST314 in China

In a pan-genome analysis of 1,889 strains of S. Kentucky ST198 (n = 1,640) and ST314 (n = 249) globally, a total of 37,501 pan-genome features were identified. When comparing strains from different countries, it was observed that ST314 strains from China carried 1,690 (14.47%) endemic genes, a higher number compared to ST198 strains from China which carried 1,679 (6.5%) endemic genes (P < 0.0001, Figure 5a, b). Phylogenetic analysis of S. Kentucky isolates from China revealed that ST314 strains from China were more closely related, with an average of SNPs=14.35, a smaller value than that observed for ST198 strains (average SNPs = 53.77) from China (P < 0.0001, Figure 5c). These findings suggest that ST314 strains underwent local adaptation upon entering China. To investigate the key factors driving the localized evolution of S. Kentucky ST314 in China, machine learning models were developed based on 4,995 known functional genes and plasmid information from the pan-genome of 249 S. Kentucky ST314 samples worldwide. After evaluating and comparing the performance of six machine learning models, the Random Forest model was chosen for its superior performance, with an accuracy of 0.9672, precision of 0.9375, recall of 1.0000, and an F1 score of 0.9677 (Figure 5d). Using this model, the top 10 key features were identified to distinguish the Chinese ST314 strains from those in other countries. Among these features, only the IncR plasmid was a characteristic feature of ST314 strains from China, while the remaining nine genes (yfdx, ttrC, kduD, yqeG, rcnA, papD, rcnR, mrpA_2, pdeC) were negatively correlated with the Chinese ST314 strains (Figure 5e, S7). Significantly, the IncR plasmid was utilized as a classifier feature to reconstruct the random forest machine learning model, revealing a strong correlation between the IncR plasmid in Chinese strains and ARGs, including the PMQR gene aac(6’)-lb-cr (Figure 5f, S7).

Global spread patterns of antimicrobial resistant S. Kentucky

To gain a deeper insight into the spatiotemporal spread and evolutionary origins of S. Kentucky globally as well as to elucidate the genetic evolutionary connections between Chinese and worldwide S. Kentucky strains, we developed a Bayesian phylogenetic tree encompassing all ST314 strains and a selection of ST198 strains from various regions. Our findings reveal that the MRCA of global ST198 strains can be traced back to the year 1943 (95% Highest Posterior Density [HPD]: 1928-1957) (Figures 6a, S8). Bayesian molecular clock analysis showed that the evolutionary rate of the ST198 strains was estimated at 4.01 × 10-⁴ substitutions/site/year (95% HPD: 2.69 × 10-⁴ to 3.22 × 10-³ substitutions/site/year). Bayesian estimation of the effective population size (Ne) further elucidated the population dynamics of global S. Kentucky ST198. The results showed that the global ST198 population remained relatively stable from the 1960s to the 2000s, underwent rapid expansion from 2009 to 2019, and began to decline after 2020 (Figure 6c). Further phylogeographic analysis of global S. Kentucky ST198 suggested that Argentina might be the initial source of its global dissemination, subsequently disseminating from Argentina to the Europe, Americas and Asia, and later extending to Africa and Australasia (Bayesian Factor [BF] = 3.6- 3.63012E + 13; Figure 6a, d). Notably, a phenomenon of transmission from Asia back to Europe was identified (BF = 835.02; Figure 6d). MDR ST198 strains were predominantly dispersed among distinct subclusters of ST198.2, whose ancestral root can be traced back to the USA in 1958 (Figure 6a, S8). The QRDR mutations and β-lactamase genes predominantly align with different subgroups within ST198.2, with all QRDR variants exclusively appearing in 198.2. The initial mutations were observed in GyrA (S83F, TCC to TTC) in Pakistan, originating in the USA in 1995, succeeded by ParC (S80I, AGC to ATC) in Ethiopia, originating in Ethiopia, Africa, in 2014. These mutations were accompanied by significant clonal expansion, spreading across countries and regions in Africa, Asia, Europe, Australasia and the Americas.

Subsequently, mutations at codon 87 of GyrA (D87G, GAC to GGC; D87N, GAC to AAC; D87Y, GAC to TAC) were noted. Additionally, blaTEM-1b, blaTEM-141, blaCTX-M-55, and blaCTX-M-14b correspond to ST198.2-2/-3/-5, ST198.2-5, ST198.2-5, and ST198.2-4, respectively (Figure 6a). An intriguing observation is that ST198 strains, primarily originating from American animals, tend to group within the more confined population structure of 198.1, lacking gyrA/parC mutations and β-lactamase genes (Figure 6a).

The MRCA of the Chinese S. Kentucky ST198 can be traced back to 1985 (95% HPD: 1973-1996). The evolutionary rate was estimated at 1.3718 × 10−3 substitutions/site/year (95% HPD: 1.0154 × 10−3 to 1.5156 × 10−3 substitutions/site/year). Chinese S. Kentucky ST198 exhibited a similar population dynamic trend to global ST198 strains, though its rapid expansion occurred later – starting in 2016 – followed by a population decline in 2020 (Figure 6a, c). In China, a country with numerous ST198 transmission events, indigenous strains were widely distributed across various branches of ST198, with most strains clustered together within each branch, indicating diverse geographic origins of ST198 in China (Figure 6a). Upon analysis, it was determined that ST198 in China was predominantly introduced from the USA (BF = 14.13) and the United States (BF = 3.63 × 1013) (Figure 6d). Subsequently, it spread from Taiwan to Shanghai (BF = 8.23) and Anhui (BF = 9.64), and from Shanghai, it extended to Zhejiang (BF = 49.18), Beijing (BF = 8.50), Fujian (BF = 86.45), Guangxi (BF = 648.87), and Xinjiang (BF = 60.29), then further expanded towards northern China through Guangdong and Guangxi in southern China (BF = 3.88-1.43 × 1013), culminating in the establishment of a national transmission network where the northern and southern regions interlinked (Figure 6d). Notably, blaTEM-141 and blaCTX-M-55, the factors contributing to the clonal expansion of ST198.2-5, were initially identified in China. Interestingly, subsequent to its introduction to China, ST198 spread from China to Europe (BF = 835.02; Figure 6a, d).

The ancestral root of global ST314 strains can be traced back to the year 1987 (95% HPD: 1979-1994). The evolutionary rate of global ST314 strains was estimated at 1.2561 × 10−4 substitutions/site/year (95% HPD: 1.9357 × 10−5 to 2.9802 × 10−5). The global S. Kentucky ST314 population underwent two expanded events: one in 1996 when they emerged, and another in 2010, followed by a decline in 2016 and stabilized thereafter (Figure 6b, c, S8). The United Kingdom might be the initial source for the global dissemination of ST314, from where ST314 spread to Asia, Africa, various European countries, and North America (Figure 6b, e, S8). Remarkably, nearly all PMQR genes are associated with the Chinese S. Kentucky ST314 branch, with all Chinese ST314 strains forming a distinct cluster except for strain “23” and “GCA_011447095.1” (Figure 6b). Furthermore, the antibiotic resistance plasmid IncR was exclusively identified in Chinese strains, originating in China in 2002, and showing a strong correlation with PMQR genes (Figure 6b, S8). The common ancestor of Chinese S. Kentucky ST314 strains can be traced back to 1975 (95% HPD: 1952-1994). The evolutionary rate of Chinese ST314 strains was estimated at 3.798 × 10−2 substitutions/site/year (95% HPD: 2.31 × 10−2 to 5.47 × 10−2). In contrast to global ST314 strains, the Chinese ST314 population underwent rapid expansion starting in 2015, followed by a slight decrease in 2017; however, its size remained higher than preexpansion baselines and ultimately stabilized (Figure 6b, c). Moreover, the United Kingdom (BF = 23.71) and India (BF = 37.67) being the geographical source of ST314 within China (Figure 6e). S. Kentucky ST314 was introduced from India to Taiwan (BF = 37.67), and from there spread to Guangxi Province (BF = 10.83). Subsequently, ST314 spread from Guangxi to Guangdong (BF = 5.28 × 1012) and northward (BF = 9.82-299.09) within China, eventually circling back southward to Guangdong Province (BF = 9.27; Figure 6e). Notably, Chinese ST314 strains have also disseminated to Vietnam (BF = 2020.79) and the United Kingdom (BF = 30.96), and from Vietnam to Switzerland (BF = 13.61), Thailand (BF = 24.77) and Mexico (BF = 146.67) (Figure 6e).

Discussion

S. Kentucky, an emerging serotype of Salmonella known for its global transmission, has garnered significant attention worldwide due to its development of resistance to ciprofloxacin, cephalosporins, and MDR. This study illustrates that S. Kentucky exhibits a broadening geographical distribution. Presently, S. Kentucky has disseminated to 47 countries across 6 continents globally, which is a wider spread compared to the previously documented distribution across five continents [7]. The prevalence of S. Kentucky in China ranks among the top six globally, potentially attributed to China’s substantial role as a producer and consumer of livestock and poultry [9,33]. Consequently, monitoring the prevalence of S. Kentucky in China proves vital for the prevention and control of this serotype on a global scale. However, public databases currently only contain genomic information for 56 Chinese S. Kentucky samples. Building upon this limitation, our study undertook a retrospective analysis of Salmonella infections in China spanning from 1994 to 2021. In China, the overall isolation rate of S. Kentucky stands at 2.20%, positioning it among the top 9 Salmonella serotypes. Notably, a previous study identified S. Kentucky as the 15th most prevalent serotype in China by 2019 [13]. Moreover, the isolation rate uncovered in this study surpasses the previously reported rate of 0.21% [9]. By amalgamating data from all 56 Chinese S. Kentucky samples sourced from NCBI databases, it is evident that S. Kentucky has extended its reach to 17 provinces in China, surpassing prior reports which indicated prevalence in only 5 or 8 provinces [9,34]. These findings signify the continued expansion of S. Kentucky within China and underline its global dissemination trend across various countries.

Our analysis of MLST based on WGS data unveiled a diverse and intricate MLST typing profile. Notably, ST314 emerged as a prevalent novel type alongside ST198 on a global scale, exhibiting epidemiological trends that mirror those of ST198, a clone of public health significance [4,7]. Consistent with prior findings [6,7,34], our analysis underscored that chickens and chicken products served as the primary reservoir for S. Kentucky, including the ST314 lineage. Significantly, our investigation suggested that S. Kentucky ST314 potentially disseminates throughout the chicken supply chain, and is closely associated with human infections, as diverse isolates at various points within this chain sharing ≤18 SNPs, indicating clonal dissemination [9,35,36]. Consequently, effective management of the chicken supply chain emerges as a crucial factor in curtailing the spread of S. Kentucky ST314.

In this study, a significant issue of AMR in S. Kentucky was highlighted through the detection of numerous ARGs conferring resistance across a wide range of antimicrobial classes, including third/fourth-generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones. In addition to those antibiotics commonly used in human medicine, we also tested several agents (e.g., a phenicol-class antibiotic named florfenicol, the aminoglycoside-class antibiotics amikacin and gentamicin) which were used in livestock due to the broad prevalence of S. Kentucky in food-producing animals and their products [34]. Notably, the presence of third-generation cephalosporinresistant and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, including Salmonella, as well as fluoroquinolone-resistant Salmonella, has been recognized as a concern of global public health importance by WHO [3]. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of S. Kentucky exhibited resistance to ciprofloxacin (75.34%) and cefotaxime (47.53%), both crucial first-line agents for treating severe Salmonella infections [8]. It is particularly significant that a chromosome-borne carbapenem-resistant gene, blaNDM-5, was detected in S. Kentucky for the first time in this study, where the specific strain exhibited resistance to imipenem. blaNDM-5 is frequently found on plasmids of other Salmonella serotypes in China, but this marks the first report of its detection in S. Kentucky [37-39]. In contrast to other countries, where only blaNDM-1 has been reported in S. Kentucky [4], our findings reveal a distinct resistome feature. Although prior studies have reported the integration of ARGs from plasmids into specific chromosomal locations in S. Kentucky, blaNDM-5 has consistently been described as plasmid-borne to date [11,40]. Further analysis in this study suggests that the acquisition of blaNDM-5 may have occurred via the insertion of a mobile element – facilitated by the insertion sequences ISAba125 and ISVsa3 – potentially originating from the E. coli plasmid pMCR_NDM [40]. This highlights a plausible mechanism of ARGs integration through horizontal gene transfer from plasmids to chromosomes. The presence of blaNDM-5 could complicate the antibiotic resistance profile of S. Kentucky and underscores the urgent need for enhanced global surveillance of antimicrobial resistance in this serotype.

While the AMR profile of the ST314 strains may not be as severe as that of ST198, a well-known antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella clone [7,11], the ST314 strains still exhibited concerning AMR patterns. This is evident from the fact that 80.60% of the ST314 strains displayed MDR profiles, with more than 50% of them being resistant to fluoroquinolones (70.15%). Moreover, there was an observed upward trend in ciprofloxacin resistance among ST314 strains from 2014 to 2021. These findings collectively suggest that ST314 is evolving into a significant antimicrobial-resistant clone. Furthermore, our analysis indicated that the ST314 strains may be developing distinct resistance mechanisms compared to the ST198 strains. This difference is particularly highlighted by the unique mechanisms employed by the two clones to resist fluoroquinolones, with ST314 primarily characterized by PMQR genes, while ST198 predominantly exhibited mutations in QRDRs. Of the four PMQR genes found in ST314 (aac(6’)-Ib-cr, qnrB6, qnrS1, qnrVC5), qnrS and aac(6’)-Ib-cr have been previously reported in Chinese S. Kentucky [9,41], whereas qnrB and qnrVC5 are rarely encountered in S. Kentucky. The presence of the qnrB gene is associated with ciprofloxacin resistance [42], and qnrVC5 is typically identified in Vibrio species [43]. Notably, this study marks the first detection of qnrVC5 in S. Kentucky. Unlike ST314 strains did not exhibit any QRDRs mutations, 98.31% of ST198 exhibited at least one mutation, notably featuring three amino acid substitutions in gyrA (S83-, D87-) and ParC (S80-) that are known to confer high-level resistance to fluoroquinolones, including ciprofloxacin [6,7]. This study also notably identified, for the first time, an IncR plasmid as a key mediator of antimicrobial resistance in S. Kentucky ST314 strains from China, potentially contributing to the localized evolution of S. Kentucky ST314 within the country. While the IncR plasmid has been previously recognized in reports as an antibiotic-resistant plasmid in Enterobacteriaceae, including Salmonella [44-46], the IncR plasmid characterized in the ST314 strains in this study (pIncR-H176) was found to be specifically transferable into S. Kentucky ST314 strains. Moreover, it was observed to persist within ST314 strains without imposing any discernible fitness cost. Of particular interest, the presence of the plasmid pIncR-H176 was associated with increased resistance of the strain to ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, gentamicin, tetracycline, and azithromycin. These results suggest that this IncR plasmid may play a significant role in conferring resistance of the ST314 strains in China.

In this study, we conducted an analysis of the global spread patterns of antimicrobial-resistant S. Kentucky. Consistent with previous reports [34], we identified the 198.2 cluster as the predominant MDR cluster, initially emerging from the USA in 1958. This contrasts with earlier assertions linking its origin to Egypt in 1989, potentially linked to human travel [7]. This disparity could be attributed to the substantial inclusion of animal-source isolates alongside human-derived isolates and an extended temporal scope in our study. The 198.1 cluster, mainly comprising strains sourced from livestock or poultry in the United States, appears to have a smaller population size, potentially due to a paucity of ARGs [47]. Consequently, the rise of AMR is propelling the expansion of the ST198 lineage, with current driving forces behind ST198 strains evolving towards intricate and varied trends, possibly paving the way for further outbreaks of this genotype. Moreover, China emerges as a pivotal player in the global epidemic of ST198. As a nation experiencing significant transmission events involving ST198, S. Kentucky strains in China exhibit close genetic relatedness with strains from various other countries, indicating a broad genetic diversity and a diverse geographic origin of ST198 within China. These discoveries underscore substantial role of China in the worldwide dissemination of ST198.

While previous studies had not extensively examined the global dissemination of ST314, our research has unveiled its spread from the United Kingdom to Asia, Africa, Europe, and North America. The limited global attention directed towards ST314 can be attributed to the fact that the majority of PMQR genes are predominantly concentrated in Chinese clusters, principally facilitated by the IncR plasmid, which originated in and is primarily prevalent among Chinese ST314 strains. Additionally, Chinese ST314 strains exhibit genetic stability, promoting the secure inheritance of ARGs and facilitating the clonal expansion of ST314 within China. Although the geographical dispersal of ST314 strains in China is not as extensive as that observed with ST198, they have established a comparable north–south transmission network, indicating the potential for further proliferation of ST314 within China. It is crucial to highlight that Chinese ST314 strains have already extended their reach to Vietnam and the United Kingdom, hinting at the possibility of a global expansion of MDR ST314 strains, necessitating heightened surveillance measures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study delved into a comprehensive analysis of the spatial and temporal distribution of the antimicrobial-resistant foodborne pathogen S. Kentucky. Our findings highlight the extensive geographic prevalence of S. Kentucky, with the emergence of ST314 as a novel epidemic sequence type. The chicken supply chain appears to play a pivotal role in transmitting S. Kentucky ST314 to humans. Remarkably, our investigation unveiled that S. Kentucky ST314 was not only an antimicrobial-resistant clone but also showcased a distinct resistance mechanism compared to ST198. Notably, we made a groundbreaking discovery of the chromosome-borne blaNDM-5 in S. Kentucky, shedding new light on AMR in this pathogen. Furthermore, our research identified the IncR plasmid as a crucial mediator of antimicrobial resistance in S. Kentucky ST314 strains from China, suggesting its potential involvement in the localized evolution of S. Kentucky ST314 within China. Our study also traced the global transmission patterns of S. Kentucky, revealing that antimicrobial-resistant ST314 strains were predominantly clustered within Chinese subgroups. However, recent data shows that Chinese S. Kentucky has extended its reach to other countries, such as Vietnam and the United Kingdom. Collectively, our findings underscore the global public health significance of S. Kentucky, particularly the ST314 clone, which poses heightened concerns regarding AMR issues.
