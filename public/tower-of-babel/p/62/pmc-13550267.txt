NAT10 promotes colon cancer progression by enhancing predicted N4-acetylcytidine modification of Notch2 mRNA
Authors: Wang Xiao-Jie, Luo Ming, Ni Ping, Ye Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColon cancer is a prevalent malignant tumor with high mortality, and RNA modifications play critical roles in its progression. N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10)-mediated N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification has emerged as a key epigenetic regulator in gastrointestinal cancers, but its role and mechanism in colon cancer remain unclear. This study investigated whether NAT10 regulates Notch2 via predicted ac4C modification to affect colon cancer progression.Materials and methodsNAT10 and Notch2 expression was detected in 15 pairs of colon cancer and adjacent normal tissues. Colon cancer cell lines (HCT116, SW480) and normal colonic epithelial NCM460 cells were used. NAT10 overexpression/knockdown, NAT10 inhibitor Remodelin treatment, rescue experiments in both cell lines, and in vivo xenograft models were performed. CCK-8, Transwell, flow cytometry, TUNEL, acRIP-qPCR, RIP-qPCR, dual-luciferase reporter (WT/MUT), and mRNA stability assays were applied.ResultsNAT10 and Notch2 were significantly upregulated in colon cancer tissues and cells, with a positive correlation trend (r = 0.3571, P = 0.1913). NAT10 knockdown inhibited proliferation, migration, and promoted apoptosis; overexpression exerted opposite effects. Remodelin inhibited NAT10 activity and downregulated Notch2 in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, NAT10 bound to Notch2 mRNA, enhanced its predicted ac4C modification and stability. The predicted 3’UTR ac4C site was critical for NAT10-mediated regulation. Notch2 overexpression reversed NAT10 knockdown effects in both HCT116 and SW480 cells.In vivo subcutaneous xenograft models further confirmed that NAT10 knockdown significantly inhibits colon cancer tumor growth in mice, while Notch2 overexpression effectively rescues this inhibitory effect, supporting the oncogenic role of the NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 axis in vivo.ConclusionNAT10 promotes colon cancer progression in an enzymatic activity‑dependent manner by enhancing predicted ac4C modification of Notch2 mRNA to stabilize its expression. This study reveals a novel NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 regulatory axis and provides potential therapeutic targets for colon cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05427-w.Keywords: N-acetyltransferase 10, N4-acetylcytidine, Notch2, Colon cancer, tumor progression

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most prevalent gastrointestinal malignancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Despite advances in surgical resection, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade, the prognosis of patients with advanced or metastatic CRC remains poor due to late diagnosis, high invasiveness, early metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [2]. Therefore, exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC progression is critical for identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Post-transcriptional RNA modifications, termed the epitranscriptome, have emerged as a key regulatory layer in cancer development [3].To date, more than 170 RNA modifications have been identified, including N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N7-methylguanosine (m7G), pseudouridine (Ψ), and N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) [4]. Among these, m6A has been extensively studied and shown to regulate mRNA stability, translation, splicing, and nuclear export, thereby controlling CRC cell proliferation, stemness, metastasis, and drug resistance [5]. Similarly, m5C modification mediated by NSUN2 and DNMT2 enhances mRNA stability and promotes CRC progression [6, 7]. However, the biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of ac4C modification in CRC remain largely understudied compared with m6A and m5C.

Among RNA modifications, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) is a conserved modification catalyzed exclusively by N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10), which regulates mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and biological processes in various cancers [8] Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification plays critical roles in ribosome biogenesis, cell cycle progression, DNA damage response, and cellular senescence [9]. Importantly, NAT10 has been recognized as an oncogenic driver in multiple human cancers [10].Recent studies confirm that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification contributes to gastrointestinal cancer development and drug resistance, showing its potential as a therapeutic target [11, 12]. For instance, NAT10 promotes colon cancer progression by inhibiting ferroptosis via ac4C modification of FSP1 mRNA, and facilitates metastasis through ac4C-dependent regulation of target genes [13]. Another study demonstrated that NAT10 stabilizes NANOGP8 mRNA via ac4C modification to maintain cancer stemness and chemoresistance [14].Moreover, NAT10 regulates KIF23 mRNA via ac4C to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and facilitate CRC metastasis [15].NAT10 also mediates PD-L1 mRNA ac4C acetylation to promote immune escape in CRC [16].Despite these advances, the full downstream regulatory network of NAT10/ac4C in CRC remains incompletely defined, and its crosstalk with classical oncogenic signaling pathways remains largely unknown.

The Notch signaling pathway is evolutionarily conserved and governs cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [17, 18]. Notch2, a key Notch receptor, is frequently overexpressed in colorectal cancer (CRC) and associated with poor prognosis [19]. Functional studies have confirmed that Notch2 promotes CRC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance, and contributes to cancer stem cell maintenance [20]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Notch signaling is tightly regulated at the post-transcriptional level by diverse RNA modifications; for example, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) has been widely reported to modulate Notch2 mRNA stability, splicing, and translation efficiency in multiple malignancies [21, 22], while 5-methylcytosine (m5C) and other epitranscriptomic marks have also been implicated in regulating the expression and activity of other Notch family members [23]. Although transcriptional and post-translational regulation of Notch2 has been well documented, its post-transcriptional regulation via RNA modifications remains incompletely understood, and whether Notch2 can be regulated by NAT10-mediated N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification has not been reported to date.In addition, whether the oncogenic function of NAT10 depends on its acetyltransferase activity remains to be further verified.

In the present study, we hypothesized that NAT10 promotes colon cancer progression by enhancing predicted ac4C modification of Notch2 mRNA in an enzymatic activity‑dependent manner. We detected the expression of NAT10 and Notch2 in clinical colon cancer tissues and cell lines, explored their biological functions in vitro and in vivo, and clarified the underlying molecular mechanism using NAT10 inhibitor treatment and site‑mutated dual‑luciferase assays. This study aims to provide novel insights into the epigenetic regulation of colon cancer and potential targets for clinical treatment.

Materials and methods

Tissue samples

Fifteen pairs of colon cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected from patients who underwent surgical resection at LuoDian Hospital, Shanghai, China. All patients were diagnosed with colon cancer by pathological examination and did not receive chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or other anti-tumor treatments before surgery. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ethics Committee of LuoDian Hospital (Protocol ID: LDYY-KY-2024-12). The use of clinical data collection adheres to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture and transfection

Human colon cancer cell lines HCT116 (CCL-247™) and SW480 (CCL-228™), and normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 (CRL-3265™) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Vazyme, China) prior to experimental use. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. NAT10 overexpression vector (pcDNA3.1-NAT10) and empty vector (pcDNA3.1), Notch2 overexpression vector (pcDNA3.1-Notch2) and empty vector (pcDNA3.1), short hairpin RNA targeting NAT10 (shNAT10) and negative control (shNC) were purchased from Shanghai Xuhai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All recombinant vectors were verified by Sanger sequencing to confirm the correct insertion of target gene fragments and the absence of sequence mutations before transfection. Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

NAT10 inhibitor treatment

To determine whether the regulatory effect of NAT10 on Notch2 depends on its acetyltransferase activity, HCT116 cells were treated with Remodelin (Selleck Chemicals, USA), a highly selective small‑molecule inhibitor targeting NAT10 catalytic activity. Remodelin was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted to final concentrations of 0, 5, 10, and 20 µM according to previous studies in colon cancer. Cells were incubated with Remodelin or an equal volume of DMSO vehicle control at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂for 48 h. After treatment, cells were washed twice with ice‑cold phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) and harvested for total RNA extraction and total protein isolation, respectively. The mRNA level of Notch2 and the protein level of Notch2 intracellular domain (NICD2) were determined by RT-qPCR and western blot analysis to assess the effect of NAT10 enzymatic inhibition on Notch2 expression and activation.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 PCR instrument. GAPDH was used as an internal reference. The relative expression level of mRNA was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. All RT-qPCR reactions were performed in triplicate for each sample, and the experiment was independently repeated three times. The primer sequences are as follows in Table 1.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues and cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce Biotechnology, USA). Equal amounts of protein (50 µg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) at room temperature for 2 h. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting NAT10 (Abcam, ab194297, 1:1000), Notch2 intracellular domain (NICD2, cleaved/activated form of Notch2; Cell Signaling Technology, #5732, 1:1000), cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9661, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, #97166, 1:1000). After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074 and #7076, 1:2000) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Millipore, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. Each western blot experiment was independently repeated three times, with three technical replicates included for each sample.

CCK-8 assay

HCT116 and SW480 cells were transfected with shNC, shNAT10, ov-NC, or ov-NAT10 and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well. Cell viability was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after transfection. At each time point, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was detected using a microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) to evaluate cell proliferation. Each group contained six technical replicates, and all experiments were independently repeated three times.

Transwell assay

For the cell migration assay, 5 × 10⁴ cells suspended in serum‑free medium were added into the upper chamber of transwell inserts (Corning, USA). The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL medium supplemented with 30% fetal bovine serum. After incubation for 24 h, cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Five random fields were counted under an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany). Each group was set up with three biological replicates, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

TUNEL assay

TUNEL assay was performed to detect cell apoptosis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Colon cancer cells from the sh‑NC (control) and sh‑NAT10 (knockdown) groups were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‑100, incubated with TdT enzyme and fluorescein‑labeled dUTP, and counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured under a fluorescence microscope at ×200 magnification with a scale bar of 20 μm, and five random fields were selected to count fluorescein‑labeled apoptotic cells and DAPI‑stained total cells. The apoptosis rate was calculated as the ratio of apoptotic cells to total cells. Data were obtained from three independent experiments with three biological replicates per group and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

acRIP-qPCR and RIP-qPCR

RIP assays were performed using the Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed with RIP lysis buffer, and the lysates were incubated with anti-ac4C antibody (Abcam, ab253039, 1:50), anti-NAT10 antibody (Proteintech, 12792-1-AP, 1:50), or IgG antibody (Millipore, 12–370, 1:50) conjugated with protein A/G magnetic beads at 4 °C for 4 h. After washing, RNA was extracted from the beads and analyzed by qPCR to detect the enrichment of Notch2 mRNA.All qPCR reactions for acRIP and RIP were performed in triplicate, and the entire assay was independently repeated three times.

Luciferase reporter assay

The 3’UTR of Notch2 containing the wild-type (WT) ac4C modification site and the mutant-type (MUT) ac4C modification site were cloned into the pmirGLO reporter plasmid (Promega, USA). The reporter plasmids were co-transfected with shNAT10 or shNC into HCT116 and SW480 cells. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega, USA), with Renilla luciferase as an internal control.Each transfection group was set up with six technical replicates, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

mRNA stability assay

Cells were treated with 5 µg/mL actinomycin D (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to inhibit RNA synthesis. Total RNA was extracted at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h after treatment. The relative expression level of Notch2 mRNA was detected by RT-qPCR, and the half-life of Notch2 mRNA was calculated.RT-qPCR reactions were performed in triplicate for each time point and sample, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

In vivo Xenograft tumor model

Four‑week‑old male BALB/c nude mice were housed under specific pathogen‑free (SPF) conditions, with a 12‑hour light‑dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 2°C), and free access to standard chow and water. The mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): sh‑NC, sh‑NAT10, sh‑NAT10 + Notch2, and sh‑NC + Remodelin.HCT116 cells (1 × 10⁷ cells suspended in 100 µL of sterile PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every 5 days using a digital caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume (mm3) = L×W2/2.At day 30 post‑injection, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Tumors were dissected, photographed, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent immunohistochemical staining. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of LuoDian Hospital (Protocol ID: LDYY-KY-2024-12) and performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All in vitro experiments were independently repeated a minimum of three times, with technical/biological replicates set as specified in each experimental method section. Differences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-test, and differences among multiple groups were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NAT10 and Notch2 are upregulated in colon cancer tissues and cells

To investigate the expression pattern and clinical relevance of NAT10 and Notch2 in colon cancer, we first analyzed their mRNA and protein levels in 15 pairs of colon cancer tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues. RT-qPCR results showed that both NAT10 and Notch2 mRNA levels were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1a-b, P < 0.001). Consistently, Western blot analysis confirmed that the protein levels of NAT10 and NICD2 (Notch2 intracellular domain) were also markedly upregulated in colon cancer tissues (Fig. 1c). Pearson correlation analysis further revealed a positive correlation trend between NAT10 and Notch2 mRNA expression in these clinical samples (r = 0.3571, P = 0.1913, Fig. 1d). To validate these findings in vitro, we examined their expression in colon cancer cell lines (HCT116 and SW480) and the normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460. Both RT-qPCR and Western blot results demonstrated that NAT10 and Notch2 mRNA, as well as NAT10 and NICD2 protein, were significantly increased in colon cancer cells relative to NCM460 cells (Fig. 1e-g, P < 0.001). Collectively, these data indicate that NAT10 and Notch2 are coordinately upregulated in colon cancer tissues and cell lines, supporting their potential roles in colon cancer progression.

NAT10 promotes colon cancer cell proliferation, migration and inhibits apoptosis

To investigate the functional role of NAT10 in colon cancer progression, we first established stable NAT10 knockdown (sh‑NAT10) and overexpression (ov‑NAT10) models in HCT116 and SW480 cells. RT‑qPCR results confirmed that NAT10 mRNA expression was significantly reduced in sh‑NAT10 cells (both HCT116 and SW480) compared with the sh‑NC control group, while NAT10 mRNA levels were markedly increased in ov‑NAT10 cells compared with the ov‑NC control group (Fig. 2A, P < 0.01 or P < 0.001). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that NAT10 protein expression followed the same trend, with a significant decrease in sh‑NAT10 cells and a notable increase in ov‑NAT10 cells (Fig. 2B). To assess the effect of NAT10 on cell proliferation, we performed CCK‑8 assays over a 72‑hour time course. The results showed that NAT10 knockdown significantly inhibited cell viability in both HCT116 and SW480 cells, with a gradual reduction in proliferation rate over time, whereas NAT10 overexpression significantly accelerated cell proliferation, leading to higher cell viability than the corresponding control groups at 48 and 72 h (Fig. 2C, D, all P < 0.001). We next examined the effect of NAT10 on cell migration using Transwell assays, which showed that compared with the sh‑NC control group, the migratory capacity of HCT116 and SW480 cells was significantly reduced after NAT10 knockdown(Fig. 2E). To determine whether NAT10 affects apoptosis, we performed TUNEL staining, which revealed that the number of TUNEL‑positive apoptotic cells was significantly increased in the sh‑NAT10 group compared with the sh‑NC control group in both cell lines (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these functional assays demonstrate that NAT10 exerts oncogenic effects in colon cancer cells, promoting cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis.

NAT10 enhances ac4C modification and stability of Notch2 mRNA

To explore the molecular mechanism by which NAT10 regulates colon cancer progression, we predicted potential target genes of NAT10. Bioinformatics analysis showed that Notch2 mRNA contains ac4C modification sites (Fig. 3a).We first detected the effect of NAT10 on Notch2 expression in HCT116 and SW480. RT-qPCR and western blot results showed that NAT10 knockdown significantly reduced Notch2 mRNA and protein expressions, while NAT10 overexpression increased Notch2 expression (Fig. 3b-c, P < 0.001). acRIP-qPCR results indicated that NAT10 knockdown reduced the ac4C modification level of Notch2 mRNA (Fig. 3d, P < 0.001). RIP-qPCR assay confirmed that NAT10 could bind to Notch2 mRNA (Fig. 3e, P < 0.001). Luciferase reporter assay showed that NAT10 knockdown significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the wild-type Notch2 3’UTR reporter plasmid (WT), but had no effect on the mutant-type reporter plasmid (MUT) (Fig. 3f, P < 0.001). mRNA stability assay showed that NAT10 knockdown shortened the half-life of Notch2 mRNA (Fig. 3g, P < 0.01). These results suggest that NAT10 binds to Notch2 mRNA and enhances its ac4C modification, thereby improving Notch2 mRNA stability in an ac4C-dependent manner, relying on its acetyltransferase activity.

Notch2 overexpression reverses the inhibitory effect of NAT10 knockdown on colon cancer progression

To confirm that NAT10 promotes colon cancer progression through Notch2, rescue experiments were performed in both HCT116 and SW480 cells. Cells were transfected with shNAT10 alone or together with pcDNA3.1-Notch2. CCK-8 assay showed that Notch2 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of NAT10 knockdown on cell proliferation (Fig. 4A, P < 0.01). Transwell assay results indicated that Notch2 overexpression restored the migration abilities of NAT10 knockdown cells (Fig. 4B, P < 0.01). Western blot analysis showed that Notch2 overexpression reduced cleaved caspase-3 expression in NAT10 knockdown cells (Fig. 4C-D, P < 0.01).These results confirm that NAT10 promotes colon cancer progression mainly by upregulating Notch2.

NAT10 promotes colon tumor growth in vivo via the NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 axis

To validate the oncogenic role of NAT10 and its dependence on Notch2 in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenograft tumor models in nude mice. Tumor volume was measured every 5 days for 30 days, and tumor weight was recorded at the endpoint.

As shown in Fig. 5A-C, compared with the sh-NC control group, the sh-NAT10 knockdown group exhibited significantly reduced tumor volume and tumor weight (both ***P < 0.001). Similarly, treatment with the NAT10-specific inhibitor Remodelin also markedly suppressed tumor growth, with a comparable inhibitory effect to genetic NAT10 knockdown (***P < 0.001 vs. sh‑NC). Notably, overexpression of Notch2 in sh-NAT10‑transfected cells (sh-NAT10 + Notch2 group) effectively rescued the growth-suppressive phenotype, resulting in significantly larger tumor volume and heavier tumor weight than those in the sh-NAT10 group (statistical significance shown in Fig. 5B-C).These in vivo results are fully consistent with our in vitro functional data, confirming that NAT10 promotes colon cancer tumor growth in vivo, at least partially, by upregulating Notch2 expression.

Discussion

Colon cancer represents a globally prevalent gastrointestinal malignancy characterized by high heterogeneity, metastatic potential, and therapeutic resistance [24]. Mounting evidence has highlighted post-transcriptional RNA modifications as critical regulators of tumor progression, among which N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification mediated by NAT10 has emerged as a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism governing mRNA stability, translation, and cancer malignancy [25]. In the present study, we demonstrated that NAT10 was significantly upregulated in colon cancer tissues and cell lines, where it promoted cell proliferation, migration, and inhibited apoptosis. Mechanistically, NAT10 directly bound to Notch2 mRNA, enhanced its ac4C modification, improved mRNA stability, and upregulated Notch2 expression in an acetyltransferase activity‑dependent manner. Rescue experiments and xenograft models further verified that Notch2 mediated the oncogenic function of NAT10. Collectively, our findings identified the NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 axis as a novel driver of colon cancer progression, providing new insights into epitranscriptomic regulation of colon cancer.

NAT10 has been increasingly recognized as a pivotal oncoprotein in multiple human cancers, and its dysregulation contributes extensively to tumor initiation and progression [26]. Recent studies using clinical cohorts and bioinformatic analyses have confirmed that NAT10 is overexpressed in colon cancer and positively correlates with advanced tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, and poor patient survival. Functional experiments further revealed that NAT10 facilitates cancer cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and extracellular matrix remodeling by stabilizing multiple target mRNAs through ac4C modification [27]. In line with these findings, our results confirmed that NAT10 was markedly elevated in colon cancer tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues and in colon cancer cell lines relative to normal colonic epithelial cells. Functional assays demonstrated that NAT10 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis, whereas NAT10 overexpression exerted opposite effects. These results are consistent with the pro‑tumor functions of NAT10 reported in recent studies and further validate its role as a bona fide oncogene in colon cancer. Notably, our study extended the regulatory landscape of NAT10 by identifying Notch2 as a previously unreported downstream target, thereby broadening the mechanistic understanding of NAT10‑mediated oncogenic signaling.

Notch signaling serves as a core pathway governing colon cancer cell fate, proliferation, and malignant transformation, with Notch2 acting as a key functional receptor in colorectal tumorigenesis [28].Clinical studies have shown that Notch2 is frequently overexpressed in colon cancer tissues and significantly associated with tumor invasion, distant metastasis, and unfavorable prognosis [29]. At the molecular level, Notch2 promotes cancer cell stemness, chemoresistance, and epithelial‑mesenchymal transition by regulating downstream transcriptional programs. However, post‑transcriptional regulation of Notch2, particularly at the level of RNA modifications, remains largely elusive. Although m6A modification has been reported to modulate Notch2 expression in certain cancer types, whether other RNA modifications participate in Notch2 regulation remains unclear. Our study is the first to demonstrate that Notch2 mRNA undergoes ac4C modification and is directly stabilized by NAT10, filling a critical gap in the post‑transcriptional regulatory network of Notch2 and linking the ac4C epitranscriptome to classical oncogenic signaling in colon cancer.

A central mechanistic finding of our study is that NAT10 regulates Notch2 expression in an acetyltransferase activity‑dependent manner. Emerging evidence has established that NAT10 exerts its biological functions primarily through its acetyltransferase activity, which catalyzes ac4C modification and enhances mRNA stability [30]. Pharmacological inhibition using Remodelin, a selective NAT10 inhibitor, has been shown to suppress tumor progression by blocking ac4C modification in multiple cancer models, including lung cancer, osteosarcoma, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [31, 32]. Consistent with these reports, our results demonstrated that Remodelin treatment significantly downregulated Notch2 expression in a dose‑dependent manner. Furthermore, dual‑luciferase reporter assays revealed that mutation of the predicted ac4C site in the Notch2 3’UTR completely abolished NAT10‑mediated regulation. These results are consistent with the canonical model of NAT10‑dependent mRNA stabilization and confirm that Notch2 is a direct functional target of NAT10‑mediated ac4C modification. Combined with acRIP‑qPCR, RIP‑qPCR, and mRNA stability assays, our data establish a robust molecular cascade in which NAT10 enhances Notch2 mRNA stability via ac4C modification to promote colon cancer progression.

Rescue experiments and in vivo xenograft models further confirmed the functional importance of the NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 axis. Ectopic expression of Notch2 effectively reversed the inhibitory effects of NAT10 knockdown on cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis, indicating that Notch2 is a critical downstream effector of NAT10. In vivo, NAT10 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth, whereas Notch2 overexpression restored tumorigenic potential. Moreover, Remodelin treatment mirrored the effects of NAT10 knockdown, suppressing tumor growth to a similar extent. These findings are consistent with recent studies demonstrating the anti‑tumor efficacy of NAT10 inhibition in preclinical models and highlight the translational potential of targeting NAT10 in colon cancer. Collectively, our in vitro and in vivo data provide comprehensive evidence supporting the oncogenic role of the NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 axis in colon cancer.

Despite these novel findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the clinical sample size was relatively small, which may limit the statistical power of correlation analysis. Second, subcutaneous xenograft models cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and metastatic cascade. Third, the precise endogenous ac4C site on Notch2 mRNA remains to be mapped at single‑nucleotide resolution using high‑throughput ac4C sequencing. Fourth, our rescue experiments were solely performed by ectopic overexpression of Notch2, and we did not validate the NAT10/Notch2 axis using exogenous Notch ligands such as DLL or JAG family proteins to activate canonical Notch signaling. This represents a limitation of the present study, and further validation using ligand‑mediated Notch activation will be included in our future research. Future studies with larger multi‑center cohorts, orthotopic transplantation models, and high‑precision mapping techniques are warranted to further validate our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study elucidates that N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) is significantly upregulated in colon cancer tissues and cell lines, and exerts an oncogenic role in colon cancer progression by directly binding to Notch2 mRNA and enhancing its N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification at the 3’UTR region. This post-transcriptional modification event effectively stabilizes Notch2 mRNA, upregulates Notch2 expression, and ultimately promotes colon cancer cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptotic processes. Our findings identify a novel NAT10/ac4C/Notch2 regulatory axis in colon cancer, which not only enriches the molecular mechanism of ac4C RNA modification in regulating the malignant phenotype of colon cancer, but also reveals the new regulatory mode of Notch2 expression at the RNA modification level.
