Alumina nanoparticles-induced reduction in Ito, f contributes to the occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias
Authors: Haoze Deng, Mengdie Xia, Yiyang Li, Jing Yang, Xiaodong Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAluminum oxide nanoparticles (Al₂O₃-NPs) are currently widely applied, although they are known to induce electrocardiographic abnormalities, their crystal phase-dependent effects (including α- and γ-phases) and underlying electrophysiological mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsCardiac injury of α-, γ-, and mix-phase Al₂O₃-NPs was investigated via intraperitoneal injection (100 mg/kg) in rats and exposure (20–100 µg/mL) in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs). Multidimensional analyses included electrocardiography (ECG), monophasic action potential (MAP) recording, patch-clamp electrophysiology (Ito, f current), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).Resultsα-Al₂O₃-NPs significantly prolonged QT intervals (119.53 ± 9.59 ms, increased by 23.34%; p < 0.001) and elevated arrhythmia scores (2.6 ± 0.6 vs. 0 in controls; p < 0.01), whereas γ-Al₂O₃-NPs showed no such effects. The monophasic action potential duration at 90% repolarization (MAPD₉₀) in the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group was 187.6 ± 4.9 ms, which was significantly increased compared with the control group (p < 0.001). α-Al₂O₃-NPs were found to inhibit Ito, f current density in a dose-dependent manner, with a significant reduction of 81.8% observed at 100 µg/mL (p < 0.001). Ultrastructural damage (myofibril disorganization, mitochondrial cristae fragmentation) and intracellular accumulation of nanoparticles were observed in α-Al₂O₃-NPs group by TEM, with no observed effects for γ-Al₂O₃-NPs.ConclusionAlumina nanoparticles exhibit crystal phase-dependent cardiotoxicity. α-Al₂O₃-NPs induce QT prolongation, MAPD prolongation, ventricular arrhythmias, and ultrastructural damage in rat hearts. In parallel, α-Al₂O₃-NPs suppress Ito, f currents in cultured neonatal rat ventricular myocytes. These findings suggest that Ito, f inhibition may contribute, to the electrophysiological abnormalities induced by α-Al₂O₃-NPs and provide new insights into the potential mechanisms underlying nanoparticle-induced ventricular arrhythmias.

Introduction

In recent decades, metal oxide nanoparticles have been widely applied across various fields owing to their unique physical and chemical properties [1]. In industrial production, aluminum oxide nanoparticles (Al₂O₃-NPs) are predominantly employed in anti-reflective coatings [2], ceramic manufacturing [3], catalysts, and gas diffusion barriers [4], owing to their structural diversity, including variations in crystal structures, morphologies, and surface characteristics. In the biomedical field, Al₂O₃-NPs function not only as biosensors for monitoring high-risk pollutants but also as drug carriers in therapeutic applications [5]. Furthermore, these nanoparticles exhibit considerable potential in areas such as sustainable agriculture and the textile industry [6, 7]. The crystal structure of Al₂O₃ NPs represents the primary determinant of their physicochemical properties. The most common crystal phases comprise α-Al₂O₃ and γ-Al₂O₃. Specifically, α-Al₂O₃ is characterized by a high-temperature stable phase (> 1200 °C), exceptional hardness (Mohs hardness 9), low specific surface area (< 10 m²/g), outstanding wear resistance, and chemical inertness, which collectively enable its widespread use in ceramic reinforcement materials, high-temperature coatings, and bio-inert implants [8]. In contrast, γ-Al₂O₃ is primarily used in catalyst supports, pollutant adsorption systems, and gas sensing devices owing to its substantially higher specific surface area (200–300 m²/g), well-defined porous architecture, abundant surface hydroxyl (-OH) groups, and Lewis acidic Al³⁺ sites [9].

The large-scale production and commercialization of Al₂O₃-NPs for diverse applications have increased the risk of human and environmental exposure through fugitive emissions, accidental spills, and improper post-use disposal. Recent studies have shown that Al₂O₃-NPs pose potential threats to aquatic ecosystems and soil environments, causing adverse effects on aquatic organisms and crops [10, 11]. Although experimental evidence indicates that Al₂O₃-NPs exhibit limited transdermal permeability [12], these nanomaterials display a notable ability to traverse various biological barriers (e.g., respiratory and gastrointestinal systems) [13, 14], potentially accumulating in highly sensitive tissue/organ systems [15].

Chronic oral administration of Al₂O₃-NPs (rod-type, 1.5, 3, and 6 mg/kg) in Sprague-Dawley rats over 13 weeks revealed significant aluminum deposition in major organs, including cardiac accumulation patterns [15]. Elevated levels of oxidative stress markers (e.g., TBARS and NOx) and cardiac creatine kinase (CK) activity were observed following long-term oral exposure [16]. These findings collectively indicate that aluminum oxide nanoparticles can cause severe myocardial injury. Notably, Al₂O₃-NP-exposed rats also exhibited characteristic electrocardiographic abnormalities, including QT interval prolongation [17], suggesting the arrhythmogenic potential of alumina nanoparticles.

The rapid transient outward potassium current (Ito, f )is a key component of the initial repolarization phase (Phase 1) of the cardiac action potential, mediated by K⁺ efflux and contributing to action potential (AP) formation. Ito, f channels are predominantly encoded by Kv4.2/Kv4.3 subunits and exhibit prominent expression in ventricular epicardium and mid-myocardial layers (M cells) [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that a reduction in Ito, f prolongs the action potential duration (APD), creating a substrate for ventricular arrhythmias [19]. The impact and underlying mechanisms of Al₂O₃-NPs, including α- and γ-subtypes, on ventricular arrhythmias remain unclear. This study explores the effects of Al₂O₃-NPs on Ito, f and aims to elucidate the potential arrhythmogenic mechanisms.

Method

Nanoparticle characterization

α-Al₂O₃ nanoparticles (30 nm) were obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China, Cat# A800207), and γ-Al₂O₃ nanoparticles (20 nm) were also purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China, Cat# A800208). Alumina nanoparticles (Al₂O₃ NPs) were dispersed in double-distilled (dd) water using a tip-type ultrasonicator (Ulh700S; 19 kHz, 30 min). Nanoparticle characterization was subsequently performed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Animals and ethical approval

All adult Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats and 1–3-day-old neonatal SD rats were purchased from Henan Sibeisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Adult SD rats were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Soochow University under controlled temperature and humidity conditions with a 12:12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water.

All adult SD rats were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 40–50 mg/kg. At the end of the experiments, the rats were euthanized under deep anesthesia by exsanguination and/or cervical dislocation, and death was confirmed by the absence of heartbeat, respiration, and reflexes.Neonatal SD rats aged 1–3 days were anesthetized by hypothermia before rapid heart excision. Death was confirmed after heart removal.

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Soochow University(Approval No. SUDA20251107A03) and strictly followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Academies Press, 8th edition, 2011).

ECG recording

Twenty-four male Sprague-Dawley rats (300 g) were equally divided into four groups: a control group, an mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group, an α-Al₂O₃-NPs group, and a γ-Al₂O₃-NPs group, with six rats in each group. All groups received intraperitoneal injections of nanoparticles at a dose of 100 mg/kg, with the control group receiving an equal volume of dd water. After 90 min of treatment, ECG recordings were performed. The rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital and placed in a prone position. Lead II electrocardiograms were recorded using a RM6240EC physiological signal acquisition system (Chengdu Instrument Factory) via three limb electrodes. The QT interval was measured from the onset of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave and corrected using Fridericia’s formula (QTc = QT / RR1/3), which provides a more stable correction under tachycardic conditions [20]. Arrhythmia scores were determined as follows: 0 points for no arrhythmia; 1 point for atrial or ventricular premature beats; 2 points for supraventricular tachycardia or paired ventricular premature beats; 3 points for bigeminy/trigeminy or nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (≥ 3 but < 10 consecutive ventricular premature beats); and 4 points for sustained ventricular tachycardia (≥ 10 consecutive ventricular premature beats) [21].

The intraperitoneal dose of 100 mg/kg was selected as an acute challenge dose to evaluate the phase-dependent arrhythmogenic potential of Al₂O₃-NPs within a short experimental window. This dose was not intended to reproduce environmental or occupational human exposure levels. Rather, it was chosen based on previous rodent nanotoxicology studies in which Al₂O₃-NPs were administered at comparable dose magnitudes and induced systemic or cardiac toxicity. Intraperitoneal administration was used to achieve relatively reproducible systemic exposure and to minimize variability related to pulmonary or gastrointestinal absorption.

MAP recording

Following completion of ECG recordings, animals from experimental groups exhibiting significant ECG alterations were selected. Their hearts were rapidly excised and immersed in ice-cold Krebs-Henseleit bicarbonate buffer comprising (in mM): 118 NaCl, 4.7 KCl, 2.5 CaCl₂, 1.2 MgSO₄, 25 NaHCO₃, and 11 glucose, with the pH adjusted to 7.4. The aortic stump of the heart was cannulated retrogradely and connected to a Langendorff perfusion apparatus. The heart was then perfused retrogradely via the aorta with Krebs-Henseleit bicarbonate buffer, continuously oxygenated with a mixture of 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂. The environmental temperature was maintained at 36 ± 1 °C to mimic physiological conditions. Monophasic action potentials (MAPs) were recorded using the RM6240EC physiological signal acquisition system (Chengdu Instrument Factory, China). A suction electrode was positioned on the left ventricular epicardium to record monophasic action potentials (MAPs). A stimulating electrode was placed on the right atrium. Stimulation was delivered at a frequency of 4 Hz, with a pulse width of 2 ms and an intensity of 1.5× threshold [22, 23]. After 10 min of continuous, stable pacing, eight consecutive steady-state MAP waveforms were recorded. Action potential duration at 50% repolarization (APD₅₀) and 90% repolarization (APD₉₀) were assessed, and mean values were calculated for subsequent analysis.

Isolation of NRVMs and cell culture

Neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) were isolated as described previously [24]. Briefly, hearts were excised from 1–3-day-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat pups under aseptic conditions. The cardiac apex was minced into fragments approximately 2 mm in size using sterile surgical instruments. The tissue fragments were washed 2–3 times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then immersed in a solution containing 0.1% trypsin. The fragments in trypsin solution were placed at 4 °C on a shaker for overnight digestion (approximately 12–18 h). The following day, the trypsin solution was discarded, and the tissue was subjected to repeated digestion with 1 mg/mL collagenase II, with each digestion lasting 3–5 min and a total of approximately 8 digestion cycles. The suspended cells released into the supernatant were collected, centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were incubated in a humidified 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 90–120 min to facilitate fibroblast depletion via differential adhesion. The supernatant containing non-adherent cells (predominantly NRVMs) was collected and transferred to culture dishes for continued incubation.

Transmission electron microscope

The alumina nanoparticles that induced significant ECG alterations in SD rats were selected for TEM assessment of their effects on animal tissues and cells. Cardiac tissue samples were obtained from male Sprague-Dawley rats (300 g). Rats received an intraperitoneal injection of nanoparticle suspension (100 mg/kg). After 90 min of exposure, hearts were excised and sectioned into 1 mm³ pieces. The tissue fragments were immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). NRVMs were exposed to nanoparticles (100 µg/mL) for 30 min, gently digested with 0.2% trypsin, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and fixed in fresh 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Both tissue and cell samples were subsequently processed for TEM.

Ito, f recording

The nanoparticles that induced significant ECG alterations in SD rats were selected for patch-clamp recordings. NRVMs were exposed to these nanoparticles at graded concentrations (20, 50, and 100 µg/mL) for 30 min before whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Signals were processed using an Axon MultiClamp 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA), filtered at 2 kHz, and digitized at 10 kHz using a Digidata 1440 A digitizer (Molecular Devices). Ventricular myocytes were bathed in an external solution during recordings. Patch pipettes with resistances of 2–5 MΩ were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries using a MODEL-P97 micropipette puller (Sutter Instrument Company) and filled with internal solution. After achieving a gigaohm seal, gentle suction was applied to rupture the membrane and establish whole-cell configuration. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed in voltage-clamp mode to measure ion channel currents. For recording the fast transient outward potassium current (Ito, f ), the extracellular solution contained (in mM): 140 NaCl, 4 KCl, 2 CaCl₂, 1 MgCl₂·6 H₂O, 10 HEPES, 10 D-Glucose, and 0.5 CdCl₂·2.5 H₂O, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. The pipette (intracellular) solution contained (in mM): 140 KCl, 1 MgCl₂·6 H₂O, 10 EGTA, 10 HEPES, and 5 MgATP, adjusted to pH 7.25 with KOH. Ito, f was recorded using a kinetic isolation protocol at room temperature. Cells were stepped to − 80 mV for 150 ms from a holding potential of − 20 mV to allow recovery of Ito, f but not Ito, s, followed by a 50 ms prepulse to − 40 mV to inactivate Na⁺ current before a series of 500 ms depolarizing pulses from − 40 to + 60 mV [25]. The current–voltage (I–V) relationship was plotted using OriginPro 2025.

The concentrations of 20, 50, and 100 µg/mL were selected to assess concentration-dependent acute electrophysiological effects of Al₂O₃-NPs in NRVMs. This concentration range was chosen based on previously published in vitro nanotoxicology studies and the feasibility of stable whole-cell patch-clamp recordings after short-term nanoparticle exposure. These concentrations were used for mechanistic evaluation and should not be interpreted as direct equivalents of environmental exposure levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro 2025 software (OriginLab Corporation, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (for multiple groups). Arrhythmia scores and EAD frequencies are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

Results

Characterization of nanoparticles

Based on TEM image analysis, the average diameter of α-Al₂O₃-NPs was measured as 29.2 ± 4.53 nm, while that of γ-Al₂O₃-NPs was 21.5 ± 4.53 nm (Fig. 1).These values closely matched the specifications provided by Macklin Inc.

Electrophysiological effects

Compared to the Control group (QTc: 96.92 ± 6.85 ms, n = 6), both the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group and the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group exhibited significantly prolonged QT interval and corrected QT interval (QTc) in SD rats (p < 0.001)(Fig. 2, A). Among them, the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group showed the highest QTc value (119.53 ± 9.59 ms), representing a 23.34% increase relative to the Control group, followed by the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group (112.53 ± 9.59 ms), which increased by 16.11% (Fig. 2,C and D). In contrast, the γ-Al₂O₃-NPs group did not show any significant change in QT interval (p > 0.05). A large number of premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), including typical ventricular bigeminy patterns, were observed in the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group. In addition, compared with the control group, an increased frequency of PVCs was also observed in the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group, whereas no significant PVCs were detected in the γ-Al₂O₃-NPs group. Arrhythmia scores revealed that the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group had the highest score (2.5 (2–3)), followed by the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group (1 (1–2)), while the arrhythmia score of the γ-Al₂O₃-NPs group showed no significant difference compared to the Control group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2, E).

Given that alumina nanoparticles significantly prolong the QT interval and induce a spectrum of arrhythmic events in rats, and considering that alterations in cardiac action potentials underlie these QT interval changes, it is imperative to determine the impact of alumina nanoparticles on ventricular myocardial action potentials. Alterations in the monophasic action potential are illustrated in the corresponding figure. Alumina nanoparticles significantly prolonged the monophasic action potential duration (MAPD) in rat myocardium(Fig. 3, A). The α-Al₂O₃-NPs group exhibited the highest values for both MAPD₅₀ (111.6 ± 3.4 ms) and MAPD₉₀ (187.6 ± 4.9 ms). Although, in the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group, the MAPD₅₀ (99.2 ± 5.2 ms) and MAPD₉₀ (173.0 ± 12.0 ms) values were lower than those in the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group, they remained significantly prolonged compared to the Control group.(Fig. 3, B). This pattern of prolongation explains the trend observed for QT interval prolongation. In addition, compared with the control group, both the α-Al₂O₃-NPs and mix-Al₂O₃-NPs groups exhibited an increased frequency of early afterdepolarizations (EADs) per minute, with the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group showing the most pronounced increase (30.5 (25–34) events/min). (Fig. 3, C, D). Notably, we observed that α-Al₂O₃-NPs induced ventricular fibrillation-like waveforms(Fig. 3, E).

The Ito, f current density was significantly suppressed by alumina nanoparticles in a dose-dependent manner in vitro(Fig. 4,A). Among the concentration gradients tested, NRVMs exposed to 100 µg/mL nanoparticles showed the most pronounced reduction in peak current density: a decrease of 70.7 ± 1.9% in the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group and 81.8 ± 0.8% in the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group(Fig. 4,B, C). A significant reduction was also observed in the 50 µg/mL group: a decrease of 32.4 ± 12.3% in the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group and 43.1 ± 8.1% in the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group(Fig. 4,B, D). Since the Ito, f current is one of the major early repolarizing currents contributing to the cardiac action potential (AP), its suppression can lead to prolonged action potential duration (APD). Alumina nanoparticles significantly inhibited the Ito, f current in NRVMs, and α-Al₂O₃-NPs exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect on Ito, f than mix-Al₂O₃-NPs, which might explain the observed MAPD prolongation.

Transmission electron microscopy

To elucidate the structural alterations inflicted by nanoparticles on rat cardiomyocytes, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to analyze cardiac tissue. In the Control group, myocardial fibers were densely packed with clearly discernible structures such as Z-lines and H-zones(Fig. 5,A, B). Mitochondria exhibited intact double membranes and well-organized, clearly defined cristae. In contrast, both the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group and the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group displayed disorganized myofibril arrangement. Mitochondria in these groups frequently exhibited ruptured outer membranes, accompanied by cristae that were indistinct, fragmented, and reduced in number (Fig. 5, C-F).

Additionally, a small number of autophagolysosomes and intracellular nanoparticles were observed within cardiomyocytes. Notably, while nanoparticles were occasionally observed in the cardiac tissue of the γ-Al₂O₃-NPs group, the myofibril architecture remained largely preserved (Fig. 5,G, H). Discussion

Over recent decades, alumina nanoparticles (Al₂O₃-NPs) have found extensive applications across various fields, including industrial production, agriculture, textiles, and biomedicine [26]. Existing research has demonstrated that alumina nanoparticles induce significant myocardial inflammation, oxidative stress damage, and cause notable alterations in the electrocardiograms (ECGs) of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats [17]. However, their further electrophysiological effects remain largely unexplored. We propose that alumina nanoparticles exhibit crystal phase-dependent proarrhythmic properties. Ultrastructural analysis revealed that α-alumina nanoparticles disrupt the alignment of ventricular myocardial fibers, damage the mitochondrial outer membrane and cristae, and induce the formation of autophagolysosomes in rat hearts. Electrophysiologically, α-alumina nanoparticles induced QT prolongation, MAPD prolongation, and arrhythmias in adult rats. In parallel, α-alumina nanoparticles suppressed Ito, f in cultured neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs), suggesting that inhibition of transient outward potassium current may contribute, at least in part, to the observed electrophysiological abnormalities.

Although previous studies have indicated that Al₂O₃-NPs can alter ECGs in SD rats, research specifically distinguishing their effects based on different crystal structures remains limited. The crystal structure of alumina nanoparticles is a critical determinant of their physicochemical properties and biological behavior. Therefore, we selected the two most common crystal phases for investigation. We observed that the QT interval increased with escalating nanoparticle concentrations in both the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group and the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group, consistent with prior findings. However, γ-Al₂O₃-NPs did not induce a significant prolongation of the QT interval. Furthermore, the monophasic action potential duration (MAPD) in the ventricular myocardium was significantly prolonged in the α-Al₂O₃-NPs group and the mix-Al₂O₃-NPs group compared to the control group. These results support our hypothesis that Al₂O₃ NPs exhibit crystal structure-specific electrophysiological effects. α-Alumina is thermodynamically stable, possessing a rigid, close-packed crystal structure (Mohs hardness 9). Under physiological pH, it primarily exposes neutral Al–O surface terminations [27]. In contrast, γ-alumina adopts a metastable spinel structure, characterized by a high specific surface area (200–300 m²/g) and Lewis acidic Al³⁺ sites with surface hydroxyl groups [28]. We postulate that the α-Al₂O₃ surface, dominated by neutral Al–O terminations, exhibits hydrophobicity (high water contact angle > 90°). Its low hydroxyl density impairs hydration layer formation, reducing the protein corona thickness [29]. Consequently, this increases the probability of direct interaction with the hydrophobic regions of the phospholipid bilayer, promoting cellular internalization. Conversely, the γ-Al₂O₃ surface is rich in high-density structural hydroxyl groups (–OH), facilitating rapid hydration in aqueous environments and the formation of a hydrogen-bond network. This property enhances the adsorption of proteins, leading to a dense protein corona, which in turn reduces membrane penetration efficiency and hinders internalization [30]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results further demonstrated that after 90 min of exposure, the intracellular load of α-Al₂O₃-NPs was substantially higher than that of γ-Al₂O₃-NPs, supporting our hypothesis.

In rodent ventricular cardiomyocytes, the early repolarization phase of the action potential, mediated by Ito, f, is crucial for maintaining transmural repolarization dispersion. A reduction in repolarizing K⁺ currents, particularly Ito, f, prolongs the action potential duration (APD) [25, 31–33]. Prolongation of both APD and the QT interval can trigger a series of arrhythmic events, including premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), ventricular tachycardia (VT), and ventricular fibrillation (VF) [34]. The concentration-dependent inhibition of Ito, f observed in our study (≈ 80% at 100 µg mL⁻¹) was accompanied by APD prolongation and increased arrhythmic susceptibility in vivo, indicating that Ito, f suppression is mechanistically consistent with the observed electrophysiological phenotype. Although direct evidence demonstrating metal ion blockade of Ito, f channels is currently lacking, we propose two potential mechanisms: (1) α-Al₂O₃-NP-induced oxidative stress may downregulate Kv4.2 transcription via the NF-κB signaling pathway [35]; and (2) Owing to its rigid coordination geometry and strong Lewis acidity, Al³⁺ may disrupt the conformational transitions required for normal channel gating by forming stable complexes with critical coordinating residues, such as histidine and cysteine, on ion channel proteins36. This interaction could slow gating kinetics and thereby impair the activation and/or deactivation of voltage-gated currents [36]. While the present findings primarily support an effect on Kv4.x-mediated Ito,f, this mechanism may not be unique to Ito, f channels and could potentially extend to other cardiac ion channels. Such broader channel perturbations may influence action potential morphology, repolarization reserve, and susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias. Further studies are needed to define the channel selectivity and molecular basis of these effects.

Beyond modulating sarcolemmal ion channels, α-Al₂O₃-NPs also induced mitochondrial membrane rupture, cristae disruption, and autophagosome formation. We hypothesize that this results from endocytosed nanoparticles fusing with lysosomes, where their high rigidity and physiological insolubility compromise lysosomal membrane integrity. Subsequent lysosomal rupture releases hydrolytic enzymes, inflicting damage on multiple cellular structures—including mitochondria. Given the particular abundance of mitochondria in cardiac tissue and their structural integrity being essential for reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction coupled with ROS accumulation is recognized as a critical contributor to cardiac pathology [37, 38]. Existing evidence suggests that ROS accumulation can directly inhibit Kv4.2 channel function [39, 40]. The function of the Kv4.2 channel is ATP-dependent, requiring phosphorylation (e.g., via the PKA pathway) [41]. ATP depletion enhances dephosphorylation, accelerating channel inactivation and thereby suppressing Ito, f channel activity. Concurrently, ATP depletion impairs the function of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Ca²⁺-ATPase pumps, leading to intracellular calcium overload, which may potentially further exacerbate Ito, f suppression.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, because the in vitro experiments were performed in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes rather than adult cardiomyocytes, the present findings should be interpreted with caution. Developmental differences in ion channel expression and electrophysiological properties may limit the direct extrapolation of NRVM data to the adult heart. Therefore, inhibition of Ito, f should be regarded as a potential contributor to, rather than a definitive explanation for, the electrophysiological abnormalities observed in vivo. Second, limitations related to dose selection and exposure route should be acknowledged. The single intraperitoneal dose of 100 mg/kg represents an acute challenge model designed for hazard identification and mechanistic electrophysiological assessment, rather than a direct simulation of human exposure. In real-world settings, alumina nanoparticles are more likely to enter the body through inhalation, oral ingestion, or dermal contact, particularly in occupational environments. Therefore, the toxicokinetic profile of intraperitoneal administration may differ substantially from that of environmentally relevant exposure routes. Although the selected dose was informed by previous rodent nanotoxicology studies, the possibility of artificial toxicity related to acute particle overload, local irritation, osmotic stress, or nonspecific injury cannot be fully excluded. However, the observation that γ-Al₂O₃-NPs administered at the same mass dose and in the same vehicle did not induce comparable QT prolongation, MAPD prolongation, arrhythmic events, or Ito, f inhibition suggests that the observed effects were not solely attributable to nonspecific injection-related or particle-load effects. Future studies using lower-dose, chronic inhalation or oral exposure models are required to better evaluate the cardiovascular risks of Al₂O₃-NPs under environmentally and occupationally relevant conditions.

Conclusion

Alumina nanoparticles (Al₂O₃-NPs) are currently being extensively utilized across diverse fields. This study investigated their cardiotoxicity with a focus on electrophysiological effects. We demonstrate that Al₂O₃-NPs exhibit crystal phase-dependent proarrhythmic properties. Specifically, α-alumina nanoparticles dose-dependently suppress the fast transient outward K⁺ current (Ito, f), prolong monophasic action potential duration (MAPD), and thereby contribute to QT interval prolongation, ultimately triggering a series of ventricular arrhythmic events. Concurrently, α-Al₂O₃-NPs induce significant ultrastructural damage in cardiac tissue, including disorganized myofibril alignment, disrupted mitochondrial cristae, and promoted autolysosome formation. Collectively, our findings reveal that suppression of Ito, f by α-Al₂O₃-NPs likely constitutes a key mechanism underlying Al₂O₃-NP-induced ventricular arrhythmogenesis. Future investigations will prioritize chronic exposure effects and explore therapeutic approaches targeting Ito, f channel dysfunction.
