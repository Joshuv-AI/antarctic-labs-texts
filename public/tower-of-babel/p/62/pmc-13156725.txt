Pectoralis major tendon repair outcomes: comparison of transosseous tunnel and cortical button fixation techniques
Authors: Stanley Liu, Andrew L. Chen, Samuel E. Mircoff, Krishin Shivdasani, Nickolas G. Garbis, Dane H. Salazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe traditional gold standard for repairing pectoralis major tendon ruptures involves the utilization of a cortical trough and transosseous bone tunnels. However, it has been shown to create stress risers that could increase the risk of developing humeral fractures. Modern repair techniques have evolved using either anchors or cortical button fixation. There is currently no clinical outcome data comparing the outcomes between the 2 repair methods. This retrospective cohort study aims to compare the clinical and patient-reported outcomes between the cortical button and transosseous tunnel repair techniques in pectoralis major tendon ruptures.MethodsA retrospective chart review for pectoralis major tendon repairs was conducted, identifying 10 patients who underwent cortical-button fixation and 9 patients who underwent transosseous tunnel fixation between January 2011 and December 2021. Patient demographics and case characteristics were collected for comparison in addition to follow-up data on post-operative patient-reported outcomes, strength, range of motion, and complications.ResultsAt a median follow-up of 48 months (quartiles: 41, 50), we found that transosseous tunnel repair patients reported significantly worse scores compared to cortical button repair patients on both the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand surveys (5.0 [quartiles: 3.3, 8.3] vs. 1.3 [quartiles: 0.0, 2.3], P = .049) and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons surveys (88.3 [quartiles: 88.3, 91.7] vs. 98.5 [quartiles: 95.0, 100.0], P = .028).ConclusionThe cortical button repair method for pectoralis major tendon ruptures may be associated with better long-term pain and functional outcomes as reported on the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand surveys compared to the transosseous tunnel repair method, but further investigation is needed.

The pectoralis major muscle is a prominent chest muscle crucial for upper body strength. Ruptures of the pectoralis major are rare but characterized by an acute traumatic injury resulting in pain, swelling, bruising, and often chest deformity. It is commonly injured during activities that involve intense muscle contractions, such as weightlifting and collision sports.9, 10, 11 It can result in weakness of the upper extremity, reduced shoulder movement, and asymmetry of the chest wall and axilla. Pectoralis major tears are primarily observed in men, particularly active men in their thirties to fifties. Although the incidence has been increasing since 1990, very few cases are reported in elderly women, and no tears have been documented in women under 40 years old.9,22

Surgery is generally recommended for full-thickness complete tears in young active individuals, as it offers improved function and less risk of deformities compared to conservative treatment. Conversely, nonsurgical treatment may be more suitable for patients with partial or muscle mass rupture, older adults, and individuals with low functional demands.10 Following the procedure, a period of rehabilitation and physical therapy is typically prescribed to help restore range of motion, rebuild strength, and optimize functional outcomes. It is necessary to balance restricting range of motion to promote proper soft-tissue healing while also allowing sufficient activity and movement to restore shoulder mobility.9

The traditional approach for repairing pectoralis major tendon ruptures involves the creation of a cortical trough to dock the tendon and transosseous bone tunnels to provide a secure anchor point for reattachment. However, this technique can create a significant cortical defect in the proximal humerus through multiple unicortical tunnels and the docking trough, thereby increasing the presence of stress risers.21,23,25 While this method has historically demonstrated positive clinical outcomes and favorable functional scores after surgery, the potential creation of a stress riser during the tendon docking and securing process remains a critical concern amongst many orthopedic surgeons, as it can lead to an increased risk of humeral fractures.8,18,21

Bone anchor or cortical button fixation has been adopted by many surgeons as a more modern method of repairing the pectoralis major tendon that involves reattaching the tendon to its original position and recreating its natural anatomic path. This has both clinical and cosmetic benefits as it restores the muscle's length–tension relationship, optimizing its function to closely resemble its preinjury state.10,17 The current biomechanical evidence on which fixation technique is stronger is conflicting.6,14,20

There have been relatively few studies assessing the clinical outcomes of the transosseous tunnel and cortical button repair techniques and even fewer comparing the 2. Previous studies involving cadaver models or systematic review of case series have suggested no differences in biomechanical or clinical outcomes between the transosseous tunnel and cortical button repair techniques.3,18,20 However, to our knowledge, there has been no direct comparative research between the 2 repair methods in the clinical setting. This study aims to retrospectively evaluate the post-operative patient-reported outcomes between transosseous tunnel and cortical button fixation in pectoralis major tendon repairs. We hypothesize that there will be no clinical differences between the 2 repair techniques.

Materials and methods

Subject selection

Following institutional review board approval, a retrospective review was performed of medical records and imaging for patients who underwent pectoralis major tendon repair between January 2011 and December 2021. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients at least 18 years of age with undergoing primary pectoralis major tendon repair identified using Common Procedural Terminology code 24341. This consecutive case series reflects the practice of a single fellowship-trained orthopedic shoulder and elbow surgeon. From 2011 to 2015, all procedures were performed using a trough transosseous tunnel repair technique. In 2015, the senior author adopted a technique using a commercially available pectoralis major repair system utilizing cortical buttons, which was used for all subsequent cases. Owing to the surgeons practice setting, the patient population included a mixture of civilian and military patients.

Surgical protocol

All operations were performed under general anesthesia with adjunctive interscalene blocks to aid with perioperative pain control. Patients were positioned semiupright in the beach chair position. The caudal portion of a standard deltopectoral incision was used for the approach. With the cephalic vein and deltoid muscle retracted laterally, the medially retracted stump of the pectoralis major tendon was identified and retrieved. The footprint of the avulsed pectoralis major insertion was identified just lateral to the extra-articular portion of the long head of the biceps' tendon located within the bicipital groove. Technique-specific preparation and fixation methods are detailed below.

Transosseous trough technique

Two #5 ultra–high tensile strength kevlar core sutures (FiberWire; Arthrex, Naples, Florida, USA) were placed in a locking fashion into the proximal stump of the pectoralis major tendon (Fig. 1). A cortical trough was created at the anatomic footprint of the avulsed pectoralis major insertion. In the index case, the trough was formed with a curette; in all subsequent cases, a 4-mm high-speed motorized burr was used (Fig. 2). Next, three 2.0-mm drill holes were placed in the humerus lateral to the trough. The free ends of the suture were then shuttled through the trough and out the drill holes (Fig. 3), and the tendon was tied down over an osseous bridge. Unicortical button technique

Three ultra–high tensile strength kevlar tapes (FiberTape; Arthrex, Naples, Florida, USA) were placed in a locking fashion into the proximal stump of the pectoralis major tendon. The cortical bone at the anatomic footprint of the avulsed pectoralis major insertion was prepared by clearing soft tissue and mechanical abrasion. Three 3.2-mm drill holes were placed into the anterior cortex of the proximal humerus, spanning the length of the pectoralis major footprint. The free tails of the suture tapes were then loaded into three 3.2 × 11-mm cortical buttons (Pec-Repair Kit; Arthrex, Naples, Florida, USA), which were sequentially placed through the drill holes into the intramedullary canal. The tendon stump was then reduced down onto the bone, and the suture tapes were tied to ensure secure fixation.

Post-operative protocol

All patients underwent a similar post-operative rehabilitation protocol. Patients were placed in a shoulder sling for a total of 6 weeks following the procedure and were encouraged to undergo elbow, wrist, and hand range of motion exercises for the first 2 weeks, pendulum shoulder exercises for weeks 2-4, and then passive shoulder flexion range of motion exercises from weeks 4-6. After discontinuation of the shoulder sling, patients were encouraged to begin active shoulder flexion and abduction range of motion exercises followed by gradually increasing isometric strengthening exercises at 3 months and return to full activity at 6 months.

Outcomes

Demographic and operative data, including patient age, gender, injury site, and Tietjen rupture classification based on imaging studies and intraoperative findings, were collected for assessment.18,24 Patient-reported outcomes were collected at a minimum follow-up of 2 years through contact by the research team using the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) survey, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) survey, and the Arrington questionnaire1 (a standardized set of survey questions regarding return to work, cosmetic, and changes in recreational and daily activities). Upper extremity adduction and internal rotation strength, along with active forward flexion, external rotation, and internal rotation range of motion data, were measured and collected. Complications consisting of readmission within 90 days of surgery, mortality within 365 days of surgery, and deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism within 365 days of surgery were also recorded based on chart review.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk tests. Given that a majority of variables were determined to be non-normally distributed, nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to make comparisons. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical data. All analyses were conducted in R Version 4.4.1.

Results

Between January 2011 and December 2021, 25 patients underwent primary surgical repair of a ruptured pectoralis major tendon. Nineteen patients met the inclusion criteria and remained for analysis. Ten of the patients received cortical button fixation, and 9 patients underwent primary trough and transosseous tunnel fixation for ruptured pectoralis major tendon. All surgeries were performed by a fellowship-trained shoulder and elbow surgeon.

Demographics and case characteristics

Patients across both groups were exclusively male. Overall, the median age was 32 years (quartiles: 27, 41), and the median time frame between initial injury and surgery was 14 days (quartiles: 10, 25). There were no significant differences in age, injury to surgery time, smoking status, or history of anabolic steroid use between the cortical button and transosseous tunnel fixation groups.

All 9 (100%) patients in the transosseous tunnel fixation group sustained their injury while performing the bench press compared to 5 (50%) patients in the cortical button fixation group. Of the remaining 5 (50%) cases, 2 (20%) occurred in water skiing accidents, 2 (20%) occurred while applying force to lock cutters, and 1 (10%) occurred while moving a heavy object.

All 10 (100%) patients in the cortical button fixation group had Tietjen type III D pectoralis major tears, characterized as complete tears at the tendinous insertion, compared to 7 (78%) patients in the transosseous tunnel fixation group. The remaining 2 (22%) patients had a complete tear at the musculotendinous junction (Tietjen type III C) and a partial tear (Tietjen type II). Patient-reported outcomes

At a median follow-up of 48 months (quartiles: 41, 50), it was revealed that the median DASH disability score in the transosseous tunnel fixation group (5.0 [quartiles: 3.3, 8.3]) was significantly worse compared to the cortical button fixation group (1.3 [quartiles: 0.0, 2.3]) (P = .049). The median ASES functional score was also significantly worse in the transosseous tunnel fixation group (88.3 [quartiles: 88.3, 91.7]) compared to the cortical button fixation group (98.5 [quartiles: 95.0, 100.0]) (P = .028). The majority of patients across both groups reported either no change or a decrease in their maximum bench press and push-ups compared to before injury, with no significant differences between the 2 groups. One (10%) patient from the cortical button fixation group reported an increase in their max bench press weight, while 1 (11%) patient from the transosseous tunnel fixation group reported an increase in their max number of push-ups. No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups for the time it took to return to work (P = .050), return to work at full function (P = .321), or return to lifting (P = .062). The majority of patients (>50%) in both groups were able to return to work within 3 months, return to work at full function within 6 months, and return to lifting within 9 months. Overall function and satisfaction.

Lastly, all patients with regular exercise routines across both groups reported decreasing their intensity while increasing repetitions. In addition, 10 (100%) patients from the cortical button fixation group reported concern with scarring compared to 7 (78%) patients from the transosseous tunnel fixation group. Furthermore, 3 (30%) patients from the cortical button fixation group had concerns with deformity compared to 5 (56%) patients from the transosseous tunnel fixation group. The differences were not significant (P = 1.000).

Strength and range of motion

At a median follow-up of 46 months (quartiles: 37, 74), 8 (80%) patients in the cortical button fixation group achieved 5/5 strength for upper extremity adduction and internal rotation. In addition, median active forward flexion and active external rotation were measured at 180° (quartiles: 180°, 180°) and 88° (quartiles: 76°, 90°), respectively. All patients in the cortical button fixation group were able to internally rotate to or above their thoracolumbar junction. Strength and range of motion outcomes were not recorded for the transosseous tunnel fixation group, so no comparisons were made.

Complications

No patients from either group required revision surgery, were readmitted within 90 days of surgery, died within 365 days of surgery, or had a deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism within 365 days of surgery.

Discussion

We hypothesized that there would be no differences in clinical outcomes between the transosseous tunnel and cortical button techniques for pectoralis major tendon repair. However, at a median follow-up of 48 months (quartiles: 41, 50), we found that transosseous tunnel fixation patients reported significantly worse outcome scores compared to cortical button fixation patients on both the DASH (P = .049) and ASES surveys (P = .028). Otherwise, on a qualitative scale, patients between the 2 groups had similar changes in activities and return to work time frames while reporting good to excellent overall function and high levels of satisfaction at long-term follow-up.

Pectoralis major ruptures are fairly uncommon injuries that disproportionately occur in men.9,11,14 Traditionally, a transosseous tunnel repair technique is used to surgically repair the tear with good functional results. However, there is a concern for increased risk of humeral fractures with this method.21,23 Many surgeons have adopted a technique that involves the use of cortical button fixation or suture anchor that theoretically provides better clinical and cosmetic outcomes.17 Previous studies have suggested that there are no differences in outcomes between the 2 repair methods. A systematic review of biomechanical studies published by Sequeira et al18 demonstrated that there was no difference in load to failure or stiffness between the transosseous tunnel and cortical button repair techniques. In addition, a systematic review of clinical case series and cohorts published by Bondedorfer et al3 reported no significant differences in clinical outcomes as measured by Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, ASES, DASH, and functional outcome scores between various methods of repair, including transosseous tunnels, cortical buttons, and suture anchors. On the other hand, a systematic review of case series and case reports published by Gupton et al7 found that cortical button fixation may have worse clinical outcomes compared to transosseous tunnels and suture anchors. However, the results were skewed by a single case series, with the majority of patients reporting poor outcomes related to cosmetic concerns. Furthermore, the majority of those patients had Tietjen type III C injuries where sutures are more commonly used for repair instead of cortical buttons, which may have affected outcomes.5

The results from our study are supported by a biomechanical analysis published by Edgar et al,6 which revealed that cortical button fixation with larger caliber suture and suture tapes results in better peak failure load and smaller displacement after cyclic loading compared to bone trough techniques, which may lead to better clinical outcomes. However, direct comparison studies of clinical and patient-reported outcomes between cortical button and transosseous tunnel fixation are lacking. To our knowledge, we report the first clinical comparative study assessing pain and functional outcomes between the 2 repair techniques. Further comparative studies are needed to support our findings suggesting long-term differences in pain and functional outcomes as reported on the ASES and DASH surveys for cortical button fixation compared to transosseous tunnel fixation in pectoralis major tendon repairs.

This study is limited due to its retrospective nature. Data that could have affected the interpretation of outcomes, such as the Charlson-Deyo Comorbidity Index, body mass index, and pre-operative pain and functional scores, were not included in the analysis. Furthermore, the trough and transosseous tunnel group included members from the military population, and the results may fail to accurately represent the outcomes experienced by the general population.1,2,4,12,13,15,16,19 Lastly, the sample size of the study was small, with a total of 19 patients between the 2 groups. Results are less generalizable and the statistical tests performed had low power and lacked multivariable regression analysis. Future studies should incorporate a multicenter, prospective study design with larger sample sizes to better control for covariates and critically assess long-term clinical outcomes between different methods of fixation for pectoralis major tendon repairs.

Conclusion

The cortical button repair method for pectoralis major tendon ruptures may be associated with better long-term pain and functional outcomes as reported on the ASES and DASH surveys compared to the transosseous tunnel repair method, but further investigation is needed.
