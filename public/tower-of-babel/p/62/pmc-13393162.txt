Exploring the readiness of Pakistani pharmacists to become independent prescribers: A qualitative study
Authors: Sana Ejaz Qureshi, Matti Ullah, Asif Ali, Sadia Shafiq, Uroosa Kanwal, Huma Naeem, Inaam ul Hakeem, Muhammad Ali, Mahsoona Bashir, Rabia Noor, Muhammad Abubakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn Pakistan, overburdened healthcare system limits access to timely treatment which remains a critical challenge. Pharmacist independent prescribers (PIPs) could bridge this gap, especially in rural and underserved communities where doctors are scarce. This role has the potential to empower patients, reduces strain on physicians, and optimizes public health outcomes. The aim of this study to explore the perceptions of pharmacists in Pakistan regarding independent prescribing.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted from September–November 2024. Purposive based sampling technique was used to conducted the interviews by a trained principal investigator in qualitative studies. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews with clinical/community pharmacists across various cities of Pakistan. All interviewed were analyzed by inductive thematic analysis technique and themes were generated.ResultsA total of 25 interviews were conducted with interview durations ranging from 10 to 25 min with a mean of 17.5 mins. Six major themes emerged from the study that were: i) Importance of a pharmacist as independent prescriber, ii) Pharmacist confidence to accept this responsibility, iii) Role of the health care system of Pakistan, iv) Barriers regarding implementation of the PIP role, v) Improvements required in the health care system and vi) Willingness to undergo further training.ConclusionsPakistani pharmacists demonstrate a clear willingness, confidence and readiness to assume the role of independent prescriber, backed by their medication expertise and patient-care commitment. There is clear demand for specialized, one-year training programs to ultimately improve their skills and professional confidence.

Introduction

An independent prescriber is a practitioner—who may be a doctor, nurse, or pharmacist—responsible for assessing patients with medical conditions and making decisions regarding their clinical management, including prescribing.1 A pharmacist is a healthcare professional who, through education, training, and experience, possesses expertise in the preparation, use, and distribution of medicines. Pharmacists provide patient care services to ensure optimal medication therapy outcomes, thereby improving both the clinical and economic outcomes of treating patients' diseases and conditions. The role of pharmacists has evolved significantly and now extends beyond traditional functions such as manufacturing, dispensing, and compounding to include areas like cosmetic sciences and independent prescribing.2 As qualified medication experts with comprehensive understanding and professional skills, pharmacists are well-positioned to improve healthcare access and reduce waiting times for general practice appointments, ultimately strengthening the healthcare system.3 Healthcare systems globally face mounting challenges driven by increasing life expectancies, rising rates of comorbid conditions, and an anticipated shortfall of 10 million healthcare workers by 2030.4

In response, the scope of pharmacy practice has expanded worldwide. Prescribing authority, traditionally held exclusively by physicians and dentists, has been extended to pharmacists as independent prescribers through recent legislative changes in various countries. For example, independent prescribing by pharmacists was introduced in Norway in 2001, the United Kingdom in 2006, Belgium in 2014, and France in 2016 within primary healthcare settings. New Zealand introduced pharmacist independent prescribers in 2013, and since 2019, pharmacists in Portugal have played a vital role in administering immunization programs.5 In Great Britain, registered pharmacists seeking to qualify as independent prescribers must complete a postgraduate qualification in Independent Prescribing delivered by a General Pharmaceutical Council-accredited Higher Education Institution.6 Currently, many pharmacists worldwide practice as independent prescribers, with responsibilities encompassing medication reviews, management of long-term conditions, online consultations, and patient assessments.7 Prescribing rights have been granted to pharmacists for specific medical conditions, enabling them to apply their medication expertise to enhance patient care. Their skills and knowledge contribute to improved healthcare systems through safer medicine use and increased access to treatment. An independent prescriber assumes responsibility for assessing patients with undiagnosed conditions and making prescribing decisions.8 The UK and Canada are the two most experienced countries in the practice of pharmacist prescribing.9 In the USA, 49 states currently allow the pharmacist to prescribe under the dependent prescribing model.10 Israel has implemented both dependent and independent prescribing since regulations were established in 2014 and 2016.11 The pharmacist in Canada is both dependent and independent.12

In Pakistan, doctors are reported to be extremely overburdened and unable to spend the appropriate duration of time dealing with patients, leading to compromised work quality.17 In Pakistan, doctors face many challenges on a daily basis, as they face heavy workload, workplace stress, lack of resources, lack of support, and boundary issues are also common problems faced by doctors.18 The importance of an independent prescriber emerges when there are a shortage of physicians and the demand for appointments is continuously increasing which leading the difficulty in consultation with physicians, and appointment delays are increasing. To understand this issue, several countries have started with different layouts of independent prescribing through community pharmacies.19 Understanding physicians' work experiences and their relationship to decent work concepts is crucial for developing and sustaining quality healthcare. However, a notable research gap exists in exploring these concepts, particularly within Pakistan's non-Western context.20 Given the growing global evidence supporting pharmacist independent prescribing but at the same time, they are not legally authorized for prescribing and direct patient involvement in Pakistan as per guidelines of regulatory authorities. To raise these issues; firstly, we known the reediness of pharmacist to accept and adopt the role of independent prescribing. So, this study aimed to qualitatively assess the readiness, perceptions, and willingness of Pakistani pharmacists to assume the role of independent prescribers.

Methods

Study design and study population

A descriptive qualitative study design was employed in which face to face and telephonic was conducted with community and clinical pharmacist. A semi-structured interview-guides was used to gain an in-depth understanding of pharmacists' perceptions regarding independent prescribing in Pakistan. Interview guide based on following questions such as i) Perceived importance of pharmacist independent prescribing, ii) Professional confidence and readiness, iii) Training and educational needs, iv) Barriers and facilitators, v) Healthcare system requirements and vi) Future implementation considerations with prompts attach as supplementary file-S1.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted across multiple cities in Pakistan. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure inclusion of pharmacists with relevant experience and diverse perspectives and agree to participate in the study. Eligible participants were registered pharmacists with a Doctor of Pharmacy degree and a minimum of two years of clinical or community pharmacy practice experience. Pharmacists were identified through professional networks and pharmacy practice settings across multiple cities. Eligible participants were approached directly via email, telephone, and professional contacts.

Data collection and analysis

Interviews were conducted by principal investigator who has been trained in qualitative studies and she is MPhil research scholar at Hamdard University Islamabad campus. All interviews were conducted in national language Urdu with open ended questions and participants were encourage to explain their thoughts freely. All interviews were recorded after getting consent from the participants. Interviews were conducted until the saturation point was achieved. Saturation was considered achieved when no new codes, concepts, or themes emerged from consecutive interviews. Saturation was observed after the 22nd interview and confirmed through three additional interviews. All the methods were used in the current study were in compliance with institutional guidelines and according to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research checklist21 for qualitative study (Supplementary file 2).

All recorded interviews were evaluated by using inductive thematic analysis technique.17, 22. To ensure data authenticity, audio recordings were repeatedly listened to and transcribed verbatim into English by forward backward translation method. The transcripts were read multiple times to achieve familiarization and deeper understanding of the data. Manual coding performed with meaningful information labelled to generate initial codes. Two researchers independently reviewed transcripts and generated preliminary codes. Coding discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. An audit trail was maintained throughout the analysis process. To enhance consistency, code frequencies were tabulated and quantified. Similar codes were subsequently grouped into subcategories, which were then organized into sub-themes and overarching themes. Throughout this process, transcripts were iteratively reviewed, and the data were continuously refined. Member checking was conducted during interviews by paraphrasing participants' responses back to them to confirm accurate interpretation. For analysis, each distinct statement was counted only once per participant; recurring statements were considered indicative of significant findings.

Theme 1: Importance of pharmacist as independent prescriber

Participants acknowledged the possible implications of pharmacist independent prescribing in Pakistan. Their answers underscored three sub-themes: pharmacists as a link between doctors and patients, decreasing the burden on physicians, and enhancing patient confidence. Due to their medication knowledge, pharmacists believed they could integrate physicians and patients.“Pharmacist acts as a bridge between physician and patient and scrutinizes the medication profile of the patient prescribed by the physician as per the pharmaceutical and pharmacotherapeutic expertise”(Participant #09)

“Pharmacist acts as a bridge between physician and patient and scrutinizes the medication profile of the patient prescribed by the physician as per the pharmaceutical and pharmacotherapeutic expertise”

In a healthcare system where doctors are severely constrained by their schedules, time wise, this bridging role becomes even more important. Most participants noted that pharmacists could address important issues in patient care by making sure that medications are safe and appropriate. Most participants talked about the possibility of ‘deflecting’ work away from the physicians, and this seemed to be the most important positive aspect of the role. As a result of independent prescribing, the work expectations placed on the medical profession would become more reasonable and even out the burden, and this was noted by 15 participants.“If we are able to implement this in the future or our regulatory bodies, Physician's workload, even nursing's workload, will be very low because nursing, who doesn't know what medicine is going to be different”(Participant#01)

“If we are able to implement this in the future or our regulatory bodies, Physician's workload, even nursing's workload, will be very low because nursing, who doesn't know what medicine is going to be different”

Another participant connected this benefit directly to patient outcomes, noting that physician shortages often force patients to seek care from unqualified providers.“It has the great importance especially in country like ours where there is a huge patient burden on physician, likewise, can't sometimes afford the doctor so they unfortunately go for the folks which they consider as a cheaper option and as a result they face the severe consequences”(Participant#10)

“It has the great importance especially in country like ours where there is a huge patient burden on physician, likewise, can't sometimes afford the doctor so they unfortunately go for the folks which they consider as a cheaper option and as a result they face the severe consequences”

Beyond reducing physician burden, participants believed that independent prescribing would transform the patient-pharmacist relationship. Twelve participants discussed how this expanded role would build trust and improve patient engagement with pharmacy services. A community pharmacist from Sargodha with three years of experience reflected.“At a patient level. I think their trust with a pharmacist will develop. Since pharmacists will be more engaged in disease management counselling and follow up. Patients will develop their good relations, but again if the role of pharmacist as patient counselor and risk manager is accepted and well versed to a doctor. Unfortunately, doctors feel insecure when pharmacist intervene their treatment plan”(Participant#11)

“At a patient level. I think their trust with a pharmacist will develop. Since pharmacists will be more engaged in disease management counselling and follow up. Patients will develop their good relations, but again if the role of pharmacist as patient counselor and risk manager is accepted and well versed to a doctor. Unfortunately, doctors feel insecure when pharmacist intervene their treatment plan”

However, this participant also acknowledged an important caveat—that physician acceptance of this expanded pharmacist role would be essential for building patient trust.“I think it would do wonders for patients if they can just walk into a Pharmacy near their home, discuss their problem and get quality healthcare and medicines from the same place rather than wasting long time in hospitals and paying expensive fees for doctors' consultations”(Participant#09)

“I think it would do wonders for patients if they can just walk into a Pharmacy near their home, discuss their problem and get quality healthcare and medicines from the same place rather than wasting long time in hospitals and paying expensive fees for doctors' consultations”

Theme 5: Improvements required in the healthcare system

Participants identified several systemic improvements that could facilitate the successful implementation of pharmacist independent prescribing. A key anticipated benefit was the potential to enhance rational medicine use. As one pharmacist noted:“Independent prescriber can minimize the risk of irrational use of medicines”(Participant#05)

“Independent prescriber can minimize the risk of irrational use of medicines”

Respondents also emphasized the need for greater public awareness to optimize this role. They suggested that educating patients and communities about pharmacists' capabilities would ultimately improve patient trust and confidence in pharmacist-delivered care. This was viewed as a timely and necessary step to strengthen the healthcare system. Furthermore, participants highlighted the potential for independent prescribing to alleviate pressure on secondary care services. Leveraging the easy accessibility of community pharmacies was seen as a strategy to reduce hospital burden. This sentiment was echoed by another participant:“It will reduce the burden from physician. Patients have easy access to pharmacies. Less burden on hospital”(Participant#04)

“It will reduce the burden from physician. Patients have easy access to pharmacies. Less burden on hospital”

In addition to finding people who can help participants kept saying that making this work would require changes to the healthcare system. Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that Pakistani pharmacists demonstrate considerable readiness and willingness to assume independent prescribing responsibilities, grounded in their medication expertise and commitment to patient care. While participants expressed confidence in their clinical abilities, they simultaneously highlighted systemic barriers, particularly physician resistance, regulatory limitations, and the necessity for specialized training that must be addressed before successful implementation can occur.

The prescribing competency levels of prescribers directly influence to their willingness to take the responsibility for prescribing medicines.23 Facing a growing and aging population with a shortage of doctors, health systems globally are under strain to increase access to primary care, leading governments to expand the role of pharmacists through new policies recognizing their ability to diagnose and treat minor ailments a shift further supported by the trust pharmacists built during the COVID-19 pandemic.24

Our findings align with this international trajectory, as Pakistani pharmacists recognized the potential of independent prescribing to bridge healthcare gaps, reduce physician burden, and improve patient access to medications. However, despite the global acceptance of pharmacist prescribing, the confidence of pharmacists in this role is not fully understood. The primary challenge remains their uncertainty in making independent clinical decisions; although they possess strong knowledge of drug therapy, complex cases can cause doubt. Concerns about legal liability and professional accountability also contribute to this uncertainty, often due to a lack of mentorship and inconsistent support from general practitioners. Additionally, their role within healthcare teams remains unclear, with many pharmacists feeling their prescribing authority is not fully recognized by other medical professionals and regulatory bodies.25

In our study, participants expressed confidence in their abilities while simultaneously acknowledging these professional risks, suggesting that Pakistani pharmacists are cognizant of both the opportunities and challenges accompanying scope expansion. For most pharmacists who remain in the profession, their role is evolving to meet national healthcare demands. This progression, however, introduces greater risks as it also increases personal liability, with prescribing becoming a central and fundamental duty of the job.26

Our findings support this assertion, as participants with more than five years of practice expressed heightened confidence, attributing this to accumulated clinical experience that complemented and reinforced their foundational education. This suggests that any training program developed in Pakistan must prioritize not only knowledge acquisition but also the quality of clinical exposure and mentorship provided. The world widely accepted role of pharmacist independent prescriber,while experience was seen as key to building confidence, the quality of that experience mattered more than how much experience a prescriber had.27

Our participants echoed these concerns, highlighting that Pakistan's overburdened healthcare system similarly struggles with physician shortages and patient access barriers, particularly in rural and underserved areas. In the recent study published in IJPP the independent prescribing service has allowed pharmacists to manage and treat patient conditions within the community pharmacy, offering potential benefits to the increasing demand for general practitioners. An increase in IP consultations is evident, with 16,005 consultations recorded across 33 out of the 713 community pharmacies in Wales since 2016.28 Similarly, a recent study about the community pharmacist role as an independent prescriber revealed that due to the shortage of professionals and the ever-increasing demand for appointments and consultation with general physicians, access is becoming increasingly difficult and appointment delays are growing.19

Our community pharmacists are ready to accept this new role in Pakistan if it is implemented but they are concerned about the acceptance of this role in the community as it is also observed in one of the studies that understanding the contribution that this role can offer in the healthcare process, and investigating pharmacist independent prescriber acceptance is of utmost importance for the countries that implement and regulate this type of system.12 Participants in our study believe that public awareness about pharmacist independent prescribers is currently inadequate; they emphasized that if implementation proceeds, creating public awareness must be the first task, as this will ultimately build trust in the patient-pharmacist relationship. They also expressed concern about Pakistan's pharmacy practice regulatory bodies and the need for their support to implement the pharmacist independent prescribing role. A recent study suggested that gaining an understanding of what influences independent prescribers' decision-making can help educators train prescribers, enabling independent prescribers to deliberately engage their knowledge, skills, and attitude in areas requiring development to improve diagnostic and prescribing processes.28

Pharmacist education and training are the other challenges before implementation of this role as this is a specialized role and it requires a proper training, to become pharmacist independent prescriber exclusive training program is needed after completing the 05 year degree program of doctor of pharmacy, the training program should make them expert in diagnosis, decision making and then independently prescribing, one of the latest study stated that in the UK, in November 2020, National Health Service (NHS) Scotland launched NHS Pharmacy First Plus (PFP) with the aim of supporting patients to access advice and treatment for common clinical conditions from pharmacist IPs in community pharmacy (CP).29

In the current study, many pharmacist participants emphasize the continuous medical education even after completing the training course of independent prescriber as CME improves the continuous changes and advancement in the medical field and ultimately improves the patient outcome and also the experience they will gain is another advantage in this role, the more your practice the more expert you become in the medical field, in this study we only include those pharmacist who are having more than 2 years clinical experience so they can better understand and evaluate the importance of pharmacist independent prescriber and their need to implement in Pakistan. Pakistan is an underdeveloped country where everybody don't have the proper health access specially if we talk about the rural areas of Pakistan there is a lack of basic health facilities, they have to travel from rural to urban areas to get them checked properly or for their any kind of disease treatment in a recent study it is observed that if a patient with diabetes or asthma is experiencing a problem in medication access, they may be forced to travel far distances, often in a compromised health condition, or visit urgent or emergency care services. With the introduction of HB191, all patients who have run out of medication or are in an emergency can access the 894 pharmacies in the state between visits, which may now provide preventative and fast-acting medications such as inhalers and insulin pen needles.30

Our pharmacist believe that together work with the physicians and nurses enables the better patient outcome and overall improves the quality of life. Inter professional collaboration works promote the better health care system of Pakistan. Pharmacist independent prescriber numbers is continuously increasing, In 2025/2026, independent prescribing will be integrated into the foundation year of pharmacy training worldwide.26

Several limitations are present in this research. First, qualitative nature of the study, relatively small and purposive sample size, only pharmacists' perspectives were considered, and the views of other healthcare professionals, such as physicians, patients, and policymakers, were not included. Second, most participants were from urban areas, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to rural settings. Thirdly, the people we talked to in the study their thoughts might not be the same as what all healthcare professionals or policymakers, in Pakistan think. Lastly, participation was voluntary, meaning only pharmacists with interest in the topic were likely to take part.

The results clearly show that participants were willing to take on the responsibility of independent prescribing if they received proper training to improve their skills, had regulatory support, and met the necessary competency standards. The study identified various factors that either support or hinder pharmacists' ability to expand their prescribing role. Based on these findings, establishing a one-year training program for pharmacists in Pakistan is essential, similar to programs in countries like the UK, Canada, and Norway.

The results also highlight the need for a regulatory framework, along with competency-based training, to support independent prescribing by pharmacists in Pakistan. Pilot programs involving pharmacists, doctors, regulators, and patients should be initiated to assess the program's feasibility, safety, and acceptance.
