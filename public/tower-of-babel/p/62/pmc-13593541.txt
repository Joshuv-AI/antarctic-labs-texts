Introduction

Radiofrequency (RF) catheter ablation is an established therapy for ventricular tachycardia (VT) but has limitations when critical components of the isthmus are intramural or 3-dimensionally involve areas that are very difficult to reach from the endocardium and epicardium.1 Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is an emerging nonthermal energy modality increasingly used for the management of atrial fibrillation with advantages of tissue selectivity and the potential to drive deeper lesions than conventional irrigated RF.2 In the era of percutaneous revascularization, some cases of post-infarct VT scar may contain isthmuses close to revascularized coronaries. Although PFA carries a risk of vasospasm and some intimal remodeling, when lesions are required close to or on top of coronaries, PFA may be preferable to RF.3,4 We present a case of refractory VT successfully treated with compassionate use of endocardial and epicardial PFA with delivery of PFA directly on a coronary artery.

Case report

A 66-year-old man with a history of ischemic cardiomyopathy (left ventricular ejection fraction: 35%–40%), coronary artery disease with prior inferior myocardial infarction and percutaneous coronary intervention of the right coronary artery (RCA), and prior VT storm with implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement presented with recurrent VT storm despite 2 prior endocardial ablations.

He first underwent endocardial VT ablation in 2020 for VT storm. Coronary angiogram at the time demonstrated a patent RCA stent and no new obstructive disease. Mapping identified an isthmus along the inferolateral left ventricle at the level of the mitral annulus with mid-diastolic potentials.

The patient later developed recurrent VT and underwent repeat endocardial ablation in 2024. Substrate mapping demonstrated significant fractionated electrograms at the base of the posteromedial papillary muscle (PMPM) extending toward the region of the left posterior fascicle, which was targeted. Of note, after each of the 2 prior ablations, he remained inducible for a broad and slurred VT with a long intrinsicoid deflection consistent with a non-clinical epicardial VT.

He later developed recurrent VT despite amiodarone and lidocaine drips. He was planned for compassionate use PFA for VT. Before general anesthesia induction, a radial arterial line was placed for hemodynamic monitoring. Epicardial access was obtained via a subxiphoid approach using a Tuohy needle under fluoroscopic and intracardiac echocardiographic guidance (ICE). No contrast was administered as epicardial access was obtained by tactile feedback, and wire course was verified fluoroscopically in the left anterior oblique view. ICE was used to verify the absence of right ventricular (RV) access by visualizing lack of wire in the RV and RV outflow tract, which is our standard approach. An epicardial Agilis sheath (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL) was then advanced over the wire into the pericardial space, and heparin was administered to achieve an activated clotting time of 250–300 seconds.

Epicardial substrate mapping using an HD Grid catheter (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL) revealed a large inferolateral scar with deceleration zones located opposite the PMPM (Figure 1A and B). Programmed ventricular stimulation induced the clinical VT. The clinical VT (VT1) had a cycle length of 380 ms with right bundle morphology, superior axis, V3 transition, and QRS duration of 190 ms (Figure 1C). The entrance to the isthmus for VT1 was localized to the deceleration zone in the epicardium based upon early diastolic potentials in VT (Figure 1C). Entrainment was not performed due to hemodynamic instability. These data were consistent with a 3-dimensional isthmus involving an intramural course at the region of the PMPM.

Epicardial bipolar map (A) and isochronal late activation map (B). A tracing of the clinical ventricular tachycardia (VT) with the HD Grid Mapping catheter in the epicardium identifying early diastolic potentials (C).

Coronary angiography demonstrated that the epicardial ablation site was located at the course of the right posterolateral branch of the RCA distal to a widely patent RCA stent (Figure 2A and B). Given this anatomic constraint, conventional RF ablation was deferred as RF ablation performed with some distance from the coronary likely would have been ineffective, and a decision was made to proceed with epicardial PFA. Similarly, intracoronary ethanol was considered but deferred given this likely would have been technically challenging to ablate through a small patent stent and would have yielded a less precise approach. After injecting 2 mg nitroglycerin into the right 8-Fr short common femoral vein sheath, hemodynamics were monitored meticulously, and following nitroglycerin administration, pressor requirements increased throughout the remainder of the procedure. After a 2-minute waiting period, PFA was delivered with 8 applications per location using the nominal waveform targeting the entrance of the VT isthmus using the Tactiflex Duo system (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL). A total of 48 PFA lesions were delivered in the epicardium over the course of 7 minutes, and no coronary vasospasm was observed. We typically would administer nitroglycerin every 5–7 minutes, but the patient clearly felt the hemodynamic effects of nitroglycerin for a prolonged period, and further nitroglycerin was deferred. Retrograde access was then obtained using a medium support Ventrax sheath (Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT). An anatomic approach was taken in the endocardium to deliver lesions opposite the epicardial ablation site circumferentially around the posterior medial papillary muscle (Figure 3B and C).

Angiographic orthogonal views revealing the location of the epicardial ablation catheter to be along the course of a right posterolateral branch of the right coronary artery.

A: 12-lead tracing of the second induced ventricular tachycardia. B, C: Intracardiac echocardiography of the ablation catheter applying pulsed field ablation in the endocardium on either side of the posteromedial papillary muscle close to a mid-myocardial stripe.

Repeat induction demonstrated a second VT (VT2) with a similar morphology to VT1 but with an earlier transition (V2), suggesting a closely related isthmus with a likely exit just apical to VT1 (Figure 3A). A mid-myocardial to epicardial substrate strip was identified apical and lateral to the PMPM just opposite the epicardial scar on ICE (Figure 3B and C). Further consolidation PFA applications were delivered in the endocardium at this region of 3-dimensional scar. A total of 89 PFA applications were delivered in the endocardium.

At the conclusion of the case, the patient was non-inducible with aggressive programmed ventricular stimulation with quadruple extrastimuli. A final coronary angiogram revealed an unchanged angiographic appearance of the RCA (Figure 4).

Discussion

VT substrates are often transmural, yielding circuits that are 3-dimensional, limiting the effectiveness of conventional ablation strategies, especially when a significant intramural component exists. In such cases, critical components of the circuit likely lie beyond the reach of standard RF lesions. RF ablation remains limited by its inability to consistently achieve deep lesions in high-impedance scarred ventricular myocardium, contributing to a risk of arrhythmia recurrence.5

Pulsed field ablation has emerged as a promising modality to address this limitation. Unlike thermal energy, PFA induces myocardial injury via irreversible electroporation and shows less attenuation by myocardial scarring and high impedance.6 Research efforts remain underway to create deep, transmural ventricular lesions, even with only endocardial PFA applications. Early clinical studies have demonstrated that this approach can produce wide transmural ventricular lesions and achieve high rates of VT noninducibility with significant reductions in overall arrhythmia burden.7

While an important goal remains achieving transmural lesions with endocardial PFA, our case extends this concept by demonstrating the feasibility of direct epicardial PFA in a patient with refractory VT and a clearly identified 3-dimensional circuit with an epicardial isthmus entrance. This case demonstrates the safety and feasibility of direct epicardial PFA at the location of a coronary artery in a highly refractory substrate with a solid tip dual energy focal catheter.

While coronary vasospasm remains a significant concern when delivering PFA close to coronary arteries, high doses of intravenous nitroglycerin have been successful in attenuating this issue.8 However, optical coherence tomography studies have revealed chronic lesion formation in some cases of unknown clinical implications.4 Although delivery of RF energy affords the ability to avoid high doses of nitroglycerin and minimize vasospasm, it can cause irreversible coronary damage when delivered too close to coronary arteries and thus was not an option in our patient.9 PFA afforded the unique opportunity to deliver a lesion at the location of the coronary artery in the epicardium. To our knowledge, this is the first report of such a lesion, although others have reported PFA delivery close to coronaries. It is important to note that while no spasm was noted in our procedure with intravenous nitroglycerin, this case used a different PFA system and reported vasospasm and required multiple administrations of intracoronary isosorbide dinitrate.10 Our example highlights an approach that may offer a complementary or alternative strategy in cases where endocardial ablation fails or when the arrhythmogenic substrate has a large epicardial component.

We acknowledge that PFA for VT remains investigational, with current data derived from small, first-in-human studies using specialized catheter systems. Although early results demonstrate favorable acute efficacy, long-term durability and safety profiles need further investigation.

Conclusion

Our case suggests that epicardial PFA may represent a novel and effective strategy for treating refractory VT involving epicardial and intramural substrates even at the location of epicardial coronary arteries with appropriate precautions. When conventional thermal approaches are limited by inadequate lesion depth and anatomic constraints, PFA has potential for treating the most refractory substrates.
