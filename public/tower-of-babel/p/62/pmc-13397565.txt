Structural Engineering of Cyanine Dyes to Access Shortwave Infrared-Emissive J‑Aggregates
Authors: Jillian A. Williams, Austin D. Bailey, Monica Pengshung, Diankai Liu, Arundhati P. Deshmukh, Cesar Garcia, John T. Cao, Tim Duong, Eric Y. Lin, Barry Y. Li, Nadine C. Bradbury, Alexandra Wright, Sohan D. Jadhav, Chengyu Kuang, Chern Chuang, Daniel Neuhauser, Justin R. Caram, Ellen M. Sletten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-cost, safe materials that absorb and emit light at near-infrared (NIR) and shortwave infrared (SWIR) wavelengths are of interest for optical communications, sensors, and diagnostics. One promising avenue to access the NIR/SWIR regions is to self-assemble chromophores into J-aggregates. However, the molecular design of two-dimensional (2D) or tubular J-aggregates with NIR and SWIR emissions is challenging, as small monomeric changes can drastically affect the resulting aggregates’ morphologies and photophysical properties. Here, by synthesizing four new heptamethine benzothiazole dyes with increasing steric bulk on the polymethine chain, we rationally design SWIR-emissive 2D J-aggregates. Adding steric bulk drastically changes the self-assembled mesophases and photophysical properties, leading to an unprecedented 3–4 distinct aggregates for each dye. We isolate a total of thirteen new NIR/SWIR aggregates, out of which nine exhibit room-temperature emission. Excitingly, one of the aggregates also shows a tubular morphologythe first SWIR tubular aggregate characterized. A series of interconversion studies, structural characterizations, and modeling provide insights into the molecular packing arrangements of each mesophase. This thorough understanding of the J-aggregate mesophases allowed us to perform four-color imaging using a single chromophore scaffold by selective excitation of the monomeric fluorophore and distinct aggregate structures. Overall, this work demonstrates a rational design strategy for accessing NIR/SWIR emission via supramolecular aggregation and highlights opportunities in energy transport and multiplexed imaging derived from this fundamental understanding of long-wavelength J-aggregates.

Introduction

Fluorescence in the near- and shortwave infrared regions of the electromagnetic spectrum (NIR, 700–1000 nm; SWIR, 1000–2000 nm) enables enhanced sensitivity and detection through complex media such as biological tissue or atmospheric fog. , Consequently, developing emissive materials for these spectral regions has become a significant area of research. − Polymethine fluorophores have emerged as favorable NIR and SWIR contrast agents due to their nontoxicity, large spectral tunability, and excellent absorption properties. − We, and others, have capitalized on the beneficial properties of polymethine fluorophores to perform multiplexed, noninvasive SWIR imaging. − Additional applications in photovoltaics, − optoelectronics, − and polaritonics − make these fluorophores valuable chemical tools.

Coupling the transition dipole moments of several chromophores together via molecular aggregation allows one to access unusual photophysical properties without covalent modifications. For example, J-aggregates are bathochromically shifted compared to the parent monomeric fluorophores and display increased peak absorptivity (ϵmax ) (due to peak narrowing) and often increased radiative rates (k r) (Figure a, (i)). − This is in contrast to H-aggregation that typically results in broad, hypsochromically shifted absorption and quenched emission. Tuning the behavior of an excitonic aggregate requires control over the sign and amount of transition dipole moment coupling between monomers in the extended solid, which, in turn, is set by the slip between monomeric units (Figure a, (ii)). Extended aggregates have many excitonic states (described as a density of states), but only one state with a significantly enhanced transition dipole moment, the “bright state.” For J-aggregates, the bright state is located at the lower band edge (no lower energy states), while that of H-aggregate is at a higher energy bright state than that of the monomer. In 2D, between H- and J-aggregates, we have also described an intermediate I-aggregate with red-shifted absorption but a bright state that sits in the middle of the band (and is thus nonemissive). Tuning between these band structures is a matter of changing the slip between monomeric units.

J-aggregates display favorable photophysical properties for photonic and bioimaging applications. The J-aggregates of ICG, an FDA-approved contrast agent, have been widely applied in fluorescence imaging, photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy, and photoacoustic imaging. However, systematic methods to promote emissive J-aggregation (and to prohibit H-aggregation and nonemissive J-aggregation) are limited. This is particularly the case for NIR- and SWIR-fluorophores. Recent research has shown how modifications to the heterocycles , or the central position of polymethine dyes affect the resulting aggregate photophysical properties, , but a set of design rules relating the monomer structure to aggregate photophysical properties is lacking.

Cyanine dyes, the most popular class of polymethine dyes, have been characterized in the visible region to form a wide range of geometries, including sheet-like aggregates, double- and single-walled nanotubes, bundles of tubes, and ribbons. ,,, 2D aggregates are favorable as they often display large redshifts (2,000–3,000 cm–1), increased exciton delocalization, larger changes in radiative rates, narrower absorption line widths, and longer-range energy transfer owing to the denser chromophore packing as compared to their 1D counterparts. ,, In addition, the ability to access tubular structures may result in enhanced photophysical properties due to the mixed 1D/2D character of these aggregate assemblies. , To date, for NIR and SWIR aggregating polymethine dyes, only weakly or nonemissive sheet- and ribbon-like structures (Figure a,iii) have been reported. This low emission can be attributed to short-range interactions that have H-aggregate character, although the system is overall red-shifted due to long-range dipole–dipole coupling (an effect we termed I-aggregation). ,,− Ultimately, a molecular understanding of how to access a range of emissive aggregate structures in the NIR and SWIR regions will greatly expand their applications toward diagnostics, sensors, light-harvesting materials, and beyond. Furthermore, structure–property relationships for the creation of self-assembled 2D materials are limited. , The sensitivity of excitonic coupling provides a facile avenue to probe small changes in morphology that are otherwise challenging to elucidate.

We hypothesized that introducing increasingly bulky groups off the middle of the polymethine chain, at the 4′ position, would allow the slip angle to be modulated in a systematic manner, leading to more red-shifted emissive aggregates. We report a series of dyes that vary in steric bulk at the 4′ position (Figure b, (i)): phenyl (1a), 3,5-dimethylphenyl (1b), 3,5-diethylphenyl (1c), and 3,5-di-tert-butylphenyl (1d). The introduction of steric bulk using aryl group substitutions was specifically chosen to limit changes in solubility or electronic states of the dye monomer. Upon screening each dye for aggregate formation, we were surprised to find multiple interconnected NIR/SWIR J-aggregate mesophases with distinct photophysical properties, despite deriving from highly similar fluorophores with nearly identical photophysical properties in ethanol (Figure b, (ii)). We fully characterize the aggregate mesophases of each dye via cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (TEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) and establish a model for their assembly and interconversion (Figure b, (iii)). Through this process, we have discovered the first tubular SWIR J-aggregate and characterized its linear dichroism (LD), elucidating its structure. Finally, having a thorough understanding of the aggregate mesophases, we characterize 70% of the aggregate mesophases to be emissive and demonstrate four-color SWIR imaging with the different aggregate phases of a single chromophore scaffold (Figure b, (iv)).

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Aggregate Screening of Isoelectronic Benzothiazole Heptamethine Dyes

Benzothiazole heptamethine dyes 1a–d (Figure a) were synthesized in two steps from standard polymethine dye building blocks and commercial boronic acids. First, Suzuki coupling of linker 2 containing a vinyl chloride with aryl boronic acids bearing the various steric substituents (3a–d) provided electrophilic linkers with varying steric bulk 4a–d. These linkers were then reacted with heterocycle 5 in the presence of a base to yield 1a–d in 9–54% yield.

Dye synthesis and photophysical characterization of solubilized dyes. a) Synthesis of sterically hindered cyanines 1a–d. b) Absorption spectra for 1a–d monomers in ethanol. c) Tabulated monomer dye photophysical properties in ethanol.

We characterized the photophysical properties of heptamethine dyes 1a–d in ethanol, where they were readily solubilized. We found that the unaggregated dyes all share nearly identical absorption (798 nm) and emission (∼813 nm) wavelengths (Figure b), as well as similar molar absorptivity (ε) and fluorescence quantum yield (Φ F ) (Figure c), demonstrating that the different alkyl chains are not affecting the electronics of the fluorophores.

Next, we surveyed the aggregation landscape of each dye. In stark contrast to the parent monomeric dyes, we observed distinct differences in the aggregation of 1a–d when introducing them to methanol:water mixtures, supporting our hypothesis that the 4′ substituents could modulate the resultant aggregate. What was unexpected was the degree of complexity observed in these initial aggregate screens, where multiple red-shifted species were observed for each fluorophore, unlike any previously reported SWIR aggregates. ,, To isolate each of the individual J-aggregates, we performed a systematic series of aggregation studies based on our previously developed method, where the dye concentration and solvent:cosolvent ratio are independently controlled. We first prepared 20% (v/v) methanol/water samples with dye concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mM (Figure S1a). Next, we prepared 0.1 mM dye solutions with methanol/water compositions that varied from 0–40% in 5 to 10% increments (Figure a, Figure S1b). We then performed time-course studies for each aggregate at selected methanol percentages (Figure b, Figure S1b-c). Lastly, we prepared 0% methanol/water samples at 0 °C to isolate the 1c and 1d J1-aggregates (Figure S1b).

J-aggregate mesophases from dyes 1a–1d. a) Absorption of 0.1 mM 1b in different MeOH/H2O composition solutions after 24 h. b) Kinetic absorption screening of 0.1 mM 1b in 5% MeOH/H2O 1b after different time points. c) Isolated aggregate absorption spectra for J1, J2, J3, and J4 of 1a–d.

Through aggregate screening and kinetic investigation, we observe that the dyes form a minimum of three unique aggregates, which can be isolated with a known combination of methanol/water ratio, time, and temperature. We enumerate the J-aggregates as J1, J2, J3, and J4 based on their self-assembly conditions and resulting (photo)­physical characterizations (vide infra). In short, J1 represents sheet aggregates formed at low (∼5%) methanol and short times, J2 is a special case only forming from 1b at 20% methanol that has a tubular morphology, J3 is stable at higher (∼30%) methanol, and J4 slowly forms from J1 aggregates (∼5% methanol) over 1–2 weeks. Figure c displays the normalized absorption of each isolated aggregate from dyes 1a–d which corresponds to the ideal solvation conditions for isolating that specific aggregate. The abundance of J-aggregates obtained from a single monomer is highly unusual among polymethine dyes, where one, sometimes two, unique J-aggregates are typically identified. In total, we have isolated 13 different aggregates from the four analogous dyes. Benzothiazole dye 1b is the first example of a single cyanine chromophore aggregating to form up to four distinct spectra that can be isolated at room temperature.

The photophysical properties of the 13 aggregates are presented in Tables , S1and Figure S2. We observe that the J1 analogues experience a redshift as the steric bulk of the monomer is increased from H (1a) to tert-butyl (1d), while the J3 analogues experience a blueshift as the steric bulk increases. The J1 and J3 aggregates for 1a and 1b display decreased full width half max (FWHM), as expected for J-aggregates (Table S1). Benzothiazole 1b also displays a unique, narrow J2 mesophase that is not observed for 1a, 1c, or 1d. Overall, the 1c and 1d aggregates are less stable than the 1a/1b aggregates, which we believe contributes to the larger FWHM observed for 1c and 1d. Finally, the J4 aggregates are generally broader, but a clear redshift exists for the most sterically bulky di-tert-butyl analogue. These absorbance trends and changes to FWHM are directly related to the distinct monomer packing that differentiates the aggregates. Notably, the 1a J4 and the 1b J2 and J4 aggregates each exhibited two distinct spectral peaks, while the remaining dyes only produced one. The appearance of a second peak indicates an out-of-plane bright state. About 70% of the aggregate mesophases were emissive, with lifetimes shorter than the monomer (Figure S3). Taken together, the redshifted λmax,abs, narrowed FWHM, shortened lifetime, and elimination of the 0–1 vibronic shoulder support that these mesophases are J-aggregates.

Characterization of J-Aggregate Mesophases

With the unprecedented finding that the 4′ functionalized benzothiazole heptamethine dyes form 3–4 different J-aggregates, we aimed to understand the supramolecular differences in the structures using a combination of cryogenic electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). In Figure a,b, we focus on dye 1b which readily forms four aggregates (J1–J4) that can be easily isolated at room temperature. Cryo-EM was employed to view the native morphologies of each aggregate mesophase of 1b. The J1, J3, and J4 aggregates were all found to have a sheet-like 2D morphology, while the J2 aggregate of 1b consists of micron-scale nanotubes measuring ∼5 nm in diameter (Figure a). Imaging performed on the aggregates of the remaining dyes (1a, 1c, 1d) also shows 2D sheet structures (Figure S4). The J1 aggregate of the 1c and 1d dyes and the J3 aggregate of the 1c dyes were unable to be isolated for imaging, but based on the single-peak lineshapes observed in the absorption spectra, it is most likely that these dyes also produce 2D sheet-like structures. These results indicate that 1b is privileged in its formation of the tubular J2 aggregate.

Although the J1, J3, and J4 aggregates share a similar sheet-like morphology, the packing geometries of these aggregates must be distinctly different to produce absorption energies that vary by up to 100 nm (from ∼900 to 1000 nm; 1111 cm–1). This indicates that a single cyanine dye can self-aggregate to form three distinct packing geometrieswith distinct photophysical propertiesthat can be controlled via solvent to cosolvent ratio. The similar morphologies associated with these aggregates makes elucidating the differences in the packing geometries a challenge. Traditional techniques for solving mesoscale structures, such as X-ray scattering, are particularly challenging here due to low periodic order and the lack of long-range symmetry elements preventing crystallization. Instead, we employ several experimental techniques (AFM, DLS, and kinetic experiments) to gain additional information on the morphological phases.

To elucidate the differences between the sheet mesophases, we measured the aggregate heights via AFM. The AFM images of the four 1b mesophases are shown in Figure b, along with an example showing how the heights were fitted (Figure c). For each aggregate, we measure the average heights across five spots by fitting the line scans to a step function (Figures c and S5–S6). The obtained height distributions are presented in Figure d and Table S2. The heights of the sheet-like aggregates range from 1.3 to 2.1 nm, where J1 < J3 < J4 holds true for 1a and 1b. In the case of 1c, the aggregate heights followed the trend J4 < J3. These heights are consistent with a single monomer height for each dye, and we hypothesize that the changes in the aggregate dye heights are caused by out-of-plane or alternating orientations of the monomers within the aggregate structure. We will elaborate on how this preliminary structural assignment is consistent with observed spectroscopic changes between different chromophores in the interconversion of aggregate mesophase section below.

In addition, we employed dynamic light scattering (DLS) to obtain a relative estimate of the aggregate size in solution (Figures e and S7). DLS analysis revealed general patterns, namely, that the J1 aggregates appeared smaller than their J3 and J4 counterparts across dyes 1a and 1b. The aggregates of 1c, which were too small to observe by cryo-EM, also appeared to be smaller than the others by DLS. We note that DLS sizes refer to the hydrodynamic radii of the freely moving aggregates in solution, which provides an inaccurate description of the true sizes for pseudo-1D and 2D morphologies. However, DLS does provide a statistical comparison of the extent of aggregation in bulk samples in solution at room temperature.

Tubular Aggregate J2

The formation of tubular J2 aggregate from 1b represents the first SWIR tubular J-aggregate. We quantified the diameter of the tubular aggregates through both AFM and cryo-EM to be ∼5 nm (Figure S8). The small diameter, two peaks observed in the cross-sectional cryo-EM analysis (Figure S8b), and the single pair of parallel and perpendicular peaks in the absorption spectra lead us to conclude that this is a single-walled tube.

We further analyzed the parallel and perpendicular contributions to the excitonic spectra to determine the chiral angle of the monomeric units relative to the tube axis (Supplementary Note 2). Here, μ 0 is the transition dipole amplitude of the monomer, r is the tube radius, A 0 is the area of each molecular site projected onto the tube surface, and θ is the angle between the transition dipole moment and the cylindrical axis. Linear dichroism (LD) spectra are shown in Figure S9. With this information, we estimate the chiral angle θ = 43.5° for these tubular structures. Although further experimentation is needed to fully solve the structure of the aggregate, the chiral angle provides a useful starting point for modeling J-aggregate photophysical properties and antenna-based energy transport. As tubes combine features of 2D aggregates (dense chromophore packing, robustness to disorder) with directional transport, a tubular SWIR aggregate opens up opportunities for SWIR sensitization.

Interconversion of Aggregate Mesophases

Having characterized each of the individual mesophases and correlated them across dye structures, we next looked to understand their interconversion. To accomplish this, we performed a thorough investigation of the kinetics of the aggregates derived from 1b to rationalize the conversion among J1, J2, J3, and J4 morphologies. We rapidly collected absorbance spectra (every ∼30 s) immediately upon mixing the dye stock solution with water for a period of at least 10 min. We then continued collecting spectra at longer time intervals (every 10 min to every 2 h) to carefully observe the self-assembly process unfold. The absorption data from initial and extended time points is presented in Figure a.

Interconversion of J-aggregate mesophases and structural model. a) Absorption profiles of 1b aggregates under three different preparation conditions (% MeOH) after varying aggregation times. b) Potential energy diagram showing the interconversions between mesophases at low (red) and high (black) methanol content. c) Cartoon depicting self-assembly for aggregates of 1b.

Figure a shows that the 1b J1 (0–15% methanol) initializes with a small amount of H-aggregate (∼600 nm), which becomes much less prominent after 1 h. Depending on the percent methanol content of the solution, the J1 will interconvert to J4 over time, with higher percentages converting faster. At 5% methanol, the full J1 to J4 conversion of 1b occurs over approximately 14 days at room temperature (Figure a, (i)). The 1b J2 aggregate (20% methanol) also initializes with a small amount of H-aggregate, suggesting that the J1 and J2 aggregates are formed directly from an H-aggregate at very early time points (before 1 s), before stabilizing in the tubular structure with the characteristic two J-bands (Figure a, (ii)). Interestingly, the 1b J3 (30% methanol) aggregate initializes in the J2 aggregate and quickly converts to the fully J3 species over roughly 1 h, which is then stable for 2 weeks (Figure a, (iii)). We are unable to access J4 of 1b through any initial rates experiment, as the fastest J4 was observed at room temperature was after 7 days in 10% methanol. However, this conversion can be accelerated with heat. We never observed J4 interconvert to the other mesophases at low methanol concentrations.

Beyond initialization experiments, we conducted methanol and water dilutions to see how the aggregates of 1b respond to changing methanol/water ratios (Figure S10). Interestingly, a high degree of conversion between the J1–J3 aggregates can be obtained. By diluting the J1 with methanol up to 20% or 30%, we observe conversion to the J2 and J3 aggregates, respectively. We also observe conversion of J1 to J2 by the introduction of J2 seeds, even if the percent methanol is not optimized for J2 formation (Figure S11). By diluting the J2 aggregate up to 30% methanol, we observed conversion from J2 to J3. Additionally, dilution of the J3 aggregate (30% methanol) with water down to 20% and 10% methanol allows for interconversion back to the J2 and J1 aggregates, respectively. Rapid interconversion between J2 (tube) and J3 (sheets) suggests that they share a similar unit cell structure, but the mesophase formation depends on the relative solubility difference between 20% and 30% water/methanol mixtures. Thus, the resulting aggregate can be easily tuned by the solvent conditions. The J4 aggregate converts if the methanol concentration is increased to 40%, indicating that at higher methanol levels, J3 is the most thermodynamically favorable mesophase.

Overall, trends between different mesophases and dye structures allow us to make tentative assignments of underlying aggregate assembly. Specifically, we focus on the trends in the self-assembly and stability of these aggregates as we change the steric bulk. We observed that the J1 aggregates became increasingly unstable as steric bulk increased. For example, the J1 aggregates of 1a and 1b are readily isolated at room temperature and convert over weeks (1a) to days (1b) to the J4 species, depending on methanol composition (Figure S1b-c); however, J1 aggregates of 1c and 1d could only be isolated at lower temperatures (∼0–5 °C), meaning they convert immediately to a different species (J4) upon preparation at room temperature (Figure S1b). Additionally, the J1 formed from 1a and 1b does not convert, even in small amounts, to J4 at low temperatures (4–8 °C) over several weeks (Figure S1c). These data imply that the J1 species is a kinetic trap which, if given sufficient activation energy, interconverts to the more stable aggregate morphology J4. Further, the activation energy is lower for dyes with bulkier substituents.

The kinetic data on the interconversion of the mesophases suggest direct relationships between J1 and J4, as well as J2 and J3. First, considering the relationship between J1 and J4, in all cases, J1 converts to J4 over time, indicating that J4 is the thermodynamic product. Both the steric bulk of the monomer dye and the methanol composition seem to affect the energy barrier between J1 and J4. Because dyes 1c and 1d do not form J1 aggregates at room temperature, the barrier to interconversion must be lowered by steric hindrance. Additionally, because the J1 to J4 interconversion occurs more quickly at high methanol percentages, the barrier must also be lowered as the solvent-to-cosolvent ratio increases (Figure b). Next, we examined the interconversion of J2 to J3, which can only be analyzed for dye 1b. For J2 and J3 of 1b, the interconversion is sensitive to the amount of methanol present. With a high percentage of methanol (30%), J2 rapidly converts to J3, suggesting that J3 is the more thermodynamically stable aggregate. Lowering the methanol to 20% prevents the interconversion of J2 to J3, likely due to an increased barrier of interconversion (Figure b), although we cannot rule out changes to the relative energies of the J2 and J3 aggregates as a function of the methanol percentage.

We further investigated the J1 and J2 mesophases of 1b in concentration-dependent experiments with consistent methanol percentages. We measured the absorbance spectra of 1b J1 from 0.1 μM to 0.2 mM in 5% methanol and 1b J2 from 2 μM to 0.8 mM in 20% methanol (Figure S12). The maximum concentrations tested were determined based on the solubility of 1b in methanol. For the sheet mesophase J1, we observe the conversion to the J1 aggregate at concentrations above ∼5 μM, consistent with a quasi-phase transition associated with 2D aggregates. , For the tubular aggregate, more complex behavior was observed that predominantly shows J2 with a more gradual emergence of an H-aggregate feature. These data suggest an isodesmic or cooperative assembly model for the tubular aggregate, although further studies are warranted.

Structural Model of Aggregates

We combine all our data to produce a model for each mesophase. To summarize, the cryo-EM showed us that J1, J3, and J4 share 2D sheet-like morphologies, and that J2 is a single-walled nanotube. Because there are only a small number of ways to pattern the dye monomer such that a single molecule-thick 2D sheet organizes from its constituent unit, we began by considering the simplest possible cases of supramolecular organization where each dye has the same orientation, and monomers are slipped from their nearest neighbors. We attribute this structure to the J1 species, which becomes increasingly unstable with steric bulk (Figure c). We hypothesize that the pendant groups begin to approach each other and therefore push the dyes further apart, leading to a redshift. This simple organization allows the dyes to rapidly assemble, though it also explains why J1 is kinetically disfavored in dyes 1c and 1dthe energy penalty of steric clash from their pendant groups becomes large enough that other morphologies (J3 and J4) form immediately, as opposed to the slow conversion over time observed for 1a and 1b. In all cases, J1 is a kinetic trap that, while relatively unstable, can organize quickly or slowly to find a lower-energy conformation over time. Additionally, the AFM reveals that J1 aggregates show the smallest monolayer thickness, which agrees with the proposed structure. DLS also consistently reports a smaller size for J1, suggesting the structure is unstable or experiencing increased size-dependent strain, limiting aggregation. Finally, J1 displays increasing redshift with increased steric bulk compared to J3 and J4 morphologies, suggesting increased slip among monomers results in stronger excitonic coupling.

We assign the J4 structure to a frustrated, slightly larger, and more disordered morphology. J4 and J1 are connected mesophases, showing interconversion over time without methanol. However, AFM shows that J4 is a thicker monolayer (2.08 vs 1.34 nm), meaning that some change must occur which widens the monolayer’s thickness. For 1a and 1b, we see that the J4 aggregate has a secondary, blueshifted peak that photobleaches concurrently with the main peak (Figure S13), and we attribute this side feature to an out-of-plane transition within the 2D sheet (Figure c). The secondary transition suggests a slightly angled orientation of dye monomers within the extended sheet. We note, however, that another potential explanation for the appearance of a second peak in 1a and 1b J4 may be donor–acceptor coupling, as is seen in squaraine J-aggregates. , As cyanine dyes contain excited states that follow the donor–acceptor–donor motif described in squaraine dyes, such coupling is possible, though, to our knowledge, it has not been reported for cyanine aggregates. ,

In considering J2 and J3, we hypothesize that they feature dyes in a configuration such that nearest neighbors are rotated 180° (i.e., antiparallel) from one another. The formation of single-walled nanotubes in the J2 of 1b indicates that there are hydrophilic groups (i.e., sulfonates) present on the inner and outer walls of the tube, which would intuitively require the antiparallel packing. As J3 appears to be a connected mesophase to J2, we expect it to have a similar arrangement. This structural characterization is bolstered by the observation that the J3 aggregates blueshift with increasing steric bulk, meaning that as the pendant group grows larger, the dyes become less slipped from one another, thereby decreasing the excitonic coupling (blueshifting) (Figure c). Assuming this alternating packing arrangement, bulkier central groups would be more sterically hindered by the sulfonate groups of the neighboring dyes. Conversely, smaller central substituents would be less restricting, allowing for a larger slip between monomers. Additionally, we know that the J3 dyes are thicker than J1 based on AFM (1.62 vs 1.34 nm), and lateral sizing from DLS (600 vs 200 nm) characterizations shows slightly larger monolayers, further suggesting this antiparallel arrangement.

Computational Modeling of J1 Mesophase

After understanding the distinct J-aggregate structures obtained from dyes 1a–1d, we can return to our initial hypothesis that varying the steric bulk at the 4′ position can control the slip angle of J-aggregates. The J1 mesophase displays a redshift as the steric bulk is increased. Having established that the J1 orientation is a parallel, slip-stacked sheet, we employed a computational approach that allowed us to estimate the relative slip for a given 4′ substitution. We used the density functional theory (DFT) method to optimize the ground-state geometries of each dye (1a–d) in a dimer arrangement, then calculated the energies of those dimers as they are slipped across each other from a distance of 1 to 7 Å. We observed a trend that, as steric bulk increased (i.e., from 1a to 1d), the slip that produces the lowest energy for the dimers increased, as visualized in Figures a and S14. Because previous reports have shown that the dye-to-dye slip increases with the number of monomer units, we added 2 Å of slip to each estimate to compensate for the dimeric limit. As described in Supplementary Note 3, we used both a simple dipole–dipole coupling model and a more sophisticated transition monopole model to compute the expected redshift for J1 aggregates as a function of slip and compared it to our estimates for 1a–d, shown in Figure b. In general, we observed that both models show a few 100 cm–1 shift for the estimated slip parameters. This correlation between the slip and the J-coupling gives further evidence that the chromophore packing parameters and the resulting photophysical properties are “designable”. Our estimated slip of 4.6 Å for the least sterically hindered 1a J1 aggregate and an estimated slip of 5.2 Å for the most sterically hindered 1d J1 aggregate is consistent with the spectroscopic trend. As evidenced by the inability to form the 1c and 1d J1 aggregates at room temperature, the stability of the J1 aggregate decreases as the steric bulk increases (Figure c), suggesting that a larger slip between monomers creates unfavorable strain within the mesostructure. These findings support our hypothesis that increasing the size of the central substituent would induce a larger slip and thus a redshift in aggregates with a parallel alignment of monomers. The complex network of aggregates uncovered in this work has demonstrated that unpredicted packing arrangements and the stability of the aggregates will challenge initial hypothesis generation and aggregate prediction.

SWIR Emission and Multiplexed Fluorescence Imaging

The majority of SWIR polymethine J-aggregates are nonemissive or very weakly emissive. , By controlling the slip angle of the NIR/SWIR J-aggregates and accessing the lowest energy portion of the density of excitonic states, we sought emissive NIR/SWIR J-aggregates. We thus collected emission spectra of each aggregate mesophase and found that nine of the thirteen aggregates were fluorescent (Figures a and S18). For each emissive aggregate, narrow emission with negligible Stokes shiftsclassic characteristics of J-aggregateswas observed. In every case, the J1 mesophase displayed emission with 1b J1 having the highest quantum yield (ΦF = 0.12%) of the four J1 mesophases (Figure b, Table ). Overall, 1b aggregates displayed the highest fluorescence with the J2 having a ΦF = 0.05% and the J3 displaying the highest value obtained in this study of ΦF = 0.15%. The J4 aggregate for 1b as well as 1a and 1c were all nonemissive, likely due to the increased disorder allowing for more internal conversion pathways. The only J4 aggregate that displayed emission was from 1d, which also has the smallest FWHM of all the J4 aggregates, suggesting it is more ordered.

The multicolor imaging in vitro demonstrated the potential for high-contrast SWIR imaging in vivo. Overall, the ability to achieve excitation at four distinct wavelengths using a single monomer demonstrates how an understanding of the aggregation properties of these fluorophores can be harnessed for future in vivo imaging applications.

Conclusion

Working from the simple hypothesis that increasing the steric bulk at the 4′ position of benzothiazole heptamethine dyes would increase the redshift of the resulting J-aggregates, we designed and synthesized four new heptamethine dyes. We quickly realized that these dyes displayed rich, unprecedented aggregation behavior, leading to a total of 13 NIR and SWIR J-aggregates with unique spectral signatures and emission. Combining our findings from structural characterization with interconversion studies of the J-aggregate mesophases resulted in a structural model for each aggregate mesophase. For the most common parallel orientation of dyes in a 2D sheet, we confirmed our hypothesis that increased steric bulk increases the slip angle, leading to the most redshifted benzothiazole J-aggregate to date. This allowed for the formation of SWIR-emissive 2D aggregates, as increasing the slip also pushes the bright state closer to the band edge. We assigned this parallel sheet orientation to the J1 mesophase, which is the least stable of the mesophases, as it converts into J4, a more disordered, primarily nonemissive, blueshifted sheet with some out-of-plane components over time. The antiparallel sheet J3 shows generally good stability, the exception being dye 1c which contains ethyl groups which have more available conformers that we believe are detrimental to aggregate assembly. Most excitingly, we found that the J2 structure was a single-walled tube and represents the first SWIR tubular aggregate, a system that may enable directional energy transport and polarized absorption/emission at SWIR wavelengths.

We show that minute modifications to a dye's structure can lead to extreme changes in the resulting J-aggregate’s morphology and photophysical properties. The addition of substituents to the 4′ position of a cyanine dye is an effective way to induce systematic changes in the chromophore’s scaffold without significantly altering solubility or electronic structure. From dyes with identical absorbance at 798 nm, we can isolate J-aggregates ranging from 900 to 1034 nm, which is a broad enough spectral range to enable multiplexed imaging experiments. The high percentage of emissive J-aggregates we obtained is also notable, given the challenges in obtaining SWIR-emissive materials.

Beyond our desire for control over photophysical properties, we discovered a rich morphological phase space arising from subtle changes in solubility and cosolvent concentrations. Predicting 2D material growth and assembly is a significant challenge in the field due to the subtleties of intramolecular interactions and the difficulty in characterizing these interactions within the larger material. , Here, we show that strong chromophore coupling can lead to clear changes in photophysical properties that provide insight into the 2D structure. Thus, beyond acting as a useful parameter for fluorophore design, excitonic photophysics can be a tool to study the supramolecular structure and kinetics. Future efforts to append J-aggregating chromophores to peptides or polymers to provide a readout on their assembly are an exciting extension of this work. In addition, we expect the results herein to further the utility of J-aggregates in SWIR imaging, , excitonic energy transfer, polaritonics, and telecommunications.
