Mechanical Power Indexed to Estimated Functional Residual Capacity as a Predictor of Mortality in Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: Post Hoc Analysis
Authors: Enrique Monares-Zepeda, Christopher Barrera-Hoffmann, Jesús S Sanchez‑Diaz, Karla G Peniche‑Moguel, Diego Escarraman‑Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsMechanical power (MP) reflects the energy delivered by the ventilator and has been associated with mortality in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). We evaluated whether MP indexed to estimated functional residual capacity (MP/FRC) was associated with intensive care unit (ICU) mortality in ARDS.Patients and methodsThis post hoc analysis included 158 adult patients with ARDS who required invasive mechanical ventilation between 2016 and 2022. Functional residual capacity was physiologically estimated from standard ventilatory variables. Mechanical power, MP/FRC, dynamic strain, and global strain were calculated. The primary outcome was ICU mortality. Discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and survival was assessed using Cox regression.ResultsEighty-one patients (51%) died during ICU stay. Mechanical power/FRC demonstrated the highest observed discrimination for ICU mortality among the evaluated respiratory indices [area under the curve (AUC) 0.71; 95% CI 0.62–0.81]. A cutoff value of 33.94 J/min/L was associated with significantly lower ICU survival (log-rank p < 0.001). Mechanical power/FRC remained independently associated with ICU mortality in multivariable analysis [hazard ratio (HR) 1.06 per unit increase; p = 0.005].ConclusionMechanical power/FRC was independently associated with ICU mortality and showed the highest observed discriminative performance among the evaluated respiratory indices. Because FRC was estimated rather than directly measured, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation.

Highlights

Mechanical power (MP) indexed to estimated functional residual capacity (MP/FRC) was independently associated with intensive care unit (ICU) mortality in ARDS. Estimated MP/FRC demonstrated the highest observed discriminative performance among the evaluated respiratory indices. Because functional residual capacity was indirectly estimated from respiratory mechanics, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation.

Introduction

Mechanical power integrates tidal volume, airway pressures, flow, and respiratory rate into a single metric representing the energy delivered by the ventilator to the respiratory system and a key determinant of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI).1–3

The concept of MP arises from the understanding that lung injury is not caused by any single ventilatory parameter alone, but rather by the combination of forces—such as pressure, volume, and frequency—acting together over time. Excessive MP has been associated with increased lung stress and strain, potentially exacerbating alveolar overdistension, cyclic opening and closing, and the development of VILI.

Previous studies have shown that higher MP is associated with increased mortality in critically ill and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients.4,5 In a large analysis of two observational intensive care unit (ICU) cohorts, Serpa Neto et al. reported that the risk of in-hospital mortality increased progressively when MP exceeded approximately 17 J/min.4 However, the same level of MP may be relatively well tolerated in a lung with a functional residual capacity (FRC) of 3 L but potentially injurious in a lung with an FRC of 1.2 L. This concept is consistent with the “baby lung” model of ARDS, in which the functional aerated lung available for ventilation is markedly reduced.6 Consequently, MP/FRC has been proposed as a strategy to normalize delivered ventilatory energy to a surrogate of functional lung volume and potentially provide a more physiologically informed assessment of ventilatory load.7,8

Because direct measurement of FRC is difficult to perform at the bedside in mechanically ventilated patients, several surrogate approaches have been proposed to approximate lung size. Indexing MP to predicted body weight reflects the expected FRC of a healthy lung but does not account for the reduction in aerated lung volume characteristic of ARDS. Other approaches relate ventilatory energy to respiratory system compliance, which maintains a modest relationship with effective lung volume. In the present study, FRC was physiologically estimated from standard ventilatory variables, allowing MP to be indexed to a physiologically estimated surrogate of functional lung size.5

We hypothesized that MP/FRC would outperform dynamic strain and global strain in predicting ICU mortality in patients with ARDS.

Patients and Methods

This study was a post hoc subanalysis of a previously published retrospective observational cohort of mechanically ventilated patients with ARDS.9 The study was conducted in a tertiary referral ICU in Mexico and included adult patients admitted between 2016 and 2022.

The institutional research ethics committee approved the study (Approval ID: R-2023-3001-019) and waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective design. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years and fulfillment of the Berlin definition of ARDS. Exclusion criteria included patients managed with noninvasive ventilation, death within 48 hours of ICU admission, or incomplete ventilatory data required for MP calculations.

The final cohort consisted of 158 patients.

Ventilatory Management and Data Collection

Ventilatory management followed a predefined institutional protocol based on contemporary ARDS recommendations. All patients were placed in the prone position within the first hour after initiation of invasive mechanical ventilation and remained prone for 24–48 hours. Continuous neuromuscular blockade with vecuronium was administered during the first 48 hours.

Demographic and clinical data collected included age, sex, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), arterial lactate levels, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, arterial pH, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation.

Ventilatory variables included tidal volume (mL/kg predicted body weight), respiratory rate, plateau pressure, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and minute ventilation.

All ventilatory variables were recorded once, within 30 minutes after achieving protocolized ARDS ventilatory settings.

Derived Respiratory Indices

In addition to MP/FRC, other ventilatory indices were calculated from the recorded ventilatory variables, including driving pressure, dynamic strain, and global strain.

These indices were treated as exposure variables and compared with MP/FRC to evaluate their association with ICU mortality.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was ICU mortality.

Secondary outcomes included survival time and the discriminative performance of MP/FRC compared with non-indexed MP and strain-based indices.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

The discriminative performance of MP/FRC for ICU mortality was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC).

Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank testing. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the independent association between MP/FRC and ICU mortality after adjustment for age, sex, lactate levels, and SAPS II score.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 4.03).

Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed that MP/FRC had the highest observed AUC for ICU mortality, reaching 0.71 (95% CI 0.62–0.81). A cutoff value of 33.94 J/min/L yielded a sensitivity of 72% and specificity of 69%. In comparison, final dynamic strain and final global strain demonstrated lower discriminative ability, with AUC values of 0.635 (95% CI 0.532–0.737) and 0.639 (95% CI 0.537–0.741), respectively. Both indices showed higher sensitivity (91%) but markedly lower specificity (34%). The corresponding ROC curves are shown in Supplementary Figures S1–S3.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly reduced ICU survival in patients with MP/FRC above 33.94 J/min/L (log-rank p < 0.001), corresponding to a crude hazard ratio (HR) of 3.3 (Fig. 1). Similarly, final dynamic strain > 0.93 and final global strain > 1.33 were associated with crude HRs of 2.3 (p = 0.0013 and p = 0.0021, respectively).

In multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, lactate, and SAPS II, MP/FRC remained independently associated with ICU mortality when modeled as a continuous variable (HR 1.06 per unit increase; p = 0.005). Final dynamic strain (HR 1.28; 95% CI 1.07–1.52; p = 0.005) and final global strain (HR 1.27; 95% CI 1.08–1.50; p = 0.004) were also independently associated with mortality. SAPS II remained an independent predictor in all models (p < 0.001). Discussion

This study suggests that indexing MP to estimated FRC may improve its ability to predict ICU mortality in ARDS. Unlike non-indexed MP, this metric incorporates a physiology-based estimate of lung volume derived from respiratory mechanics, aligning conceptually with the ARDS “baby lung” model, in which the functional aerated lung available for ventilation is markedly reduced.

Previous investigations have established the association between higher MP and mortality in mechanically ventilated patients. However, these studies relied on absolute thresholds of energy delivery without accounting for interindividual differences in lung size. Experimental evidence suggests that similar levels of MP generated through different combinations of ventilatory variables can produce different degrees of lung injury, highlighting that both the magnitude and distribution of ventilatory energy—not just its absolute value—contribute to VILI.14 Our findings extend this concept by suggesting that normalizing MP to an estimated surrogate of functional lung volume may provide additional prognostic information beyond that captured by global or dynamic strain indices.

In our cohort, MP/FRC demonstrated better discrimination for ICU mortality than the evaluated strain-based indices. Although strain measures showed higher sensitivity, their low specificity suggests that they may overestimate risk when used as isolated predictors. In contrast, MP/FRC provided a more balanced diagnostic performance and greater separation of survival curves. The consistent association observed across ROC analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and multivariable Cox regression suggests a relationship between ventilatory energy normalized to estimated lung size and mortality.

These findings are physiologically plausible. In ARDS, the effective lung volume available for ventilation is substantially reduced, meaning that a given amount of ventilatory energy is distributed over a smaller portion of aerated lung tissue. As a result, normalizing MP to a surrogate of functional lung size may provide additional information beyond absolute energy delivery alone. Importantly, the estimated FRC used in this study should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of anatomical lung volume or CT-derived aerated lung volume. Rather, it represents a physiology-based surrogate derived from respiratory mechanics that may partially reflect the functional size of the ventilated lung. Therefore, the observed association between MP/FRC and mortality should be interpreted within the limitations of this estimation method.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its real-world clinical setting and the use of standardized measurement techniques. This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may limit generalizability. Second, FRC was indirectly estimated from respiratory mechanics rather than directly measured. This approach relies on simplifying assumptions regarding lung elastance, pressure–volume relationships, and the homogeneity of respiratory mechanics, which may not hold in patients with ARDS. Furthermore, the derived FRC represents a physiological surrogate and was not validated against reference techniques such as nitrogen washout, computed tomography-derived aerated lung volume, or electrical impedance tomography in the present cohort. In addition, because the estimated FRC was derived from respiratory mechanics variables that are mathematically related to components of MP, mathematical coupling between the numerator and denominator of the MP/FRC index cannot be excluded. Therefore, part of the observed association may reflect shared mathematical structure rather than independent physiological information. Future studies using directly measured lung volumes will be necessary to determine whether the prognostic value of MP/FRC persists when lung volume is measured independently of respiratory mechanics. Furthermore, although the multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, lactate levels, and SAPS II score, residual confounding from ventilatory variables such as driving pressure, plateau pressure, PEEP, and oxygenation severity cannot be excluded. Consequently, the observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation using direct measurements of lung volume.

Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to determine whether indexed MP provides clinically meaningful information beyond conventional ventilatory variables. Finally, the sample size may reduce the power to detect smaller effects.

Conclusion

Mechanical power indexed to estimated FRC was independently associated with ICU mortality in mechanically ventilated patients with ARDS and demonstrated the highest observed discriminative performance among the evaluated respiratory indices in this cohort. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation using direct measurements of lung volume.
