Prehabilitation in older adults with cancer: scoping review of surgical outcomes

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdults aged ≥65 years constitute a growing proportion of surgical oncology patients and often experience frailty, multimorbidity and reduced physiological reserve, increasing their risk of postoperative complications and functional decline. Multimodal prehabilitation has emerged as a strategy to improve surgical resilience; however, evidence specific to older cancer patients’ remains fragmented. This study aimed to map the existing evidence on prehabilitation for older adult patients undergoing cancer surgery, characterise intervention components, summarise functional and surgical outcomes and identify research gaps.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines and a protocol registered with the Open Science Framework, searches were conducted in PubMed, Science Direct, the Cochrane Library and Google Scholar up to November 2025. Eligible studies included older adults undergoing cancer-related surgery who received any form of prehabilitation. Two reviewers independently screened studies and extracted data. Due to heterogeneity, findings were synthesised narratively.ResultsThirteen studies were included, comprised of six randomised controlled trials (RCT), three cohort studies, two quasi-experimental studies, one feasibility study and one RCT protocol. Most interventions were multimodal and delivered over 1 to 6 weeks. Improvements in preoperative functional capacity—particularly 6 min walk distance, aerobic endurance, muscle strength and respiratory function—were consistently reported. High-intensity or supervised programmes were reduced severity of postoperative complications and shorter hospital stay, while low-intensity or minimally supervised interventions showed limited postoperative benefit. Nutritional optimisation improved preoperative nutritional status and supported functional gains. Feasibility and acceptable adherence were reported even among frail and very elderly patients. Evidence gaps included variable intervention dosage, inconsistent adherence reporting, heterogeneous outcome measures and limited long-term follow-up.ConclusionMultimodal prehabilitation is feasible, safe and beneficial for older adults undergoing cancer surgery, improving preoperative function and, when sufficiently intensive, reducing postoperative morbidity and hospital stay. Further research should standardise outcomes, define optimal intervention parameters and evaluate long-term recovery using geriatric-focused models.

Background

As the population ages, cancer in older adults has become a growing global health concern, with a substantial proportion of diagnoses occurring in patients aged 65 years and above.1 Cancer incidence increases exponentially with age, making oncology care a predominant health concern for older adults.2 Surgical resection remains the cornerstone curative treatment for many solid tumours, yet older cancer patients often face disproportionately higher postoperative risks due to age-related physiological decline.3 4 However, older adults presenting for cancer surgery constitute a clinically complex and heterogeneous population, as they frequently exhibit frailty—a state of decreased physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to stressors—alongside age-related comorbidities, reduced cardiorespiratory fitness and sarcopenia, all of which collectively diminish surgical resilience and increase the risk of postoperative complications and mortality.5 6 Consequently, compared with their younger counterparts, older surgical oncology patients are at a significantly elevated risk for adverse outcomes, including major morbidity, functional decline, prolonged hospital stays and loss of independence.7

In response to these challenges, prehabilitation has emerged as a proactive strategy to optimise patient capacity before surgery, defined as a set of preoperative interventions aimed at enhancing a patient’s functional and physiological reserves to better withstand the stress of surgery—particularly in older adults.8 9 Prehabilitation is a multimodal intervention combining structured physical exercise, nutritional optimisation and psychological support to enhance postoperative recovery by building physiological reserve, reducing susceptibility to surgical stress, and promoting faster recovery.10 11

Despite growing evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses showing that prehabilitation can reduce postoperative complications and hospital length of stay in frail and mixed-age surgical oncology populations,12 13 its specific application and effectiveness for older adults remain inadequately defined. The physiological and psychosocial complexities of this demographic suggest that their response to prehabilitation, the most relevant outcome measures (eg, functional independence vs sheer survival) and the feasibility of standard protocols may differ substantially from those of younger patients. Existing systematic reviews often lack a specific focus on this age group, and the literature appears fragmented across various cancer types and intervention models.14 A dedicated scoping review is, therefore, warranted to map the available evidence, identify effective components of prehabilitation, highlight deficiencies in current research and provide direction for future trials aimed at improving surgical and functional outcomes in elderly cancer patients, making it the most appropriate methodology to systematically explore this emerging field.

Methods

Study design and framework

This scoping review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines to systematically map the existing evidence on prehabilitation interventions in older adults undergoing cancer surgery. A protocol outlining the objectives, eligibility criteria and methodology was developed a priori and registered on the Open Science Framework under the Registration DOI:10.17605/OSF.IO/P2A5V to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Review question (PCC framework)

Population (P): older adults with cancer undergoing surgery. Concept (C): Prehabilitation interventions (exercise-based or multimodal). Context (C): Surgical settings, perioperative period, all countries and healthcare systems.

Primary review question

What is the effect of prehabilitation interventions on surgical outcomes among older adults undergoing cancer-related surgery?

Secondary questions

What types and components of prehabilitation interventions have been implemented for older adults with cancer undergoing surgery? Which surgical and perioperative outcomes have been evaluated in studies of prehabilitation in this population? What are the existing gaps in the evidence regarding prehabilitation for older adults undergoing cancer surgery?

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they involved older adults with cancer undergoing surgical treatment, defined as individuals aged ≥65 years. Studies including mixed-age populations were also considered eligible provided that either data specific to participants aged ≥65 years could be clearly extracted or the overall study population had a mean or median age of ≥65 years, indicating a predominance of older adults. The review will include studies examining prehabilitation interventions, such as unimodal approaches or multimodal approaches that incorporate components like exercise, nutrition and psychological support. Acceptable study designs include randomised controlled trials (RCT), cohort studies, case–control studies, feasibility or pilot studies, quasi-experimental studies and case series. Outcomes of interest may include postoperative complications, LOS, functional or physical performance measures, quality of life (QoL), readmission rates, mortality and long-term functional outcomes. There will be no restrictions on publication year or country; however, only studies published in English will be included.

Exclusion criteria consist of studies focusing on non-cancer surgeries, studies lacking age information, or those from which elderly specific data cannot be extracted as well as non-primary sources such as reviews, editorials, letters and commentaries, which will only be used for reference mining. Additionally, studies evaluating non-surgical forms of prehabilitation, such as chemotherapy-only preparation, will be excluded.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Science Direct and Cochrane Library as well as grey literature source Google Scholar. The search strategy incorporated terms related to ‘prehabilitation’, ‘preoperative exercise’, ‘older adults’, ‘frailty’, ‘cancer patients’ and ‘Surgical outcomes’, combining keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to maximise sensitivity. The search strategy for the PubMed database was as follows: (((((((Prehabilitation[Title/Abstract]) OR (“preoperative exercise”[MeSH Terms])) OR (“Pre-operative Exercise “[Title/Abstract])) OR (" Preoperative Exercise”[Title/Abstract])) OR (" Pre-operative Rehabilitation”[Title/Abstract])) AND (((“Cancer Patients “[Title/Abstract]) OR (“Oncologic patients”[Title/Abstract]))) AND ((((((" Aged 65 Years and Above “[Title/Abstract]) OR (Elderly[Title/Abstract])) OR (Old age[Title/Abstract])) OR (Older adult[Title/Abstract])) OR (Geriatrics[Title/Abstract])) OR (Frailty[Title/Abstract]))) AND ((“Surgical Outcomes”[Title/Abstract]) OR (“Cancer surgery”[Title/Abstract])). The search was conducted up to November 2025 (online supplemental file 1).

Study selection process

All identified records were imported into EndNote V.21 for deduplication and management. Two reviewers (TBA and MAT) independently screened titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant studies. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (NZG), and the selection process was documented using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Data extraction (charting the data)

Data extraction was performed using a predesigned standardised form, which was piloted on a subset of studies to ensure consistency. Two reviewers (TBA and HBW) independently extracted data on study characteristics (author, year, country, design), population details (age and cancer type), sample size, intervention components (exercise type, nutritional and psychological support, respiratory or geriatric assessment), comparator details, duration of prehabilitation and key outcomes including functional measures (eg, 6 min walk distance, postoperative complications, LOS, QoL, adherence and feasibility). Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion to achieve consensus.

Data analysis and synthesis

Given the broad scope of this review and heterogeneity in interventions and outcomes, a narrative synthesis approach was employed. Included studies were grouped and summarised by type of cancer, intervention modality (eg, multimodal supervised, home-based, geriatric-focused), study design and outcome domains. Key findings were described with emphasis on functional improvements, surgical outcomes, feasibility and adherence, while knowledge gaps and areas for future research were identified.

Results

Study selection

The systematic search and selection process is summarised in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (figure 1). The initial database search yielded 5719 records. After the removal of 5510 for duplicates and other reasons, 209 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Following this, 21 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in the final inclusion of 13 studies that investigated prehabilitation in cancer patients aged 65 years or older. The primary reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were wrong patient population (eg, not exclusively or predominantly ≥65 years) and intervention not meeting the definition of prehabilitation (Figure 1).

A variety of methodologies were employed. RCTs were the most common design (n=6),15 16 18 20 21 27 followed by prospective cohort studies (n=2),23 25 one quasi-experimental study,17 one propensity score-matched cohort study,19 one emulated target trial,22 one pilot feasibility study24 and one study was a protocol for an ongoing RCT26 (table 1).

Prehabilitation interventions

The vast majority of studies (12 out of 13)15 17–27 implemented multimodal prehabilitation, integrating physical exercise with nutritional support and, often psychological counselling. Exercise components typically combined aerobic and resistance training, with one study specifying high-intensity interval training or inspiratory muscle training.16 Two studies24 25 implemented a comprehensive geriatric prehabilitation model, which included additional components like comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), medication review (deprescribing) and anaemia correction. The duration of prehabilitation programmes varied, typically ranging from 2 to 6 weeks, with a median of approximately 4 weeks. One study implemented a short-term, in-hospital programme with a median duration of 7 days19 (table 2).

Reported surgical and functional outcomes

A wide array of outcomes was reported across the studies, as summarised in table 2.

Functional capacity

Across nearly all studies, multimodal prehabilitation regardless of whether it was supervised, home-based or hospital-based consistently improved preoperative functional status. Improvements were most frequently observed in the 6 min walk distance, aerobic endurance and measures of strength and respiratory capacity. The strongest functional gains were reported in supervised or higher-intensity programmes18 19 27 as well as one study21 found that a high-adherence subgroup (≥80%) demonstrated a clinically important improvement of 76 m, highlighting the role of adherence. Even when studies did not demonstrate postoperative improvements, most acknowledged significant preoperative enhancements in mobility or performance, suggesting that functional capacity is the most responsive domain to prehabilitation in older adults.

Postoperative complications and severity

Findings regarding postoperative complications were mixed, with clear benefits observed primarily in studies implementing structured, supervised and nutritionally intensive interventions. One trial27 demonstrated a significant reduction in severe complications among colorectal cancer patients, while the emulated target trial22 similarly reported a marked decrease in overall complication severity and comprehensive complication scores. Another trial18 in frail gastric cancer patients also showed reductions in severe and medical complications. In contrast, several earlier or less intensive RCTs15 16 20 found no significant differences in overall complication rates, suggesting that programme intensity, supervision and adherence likely determine postoperative impact. Evidence from very old and frail cohorts25 further indicated that prehabilitation may mitigate risk even in high-vulnerability groups, although these findings stem from non-comparative designs.

Length of stay and other outcomes

A reduction in hospital stay was not universally reported but emerged in several studies where prehabilitation was more structured or delivered with high supervision. A study19 showed shorter length of stay (LOS) among older Chinese colorectal cancer patients, and one trial22 also demonstrated a decrease from 7 to 5 days following a rigorous prehabilitation programme. Other RCTs16 20 with lower intensity or less adherence reported no significant difference, reinforcing the pattern that LOS reduction is achievable but dependent on programme quality and implementation fidelity.

QoL and patient-reported outcomes

Improvements in QoL and psychological well-being were observed in several interventions that included psychological support, counselling or behaviour-change components. Studies,17 19 both Zhou and Wang, documented enhanced mental and emotional QoL scores preoperatively. However, postoperative QoL findings were less consistent, with several trials20 21 such as Carli’s and McIsaac’s showing no sustained differences beyond the immediate preoperative period. These results suggest that QoL benefits may be concentrated before surgery and may diminish without continued rehabilitation postoperatively.

Feasibility, adherence and special populations

Feasibility and adherence

The pilot study24 and the cohort study25 explicitly concluded that multimodal, geriatrically-tailored prehabilitation is feasible in older and frail patients. Adherence was a critical factor, the study21 demonstrating that high adherence was linked to significantly better outcomes. Adherence rates, where reported, varied but were generally acceptable.

Benefits across age and frailty

A study23 directly compared older (≥65 years) and younger patients and found that both groups derived similar functional benefits from prehabilitation. Furthermore, studies focusing on the frailest patients, including octogenarians and non-agenarians,25 and those with gastric cancer,18 demonstrated the feasibility and potential effectiveness of prehabilitation in these high-risk subgroups.

Nutritional optimisation

Studies integrating dietitian-led counselling and protein supplementation consistently showed improvements in preoperative nutritional metrics, although postoperative clinical effects were variable. Interventions in studies22 27 emphasised high-protein targets and pre-exercise protein loading, appeared to coincide with stronger postoperative benefits. A study17 also reported improved nutritional markers, although these did not translate into complication reduction. Collectively, evidence suggests that nutritional optimisation is a reliable mechanism for enhancing preoperative readiness but may require synergistic high-intensity exercise to influence clinical outcomes.

Nutritional intervention components across included studies

Nutritional interventions across the 13 included studies consistently combined systematic assessment, individualised dietary counselling and targeted supplementation. Nutritional assessment was conducted using validated tools, most commonly Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) (n=7), (PG-) Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) (n=6), Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) (n=2) and Short Nutritional Assessment Questionnaire (SNAQ) (n=1), complemented by laboratory markers including albumin and prealbumin. Dietary counselling, delivered by dietitians in 11 studies, focused on protein optimisation, caloric balance and glycaemic control, guided by national dietary guidelines or ESPEN (European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism) recommendations. Oral nutritional supplementation, predominantly whey protein, was prescribed in 11 studies, with protein targets ranging from 1.2 to 1.9 g/kg/day (most commonly 1.5 g/kg/day; n=6). Supplement timing was specified in seven studies, typically within 1 hour postexercise. Four studies addressed anaemia or micronutrient deficiencies (iron, vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D). Two studies incorporated preoperative immunonutrition, one included carbohydrate loading and two described escalation protocols from oral supplements to enteral or parenteral nutrition for patients not meeting targets (online supplemental file 2).

Nutritional assessment tools

Across the included studies, nutritional status was assessed using tools such as the NRS-2002, SGA and Patient-Generated SGA, alongside anthropometric, dietary and laboratory measures, with some incorporating geriatric-focused instruments like the MNA and SNAQ.15–27 QoL was most commonly measured using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), with additional instruments including the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) and EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D), although some studies did not report standardised QoL tools.15 17–21 23 24 26 27 Postoperative complications were consistently evaluated using the Clavien-Dindo classification and the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI), alongside outcomes such as LOS, intensive care unit admission, readmissions and mortality, though overall heterogeneity remained in nutritional and QoL assessment approaches (table 3).

Overall synthesis

Taken together, the evidence indicates that prehabilitation consistently improves preoperative functional capacity, frequently enhances nutritional status and psychological readiness, and, when delivered with sufficient intensity and supervision, can lead to meaningful reductions in postoperative complication severity and LOS. Benefits are most pronounced in multimodal, supervised programmes lasting at least 3–6 weeks, particularly when combined with dietitian guidance and structured exercise. Less intensive or poorly adhered-to interventions tend to show neutral postoperative effects. The feasibility of prehabilitation across elderly, frail and very elderly patients is well supported.

Discussion

This review synthesises evidence from a diverse range of studies examining prehabilitation in older adults undergoing oncologic surgery. The findings suggest that multimodal prehabilitation programmes—particularly those that integrate structured exercise, nutritional optimisation and psychological or behavioural support—consistently enhance preoperative functional capacity and may reduce postoperative morbidity in this vulnerable population. These benefits were observed across various cancer types, healthcare systems and levels of patient frailty.

Before interpreting outcomes in relation to frailty, it is essential to clarify how frailty was assessed and operationalised across the included studies. Frailty assessment was heterogeneous, reflecting the absence of a consensus gold standard in surgical oncology populations.28 29 The Fried Frailty Index—which operationalises frailty based on five criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness (grip strength), slow walking speed and low physical activity—was the most commonly employed tool, with scores ≥2 defining frailty.18 20 25 This aligns with evidence that the Fried phenotype demonstrates high predictive accuracy for postoperative complications compared with other frailty instruments.30 The Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) was also utilised, with scores ≥4 (very mild to moderate frailty) serving as the inclusion threshold.16 21 26 Other instruments included the modified frailty index based on comorbidity accumulation and surrogate markers such as low physical fitness (eg, maximal walking speed <2 m/s, anaerobic threshold <11 mL/kg/min).16 26 Notably, comorbidity-based indices may inadequately capture the multidimensional nature of frailty, whereas multidimensional tools incorporating functional measures provide superior risk stratification.31 32 This diversity in frailty operationalisation—ranging from phenotypic to deficit accumulation models—means that patients categorised as ‘frail’ across studies may have had varying degrees and domains of vulnerability, thereby contributing to heterogeneity in observed prehabilitation outcomes. Prehabilitation consistently produced measurable gains in preoperative functional capacity among older adults, with improvements in 6 min walk distance, aerobic fitness, muscle strength and respiratory performance reported across most included trials conducted in China,17 18 international multicentre collaborations27 and Canada.21 This pattern aligns closely with the conclusions of earlier scoping work,11 which also found functional improvement to be the most reproducible effect in older or frail surgical populations, a similarity likely reflecting the strong responsiveness of older adults to structured aerobic and resistance training when delivered with sufficient intensity and oversight. However, several lower intensity or minimally supervised RCTs in the present review conducted in Sweden16 and Canada20 reported only modest or non-significant functional gains. This discrepancy likely arises from differences in exercise dose, adherence levels and supervision, consistent with the conclusions of that adherence ≥80% was required to produce clinically relevant functional change.21

Reductions in severe or medical postoperative complications were observed primarily in high-intensity, multimodal and supervised interventions in studies conducted in China,18 international multicentre collaborations,27 and Netherlands.22 These findings converge with the earlier conducted review,1 which reported that multimodal programmes with nutrition and exercise components most reliably reduced postoperative morbidity in older surgical oncology patients, a similarity explained by shared mechanisms of increased resilience to surgical stress.

Nevertheless, several trials conducted in Canada15 20 and Sweden16 did not demonstrate significant reductions in complication rates, diverging from the more uniformly positive trends noted in mixed-age systematic review.9 This difference is likely attributable to shorter programme duration, lower supervision, variable adherence and inclusion of participants with lower baseline frailty (ie, patients who were more robust at baseline with greater physiological reserve), all of which limit the capacity of prehabilitation to influence postoperative complications. Clarifying this concept is important: frailty represents a state of diminished physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors such as surgery.33 Patients with higher baseline functional capacity have less room for measurable improvement—a ceiling effect—which may limit the ability to detect significant postoperative benefits from prehabilitation.34–36 Conversely, patients with greater preoperative deficits (ie, higher frailty burden) have more physiological reserve to recover and more capacity for functional gain.37 This interpretation is supported by subgroup analyses in several included studies,18 27 which reported larger treatment effects in more frail or deconditioned participants, and aligns with the broader principle that prehabilitation is most effective when targeted at patients with identifiable deficits who have the greatest potential for benefit.21 Indeed, a recent secondary analysis of a prehabilitation trial demonstrated that frail patients who adhered to the intervention showed improved functional recovery, suggesting that frailty itself should not preclude prehabilitation but rather identify those most likely to benefit.20

LOS reductions were seen in a subset of studies19 22 employing structured or supervised approaches. These observations are consistent with findings reported in the scoping review11 and the meta-analysis,13 which likewise found LOS benefits in more intensive programmes. In contrast, trials16 20 did not show LOS reductions, reflecting the same inconsistencies identified in prior scoping reviews in which short or unsupervised programmes rarely demonstrated clinically meaningful LOS effects.

Evidence from very old or highly frail cohorts—including the studies24 25—indicated that prehabilitation remains feasible and beneficial even among octogenarians and nonagenarians. This finding mirrors the conclusions of umbrella review,38 which noted that older age does not diminish physiological responsiveness. The similarity likely stems from tailored geriatric components such as CGA, medication review, anaemia correction and nutritional optimisation that directly target modifiable vulnerabilities common in geriatric oncology.24 25 Moreover, a study23 demonstrated that older and younger adults obtained comparable functional improvements, reinforcing the premise that age alone should not limit prehabilitation access.

Nutritional optimisation emerged as a particularly influential intervention component in both the current review and previous scoping analyses.1 4 11 38 Studies17 22 27 incorporating dietitian-guided protein supplementation demonstrated improved perioperative nutritional status and, in some cases, reductions in complication severity. This similarity across reviews likely reflects convergent mechanistic pathways where protein intake enhances muscle protein synthesis, supports immune function, and augments the physiological benefits of exercise.

Across studies, multimodal prehabilitation was consistently superior to unimodal approaches, reflecting conclusions from earlier evidence syntheses.1 11 The convergence of evidence likely arises from the multidimensional nature of vulnerabilities in older cancer patients frailty, sarcopenia, malnutrition, polypharmacy and psychological distress—which require integrated rather than isolated interventions.24 27 Taken together, similarities with previous scoping reviews strongly support multimodal, supervised and nutritionally optimised prehabilitation as the most effective strategy, while discrepancies in outcomes across individual studies appear largely attributable to variability in intervention dose, supervision intensity, adherence and patient risk profiles.

Strength and limitations

This scoping review comprehensively maps current evidence on prehabilitation in older surgical oncology patients, including frail and high-risk populations often underrepresented in research. It incorporates diverse study designs and settings, summarising interventions and outcomes in a structured, transparent manner. This review has also several limitations such as the included studies differed considerably in design, intervention components, duration, supervision intensity, outcome measures and cancer types, making direct comparisons challenging. Some studies had small sample sizes or were designed primarily to assess feasibility, which limits the strength of conclusions regarding postoperative outcomes. The inclusion of study protocols and real-world observational designs introduced additional variability in methodological rigour. Furthermore, many studies focused on short-term perioperative outcomes and did not evaluate longer-term recovery, functional independence or survival. Reporting of adherence was inconsistent, and programme fidelity varied across settings. Although this review focused on older adults, some studies included mixed-age cohorts, which may have diluted age-specific effects.

Implications for practice and future research

The collective findings of this scoping review have several important implications for clinical care. For practice, the results suggest that multimodal prehabilitation should be strongly considered for older adults undergoing cancer surgery, particularly those who are frail or have low baseline functional capacity. Clinicians should prioritise interventions that include supervised or structured exercise, protein-focused nutritional support and psychological readiness training, as these components appear most strongly associated with improved outcomes. Health systems may need to develop integrated prehabilitation pathways that emphasise early referral at diagnosis, interdisciplinary collaboration (including physiotherapists, dietitians, geriatricians and psychologists) and mechanisms to promote adherence such as remote monitoring or patient coaching. Given the clear feasibility in frail and very elderly populations, exclusion of older adults based solely on age is not justified. Instead, prehabilitation should be viewed as a means of mitigating frailty and enhancing resilience prior to surgery.

For future research, several areas require targeted investigation. There remains a need to determine the optimal ‘dose’ and duration of prehabilitation, including the minimum intensity required to achieve meaningful postoperative benefits. Trials should also explore the comparative effectiveness of supervised versus home-based models, particularly in low-resource settings. Another important priority is the standardisation of outcome measures, including functional capacity metrics, frailty indices, complication definitions and patient-reported outcomes, to allow greater comparability across studies and meta-analytic synthesis. Long-term outcomes remain understudied; future trials should evaluate whether prehabilitation influences longer term recovery trajectories, functional independence, disability-free survival and QoL months after surgery.

Moreover, future research should specifically address equity and accessibility, as older adults may face social, logistical or economic barriers that limit participation. Digital health strategies, caregiver-supported programmes and community-based exercise models may enhance inclusion, particularly in regions with limited healthcare infrastructure. Finally, as the evidence increasingly supports the benefits of multimodal interventions, future trials should consider adaptive or personalised prehabilitation models that tailor components based on individual frailty profiles or patient preferences.

Conclusion

The evidence from this review demonstrates that multimodal prehabilitation offers significant benefits for older adults undergoing oncologic surgery. It improves preoperative function, enhances physiological reserve and, in several well-designed programmes, reduces postoperative complications and shortens hospital stays. The strongest effects were seen in programmes that were supervised, of sufficient duration and accompanied by high levels of patient adherence. Importantly, prehabilitation is both feasible and safe in very old and frail individuals, indicating that age alone should not preclude participation. As the surgical oncology population continues to age, integrating personalised, multimodal prehabilitation into routine perioperative care has the potential to meaningfully improve outcomes.

Future research should aim to standardise intervention components, determine optimal programme duration and intensity and develop strategies to enhance adherence among older adults. Expanding access to scalable, hybrid and geriatric-focused prehabilitation services may represent a key opportunity to improve perioperative outcomes and long-term recovery in this growing patient population.
