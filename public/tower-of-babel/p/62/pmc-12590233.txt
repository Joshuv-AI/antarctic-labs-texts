Sleep-disordered breathing in Prader-Willi syndrome: Two illustrative examples

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPrader-Willi syndrome (PWS) is a genetic disorder characterized by hypothalamic dysfunction, resulting in a combination of neurodevelopmental, metabolic, and behavioral abnormalities. Sleep disorders are frequent and often severe and constitute a major component of the clinical phenotype. However, sleep disorders affecting PWS patients remain under-recognized and undertreated.Case reportWe report two pediatric cases of PWS illustrating divergently evolving pattern of sleep-disordered breathing.The first patient, L.K., aged 10, presented with severe sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome including a prominent central component (AHI: 31.8/h; central apneas: 15.2/h). Introduction

Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare genetic condition affecting approximately 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 30,000 live births [1] that was first described in 1956 by Prader, Labhart, and Willi [2]. PWS results from an imprinting defect on the 15q11–q13 chromosomal region and is characterized by a constellation of features primarily manifesting as neonatal hypotonia, failure to thrive, hyperphagia, obesity, and endocrine, cognitive, and behavioral disorders.

Sleep disorders are a major and often underestimated and underdiagnosed component of PWS. Excessive daytime sleepiness affects over 60 % of patients [3,4], interfering with quality of life, learning, and behavioral stability [[5], [6], [7]]. Among the various sleep perturbations frequently observed in PWS patients, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS), central sleep apnea (CSA), nocturnal hypoventilation, and narcoleptic-like phenotypes with reduced REM latency and SOREMPs have all been documented [4,[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

Although obesity and hypotonia may partly account for the occurrence of OSAS, the presence of persistent sleepiness despite wake-promoting agent treatment and that of central ventilatory anomalies suggest a major role for hypothalamic dysfunction in sleep–wake regulation [12,13,15]. Studies have shown that ventilatory responses to hypercapnia and hypoxia are impaired in PWS, independently of mechanical factors [[13], [14], [15]]. Notwithstanding the compelling evidence pointing to the elevated risk for sleep disorders in PWS patients, these issues remain under-investigated in routine clinical practice [5].

Here we present two female patients with PWS followed in a sleep medicine center to illustrate the respiratory and hypersomnolent features and their evolution under treatment, while also illustrating the significant challenges associated with long-term adherence to therapy.

Case report 1

L.K., currently 10 and a half years old, is being followed in our sleep center for suspected nocturnal respiratory disorders in the context of a genetically confirmed diagnosis of Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) made in early infancy. She was born by cesarean section at 9 months of gestation, with a birth weight of 3 kg. There were no infections during pregnancy, but the mother reported reduced fetal movements compared to her previous two pregnancies. At birth, no respiratory assistance was required, but the patient presented with severe hypotonia, poor suction, and a lack of interest in feeding, which led to an initial hospitalization of 17 days in another hospital (erroneously treated as a neonatal infection), followed by a second hospitalization in the city of Melilla, where a thorough biological workup was conducted.

A neonatal transfer report (October 02, 2014) described generalized hypotonia, apathy, absence of crying and suction, and a poorly reactive neurological status. Initial tests (CRP, hematology, brain imaging) were normal. The clinical picture was suggestive of a syndromic encephalopathy of genetic origin, with early suspicion of PWS.

Genetic diagnosis was confirmed at the age of 3 months by methylation-specific PCR targeting the SNRPN gene, showing exclusive amplification of the maternal allele. A CGH-array performed in 2015 revealed a 4.8 Mb interstitial deletion in the 15q11.2–q13.1 region. Cytogenetic analysis revealed mosaicism combining a normal cell line (46,XX) and another carrying a ring chromosome 18 (r(18)). Parental analysis was normal, suggesting a de novo anomaly.

From a developmental standpoint, the patient exhibited severe axial hypotonia with absent head control at 7 months, major postural and motor delays (no rolling, no sitting, no voluntary grasping), and poor social contact. She did not vocalize responsively, failed to recognize her mother, and showed no social smiling. She acquired the sitting position at 9 months but was still unable to hold her head properly, and walked at the age of 2. The absence of language during the first two years required intensive physical therapy. She currently presents with persistent dyslexia and severe cognitive delay, with a school level estimated to match that of a first-grade student, despite being enrolled in the 4th year of primary school. She has major difficulties in mathematics and French, and limited knowledge of Arabic. She has repeated one academic year.

Since the age of 9, her mother has reported rapid weight gain (+12 kg in one year), increasing irritability, frequent temper outbursts, crying episodes, and self-injurious behaviors when frustrated. The patient does not exhibit phobias or diabetes but is followed for pre-diabetes under a specialized dietary regimen.

ENT examination revealed retrognathia, a bulging nasopharynx, high-arched palate, upper dental crowding, dental malocclusion, Mallampati II, Friedman II, and moderate inferior turbinate hypertrophy, without oronasopharyngeal obstruction. The patient was lost to follow-up for a year and re-evaluated in April 2025. Review of the initial CPAP titration confirmed good control of obstructive events at 7 cmH2O, but short-duration central events were noted during leaks, stage transitions, or positional changes, suggesting high loop gain (Fig. 7). A final titration was conducted under fixed CPAP using a full-face AirFit mask (size S), which allowed respiratory stabilization, reduced respiratory rate, and normalized AHI to 5/h, consisting mainly of two-cycle isolated events preceded by sighs.

The patient was then placed on home-based fixed-pressure CPAP with regular follow-up.

The AHI, initially elevated at 31.8 events/hour, decreased to 5 events/hour under fixed CPAP, indicating effective ventilatory treatment. Case report 2

R.K. is a 17-year-old adolescent with Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) followed in the sleep unit for nocturnal respiratory disorders associated with daytime sleepiness. The genetic diagnosis of PWS was established at the age of 3 years based on DNA analysis revealing a maternal uniparental disomy of the 15q11–q13 region with absence of expression of the paternal allele.

She was born at term via vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 2.5 kg. The neonatal period was marked by generalized hypotonia, a weak cry, ineffective sucking both at the breast and with a bottle, extended sleep duration, and a lack of interest in feeding. A global psychomotor developmental delay was noted as well as persistent language delays.

As early as one year of age significant weight gain was observed along with hyperphagia and temper outbursts in response to frustration. Cognitively, the patient displayed moderate intellectual disability, attention deficits, learning difficulties, and impaired social skills. Nevertheless, she maintains good peer relationships. Self-injurious behavior was limited to persistent skin picking.

After a long interruption in follow-up, the patient was admitted to the endocrinology department at the age of 9 years. Somatically, she presented with moderate obesity (weight: 48 kg; height: 136 cm; BMI: 25.95 kg/m2), acromicria, almond-shaped eyes, dental caries, and excoriation lesions on both upper and lower limbs (Fig. 10, Fig. 11). Hormonal evaluation was reassuring regarding the thyrotropic and corticotropic axes. An ACTH stimulation test showed a normal post-stimulation cortisol level of 21.2 μg/dl. Gonadotropic evaluation was consistent with an advanced pubertal stage (LH: 6.76 IU/L; FSH: 9.16 IU/L; estradiol: 12 pg/ml). Discussion

Sleep disorders are particularly prevalent in the context of PWS) and constitute a major component of its clinical phenotype. While early manifestations are dominated by severe hypotonia, poor sucking, and developmental delays, the symptom cluster evolves with age toward behavioral disorders, hyperphagia leading to morbid obesity, and most notably, major sleep disturbances. The two clinical cases reported herein illustrate some of the diversity of clinical presentations and the potentially severe progression of respiratory problems during sleep in this population.

Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) is a cardinal symptom of PWS, often present from childhood and highly disabling [4,8]. Gherjan et al. [3] reported a prevalence of 67 % in adults, with a significant frequency of narcoleptic-like phenotypes, including Sleep-Onset REM Periods (SOREMPs) and, occasionally, cataplexy [4,8,9,16]. In our two patients, EDS was present as illustrated by their elevated Epworth scores (16/24 and 15/24), coupled with family reports and clinical observations. This persistent sleepiness, even after correction of obstructive apneas, may reflect central hypothalamic dysfunction [4,[13], [14], [15]].

Studies also report reduced REM latency, fragmented sleep, and altered architecture, findings that were partially documented in our polysomnographic recordings (e.g., REM <5 % of TST in L.K). Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), including both obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) and central sleep apnea (CSA), is highly prevalent in patients with PWS, with reported rates ranging from 50 % to 100 % depending on the series [17,18].

The pathophysiology of SDB in PWS is likely based on a combination of central and peripheral mechanisms. Hypotonia of respiratory and pharyngeal muscles, scoliosis, and obesity contribute to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), while central hypothalamic dysfunction underlies central ventilatory disturbances. Gozal et al. [19] demonstrated absent peripheral chemosensitivity, while Arens et al. [20] reported blunted ventilatory responses to both hypercapnia and hypoxia in children with PWS. Furthermore, arousal responses to hypoxia are significantly impaired during sleep [Arens et al., [21]. These abnormalities result in nocturnal hypoventilation and high prevalence of central sleep apnea (CSA). In both cases presented here, initiation of nocturnal ventilation led to marked improvements in polysomnographic indices, sleep efficiency, and oxygenation. These findings are consistent with those of Festen et al. [22], Sanner et al. [22], and Gozal et al. [23], showing that treating the central component of SDB can improve ventilatory responses and may help avoid invasive interventions such as tracheostomy.

Beyond respiratory benefits, treatment of sleep-disordered breathing may positively influence behavioral regulation. In the case reported by Gozal et al. [23], normalization of the hypercapnic ventilatory drive following tracheostomy was accompanied by notable improvements in behavior and activity level, suggesting that central ventilatory control may be closely linked to neurobehavioral function in PWS. Similarly, in our first case, caregivers reported a reduction in irritability and improved mood following PAP therapy, further supporting this link.

The GH/IGF-1 axis deficiency characteristic of PWS plays a key role in the genesis of ventilatory disorders. This deficiency leads to muscular hypotonia, reduced lean body mass, and decreased central sensitivity to hypercapnia [22]. GH therapy improves CO2 sensitivity, reduces CSA events, and enhances sleep quality. However, it can also promote the occurrence of adenotonsillar hypertrophy, with the attendant risk of transient worsening of OSAS, especially during upper respiratory infections [22]. The safety of GH therapy remains controversial. Early deaths have been reported, particularly within the first three months of therapy initiation, sometimes related to worsening pre-existing SDB [24]. This underscores the need for systematic polysomnographic evaluation before initiating GH therapy, and close monitoring during the initial months. Moreover, early GH treatment often causes fluid retention, increasing ventilatory load and the risk of nocturnal hypoventilation, particularly in obese patients or those with scoliosis. Furthermore, the hypothalamic dysfunction in PWS promotes the risk of instability in central ventilatory control, which may be exacerbated by metabolic variations during GH therapy. These factors fully justify baseline polysomnography to detect latent OSAS or hypoventilation and prevent potentially fatal respiratory decompensation under GH.

Excessive sleepiness, non-restorative sleep, and frequent awakenings significantly impact cognitive and behavioral functioning in children with PWS [[5], [6], [7]]. In L.K., the mother reported an improvement in irritability and mood disturbances after the initiation of CPAP, suggesting a direct functional link between sleep quality and behavioral regulation. Psychiatric disorders (OCD, anxiety, psychotic episodes) reported during adolescence [25] can also be exacerbated by chronic sleep deprivation and nocturnal fragmentation. Thus, integrating neuropsychological and behavioral management is essential in children with SDB in this context.

Our observations confirm the necessity of systematic screening for sleep disorders in all PWS patients, even in the absence of explicit complaints; complete polysomnography, including SOREMP detection and central ventilatory disorder assessment; early, personalized ventilatory management, with mode adaptation based on obstructive or central dominance; and periodic monitoring during GH initiation, especially in obese patients or in those with enlarged tonsils/adenoids. Longitudinal data remain rare in the literature. Sustained ventilatory adherence over time, as seen in R.K. after six years of follow-up, remains exceptional, but is encouraging.

Conclusion

Sleep disorders in Prader-Willi syndrome are frequent, polymorphic, and often underdiagnosed. Our two cases illustrate emergence of complex patterns combining obstructive and central apneas and marked ventilatory instability. Polysomnographic analysis guides ventilatory strategy, which must be tailored to each clinical phenotype. The effectiveness of nocturnal ventilation is evident, both on respiratory parameters and on sleepiness and behavior. Long-term adherent ventilatory support remains difficult but achievable. Multidisciplinary management and systematic screening for sleep disorders are essential in these patients.
