Bridging the evidence gap: research equity in conflict-affected health systems
Authors: Nidal Derar Muna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite bearing a disproportionate share of the global disease burden, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) contribute minimally to clinical trial data. Using the Palestinian Territories as a case study, this commentary explores how structural barriers in conflict-affected health systems create gaps in clinical evidence. Fragmented health services and inequitable publishing opportunities disrupt continuity of care and limit participation in global research. These barriers are rarely acknowledged in the medical literature, yet they profoundly shape clinical outcomes and research capacity. Addressing this evidence gap requires structural reform, equitable authorship, and locally led research agendas. Without including structurally excluded regions in evidence production, global clinical guidelines remain incomplete and inequitable.Keywords: Clinical research equity, Structural barriers, Conflict-affected health systems, Health equity, Research participation

Introduction

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) host less than 20% of registered clinical trials, although accounting for more than 80% of the global disease burden [1]. This disparity is a structural artifact that influences which treatments become standard of care and whose results guide medical practice, not a statistical anomaly. Although overall data indicates a worldwide disparity, place-based circumstances provide the most insight into the underlying causes. The Palestinian Territories are presented here as a representative example of how structural barriers in conflict-affected low- and middle-income countries impede evidence generation.

In this commentary, the term ‘clinical research’ is used broadly to encompass both interventional trials and observational studies, with particular attention to gaps in clinical trial participation in conflict-affected settings.

Lived experience of an evidence vacuum

During clinical rotations in hospitals such as Al-Makassed and Augusta Victoria, I observed firsthand the impact of restricted mobility and fragmented care on patient outcomes and research. Many patients, particularly those holding West Bank or Gaza identification, require special permits to access Jerusalem-based hospitals. Delays or denials of these permits disrupt treatment continuity and longitudinal research follow-up.

For instance, treatment interruptions caused by mobility restrictions have been associated with delayed interventions and suboptimal outcomes in oncology and chronic disease management [2], illustrating the tangible consequences of evidence gaps on patient care.

Even when data is collected, publishing challenges remain substantial. High article-processing charges (APCs) and limited domestic research funding reduce the visibility of locally generated evidence. These obstacles reinforce the global evidence gap, making it essential to support locally led clinical research and equitable collaborations that reflect the realities of conflict-affected health systems.

Structural barriers to clinical research

Fragmented governance compounds these challenges. Health services are delivered by multiple organizations, including ministries, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and United Nations (UN) agencies, with separate electronic records and ethics approvals. This fragmentation complicates multi-center research and contributes to administrative inefficiency.

Infrastructure and funding gaps further limit research capacity. Furthermore, low domestic research budgets constrain investigator-initiated studies. Even when data are generated, high publication fees remain a formidable barrier, impeding dissemination.

Epistemic inequity is also evident. Collaborations with high-income country partners often place local researchers in data-collection roles rather than leadership positions, restricting authorship opportunities and limiting capacity-building.

Palestinian territories as a microcosm of global inequity

While LMIC underrepresentation in clinical research is often attributed to resource scarcity, structural determinants are central [3]. For example, landmark cardiovascular trials such as SPRINT [4] were conducted exclusively in high-income countries. Applying such evidence to contexts with distinct sociocultural and health-system constraints can compromise external validity. The Palestinian Territories thus exemplify how systemic barriers affect research inclusion and clinical evidence generation.

National‑level research inertia

The research system in the Palestinian Territories lacks unified governance, coordination, or streamlined ethics processes [5]. Multi-institutional studies face administrative hurdles, and researchers must contend with steep APCs, sometimes equivalent to six months’ salary for junior academics [6]. Consequently, local data often remain siloed in grey literature or conference abstracts, diminishing visibility and limiting inclusion in global evidence syntheses.

Towards evidence equity

Addressing these structural inequities requires systemic reforms. Funding should support investigator-initiated studies led by local principal investigators (PIs) and aligned with national health priorities. Harmonizing institutional review board (IRB) processes across institutions can reduce administrative burdens and facilitate multi-center research, while agreements ensuring local data ownership and equitable authorship promote capacity building. Journals can also enhance transparency in waiver eligibility, coupled with structured mentorship programs to enhance publication readiness.

Emerging local initiatives illustrate feasible approaches to bridging these gaps. For example, universities have piloted shared IRB arrangements to streamline multi-center approvals, and collaborations with regional hospitals have facilitated data-sharing and capacity-building despite structural constraints. These early efforts demonstrate practical strategies that can be scaled to enhance research inclusion in conflict-affected health systems, offering a roadmap for underrepresented regions to contribute meaningfully to the global evidence base.

Conclusion

Clinical research equity cannot be achieved without intentional inclusion of underrepresented populations. The Palestinian Territories illustrate how structural and funding-related challenges perpetuate exclusion. By identifying barriers and offering actionable solutions, we underscore the need for global frameworks that integrate local realities. Lessons drawn from this context extend to other marginalized populations, reinforcing the principle that research equity is fundamental to health equity.
