Liver response to early embryonic stimulation with xylo-and mannooligosaccharides in broiler chickens
Authors: Aleksandra Bełdowska, Marcin Barszcz, Anna Tuśnio, Adrianna Konopka, Kamil Gawin, Ewa Święch, Maria Siwek, Aleksandra Dunisławska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gut microbiota plays an important role in signaling between the intestines and the liver. These two organs are connected by a bidirectional relationship known as the gut-liver axis. The aim of this study was to determine the impact of oligosaccharides delivered in ovo on liver physiology in broiler chickens. On the 12th day of incubation, the following substances were injected into the eggs of ROSS 308 broiler chickens: saline (control), xylotriose (XOS3), xylotetrose (XOS4), mannotriose (MOS3) or mannotetrose (MOS4). The dose of oligosaccharides was 0.5 mg/0.2 ml saline. Blood, bile and liver samples were collected post-mortem on day 42. Compared to the other groups, the MOS3 significantly increased the level of uric acid in the blood, while the XOS3, XOS4 and MOS3 groups had a higher magnesium concentration than the control group. All oligosaccharides decreased catalase and total superoxide dismutase activity. Compared to the other groups, the administration of MOS3 and MOS4 increased the content of oxidants in the liver of chickens. XOS4 increased the expression of the ANGPTL4, CYR61, KLHL6, NR4A3 and SYK genes compared to the control group, while MOS3 increased the expression of ANGPTL4, KLHL6 and NR4A3 genes. MOS3 increased and MOS4 decreased the level of DNA methylation for the ANGPTL4 gene. XOS4 and both MOS3 and MOS4 increased the methylation of NR4A3 gene. Therefore, it can be concluded that XOS and MOS decrease antioxidant status of the liver but increased the expression of genes related to the immunity and metabolism.Keywords: Broiler chicken, In ovo, Liver, Prebiotics, Oxidative stress, DNA methylation

Introduction

The liver is the main detoxifying organ in the body. It is characterized by high metabolic activity and provides energetic substrates for brain, muscle and other organs. It plays a central role in the regulation of lipid metabolism as it synthesizes fatty acids, cholesterol, and its derivatives such as bile acids (Bechmann et al., 2012). In the liver, amino acids are used as energetic substrates, metabolized to glucose during gluconeogenesis, and used for the synthesis of proteins and other bioactive compounds (Rui, 2014). Another function of the liver is the synthesis and storage of glycogen, which serves as a source of glucose for the body (Rosebrough et al., 1984). The activity of enzymes involved in these metabolic pathways is crucial for the proper functioning of the liver. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) participates in amino acid metabolism by catalyzing the conversion of alanine to pyruvate. Alanine is the main amino acid used in the process of gluconeogenesis in the liver. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) catalyzes a transamination reaction in which glutamate is converted into oxaloacetate (Holeček, 2024). Creatine kinase (CK) plays an important role in maintaining the energy homeostasis of cells with high energy demand by facilitating the transport of phosphates in the form of phosphocreatine (Kitzenberg et al., 2016). Enzymes such as ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGTP) serve as markers of liver injury. Fluctuations of their activity in blood may indicate various pathological conditions, such as acute infection, sepsis, or bile duct obstruction. Other markers of liver functions are albumin and bilirubin (Sharma et al., 2021). The latter is a product of heme catabolism with cytoprotective properties exerted via its antioxidant activity. Recently, bilirubin has been also shown to have an anti-inflammatory effects in the body and the gut microbiota-regulating properties (Hamoud et al., 2018).

As an organ with high metabolic activity, the liver is particularly susceptible to the oxidative stress. However, antioxidant enzymes such as catalase (Tehrani and Moosavi-Movahedi, 2018), glutathione peroxidase (GSHPX), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) protect against reactive oxygen species (Halliwell, 2006). Considering the oxidative stress a challenge in an intensive broiler production systems, it is important to find a way to reduce its negative impact on the body. Recent studies have shown that oligosaccharides are promising compounds that can support antioxidant mechanisms in broiler chickens (Alyileili et al., 2020; Al-Surrayai and Al-Khalaifah, 2022; Lan et al., 2024).

Oligosaccharides are mostly known for their prebiotic functions. Galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), fructooligosaccharides (FOS), mannooligosaccharides (MOS) and xylooligosaccharides (XOS) selectively stimulate the growth and activity of beneficial microorganisms and limit colonization by pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. in the gastrointestinal tract (Ding et al., 2018; Biswas et al., 2021; Fadl et al., 2020). XOS are composed of xylose residues linked by β-(Al-Surrayai and Al-Khalaifah 2022, Alyileili et al. 2020, Allen and Ruff 1981, Barszcz et al. 2021) bonds and are produced during hydrolysis of cereal arabinoxylans (Broekaert et a., 2011). MOS are produced from the cell wall of yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and derived from mannan, in which mannose units are linked by α-glycosidic linkages (Kango et al., 2022). Previous studies of Bełdowska et al. (2024a) demonstrated that oligosaccharides differ in their effects on the relative abundance of cecal bacteria, and the stimulation with MOS was more effective than that with XOS. On the other hand, in ovo injection of xylotetrose tended to reduce acetic acid concentration in the cecum of birds, which might be of importance from the point of view of lipid metabolism in the liver (Bełdowska et al., 2024a).

The beneficial effects of prebiotics extend beyond the intestine. Bacterial metabolites produced in the gut are transported through the portal vein to the liver, while bile salts are transported into the intestinal lumen through the bile ducts. This bidirectional relationship is called the gut-liver axis and emphasizes the close interplay between the intestinal microbiota and hepatic physiology (Tripathi et al., 2018). The liver performs both metabolic and immunological functions (Hastings et al., 2020) and both of them can be affected by in ovo treatment with oligosaccharides. Recently, it has been shown that in ovo stimulation with synbiotic composed of galactooligosaccharide and Lactococcus lactis subsp. cremoris decreased the expression of immunity-related genes in Ross 308 chickens but increased it in Green-legged partridge-like chickens. In the case of genes encoding proteins with metabolic functions, the stimulation of embryos of Green-legged partridge-like chickens with prebiotic, probiotic and synbiotic upregulated their expression in the liver but had no effect on Ross 308 chickens (Dunisławska et al., 2023b).

Modification of animals phenotype can be achieved via the effect of nutrients and other bioactive compounds on epigenetic mechanisms. One of the most important is DNA methylation (Zhang, 2015), which occurs mainly at CpG dinucleotide sites. Methylation of the CpG islands in the promoter region inhibits gene transcription. Therefore, this epigenetic event regulates gene expression (Vinoth et al., 2018).

To specifically assess molecular responses in the liver, a targeted gene panel was selected based on both functional relevance and preliminary transcriptomic evidence. ANGPTL4 (angiopoietin‑like 4) was included due to its role in lipid metabolism and modulation of inflammatory processes, linking metabolic regulation with immune homeostasis in the liver (Zuo et al., 2023). CYR61 (cysteine‑rich angiogenic inducer 61) participates in extracellular matrix signaling and has been implicated in macrophage activation and NF‑κB mediated inflammatory pathways, making it relevant for tissue remodeling and immune responses (Eun et al., 2024). KLHL6 (Kelch‑like family member 6) is involved in immune cell signaling and has been associated with regulation of B and T lymphocyte function in adaptive immunity. NR4A3 (nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A member 3) is a transcription factor responsive to metabolic and inflammatory stimuli, linking stress signaling with gene regulation in immune cells and metabolic tissues (Murphy and Crean, 2022). SYK (spleen tyrosine kinase) is a non‑receptor tyrosine kinase essential for signaling in both innate and adaptive immune responses, including pathways regulating inflammation and immune cell activation (Turner et al., 2000). Based on their functional relevance and prior evidence of differential expression in poultry, these genes were chosen to examine how in ovo XOS and MOS administration may affect liver metabolism, immune signaling, and epigenetic regulation.

Xylo‑ and mannooligosaccharides were selected due to their documented positive effects on gut health, including modulation of gut microbiota and enhancement of short-chain fatty acid production. Being water-soluble, these compounds are suitable for in ovo delivery. The doses tested in this study were based on preliminary data and literature reports, with the goal of exploring dose-dependent responses and identifying optimal concentrations for subsequent experiments. It was hypothesized that in ovo stimulation with XOS and MOS would affect liver physiology in broiler chicken. The aim of this study was to determine the effect of in ovo administration of different oligosaccharides on biochemical blood parameters, bile acid secretion, liver biochemistry, including its antioxidant status, as well as on the expression and methylation of genes related to immunological and metabolic functions of the liver.

These parameters were chosen because of the dual metabolic and immunological role of the liver and its sensitivity to nutritional and microbial changes during embryonic development.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

Hatching eggs from the parent stock of Ross 308 broilers were purchased from a commercial hatchery. Three hundred fertilized eggs were used in the experiment. On the 12th day of incubation, the eggs were randomly divided into five groups (60 eggs in each group). The selected substances were injected into the air chamber of the egg. The application of 0.2 mL of aqueous solution into the air cell was performed using a syringe equipped with a 4-mm needle. After injection, the opening was sealed with non-toxic glue to prevent embryo contamination and reduce moisture loss. The control group received 0.2 mL of physiological saline solution (0.2 mmol/L), whereas the experimental groups received one of the prebiotic solutions: xylotriose (XOS3), xylotetrose (XOS4), mannotriose (MOS3), or mannotetrose (MOS4). Each prebiotic was administered at a dose of 0.5 mg dissolved in 0.2 mL of NaCl solution. Incubation was carried out in a single-stage incubator and hatcher (Jarson, Gostyń, Poland). Eggs were incubated for a total of 21 days. During the first stage of incubation (days 1–18), eggs were maintained in an incubator, whereas from days 19 to 21 they were transferred to a hatcher. The incubator operated at a temperature of 37.7 °C with relative humidity maintained between 55 and 60%, and ventilation set at 50–60% with the air inlet open. In the incubator, eggs were placed on six trays, each serving as a replication and containing 10 eggs per experimental group, and were automatically rotated by 45° once every hour. In the hatcher, environmental conditions were adjusted to 37.5 °C, 70% relative humidity, and 80% ventilation. Candling was performed on day 7 of embryonic development to remove unfertilized eggs and those containing embryos that had died at an early stage. A second inspection was conducted on day 18, when eggs were transferred to the hatcher. After hatching, chicks from each experimental group were housed in floor pens with chopped wheat-straw bedding. Birds had unrestricted access to feed and drinking water throughout the rearing period. Commercial starter, grower, and finisher diets were provided, formulated according to recommended nutritional requirements for broiler chickens. Hatchability parameters were monitored throughout incubation. On day 18, most groups retained 60 eggs transferred to the hatcher, while in the MOS3 group two eggs containing dead embryos were removed (58 eggs). The number of hatched chicks ranged from 53 to 57 among treatments. Hatchability calculated from the total number of eggs ranged from 88.33% to 95.00%, and hatchability calculated from eggs placed in the hatcher ranged from 88.33% to 95.00%. No statistically significant differences among treatments were observed for hatchability parameters (P > 0.05). The proportion of weak or crippled chicks was very low (0–1.67%) and did not differ between groups. Similarly, the percentage of unhatched eggs ranged from 5.00% to 11.67% and was not affected by the treatment. After hatching, healthy chicks were selected and standardized to 48 birds per treatment to ensure equal stocking density and comparable rearing conditions. These birds were then randomly allocated to four pens per treatment (12 birds per pen) and kept at a density not exceeding 33 kg of live animals per 1 m2. Detailed data on hatching mortality and growth were presented in our earlier publication (Bełdowska et al. 2024a). Environmental conditions were provided for broiler chickens as described by Biesek et al. (2022). On day 42 of age, after an overnight complete fasting, ten random birds from each group were selected and sacrificed to collect blood and liver samples. Ten birds were selected from each pen to ensure representative and statistically valid biological replication.

The experiment was conducted following the applicable regulations in Poland. The slaughter of the birds was carried out under the applicable regulations on the handling of animals during slaughter, including humane treatment. According to directive no. 2010/63/EU of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, the consent of the Ethics Committee was not required. According to Act of January 15, 2015 on the protection of animals used for scientific or educational purposes (item 266, Journal of Laws of the Republic of Poland) slaughtering to collect tissues and organs from animals, is not a procedure. The chickens were stunned using percussive blows to the head (firm and accurate blows to the head provoking severe damage to the brain). It was done following applicable acts: Council Regulation (EC) No 1099/2009 of 24 September 2009 on the protection of animals at the time of killing (mechanical methods) and Directive no. 2010/63/EU of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes (methods of animal killing). Decapitation was performed by cutting off the head between the occipital condyle and the first cervical vertebra. There was rapid bleeding of the carcass.

Sample collection

After decapitation, blood was collected during exsanguination from jugular veins and carotid arteries, placed into heparinized tubes, and centrifuged at 3350 g for 10 min. Plasma samples were stored at −40 °C until further biochemical analyses. After excision, the liver samples for gene expression analysis were placed directly in a stabilizing buffer (fixRNA, EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). Thus stabilized, it was stored at −20 °C until further analyses. Liver samples for biochemical assays were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Bile samples were taken by a gall bladder puncture, frozen in dry ice and stored at −40 °C.

Analyses of basic biochemical blood parameters

Biochemical parameters of blood plasma were determined spectrophotometrically on a MAXMAT PL analyzer (Erba Diagnostics France SARL, Montpellier, France) using diagnostic kits of ELITech Clinical Systems SAS (Sées, France). Sodium and potassium concentrations were measured using an EasyLyte Na/K Analyzer (Medica, Bedford, MA, USA).

Bile acid assay

Concentration of bile acids in blood, bile, and cecal digesta was measured photometrically using Chicken TBA (Total Bile Acid) ELISA Kit (ELK Biotechnology CO., Ltd., Denver, CO, USA). Blood and bile samples were analyzed directly, while the cecal digesta samples weighing 0.1 g were homogenized in 0.9 ml phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4, by vortexing at high speed for 30 s. After centrifugation (10000 g, 10 min, 4 °C), the supernatants were used for the assay. The absorbance was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, LLC., San Jose, CA, USA).

Evaluation of liver biochemistry

The concentrations of bilirubin, cholesterol, high-density lipoproteins (HDL), low-density lipoproteins (LDL), and triglycerides, as well as activities of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and cholinesterase in liver supernatants were determined using the reagents of ELITech Clinical Systems SAS and a MAXMAT PL analyzer. Total bilirubin was determined based on the reaction of diazotized sulphanilic acid with conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin in the presence of cetrimide as an accelerator. HDL concentration was quantified in a two-step reaction consisting of elimination of cholesterol of non-HDL lipoproteins using a selective accelerator and HDL solubilization with a specific detergent, followed by enzymatic reaction catalyzed by cholesterol esterase and peroxidase. LDL concentration was analyzed in a similar two-step procedure consisting of solubilization of non-LDL lipoproteins and enzymatic elimination of cholesterol, followed by solubilization of LDL by a detergent and enzymatic reactions catalyzed by cholesterol esterase and peroxidase. The supernatants were diluted in physiological saline to fit the linear range of the methods. For the purpose of lipid profile analysis, liver samples weighing 0.5 g were homogenized in 1 ml of ice-cold 0.05 M 1,4-piperazinediethanesulphonic acid, pH 7.0, and centrifuged (12850 g, 30 min, 4 °C). For the analyses of bilirubin and enzymes, the samples were prepared as described below for the TBARS assay. All diagnostics kits were validated for use in liver homogenates in a preliminary experiments. In each assay, the effect of sample background was eliminated by measuring the absorbance at a specific reference wavelength. For the purpose of normalization of enzyme activities, the total protein concentration in the liver was measured using Bio-Rad Protein Assay Kit II (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader.

To isolate glycogen, 1.5 g of liver was boiled with 2 ml of 30% KOH for 20 min with frequent vortexing. After boiling, samples were chilled in an ice bath and 200 µl of saturated Na2SO4 were added and incubated for 10 min with occasional shaking. Then, 5 ml of 96% ethanol were added and incubated on ice for 5 min. After glycogen precipitation, samples were centrifuged (10 min, 4500 g, room temperature) and the supernatant was discarded. Then, 2.5 ml of ultra-pure water was added to the pellet, which was then incubated in a boiling water bath until it dissolved. The sample volume was then adjusted to 5 ml with water and 2 ml of glycogen solution were taken for acid hydrolysis to glucose. After 30 min of incubation with 5 ml of 2 M HCl in a boiling water bath, 5 ml of 2 M NaOH were added to stop the hydrolysis. After cooling to room temperature, glucose concentration was analysed spectrophotometrically after the reaction with copper solution (16 mM CuSO4, 1.3 M Na2SO4, 226 mM Na2CO3, 190 mM NaHCO3, 43 mM sodium-potassium tartrate) and arsenate-molybdate solution (40 mM molybdic acid, 19 mM arsenic acid, 756 mM H2SO4) as described by Barszcz et al. (2021). The absorbance was measured on a 96-well microplate using a SpectraMax iD3 reader. The quantity of glucose was calculated from a standard curve and then converted to glycogen using the 0.95 coefficient.

Analysis of secretory IgA (SIgA) and electrolyte concentration in bile

The concentration of SIgA in bile was determined using Chicken Secretory Immunoglobulin A ELISA Kit (BT LAB) and a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader. Sodium and potassium concentrations were measured using an EasyLyte Na/K Analyzer, while phosphorus, magnesium, and calcium levels were analyzed spectrophotometrically on a MAXMAT PL analyzer using the diagnostic kits of ELITech Clinical Systems SAS.

Analysis of thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS)

The concentration of TBARS in blood and liver of chickens was measured photometrically as described previously by Chodkowska et al. (2022). Before analysis, liver samples weighing 0.2 g were homogenized in 0.8 ml of ice-cold 0.9% NaCl, and centrifuged (12850 g, 10 min, 4 °C), while blood plasma samples were assayed directly. The absorbance was read on a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader at a wavelength of 532 nm. TBARS concentration was calculated from a standard curve prepared using a serial dilutions of malonyldialdehyde.

Measurement of prooxidants-antioxidants balance

The prooxidants-antioxidants balance (PAB) was determined spectrophotometrically according to the microplate method of Koliakos and Hamidi Alamdari (2009). Liver samples were prepared as described above. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm and 620 nm (reference wavelength) on a SpectraMax iD3 reader. The PAB was calculated from the standard curve prepared using 1 mM H2O2 (representative of oxidants) and 6 mM uric acid (representative of antioxidants), and expressed in arbitrary units of Hamidi Alamadari and Koliakos (HKU) per ml of blood plasma or g of liver.

Analyses of antioxidant enzyme activity

Activity of catalase, superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase in blood and liver were determined spectrophotometrically using a microplate techniques as described by Wypych et al. (2024). Liver samples (0.2 g) were homogenized in 2 ml of 0.9% NaCl and centrifuged (12850 g, 10 min, 4 °C). Catalase activity was measured using the Catalase Assay Kit (Megazyme, Bray, Ireland), while that of glutathione peroxidase using Glutathione Peroxidase (GSH-PX) Assay Kit (BT LAB, Shanghai, China). Total activity of superoxide dismutase, as well as that of cytosolic (CuZnSOD, SOD-1) and mitochondrial (MnSOD, SOD-2) was analyzed according to the modified method of Marklund and Marklund (1974). Total protein concentration in the liver was measured using Bio-Rad Protein Assay Kit II (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). All absorbance readings were performed on a SpectraMax iD3 reader.

Gene expression

Total RNA was isolated from approximately 150 mg of liver, which was homogenized in 0.2 ml of chloroform and 1 ml RNA Extracol (EURx, Gdansk, Poland) using a TissuesRuptor homogenizer (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). RNA was purified from the solution and contaminants using a GeneMATRIX Universal RNA Purification Kit (EURx, Gdansk, Poland) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each RNA sample was quantitatively and qualitatively evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific Products, Wilmington, USA). RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Gene expression analysis was performed for the gene panel shown in Table 1, which included: ANGPTL4, CYR61, KLHL6, NR4A3 and SYK. cDNA was synthesized using the smART First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). Quantitative PCR reactions were performed using SG onTaq qPCR Master Mix (2x) (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland), 1 µM of each primer (synthesized by Merck Darmstadt, Germany), 70 ng of cDNA and all samples were analyzed in technical duplicates. The thermal program was performed in a LightCycler 480 II (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The thermal profile of the qPCR reaction was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, then 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 58 °C for 15 s and extension at 72 °C for 45 s and the curve melting. To ensure data reliability, appropriate negative controls were included in each run. No-template controls (NTC) were used to exclude reagent contamination, and no-reverse transcription controls (no-RT) were included to verify the absence of genomic DNA contamination. No amplification was detected in these controls. Gene expression levels were normalized to the two reference genes ACTB and GAPDH (19.78). The reference genes used in this study were selected according to the recommendations reported by Sevane et al. 2014, who evaluated the stability of several candidate housekeeping genes in chicken liver. The selection was also supported by our previous experiments involving tissues collected from chickens subjected to in ovo treatment by Dunisławska et al. (2021). The relative expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT formula (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Prior to applying this approach, amplification efficiencies for both the target and reference genes were determined using standard curves generated from serial cDNA dilutions. The efficiencies were comparable and within the acceptable range (approximately 90–110%), confirming that the assumptions required for the ΔΔCt method were met.

Global methylation

Global DNA methylation was analyzed using the MethylFlash™ Methylated 5mC DNA Quantification Kit (Colorimetric) (EpigenTek, New York) according to the manufacturer instruction.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS statistical software (SAS Enterprise Guide 8.3; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance, followed by the Tukey HSD test to determine differences between groups. Significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Basic biochemical blood parameters

Among analyzed indices, only uric acid (P < 0.05) and magnesium (P < 0.05) concentrations were affected. Stimulation with MOS3 significantly increased uric acid level in blood as compared to the other groups, while that with XOS3, XOS4, and MOS3 increased magnesium concentration in comparison with the control group (Table 3). There was also a tendency towards greater activity of ALT in birds stimulated with XOS3 as compared to the other groups.

Discussion

The liver plays a crucial role in metabolic and immunological functions, meaning that its physiological state is an important indicator of overall health. The presented study explores the impact of administering different oligosaccharides, i.e. XOS and MOS, during broilers egg incubation on liver physiology. Through the examination of biochemical blood parameters, bile acid secretion, antioxidant status, and genes related to liver function that have undergone methylation and expression, this research aims to shed light on how these prebiotics influence hepatic health and development.

In the current study, biochemical blood parameters were at similar levels to those obtained in other studies on broiler chickens (Chodkowska et al., 2022; Di Gregorio et al., 2023; Bełdowska et al., 2024b) and indicated good health status of birds stimulated in ovo with different oligosaccharides. The lack of effect on transaminases, cholinesterase, bilirubin, markers of lipid metabolism as well as total protein and albumin show that hepatocyte physiology was not impaired. The results of analyses of biochemical markers in the liver confirm this and show that metabolism of amino acids, synthesis of cholesterol and triglycerides, as well as energetic process involving the activity of CK and LDH were not affected by oligosaccharides administered in ovo.

Compared to the control group, stimulation with MOS3 increased the level of uric acid in the blood by almost three times. This effect was similar to that observed after in ovo administration of 0.5% sodium butyrate (Bełdowska et al., 2024b). It was suggested that the increase in uric acid concentration might result from lower uricase activity and reduced degradation to ammonia in the cecum but renal insufficiency was also considered (Bełdowska et al., 2024b). Uric acid is the end product of exo- and endogenous purine metabolism, synthesized in the liver, intestines, muscles, kidneys and the vascular endothelium (El Ridi and Tallima, 2017). Thus, its higher level in blood may also indicate intensified synthesis in these organs.

Magnesium circulating in the blood constitutes about 1% of the total pool. This element is necessary as cofactor for many enzymes and plays a pivotal role in energy metabolism, synthesis of nucleic acids, proteins, and fats, muscle contraction, neurological functions, and bone formation (Jahnen-Dechent and Ketteler, 2012). Magnesium is the second most common intracellular cation (Quamme and Rouffignac, 2000). Its homeostasis is regulated by intestinal absorption, storage in bones and renal excretion (Konrad et al., 2004; Jahnen-Dechent and Ketteler, 2012). Transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 6 (TRPM6) plays a crucial role in epithelial magnesium transport and its active absorption in the kidney and intestine (Chubanov et al., 2005). It is a key regulator of magnesium homeostasis in the body (Schlingmann and Gudermann, 2005; Xie et al., 2011; Robak et al., 2016). This channel is regulated by intracellular magnesium concentration and is constitutively open. Its homologue, TRPM7 is expressed ubiquitously in the body and regulates cellular homeostasis of magnesium in vertebrates (Schmitz et al., 2003). In the present study, it was found that in ovo stimulation with XOS3, XOS4, and MOS3 increased magnesium concentration in the blood of chickens. This effect may be ascribed to an upregulation of TRPM6 expression in the chicken body. Studies on pigs showed that dietary supplementation with long-chain inulin with degree of polymerization of 23 increased TRPM6 expression in the renal cortex and medulla which might be related to an enhancement of magnesium reabsorption (Robak et al., 2016). The available literature lacks information on similar studies conducted on broiler chickens. However, it was found that TRPM6 expression in the chorioallantoic membrane was higher on day 13 of embryonic development of chickens than on day 9, which indicated that magnesium transport was initiated on day 13 (Huang et al., 2022). In the current study, in ovo stimulation with oligosaccharides was performed on day 12 of egg incubation and coincided in time with initiation of magnesium transport by chorioallantoic membrane. The injection of oligosaccharides might increase TRPM6 expression in the membrane and contribute to higher blood magnesium concentration later in the chickens’ life.

The exocrine liver function was not affected by in ovo stimulation with oligosaccharides, which was shown by the lack of differences between groups in bile acid concentration in bile. The lowest concentration of bile acids in the cecum of birds treated with MOS3 and MOS4 indicate that this oligosaccharide affected enterohepatic circulation of these compounds. However, these results are not in line with those regarding bacterial β-glucuronidase activity in the ileal and cecal digesta of the same birds, which was described previously by Bełdowska et al. (2024a). This bacterial enzymes hydrolyses glucuronides, which are synthesized in the liver and secreted with bile to the intestine (Pellock and Redinbo, 2017). Thus, the bile acid concentration in the cecal digesta indicates an availability of glucuronides as substrates for β-glucuronidase. In ovo stimulation had no effect on the activity of this enzyme (Bełdowska et al., 2024a). This may indicate that the differences in bile acid content found between birds from the MOS3 and MOS4 groups and the other groups, although highly significant, were too small to affect β-glucuronidase activity.

Bile is a source of immunoreactive proteins such as SIgA, which exerts local antimicrobial effects (Leslie and Martin, 1973; Li et al., 2025). In the current research, it was shown that in ovo stimulation did not affect immune function of the liver and biliary tract. Another important function of the liver is the synthesis and storage of glycogen, which is a convenient form of readily available glucose for the regulation of its homeostasis in the body (Rosebrough et al., 1984). During chicken embryogenesis, the glycogen synthesis in the liver starts at 6-7th day of incubation and it is associated with the decrease of respiration rate (O’connor, 1953). In ovo stimulation with oligosaccharides may affect glycogen synthesis or its mobilization later in the chickens’ life, which was shown by the reduction of its content by XOS3 and XOS4. Allen and Ruff (1981) showed that feed withdrawal causes rapid reduction of glycogen content in the chicken liver. In the current study, it was not possible to collect liver samples from all birds at the same time, which could have influenced the results. Nonetheless, broilers from the MOS3 and MOS4 groups had slightly greater glycogen content than XOS-treated birds, despite they were sacrificed as the last one. Therefore, further research is required to unravel the mechanisms underlying the effect of in ovo-administered oligosaccharides on glycogen concentration in the liver.

Dietary supplementation with 0.3–0.7% fructooligosaccharides reduced malonyldialdehyde concentration in blood and liver and increased catalase activity (0.5 and 0.7% addition) in the liver of chickens, showing the antioxidative effects of these compounds (Al-Surrayai and Al-Khalaifah, 2022). Antioxidant properties of 0.2% dietary addition of MOS were also shown in the case of TBARS content in a fresh chicken meat (Biswas et al., 2021). As for the in ovo research, previously it was shown that the stimulation with XOS4 and MOS3 increased MnSOD expression in the cecal mucosa, while XOS3, XOS4, and MOS3 decreased it in the cecal tonsils (Dunisławska et al., 2023c). In the current study, it was found that XOS4, MOS3, and MOS4 reduced MnSOD activity in blood, while all oligosaccharides decreased catalase and total SOD activity in the liver of chickens. The activity of liver MnSOD was reduced by the stimulation with XOS3, XOS4, and MOS3, which is in line with the results of Dunisławska et al. (2023c) concerning the effect on cecal tonsils and may indicate a down-regulation of gene encoding this enzyme. Moreover, in ovo administration of MOS3 and MOS4 increased the level of oxidants in the liver of chickens, which was shown by higher PAB values. This marker is useful in studies on the antioxidative effect of different bioactive substances in animals (Herosimczyk et al. 2018; Lepczyński et al. 2021; Chodkowska et al. 2022, 2024). Higher PAB values indicate greater risk of oxidative stress. However, in the liver of chickens treated with MOS, there was no increase in TBARS concentration, which is a marker of lipid peroxidation. This may be explained by the lack of negative effect on the activity of GSHPX, which efficiently removed reactive oxygen species. Considering that PAB value shows the balance not a damage, the apparent dissociation between the results of PAB and TBARS analyses suggests the absence of oxidative stress and an adaptive regulation of antioxidant defense system. Discrepancies between the current results and those obtained by the other authors may result from the route of prebiotic administration. In the current study, oligosaccharides were given once, during embryonic development, while in the nutritional studies on chickens prebiotics are given in a diet usually throughout the entire 42-day-long experiment. In the available literature, there is a lack of information on the effect of substances administered in ovo on the activity of antioxidant enzymes and levels of oxidative stress markers in chickens. Therefore, the obtained results require further research, including analyses of greater numbers of oxidative stress markers, to elucidate the effect of in ovo stimulation with oligosaccharides.

ANGPTL4 is an angiogenic factor involved in maintaining energy homeostasis and regulating lipid metabolism, including the modulation of triglyceride levels and potentially reactive oxygen species. In addition, it is responsible for metabolic changes in the liver in response to heat conditions. Lan et al. (2016) observed elevated expression levels of ANGPTL4 in more heat-tolerant breeds. In the present study, a significant increase in ANGPTL4 gene expression was noted in the XOS4, MOS3 and MOS4 groups relative to the control. The protein encoded by CYR61 plays a key role in cell proliferation, differentiation, angiogenesis and apoptosis. This protein is also a growth factor that promotes endothelial cell adhesion (Dunisławska et al. 2023a). Dev et al. (2021) shown a significant effect of MOS supplementation on changes occurring in the liver, including the expression of selected genes. It was noted that broiler chickens supplemented with synbiotic, i.e. MOS with Bifidobacterium bifidum, showed increased expression of PPAR-α indicating an increase in the expression of genes responsible for fatty acid catabolism. In our studies, stimulation with MOS also increased the level of expression of genes responsible for the metabolism. This includes the NR4A3 gene, which is involved in lipid and glucose metabolism (Zhang et al., 2020) and Cyr61, which may influence lipid metabolism by regulating signaling pathways that are involved in lipid homeostasis (Heo et al., 2024). For some studied genes: ANGPTL4 and NR4A3, simultaneous changes in both expression levels and DNA methylation are observed. The relationship between methylation and gene expression is complex, as hypermethylation of promoter regions is often associated with decreased gene expression, but the effect of methylation depends on the location of the modified CpG sites. In some cases, increased methylation may coincide with increased expression, particularly in gene body regions or other regulatory elements, where methylation may positively correlate with transcriptional activity (Jones, 2012).

Conclusions

It can be concluded that in ovo stimulation with XOS and MOS negatively affects antioxidant status of the liver by reducing the total SOD and catalase activity and shifting the prooxidants-antioxidants balance towards the former. Nonetheless, the injection of oligosaccharides to the embryo does not impair exocrine liver function and activity of basic liver enzymes as well as cholesterol and triglycerides synthesis later in the chickens’ life. XOS and MOS may increase the expression of liver genes related to the immunity and metabolism but this does not seem to be associated with the methylation level of CpG islands in their gene promoters.
