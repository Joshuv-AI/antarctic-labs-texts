A Glycolytic Gatekeeper in Developmental Cardiomyopathy
Authors: James Rhee, Haobo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Cardiomyopathy and congenital heart disease (CHD) have historically been associated with mutations in structural genes and networks of transcription factors and key signaling pathways that dictate morphogenesis. Mutations in such factors as NKX2-5, GATA4, and NOTCH1 have been implicated in a broad range of CHD.1 However, attention has gradually shifted toward the role of altered metabolism as a key driver in cardiac disease and development.2 For instance, impaired mitochondrial function contributes to heart failure,3 altered substrate use characterizes diabetic cardiomyopathy,4 and defects in fatty acid oxidation underlie inherited metabolic cardiomyopathies.5 Although metabolic dysfunction in the adult heart largely compromises contractile performance, its presence in the fetal heart also disrupts the energy-demanding processes of myocyte proliferation and morphogenesis. Systemic metabolic perturbation seen in such states as maternal diabetes or obesity clearly carry an increased risk of fetal heart disease. However, direct genetic evidence linking core metabolic enzymes to structural cardiac malformations has been more limited. In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Zhang et al6 identify a mutation in PFKP, the platelet isoform of the glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase -1 (PFK1) as a cause of myocardial hypoplasia during embryogenesis.

Energy production in the adult myocardium is dominated by oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, whereas the fetal heart relies more heavily on glycolysis to meet the energetic and biosynthetic demands of rapid cardiomyocyte proliferation and growth in the relatively hypoxic environment of the uterus.7 Indeed, high glycolytic flux and low oxidative stress are fundamental requirements for cardiomyocyte division.8 Thus, it follows that defects in glycolysis during critical embryonic stages might be expected to impair cardiac development. PFKP catalyzes the central rate-limiting step in glycolysis—the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F-1,6-BP). This step serves as a critical metabolic checkpoint, regulating glycolytic flux and coordinating anabolic pathways, such as nucleotide synthesis via the pentose phosphate pathway. Zhang et al showed that a heterozygous missense mutation (R755W) in PFKP causes myocardial ventricular thinning, septal defects, and impaired cardiac function. This mutation cosegregates in 2 independent families, with a strong phenotypical correlation. Isogenic correction in patient-derived hiPSC cardiomyocytes confirms causality. Furthermore, knock-in of this point mutation in PFKP in mice recapitulates the myocardial hypoplasia seen in affected patients. Finally, in vitro assays confirm that the mutation causes impaired PFK1 activity, glycolysis, and cardiomyocyte proliferation.

Several important theranostic insights emerge from this study. First, the identification of this novel PFKP mutation suggests that unexplained perinatal cardiomyopathy may have metabolic etiologies rooted in glycolysis and that genetic testing of enzymes in this pathway may deserve an increased role in screening for CHD. Comprehensive metabolomics using isotope tracing in patient-derived iPS cells will help shed light on which specific bioenergetic pathways are most perturbed. Second, defects in glycolysis may reduce adenosine triphosphate levels and glycolytic flux below a critical threshold required for adequate cellular proliferation, underscoring how this specific form of disease is driven by metabolic insufficiency rather than irreversible structural defects. In terms of therapeutic relevance, bypassing metabolic blockade with downstream pathway intermediates, as opposed to enzyme replacement therapy, is considered a tantalizing possibility. Supplementation with the downstream metabolite F-1,6-BP rescues the cardiac dysfunction described here by increasing cardiomyocyte proliferation in vitro and myocardial wall thickness in vivo, confirming that the disease phenotype arises from inadequate glycolytic flux rather than toxic buildup of intermediates. The successful rescue via intra-amniotic metabolite delivery provides a proof of concept for prenatal metabolic interventions that then prevent developmental defects before birth. The translational implications extend beyond this specific mutation because other defects in metabolic pathways could potentially be similarly modified in utero, with careful consideration to optimal timing and dosing. Combined with preimplantation genetic testing, this study has far-reaching clinical applicability and real-world integration into reproductive strategies.

As with all interesting and provocative work, the study by Zhang et al raises several important questions that warrant further investigation. First, although supplementation with F-1,6-BP rescues the cardiac deficits, the intervention was performed at Embryonic Day 12.5, when cardiac morphogenesis is already well underway. Future studies should evaluate the effects of supplementation at earlier developmental time points, which would provide important insights for guiding preventive strategies for CHD within clinically relevant therapeutic windows. Second, the phenotypic characterization of the in vivo and in vitro models in this study is largely focused on cardiomyocyte proliferation and ventricular wall thickness. However, other pathological changes, such as cardiomyocyte apoptosis and hypertrophy, also contribute to CHD pathogenesis. Therefore, a more comprehensive phenotypic analysis is needed to fully elucidate the role of the PFKP mutation in organogenesis. Third, the authors conclude that the PFKP mutation may impair cardiomyocyte proliferation through mitochondrial dysfunction and the resulting metabolic disturbances. Because the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects remain largely unknown, additional studies are needed to identify extraglycolytic signaling pathways altered by this PFKP mutant.

In conclusion, this work by Zhang et al carries implications beyond cardiac development and CHD. Loss of regenerative capacity and proliferative arrest coincide with a postnatal switch from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation.9 It is possible that different genetic and environmental insults may converge on overlapping disease mechanisms that create glycolytic bottlenecks which hinders not only adenosine triphosphate production but also anabolic pathways and redox balance. It is tempting to speculate that modulation of glycolysis, including supplementation with specific pathway intermediates, may have a role in favorable remodeling of the adult heart in failure, ischemia, or after injury.10,11 The majority of investigations into the bioenergetics of cardiac disease has focused on mitochondrial function and fatty acid oxidation, whereas the present work offers further grounds to investigate glycolysis beyond the fetal heart.
