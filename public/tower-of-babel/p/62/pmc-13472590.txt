Cystine-Binding Medication Is Poorly Accessible for the Treatment of Cystinuria
Authors: Xuemi Peng, Bart Penninx, Roos F. Marsman, Giovanna Capolongo, Marie Courbebaisse, Bertrand Knebelmann, Francesco Trepiccione, Francesco Emma, Aude Servais, Carla E.M. Hollak, Elena N. Levtchenko, Rik H.G. Olde Engberink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCystinuria is a rare inherited disorder characterized by recurrent cystine stone formation. When lifestyle modification and urine alkalinization fail, cystine-binding medication such as tiopronin and D-penicillamine are indicated. Despite proven benefit, their accessibility across Europe appears limited. We hypothesized that access to this medication is restricted and varies substantially between European countries.MethodsWe surveyed physicians affiliated with the European Reference Network for Rare Kidney Diseases (ERKNet) regarding access barriers and prescribing practices. The online questionnaire was completed by 40 clinicians from 15 countries.ResultsEighty-five percent of respondents had prescribed cystine-binding medication in the past 2 years, with tiopronin being the preferred agent. However, 30% reported having no access to tiopronin at all, and 83% of recent prescribers experienced accessibility problems, including high costs and unfilled prescriptions. Substantial differences were observed both between and within countries. Prescription rates correlated with physicians’ cystinuria caseload.DiscussionCystine-binding medication is poorly and inconsistently accessible across Europe. Coordinated efforts are urgently needed to stabilize supply and ensure equitable, affordable access.

Introduction

Cystinuria is an inherited disorder characterized by impaired transepithelial reabsorption of cystine, lysine, ornithine, and arginine in the proximal renal tubule. This leads to high urinary cystine concentrations, causing cystine to precipitate and form kidney stones [1–4]. Cystinuria often manifests early in life and is associated with substantial morbidity, including the need for surgical interventions and an increased risk of chronic kidney disease [5, 6].

The treatment of cystinuria places a significant burden on patients. The cornerstones of conservative therapy are a high fluid intake, low salt intake, low-methionine diet and urine alkalinization as cystine solubility increases with higher urine pH [1, 3]. A recent study demonstrated that the effect of salt and protein intake is limited [7]. As conservative measures alone are often insufficient to fully control the disease and prevent repeated surgical interventions, treatment with cystine-binding medication such as tiopronin and D-penicillamine is often indicated [8, 9]. Both medicines share a common mechanism of action, as they react with cystine to form a mixed disulfide with greater solubility [2].

Despite their importance in preventing kidney stone formation in certain patients, access to cystine-binding medication has come under increasing pressure. According to the Article 57 database maintained by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), which lists all medicines authorized in the European Economic Area, tiopronin and D-penicillamine are marketed by only one and four Marketing Authorization Holders (MAHs), respectively [10]. This limited number of suppliers has contributed to the poor accessibility of these medicines and has driven up the price. As a result, doctors are sometimes forced to switch patients to alternative treatments or seek other solutions to secure access. In the Netherlands, for example, a pharmacy-compounded formulation was developed by a single pharmacy to guarantee continued access to tiopronin for its registered patients. Patients that require treatment are being referred to this pharmacy. This approach has ensured sustained access since 2022. To identify access-related challenges and better understand clinical practice variation related to cystine-binding medication in Europe, we conducted a survey among physicians affiliated with the European Reference Network for Rare Kidney Diseases (ERKNet).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study among physicians affiliated with ERKNet. The study was initiated by the Metabolic Nephropathy & Stone Disorders Working Group to evaluate the use and accessibility of cystine-binding medication in clinical practice for the treatment of cystinuria. Eligible participants included physicians who had managed at least 1 patient with cystinuria in the last 2 years. Before distribution, the full questionnaire underwent internal review by the expert working group to ensure clarity, clinical relevance, and content validity. The online questionnaire was distributed via email, and data were collected using the Castor EDC platform. The data collection period was from April to November 2024.

Survey Content

The questionnaire was designed to systematically capture key aspects of clinical practice related to the use of the cystine-binding medication tiopronin and D-penicillamine. It included both multiple-choice and open-ended questions, organized into thematic sections to enable a comprehensive evaluation of prescribing patterns, drug accessibility (online suppl. Data; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553298).

We gathered background information, including the respondent’s country of practice, medical profession, years of clinical experience, prior involvement in cystinuria management, and the number of patients currently and historically under their care. We assessed which drug had been prescribed in the past 2 years, challenges related to drug acquisition methods, and insurance-based limitations on access. Respondents were asked to report typical dosing strategies for both adults and children, including average starting and maximum daily doses, preferred dosing frequency, and any adjustments to morning or bedtime administration. Participating physicians were also asked to describe observed side effects and share patient experiences such as difficulties with swallowing or unpleasant smell. The questionnaire also addressed the conditions under which physicians would initiate or discontinue cystine-binding drug therapy, including how they approach cases of poor adherence to lifestyle recommendations or inadequate urine alkalinization.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize physician characteristics and responses. Categorical variables were presented as absolute counts and percentages. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), unless otherwise stated. Linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between total number of cystinuria patients treated and the percentage of patients receiving cystine-binding medication. Physicians who had treated fewer than five cystinuria patients were excluded in this analysis. The Chi-square tests were conducted to investigate the relation between years of clinical experience and prescribing, starting, and stopping patterns. Data were analyzed using RStudio version 2024.12.0 (Posit Software, PBC) and IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical Considerations

This study collected anonymized professional opinions from clinicians and did not involve patient data. According to institutional and ERKNet policies, formal ethical approval was not required. Participation was voluntary, and completion of the questionnaire was considered to imply informed consent.

Results

We analyzed responses from 40 physicians across 15 countries (Fig. 1a), comprising pediatric nephrologists (58%), adult nephrologists (38%), and adult urologists (5%). The majority of participants reported 5–10 years (28%) or 10–20 years (35%) of clinical experience in managing cystinuria, with a median of 15 patients (IQR 8–34) treated over the course of their careers. Eighty-five percent (34/40) of the physicians had prescribed cystine-binding medication in the past 2 years. The proportion of cystinuria patients receiving cystine-binding therapy varied widely among physicians, with a median of 35% (IQR: 20–62.5) (Fig. 1b).

Medicine Accessibility

Thirty percent (12/40) of the respondents indicated that tiopronin was not accessible to them at all. Recent prescription of tiopronin was reported by 23 physicians; however, 83% (19/23) of the prescribers experienced access-related issues (Fig. 1c). Of the respondents who prescribed tiopronin, 70% (16/23) reported that the medication could not always supplied. A median of 20% (IQR: 0–35%) of their prescriptions were not filled. Insurance-related restrictions due to increasing medicine costs were noted by 22% (5/23).

D-penicillamine was prescribed in the past 2 years by 18/40 physicians (45%). Two physicians had no access to D-penicillamine. Among the prescribers, 61% (11/18) reported that not all prescriptions were filled. None of the physicians who had prescribed D-penicillamine reported insurance-related restrictions.

We observed a significant association between the amount of cystinuria patients that were treated by a physician and the percentage of patients receiving cystine-binding medication (β = 0.34, R2 = 0.17, p = 0.016; Fig. 2). No significant association was found between clinical experience (years) and prescription rate (p = 0.35). We observed that the accessibility problem exists in many countries, and even within countries, some physicians are able to obtain tiopronin, while others report it as unavailable (Fig. 3).

The reported sources of medicine procurement differed by agent. D-penicillamine was most frequently obtained from local pharmacies (67%), followed by academic hospitals (28%), whereas 6% of respondents were unaware of the source. In contrast, tiopronin was supplied through a broader range of channels, including local pharmacies (43%), academic hospitals (22%), pharmacies abroad (13%), and pharmaceutical companies either within the country (9%) or abroad (13%).

Medicine of Choice

Tiopronin was the preferred agent for 55% of respondents, while 25% favored D-penicillamine and 20% reported no preference. Among 22 physicians who had not prescribed D-penicillamine in the past 2 years, the most frequently reported reason was a preference for tiopronin (45%, 10/22), followed by the absence of a clinical indication (36%, 8/22) and lack of experience with the medicine (27%, 6/22) and lack of availability of D-penicillamine (9%, 2/22). Fear of adverse events was mentioned by 18% of respondents. Among the 17 physicians who had not prescribed tiopronin, the most frequently reported reason was lack of availability (71%, 12/17), followed by absence of a clinical indication because patients were well controlled (35%, 6/17), lack of experience with tiopronin (24%, 4/17), and preference for D-penicillamine (6%, 1/17). As this was a multiple-response question, percentages do not sum to 100%. Notably, none of the participants cited concerns about adverse events as a reason for not prescribing tiopronin.

Start and Stop Criteria

The most frequently reported indications for starting treatment with cystine-binding medication were an increased stone burden observed on imaging (73%), elevated urinary free cystine levels (63%), and the occurrence of symptomatic renal stone episodes (40%). Fewer physicians considered low cystine solubility in supersaturation testing (23%), the presence of crystalluria (10%), or a new diagnosis of cystinuria (7.5%) as indications for initiating therapy.

In patients with poor lifestyle adherence (e.g., low fluid intake or high salt intake), 70% of physicians indicated that they would consider initiating cystine-binding medication. In the setting of suboptimal urine alkalinization (e.g., urine pH <7.0), 63% would consider cystine-binding medication whereas 35% reported that they would not initiate therapy under these conditions.

The opinion about medicine discontinuation varied. While 18% of physicians reported that they consider stopping cystine-binding medication on every visit to the outpatient clinic, 15% do so very often. Most physicians only sometimes (33%), rarely (25%), or never (10%) consider medicine withdrawal. We observed no association between the restricted accessibility of medication and the physicians’ willingness to discontinue therapy (p = 0.91 for tiopronin; p = 0.86 for D-penicillamine).

Methods of Prescribing

Median starting doses for tiopronin and D-penicillamine in pediatric patients were 12.5 mg/kg (IQR: 10–15) and 20 mg/kg (IQR: 10–20), respectively. Corresponding maximum daily doses reached 30 mg/kg (IQR: 20–45) for tiopronin and 30 mg/kg (IQR: 22.5–30.5) for D-penicillamine. Among adult patients, the median starting dose was 700 mg for both agents, with IQRs of 575–800 mg for tiopronin and 300–1,000 mg for D-penicillamine. The reported maximum daily dose in adults was 1,200 mg (IQR: 925–1,525) for tiopronin and 1,100 mg (IQR: 1,000–1,650) for D-penicillamine.

Tiopronin and D-penicillamine were both administered a median of 3 times per day (IQR: 2–3). A minority of physicians reported time-specific dosing adjustments: 26% indicated using a higher bedtime dose for tiopronin, and 17% did so for D-penicillamine. Among physicians who reported a preferred starting dose [11], 67% of adult tiopronin doses, 40% of pediatric tiopronin doses, 78% of adult D-penicillamine doses, and 55% of pediatric D-penicillamine doses were within published starting dose recommendations.

Adverse Effects

Among the 18 physicians who prescribed D-penicillamine and the 23 physicians who prescribed tiopronin, the observed adverse event profile differed notably. For D-penicillamine, the most frequently reported adverse effects included gastrointestinal discomfort (22%), skin problems (18%), and hematological abnormalities (15%). Proteinuria, liver dysfunction, and nephrotic syndrome were each reported by ≤10% of respondents. In contrast, proteinuria was the most frequently reported adverse effect for tiopronin, cited by 33% of physicians, followed by nephrotic syndrome (23%). Other events included skin problems (15%), hematological abnormalities (10%), gastrointestinal discomfort (9%). Liver dysfunction was rarely reported (2.5%).

For tiopronin, 44% of physicians reported no complaints related to smell, while 52% indicated that at least some patients had expressed such concerns. With regard to swallowing difficulty, 39% of respondents reported no complaints, while 61% noted that patients had experienced difficulty swallowing. For D-penicillamine, 56% of physicians reported no patient complaints regarding pill odor, while 44% reported at least some complaints. Similarly, 56% of respondents reported no complaints related to swallowing difficulty, while 44% indicated some level of difficulty.

Discussion

We had hypothesized that cystine-binding medications would be limited in accessibility and that availability would differ substantially across European countries. Our survey confirms this hypothesis: tiopronin was inaccessible to 30% of physicians, more than 80% experienced supply-related problems, and considerable variation existed both between and within countries. These findings suggest that a substantial proportion of patients with cystinuria in Europe receive suboptimal treatment.

This survey reveals that cystine-binding medication is poorly accessible for the treatment of cystinuria across Europe. Considering their potential clinical benefit for the treatment of cystinuria that is refractory to lifestyle modifications and alkaline supplementation, the accessibility should be improved [1, 9, 12]. Nevertheless, lifestyle modification and urine alkalinization remain the cornerstone of cystinuria treatment.

The drug of choice of most physicians treating cystinuria was tiopronin. This preference may be caused by the lower incidence of skin problems and hematological abnormalities, although tiopronin is more often associated with proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome [8, 13, 14]. A similar side effect profile was found in this study. Considering the relatively high incidence of adverse effects, clinicians should balance the benefits and potential drawbacks of cystine-biding medication for every patient, even when all medication is readily available for treatment. More than 80% of the physicians experienced problems with the accessibility of tiopronin. Whereas 30% of the physicians had no access at all, others described limited access because of drug costs or prescriptions that were not always filled. This highlights the very vulnerable supply and high drug costs potentially related to the existence of only a single Marketing Authorization Holder [10, 15–17]. The accessibility of D-penicillamine was slightly better, though 61% of physicians also reported issues with accessibility.

We observed important differences across Europe. In Italy, all physicians reported having access to tiopronin with 75% of the prescription being successfully filled while physicians from Greece, Lithuania, and the United Kingdom had no access. The accessibility of tiopronin was also inconsistent within countries. Some physicians from Belgium, France, The Netherlands, Germany, Portugal, and Spain were able to prescribe tiopronin whereas other colleagues from the same country reported accessibility problems. This discrepancy within countries could represent the complicated logistics that need to be dealt with when importing cystine-binding medication from other countries. Over the past decade, these procedures have often changed with varying suppliers of tiopronin. These time-consuming procedures also put a high burden on treating physicians and navigating them likely requires a good understanding of the current prescribing logistics – which may be lacking among those treating only a few patients. We, indeed, observed that the percentage of patients who were treated with cystine-binding medication was associated with the number of patients that were being treated by a single physician. However, specific patient populations in larger centers could also play a role. These findings underscore the urgent need for coordinated efforts to ensure equitable access to cystine-binding medication across Europe.

Whereas the limited accessibility of tiopronin has been briefly noted in previous literature, few studies have systematically quantified the extent of this problem [14, 18]. The inconsistent accessibility of cystine-binding medication poses significant challenges to clinical management as physicians are compelled to delay or cancel therapy initiation [18]. For some patients, treatment must be interrupted or altered depending on what is locally accessible, which may complicate long-term adherence and therapeutic outcomes and quality of life. Such instability increases the risk of stone recurrence and, over time, may accelerate the development of chronic kidney disease [3, 6].

The limited accessibility of cystine-binding medication is closely linked to market structure. The supply of tiopronin in most European countries relies on a single producer, whereas D-penicillamine is available from just a few manufacturers. For many orphan medicines, the absence of generics even after patent expiry, has allowed prices to remain high [17, 19, 20]. The limited availability of cystine-binding medicines exemplifies a systemic issue in orphan drug markets, where inadequate economic returns fail to motivate competitive entry. As a result, the burden of treatment access is shifted onto healthcare providers and patients. In cases of market failure, academia-driven pharmaceutical development could offer a way forward [21–24].

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was relatively small and primarily drawn from physicians affiliated with the European Reference Network for Rare Kidney Diseases (ERKNet), a network with a large proportion of pediatric centers, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the survey responses reflect physician-reported perceptions and experiences, which are inherently subject to recall bias and reporting bias. Finally, the accessibility and prescribing patterns reported here may not reflect the situation in non-European regions, where regulatory environments and healthcare infrastructures differ substantially.

This study highlights the urgent need to improve access to essential medications for rare diseases such as cystinuria. The documented barriers, encompassing both supply chain inconsistencies and patient tolerability concerns, underscore structural inadequacies that require coordinated action. In conclusion, cystine-binding medications remain poorly accessible for the treatment of cystinuria across Europe, reflecting systemic shortcomings in orphan drug markets. Future efforts should focus on strengthening supply networks, promoting socially accountable pharmacotherapy development, and ensuring equitable, sustainable and affordable access across all healthcare systems.
