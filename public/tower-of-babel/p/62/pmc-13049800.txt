Hand movements prediction via fNIRS and AI algorithms
Authors: Samira Jafari, Hamid Sharini, Maziar Jalalvandi, Amir Hossein Nabizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand movement prediction enables the creation of personalized rehabilitation programs that align with individual neural patterns. It is particularly beneficial for patients recovering from strokes or other motor impairments, allowing therapists to adjust treatments in real time based on brain activity feedback, leading to more effective patient care. To this end, this study aims at predicting hand movements using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) signals and artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms.MethodsWe employed fNIRS and collect cortical activity associated with hand movements and utilized machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict the hand movement. For the prediction task, various Machine Learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machines with polynomial (SVM Poly) and radial basis function (SVM RBF) kernels, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), Neural Networks (NN), Gradient Boosting (GB), and AdaBoost, were used.ResultsThe performance of the RF algorithm was notably superior, demonstrating high accuracy in predicting hand movements. In contrast, LR, SVM (Poly), SVM (RBF), GNB, and NN algorithms exhibited poor performance, while kNN, DT, GB, and AdaBoost algorithms showed moderate effectiveness. In addition, the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves indicated that the RF algorithm had significantly larger areas under the curve (AUC) compared to other algorithms.ConclusionThe integration of fNIRS with AI improves hand movement prediction, suggesting advancements in rehabilitation and personalized medicine. This study emphasizes the potential of these algorithms. More research is required to expand their applications across various clinical settings.Keywords: fNIRS, Artificial intelligence, Hand movements, Machine learning, Neuroscience

Introduction

Hand is a key to independence, creativity, and self-expression. From delicate surgical procedures to dramatic gestures, the hand’s versatility is truly awe-inspiring [1, 2]. However, for individuals with hand movement disabilities, daily tasks can become challenging. Disabilities can be due to various reasons including injuries, diseases, or congenital conditions, and can have a profound impact on daily life [3, 4]. Therefore, predicting hand movements is crucial for developing innovative solutions helping individuals having movement disabilities to regain control and autonomy.

Brain–computer interface (BCI) is a revolutionary technology that enables individuals with severe motor impairments, including those affecting hand movement, to communicate and interact with their environment [5, 6]. This innovative technology allows individuals who are unable to control their muscles to convey their thoughts and intentions in a more accessible and effective way [7]. The BCI uses an advanced technology to analyze brain signals and decode the patterns associated with thinking and mental processes. It allows to identify specific thoughts and intentions, enabling people to control devices and interact with their surroundings in a more natural and intuitive way [8].

Researchers employ various techniques to capture brain signals. While some methods involve direct neural interface technologies, such as cortical implants and microelectrodes, others employ non-invasive approaches like functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) [9], Electroencephalography (EEG) [10], and Magnetoencephalography (MEG) [11]. Among these, fNIRS has gained popularity in recent years as a non-invasive neuroimaging technique for investigating neural activity related to hand movements [12]. It uses near-infrared light to measure changes in hemoglobin concentrations in the cerebral cortex, which are correlated with neural activity [13–15]. It allows researchers to non-invasively monitor brain activity in real-time, providing valuable insights into the neural mechanisms underlying motor control.

EEG is popular for brain-computer interfaces due to its millisecond temporal resolution, essential for fast neural dynamics. However, it has poor spatial resolution and is prone to movement artifacts, complicating tasks involving physical motion like hand movements [16]. MEG offers high temporal and spatial resolution for mapping neural activity but is costly, immobile, and needs magnetically shielded environments, limiting its use in real-world and clinical settings, particularly in rehabilitation where portability and comfort are essential [17]. fMRI provides high spatial resolution and whole-brain coverage for precise localization of neural activity. However, its low temporal resolution, immobility, and requirement for participants to remain still limit its use in dynamic motor tasks and real-time monitoring, which are crucial for rehabilitation and BCI applications [18].

fNIRS offers a balanced alternative, with good spatial resolution in cortical areas and high movement tolerance, despite lower temporal resolution than EEG and limited spatial coverage compared to fMRI. Its portability, cost-effectiveness, and participant comfort allow for experiments in natural settings, making it especially useful for studies on repetitive hand movements where flexibility and ecological validity are crucial. fNIRS is more reliable than EEG for motor tasks due to its reduced sensitivity to electrical noise and motion artifacts. When combined with machine learning, fNIRS effectively decodes motor intentions and classifies movement-related brain activity. While EEG offers high temporal resolution essential for fast neural dynamics, it has poor spatial resolution and is prone to movement artifacts, complicating tasks involving physical motion [18, 19].

Lately, Artificial Intelligence (AI) algorithms have been increasingly applied to fNIRS data to analyze and predict brain activity [20]. Machine Learning (ML) is a subset of AI that involves training algorithms to recognize patterns in data and make predictions or take actions based on that data [21, 22]. These algorithms can be trained on large datasets of fNIRS to learn patterns and relationships between neural activity and behavioral outputs [23]. By that, there is a possibility for developing predictive algorithms of hand movement that can be used in a wide range of applications, from rehabilitation therapy to gaming and robotics [24].

Several studies have been conducted in this field. For instance, H. Khan et al. used fNIRS with machine learning to classify finger movements. They recorded brain activity while participants performed finger movements and decoded the signals with ML and deep learning (DL) algorithms, including Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Multinomial Logistic Regression (MNLR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random forest (RF), and A new DL-based model named “Hemo-Net”. Hemo-Net has demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving the accuracy of 76% [25]. In a study published in 2022, researchers created a BCI method to recognize intentions of transhumeral amputees. The interface used fNIRS to collect data from 15 healthy individuals and 3 amputees performing 6 arm movements. Artificial Neural Network (ANN) was applied to classify the actions, achieving an accuracy rate of 78% [26]. In another study, researchers focused on the prefrontal region of the brain. Here, participants performed voluntary and imagery movements, such as moving or imagining moving their hands. ML algorithms including LDA, Support Vector Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), ANN, and the results of a two-class classification showed that SVM and ANN algorithms achieved an accuracy of nearly 90%, outperforming other algorithms [27]. In another study [28], fNIRS signals were used to classify four types of brain activities: left-hand movement, right-hand movement, foot movement, and imagery movement. The fNIRS signals were used to train a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), LDA, and SVM algorithms to classify the brain activities. Here, the CNN-based method achieved an accuracy of 86.24% on average. In another study [29], the aim was to realize upper extremity rehabilitation training using the fNIRS-BCI system. In this study, conducted by C Li et al., fifty participants performed various motor tasks using their upper limbs, such as grasping and releasing. Physiological signals and movement cues were recorded using near-infrared technology. ML algorithms like Gradient Boosting (GB) and RF were tested to predict these movements. The RF classifier was chosen as the best option, achieving 94.4% recognition accuracy offline.

In addition, researchers have explored the potential of combining fNIRS signals with artificial intelligence algorithms to predict the various body parts movement, yielding promising results. A study conducted by H Hamid et al. evaluated the classification performance of a fNIRS-based BCI system for gait rehabilitation using Deep Neural Networks (DNN). fNIRS signals were collected from the left cerebral hemisphere of 9 participants during walking and resting tasks. This study used three algorithms: CNN, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM), and tested their classification accuracy. The results showed that CNN outperformed the rest of algorithms having an average classification accuracy of 88.50% [30]. Also, in a study conducted by RA Khan et al., a novel, cutting-edge system utilizing near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and BCIs was introduced, enabling individuals with locomotive impairments to control prosthetic limbs and receive targeted rehabilitation treatment. For that, the brain signals related to intentional walking and restful activities were recorded from the dominant primary motor region of the cerebral cortex in nine individuals. A diverse range of classification algorithms were evaluated, including kNN, QDA, LDA, Naive Bayes Classifier (NBC), and SVM. The results showed that SVM was the best algorithm with an accuracy of 86.7% [31]. A study that conducted by SB Lee et al. examined motion artifacts in fNIRS data. 28 healthy individuals participated in the study, undergoing data collection with various motion-induced signal contamination (specifically, saccadic eye movements, head rotations, ocular pursuit movements, dental compression, and orofacial gestures). Four algorithms were used to classify the motion artifacts: SVM, radial basis function [32], GB decision tree, and Shrinkage regularized linear discriminant analysis (SRLDA) [33]. The best-performing algorithm was SRLDA, achieving an accuracy of 90% [34].

Breaking new ground in the field of AI-powered hand movement prediction, our study takes a groundbreaking approach by comprehensively comparing the performance of various AI algorithms using fNIRS signals. Unlike previous studies, which focused on isolated aspects of this topic, our research provides a comprehensive evaluation of the algorithms’ performance across multiple datasets and metrics. We took a bold step by using two distinct datasets - one featuring active task (A participant moved his/her wrist), and another featuring passive task (A third person moved the participant’s wrist). This innovative approach allows us to gain a deeper understanding of the AI algorithms’ capabilities. In contrast to previous studies, which often relied on a single metric, we employed multiple evaluation metrics to provide a detailed and multi-faceted assessment of the algorithms’ performance. We examined accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, kappa, and area under the ROC curve giving us a rich understanding of each algorithm’s strengths and weaknesses. To train our AI algorithms and assess their performance, we extracted fNIRS signals from brain images. Our AI algorithms includes RF, Logistic Regression (LR), kNN, SVM, Decision Tree (DT), GB, Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), and Neural Network (NN). By leveraging these cutting-edge algorithms, we’re pushing the boundaries of what’s possible in hand movement prediction and opening up new avenues for research and innovation.

Methods

This section outlines the methodology used to generate our predictive algorithms. We discuss the types of data sources, the techniques employed to extract relevant information, and the algorithms implemented for the prediction tasks. Furthermore, we detail the feature reduction strategy employed to eliminate redundant data features and enhance algorithm performance. Finally, we discuss our methods to evaluate the accuracy of the results. A visual representation of our approach is shown in Fig. 1, provides a comprehensive overview of the steps involved in generating our predictive algorithm.

Our research methodology

Data collection

For brain imaging, the researchers recruited 10 right-handed volunteers (with an age range of 25 to 40 years old) who were healthy, had no neurological or psychosocial issues, and were not taking drugs that could affect the nervous system. The participants gave written consent and were aware of all aspects of the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, with approval number IR.KMU.REC.1403.455.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy recording

Data were gathered using a 48-channel fNIRS MR-compatible device (OxyMon fNIRS from Artinis), which operates at two wavelengths: 855 nm and 765 nm. It consisted of eight transmitters and eight infrared light receivers, which together generated a total of 20 channels. The transmitter-receiver distance was set at 30 millimeters, and the sampling frequency was calibrated at 10 Hz. Figure 2 illustrates the proper placement of optodes (receivers and transmitters) on individuals’ skulls.

Location of optodes and channels designed on head skull for signal picking up (orange color represents transmitter, blue color represents receiver and yellow color represents fNIRS channels)

During the recording of fNIRS signals, participants were instructed to complete two types of tasks, active and passive ones, which are described in Section “Task paradigm” and “Active task”.

Task paradigm

Participants were required to adhere to a wrist motion pattern while remaining completely still. The participants were instructed to observe the wrist pattern as per the guidelines displayed on the monitor’s screen and to follow the commands for up, down, left, and right movements. Participants received training to swiftly move their wrists in specified directions and maintain that position until the center box appeared. The duration for each direction was set at 4 s (s), while the center displayed a random interval of 3–5 s. Consequently, each step-tracking movement varied in length, lasting randomly between 5 and 7 s, followed by a 4-s rest after completing all ten movements. Throughout this assessment, each direction was referenced 20 times. In total, each stage of the test lasted 586 s, and during this period, each angle was randomly cued 20 times.

To evaluate brain activity, we focused on six key Brodmann areas: the primary somatosensory cortex (areas 3, 1, and 2), the primary motor cortex (area 4), the somatosensory association cortex (area 5), and the premotor and supplementary motor cortices (area 6).

Channels 1, 4, 7, 10, 11, 14, and 17 are associated with Brodmann area 4 and located around positions C5, C3, CZ/C1, CZ/C2, and C4. Channels 2, 5, 8, 12, 15, 18, 19 and 20 are associated with Brodmann area 6 and located around positions FC5, FC3, FC1, FC2, FC4, FC6, and CP6. Channels 3, 6, 9, 13, and 16 are associated with Brodmann areas 1–3 and 1,2 and located around positions CP5, CP3, CP1, and CP2, and CP4. Channel 20 is associated with Brodmann areas 4 and 6 and located around position C6.

In our study, the experimental paradigm for active and passive tasks was identical. The only difference lies in how the tasks were executed:

1-In the active condition: The participant personally performs the tasks

1-In the active condition: The participant personally performs the tasks

2-In the passive condition: The experimenter performs the same tasks while the participant observes

2-In the passive condition: The experimenter performs the same tasks while the participant observes

This approach allows us to identify subtle differences in data features between active and passive conditions while keeping other variables constant.

Active task

During the experiment, participants were asked to perform a step-tracking task by moving their right wrist in a series of controlled movements. The sequence consisted of four horizontal and vertical movements, which required extension, flexion, radial deviation, and ulnar deviation. To facilitate this process, participants were presented with a visual cue in the form of an image, which displayed the direction of movement. The image was randomly selected from a set of two directions, vertical (up, down) and horizontal (left, right) (Fig. 2).

Passive task

Participants were seated comfortably and instructed to remain stationary, with their torso and upper body motionless. The experiment included a hand-tracking task in which participants were instructed to move their right wrist in a manner similar to the active task (Fig. 3). Throughout the task, a random direction was selected and an operator moved the wrist of participants to that direction.

Data pre-process

Data preprocessing is essential in machine learning to ensure data quality and accuracy. It involves cleaning and refining raw data to eliminate inaccuracies, missing values, and inconsistencies, which is crucial for building reliable algorithms and insights [35].

The datasets contained no missing or outlier data, and we employed the Min-Max method [36]to normalize our continuous features, ensuring a consistent and comparable range for all variables. These datasets consisted of 60 features, including deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR), oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), and their difference of 20 channels.

Feature selection method

Signals extracted from brain images collected by the fNIRS method are considered input features for AI algorithms. These signals include HbR, HbO, and their difference. By leveraging feature selection, we can pinpoint the most influential and meaningful data variables, while eliminating the superfluous and unimportant ones. This approach offers several advantages, including minimizing the risk of algorithm overfitting [37], clarifying the predictive insights [38], expediting the training process, reducing storage requirements, and mitigating the noise introduced by redundant and irrelevant attributes [39]. In order to identify the most relevant features, we used meta-transformer method that works based on importance weights [40]. To conduct feature selection using a meta-transform approach with a RF model, we followed these steps: first, we employed the RF algorithm to compute the importance scores of each feature. Next, we selected the most relevant features by applying a threshold based on the mean importance score. Finally, the Meta-Transform method combined the feature importance-based selection with feature transformation techniques, such as scaling, to enhance feature engineering. By leveraging the pre-trained algorithm’s insights, we used the meta-transformer method to identify the most influential features, thereby streamlining our analysis and reducing noise in order to assign importance weights.

In this study, we examined a total of 60 features, identifying 26 as significant for the active task and 21 for the passive task using the meta-transform method. These features include HbO- channel (ch) 1’, HbR-ch1’, ‘Sum-ch1’, ‘Hbo-ch2’, ‘Hbr-ch2’, ‘Sum-ch2’, ‘Hbo-ch4’, ‘Hbr-ch4’, ‘Sum-ch4’, ‘Hbo-ch6’, ‘Hbr-ch6’, ‘Sum-ch6’, ‘Hbr-ch7’, ‘Sum-ch7’, ‘Hbo-ch9’, ‘Hbr-ch9’, ‘Sum-ch9’, ‘Hbr-ch10’, ‘Sum-ch10’, ‘Sum-ch11’, ‘Hbo-ch16’, ‘Sum-ch16’, ‘Sum-ch19’, ‘Hbo-ch20’, ‘Hbr-ch20’, ‘Sum-ch20’ for active task and ‘Hbo-ch1’, ‘Hbr-ch1’, ‘Sum-ch1’, ‘Sum-ch2’, ‘Hbo-ch4’, ‘Hbr-ch4’, ‘Sum-ch4’, ‘Hbo-ch6’, ‘Hbr-ch6’, ‘Sum-ch6’, ‘Hbo-ch9’, ‘Hbr-ch9’, ‘Sum-ch9’, ‘Sum-ch10’, ‘Sum-ch11’, ‘Hbr-ch15’, ‘Hbo-ch16’, ‘Sum-ch16’, ‘Hbo-ch20’, ‘Hbr-ch20’, ‘Sum-ch20’ for passive task.

Prediction algorithms

For hand movement prediction, we use the regular form of a set of AI algorithms including Random Forest (RF) [41], Decision Tree (DT) [42], k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) [43], Logistic Regression (LR) [44], Support Vector Machines (SVM) [45] with polynomial and radial basis function kernels, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) [46], Neural Networks (NN) [47], Gradient Boosting (GB) [48], and Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) [49]. for all algorithms, the exact proportions used for the training and testing sets were 75% and 25%, respectively. Also, AI algorithms were executed using Python software within the Anaconda environment.

All the mentioned algorithms are detailed below.

Random Forest (RF)

RF is a supervised learning algorithm that harnesses the strengths of multiple decision trees to create a more accurate and robust algorithm. This ensemble learning method is widely used in machine learning and data science, and its effectiveness stems from its use of bootstrap sampling to select random samples from training data. This technique ensures that each tree is trained on a unique subset of the data, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting [50]. RF offers several benefits, including high accuracy, the ability to algorithm complex interactions between predictor variables, handling of datasets with numerous features, reduced overfitting, parallel computation capabilities, and robustness to noise and outliers [51–53].

Logistic Regression (LR)

LR is a type of supervised learning algorithm that utilizes a logistic function to establish a statistical relationship between input variables and output predictions. The algorithm produces a probability output, ranging from 0 to 1, indicating the likelihood of a specific outcome or event occurring [54]. It is often used for binary classification problems, such as predicting customer churn or identifying spam emails [55], and is known for its simplicity and interpretability [56], efficiency, less prone to overfitting, and robust to noise [57].

k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)

kNN, which is commonly used for both classification and regression tasks, is a supervised learning algorithm that classifies new data points by identifying the most similar training data points [43]. It works by calculating the distance between the new data point and each training data point, and then selecting the K nearest neighbors. KNN then classifies the new data point based on the majority vote of the K nearest neighbors. The choice of K can significantly impact the algorithm’s performance [58]. As a simple, non-parametric, and robust algorithm, kNN can effectively handle noisy and high-dimensional data, making it a popular choice for real-world applications that requires minimal feature engineering and hyperparameters tuning [59, 60].

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

SVM is a supervised learning algorithm that separates data points by creating a hyperplane. It aims to find the optimal hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes, ensuring good generalization performance [61]. SVM is able to find the best separating hyperplane in a high-dimensional space, making it highly effective in handling complex classification problems, especially those with non-linear relationships between features [62]. SVM algorithm presents several benefits, such as its effectiveness in high-dimensional spaces, resilience to overfitting, ability to handle noisy data, proficiency with limited datasets, and capability to work well with non-linear data [63]. In this study, an SVM with polynomial (SVM Poly) and radial basis function (SVM RBF) kernels was used to the prediction of hand movements.

Decision Tree (DT)

DT is a machine learning algorithm that uses a tree-like structure to classify data by recursively dividing the data into smaller subsets based on the most relevant features [64]. The algorithm works by splitting the data into child nodes based on the values of a feature, and then recursively applying this process until a stopping criterion is reached. This process ultimately leads to a decision being made by traversing the tree from the root node to a leaf node, where the class label associated with that leaf node is assigned to the input data [42]. Decision Trees are known for their simplicity, interpretability, and ability to handle complex, non-linear relationships between features [65, 66].

Gradient Boosting (GB)

GB is a cutting-edge machine learning algorithm that has revolutionized the field of predictive modeling [67]. By combining multiple weak algorithms, this algorithm creates a strong, highly accurate predictive algorithm that can tackle complex problems [68]. At its core, GB works by iteratively fitting a new algorithm to the residuals of the previous algorithm, and incorporating each algorithm into the final prediction [69]. This iterative process continues until convergence is reached, resulting in a highly accurate algorithm that can deliver unparalleled results [70].

Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost)

Like GB algorithm, AdaBoost is also a machine learning algorithm that combines multiple weak algorithms to create a robust algorithm. Through an iterative process, each algorithm is trained on the training data, with the weights of the samples tuned based on how well each algorithm performs. The final algorithm is a weighted combination of the individual algorithms, with the most accurate algorithms having greater influence [49]. This algorithm has several advantages, including its ability to handle complex datasets, improve algorithm accuracy, and reduce overfitting, as well as its ease of implementation and scalability to large datasets [71, 72].

Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB)

GNB algorithm is a probabilistic classification algorithm that assumes each feature follows a normal distribution and is independent of the others. It computes the probability of each feature given a specific class label, and then combines these probabilities to make a predictive classification [46]. GNB has advantages in terms of simplicity and ease of implementation, as it is a straightforward extension of the traditional Naive Bayes algorithm to handle continuous data [73, 74]. Additionally, GNB is robust to outliers and can handle high-dimensional data, making it a popular choice for many real-world applications [75].

Neural Network (NN)

NN is a computational algorithm inspired by the human brain, composed of interconnected nodes (neurons) that process and transmit information. It is trained on data to learn patterns and relationships, allowing it to make predictions, classify inputs, or generate outputs. It is organized into layers, with each layer processing information in a hierarchical manner [76]. NN has numerous advantages, including its ability to learn complex patterns, recognize and classify data with high accuracy, and adapt to new information, making it a powerful tool for solving a wide range of problems in various fields [77, 78].

Also, random search cross-validation (RandomizedSearchCV) was used to hyperparameter tuning that is a powerful technique for optimizing the hyperparameters of a machine learning model.

Evaluation

The accurate prediction of hand movement is essential, as it ensures the algorithm’s reliability in interpreting neural data and effectively translating it into precise, actionable predictions of hand gestures. To evaluate the efficacy of our algorithms, we employed the confusion matrix, which provides a comprehensive assessment of their performance. We then calculated various metrics, including accuracy (ACC), precision (PRE), recall (RE), F1-score, kappa, and area under the ROC curve (AUC) to gain a deeper understanding of their strengths and weaknesses. ACC measures the ratio of true results (true positives and true negatives) to total cases, providing an overall view of a model’s effectiveness. PRE indicates the proportion of true positives among all positive predictions, while RE (sensitivity) measures true positives relative to all actual positives, emphasizing the model’s identification capability. The F1-score combines precision and recall for a balanced evaluation, especially useful with uneven class distributions. Kappa assesses agreement between raters, with a value of 1 indicating perfect agreement and 0 indicating random chance. The AUC evaluates model performance by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate. The high value of these metrics indicate that the opted algorithm aligns well with the data, making it a more accurate representation of the underlying relationships.

Discussion

Recent studies have explored various neuroimaging methods for hand movement prediction, including EEG, fMRI, and fNIRS. fNIRS has emerged as a promising portable brain scanning technique, capable of classifying hand motion with over 80% accuracy [12]. Comparisons between EEG and fNIRS have shown that fNIRS can achieve equivalent or superior classification accuracies in some cases, while a hybrid approach combining both modalities may improve robustness [79]. The non-invasive and portable nature of EEG-fNIRS systems makes them suitable for naturalistic experimental settings and brain-computer interface applications. These methods offer unique advantages in terms of temporal and spatial resolution, with EEG providing high temporal resolution and fNIRS offering better spatial information [8].

fNIRS was selected for its portability, cost-effectiveness, and robustness in naturalistic environments with minimal susceptibility to motion artifacts, especially during wrist movements. Additionally, fNIRS provides reliable spatial localization for cortical regions involved in motor control, which was crucial for distinguishing between active and passive wrist tasks. These properties made it a suitable choice for developing an initial predictive framework that could be deployed in clinical and rehabilitation settings with minimal setup complexity [80]. Although EEG offers superior temporal resolution, integrating EEG and fNIRS from the beginning would have significantly increased the system’s technical complexity and participant burden. Our primary goal was to validate the feasibility of using fNIRS alone for hand movement prediction. Having demonstrated the promising performance of fNIRS, especially in combination with AI algorithms, future work may explore the added value of EEG-fNIRS hybrid systems. Such integration could further enhance performance by leveraging the complementary strengths of both modalities, particularly for applications requiring both high temporal and spatial resolution [81].

However, each technique has its strengths and limitations, and the selection of a neuroimaging modality should align with the specific demands of the study—such as the need for portability, tolerance to motion, and suitability for real-time motor task analysis. In this regard, fNIRS offers a favorable balance between spatial resolution and experimental flexibility, making it especially well-suited for movement-related research in naturalistic or clinical environments [82].

Recent studies have highlighted the potential of combining functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) with artificial intelligence (AI) to predict hand movements. fNIRS is a non-invasive technique that measures changes in blood oxygen levels in the brain, offering insights into neural activity. Its portability and low cost make it an attractive option for brain-computer interface (BCI) applications, particularly in naturalistic settings where participants can move freely. In typical studies, researchers collect brain activity data from participants performing various hand movement tasks, such as active (self-directed) and passive (experimenter-directed) movements. The fNIRS technology focuses on areas related to motor control, providing valuable data for AI analysis [83].

Implications for rehabilitation

The potential applications of this technology in rehabilitation are significant. By accurately predicting hand movements, we can create personalized rehabilitation programs that align with individual neural patterns. For patients recovering from strokes or other motor impairments, this predictive ability can facilitate more effective interventions, enabling therapists to modify treatment protocols based on real-time feedback from the patient’s brain activity. This tailored approach has the potential to greatly improve recovery outcomes and enhance the quality of life for those with motor deficits. Our findings in predicting (detecting) hand movements in active and passive conditions using fNIRS and AI algorithms have significant potential for clinical applications and practical rehabilitation tools:

Stroke rehabilitation: This technology can be used to design personalized rehabilitation programs for stroke patients. By accurately detecting movement intention, even in individuals unable to move physically, mental and physical exercises can be optimized.

Brain-computer interfaces: Our findings can contribute to the development of more advanced brain-computer interfaces, enabling individuals with severe motor disabilities to control assistive devices.

Neural prosthetics: This technology can be used in designing advanced prosthetics directly controlled by brain signals, helping to improve performance and more natural control of artificial limbs.

Assessment and monitoring of rehabilitation progress: Using this method, specialists can more accurately evaluate patients’ progress throughout the rehabilitation period and adjust treatment plans based on improved brain function.

Treatment of movement disorders: This technology can be used in early diagnosis and treatment of movement disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, providing insights into brain activity patterns before physical symptoms appear.

Challenges and Limitations

Despite its promising potential, this study has several limitations that may impact the accuracy and generalizability of the results. The lower spatial resolution of fNIRS compared to fMRI [87]and PET [88]restricts precise brain activity localization, which could affect the reliability of the findings. Additionally, the limited availability of diverse, high-quality datasets poses a challenge for training AI models, potentially introducing biases and reducing generalizability across different demographic groups. Inter-subject variability in brain anatomy and physiological responses further contributes to noise in predictions, highlighting the need for adaptive algorithms that can account for these differences.

While our results demonstrate promising accuracy, further improvements can be achieved through hybrid machine learning techniques and larger datasets. However, implementing more complex models comes with increased computational costs, which may limit real-time applications. Ethical and practical considerations, such as data privacy and bias in AI-driven predictions, should also be addressed to ensure fairness and transparency. Addressing these limitations in future research will be crucial for improving the robustness and applicability of fNIRS-based AI models.

Future directions

Future research should examine the long-term effects of integrating fNIRS and AI in rehabilitation settings. Studying how ongoing exposure to predictive feedback impacts motor recovery over time could yield valuable insights into the effectiveness of this approach. Furthermore, expanding research to encompass diverse populations, various neurological conditions, and different levels of motor impairment could improve our understanding of the generalizability of these predictive algorithms. Additionally, collaboration among neuroscientists, AI researchers, and rehabilitation specialists is essential. These partnerships can promote innovation and facilitate the creation of more advanced algorithms tailored to specific motor tasks, ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The prediction of hand movements using fNIRS and AI algorithms is an exciting research area with the potential to revolutionize rehabilitation and human-computer interaction. In this study, we predicted hand movements using the fNIRS technique and AI algorithms for two types of movements, passive and active. The evaluation results showed that the RF outperformed other algorithms in terms of accuracy. Despite the limitations of our study, the integration of these technologies offers innovative solutions that could enhance outcomes for individuals with motor impairments, paving the way for a new era of personalized medicine and intuitive technology. Further exploration in this interdisciplinary field using diverse, extensive, and high quality datasets and via robust AI algorithms will provide valuable insights and advancements in our understanding of the brain and its motor functions.
