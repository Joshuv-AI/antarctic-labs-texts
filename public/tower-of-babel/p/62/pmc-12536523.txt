The rising threat of antibiotic and multidrug resistance in neonatal urinary tract infections
Authors: Mukaddes Kılıç Sağlam, Emine Yanaşoğlu, Nadide Melike Sav, Fatih Kurt, Kenan Kocabay, Emel Akbaş, Mehmet Ali Sungur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectıveUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are one of the significant causes of sepsis in neonates. Currently, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms have become an increasingly prevalent issue in newborns. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and laboratory findings, causative organisms, associated urinary tract anomalies, AMR patterns, and factors influencing the development of MDR in neonatal UTIs.MethodsMicroorganisms in urine cultures obtained through bladder catheterization from neonates were identified, and AMR profiles as well as MDR organisms were determined. Antibiotic susceptibility tests were performed using Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method and VITEK 2 Compact system. The cultures were categorized into two groups: MDR and non-MDR. A retrospective evaluation was conducted to assess the impact of clinical, laboratory, and imaging data on the development of MDR.ResultsData from 51 neonatal UTIs between 2018 and 2024 were evaluated. The most common microorganisms were Esherichia coli (E. Coli) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. Pneumoniae). A total of 75% of the E. coli strains demonstrated resistance to ampicillin. Resistance rates of E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Enterobacter spp. to gentamicin was 25%, 30.7%, and 80%, respectively. Among the microorganisms 55% of E. coli, 100% of Enterobacter spp., 38.5% of K. pneumoniae, and 20% of Enterococcus spp. were MDR. However, no significant correlation was found between MDR and clinical, laboratory, and imaging data. Blood cultures were obtained from all patients prior to the initiation of antibiotic therapy; however, no pathogenic microorganisms were isolated.ConclusionsAMR and MDR in neonatal UTIs appear to be a serious problem. Treatment options in MDR UTIs remain limited, and it is particularly important to research on the pharmacokinetics and safety profiles of antimicrobials in children is essential to expand treatment options for pediatric populations worldwide.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) in neonates can be associated with sepsis and congenital anomalies of the urinary tract. Early recognition and treatment of UTIs are crucial in preventing the development of renal scarring later in life. Escherichia coli (E. coli) is the most prevalent pathogen in neonates, responsible for approximately 80% to 90% of infections in most large-scale studies [1]. In premature infants, E. coli, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, and Klebsiella are the most commonly identified pathogens.

Antibiotics such as ampicillin, gentamicin, cefotaxime, amikacin, meropenem, and vancomycin are commonly used in the treatment of UTIs in neonates. Empirical therapy is typically initiated with a combination of ampicillin and gentamicin. The appropriate selection of antibiotics is crucial, as empirical treatment, initiated prior to obtaining culture results, has been shown to reduce both mortality and morbidity. However, AMR and the emergence of MDR strains are becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide [2, 3].

Therefore, regular monitoring of AMR is essential to minimize the use of inappropriate therapies in the treatment of UTIs. The existing literature on MDR UTIs in neonates is limited, with most studies being single-center investigations focused primarily on extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales, particularly E. coli. Consequently, the aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical and laboratory findings, the frequency of causative microorganisms, the presence of concomitant urinary tract anomalies, AMR and MDR status, as well as the factors influencing the development of MDR in neonates diagnosed with UTIs.

Materials and methods

In this study, data from 51 neonates diagnosed with UTI and followed up in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit of Düzce University Hospital between 2018 and 2024 were retrospectively evaluated. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Date: 01.07.2024, number: 2024/139). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents after being informed about the purpose and procedures of the study. Preterm infants born at < 35 weeks of gestation, those with a history of previous hospital admissions, major surgical interventions, multiple pathogens in urine cultures, or underlying systemic diseases were excluded from the study. After urine collection, samples were centrifuged, and pyuria (presence of ≥ 5 cells/mm³ in a microscopic examination at 40x magnification), nitrite positivity, and the number of leukocytes and erythrocytes in the urine were evaluated. We made the diagnosis of UTI if ≥ 50.000 CFU/ml of microorganisms were cultured from urine catheter samples [4]. Urine samples sent to the Microbiology Laboratory were inoculated onto blood agar and eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) in quantitative amounts and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h in an aerobic environment. Any bacteria suspected to be the causative agents were identified using both conventional methods and the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France). Antibiotic susceptibility tests were performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method and the VITEK 2 Compact system. The results of the antibiotic susceptibility tests were evaluated according to the recommendations of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST).

The frequencies of microorganisms in the cultures of patients with UTI, the relationships between other parameters and the resistance of these microorganisms to antibiotics were analyzed. In ultrasonography (USG) imaging hydronephrosis should be graded according to the Society for Fetal Urology grading system as follows: grade 1, mild dilation of the renal pelvis; grade 2, dilation of the renal pelvis with dilation of a few but not all of the calices [5]. According to the results of the antibiotic susceptibility test; samples were divided into 4 groups: non-resistant, single drug resistant, two drug resistant and MDR. If the bacteria are susceptible to all classes of antibiotics studied by the microbiology laboratory, they are called non-resistant; if they are resistant to single antimicrobial categories, they are defined as single drug resistant; if they are resistant to two antimicrobial categories, they are defined as two drug resistant. MDR was defined as not being susceptible to one or more antimicrobial agents in three or more antimicrobial categories for all microorganisms. For the definition of MDR in S. aureus, all MRSA isolates are considered MDR due to resistance to a key antimicrobial agent [6]. In defining MDR, only acquired antimicrobial resistance was taken into account, while intrinsic resistance was excluded. Enterobacter spp. is intrinsically resistant to ampicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanate, and Klebsiella pneumoniae exhibits natural resistance to ampicillin. Therefore, when classifying these organisms as MDR, antibiotics to which they exhibit intrinsic resistance were not included in the assessment. Cultures were divided into 2 groups as MDR and non-MDR and the effects of clinical, laboratory and imaging data on MDR were retrospectively evaluated.

Stati̇sti̇cal analyses

Statistical analyses were done by IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2013, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality assumption for numerical variables. Two groups were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test, while the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Bonferroni corrected post-hoc test was used to compare three or more groups. Categorical variables were analyzed with Pearson chi-square, Fisher’s exact, or Fisher-Freeman-Halton test according to the expected count and number of groups compared. Descriptive statistics for numerical variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, or median, quartiles, and minimum-maximum, as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Urinary USG was performed at the time of UTIS in all patients. Pathological findings were observed in 20 (39.2%) patients, including 13 (25.5%) with grade 1 hydronephrosis, 4 (7.8%) with bilateral grade 1 hydronephrosis, 2 (3.9%) with bilateral grade 2 hydronephrosis, and 1 (1.9%) with polycystic kidney. The remaining 31 (60.7%) patients had normal findings. In follow-up urinary USG performed one month later, 1 patient still had polycystic kidney, and only 3 patients had grade 1 hydronephrosis. No correlation was found between the presence of pathological findings on urinary USG and gender, presence of symptoms, gestational age, weight for gestational age, elevated CRP, or MDR (p = 0.166; 0.69; 0.163; 0.332; >0.999, respectively). Urinary USG showed pathological findings in 50% of patients with E. coli, 69.2% with K. pneumoniae, and 20% with Enterobacter spp. Renal USG findings were normal in all patients with Enterococcus spp. and other microorganisms. Hydronephrosis was more common with E. coli and K. pneumoniae (p = 0.002).

Discussion

Clinical manifestations of UTIs in newborns are nonspecific and often indistinguishable from those of sepsis. Lethargy, irritability, cyanosis, tachypnea, fever, restlessness, jaundice, decreased urine, red-colored urine, gastrointestinal symptoms such as less vigorous sucking, poor weight gain, and vomiting may be present, or the infection may be asymptomatic. In some newborns, jaundice may be the first and only sign of a UTIs [7, 8].

In our study, the most frequently observed manifestations of UTIs in neonates were jaundice (n = 16; 36.4%) and gastrointestinal symptoms (n = 14; 31.8%). These findings highlight the importance of considering UTIs in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neonatal jaundice. In fact, a study reported in the literature found that among newborns with unexplained hyperbilirubinemia persisting beyond the third postnatal day, the incidence of UTIS was as high as 12.5% [9].

The most common etiological agents of UTIs in neonates include E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, and other gram-negative bacilli (GNB) [10]. In our study, the most frequent isolated bacterium was E. coli 39.2% followed by K. pneumoniae 25.5%, Enterococcus spp. 19.6%. There was no statistically significant difference between the microorganisms isolated in urine cultures and demographic or laboratory parameters. Additionally, E. coli and K. pneumoniae were more frequently identified in the urine cultures of patients with pathological findings on renal ultrasonography. This may be attributed to the presence of various virulence factors in E. coli and K. pneumoniae, such as adhesins, which facilitate bacterial adherence to the uroepithelium, thereby significantly increasing the risk of UTIs, particularly in the presence of urinary tract anomalies [11].

The combination of ampicillin and gentamicin is recommended for the empirical treatment of UTIs in neonates [12]. Antibiotic therapy should ideally be tailored to the local microbiological epidemiology of both the patient and the neonatal unit, as emphasized in recent literature on neonatal sepsis management [13]. UTIs treatment have become increasingly challenging due to the high resistance of common pathogens to frequently prescribed antibiotics, such as aminopenicillins, and the rising global prevalence of MDR organisms [2]. A study conducted in Greece reported that E. coli exhibited lower resistance rates to ampicillin (42.0%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (26.5%), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (12.2%), third-generation cephalosporins (1.7%), nitrofurantoin (2.3%), ciprofloxacin (1.4%), and amikacin (0.9%) [14]. A study conducted in a neonatal intensive care unit in Saudi Arabia found that E. coli exhibited resistance rates of 62% to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, 33% to gentamicin, 33% to ciprofloxacin, 19% to amikacin, and 14% to meropenem [15]. A meta-analysis of 58 studies examining 77,783 E. coli isolates in children aged 0–17 years; OECD countries reported the following resistance rates: 53.4% (range: 46.0% − 60.8%) for ampicillin, 23.6% (range: 13.9% − 32.3%) for trimethoprim, 8.2% (range: 7.9% − 9.6%) for co-amoxiclav, and 2.1% (range: 0.8% − 4.4%) for ciprofloxacin. Nitrofurantoin exhibited the lowest resistance rate at 1.3% (range: 0.8% − 1.7%). Resistance rates were significantly higher in studies conducted in non-OECD countries, with reported rates of 79.8% (range: 73.0% − 87.7%) for ampicillin, 60.3% (range: 40.9% − 79.0%) for co-amoxiclav, 26.8% (range: 11.1% − 43.0%) for ciprofloxacin, and 17.0% (range: 9.8% − 24.2%) for nitrofurantoin [2]. In our study, the antibiotic resistance rates of E. coli were significantly higher compared to those reported in OECD countries and other studies in the literature, but were similar to those observed in non-OECD countries (Table 2). The highest resistance rates were found for ampicillin, while the lowest resistance rates were observed for nitrofurantoin, consistent with global trends.

Bazaid et al. [15] reported resistance rates for K. pneumoniae as follows: 45% to amikacin, 50% to gentamicin, 16% to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, 20% to ciprofloxacin, and 12% to nitrofurantoin. No resistance to meropenem was observed. Consistent with the literature, all Klebsiella strains were susceptible to meropenem. However, our resistance rates were higher (Table 2).

In AMR studies conducted in newborns in Türkiye, very different rates have been given [16]. Although our findings were lower than those reported by some centers, they were higher than the resistance rates observed in most other centers. The fact that our instutution is a tertiary-level university hospital may contribute to these results.

The literature on MDR UTIs in children is limited. In a Brazilian study involving all infants hospitalized in a neonatal intensive care unit, 18.3% were classified as MDR, with 11 (84.6%) being GNB and 2 (15.4%) being gram-positive bacteria (GPB) [17]. The existing literature primarily consists of single-center studies focusing on ESBL, particularly E. coli. The incidence of ESBL varies significantly by geographic region. In a study conducted in Israel involving infants younger than 60 days, ESBL were identified in 5.6% of cases, while gentamicin resistance was observed in 5.7%. No significant association was found between the presence of ESBL and clinical or laboratory findings [18]. Kim et al. reported that 23.4% of first UTIs in patients under 1 year of age were caused by ESBL [19]. In another study, ESBL was observed in 9% of infants younger than 60 days [20]. In our study, 23 patients (45.1%) were classified as MDR, with 91% identified as GNB and 9% as GPB. This rate was higher than the data available in the literature for ESBL positivity alone.

Maternal antibiotic use during the prenatal period is known to increase the risk of ESBL UTIs in the infants [19]. Antibiotics are frequently and unnecessarily prescribed, including during pregnancy. Step therapy is rarely employed when prescribing antibiotics, and broad-spectrum antibiotics are often prescribed without clear indication. Despite the existence of a national action plan on rational antibiotic use, Turkey remains one of the highest consumers of antibiotics globally [21]. Therefore, we saw such a high rate of MDR in the first neonatal UTI.

In our study, no association was observed between MDR organisms and demographic or laboratory parameters. However, Mahony et al. demonstrated that the risk of MDR increased in the presence of underlying genitourinary anomalies [22]. In contrast, a study conducted in Korea found no such association between ESBL and genitourinary anomalies [19]. In our study, no correlation was found between pathological findings on USG and the presence of MDR. This may be attributed to the fact that our study was conducted during the neonatal period, a time when hydronephrosis is often transient.

The course of the disease was uncomplicated in all patients, and no referrals were required. Early initiation of antibiotic treatment appears to be crucial for effective management. These findings align with the literature, which reports an uncomplicated clinical course in infants younger than 60 days [10, 23].

The study had several limitations, including its retrospective nature, a small sample size, short follow- up periods, the evaluation of culture results from a single center, and the lack of assessment of susceptibility to all antibiotic groups. Future studies should focus on larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods.

Conclusi̇on

Ampicillin, gentamicin, amikacin, cefotaxime, and meropenem were the most commonly prescribed antibiotics for neonates hospitalized with a diagnosis of UTIs. E. coli and K. pneumoniae were the most frequent pathogens responsible for UTIs in neonates and exhibited high resistance rates to ampicillin and cefotaxime. Lower resistance rates were observed against nitrofurantoin, amikacin, quinolones, and meropenem. Among the microorganisms, 50% of E. coli, 100% of Enterobacter spp., 38.5% of K. pneumoniae, and 20% of Enterococcus spp. were MDR. Two of the MDR cultures were resistant to all antibiotics except colistin. No correlation was found between MDR and clinical or laboratory findings. The management of MDR UTIs in pediatrics remains a significant challenge. Treatment options in MDR UTIs remain limited, and it is particularly important to research on the pharmacokinetics and safety profiles of antimicrobials in children is essential to expand treatment options for pediatric populations worldwide.
