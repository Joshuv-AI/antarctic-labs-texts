Isolation of Nocardia Otitidiscaviarum from a Case of Cutaneous Nocardiosis: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Bhanu Kanwar, Gautam Neha, Thakur Vibha, Kumar Vipin, Gupta Mudita, Sarma Mrinmoy, Kumar Sanjai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nocardia species are Gram-positive, partially acid-fast, filamentous bacilli commonly found in soil, water, and air. In humans, Nocardia typically causes respiratory, neurological infections and, less frequently, cutaneous infections. While Nocardia brasiliensis is the predominant species implicated in cutaneous nocardiosis, Nocardia otitidiscaviarum—a less commonly isolated species—can cause localized or disseminated infections, even in immunocompetent individuals. Mycetoma is a chronic, disfiguring granulomatous disease caused by various pathogens. Accurate identification of the causative agent is essential for determining the appropriate treatment and prognosis. Culture remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. Here, we report a rare case of actinomycetoma caused by Nocardia otitidiscaviarum in a 41-year-old immunocompetent female. The organism was isolated from purulent discharge collected from draining sinuses on her foot. Identification was achieved through microscopic examination and culture, with species confirmation performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).

Introduction

Mycetoma is a chronic granulomatous and slowly progressive infection characterized by a triad of tumefaction, sinus tract formation and the presence of grains. It is broadly classified into two categories: (i) Eumycetoma, caused by fungi, and (ii) Actinomycetoma, caused by bacteria. The clinical features, progression, prognosis, and treatment of Actinomycetoma differ significantly from those of Eumycetoma.[1] Identification of the causative agent is crucial for selecting the appropriate treatment as well as for predicting the course and prognosis of the disease. Microbiological examination and molecular diagnostic techniques are essential for this purpose.

Among the Actinomycetes group, the Nocardia genus comprises gram-positive, aerobic, facultatively intracellular, rod-shaped bacteria. These organisms are commonly found in soil, water, and air.[2] Nocardia are known to cause respiratory and cutaneous infections in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals. In rare cases, hematogenous spread can lead to involvement of the brain, bones, and joints. Cutaneous manifestations of Nocardia infections may present as pyogenic superficial lesions or deeper infections involving the dermis and subcutaneous tissues. Deep infections can manifest as cellulitis or actinomycetoma.[3]

Nocardia otitidiscaviarum, a species within the Nocardia genus, is a less common cause of primary cutaneous nocardiosis. We present the case of a 41-year-old female clinically suspected of having actinomycetoma. Culture of the discharge confirmed the presence of Nocardia, and mass spectrometry further identified the species as Nocardia otitidiscaviarum.

Case Report

A 41-year-old female, farmer by occupation, presented with an eight-month history of dull pain and swelling in her right foot. She also reported pus mixed with blood discharging from multiple openings in the area. She denied any history of trauma. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no history of chronic illnesses, smoking, alcohol use, tuberculosis, surgeries, or immunosuppressive therapy. Local examination revealed swelling of the right foot, involving the dorsal aspect and great toe. Multiple discharging sinuses were present over the dorsal and plantar aspect of the right foot. Indurated papules and nodulo-plaques were present over the plantar aspect of the right foot. The patient was admitted for further evaluation and treatment. On palpation, there was tenderness but no active bleeding. No grains were visible even after keeping a wet gauze for 24 hour. There was no lymphadenopathy. Systemic examination was within normal limits. A clinical diagnosis of actinomycetoma and differential of eumycetoma, botryomycosis, and pseudo-mycetoma were kept. The patient was empirically started on injection Amikacin IM twice daily and tablet Co-trimoxazole-DS twice daily after meals (Welsh regime).[45]

Ultrasonography of the right foot showed evolving pus with sinus tract formation in the dorsal aspect of the right foot, with subcutaneous oedema along its plantar aspect. A discharge swab and skin biopsy were taken after proper disinfection and sent for histopathological examination, fungal culture, mycobacterial culture, and bacterial culture. Histopathology of the skin biopsy was non-specific, with dense mixed inflammatory cell infiltrate composed of polymorphonuclear cells, histiocytes, few lymphocytes, and eosinophils in the dermis. Gram staining of the discharge revealed gram-positive thin filamentous bacilli [Figure 1]. Modified acid-fast staining with 1% H2SO4 revealed few acid-fast bacilli [Figure 2]. Discharge was inoculated on Blood agar, Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) (with and without actidione), Lowenstein Jensen media, and Robertson’s cooked meat media. Incubation of SDA was done at 37°C and 25°C. Chalky white, dry, raised non-haemolytic colonies with rough and verrucous surface (molar tooth appearance) grew on a blood agar plate after 2 days of incubation [Figure 3]. On SDA, colonies were white to pink/orange, powdery, and wrinkled in appearance on obverse and no pigmentation on reverse after 7–10 days [Figure 4]. No growth was obtained on anaerobic and mycobacterial culture. The isolate was provisionally recognized as Nocardia spp. and was sent to higher centre for speciation. The isolate was identified as Nocardia otitidiscaviarum by Vitek MS v 2.0 (bioMerieux Inc, Durham, NC) with a confidence value of 99.9%.

Thus, on basis of AST results, it was decided to continue with the same treatment as already prescribed. Patient improved within 2 months and was given five cycles of Welsh regime to attain microbiological cure [Figure 5].

Discussion

Mycetoma is a chronic, debilitating disease first described by John Gill in Madurai, India.[6] Depending on the causative agent, it is classified into two types: actinomycetoma (caused by bacteria) and eumycetoma (caused by fungi). While clinical and radiological features can help distinguish between these two forms, definitive differentiation requires microbiological testing. Techniques such as Gram staining, modified Ziehl-Neelsen staining, and potassium hydroxide (KOH) mounts are helpful for identifying bacterial or fungal agents.

The common bacterial agents causing actinomycetoma include Actinomadura madurae, Actinomadura pelletieri, Nocardia brasiliensis, Nocardia asteroides, and Streptomyces somaliensis. Nocardia, a genus within the class Actinobacteria, order Mycobacteriales and family Nocardiaceae, consists of aerobic, Gram-positive, filamentous bacteria that are partially acid-alcohol-resistant. Cultures grown on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) display a characteristic chalky, cotton-like appearance. Primary cutaneous nocardial infections, depending on the mode of inoculation, can present as superficial pyogenic skin infections, sporotrichoid lymphocutaneous disease, or actinomycetoma.[7] In cases of actinomycetoma caused by Nocardia, grains are typically white and very small, so not visible to the naked eye, as seen in this case.

Nocardia is a ubiquitous bacterium, with over 109 identified species, of which 54 are clinically significant.[8] Common species implicated in primary cutaneous infections include N. nova, N. brasiliensis, N. asteroides, and N. farcinica. Rare species include N. otitidiscaviarum, N. pseudobrasiliensis, N. cyriacigeorgica, and N. abscessus. Among these, N. brasiliensis accounts for approximately 80% of primary cutaneous nocardial infections, while N. otitidiscaviarum contributes to only 3% of such cases.[9] The lower prevalence of N. otitidiscaviarum is likely due to its lower concentration in soil and reduced pathogenicity.[10] N. otitidiscaviarum causing actinomycetoma have been reported by a few studies.[11] Although unclear, the prevalence as per few studies showed a variation of 1.37%–1.9% in India.[41213] Studies from India report that Nocardia species account for 5.2%–35% of actinomycetoma cases.[14]

Cutaneous presentations of N. otitidiscaviarum (formerly N.caviae) are like those caused by other Nocardia species. This bacterium, first isolated in 1924 by Snijders from an infected ear of a Sumatran guinea pig (Caviae).[15] Techniques for species identification include rolling circle amplification (RCA), loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), and Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization-Time of Flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).[16] MALDI-TOF MS is significantly faster than traditional biochemical and molecular methods offering improved accuracy by measuring the mass-to-charge ratio of bacterial proteins.[17]

Cutaneous nocardial infections may heal spontaneously. However, in cases of actinomycetoma, the resolution can take up to six weeks or longer, even with treatment. The geographical distribution and antimicrobial susceptibility of Nocardia species vary widely. Due to its ability to hydrolyse both hypoxanthine and xanthine, it shows resistance to beta lactams antibiotics and is usually susceptible to amikacin and cotrimoxazole, which is in accordance to our AST report as well as other previous AST report.[1018] Accurate species identification and antimicrobial sensitivity testing are critical for tailoring treatment and achieving a faster cure. We treated our patient with Welsh regime based on clinical improvement and antibiotic sensitivity leading to a rapid clearance.[19]

Conclusion

While Nocardia is a common causative agent of actinomycetoma, multiple species can be involved, including the rare N. otitidiscaviarum. Accurate species identification and sensitivity testing are essential to optimize treatment and ensure a quicker resolution. In this case, treatment with the Welsh regimen based on clinical improvement and antibiotic sensitivity testing led to rapid clearance of the infection.
