Biodistribution and transport of intratympanically administered drugs in the inner ear using a porcine ex-vivo chamber model
Authors: Farimah Moazzam, Samuel Holdsclaw, Fengyi Yu, Rachel Maria Titus, Adele Moatti, Alon Greenbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective pharmacotherapy for hearing loss remains a significant challenge because of the blood-labyrinth barrier, which limits systemic drug access and often necessitates local intratympanic (IT) administration. However, the distribution of drugs after IT injection between the vestibular system and cochlea is unclear. Although the round window membrane is the main recognized entry route, in this study, we explored alternative pathways, namely the oval window (OW) and the thin apical otic capsule, using an ex vivo porcine model to assess their permeability to different solvent vehicles. We demonstrated that the route of entry is critically on the solvent composition and additives; in particular, reconstituting dexamethasone-fluorescein in dimethyl sulfoxide enhanced permeability through the OW by 42-fold compared to that with methanol, establishing the OW as a pathway for this solvent type rather than the RWM. Furthermore, we identified the thin apical otic capsule as a third drug-entry route in young pigs. Surgical sealing of this pathway in vivo, which reduces perilymph drug levels, underscores its importance in human translational studies. In summary, we developed a robust ex vivo multi-barrier model integrated with FluidSim to predict the inner ear drug biodistribution.Keywords: Inner ear drug delivery, Drug biodistribution, Round window membrane, Oval window, Otic capsule, Fluidsim simulation

Introduction

Hearing loss is a global issue that affects more than one billion people, and this number is estimated to increase given the increasing age of the world population [1]. One of the major challenges hindering progress in the treatment of hearing loss is the development of effective drug delivery strategies for the inner ear. Systemic drug administration has shown limited efficacy because only a small fraction of the administered dose reaches the cochlea [2,3]. This inefficiency is largely due to the restrictive nature of the blood–labyrinth barrier, which prevents adequate drug penetration into the inner ear [4,5]. To address this challenge, intratympanic (IT) injection has been widely adopted as a therapeutic strategy, enabling localized drug delivery to the middle ear cavity [6,7]. Although IT injection results in a higher overall drug concentration within the inner ear than systemic administration [8], the subsequent distribution of drugs within the inner ear remains poorly understood. This uncertainty is compounded by the sample collection process, which can disturb the biodistribution, and the anatomical interconnection of the cochlea and vestibular system, which prevents their separate analysis.

After IT injection of the drug into the middle ear, the drug enters the inner ear primarily through the round window membrane (RWM) and into the Scala Tympani. From the Scala Tympani, the drug diffuses in the cochlea towards the hair cells [9]. However, several studies have suggested that the oval window (OW) is an additional entry portal from the middle ear to the inner ear. Drugs that enter the oval window mostly reach the vestibular system and not the cochlea [10-12]. Currently, there is no systematic method to quantify the relative passage of drugs through the RWM versus the OW. Consequently, it remains uncertain how much of the administered drug ultimately reaches the inner ear and through which route—whether predominantly via the RWM into the Scala Tympani or also through the OW into the vestibule (Fig. 1a). Although several ex vivo models have been developed to study and predict drug passage through the RWM in rodents [13,14], our understanding of drug passage through the OW and other potential pathways for drug entry remains limited. These studies typically employ an ex vivo RWM chamber, a Transwell configuration in which the excised membrane separates two fluid-filled compartments, permitting the quantification of drug permeation over time [15].

Here, we extend our previous work on an ex vivo porcine RWM model to the OW and apical otic capsule and show that these entry points influence drug distribution within the inner ear. Anatomically, the OW is largely occupied by the stapes footplate, which is anchored by the surrounding annular ligament to facilitate the transmission of sound vibrations to the cochlea (Fig. 1b). Several studies have shown the role of the OW in facilitating drug entry into the inner ear [16,17]. One particularly informative study utilized an MRI contrast agent (gadolinium chelated with diethylenetriamine penta-acetic acid (Gd-DTPA)) to examine drug distribution after IT injection in guinea pigs. Surprisingly, the study revealed that ~ 90 % of Gd-DTPA penetrated through the region adjacent to the OW stapes rather than the RWM, thus challenging the widely held assumption that the RWM is the main route of entry after IT injection [11]. A later study in guinea pigs that used trimethylphenylammonium (TMPA) uptake found that after IT injection, ~30–40 % of the drug diffused into the vestibule close to the stapes [18]. These studies underscore the significant challenge of predicting drug distribution in the inner ear following IT injection.

The above biodistribution studies were conducted in rodents [16,17], which differ from humans in several key anatomical properties. Therefore, inferring drug biodistribution in the human inner ear based on rodent studies is challenging. One major anatomical difference between humans and rodents is the size and structure of the inner ear barrier. For example, rodents, such as guinea pigs, possess a relatively thin RWM compared to the thick RWM found in pigs and humans [12], which can significantly influence drug passage [19]. For translational studies, employing models that closely replicate the structural and functional features of the human inner ear is essential, and the pig model has emerged as one of the most suitable models for this purpose [20,21]. However, despite the suitability of pigs, we identified a critical anatomical confounder specific to young pigs (~8 weeks old). The bony otic capsule surrounding the cochlear apex is significantly thinner than that in humans. This structural discrepancy creates an artificial “third window,” a drug entry route that can fundamentally alter biodistribution profiles in a way that does not exist in humans.

To address these challenges and position pigs as valid human proxies, we developed ex vivo platforms using pig tissues. These ex vivo platforms were designed to measure drug passage through the inner ear barriers following IT drug administration (Fig. 1c) and to infer biodistribution within the inner ear. Using this platform, we evaluated the passage of gadolinium-based MRI contrast agents, dexamethasone-fluorescein (DexF) and dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DSP). Notably, we discovered that a minor change in the solvent additive—reconstituting DexF in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) instead of methanol—resulted in significant changes in the OW barrier permeability but not in the RWM permeability. To validate the significance of the apical otic capsule route in vivo, we performed surgery on a porcine model, in which this region was selectively blocked using a liquid bandage. This blockade led to significantly lower DSP concentrations in the inner ear compared to unblocked controls, confirming the apical otic capsule as a viable drug entry pathway in young pigs. Finally, the permeability data from the ex vivo models were used as inputs for a computational model (FluidSim) to simulate the DSP distribution within the porcine inner ear [20,22-25]. By integrating novel ex vivo models, in vivo validation, the ability to block the apical otic capsule route, and computational modeling, we established a powerful framework for derisking and optimizing drug delivery, ultimately accelerating the development of effective therapies for hearing loss.

Results

Comparative anatomical measurements of the inner ear in humans, pigs, and guinea pigs reveal strong structural similarities between pigs and humans

To assess the pig as an anatomical model for the human inner ear, we compared its key dimensions with those of humans and guinea pigs. We focused on potential drug entry points, such as the oval window and apical otic capsule, which we refer to as the apex. Data for humans and guinea pigs were compiled from the existing literature (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2), whereas porcine measurements were newly acquired using light-sheet microscopy (Supplementary Tables S3-S5, and Fig. S1).

As summarized in Table 1, a comparative analysis was performed for several parameters: frequency range (human, 20–20,000 Hz; pig, 42–40,000 Hz; guinea pig, 150–50,000 Hz), cochlear height (human, 3.26 mm; pig, 3.49 ± 0.79 mm; guinea pig, 4.43 mm), width (human, 6.5 mm; pig, 5.43 ± 1.04 mm; guinea pig, 2.13 mm), labyrinth length (human, 30.7–42.2 mm; pig, 39 mm; guinea pig, 18–18.5 mm), round window active area (human, 2.89 mm2; pig, 2.16 mm2; guinea pig, 1.18 mm2), round window thickness (human, 70 μm; pig, 92 μm; guinea pig, 30 μm), and apex area (human, 1.886 ± 0.142 mm2; pig, 1.56 ± 0.39 mm2; guinea pig, 0.28 ± 0.163 mm2). These data indicate that the pig inner ear more closely resembles human anatomy than the guinea pig inner ear. This finding was supported by endoscopic imaging, which revealed comparable anatomical landmarks in the human and pig middle ears, including the OW, round window (RW), apex, Eustachian tube, and Jacobson’s nerve, with only minor variations (Fig. 2a-b). Note that the RW area in the table was calculated as the intersection between the RWM niche area and the Scala Tympani.

Our light-sheet 3D imaging showed that the porcine active OW area (0.63 ± 021 mm2) and stapes footplate (SF) thickness (38.31 ± 11.38 μm, minimal thickness) were smaller than the human values (3.03 ± 0.34 mm2 and 381 ± 126 μm, respectively). However, stapes footplate thickness measurements in humans were conducted using the mean value within each specimen, not the minimal value [26]. Additionally, in both humans and pigs, the total OW area was calculated to include both the stapes footplate and annular ligament, as both are potential pathways for drug passage (Fig. 2c, red area).

A significant divergence was observed in the bony otic capsule, near the apex. The apex bone in pigs was substantially thinner (22.98 ± 9.58 μm) than that in humans (606 ± 474 μm). We found that although the porcine apex diameter increased with age (newborn to 8 weeks old), the bone itself remained consistently thin, ranging from approximately 11.9–36.1 μm (Fig. 2d).

In conclusion, the porcine middle ear and its key interfaces with the inner ear provide a strong anatomical analogy to their human counterparts. However, the bony otic capsule at the cochlear apex represents a critical point of divergence, as it is substantially thinner in pigs, a discrepancy also observed in rodent models [12,27,28]. This difference is paramount when interpreting biodistribution data from in vivo models, especially in the context of IT injection.

DMSO enhances Dexamethasone-Fluorescein passage through the oval window, but not through the round window.

To determine whether the drug solvent type could alter the primary entry route into the inner ear, we tested the passage of DexF reconstituted in two different organic solvents: methanol (METH-DexF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-DexF). The excised inner ear barrier (e.g., RW, OW, or otic capsule) was mounted in a custom chamber (Fig. 1C), separating an upper drug-containing chamber from a lower sampling chamber, analogous to a Transwell insert (Supplementary Fig. S2). To characterize drug passage, media from the lower chamber were sampled at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 22, and 24 h after DexF was placed in the top chamber. To maintain the concentration gradient, the drug was reapplied at 1 h (10 μL), 2 h (20 μL), and 22 h (20 μL). Because the OW is difficult to isolate from the adjacent RW, its permeability was determined by comparing the results from the RW-only chambers with those from the combined RW + OW chambers. In the following sections, all values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Drug passage analysis revealed a strong solvent-dependent effect. For METH-DexF, no significant difference in drug concentration was observed between the RW-only and RW + OW chambers over 24 h, indicating that the OW did not provide a significant additional pathway for this solvent type (Fig. 3a). In stark contrast, the DMSO-DexF solution showed significantly greater drug passage in the RW + OW chambers than in the RW-only chambers at 22 and 24 h. Specifically, in the RW + OW chambers, the values were (7.78 ± 6.67 μg/mL) at 22 h and (10.42 ± 6.66 μg/mL) at 24 h, whereas in the RW-only chambers, the corresponding values were (0.386 ± 0.125 μg/mL) and (0.813 ± 0.988 μg/mL), respectively. A two-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences between the groups, with (p < 0.05) at 22 h and (p < 0.01) at 24 h. This suggests that DMSO specifically enhances DexF passage via the OW (Fig. 3b).

To ensure that these results were not an artifact of tissue damage, explant viability was confirmed using an AlamarBlue metabolic assay at the conclusion of the experiments (Fig. 3c-d). In the AlamarBlue metabolic assay, the reduction of the redox indicator from oxidized (blue) to reduced (fluorescent, red) is a measure of cellular metabolic activity. A high reduction rate indicates an active and healthy chamber. A 20 % metabolic activity level was used as the viability cutoff; this threshold is based on our previous findings that tissues with metabolic activity below this point often suffer from compromised chamber integrity, resulting in uncontrolled drug passage [15]. Both RW and RW + OW explants exhibited similar metabolic activity across all conditions, with no significant differences observed. This indicates that the increased passage is due to an increased permeability effect and not DMSO-induced cytotoxicity.

These findings were further supported by the permeability coefficient (Kp) calculations. The permeability of METH-DexF was comparable between the RW and RW + OW groups (Fig. 3e). Conversely, Kp for DMSO-DexF was significantly higher in the RW + OW group at later time points, mirroring the drug passage results (Fig. 3f).

Using a subtractive analysis to isolate the contribution of the OW, we calculated its inferred permeability. For the METH-DexF solution, the calculated permeability of OW was higher but not significantly different from that of RW (Fig. 3g). However, for the DMSO-DexF solution, two-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in permeability between OW and RW at both time points (**p < 0.01 at 22 h and ***p < 0.001 at 24 h). The inferred permeability of the OW (6.67 × 10−7 ± 5.86 × 10−7 m/s at 22 h, and 8.37 × 10−7 ± 5.53 × 10−7 m/s at 24 h) was approximately two orders of magnitude higher than that of the RW (1.12 × 10−9 ± 4.15 × 10−10 m/s at 22 h, and 2.26 × 10−9 ± 2.86 × 10−9 m/s at 24 h), identifying it as the dominant entry route for this solvent type (Fig. 3h). Furthermore, a direct comparative analysis was conducted to evaluate the solvent effect, specifically on the oval window. This comparison revealed that DMSO significantly enhanced OW permeability compared to methanol (*p < 0.05 at 22 h and **p < 0.01 at 24 h), as shown in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the route of entry into the inner ear depends critically on the solvent vehicle. The choice of organic solvent when reconstituting the drug can modulate whether the round or oval window serves as the primary pathway, a finding with significant implications for optimizing drug delivery to the inner ear.

Comparative evaluation of an MRI contrast agent highlights species differences in inner ear barrier permeability

Previous studies in guinea pigs using the MRI contrast agent Gd-DTPA have shown that approximately 90 % of the agent enters through the oval window rather than the round window membrane [11]. To assess whether a similar tendency was observed in pigs, we measured RW and OW permeability to Gadavist using RW-only and combined RW + OW explants. We sought to replicate the study performed with Gd-DTPA; however, because Gd-DTPA is no longer commercially available, Gadavist was used as a comparable gadolinium-based substitute [54,55].

As before, Gadavist was placed in the top chamber, and media from the bottom chamber were sampled over a 24-h period to characterize its passage. The concentration of Gadavist was quantified using a plate reader. The results showed that Gadavist concentrations did not differ significantly between the RW-only and RW + OW chambers (n = 6), indicating that the inclusion of OW did not provide an additional pathway for drug passage (Fig. 4a). Consistently, the calculated permeability coefficient values showed no significant difference between the two chamber types (Fig. 4b).

Noting that RW and OW permeabilities can vary depending on the chemical properties of the compound is important. Thus, although Gadavist showed no differential passage, other agents may exhibit distinct permeability patterns. Furthermore, viability analysis using the AlamarBlue assay confirmed that the explants remained metabolically active under all conditions, ruling out tissue damage as a confounding factor in the results (Fig. 4c).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Gadavist does not preferentially enter the OW in the porcine model. This discrepancy with previous literature may be due to differences in the chemical properties of the MRI agents (Gd-DTPA vs. Gadavist) or species-specific differences in barrier physiology. These results highlight the need to evaluate entry pathways for each drug, as even minor modifications in compound properties may lead to significantly different outcomes.

In the pig, the otic capsule near the cochlear apex serves as an entry route into the inner ear.

Previous reports have indicated that in some animal models, the thin bone near the cochlear apex may permit drug passage [56]. This is in contrast to humans, who exhibit a thick apical bone that does not allow drug passage. Because the apical bone of the pig is also thin (Fig. 2), we measured its permeability to DSP, a corticosteroid widely used in the clinic to treat sudden hearing loss and Meniere's disease (Fig. 5). If drugs pass through the apex in the porcine model, significant differences in drug biodistribution would be expected between pigs and humans.

Our ex vivo apex chamber experiments showed significant DSP passage after 2.5 h, with higher drug concentrations detected in DSP samples than in media-only controls (Fig. 5a, p < 0.05, two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test). All drug concentrations were measured using HPLC (Supplementary Figs. S4-S6). When reporting drug concentration, we quantified both DSP and its native form, dexamethasone (Dex), because DSP is rapidly converted to Dex in the presence of tissue [25], as confirmed by the retention times in our HPLC analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5). Correspondingly, the calculated permeability coefficient (Kp) was significantly higher in the DSP group than in the control group (Fig. 5b, p < 0.05, two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test). Viability analysis confirmed that explants maintained > 80 % metabolic activity, indicating that drug passage was not attributable to cell death (Fig. 5c). Note that we restricted the experimental time to 2.5 h to replicate the timing of our in vivo experiments, as pigs cannot be maintained under sedation for longer periods.

To validate that the otic capsule near the cochlear apex serves as an entry route, we performed IT DSP injections in pigs. In one group, the cochlear apex was sealed with a liquid bandage before injection, whereas the apex of the control group was left unsealed. Perilymph was sampled 2.5 h post-injection, and analysis with mass spectrometry showed significantly reduced drug concentrations in the sealed group compared to unsealed controls (Fig. 5d, p < 0.05, two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test). This directly confirms that the apex provides a functional entry route for DSP into the cochlea in vivo. A critical control experiment confirmed that the liquid bandage did not absorb DSP from the solution, ensuring that the observed reduction was due to the sealing of the pathway and not material-related drug loss (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Together, these results establish the otic capsule near the cochlear apex as a physiologically relevant pathway for drug entry in young pigs. Therefore, sealing this pathway is a necessary step to overcome the key anatomical difference between the porcine and human cochlea, thereby maintaining the clinical fidelity of this animal model.

Percentage of DSP passage through RWM, OW, and apical bone pathways.

To obtain parameters for simulating the biodistribution of DSP in the porcine inner ear, we used ex vivo chambers to assess DSP passage (4 mg/mL) through the three primary barriers: RW, OW, and apical bone. At 2.5 h, the mean DSP concentrations were 42.83 μg/mL for the RWM, 16.5 μg/mL for the OW, and 19.61 μg/mL for the apex chamber, which consisted of the thin otic capsule bone isolated from the apical turn. At 24 h, DSP concentrations increased to 125.06 μg/mL (RWM), 52.31 μg/mL (OW), and 44.63 μg/mL (apex chamber). The percentage of drug passage, normalized to the total DSP passage across all three routes, was highest for the RWM (54.25 % at 2.5 h; 56.33 % at 24 h). For the OW route, the passage was 20.9 % at 2.5 h and 23.56 % at 24 h, whereas the apex chambers exhibited 24.84 % at 2.5 h and 20.10 % at 24 h (Fig. 6a). These findings demonstrate the relative contributions of the three major barriers to DSP transport in the porcine inner ear. The calculated permeability values confirmed that DSP transport varied across the three chambers, indicating region-specific differences in the barrier properties (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, metabolic activity assays demonstrated that cell viability remained high in all chambers after the completion of the experiments (Fig. 6c).

FluidSim simulation of DSP biodistribution under multiple cochlear entry pathways.

After quantifying the ex vivo passage percentages (Fig. 6), FluidSim was used to predict the biodistribution of DSP in the porcine inner ear under three simulated conditions (Fig. 7). The three conditions were defined as follows: Condition (1) (RW-Only): Simulates the common assumption of 100 % drug entry through the RWM (Fig. 7a). Condition (2) (RW + OW): Simulated a clinical scenario in which the apical entry was blocked, with 70.53 % entry through the RW and 29.47 % through the OW (Fig. 7b). Condition (3) (RW + OW + Apex): Simulated the full ex vivo porcine model, with 56.33 % entry through the RW, 23.56 % through the OW, and 20.10 % through the apex (Fig. 7c). All simulations were based on a 4 mg/mL DSP injection (150 μL) in the middle ear. The resulting drug concentrations over 150 min in the Scala Tympani (ST), Scala Media (SM), and Scala Vestibuli (SV) are shown in Fig. 7d-f.

For condition (1), the RW-only simulation predicted that the DSP would accumulate primarily in the basal turn of the cochlea with limited apical diffusion (Fig. 7a). This resulted in the highest concentration in the Scala Tympani (2.385 μg/mL at 150 min) but minimal accumulation in the Scala Media (0.295 μg/mL) and Scala Vestibuli (0.559 μg/mL) (Fig. 7d-f).

For condition (2), introducing the OW as a second entry route (Fig. 7b) did not significantly enhance drug spread to the far apical regions, but it markedly increased drug concentrations within the basal and middle turns, particularly in the SV and SM. This condition led to the highest accumulation in the SV (7.919 μg/mL at 150 min) relative to the other compartments in this simulation. It also significantly increased SM concentrations (1.95 μg/mL) and maintained high ST levels (2.603 μg/mL; Fig. 7d-f).

For condition (3), introducing the Apex as a third entry route (Fig. 7c) resulted in the most extensive and uniform drug distribution. This condition predicted the highest concentrations across all three compartments at 150 min: 10.57 μg/mL in the SV, 6.5 μg/mL in the SM, and 3.03 μg/mL in the ST (Fig. 7d-f).

The simulation models demonstrated that although the RW is a major entry pathway, participation of the OW is critical for enhancing drug exposure to the SV and SM. The inclusion of the apical pathway (Condition (3)) resulted in the most widespread cochlear coverage. However, this route is unlikely to be significant in humans, whose dense apical bone restricts drug penetration. In contrast, rodents and young pigs have thinner apical bones, which may permit this permeability. Therefore, to improve translational relevance, experimental models should aim to mimic human anatomy by isolating or blocking the apical pathway, which would allow for a more accurate assessment of RW and OW drug entry dynamics.

Discussion

The effective delivery of therapeutics to the inner ear remains a primary obstacle in the treatment of hearing loss [57]. Although IT injection has surpassed systemic administration in efficacy, a significant gap persists in our understanding of how drugs are distributed within the cochlea and vestibular systems post-delivery [58]. Previous reports have shown that when gentamicin is applied to the oval window, it can cause substantial cochlear and vestibular damage [17]. This highlights the importance of determining whether the compounds also pass through the oval window. This study introduces a comprehensive framework that integrates novel ex vivo porcine models, in vivo validation, and computational modeling to deconstruct the complex interplay between drug formulations, anatomical entry routes, and species-specific differences. Our findings reveal that the choice of solvent used to reconstitute the drug can dramatically alter the primary route of inner ear entry and underscore a critical, often-overlooked anatomical entry pathway in widely used animal models.

Our findings confirm the strong anatomical fidelity of the porcine model to the human inner ear across several key parameters, including cochlear dimensions and round window membrane thickness [59]. However, the OW presents notable differences from its human counterpart in terms of surface area and stapes footplate thickness [26]. The difference in the surface area is a geometric variable that can be accounted for in computational simulations. If the intrinsic OW permeability is known, scaling the total drug flux for a different surface area becomes straightforward. However, a more fundamental challenge is whether the intrinsic permeability itself is translational between pigs and humans.

The challenge of interspecies translation is most apparent in stapes footplate thickness. Our measurements identified a minimal thickness of approximately 38 μm in the pigs. We selected this metric because the path of least resistance is more predictive of drug passage than the average value. This approach differs significantly from the widely cited human value of ~ 284 μm, which is the average thickness that may obscure the most permeable pathways [26]. As our data demonstrate (Fig. 2c), the average thickness measurement is highly susceptible to sampling bias, with values varying significantly by location. Therefore, the substantial gap between the porcine minimal thickness and the human average value, coupled with these disparate measurement strategies, underscores that the intrinsic permeability of the OW is the primary challenge for the model's translational relevance.

The most significant anatomical divergence between pigs and humans is the consistently thinner otic capsule near the cochlear apex in pigs (approximately 23 μm vs. > 600 μm in humans) [46,47,60]. Our measurements confirmed that this feature is present from birth through at least eight weeks of age, creating a highly permeable pathway that facilitates drug penetration to the cochlear apex—a route that is absent in humans, where the cochlea is encased in thick bone. The presence of this pathway in animal models is a serious limitation, as it can lead to biodistribution profiles that do not reflect human clinical scenarios. However, our study demonstrates that this limitation can be overcome by artificially sealing the apical entry route. Although we used a liquid bandage for proof-of-concept, this material is not approved for internal use. Therefore, future studies should focus on identifying biocompatible sealing materials to block this pathway. By implementing this modification, the key anatomical differences were mitigated.

Our permeability analysis revealed that the contribution of the oval window to drug entry was strongly dependent on the solvent vehicle. This solvent effect was most evident with Dexamethasone-Fluorescein, where switching the reconstitution from methanol to DMSO dramatically altered the entry dynamics [61]. Although methanol-based DexF showed comparable passage through both the RWM and the OW, the DMSO solution selectively enhanced permeability through the OW by 42-fold compared to methanol at later time points. DMSO is a well-known penetration enhancer; however, our results suggest that its effects are not uniform across the inner ear barriers. The unique composition of the annular ligament may render it particularly susceptible to DMSO, creating a preferential pathway. The core objective of this work is to introduce an integrated ex vivo and computational platform capable of mapping inner ear drug biodistribution, utilizing model compounds to demonstrate how minor solvent-dependent adjustments can uniquely alter specific barrier permeabilities. To ensure that our findings reflected authentic transport variations rather than tissue degradation, post-experiment AlamarBlue assays were conducted. The data revealed no significant difference in viability between tissues exposed to methanol or DMSO for 24 h, confirming that drug passage was not an artifact of cell death (Supplementary Fig. S8). However, we do not anticipate clinical application of DMSO to the middle ear due to toxicity concerns. Nevertheless, due to a ~ 200-fold middle-to-inner ear dilution effect simulated by FluidSim, the final intracochlear solvent concentration is estimated at ~ 0.04 %. This is far below the 0.1 % DMSO threshold widely reported in the literature to cause little to no damage to cochlear structures [62].

As an alternative to ex vivo chambers, the drug of interest can be conjugated to MRI contrast agents or fluorescently labeled molecules. However, this approach may lead to inaccurate biodistribution results, as attaching a label can alter the physicochemical properties of a drug and, consequently, its permeability and biodistribution [63]. For example, the RWM permeability of native dexamethasone is known to be much higher than that of its fluorescently conjugated analog, Dex-F. In contrast, ex vivo chambers allow for the direct measurement of unmodified therapeutic compounds using a range of standard analytical methods (e.g., HPLC, mass spectrometry, plate readers), thereby providing more accurate and broadly applicable data to de-risk and guide preclinical biodistribution studies.

Permeability studies with the clinically relevant MRI contrast agent Gadavist further highlight the compound- and species-specific nature of inner ear drug transport. In contrast to earlier guinea pig studies reporting predominant Gd-DTPA entry through the oval window [11], our porcine explant experiments showed no preferential passage for Gadavist. This discrepancy underscores the fact that drug-specific physicochemical properties are critical determinants of inner ear permeability and cannot be generalized across all compounds. Although our model accurately reflects static permeability, future iterations could incorporate stapes vibration to investigate its potential role in enhancing drug permeation through the OW. This focus is specific to the OW, as its mechanical coupling with the stapes is a feature that cannot be replicated at the RWM.

Because direct sampling of the interconnected inner ear fluid spaces is inherently invasive and cannot distinguish between multiple entry routes, an integrated framework is required. By combining isolated ex vivo permeability measurements (experimental) with computational FluidSim simulation (theoretical), we can predict internal drug gradients that are otherwise inaccessible to current experimental techniques. The FluidSim simulations demonstrate how the route of drug entry critically influences cochlear biodistribution [64]. When the drug entry occurred exclusively through the RWM (Condition (1), the DSP predominantly accumulated in the Scala Tympani near the basal (high-frequency) turn. Diffusion toward the Scala Media and low-frequency apex was minimal, reflecting the limitations of a single entry point and transmembrane permeability. In contrast, allowing drug entry through both the RWM and OW (Condition (2)) markedly enhanced overall cochlear exposure, particularly within the Scala Media. This simulated increase in the Scala Media concentration is likely due to the creation of a dual-diffusion pathway. With OW acting as a second entry route, a high concentration of DSP accumulates in the Scala Vestibuli. This allows the drug to diffuse into the Scala Media from two sides simultaneously: across the basilar membrane (from the Scala Tympani) and across Reissner's membrane (from the Scala Vestibuli), resulting in significantly higher endolymphatic concentrations. However, the simulations for both Conditions (1) and (2) showed that drugs applied at the base struggled to reach the cochlear apex. This suggests that even a combined RWM/OW delivery strategy may be insufficient for treating diseases that affect the low-frequency regions of the inner ear. Our ex vivo porcine model (Condition (3), Fig. 7c) showed that high apical concentrations were achieved only via a direct apical pathway. Given that this route is naturally occluded by dense bone in humans, these findings suggest that future therapeutic strategies could explore artificially creating a “third portal” or micro-fenestration at the apex to ensure adequate drug delivery to the entire cochlea.

Taken together, our results establish that drug delivery to the inner ear is governed by a complex interplay of multiple anatomical entry points—principally the round window membrane and the oval window—as well as confounding species-specific pathways, such as the cochlear apex. The relative contributions of these routes are modulated by both the structural anatomy and compound-specific properties. The demonstration that the oval window acts as a consistent entry route, with permeability modulated by solvents such as DMSO, suggests that its potential has been under-recognized in clinical drug delivery strategies. These insights collectively argue for a paradigm shift in inner ear drug delivery research: rather than assuming a single dominant entry route, future therapeutic approaches should integrate multi-portal delivery considerations tailored to both the physicochemical properties of the drug and the structural characteristics of the target species. These strategies hold promise for optimizing IT therapies and accelerating their translation into effective clinical treatments for hearing loss.

Material and methods

Experimental model

Yorkshire wild-type pigs of both sexes were used at the following postnatal time points: birth, 2, 4, and 8 weeks. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of North Carolina State University (Protocol Number 25–121) and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and the guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Tissue clearing and light sheet imaging

Inner ear samples were processed using a modified BoneClear tissue-clearing protocol optimized for large-animal cochlea [65]. Cleared cochleae were mounted in dibenzyl ether (DBE) and imaged using a light-sheet fluorescence microscope. Imaging was performed with laser excitation at 560 nm, and z-stacks were acquired at a voxel resolution optimized for the 3D reconstruction of the cochlear cytoarchitecture. The acquired datasets were stitched and visualized using IMARIS and ImageJ for three-dimensional reconstruction and analysis.

Dissection and chamber fabrication

Using an ex vivo porcine model, we established a standardized testing platform that minimizes the reliance on live animal models [66]. After removing the skin, a cranial window was created in the porcine skull using a Stryker bone saw. The brain was removed to expose the temporal bone, which was further thinned, and the inner ear was excised using a bone cutter. The round window membrane with a small portion of the adjacent bone was isolated using a dental drill (1 mm burr). For RW–OW preparation, both membranes were excised as a single unit because of the fragility of the oval bone. A segment of the apical region, where the bone is thinnest, was isolated as a potential entry site.

All drilling steps were performed in PBS containing 1 % Antibiotic/Antimycotic, which was refreshed regularly to clear debris. The excised RW, RW + OW, and apical samples were dried with sterile gauze and fixed to the base of a cut 0.5 mL Eppendorf tube using dental cement (3 M RelyX Unicem, 56830). The assembly was mounted onto a Transwell insert lacking the mesh and incubated overnight in DMEM supplemented with 1 % Antibiotic/Antimycotic and 1 % N2 (150 μL apical, 2 mL basal). The following day, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 1 % FBS, 1 % Antibiotic/Antimycotic, and 1 % N2, after which the chambers were used for experiments.

AlamarBlue viability assessment

An AlamarBlue assay was used to indirectly measure the viability of the explants [15]. The reduction of the redox indicator from its oxidized (blue) to reduced (fluorescent, red) state is a measure of cellular metabolic activity. To evaluate tissue viability, the explants were rinsed three times with PBS to remove the residual medium. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of fresh culture medium supplemented with 10 % AlamarBlue reagent (Thermo Fisher, DAL1025) was added to both the upper and lower compartments of the chamber [67,68]. The samples were incubated for 4–6 h under standard culture conditions. After incubation, the supernatant was collected, and absorbance was recorded using a microplate reader (Infinite M Plex, Tecan) at 570 nm and 600 nm. The Percent reduction values were determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Criteria for excluding non-viable or damaged chambers

Chambers were excluded from the analysis when signs of damage or compromised integrity were detected. Specifically, if small tears or perforations in the tissue barrier were identified under a dissecting microscope, the sample was excluded. Another exclusion criterion was an overnight volume loss of more than 100 μL of the medium, which suggests the presence of microscopic defects. Chamber viability was evaluated based on the AlamarBlue percent reduction (PR). At the end of each experiment, chambers with PR values below 20 % were excluded. This interpretation is consistent with our observation that membranes with low PR values (e.g., 20 %) exhibit leakage patterns similar to those of mechanically damaged chambers [15].

Drug passage assay

Dexamethasone conjugated with fluorescein (DexF; D1383, Thermo Fisher; MW 840.98) was first dissolved in methanol to a stock concentration of 5 mg/mL and subsequently diluted in the culture medium to 400 μg/mL (0.5 mM) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A parallel preparation was performed for DMSO. The final concentration of methanol or DMSO in the solution was 8.2 %. Dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DSP; D4902, Sigma) was dissolved in PBS at 4 mg/mL, sterilized through a 0.2 μm filter, and used immediately.

For permeability testing, 100 μL of culture medium (DMEM supplemented with 1 % FBS, 1 % Antibiotic/Antimycotic, 1 % N2, and 10 ng/mL EGF) containing the compound of interest was added to the top of the chamber. To mimic continuous dosing, the apical solution (initial volume 100 μl) was replenished with 10 μL of fresh compound-containing medium at the 1 h time point and 20 μL at the 2 h and 22 h time points. The basal chamber contained 2 mL of culture medium in a 24-well plate. At each time point, 100 μL of medium was withdrawn from the basal chamber following homogenization by pipetting. An equal volume of fresh medium was replaced after each collection. Drug concentrations were corrected for the dilution effect based on the cumulative withdrawn volume [15].

Fluorescence measurement of DexF

DexF in the basal medium was quantified using a microplate reader (Infinite M Plex, Tecan). Measurements were performed at an excitation wavelength of 498 nm and an emission wavelength of 526 nm, with excitation and emission bandwidths of 9 and 20 nm, respectively. Reader settings included a manual gain of 20, 22 flashes per read, an integration time of 20 μs, a lag time of 0 μs, a settle time of 0 ms, and a manual Z-position of 20,000 μm.

HPLC analysis of DSP concentrations

HPLC was employed to quantify the concentrations of Dex and DSP using an Agilent system equipped with a UV detector set at 242 nm and a 100 mm C18 column, maintained at 22 °C. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (H2O with 0.1 % TFA) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1 % TFA), delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with an injection volume of 10 μL. The gradient program was as follows: 0.0 min, 95 % A/5 % B (system equilibration and pressure stabilization); 0.0–5.0 min, linear ramp to 5 % A/95 % B; 5.0–5.5 min, hold at 5 % A/95 % B; 5.5–6.0 min, and ramp back to 95 % A/5 % B; 6.0–7.0 min, hold at 95 % A/5 % B for re-equilibration.

Initially, DSP and Dex were dissolved in pure methanol to determine their retention times. Under the applied chromatographic conditions, DSP and Dex eluted at approximately 3.2 min and 3.7 min, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4). Three experimental conditions were analyzed to assess the influence of tissue on chromatographic profiles. First, the tissue was incubated in media for 24 h without the drug, serving as a negative control to establish baseline peaks originating from the tissue and media (Supplementary Fig. S5). Second, media containing 25 μg/mL DSP incubated without tissue were used to confirm drug stability in the medium. Finally, the media containing 25 μg/mL DSP incubated with the tissue was analyzed to determine the residual concentration of the drug after tissue exposure. The extent of drug absorption by the tissue was quantified by comparing the peak areas of DSP between the media-only and tissue-incubated conditions.

To enable the accurate quantification of DSP in experimental samples, a standard calibration curve was generated using HPLC (Supplementary Fig. S6). Blank incubated media mixed with methanol (1:1) were first analyzed to identify the background peaks originating from the medium. Serial dilutions of DSP were subsequently prepared to achieve concentrations ranging from 0.78 μg/mL to 50 μg/mL. Each dilution was injected into the HPLC system under the same chromatographic conditions as those used for the experimental samples, and the peak areas corresponding to DSP were recorded. During tissue incubation, partial enzymatic conversion of DSP to Dex was observed; under the applied method, DSP and Dex consistently eluted at approximately 3.2 and 3.7 min, respectively. A linear standard curve was established by plotting the peak area against the DSP concentration, which was subsequently used to determine the unknown drug concentrations in the tissue samples.

HPLC was performed to evaluate the potential effect of liquid bandage application on DSP concentration (Supplementary Fig. S7). DSP reference standards (50 μg/mL) were prepared in PBS-methanol (PBS-Meth) solution and used as controls alongside blank PBS-Meth samples. The experimental groups were prepared by applying the DSP solution alone or in combination with a liquid bandage (n = 3).

In vivo surgery

Yorkshire pigs (both sexes, postnatal day 20–30, weighing 7–12 kg) were used in these experiments. All procedures adhered to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at North Carolina State University and complied with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Therapeutic agents were administered to the middle ear using a previously established IT approach [20]. Animals were sacrificed 2.5 h post-injection, and perilymph samples were obtained from the round window membrane using glass capillaries. For the apical sealing procedure, a liquid bandage (CVS # 369239) was first applied to the apical region and allowed to dry for 10 min. Following the setting of the bandage, DSP was injected, and a 2.5 h incubation period was observed prior to sampling the perilymph. Samples were stored at −80 °C until shipment to Carbon Dynamics for mass spectrometry quantification of Dex and DSP within the perilymph.

FluidSim simulation

FluidSim was employed to simulate the diffusion of DSP within the porcine inner ear following IT injection [69]. Experimental data obtained from ex vivo diffusion chambers were incorporated into the model to quantify the percentage of drug passage through distinct routes: the RWM (n = 5), combined RW + OW (n = 5), and apex chambers (n = 6). The simulation outputs generated DSP concentration profiles as a function of time and spatial distribution along the Scala Tympani, Scala Vestibuli, and Scala Media over a 300-min period post-injection (Fig. 7).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software. Differences between two independent groups were assessed using an unpaired t-test or unpaired Mann-Whitney U test, whereas comparisons involving multiple groups or time points were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Significance levels are indicated as follows: *p ≤ 0.05; **p ≤ 0.01; ***p ≤ 0.001; ****p ≤ 0.0001; ns = not significant.
