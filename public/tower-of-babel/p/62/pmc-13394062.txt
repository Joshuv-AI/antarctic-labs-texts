Interpreting Early‐Onset Colorectal Cancer in Japan: A Descriptive Epidemiologic Review Using Public Summary Statistics
Authors: Keitaro Matsuo, Hidemi Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly‐onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) is increasingly described as a rising disease entity, but the descriptive epidemiologic conditions required to interpret that rise remain incompletely articulated. Using Japanese public summary statistics, we conducted a reproducible descriptive epidemiology review to examine whether the observed EOCRC signal is more compatible with substantial detection‐related effects, a concurrent increase in invasive burden, or both. We integrated age‐group‐specific incidence and mortality trends and, where available, decomposed incidence into invasive disease, incidence including in situ lesions, and the in situ component. Colorectal cancer was the primary case study, and other organized screening‐target cancers were used as descriptive contrasts. In Japan, EOCRC showed increasing early‐onset‐to‐older‐adult incidence ratios, but these patterns should be interpreted alongside marked in situ amplification and limited concordance with mortality. Cross‐cancer contrasts further aided interpretation: Unlike CRC, stomach cancer and male lung cancer did not show a comparable early‐onset upswing over the 2003–2015 comparison window, arguing against a uniform early‐onset pattern across screening‐target cancers. These descriptive findings are compatible with substantial detection‐related effects, but they do not exclude biologic heterogeneity or a concurrent increase in invasive EOCRC. Although public summary statistics cannot establish etiology, they can clarify what current trend data do and do not support. In particular, incidence trends alone are insufficient to justify a true‐rise interpretation of clinically significant early‐onset colorectal cancer.Keywords: cancer registry, descriptive epidemiology, early‐onset colorectal cancer, incidence, mortality

Introduction

Early‐onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC), most commonly defined as colorectal cancer diagnosed before age 50, has become a focal point in contemporary oncology. Reports of increasing EOCRC incidence across multiple settings have raised concerns that a new wave of CRC is emerging in younger generations, with implications for screening policy, resource allocation, and etiologic research [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, the epidemiologic meaning of an “increase” in EOCRC is frequently underspecified. Incidence can rise because more clinically consequential invasive disease is occurring, but it can also rise because cancers (and pre‐cancers) are being detected earlier, classified differently, and registered more completely. When these possibilities are not explicitly differentiated, the field risks mistaking changes in screening and diagnostic framework for changes in disease biology [5].

Recent mechanistic studies have proposed compelling candidate pathways for subsets of CRC, including specific mutational processes and host–microbe interactions [6, 7]. Although valuable in themselves, these advances are increasingly invoked to support claims that EOCRC represents a distinct, expanding disease entity. This association is not automatic. While a molecular signature observed in selected tumors demonstrates plausibility for a carcinogenic process, it does not, by itself, demonstrate that a defined subtype is increasing in contribution to EOCRC incidence or burden at the population level [6].

In this review, we argue that the EOCRC debate can be made more scientifically tractable by returning to first principles of descriptive epidemiology, using transparent analyses that are reproducible from publicly available summary statistics. Using Japan as a case study, we integrate age‐group incidence and mortality trends and, where available, decompose incidence into invasive disease, incidence including in situ lesions, and the in situ component [8, 9].

A central motivation for relying on public summaries is reproducibility: Although individual‐level registry datasets enable richer analyses, they are often accessible only to specialized groups, limiting independent verification [10, 11, 12]. Accordingly, the explicit, testable criteria we propose here are intended not to definitively distinguish detection‐related change from a true increase in early‐onset invasive CRC, but to illustrate how far this question can be evaluated using publicly available summary statistics alone.

This perspective is not anti‐mechanistic, but rather sequential. Mechanistic work is strongest when anchored to a clearly described population phenomenon: What is increasing, in whom, over what interval, under which diagnostic definitions, and with what consequences for invasive burden and mortality. If these boundary conditions are not established first, however, the field can drift into a category error in which a biologically interesting pathway observed in a subset of tumors is treated as if it automatically explained the time trend of the disease category as a whole. Public summary statistics force explicit reconciliation across detection‐sensitive incidence, more clinically consequential invasive incidence, and mortality. Thus, they are not the endpoint of etiologic inference, but rather an efficient first filter against this kind of inference inflation.

Japan provides a useful setting for this exercise because its public cancer summaries make it possible to examine patterns across periods, data sources, and diagnostic definitions [10, 11]. The availability of age‐group incidence before and after nationwide registry implementation, together with national mortality summaries and selected in situ‐inclusive incidence definitions, creates an unusually instructive setting in which to ask what a “rise” in EOCRC actually consists of. Nevertheless, the substantive lesson is not Japan‐specific; rather, the same inferential problem arises anywhere that rising early‐onset incidence is discussed in isolation from changes in screening uptake, diagnostic intensity, registry completeness, or lesion classification. Our aim is therefore not to deny that subsets of EOCRC may have distinct biology, but to clarify what population‐level descriptive signatures should be demanded before a new expanding invasive entity is inferred.

Conceptual Framework: What Would an “Artifact” Look Like?

The central difficulty in EOCRC interpretation is not whether incidence curves have changed, but what those changes mean. An increase in early‐onset incidence can reflect a true increase in the occurrence of clinically consequential invasive cancer, but can also be produced by shifts in the screening and diagnostic framework, including increased access to colonoscopy, heightened clinical suspicion, improved pathology, changing registry completeness, and shifts in case definition [1, 5, 13]. Because these factors can operate differentially by age and over time, a purely descriptive statement (“EOCRC is increasing”) is not sufficient to support claims of a new disease entity or a new etiologic wave.

We therefore approach EOCRC interpretation by asking which explanations remain most compatible with the observed trends in incidence and mortality, and, where available, in situ‐inclusive incidence.

Artifact vs. True Risk: Patterns and Testable Criteria

We propose a pragmatic set of patterns that can be evaluated using routinely reported summary statistics. No single pattern is definitive, and the goal is triangulation rather than adjudication. When multiple signals align, they can help constrain interpretation, but they do not by themselves establish etiology [5].

Patterns more compatible with detection‐related change (artifact‐leaning) include disproportionate growth of in situ–inclusive incidence and an increasing in situ component (where definable); divergence between rising incidence and stable or declining mortality in younger age groups; and discontinuities aligned with period shocks such as registry transitions or the COVID‐19 disruption [5, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Mechanistic signatures discovered in selected tumors should be treated as hypotheses for subtype definition and etiologic attribution, and not by themselves as evidence that a distinct subtype is increasing at the population level [6].

Patterns more compatible with a true increase in invasive risk (risk‐leaning) include increases in invasive incidence that are robust to case‐definition choices and sensitivity analyses; evidence of increasing advanced‐stage incidence among younger adults; cohort carryover in longer series; and coherent population‐level burden under plausible lag and treatment‐improvement scenarios [18, 19].

Why Incidence–Mortality Integration Matters (Even With Limitations)

Because mortality is influenced by time‐to‐death lags and improvements in therapy, incidence and mortality will never align perfectly. Nonetheless, an integrated incidence–mortality view is a useful but limited guardrail against overinterpreting incidence increases driven primarily by diagnostic intensity or stage shift. If early‐onset incidence rises while mortality in the same age group remains stable or declines over extended periods, one compatible interpretation is that earlier detection, stage shift, improved therapy, or a combination of these factors is shaping the observed signal. Such a pattern does not exclude a concurrent increase in invasive EOCRC, but it does caution against treating rising incidence curves alone as direct evidence of an expanding clinically consequential disease burden [5] (Box 1).

Data & Reproducible Approach Using Public Summaries

Data Sources in Japan

Incidence and mortality data were obtained from Cancer Statistics, Cancer Information Service, National Cancer Center, Japan. Mortality was based on the Vital Statistics of Japan (Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare) [8]. Incidence for 2016 onward was based on the Cancer Registry (Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare) [9], while incidence prior to registry implementation was based on the Monitoring of Cancer Incidence in Japan (MCIJ) project [10].

Note on denominators: Incidence and mortality rates were computed using the population denominators provided in each respective public summary dataset; therefore, incidence and mortality rates may rely on different population definitions. Within‐dataset temporal trends and within‐year EO ratios are internally consistent.

Minimal Reproducible Workflow

Our analytic strategy intentionally prioritizes simplicity and reproducibility. Using age‐group–specific case counts and denominators, we compute age‐group incidence and mortality rates. Where in situ–inclusive definitions exist, we compute the in situ component as a simple difference and the in situ fraction. Changes in the EO ratio may reflect changes in either the numerator (20–49 incidence) or the denominator (50–64 incidence), or in both; therefore, we interpret EO‐ratio shifts alongside age‐group‐specific incidence trajectories (Figure 1; Figures [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]). All data processing and visualization were performed in Python (Python 3.13.5; pandas 2.2.3; matplotlib 3.10.8).

Three design choices warrant explicit justification. First, we use ages 20–49 years as the early‐onset band because this is the most common epidemiologic definition of EOCRC [22, 23, 24] and is directly supported by the public tables. Second, we use ages 50–64 years as the adjacent comparator because it sits closest to the early‐onset range while remaining largely outside the conventional EOCRC category. This makes the EO ratio interpretable as a compact measure of relative age shift rather than as a comparison against the highly heterogeneous 65+ population, in which secular changes in comorbidity, treatment, competing mortality, and the composition of the oldest‐old can dominate trends. Third, we retain 65+ years as an older reference band in the time‐series figures because it provides context for whether a signal appears genuinely early‐onset selective or instead reflects broader cross‐age movement.

We intentionally rely on crude age‐specific rates within fixed age‐groups rather than age‐standardized summary measures. Age standardization is indispensable when the question concerns comparisons of overall burden across populations with different age structures. Here, however, the primary question is narrower: Within the same sex, site, year, and source dataset, is the balance of burden shifting toward younger adults, and is that shift accompanied by evidence more consistent with invasive disease or with detection‐sensitive inflation? In that setting, fixed age‐group crude rates preserve the interpretability of the public tables and avoid introducing a second layer of modeling that could blur the distinction between invasive and in situ‐inclusive definitions. The EO ratio is therefore best viewed as a descriptive compression of the age‐shift signal, not as a stand‐alone causal statistic.

Additional technical details of data extraction, harmonization, age‐group aggregation, metric construction, and cross‐year summary procedures are provided in Doc S1. To make this workflow directly inspectable, we also prepared reproducible analysis code and documentation (Doc S2; main source‐code listing, run_eocrc_full_pipeline.py) that regenerates figures and tables from the three public source workbooks distributed through the Cancer Information Service, Japan. The code reads the published summary tables directly; harmonizes site, sex, and age labels across source periods; and performs figure and table generation without reliance on manually edited intermediate spreadsheets.

Sensitivity Analyses and Interpretive Safeguards

We distinguish age‐specific crude rates from age‐standardized rates, and treat known period shocks (e.g., registry transitions; COVID‐19 screening disruptions) as sensitivity considerations rather than evidence of etiology [5, 15, 17].

CRC As the Main Case Study

Incidence: Invasive Vs. Including in Situ, and What the in Situ Component Implies

CRC is an instructive test case because it sits at the intersection of rising early‐onset incidence narratives and a screening and diagnostic framework that has become progressively more capable of identifying early colorectal lesions [22, 24]. In Japan, recommended colorectal cancer screening starts at age 40, although starting at age 45 or 50 is also permitted, and uses fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) [25]. Screening opportunities are not limited to municipal population‐based programs; in practice, workplace‐based screening and opportunistic health checkups also provide FIT‐based screening, while target ages and implementation vary across insurers and employers. A recent Japanese single‐center retrospective series further reported that EOCRC was more frequently detected through FIT screening than late‐onset CRC, supporting the view that FIT‐based detection outside the narrow frame of municipal programs may partly contribute to colorectal cancer case finding in younger adults [26]. Because FIT can be completed outside a medical facility and incorporated into workplace‐based or opportunistic health checkups, it may have shifted some diagnoses earlier within younger adults, especially around ages 40–49, even if it does not fully explain incidence patterns at younger ages. When incidence is decomposed into invasive CRC, incidence including in situ lesions, and the in situ component, the apparent early‐onset increase is often amplified by growth in the in situ component. This pattern is compatible with intensified detection and earlier lesion capture. At the same time, it does not exclude a concurrent increase in invasive disease, and therefore should be interpreted as supportive descriptive evidence rather than as definitive evidence against a true rise in invasive EOCRC (Figure 1).

Mortality: Does Early‐Onset CRC Mortality Rise in Parallel?

Mortality trends provide a complementary but limited check. If early‐onset CRC were rising primarily as a clinically consequential invasive entity, some concordance with early‐onset mortality might be expected over sustained periods, although such comparisons are inherently influenced by lag structure, earlier detection, treatment improvement, and stage migration. In Japan, the juxtaposition of rising incidence with stable or declining early‐onset mortality is compatible with a substantial role for earlier detection, stage shift, and therapeutic progress, but does not by itself exclude a concurrent increase in invasive EOCRC (Figure 1) [19].

Subsite Patterns: Colon Vs. Rectum as an Internal Check

CRC is not a monolith. Subsite heterogeneity (colon vs. rectum) provides an internal check and can help identify where residual invasive increases may be most informative for etiologic work [13].

Interim Interpretation

Public summaries can demonstrate in situ amplification, incidence–mortality divergence, and period‐aligned discontinuities. Taken together, these signatures support an evidence‐based stance that substantial detection‐related shifts likely contribute to the observed early‐onset signal, while not excluding a concurrent increase in invasive EOCRC. Although not definitive, the absence of a corresponding rise in early‐onset mortality also makes a substantial increase in lethal EOCRC burden less likely at the population level. They also help define explicit evidentiary requirements for stronger claims of a true invasive risk increase or a new expanding entity (Figure 1) [18].

Thus, the relevant temporal relation may be less the year of program introduction itself than the subsequent maturation of a FIT‐based detection system. In that sense, the CRC pattern seen in Figure 1 is temporally compatible with a cumulative screening‐and‐detection effect, rather than a simple step change tied to a single policy year.

Exploratory age‐group analyses of CRC further suggested that the pattern is not a simple one‐for‐one age shift. Early‐onset case counts continued to rise, whereas net burden in ages 50–64 appeared to attenuate or plateau over time (Figure S6; Table S3). Although not definitive on its own, this pattern is at least partly compatible with an age‐shift component.

Figure S6 also helps place the EO signal in scale. The early‐onset pattern is visible, but the absolute burden in ages 20–49 remains much smaller than that in older age groups. In that sense, the descriptive importance of the EO shift should be distinguished from the overall burden carried by later‐onset CRC.

Interpretation note: Not all screening‐target cancers show an increasing early‐onset shift. In our analyses, stomach cancer (both sexes) and lung cancer (men) showed a decreasing EO ratio, plausibly reflecting cohort‐level reductions in major causal exposures—declining Helicobacter pylori prevalence for stomach cancer [27] and declining smoking exposure for lung cancer (including shifts from nonfilter to filter cigarettes) [28, 29, 30]—although the EO ratio can also be influenced by trends in the 50–64 denominator. This contrast underscores that the EO ratio captures net relative shifts and should be interpreted alongside age‐group–specific incidence trajectories (Figure 1; Figures [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]).

These cross‐site comparisons should not be over‐read as formal etiologic controls. Stomach, lung, breast, cervix, and colorectal cancer differ in biology, background risk, screening modality, and the extent to which in situ lesions are represented in public summaries. Their value here is interpretive rather than mechanistic. Together, they help identify which features of EOCRC are likely to be site‐specific and which are better understood as manifestations of a broader diagnostic environment. Put differently, the control‐panel logic is designed to slow premature generalization: If early‐onset shifts move differently across cancers that share parts of the same screening and diagnostic framework, then “rising early‐onset cancer” cannot be treated as a unitary phenomenon without site‐specific evidence. The value of this comparison is not to establish causal equivalence, but to demonstrate that early‐onset shifts do not move uniformly across cancers even within a shared screening and diagnostic framework.

Breast and cervical cancer are useful because their in situ‐inclusive definitions make detection‐sensitive amplification visible in a way that is often hidden when only invasive rates are plotted. Stomach cancer is useful for the opposite reason: The public tables do not provide an in situ‐inclusive definition, so interpretation must lean more heavily on incidence–mortality coherence and on whether the early‐onset shift moves in the same direction as CRC. Male lung cancer adds a further contrast because an early‐onset ratio can fall when major causal exposures become less common in successive birth cohorts. The purpose of invoking these contrasts is not to claim direct etiologic equivalence with CRC, but to show that population‐level descriptive epidemiology can distinguish a generalized screening‐and‐diagnostic‐framework story from a broad, indiscriminate early‐onset wave.

Early‐onset–leaning age shifts are not necessarily confined to cancers subject to organized screening. In Japan, universal public health insurance and relatively low barriers to diagnostic work‐up may also facilitate earlier clinical detection in some non‐screening‐target cancers through symptom‐driven evaluation or incidental diagnosis. We therefore do not assume that younger‐vs.‐older age shifts are unique to screening‐target cancers. Our reason for centering the present analysis on screening‐target cancers is interpretive rather than exclusive: These cancers provide a more coherent descriptive setting in which detection‐related change, in situ burden, and mortality can be considered together. For many non‐screening‐target cancers, those components are less directly aligned, making broader cross‐cancer comparison possible but less analytically coherent within a single framework.

The contrast is also visible when early‐onset case counts are considered against the background population. In Japan, the population aged 20–49 declined over time, but the screening‐target cancers did not show a uniform pattern within that demographic setting (Figure S5). CRC showed increasing early‐onset case counts despite a shrinking younger population, whereas stomach cancer showed declining early‐onset case counts together with declining incidence rates. These observations reinforce our view that screening‐target cancers should not be interpreted as a uniform group and that broad early‐onset narratives require site‐specific scrutiny.

The stomach cancer data are also informative across the 2016 registry transition. If the absence of an EO signal before 2016 mainly reflected incomplete ascertainment, a more EO‐like pattern might be expected after nationwide registry implementation. That is not what the Japanese stomach data suggest.

Limitations

The most important limitation is that public summary statistics do not provide consistent stage‐stratified incidence trends, particularly for advanced or metastatic disease in younger adults. Accordingly, the present study cannot definitively distinguish detection‐related change from a concurrent true increase in clinically significant invasive EOCRC. This limitation is central to the interpretation of the present findings.

Public summaries generally lack consistent stage‐specific incidence; mortality is shaped by lag and therapeutic progress [31]; and registry transitions and COVID‐related disruptions can perturb detection intensity [14].

Birth‐cohort analyses are conceptually appealing but are limited by the well‐known identifiability problem in age–period–cohort modeling [20, 32], which requires careful modeling choices and transparent sensitivity analyses. We therefore treat cohort‐based results as triangulation rather than as stand‐alone proof of etiology.

A related limitation is that descriptive epidemiology can tell us more readily when a strong claim is poorly supported than when a mechanistic explanation is fully established [33]. That asymmetry is useful for interpretation but is also a limitation. A pattern that looks artifact‐leaning does not mean that no biologically meaningful EOCRC subgroups exist; it means only that the population signal, as currently summarized, does not by itself justify reifying the entire category as a growing invasive entity. Conversely, even a risk‐leaning pattern in invasive incidence would still require additional work to distinguish secular exposure change, cohort replacement, earlier diagnosis of the same disease burden, and stage redistribution. Our framework should therefore be read as a set of falsification‐oriented checkpoints rather than as a complete etiologic model.

Even with these limitations, the present analyses show that incidence trends alone are insufficient to justify a true‐rise interpretation of clinically significant EOCRC.

Future Directions

Stage‐resolved incidence in younger adults, especially distant metastatic disease [18], is the most decisive next step for clarifying whether true risk is increasing. Mechanistic signatures should be paired with explicit subtype definitions and population‐level time trends of subtype incidence or proportion. Cohort carryover analyses provide complementary evidence over longer horizons [20, 32].

Minimal evidentiary requirements for stronger “true rise/new entity” claims: To argue that early‐onset CRC represents a growing etiologic entity (or that a mechanistic subtype is expanding), the field should report explicit, testable population‐level signatures rather than mechanistic plausibility alone. At minimum, this includes: (1) invasive‐only robustness (the increase persists when restricted to invasive CRC and is not driven primarily by in situ inflation); (2) corroboration in advanced disease among younger adults (e.g., increasing distant metastatic incidence) [18]; (3) time trends of clearly defined molecular/clinicopathologic subtypes (incidence or proportion increasing over time within early‐onset invasive CRC) [33]; (4) cohort carryover consistent with etiologic change [20, 32]; and (5) sensitivity to screening and diagnostic framework change (screening/diagnostic intensity proxies) to demonstrate that the signal cannot be explained by detection alone.

From a practical standpoint, the most useful next studies will be those that connect levels of evidence that are too often discussed separately. Registry‐based stage trends, subtype‐resolved incidence, screening utilization, interval‐to‐diagnosis measures, and cohort‐pattern analyses should be examined within a single interpretive frame rather than in parallel literatures that rarely confront one another. Just as importantly, reproducibility should extend to the descriptive base itself. Because our present workflow can be rerun from the three public source workbooks, disagreements about interpretation can be based on shared underlying summaries rather than opaque intermediate data handling. This kind of transparency does not settle substantive debates, but does meaningfully raise the bar for claims that a true rise has been demonstrated.

This review also underscores a broader methodological point. Descriptive epidemiology is most useful not when it is asked to confirm a preferred narrative, but when it is used to test whether that narrative remains compatible with the available population‐level evidence. In EOCRC research, that compatibility requires particular attention to detection‐sensitive incidence, mortality, in situ burden, and demographic context.

Conclusions

Using Japan's publicly available summary statistics, EOCRC trends are most informatively interpreted through integration of age‐group‐specific incidence, mortality, and, where available, in situ‐inclusive decomposition. A graphical summary of this interpretive framework is provided in Figure 3. The resulting descriptive signatures are compatible with substantial detection‐related effects and therapeutic progress, but they do not exclude a concurrent increase in invasive EOCRC. Stronger claims of a growing EOCRC entity should therefore be supported by stage‐resolved incidence trends in younger adults, particularly advanced or metastatic disease, and/or time trends of clearly defined molecular or clinicopathologic subtypes, rather than by mechanistic plausibility alone. Public summary statistics cannot establish etiology, but they can clarify what current trend data do and do not support, and can provide transparent, reproducible checks against inference inflation.
