Cystic dysplasia of the rete testis in adulthood: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Oussama G. Nasrallah, Mohammad W. El Mir, Mohammad W. Fawaz, Jana H. Mahdi, Riad A. Khouzami, Bassel G. Bachir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Rete testis dysplasia is a cystic anomaly arising from the rete testis presenting normally in the pediatric population. These cases usually regress spontaneously without the need for surgical intervention. There are rare, reported cases of rete testis dysplasia in adulthood, which have been managed surgically.Case presentation:A 58-year-old man presented with lower urinary tracts symptoms and found to have a slightly larger right testicle namely in the lower pole near the epididymis. Investigation was done using ultrasound of the testicles with Doppler showing an 8 mm cyst contained in 16.5×12.1 mm cystic dysplasia near the rete testis. MRI of the testicles revealed a small intratesticular cyst with adjacent band-like signals, in keeping with rete testis dysplasia. This patient was placed under surveillance and the rete testis dysplasia is stable after 6 months and will not be operated on unless progression on ultrasound is encountered.Clinical discussion:The management of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis has been evolving with time. While there have never been clear-cut guidelines on the treatment of this condition, a radical orchiectomy of the affected testicle had traditionally been the preferred treatment option. There have only been three case reports of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis in adults, none of which are known to have been managed by observation.Conclusion:In conclusion, the authors report a unique case of rete testis dysplasia being managed conservatively showing the benign features of such a pathology, which may be actively surveyed through sequential imaging.

Rete testes is a collection of tubules in the mediastinum testes that serve to carry sperm from the seminiferous tubules to the vasa efferentia. One rare benign congenital anomaly of the rete testes is cystic dysplasia, which generally presents in the pediatric age group1. It usually presents as a unilateral painless testicular trans-illuminating enlargement caused by a group of cystic lesions that compress the parenchyma of the surrounding mediastinum testes1,2. We present a rare case of an adult found to have cystic dysplasia of the rete testes, upon investigation of with a right testicular nodular mass, with no other urologic abnormalities, who was placed on active surveillance for monitoring of this condition.

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria3.

Case presentation

We present a case of a 58-year-old male known to have dyslipidemia and benign prostatic hyperplasia presenting for irritative lower urinary tract symptoms, which are urgency, frequency, nocturia. The patient denied having any testicular pain or discomfort and denied any history of testicular trauma or torsion in childhood. Additionally, he has never had any surgical intervention in the inguinal area or the testicles. On physical examination, he was noted to have normal-sized testicles with a slightly larger and firmer contour in the lower pole of the right testicle near the epididymis and a right inguinal bulge. Upon questioning this physical exam finding, the patient confirmed having known about it since his hydrocelectomy 30 years ago. Patient underwent ultrasound examination of the testicles with a Doppler to characterize the lesion felt on exam, which showed a 8 mm cyst in diameter contained in the 16.5×12.1 mm cystic dysplasia near rete testis as shown in Figure 1. To complete the evaluation of a testicular lesion, serum alpha fetoprotein, b-HCG and LDH were performed and were all in the normal range: 3.4 ng/ml, 0 mIU/ml, and 230 IU/L, respectively. MRI of the pelvis was performed visualizing 6 mm cyst in the lower posterior aspect of the right testis with a lesion with a 4 mm isointense, enhancing nodule abutting the cyst consistent with rete testis dysplasia as shown in Figure 2A. Additionally, a fat-containing right inguinal hernia was noted on MRI as shown in Figure 2B. An Ultrasound examination was done for the kidneys and pelvis, which revealed the presence of normal bilateral kidneys. The patient was placed under surveillance for repeat Ultrasound examinations scheduled every 3–6 months.

The visualized lesion was deemed stable in size and the patient is to be kept on active surveillance every 6 months and will not be operated on unless a progression or novel finding on ultrasound is encountered.

Discussion

Definition and associated conditions

Cystic dysplasia of the rete testis is a rare benign congenital anomaly of the genitourinary system mainly seen in the pediatric population1. Since its initial description in 19734, 66 cases of this anomaly have been reported in the literature1. A systematic review of these case reports revealed that this anomaly presents at any time between birth and the age of 18 years, with a mean of 5.2 years1. It is characterized by a unilateral testicular enlargement caused by multiple irregular cystic lesions of various sizes compressing the surrounding testicular parenchyma at the rete testis or mediastinum testis2. While the most common clinical presentation of cystic dysplasia of rete testis is a painless unilaterally enlarged testicle that transilluminates, this entity has been reported to be associated with penile or testicular pain in some cases1. Interestingly, this rare entity is often not merely an isolated finding, as several cases of cystic dysplasia of rete testis have also reported associated ipsilateral genitourinary anomalies, most frequently renal agenesis and multicystic dysplasia of the kidney5. These genitourinary abnormalities have also been reported contralaterally to the cystic dysplasia of rete testis in some cases1. Many hypotheses have been postulated to establish an etiology for this anomaly. The leading hypothesis was laid down by Leissring et al. 4; it is based on an embryological failure of reciprocal induction early on in gestation between the caudal end mesonephric duct, which ultimately forms the epididymis and vas deferens, and the germinal epithelium, which ends up forming the rete testis. This hypothesis also attributes the associated ipsilateral genitourinary abnormalities to disruptions at the cephalic end of the mesonephric duct that interfere with normal urogenital development6. Another hypothesis proposed by Nistal et al. 7 owes this cystic dysplasia to the early maturation of the rete testis prior to puberty, a time when the seminiferous tubules have not yet matured and canalized. This early maturation of the rete testis leads to their secretion of fluid that accumulates and cannot be drained by the immature tubules, resulting in cystic dysplasia6. By puberty, the seminiferous tubules undergo canalization, developing a lumen that allows drainage from the fluid from the rete testis6. This hypothesis aligns with the spontaneous regression of the cystic dysplasia seen in several cases of this anomaly.

Clinical presentation and differential diagnosis

The clinical presentation of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis is not specific and may resemble other benign and malignant conditions2. This emphasizes the critical role of a thorough diagnostic workup for this anomaly. Hence, ultrasound remains the gold standard when it comes to the initial diagnosis, and with the latest advancements in Doppler technology and the available ultrasound modalities, its role in the diagnosis has become even more important due to its ability to differentiate benign and malignant conditions with greater accuracy2. Nevertheless, ultrasound alone is not sufficient to rule in a diagnosis of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis, even when its findings are highly suggestive of the condition. The differential diagnosis that is generated upon encountering such a finding on ultrasound includes: epidermoid, dermoid, inflammatory, post-traumatic, or congenital testicular cysts8. In addition, tumors such as teratoma, granulosa cell tumor, and testicular cystic lymphangioma should be ruled out as they are a main part in the differential9. For that reason, testicular tumor markers such as human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), alpha fetoprotein (AFP) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) should also be obtained to determine the nature of cystic testicular growth on ultrasound2. MRI may also be utilized in the diagnostic assessment, but it is only complimentary to the ultrasound and can confirm the subtle findings visualized on ultrasound2. The results of the above workup guide the physician’s suspicion toward a probable diagnosis of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis. If the results are inconclusive, a surgical frozen biopsy must be performed to confirm the pathophysiology underlying the presentation of testicular swelling2.

Management

The management of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis has been evolving with time. While there have never been clear-cut guidelines on the treatment of this condition, a radical orchiectomy of the affected testicle had traditionally been the preferred treatment option10. This is mainly due to the incomplete understanding of the benign nature of this condition, not to mention the lack of advanced imaging modalities in the past2. Therefore, with the aim to preserve all aspects of testicular function, other more conservative treatment routes have been followed in numerous cases of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis, one of which is testicular-sparing surgery2. However, this approach has not provided definitive resolution of dysplasia, compared to the radical orchiectomy. Thus, recurrence of the dysplasia has been reported in 45.5% of the patients who opted for the testicular-sparing surgical route after a median duration of 12 months1. Another more conservative approach is one of observation only via a series of physician follow-ups. The theme of this approach is watchful waiting, whereby a series of monthly follow-ups for the first 3–6 months after diagnosis is implemented2. An ultrasound is performed at every visit to inspect the size of the cystic growth and the status of the dysplasia2. This approach is especially useful in cases where the diagnostic suspicion of cystic dysplasia or rete testis is high, characterized by multicystic testicular changes on ultrasound and negative tumor markers5. Contini et al. 1 have shown it to be promising, as 43.8% of patients who were only observed displayed regression of the dysplasia at the rete testis on ultrasound, and complete resolution of the dysplasia was observed after a median duration of 59.6 months after the initial diagnosis. The risks of conservative management include the upstaging of testicular tumors through misdiagnosis, which in turn would delay treatment for the patient, loss of follow-up with the patient, which in turn would delay diagnosis and detection of growth or progression, and the psychological impact on the patient in living with a condition that has not been diagnosed through pathologic examination.

Summary of similar findings in the literature

The available findings represent a promising advocate for management by watchful observation. However, given the rare nature of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis, the data on it may be considered insufficient and unreliable, as this data has only been extrapolated from case reports. Add to it the fact that the patient in our case is an adult, even though cystic dysplasia of the rete testis is predominantly seen in the pediatric age group. Adults may suffer from tubular ectasia of the rete testis, a condition that is very similar sonographically and histologically to cystic dysplasia, though being mostly a bilateral anomaly not associated with other genitourinary abnormalities11. There have only been 3 case reports of cystic dysplasia of the rete testis in adults, aged 23, 31, and 63, respectively, none of which are known to have been managed by observation12.

Conclusion

This is the first reported case whereby a conservative observation approach is reported in an adult. This case may represent a first step towards future research of the behavior of such an entity and the best treatment approach to provide for the patient as watchful observation and close follow-up may be done in select patients who are compliant. The differential diagnosis of such an entity is broad and malignant conditions need to be ruled out prior to conservative management.
