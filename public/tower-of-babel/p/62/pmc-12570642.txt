Triglyceride-glucose index, a silent predictor for osteosarcopenic adiposity occurrence and risk of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality
Authors: Xue Shao, Ludan Liang, Xiujin Shen, Xiabing Lang, Yingying Wang, Weiqiang Lin, Rending Wang, Chunhua Weng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe evidence linking the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index to osteosarcopenic adiposity (OSA) in young and middle-aged populations is limited. This study aimed to investigate the association between the TyG index and OSA, and to determine the predictive value of the TyG index for all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in OSA patients.MethodsThis cross-sectional and cohort study enrolled 8,098 participants aged 20–59 in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (1999–2006 and 2011–2018). Mortality was determined by linkage to the National Death Index through December 31, 2019. Multivariable logistic regression models and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were constructed to determine the association between the TyG index and the prevalence of OSA. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression and competing risk analyses were employed to evaluate the association between the TyG index and mortality among OSA patients.ResultsThere were 4,153 participants in the 1999–2006 cycle (weighted proportion male, 50.8%; mean [SD] age, 39.53 [10.98] years), and 3,945 in the 2011–2018 cycle (weighted proportion male, 50.7%; mean [SD] age, 39.00 [11.72] years). A higher TyG index was significantly associated with a greater prevalence of OSA, with an odds ratio (OR) of 4.52 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.78, 5.42) for the highest quartile. A J-shaped association was identified between the TyG index and the prevalence of OSA. Additionally, during a mean follow-up period of 131.1 months, OSA patients with the TyG index ≥ 8.5 exhibited a significantly elevated risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.37, 2.71) and CVD mortality (HR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.13, 3.88). Furthermore, sex disparity markedly affected the association between the TyG index and the prevalence of OSA and all-cause mortality in OSA patients.ConclusionsThis study revealed that a higher TyG index was associated with an increased risk of OSA and predicted a higher all-cause and CVD mortality in OSA patients. The implementation of public health interventions to reduce the TyG index may prove an effective method to decrease the incidence of OSA and related mortalities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01985-1.

Background

Bone, muscle, and adipose tissues, which constitute the primary body compositions, function as endocrine organs that maintain the crosstalk and homeostasis among themselves and other organs through a complex network of molecular signaling [1, 2]. However, pathological conditions such as aging, malnutrition, and inflammation disrupt this homeostasis, causing impairments of these tissues and exacerbating each other. This eventually leads to the development of osteopenia/osteoporosis, sarcopenia, adiposity, or even a combination of all three conditions, which is known as osteosarcopenic adiposity (OSA) syndrome [1, 3–5].

OSA has been demonstrated to increase the risk of falls, fractures, and mortality [1]. It also contributes to the development of chronic diseases, including cardiometabolic diseases and restrictive spirometry patterns, which further exacerbate OSA [6, 7]. Thus, OSA has a profound impact on daily life, life expectancy, and socio-economic burdens. Notably, early-onset OSA has also been identified in young adults aged 18–21 [8]. This is comparable to early-onset type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which may lead to more severe outcomes and a higher risk of early mortality [9–11]. Therefore, it is imperative to identify the risk factors and the potential mechanisms of OSA to prevent the occurrence and delay the progression as early as possible.

Insulin resistance (IR) is defined as a state of reduced responsiveness to insulin, which often precedes the onset of diabetes [12]. Recent studies have indicated that IR plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and adiposity, respectively. Furthermore, these body composition abnormalities have been demonstrated to in turn contribute to the development of IR [13]. A novel scoring system, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, has recently been developed as an insulin-independent measure of IR, which is calculated from fasting blood glucose (FBG) and triglyceride (TG) levels [14–16]. The TyG index is a highly sensitive and specific diagnostic tool for IR [14, 17, 18], exhibiting a strong correlation with the gold standard hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HIEC) [19]. Moreover, the TyG index could predict the incidence of visceral obesity, cardiovascular disease (CVD), metabolic syndrome (MetS), and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), as well as mortality in these conditions [16, 20–23]. Additionally, it is associated with bone mineral density (BMD) and sarcopenia [24–26], but the conclusions remain controversial and limited.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between the TyG index and OSA, and the prognostic value of the TyG index for all-cause and CVD mortality in patients with OSA, based on pooled data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study design and population

The data analyzed in this study were extracted from NHANES for the 1999–2006 and 2011–2018 cycles. NHANES is a major scientific program conducted biennially by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The purpose of this program is to measure the health and nutritional status of all residents of the United States by questionnaires, physical examinations, and biospecimen testing. NHANES received approval from the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [27].

Our study population consisted of individuals aged 20 to 59 years. The following participants were excluded from the study: (1) pregnant participants; (2) participants lacking the information required to calculate the TyG index and to diagnose OSA; and (3) participants without critical covariates (as described in the section on Definition of covariates). The final study population comprised a total of 8,098 participants, with 4,153 in the 1999–2006 cycle and 3,945 in the 2011–2018 cycle (Fig. 1).

Assessment of OSA

Body composition, including lean body mass and BMD, was evaluated using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Sarcopenia was defined by the criteria of the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) and characterized by an appendicular lean body mass (ALM) adjusted for body mass index (BMI) < 0.789 for men and < 0.512 for women [28]. ALM was calculated as the sum of the lean mass in both arms and legs [28]. The BMD of the lumbar spine was calculated as the mean of the BMD values of the first through fourth lumbar vertebrae (measured in grams/cm2). According to the recommendations of the World Health Organization (WHO), osteoporosis was defined as a T-score ≤ − 2.5, while osteopenia was defined as a T-score between − 1.0 and − 2.5 [29]. T-scores were calculated as (BMD respondent - mean BMD reference group)/Standard deviation (SD) reference group. The reference group for calculation of T-scores at the lumbar spine was obtained from non-Hispanic white men and women aged 20 to 29 years in the NHANES 2005–2008 [30]. Obesity was defined as the relative fat mass (RFM) >40% for women and >30% for men. The RFM was calculated as follows: 64 − (20 × height/waist circumference) + (12 × sex), with sex equaling 0 for men and 1 for women [31].

The participants were classified into four categories based on the severity of OSA: OSA 0 (control; without osteoporosis/osteopenia, sarcopenia, or obesity), OSA 1 (with either of the conditions), OSA 2 (with any two of the conditions), and OSA 3 (osteosarcopenic adiposity; with all the three conditions) [32].

Assessment of the TyG index

The TyG index was calculated using the following formula: TyG = Ln (fasting triglycerides [mg/dL] × fasting glucose [mg/dL]/2) [14]. Considering the considerable variability in the TyG index among different populations and the absence of recommended cutoff points in the literature [19, 33], the median TyG value (8.5) of the population in this study was applied to categorize the continuous TyG index variables into insulin-sensitive (TyG < 8.5) and insulin-resistant (TyG ≥ 8.5) groups. To further analyze the association between OSA and the TyG index, the participants were categorized into four groups (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4) by the quartiles of TyG index based on the weighted pooled data.

Determination of mortality

To determine the mortality in the study populations during follow-up, we employed the NHANES public-use linked mortality files linked with the National Death Index (NDI) through December 31, 2019. All-cause mortality refers to deaths from any cause. CVD mortality was determined following the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10, I00–I09, I11, I13, and I20–I51). The participants who died from causes unrelated to CVD were attributed to non-CVD deaths.

Definition of covariates

Given the potential impact of other variables on the association between the TyG index and OSA, a range of covariates were incorporated into this analysis. These included demographic factors (age, sex, race), socioeconomic factors (education, smoking, alcohol consumption, poverty status, marital status) and comorbidities (hypertension, chronic kidney disease [CKD], and CVD). The categorical variables were defined in accordance with relevant guidelines and literature. Race was categorized as Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other [34]. Participants were additionally classified by age group (20–44 and 45–59 years) [35, 36]. Educational level was categorized as below high school, high school graduate, and college graduate or above [34]. Smoking status was determined as never smokers, former smokers, and current smokers based on the criteria of having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and of having quit smoking within the past year [37]. The participants’ alcohol consumption was dichotomized based on the criterion of having consumed at least 12 drinks per year [37]. Poverty status was assessed using the poverty income ratio (PIR) and was stratified into three categories: < 1 (below the poverty threshold), 1–3 (1–3 times above the poverty threshold), >3 (more than 3 times above the poverty threshold) [38]. Marital status was stratified as single, widow, divorced, separated, married, and living with a partner [34].

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 80 mmHg according to the recommendation of the 2017 ACC/AHA high blood pressure guideline [39]. In addition, the participants with self-reported diagnoses of hypertension or using antihypertensive medications were also included in this study. The participants with a history of congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary heart disease (CHD), angina pectoris, heart attack, and stroke were regarded as having a diagnosis of CVD [40]. In accordance with the 2012 Clinical Practice Guideline from the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) organization, the risk of CKD progression was classified into four categories: low, moderate, high, and very high [41].

All covariates in this study were assessed at baseline. The information of age, sex, race, education, smoking, PIR, marital status, self-reported hypertension, and CVD was obtained from the household interview prior to the physical examination at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). Information on drinking was derived from personal interviews during physical examinations, while blood pressure (BP) measurements were taken in the MEC. The albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR) and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) were determined using blood and urine specimens collected in the MEC for subsequent laboratory analysis.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the relationship between the TyG index and OSA, logistic regression models were employed, with adjustment for covariates. The regression models were run separately using data from the 1999–2006 cycle, the 2011–2018 cycle, and the combined data from the 1999–2006 and 2011–2018 cycles. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on logistic regression models were constructed to explore the association between the continuous TyG index variable and the prevalence of OSA. The reference values (odds ratio [OR] = 1) were established at the median TyG value (8.5), with the knots set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the TyG levels.

Person years of follow-up were computed from the date of entry into the study until the date of death or study termination. Cox proportional hazards regression models with adjustments for covariates were constructed to evaluate the independent predictive value of the TyG index in all-cause mortality among participants with OSA. Additionally, competing risk analyses were performed using the Fine and Gray models to assess the association between the TyG index and CVD mortality in individuals with OSA, with non-CVD mortality regarded as the competing risk. Subgroup analyses were conducted on the logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression models, respectively.

The selection of covariates in the present study was based on previous literature investigating the incidence and survival of OSA, and the TyG index in various diseases [6, 21, 42]. Detailly, Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, and race). Model 3 was adjusted for the variables in Model 2 and socioeconomic factors (education, PIR, marital status, alcohol consumption, and smoking). Model 4 was adjusted for the variables in Model 3 and comorbidities (hypertension, CKD risk, and CVD).

The statistical analysis was conducted using the R software (version 4.4.1). All analyses were performed with sample weights incorporated under the NCHS analytical guidelines. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of the participants stratified by the median of the TyG index for each cycle. In total, there were 4,153 participants aged 20 to 59 representing 134,674,296 individuals in the 1999–2006 cycle (weighted proportion male, 50.8%; mean [SD] age, 39.53 [10.98] years) and 3,945 participants representing 109,473,867 individuals in the 2011–2018 cycle (weighted proportion male, 50.7%; mean [SD] age, 39.00 [11.72] years). Participants with the TyG index ≥ 8.5 were more likely to be older, male, of Mexican American and Non-Hispanic White, married and living with a partner, with a lower level of education and family income, a former or current smoker, and with a higher BMI compared with those with the TyG index < 8.5. Furthermore, the prevalence of CVD, high risk of CKD progression, diabetes mellitus (DM), pre-DM, and hypertension was significantly higher in participants with the TyG index ≥ 8.5. Additionally, a greater proportion of participants with OSA 3 was found in Q3 and Q4 compared to Q1 and Q2 of the TyG index (2.7% in Q3 and 1.5% in Q4 in the 1999–2006 cycle; 3.5% in Q3 and 3.8% in Q4 in the 2011–2018 cycle; 3.0% in Q3 and 2.6% in Q4 in the pooled sample; Fig. 2a).

The association between the TyG index and OSA

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the relationship between the TyG index and OSA, as presented in Table 2. The TyG index demonstrated a markedly positive association with the prevalence of OSA 1–3 across the 1999–2005 and 2011–2018 cycles, as well as in the pooled analytic sample. After adjusting for all covariates in Model 4 (Table 2), a significant increase in the prevalence of OSA 1–3 was still observed in Q2, Q3, and Q4 compared to Q1 of the TyG index (the pooled sample: OR = 1.69, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.45, 1.98 for Q2, OR = 2.71, 95% CI: 2.29, 3.22 for Q3, and OR = 4.52, 95% CI: 3.78, 5.42 for Q4, p for trend < 0.001).

According to multivariable-adjusted RCS analyses, the association between the TyG index and the prevalence of OSA 1–3 followed a J-shaped curve (nonlinear p < 0.001, Fig. 2b). Moreover, our findings indicated a significant non-linear relationship between the TyG index and the prevalence of OSA 3 (nonlinear p = 0.002, Fig. 2c), with the prevalence of OSA 3 reaching a plateau when the TyG index exceeded a specific threshold.

The value of the TyG index for risk stratification in OSA was further investigated in multiple subgroups of the population (Supplemental Fig. 1). After adjustment for all covariates, the prevalence of OSA in the TyG index ≥ 8.5 group was significantly higher in each subgroup than in the TyG index < 8.5 group. Additionally, our findings indicated that the positive association between the TyG index and OSA was affected by sex and alcohol consumption (sex, p for interaction < 0.001; alcohol consumption, p for interaction = 0.017).

Mortality risk based on the TyG index in patients with OSA

During a mean follow-up of 131.1 months (interquartile range: 64.0–201.0 months), a total of 374 all-cause deaths occurred in the pooled analytic sample, with 97 CVD deaths and 277 non-CVD deaths. The cumulative incidences of all-cause, CVD and non-CVD mortality were highest among patients with OSA 1–3 and the TyG index ≥ 8.5 at different time intervals, in comparison to other groups (p < 0.001, Supplemental Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 1). To further explore the independent association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality in OSA, Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed (Table 3). The TyG ≥ 8.5 group of patients with OSA 1–3 exhibited a significantly elevated risk of all-cause mortality in comparison to the TyG < 8.5 group (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.37, 2.71, p < 0.001). Moreover, the risk of CVD mortality was investigated using competing risk analyses (Table 3). In the multivariable models, the adjusted HR (95% CI) for CVD mortality in the TyG ≥ 8.5 group of patients with OSA 1–3 was 2.10 (1.13, 3.88), compared to the TyG < 8.5 group (p = 0.018). The TyG ≥ 8.5 group also presented a higher risk of non-CVD mortality in reference to the TyG < 8.5 group (HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.06, 2.22, p = 0.024). Among patients with OSA 0, there was an increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.14, 2.34, p = 0.008) and non-CVD mortality (HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.08, 2.36, p = 0.019) in the TyG ≥ 8.5 group compared to the TyG < 8.5 group in Model 1. However, these findings were not significant in Model 2 and Model 3. The models did not identify a significant difference in CVD mortality in patients with OSA 0.

Discussion

In this nationally representative study, we observed a significant positive association between the TyG index and OSA in adults aged 20–59 years in the United States. Moreover, our study suggested that the TyG index was a predictor of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality among patients with OSA.

With the increase in aging population and obesity rates, OSA has drawn growing concern [43]. It has been estimated 8% of middle-aged and older adults globally and 70.8% women and 47.8% men among nursing home residents in Croatia are affected by OSA [44, 45]. OSA increases the risk of disability and accelerates the progression of various chronic diseases [46]. A large-scale prospective cohort study in the UK demonstrated that patients with OSA exhibit a higher risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease-related dementia (ADRD), and that maintaining a healthy cardiovascular status is a protective factor reducing ADRD risk [47]. In women with breast cancer exposed to aromatase inhibitors, there is a significant association between fat body mass, low lean body mass and vertebral fractures progression [48, 49]. OSA has been proposed as a prevalent target for the prevention and treatment of metabolic complications associated with hormone deprivation therapies among cancer patients [50]. Notably, OSA is significantly associated with elevated all-cause mortality and reduced life expectancy [51]. In consideration of the rising incidence and poor prognosis of OSA, it is imperative to screen for and to identify this condition in its early stage.

The TyG index has been confirmed as a cost-effective, readily available, and accurate surrogate for identifying IR [14]. The principle underlying this static index of IR is that both glucose and triglycerides exhibit an increase in response to IR [14, 52]. A significant negative correlation has been demonstrated between the TyG index and the gold standard HIEC in the assessment of insulin sensitivity, which is characterized by a high sensitivity (96.5%) and specificity (85.0%) [19]. Compared with another commonly utilized indicator of IR, the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), the TyG index performs equally efficacious in evaluating IR [19]and even superior in certain populations [53, 54]. HOMA-IR displays a stronger association with dietary, anthropometric, and inflammatory factors in comparison to the TyG index. Meanwhile, the TyG index represents as a superior predictor of CVD, metabolic syndrome, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [52]. Thus, the TyG index and HOMA-IR can be employed in conjunction to explored the impact of IR on various pathologies. In contrast to the expensive, invasive, and technically demanding HIEC and restricted insulin assay limitations of HOMA-IR, the TyG index is less costly and available in almost all clinical laboratories, which is fully meets the need for widespread screening of OSA.

Previous clinical studies have explored the association between the TyG index and osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and obesity, respectively. A Chinese cohort study has found that the TyG index is an independent risk factor for visceral obesity in patients with T2DM [23]. The Korea National Health and Nutritional Examination Survey (KNHANES) of 2008–2011 has shown a significant positive association between a high TyG index and the risk of sarcopenic obesity in participants aged 60 and above [55]. An analysis of Chinese nationwide health check-up populations has demonstrated a positive association between the TyG index and low bone mass and osteoporosis [24]. Here we propose a novel perspective that an elevated TyG index is an independent risk factor for OSA incidence in young and middle-aged populations.

Moreover, there was a non-linear association with a threshold effect between a higher TyG index and an increased risk of OSA. This phenomenon may indicate, on the one hand, the clinical significance of the TyG index in identifying patients at high risk of OSA. The precise point of the TyG index in the non-linearity is of critical importance in guiding clinical monitoring and interventions for patients with OSA in young and middle-aged populations. On the other hand, the plateau observed in the RCS curve may be attributed to the fact that in participants with exceedingly high levels of the TyG index, the mechanism of OSA is likely to be more complicated. Individuals with high levels of the TyG index had a significantly increased incidence of other metabolic-related diseases, such as coronary heart disease and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [6, 56, 57]. Nevertheless, the role of these metabolic disorders in the pathogenesis of OSA remains unclear [58]. Further stratified analyses according to other metabolic comorbidities in large-scale populations may elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

This is the first study to investigate the association between the TyG index and mortality in OSA patients. The Cox proportional hazards regression models indicated that a higher TyG index was associated with an increased risk of all-cause, CVD, and non-CVD mortality in OSA patients. Moreover, subgroup analyses demonstrated that sex disparity had a significant impact on the association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality. Although the biological mechanisms remain unknown, it seems likely that IR presents a key factor. IR plays a critical role in lipotoxicity, muscle damage, and alterations in bone density and microarchitecture [59–61], which contribute to osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and obesity [62–64]. A study of Korean men and postmenopausal women, which employed HOMA-IR ≥ 2.5 to define IR, has suggested a significant discrepancy in the prevalence of IR among participants with OSA and other abnormal body compositions [65]. In addition, previous studies have proven that differences in sex hormones, sex chromosomes, and epigenetic modifications result in a higher degree of insulin sensitivity and a greater capacity for insulin secretion in women compared to men [66]. Our results highlighted that female patients with the TyG index ≥ 8.5 may be at the highest risk of all-cause mortality in OSA.

Furthermore, IR has been recognized as the principal pathogenic cause and a significant risk factor for CVD in both the general population and those with DM [15, 20]. IR plays a pivotal role in the activation of the vascular endothelial Na+ channel and the reduction of bioavailable nitric oxide. These processes contribute to the development of vascular and cardiac fibrosis and stiffness, ultimately leading to poor outcomes of CVD and high mortality [67]. Therefore, the TyG index, as a validated surrogate for IR, can accurately identify individuals at high risk of CVD mortality among OSA patients. In future studies, we intend to integrate the TyG index with other indicators to provide a multi-dimensional assessment of IR, thereby enhancing the accuracy of predicting OSA incidence and long-term mortality.

There were several limitations in our study. Firstly, the TyG index was only measured at baseline, which may not fully capture its dynamic effects on the incidence and mortality of OSA. Secondly, although the survival status and causes of death were determined by the mortality files linked to NDI, NHANES were based on a cross-sectional survey conducted in a single center. Thus, the causality between the TyG index and mortality in OSA patients could not be fully elucidated. A multi-center longitudinal study would comprehensively monitor changes in the TyG index, the onset of OSA, and mortality. Thirdly, the diagnosis of hypertension and CVD was primarily dependent on self-report, which may prone to misclassification, although certain measures have been taken during data collection to avoid this potential bias [68]. Lastly, given the substantial variability of triglyceride levels across diverse ethnic groups, the optimal cut-off value of the TyG index for IR has not been standardized. Our study employed the median value in this population as the reference standard. Therefore, the applicability of the TyG index and its optimal cut-off value need to be further evaluated in different populations.

Conclusions

In this national sample of US participants aged 20–59, a higher TyG index was associated with an increased risk of OSA, and was predictive of higher all-cause and CVD mortality in OSA patients. Therefore, the TyG index is a valuable tool for assessing the risk of OSA and predicting the prognosis of OSA patients. Further prospective research is required to investigate whether intervention with the TyG index would enhance the clinical outcomes in young and middle-aged OSA patients.
