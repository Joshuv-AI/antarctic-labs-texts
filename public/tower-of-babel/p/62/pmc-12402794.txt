Preclinical Evaluation of PTK7-Targeted Radionuclide Therapy
Authors: Kim Lindland, Sara Westrøm, Srdan M. Dragovic, Ruth Gong Li, Marion Masitsa Malenge, Betty Ho, Asta Juzeniene, Tina Bjørnlund Bønsdorff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein tyrosine kinase 7 (PTK7), a receptor found in tumor-initiating cells, is expressed in various malignancies, including ovarian cancer. Whereas PTK7 has been explored as a target for antibody–drug conjugates, this study is the first to investigate its potential for targeted radionuclide therapy. We developed a murine monoclonal IgG1 antibody (mOI-1) using hybridoma technology and generated a chimeric version (chOI-1) with human IgG1 constant regions. A cell-based screening approach using a library of 6,100 cell surface proteins identified PTK7 as the target, confirmed by flow cytometry and surface plasmon resonance analyses. IHC showed strong PTK7 expression in ovarian cancer tissues, and in vitro studies demonstrated specific binding and internalization of OI-1 in the ovarian cancer cell line SKOV-3-luc. Biodistribution studies using 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 injected intravenously in xenograft mice with subcutaneous SKOV-3-luc revealed high tumor uptake and retention. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed by intraperitoneal treatment with 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 in an intraperitoneal xenograft model, showing significant tumor growth inhibition compared with nonradioactive controls. This study provides the first investigation of a PTK7-targeting antibody (OI-1) as an antibody–radionuclide conjugate (212Pb–labeled) in a preclinical model of intraperitoneal ovarian cancer. These results support further investigation of OI-1 as a candidate for targeted radionuclide therapy in PTK7-expressing cancers.

Introduction

Protein tyrosine kinase 7 (PTK7) is expressed in various malignancies, including ovarian cancer, and its elevated expression correlates with a poor prognosis (1–4). It is involved in Wnt signaling and can modulate cancer progression and metastasis through heterodimerization with active receptor tyrosine kinases (5, 6). As a pseudokinase lacking catalytic activity in its intracellular domain, PTK7 is insensitive to traditional tyrosine kinase inhibitors, necessitating alternative therapeutic strategies (5). Interest in PTK7 as a therapeutic target has predominantly focused on its overexpression in tumors, such as antibody–drug conjugates and chimeric antigen receptor T cells (2, 7–9). Additionally, preclinical data suggest that anti-PTK7 antibodies can directly inhibit tumor growth (10) and angiogenesis (11). Despite the interest, there has been no investigation into PTK7 as a target for radionuclide therapy. Our study aims to address this gap by evaluating the potential of PTK7-targeted α therapy using a lead-212 (212Pb) labeled antibody, offering a novel approach for treating PTK7-expressing malignancies.

Ovarian cancer, the seventh most common cancer among women worldwide, has poor prognosis and presents significant treatment challenges (12), with a global five-year net survival rate of 30% to 50% (13). Late-stage diagnosis is a major factor contributing to poor outcomes, as most cases are detected after the cancer has spread within the peritoneal cavity (14). Despite advancements in treatment protocols, including cytoreductive surgery, platinum-based chemotherapy, and targeted therapies, such as bevacizumab and PARP inhibitors, the recurrence rate in patients with ovarian carcinoma exceeds 70% (15–18). This high recurrence rate, particularly due to intraperitoneal (IP) disease spread, underscores the unmet need for novel and more effective therapeutic approaches.

Recent clinical data have demonstrated the potential of PTK7-targeted therapies for ovarian cancer. A first-in-human phase I study of cofetuzumab pelidotin (PF-06647020), a PTK7-targeted antibody–drug conjugate, showed encouraging results in previously treated patients with advanced platinum-resistant ovarian cancer (8). The study reported an overall objective response rate of 27% in patients with ovarian cancer (n = 63), with responders tending to have moderate or high PTK7 tumor expression, as determined by IHC (8). Recent multiomics characterization has implicated PTK7 in epithelial–mesenchymal transition in ovarian cancer (19), which is interesting because PTK7, as a regulator of Wnt signaling, plays a significant role in epithelial–mesenchymal transition by promoting mesenchymal traits that are associated with enhanced tumor invasiveness and chemoresistance, as evidenced by its correlation with poor prognosis and aggressive disease phenotypes in ovarian carcinoma (20). Our study aimed to build upon these findings by exploring PTK7 as a target for radionuclide therapy in ovarian cancer.

Targeted radionuclide therapy leverages the unique properties of radionuclides to deliver cytotoxic radiation directly to cancer cells, thereby minimizing damage to the surrounding healthy tissues (21). This approach can be broadly categorized as therapies that use α and β emitters, each with distinct characteristics and applications. Targeted α therapy harnesses the high linear energy transfer of α particles, which deposit a large amount of energy over a short distance (40–100 µm), to effectively eradicate micrometastases while minimizing damage to adjacent healthy tissues, making α-emitting radionuclides potent cytotoxic agents well suited for treating micrometastatic diseases (22). Furthermore, α radiation offers a potent cytotoxic payload that is independent of intracellular processing or immune effector functions. Importantly, there are no known cellular resistance mechanisms to α radiation, and clinical data suggest that patients with cancers refractory to all available treatment modalities may still benefit from α radiotherapy (23).

Lead-212, a β-emitting isotope with a 10.6-hour half-life, is suitable for targeted α therapy due of its α-emitting progeny, bismuth-212 (60.6-minute half-life), and polonium-212 (0.3-microsecond half-life; ref. 24). The bifunctional chelator S-2-(4-isothiocyanatobenzyl)-1,4,7,10-tetraaza-1,4,7,10-tetra(2-carbamoylmethyl) cyclododecane (p-SCN-Bn-TCMC; TCMC) enables 212Pb to bind to specific targeting agents, allowing the antibody–radionuclide conjugate to deliver cytotoxic radiation to cancer cells (25). Clinical trials of 212Pb–TCMC–trastuzumab for ovarian cancer have shown promising outcomes, with mild and transient adverse events (26, 27). Additionally, other α-emitting radiopharmaceuticals, such as 211At-MX35-F(ab′)2, have been explored in clinical trials for intraperitoneal administration, whereas preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential of targeting molecular markers like TAG-72 and mesothelin in ovarian cancer models (28–30).

Our evaluation of the novel mAb OI-1 included target identification, IHC, binding and cellular internalization studies, biodistribution analysis using lutetium-177 (177Lu), and efficacy studies using 212Pb in a preclinical xenograft ovarian cancer model.

Materials and Methods

Antibodies

A murine monoclonal IgG1 antibody with an unknown target, designated as mOI-1, was generated using mouse immunization and standard hybridoma technology. Specifically, mice were immunized with whole tumor cells, followed by booster immunization with extracts of cellular membrane proteins. Spleen cells from immunized mice were fused with myeloma cells to create hybridomas, which were screened for specific binding to cancer cell lines by flow cytometry. Positive clones were further screened for lack of binding to human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. The antibody-producing hybridoma was cultured for the purification of mOI-1 antibody by Diatec Monoclonals. A patent application describing the OI-1 antibody, including its complete sequence and preparation method, is published and publicly available (31). A chimeric version of OI-1, designated as chOI-1, containing murine variable regions, human IgG1, and kappa constant regions, was produced by GenScript using recombinant cloning and was expressed in Chinese hamster ovary cells. mOI-1 corresponds to WO2025040588A1, examples 1 and 2; the chimeric IgG1 variant corresponds to examples 9A and 9B (31). All antibody preparations were >99% pure (size-exclusion chromatography–high-performance liquid chromatography) with endotoxin levels ≤0.1 EU/mg as determined by the manufacturer. The human IgG isotype control used for in vivo experiments (31154, lot WJ3400921, RRID: AB_243591) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Ultra-LEAF purified human IgG1 isotype control used for in vitro experiments was acquired from Nordic Biosite (403502, clone QA16A12, Kristiansand, Norway, RRID: AB_3665710). The antibodies were labeled using Alexa Fluor 488 (Alexa 488) Protein Labeling Kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (A10235, Oslo, Norway) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Goat anti-human Alexa 488 secondary antibody was procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific (A11013, lot 2273669, RRID: AB_2534080).

Identification of target

Retrogenix Cell Microarray Technology [Charles River Laboratories (formerly Retrogenix)] was used to identify the target antigen of the OI-1 antibody. This involved a multiphase screening process using a library of transfected HEK293 cells (RRID: CVCL_0045). The microarray technology is based on slides spotted with vectors expressing individual targets as well as ZsGreen for transfection control. The library of target genes encodes the full-length human plasma membrane proteins, cell surface–tethered human secreted proteins, and heterodimeric proteins to be screened and is reverse-transfected into HEK293 cells. Initially, a prescreen was performed with slides spotted with expression vectors encoding negative and positive controls (mouse FCGR1, human CD86, and EGFR). The purpose of the prescreen was to assess transfection efficiency and evaluate potential nonspecific background binding of the mOI-1 antibody to native, untransfected HEK293 cells. The mOI-1 antibody was tested at concentrations of 2, 5, or 20 µg/mL at 4°C alongside antibody controls and added to live transfected cells before fixation. Binding was assessed using an AF647 anti-mIgG H + L detection antibody validated for this system. The following library screen involved more than 6,100 expression vectors representing human plasma membrane proteins, secreted proteins, and 396 heterodimers arrayed in duplicate. The mOI-1 antibody (20 μg/mL) was screened against two replicates of slide sets in the library screen, and interactions were defined by increased signal intensity compared with the background and analyzed using ImageQuant software (GE Healthcare, RRID: SCR_014246). The interactions were categorized as very weak to strong.

Confirmation screen arrays, in which each interaction identified in the library screen along with controls (mouse FCGR1, human CD86, and EGFR), were incubated with 20 µg/mL mOI-1 antibody, murine isotype control, or anti-CD86 CTLA4-mFc. Confirmation screens were performed with antibody incubation on either arrays of live cells before fixation (n = 2 slides) or live cells without fixation (n = 1 slide). Binding was assessed by fluorescence imaging. A verification of target interactions was performed by flow cytometry using HEK293 cells transfected with expression vectors encoding ZsGreen1 alone or in combination with various proteins identified as potential targets in previous steps. Specifically, the transfected proteins included PTK7 (either isoform 1 or isoform 4), ASGR2 (either isoform 1 or isoform 3), GH1, THPO, ROBO2, and CD86, which served as an assay control. The transfections were performed in duplicate to ensure reproducibility. Following transfection, live cells were incubated with either 20 µg/mL of the mOI-1 antibody, 0.2 µg/mL CTLA4-mFc (used as a positive control), or assay buffer alone (negative control).

Validation of target by surface plasmon resonance analysis to recombinant human PTK7 protein and characterization of affinity

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analyses of chOI-1 was conducted by Abzena to validate the target and characterize the affinity. The study utilized two proteins: PTK7 from Acro Biosystems (cat. no: PT7-H52H3, lot 4313A-21BVF1-14Z, PTK7 isoform 1) and ROBO2 from Sino Biological (cat. no: 10310-H08H, lot LC17JA1720). SPR was performed on a Biacore 8K system using a protein A capture sensor chip (Cytiva, cat. no. 29127555) with HBS-P+ running buffer containing 1 mg/mL BSA. The analysis temperature was maintained at 25°C with a flow rate of 30 μL/minutes. The association and dissociation times were set to 240 and 900 seconds, respectively. Chip regeneration was performed using 10 mmol/L glycine-HCl (pH 1.5). chOI-1 was captured at approximately 100 RU, whereas ROBO2 and PTK7 analytes, after being buffer-exchanged into the running buffer, were used in an 8-point, 2-fold dilution series (1.56–200 nmol/L).

To optimize experimental conditions, both multicycle kinetics and single-cycle kinetics (SCK) approaches were evaluated during the method development phase. Multicycle kinetics allowed for detailed optimization of binding parameters by performing alternating cycles of analyte injections and surface regeneration for each concentration. However, based on the results of the optimization phase, SCK was determined to provide the most reliable data for this study and was selected as the final setup for characterizing chOI-1 binding to PTK7. The optimization data are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

TCMC-conjugated chOI-1 antibodies were tested using a setup similar to that used in the experiment described above with modifications: SCK was used in this experiment. A five point 2-fold dilution series of PTK7 from 12.5 to 200 nmol/L without regeneration between each concentration was used, and the association and dissociation times were adjusted to 180 and 300 seconds, respectively.

IHC staining

IHC was performed by LabCorp. The test system consisted of cryosections prepared from primary ovarian cancer tissues. mOI-1 and chOI-1 and control antibodies (human IgG1, cat. 02-7102, Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: AB_2532958 and mouse IgG1, cat. 02-6100; Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: AB_2532935) were conjugated with Alexa 488 fluorescent dyes to facilitate IHC detection. Methods were developed to generate a suitable IHC staining protocol involving titration of test articles and control antibodies at various concentrations (10, 2.5, and 0.625 μg/mL) in ovarian cancer tissue sections. Staining intensity was scored on a scale from 0 to 4, with 0 indicating no staining, 1 indicating weak intensity, 2 indicating mild intensity, 3 indicating moderate intensity, and 4 indicating high-intensity staining that may obscure cellular structures. The staining frequency was graded from − to ++++ based on the percentage of cells showing positive staining, in which − indicates no staining and + indicates positive staining in ≤25% of cells, ++ in 26% to 50% of cells, +++ in 51% to 75% of cells, and ++++ in ≥76% of cells. The assessment was performed by trained histologists and reviewed by a qualified pathologist when required at LabCorp.

Cell lines

The human ovarian cancer cell line SKOV-3-luc, obtained from Bioware/Caliper Life Sciences (cat. no. 78425 Hopkinton, MA, USA, RRID: CVCL_M089) and the osteosarcoma cell line OHS (established at the Norwegian Radium Hospital, RRID: CVCL_B450; ref. 32) were used in this study. SKOV-3-luc cells were established from ascites of a patient with ovarian adenocarcinoma (33). OHS cells originated from a patient with osteosarcoma (32). Both cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat profiling and tested for Mycoplasma contamination prior to experimentation. These procedures were conducted by IDEXX BioAnalytics. Mycoplasma testing was performed as part of the authentication process using PCR-based assays, and no contamination was detected. Cells were used at low passage numbers, typically between 3 to 5 passages after thawing for in vivo experiments, with a maximum of 20 passages for all other experiments. The cells were cultured in cell medium: McCoy’s 5a for SKOV-3-luc (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or RPMI 1640 for OHS (Life Technologies, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. At 80% to 90% confluence, the cells were propagated and harvested by brief detachment with TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 5 minutes. Cells were counted and measured for viability using an Invitrogen Countess II Automated Cell Counter (Life Technologies, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_025370) with trypan blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific) staining to distinguish live and dead cells.

Measurement of PTK7 expression on SKOV-3-luc ovarian cancer cells

The SKOV-3-luc ovarian cancer cell line was harvested and washed with Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS, VWR), and the cell number and viability were determined using a Countess Cell Counter (Invitrogen). The cells were diluted in flow buffer—DPBS containing 0.5% BSA (VWR). For saturation experiments, Alexa 488–labeled chOI-1 antibody was added to 0.2 × 106 cells per tube at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 100 nmol/L. Cells were incubated at 4°C for 3 hours with occasional shaking. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with 500 µL flow buffer and centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 5 minutes. All cells were stained with MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes to specifically label mitochondria in live cells (34). The cells were then washed twice with flow buffer. The washed cell pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of flow buffer with 1 µmol/L DRAQ5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) added 10 minutes before sample collection. DRAQ5 is a cell-permeable reagent with high affinity for double-stranded DNA and is used to exclude dead cells (35). The samples were collected in triplicate using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The experiments were repeated at least twice on independent days. Data were analyzed using CytExpert version 2.4 (Beckman Coulter). Gating was performed on singlets, followed by the specific gating of MitoTracker Red CMXRos and DRAQ5 double-positive populations to exclude dead cells. The assay was not corrected for nonspecific binding; however, the cells were treated with 0.5% BSA 30 minutes before the addition of Alexa 488–labeled chOI-1 to reduce nonspecific binding. Appropriate isotype controls were included in all experiments to set gating thresholds and ensure the accurate quantification of specific binding. The level of nonspecific binding was low and did not significantly affect the interpretation of the results (Fig. 1D). For statistical analysis, the relative median fluorescence intensity (rMFI) was calculated and compared with that of unstained cells using the following formula: (rMFI sample – rMFI unstained)/rMFI unstained. Means and SDs were calculated from triplicate samples for each experiment. For the final graphs, averages and SDs were calculated from multiple experiments. Y is the rMFI, Bmax is the maximum specific binding (maximum rMFI), and X is the concentration of Alexa 488–labeled chOI-1. This analysis was used to determine the apparent equilibrium dissociation constant (KDAPP) of chOI-1 in the SKOV-3-luc cells.

Internalization assay

The internalization of chOI-1 in SKOV-3-luc ovarian cancer cells was assessed by flow cytometry using Alexa Fluor 488–labeled antibodies. On the day of the experiment, 0.35 × 106 SKOV-3-luc cells per well were seeded in 24-well plates (Corning) containing 10% complete cell medium. Alexa 488–conjugated chOI-1 antibody (0.25 µg) was added to the cells and incubated at 37°C on an orbital shaker for the indicated time periods (1, 6, and 24 hours). Thirty to forty-five minutes before the end of the incubation period, the cells were incubated with MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to specifically label the mitochondria in live cells. The culture medium was removed at designated time points, and the cells were washed twice with DPBS. To remove surface-bound antibodies, a stripping buffer containing trypsin (0.01 g/mL in DPBS, pH 8–9, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the cells and incubated for 1 hour, following the method of Lin and colleagues (36). After stripping, the cells were washed twice with flow buffer. Flow cytometry was performed on both nonstripped and stripped cell samples to assess the change in the Alexa 488 signal under the described conditions for each time point. The cells were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) to determine the degree of receptor internalization compared with the total signal. Surface binding was assessed as the rMFI, as previously described. The percentage of internalized signals was calculated as the internalized rMFI signal (trypsin-stripped cells) compared with the total-bound rMFI (nonstripped cells), rMFIInternalized/rMFItotal × 100%.

Preparation of radionuclides and radioactivity measurements

Lead-212 was produced by emanating 220Rn from 228Th, sourced from the Oak Ridge National Laboratory, or from a 224Ra solution, prepared as previously described (37). A simplified single-chamber system was used, consisting of an inverted 100-mL glass flask with a removable cap containing the radionuclide source. 228Th in 1 mol/L HCl was applied to a piece of quartz wool inside the cap that served as the holding material. During decay, 220Rn emanated from the quartz wool and was adsorbed onto the interior surface of the flask as 212Pb. After 1 to 2 days of decay, the flask was carefully detached from the source cap and rinsed with 0.1 mol/L HCl to collect the deposited 212Pb. The activity of 212Pb was measured using a Hidex Automatic Gamma Counter (Hidex Oy) with a 60 to 110 keV counting window or a Capintec CRC-25R radioisotope dose calibrator (Capintec Inc.) with dial setting 662, a dial setting specifically established for the instrument used (38).

177LuCl3 solution was obtained from Isotope Technologies Garching (ITG). 177Lu radioactivity was measured using a Cobra II automatic γ detector (Packard Instrument Company) with a counting window of 15 to 2,000 keV.

Radiolabeling of antibodies with 212Pb and177Lu and subsequent immunoreactive fraction measurements

Antibodies mOI-1, chOI-1, and hIgG1 in carbonate buffer were conjugated with 5- to 20-fold molar excess of TCMC (Macrocyclics Inc) in 5 mmol/L HCl (Merck) at room temperature for 2 hours. Unbound TCMC was removed by exchanging carbonate buffer with 0.9% NaCl (Merck). The extracted 212Pb in 0.1 mol/L HCl obtained from the emanation generator was adjusted to pH 5 to 6 with 5 mol/L sodium acetate (Merck) and mixed with TCMC-mAbs with specific activities of 1 to 50 MBq/mg. The solution was incubated for 30 to 35 minutes in a ThermoMixer (Eppendorf) at 37°C with shaking at 350 rpm and then diluted in a formulation buffer consisting of DPBS with 7.5% (v/v) recombinant albumin (Octapharma), 200 mmol/L sodium ascorbate (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1 mmol/L EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich) at pH 7.4, resulting in 212Pb–TCMC–mAb. The radiochemical purity (RCP) of the final products was assessed using chromatography strips from Biodex and γ counting as previously described. All the 212Pb conjugates used in this study had a RCP of >96%.

For 177Lu radiolabeling, mOI-1 antibody was conjugated to p-SCN-Bn-DOTA (Macrocyclics) at a fivefold molar excess. p-SCN-Bn-DOTA was dissolved in 5 mmol/L HCl and added to the mOI-1 antibody. The reaction mixture was pH-adjusted to approximately 8.5 using 0.1 mol/L carbonate buffer and incubated at 20°C for 2 hours with gentle shaking. The unconjugated chelator was separated from the DOTA-conjugated antibody using a centrifuge filtering cartridge (Vivaspin 15R, 50 kDa MWCO, Sartorius Stedim Biotech), and the buffer was exchanged with 0.9% NaCl. The 177Lu solution in 10 mmol/L HCl (ITG) was mixed with the DOTA-conjugated mOI-1 antibody in 0.5 mol/L ammonium acetate to adjust the pH to 5.5 to 6.0 and incubated at 37°C for 15 to 45 minutes. The RCP of the products was evaluated using chromatographic strips (Biodex). If the purity was below 95%, the conjugate was further purified using a PD10 gel filtration column (GE Healthcare Biosciences). The RCP of the final product was greater than 96%.

The immunoreactive fraction (IRF) assay was performed using cultured SKOV-3-luc cells or cultured or frozen stocks of the high–PTK7 antigen–positive OHS cell line. The use of frozen OHS cells simplified the assay procedure while maintaining its effectiveness in quickly and easily evaluating the IRF of radiolabeled antibodies. IRF was measured using one-point binding assays in vitro as previously described (39). Briefly, a cell suspension, ranging from 10 to 15 × 106 cells with viability >90%, was prepared in flow buffer. Triplicate samples, each consisting of 0.2 mL a cell solution, were incubated at room temperature for approximately 60 minutes with gentle shaking in the presence of 2 ng of radiolabeled mAb. To estimate nonspecific binding, two additional samples were preincubated for 15 to 30 minutes with 20 µg of unlabeled mAb before the addition of the radiolabeled mAb. The total radioactivity of each sample was measured using a Hidex automatic γ counter or Cobra II automatic γ detector. Following centrifugation and washing of the cells three times with flow buffer, cell-bound radioactivity was measured in the cell pellets. The fraction of bound mAb was calculated by dividing the amount of cell-bound radioactivity by the total added radioactivity, and immunoreactivity was determined by subtracting the fraction of nonspecifically bound mAb from the fraction of bound mAb. The mean IRF and RCP results for each type of radiolabeling are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The statistical significance of the differences in IRF between the different TCMC:mAb ratios was determined using GraphPad software with a two-sided unpaired t-test, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Determination of number of antigens per SKOV-3-luc cell

A saturation-binding assay was performed to determine the binding characteristics of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 to SKOV-3-luc cells. The cells were harvested and resuspended in a flow buffer at a concentration of 10 × 106 cells/mL. Increasing concentrations of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 were added to 0.1 mL cell samples in quadruplicate, resulting in final concentrations ranging from 1.6 to 144 nmol/L. Samples were incubated with gentle agitation for 65 to 95 minutes at 4°C. Nonspecific binding was determined by preincubating half of the samples with approximately 100 µg/mL unlabeled mAb for 30 to 60 minutes before adding 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1. After incubation, the cells were separated from the unbound radioligands by centrifugation, and radioactivity in the cell pellet was measured using a Cobra II γ counter. The values of the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) and the maximum number of binding sites (Bmax) were determined using a one-site binding model, which was plotted as a hyperbolic curve using GraphPad software.B=Bmax[T]KD+[T](B)In which B is the number of antigen sites per cell and [T] is the total concentration of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1. The assay was performed in two independent biological replicates.

Mass spectrometry analysis of chOI-1 with different TCMC:Ab ratios

An experimental workflow was established and performed by GenScript Biotech to estimate the number of TCMCs conjugated to chOI-1. For proprietary reasons, certain aspects of the mass spectrometry method remain undisclosed by GenScript Biotech. For the experimental workflow, enzyme cleavage was performed using PNGase at 37°C for 4 hours. The PNGase enzyme produced by GenScript was used at a protein-to-enzyme ratio of 50:1. Following enzymatic cleavage, the samples were loaded into an electrospray ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer (BioAccord System) system from Waters for detection. The specific elution procedure used in the electrospray ionization time-of-flight analysis is confidential. For quality analysis, the detected signal was compared with the theoretical molecular weight to determine the number of TCMCs coupled to the protein. Quantity analysis involves quantification of proteins coupled to different TCMCs through signal responses.

Animals

All animal-related procedures adhered to the Norwegian and European Union guidelines for animal research as well as the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/). The study was approved by the Institutional Committee on Research Animal Care of the Department of Comparative Medicine, Oslo University Hospital, and the Norwegian Food Safety Authority (FOTS permit ID: 5734 and 23766). In vivo experiments were conducted using female athymic nude mice (Hsd: Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu, RRID: IMSR_ENV:HSD-069) ages approximately 7 to 11 weeks, obtained from the Department of Comparative Medicine at the Norwegian Radium Hospital, Oslo University Hospital. In total, 64 animals were included in the study.

Biodistribution of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1

Biodistribution experiments were performed using 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 on 16 athymic female nude mice with subcutaneous SKOV-3-luc xenografts. Mice were injected with 10 × 106 SKOV-3-luc cells in both flanks, and tumor growth was monitored 2 to 3 times weekly using an electronic caliper. Biodistribution experiments commenced when tumor diameters reached 4 to 15 mm, on day 35 post after inoculation. The maximum tumor size for the mice was defined as 20 mm according to our application to the national animal research authority (FOTS ID 5734). Sixteen mice were intravenously injected with 450 kBq of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation on days 1, 3, 4, and 7 after injection. Selected organs and blood were harvested, and their radioactivity was quantified using a Cobra II γ counter. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad software. Unpaired t-tests were used to determine statistically significant differences in uptake across time points for individual tissues and between tumors and different tissues at each time point. P values were adjusted using the Holm–Sidak method to account for multiple comparisons, with Padj < 0.05 set as the significance threshold.

Therapeutic efficacy 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1

Tumors were established in 48 female nude mice ages 7 weeks by IP inoculation with 5 × 106 SKOV-3-luc cells. Three days after cell inoculation, the mice were randomized into six groups, with eight mice per group: each mouse received an IP bolus of either (i) saline, (ii) chOI-1, (iii) 211 kBq 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG, (iv) 384 kBq 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG, (v) 180 kBq 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1, or (vi) 405 kBq 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 (10:1 TCMC:Ab ratio for both OI-1 and hIgG) with an antibody amount of 10 µg per mouse for groups 2, 4, and 6, and 5 µg for groups 3 and 5.

Tumor growth was visualized by bioluminescence imaging on day 43 using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, RRID: SCR_018621). For imaging, each mouse was injected intraperitoneally with 0.2 mL of D-luciferin (Biosynth AG) dissolved in DPBS at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. The mice were anesthetized with sevoflurane and imaged 10 minutes after the luciferin injection.

Mice were observed throughout the study by monitoring their body weights (Supplementary Fig. S1), activity level, posture, and IP tumor development every 2 to 3 days. All animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation at the study endpoint (day 52/53) when the tumor burden was high in the control group, in which the mice were close to the humane endpoint. The tumors were excised, and the total IP tumor weight in each mouse was measured at the study endpoint.

Statistically significant differences between the resulting tumor weights were evaluated using the GraphPad software. One-way ANOVA was used, and the obtained P values were adjusted using the Tukey method for multiple comparisons, with a significance threshold of Padj < 0.05.

Tumor-free fraction (TFF) analysis was performed to evaluate treatment efficacy. Mice were considered tumor-free if their tumor weight was ≤0.020 g (lower limit of the scale was 0.020 g, controlled with a standard weight of 20.0 mg) at the end of the study period. The TFF was calculated for each group by dividing the number of tumor-free mice by the total number of mice in each group. The statistical significance of the differences in the TFF between the 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1–treated groups and the control groups was determined using the GraphPad software and two-sided Fisher exact test, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Identification and validation of the target of OI-1 and affinity measurements

The mOI-1 antibody was screened for binding against a microarray of transfected HEK293 cells expressing 6,100 individual full-length human plasma membrane proteins, cell surface–tethered human secreted proteins, and 396 human heterodimers. The prescreen confirmed minimal nonspecific background binding of the mOI-1 antibody to untransfected HEK293 cells and no binding to any of the spotted targets when added before fixation, ensuring that interactions with target transfected cells in the library and confirmation screens would be distinguishable from untransfected cells. The following library screen identified 36 library interactions, with spot intensities ranging from weak to strong. In the confirmation screen performed on live cells before fixation, 32 of the 36 library interactions were reproducibly observed using the mOI-1 antibody. Twenty-five of the 36 library interactions were classified as nonspecific because they were bound by the mOI-1 antibody and at least one of the negative control antibodies. These nonspecific interactions included Fc γ receptors.

After excluding nonreproducible and nonspecific interactions, seven interactions remained. The mOI-1 antibody specifically interacted with PTK7 (isoform 4, medium intensity; isoform 1, strong intensity), TEK (very weak and weak intensity), ASGR2 (isoform 3, strong intensity; isoform 1, medium intensity), GH1 (weak/medium and medium intensity), and THPO (weak/medium intensity; Table 1). Although classified as nonspecific, the mOI-1 antibody showed an interaction with ROBO2 that was of greater intensity with the mOI-1 than with the controls (Table 1).

IHC results with OI-1 on human primary ovarian cancer

Slide evaluation of the ovarian cancer tissue sections revealed positive membranous staining for both chOI-1 and mOI-1 antibodies in the epithelium and tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. S2). The staining pattern was consistent between the chimeric and murine versions of the antibody.

Cell surface expression and internalization of chOI-1 on SKOV-3-luc

Flow cytometry confirmed the cell surface expression of PTK7 in the SKOV-3-luc cancer cell line using various concentrations of chOI-1 antibody (Fig. 1A). A saturation-binding experiment measured the equilibrium binding to PTK7 in SKOV-3-luc cells, determining the apparent KDAPP of 15.9 ± 2.8 nmol/L based on rMFI data (Fig. 1B).

Similarly, the saturation-binding assay of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 revealed a KD of 7.9 ± 2.9 nmol/L (n = 2; Fig. 1C). The maximum number of binding sites per cell (Bmax) was determined to be 68,605 ± 4,490. These results demonstrated that 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 binds specifically and with high affinity for SKOV-3-luc cells.

The internalization of the chOI-1 antibody in SKOV-3-luc cells was investigated using flow cytometry (Fig. 1D). After 1 hour of incubation of SKOV-3-luc cells with chOI-1 Alexa 488, the surface-bound mAb was stripped using trypsin. The Alexa 488 signal was 2.6 ± 0.3% of the total signal detected in nonstripped cells (Fig. 1E). At 6 and 24 hours, the signal increased to 71.8 ± 12.5% and 68.3 ± 9.9%, respectively (Fig. 1E), indicating significant internalization of chOI-1 over a 6 hour time period followed by stabilization of internalization. The rMFI data at the indicated time points showed a similar trend, with maximum internalization at 6 hours (Fig. 1E). In contrast, the human isotype control Alexa 488 showed no internalization during this time period (Fig. 1E).

Effect of molar ratios of TCMC during chelation reaction of mAb on antibody binding to PTK7

The effect of the molar ratio of TCMC during the chelation reaction of mAb on antibody binding to PTK7 was investigated. The IRF results for 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between the TCMC-to-antibody ratio and IRF values (Supplementary Table S2). As the TCMC-to-antibody ratio increased from 5:1 to 10:1 and 20:1, the IRF decreased significantly (P < 0.01). For the SKOV-3-luc cell line, the IRF values were 65.3 ± 4.9%, 53.5 ± 3.9%, and 43.3 ± 3.2% for the 5:1, 10:1, and 20:1 ratios, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). Similarly, for the OHS cell line, IRF values were 59.6 ± 2.7%, 46.3 ± 8.2%, and 40.3 ± 1.5% at the same ratios (Supplementary Table S2). This trend indicated that a higher molar ratio of TCMC negatively affected the ability of the antibody to bind to its target antigen.

Mass spectrometry was used to determine the number of TCMC attached to chOI-1 (Supplementary Fig. S3). At a 5:1 ratio, most antibodies had 1 to 2 TCMC molecules conjugated to each mAb, with approximately 15% of chOI-1 not bound to TCMC. A 10:1 ratio resulted in 3 to 6 TCMC molecules attached per antibody, up to 7, with approximately 5% unbound chOI-1. At a 20:1 ratio, most antibodies contained up to seven TCMC molecules, with some reaching concentrations as high as 10.

SPR analysis was used to determine the influence of an increased number of conjugated TCMC in chOI-1 on KD measurements using human PTK7. The KD values for different TCMC-conjugated antibodies showed a decreasing trend in affinity with increasing chelator-to-antibody ratios (Table 2). Notably, the 5:1 and 10:1 TCMC:Ab ratio conjugates maintained affinities within 2-fold of the unconjugated chOI-1 (Table 2), whereas the 20:1 TCMC conjugate showed approximately 2.5-fold lower affinity. An overall decrease in signal was observed with increasing TCMC conjugation, suggesting a reduction in the proportion of antibody molecules capable of binding to the antigen. Based on these data, only 5- and 10-fold TCMC ratios were used in the subsequent studies.

Biodistribution of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 in SKOV-3-luc xenograft models

In vivo biodistribution studies were conducted in mice bearing subcutaneous SKOV-3-luc xenografts to evaluate the tumor-targeting capabilities of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 following intravenous administration. The percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (% ID/g) was measured over a 7-day period in the tumors and normal organs (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S3). Tumor uptake progressed significantly from A at day 1 to B at day 7 (Padj < 0.03). In contrast, most normal tissues show decreased or stable uptake over time. The blood retention of 177Lu–DOTA–mOI-1 decreased significantly from 15.4 ± 4.4% ID/g on day 1 to 4.5 ± 1.3% ID/g by day 7 (Padj < 0.003). In addition, the muscle and small bowel showed a significant decrease in uptake from days 1 to 7 (Padj < 0.03). A significant difference was observed between blood and tumor uptake on days 3, 4, and 7 (Padj < 0.01). All other normal tissues showed significantly less uptake than the tumor (Padj < 0.004).

Therapeutic efficacy of 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 in intraperitoneal ovarian cancer

The administration of 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 resulted in a reduction in tumor burden in mice with IP ovarian cancer xenografts. Compared with the nonradioactive groups, the 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1–treated groups showed a statistically significant reduction in tumor burden at the study endpoint (day 52/53; Fig. 3B; P < 0.02). TFF analysis showed that the group treated with 180 kBq of 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 achieved a TFF of 100%, indicating complete tumor eradication, whereas the 405 kBq group achieved a TFF of 87.5% (Supplementary Table S4). In contrast, the nonradioactive control groups had a TFF of 0%. Bioluminescence imaging on day 43 also revealed markedly lower tumor burden in the 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1–treated groups compared with nonradioactive controls (Fig. 3C).

The 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG-treated groups showed reduction in tumor weight compared with nonradioactive groups. However, when compared with the 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1, the 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG-treated groups achieved a TFF of only 25%, significantly lower than the TFF observed in both 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1–treated groups. Specifically, statistical analysis showed that the 180 kBq 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 group had a significant difference in the TFF compared with both 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG groups (P = 0.007), as did the 405 kBq group (P = 0.04). These findings suggest an effect of the targeting specificity of OI-1 for PTK7. Imaging data further support this, as bioluminescence imaging showed more pronounced tumor reduction in 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1–treated groups compared with 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG-treated groups (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

The current study is, to our knowledge, the first to explore targeting of the PTK7 receptor for tumor selective delivery and effect of radionuclide therapy. The human PTK7 antigen was identified and validated as the target of the murine OI-1 antibody. SPR analysis confirmed that chOI-1 exhibited specific binding to PTK7 with no interaction with ROBO2, validating its target specificity. Interestingly, the binding of chOI-1 to PTK7 was best fitted by a two-state analysis model rather than by a typical 1-to-1 binding profile. This suggests that chOI-1 binding may involve a complex interaction mechanism, potentially involving conformational changes in PTK7 upon antibody binding. Such findings highlight the intricate nature of this molecular interaction and suggest that OI-1 may recognize a conformational epitope on PTK7, which is particularly interesting given the existence of multiple PTK7 splice variants. To date, five spliced PTK7 mRNAs have been identified, with some variants lacking specific immunoglobulin-like loops in the extracellular domain or even in the entire kinase domain (5). Our results showed weaker binding to PTK7 isoform 4. Whereas the reasons for this are unclear, isoform 4 is less expressed, not well characterized, and structurally similar to isoform 1, although it is slightly shorter (1,014 vs. 1,070 amino acids; ref. 40). In this study, we focused on PTK7 isoform 1 because of its structural relevance and established role in cancer biology (5). Future studies could expand this analysis to include other PTK7 isoforms to further characterize OI-1’s binding. The chOI-1 antibody showed binding affinity of 64.6 nmol/L to human PTK7 in SPR analysis, which is lower than those reported for other PTK7-targeting antibodies in similar assays, cofetuzumab (0.7–1.4 nmol/L; ref. 2) and mAb13 (0.06 nmol/L; ref. 7). Although lower affinity might reduce the immediate binding strength, it can enhance tissue penetration and distribution within tumors (41).

IHC analysis demonstrated PTK7 expression in the examined human primary ovarian cancer tissues. This observation aligns with previous findings by Kong and colleagues (7), Damelin and colleagues (2), and Xu and colleagues (9), who reported PTK7 expression in ovarian cancer. The role of PTK7 in ovarian cancer has been highlighted as complex by Wang and colleagues (4), who reported lower PTK7 expression in epithelial ovarian carcinomas than in benign tumors. However, clinical data from the phase 1 trial investigating a cofetuzumab–drug conjugate revealed that more than 80% of patients with ovarian cancer were positive for PTK7 expression despite the absence of preselection based on PTK7 levels (8). In contrast, patients with triple-negative breast cancer and non–small cell lung cancer were screened and required to have moderate-to-high PTK7 expression (8).

Whereas our IHC study did not include normal ovarian tissue controls to establish baseline staining, Kong and colleagues (7) found no PTK7 expression in normal human ovarian tissue, and Damelin and colleagues (2) reported PTK7 expression only in ovarian stroma (which is also supported by IHC data from the Human Protein Atlas, http://proteinatlas.org/). These findings are nuanced by the data from Wang and colleagues (4), in which the normal fallopian tube epithelium was positive for PTK7, and also IHC data from the Human Protein Atlas, which show PTK7 expression in several tissues of the female reproductive system. Future investigations with the OI-1 antibody should therefore incorporate such controls, preferentially along with a broader panel of normal tissues, to validate the specificity of the OI-1 antibody. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of PTK7 as a target for ovarian cancer while emphasizing the need for further research to clarify its expression patterns and the implications for therapy.

Our in vitro studies using the human ovarian cancer cell line SKOV-3-luc confirmed PTK7 expression and internalization with properties similar to those reported by Kong and colleagues (7) for their anti-PTK7 mAb.

In drug-loaded therapies including antibody–radionuclide conjugates, optimizing the chelator-to-antibody ratio is important for balancing the radiolabeling efficiency and maintaining the functional integrity of the antibody. In our study, we found that a molar ratio of 5:1 to 10:1 TCMC:Ab during the chelation reaction provided suitable antibody-binding properties for 212Pb–TCMC–mAb conjugates. Our findings align with those of previous research on DOTA-based conjugation (a chelator structurally similar to TCMC (42, 43). Although higher chelator-to-antibody ratios can increase the specific activity by attaching more radionuclides per antibody, this may come at the cost of reduced binding affinity due to steric hindrance or conformational changes. For 212Pb-based therapies, the specific activities reported in the literature typically range from 37 to 370 MBq/mg (44, 45), depending on the experimental setup. In our therapy study, we used specific activities ranging from 36 to 42 MBq/mg, with an average 39 MBq/mg, which is within the range typically used for studies using 212Pb.

Our study evaluated the biodistribution of mOI-1 in mice bearing subcutaneous tumors, using 177Lu (t1/2 = 6.7 days) to align with the systemic pharmacokinetics of the antibody and assess tumor targeting over several days. The biodistribution demonstrated consistently higher tumor uptake compared with normal organs, which demonstrate that OI-1 can bind to SKOV-3-luc xenografts in vivo, validating its transition from in vitro binding to an in vivo setting. Whereas some uptake was observed in normal tissues such as the liver and kidney, this is likely due to physiologic clearance rather than specific PTK7 expression (46). Furthermore, PTK7’s low-level expression in some normal tissues must be considered when translating findings from mice to humans. The high protein sequence similarity between human and murine PTK7 (93% identity and 97% positivity) supports species cross-reactivity of the OI-1 antibody, further validating the use of this model system for preclinical studies.

Although the subcutaneous model does not replicate peritoneal dissemination, it serves as a foundation for understanding the antibody’s ability to target SKOV-3-luc tumors in vivo. Our subsequent focus shifted to IP therapy with 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1. The high linear energy transfer radiation of 212Pb makes it particularly effective against micrometastases, whereas IP administration achieves higher local concentrations at tumor sites and reduces systemic exposure (47, 48). The control 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG-treated group showed tumor growth reduction compared with nonradioactive controls, which may be attributed to the localized delivery method. However, treatment with 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 achieved significantly higher TFF, suggesting an effect of the targeting specificity of OI-1 for PTK7.

Whereas our study demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity of 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 in an IP ovarian cancer model in mice, there are some limitations to consider. The comparison of tumor masses at a single time point may have limited our ability to observe the full extent of the treatment’s effects. A longer observation period could have revealed a more pronounced difference between the 212Pb–TCMC–hIgG and 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 groups, as allowing the mice to live longer might have provided clearer evidence of therapeutic efficacy and specificity. Further studies are needed to examine the extent to which the observed effects are attributable to the targeting specificity of OI-1 for PTK7. Although our biodistribution analysis included normal tissue evaluation, we did not conduct comprehensive toxicity assessments beyond monitoring body weight in our therapy study, and further investigations, including blood counts and detailed histopathologic analysis of normal tissues, are needed to fully assess its safety. Future preclinical studies should focus on evaluating the therapeutic effects over longer time periods (e.g., through survival studies), conducting toxicity assessments, and optimizing treatment parameters. Additionally, biodistribution analyses in IP models are warranted. Given the correlation between treatment response and PTK7 expression in the phase 1 of cofetuzumab pelidotin (8), it would also be of interest to examine the therapeutic effect of 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 in another IP ovarian cancer model in mice, with different PTK7 expression level compared with SKOV-3-luc.

Our work with PTK7-targeted 212Pb–TCMC–chOI-1 contributes to the growing field of α radiotherapy in ovarian cancer. Previous studies have explored various antibody-based approaches for delivering α emitters to ovarian tumors, including clinical trials investigating 212Pb–TCMC–trastuzumab and 211At-MX35-F(ab′)2 for IP administration in patients with HER2-positive or NaPi2b peritoneal carcinomatosis (26–28, 49). Other molecular targets, such as TAG-72 and mesothelin, have also shown promise in preclinical ovarian cancer models (28–30).

Given the PTK7 expression in various malignancies, including triple-negative breast cancer, non–small cell lung cancer (5), and bladder cancer (50), these preliminary results suggest the possibility of expanding this therapeutic approach to other PTK7-expressing cancers.
