Inferior Vena Cava Stenosis Mimicking Right Heart Failure in Carcinoid Heart Disease
Authors: Sofía Vila-Sanjuán, Nuria Vallejo, Gerard Martí-Aguasca, Hug Cuéllar, Mercedes Pérez Lafuente, Jorge Hernando, Alejandro García-Alvárez, Jaume Capdevila, Ignacio Ferreira-González, Gerard Oristrell-Santamaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarcinoid heart disease is a well-recognized complication of serotonin-secreting neuroendocrine tumors. Chronic exposure to vasoactive substances leads to endocardial fibrosis, most commonly affecting right-sided valves. Patients typically present with progressive right heart failure due to severe tricuspid and pulmonary regurgitation. While valvular lesions dominate the clinical picture, serotonin-mediated fibrosis can extend beyond the endocardium to the vascular endothelium. Reports of venous outflow obstruction caused by carcinoid-related fibrosis are exceedingly rare but may produce a phenotype indistinguishable from cardiac decompensation. We describe 2 patients with advanced carcinoid heart disease who developed profound systemic congestion despite normal intracardiac pressures, ultimately attributed to inferior vena cava and renal vein stenosis. This series highlights a diagnostic challenge with important therapeutic implications.Key words: cardiac magnetic resonance, chronic heart failure, echocardiography, imaging, peripheral edema, right-sided catheterization, right ventricle, tricuspid valve

Case Presentation

Patient 1

Clinical presentation

A 52-year-old woman with a grade 1 metastatic intestinal neuroendocrine tumor (Ki67: 0.6%) under second-line everolimus therapy and established carcinoid heart disease (CHD) presented with progressive anasarca and 10-kg weight gain despite escalating oral diuretics.

Diagnostic work-up

Physical examination revealed tense lower limb edema extending up to the waist. Echocardiography demonstrated thickened tricuspid and pulmonary valve leaflets with massive tricuspid regurgitation, severe pulmonary regurgitation, and mild pulmonary stenosis (Figures 1 and 2, Videos 1 and 2). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed no dilatation of the right ventricle, with an end-diastolic volume of 70 mL/m2 and right ventricular ejection fraction of 62%, without myocardial fibrosis (Figure 3).

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging of Patient 1

Imaging demonstrated nondilated right ventricle with preserved volumes and normal systolic function.

Despite aggressive intravenous diuretic therapy, congestion persisted with progressive renal dysfunction, requiring initiation of hemodialysis. Right heart catheterization revealed only mildly elevated right atrial pressure (11 mm Hg), with otherwise normal filling pressures and no pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary arterial pressure: 13/7/10 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure: 6 mm Hg, cardiac index: 2.2 L/min/m2). There was a marked discrepancy between the imaging findings (absence of right ventricular dilatation), the hemodynamic data (near-normal right-sided pressures), and the clinical picture of severe refractory systemic congestion. This incongruity suggested that the congestion might not be explained by cardiac dysfunction alone, prompting further evaluation for extracardiac causes of venous hypertension.

Computed tomography (CT) angiography showed subocclusion of the infrarenal inferior vena cava (IVC) extending into both renal veins. The infrarenal aorta and iliac arteries showed mild narrowing, and positron emission tomography (PET)/CT excluded extrinsic compression or inflammatory vasculitis (Figure 4). Furthermore, laboratory data included negative antinuclear antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, anti–double-stranded DNA, complement levels, rheumatoid factor, antiphospholipid antibodies, and inflammatory markers, ruling out systemic vasculitis.

Expert commentary

The coexistence of massive systemic congestion and normal intracardiac filling pressures should immediately raise suspicion of posthepatic or systemic venous obstruction rather than isolated cardiac failure.1

In patients with CHD, this discrepancy reflects the potential extension of serotonin-induced fibrotic remodeling beyond valvular structures to the venous endothelium. Chronic exposure to vasoactive substances can trigger progressive thickening of the venous wall, leading to luminal narrowing of the inferior vena cava and renal veins.2,3

This phenomenon, although rare, represents a plausible pathophysiologic continuum of carcinoid fibrosis and may clinically mimic refractory right heart failure. Recognition of this mechanism is crucial, as failure to identify venous obstruction can delay appropriate intervention and contribute to irreversible injury.

Intervention and outcomes

Percutaneous stenting of the iliac veins and infrarenal IVC was performed, restoring venous flow. Systemic congestion improved, but renal function did not recover, and the patient remained dialysis dependent. Two months later, she developed lower limb ischemia due to progression of infrarenal aortic stenosis, which was successfully treated by endovascular revascularization with stent implantation. Five months later, the patient died from Listeria monocytogenes sepsis.

Patient 2

Clinical presentation

A 63-year-old woman with metastatic well-differentiated grade 3 intestinal neuroendocrine tumor (Ki67: 33%) involving the liver, bone, and lymph nodes received multiple lines of therapy, including somatostatin analogs, radioligand therapy, and antiangiogenic multikinase inhibitor. Fifth-line therapy with everolimus was started in 2024.

The patient had no prior cardiac history. An echocardiography performed within the previous year showed moderate tricuspid regurgitation without clear features of carcinoid involvement. In June 2024, she developed progressive peripheral edema and fatigue.

Diagnostic work-up

Echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, right ventricular dilatation (basal diameter: 56 mm, midcavity diameter: 44 mm, longitudinal diameter: 79 mm) with septal flattening, consistent with right ventricular volume overload. The tricuspid valve leaflets were thickened and retracted, with restricted motion, findings consistent with carcinoid involvement, resulting in torrential tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 5, Video 3). Despite escalating diuretic therapy, the patient developed refractory anasarca and renal failure, requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. Right heart catheterization revealed normal pulmonary and right atrial pressures (right atrial pressure: 9 mm Hg, pulmonary arterial pressure: 13/2/8 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary pressure: 1 mm Hg, cardiac index: 3.2 L/min/m2). CT angiography showed critical stenosis of the infrahepatic IVC and renal veins due to extrinsic compression by fibrotic tissue, without tumor encasement or thrombosis (Figure 6).

Expert commentary

As in patient 1, the discordance between clinical congestion and hemodynamic findings pointed to venous obstruction. Advanced imaging is essential in these scenarios, as vascular fibrosis may evolve silently in patients with prolonged serotonin exposure. Differentiation from mechanical compression or thrombosis guides management and prognosis.

Intervention and outcomes

Endovascular stenting of the IVC and left renal vein re-established flow, but renal perfusion did not improve. The patient died shortly afterward from progressive multiorgan failure.

Discussion

CHD, a well-recognized complication of serotonin-secreting neuroendocrine tumors,2,4 results from serotonin-mediated fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition, primarily affecting right-sided valves. While valvular lesions dominate, serotonin-mediated fibrosis can extend beyond the endocardium to the vascular endothelium.5 Experimental models support a causal role of serotonin: Chronic serotonin administration induces valvular plaque formation resembling human carcinoid valve disease, and serotonin transporter–deficient mice develop spontaneous valvular and perivascular fibrosis. At the molecular level, serotonin promotes TGF-β–mediated extracellular matrix deposition through 5-HT2B receptor signaling. These pathways are not valve specific, and they have also been demonstrated in vascular tissues.3

Although most experimental work has focused on valvular involvement, the same serotonin-mediated profibrotic signaling may extend to the venous endothelium. Venous stenosis may therefore represent part of a broader continuum of carcinoid vascular injury rather than a separate entity.

Diagnostic insight: recognizing venous obstruction in CHD

In both patients, the key diagnostic clue was the dissociation between marked systemic congestion and normal or only mildly elevated intracardiac filling pressures. In true right heart failure, right atrial pressure correlates with systemic edema; when this relationship is absent, postcardiac venous obstruction should be considered.1

Cross-sectional imaging was decisive. CT angiography demonstrated IVC and renal vein stenosis without thrombosis or tumor invasion, consistent with carcinoid-related vascular fibrosis.6 Failure to recognize this mechanism may lead to inappropriate escalation of diuretic therapy or delay of potentially effective endovascular intervention.

Therapeutic and prognostic considerations

Endovascular stenting may restore venous patency and transiently relieve congestion. However, once venous nephropathy is established, renal recovery is appears limited, reflecting the irreversible nature of advanced fibrotic remodeling.

Therefore, a multidisciplinary cardio-oncology approach—including echocardiography, right heart catheterization, and targeted venous imaging—should be pursued whenever clinical congestion and invasive hemodynamics are discordant. Early detection of venous obstruction may prevent irreversible renal or hepatic injury.

Conclusions

Venous obstruction due to carcinoid-related fibrosis is a rare but clinically significant manifestation of CHD. Awareness of this mechanism is critical for accurate hemodynamic interpretation and timely management. Early recognition can improve symptom control and potentially mitigate end-organ damage in advanced carcinoid disease.
