Cardiac Postpneumonectomy Syndrome: Prevention Is Not Possible
Authors: Daniel L. Miller
Published: 2026

Reply to the Editor

We appreciate the thoughtful comments and insightful questions by Drs Galetta and Serra about our rare case of cardiac postpneumonectomy syndrome (PPS).1,2 Our standard follow-up for patients who receive adjuvant treatment after undergoing lung resection for lung cancer is every 3 months for the first year, every 6 months for the second and the third year, and annually for 7 more years. The patient underwent computed tomography scans at 3 and 6 months after the completion of his adjuvant therapy. There was minimal elevation of the right hemidiaphragm at those time points. We thought his mild symptoms at 6 months were related to recovery from the rigorous adjuvant chemotherapy and weight gain. A computed tomography scan taken 12 months after surgery revealed that his right hemidiaphragm was significantly elevated, with most of the liver within the chest cavity and the right side of his heart compressed. There was no compression of the tracheobronchial tree, which is the most common cause of PPS.

Prevention of this condition at the time of the pneumonectomy with either diaphragmatic plication or saline tissue expander placement is not recommended. Most plication procedures for a paralyzed diaphragm are left-sided, as right-sided plications are less common because of the stabilizing effect of the liver. Same-time plication is technically more complex because of the normal nonatrophied diaphragmatic muscle, which has increased risk of plication failure. Even after right-sided phrenic nerve resection, diaphragmatic elevation may not be clinically significant. Placing an intrathoracic saline implant during resection is not advised as the pleural cavity has not yet stabilized in position and size. It is best to allow the pleural cavity to normalize to its postoperative state for at least 6 months to minimize implant complications. At the time of our cardiac PPS correction, we measured the size of the pleural cavity with normal saline; 800 mL filled the cavity. We used a 750-mL prefilled saline tissue expander, which was a perfect fit. Also, at the time of the initial pneumonectomy, there are 4 exposed staple lines that could cause damage to the implant with leakage or exsanguination from disruption of one of the vascular staple lines. Within 3 to 6 months after resection, the mediastinum on the affected side develops a fibrotic layer over the staple lines, protecting against potential implant-related injuries. Furthermore, selecting a larger implant will not result in cardiac compression because the mediastinum is fixed and immobile. Placement of tissue expander implants with external injection ports in a new postpneumonectomy space is not recommended because of a substantial risk of empyema and potential overexpansion, which may lead to displacement of a mobile mediastinum, resulting in acute cardiac compression.

Thank you for allowing us to address your questions about our rare case of cardiac PPS.
