Gut microbiota influences gastric cancer through the mediation of inflammatory proteins: A Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Chenyang Yu, Fule Wang, Xinfang Zhang, Wenzhao Guo, Changchuan Bai, Guanhua Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A robust correlation exists between gastric cancer (GC) and intestinal flora, potentially facilitated by inflammatory proteins. Nevertheless, the precise relationship between GC, intestinal flora, and their respective mediators remains unclear. The objective of this study is to employ a Mendelian randomization (MR) study to determine the cause-and-effect link between GC, inflammatory proteins, and gut microbiota. The genome-wide association study data pertaining to intestinal microbiota, GC, and 91 inflammatory proteins were obtained from a public database and subjected to MR analysis to ascertain the causal relationship between intestinal microbiota and GC. To evaluate the possible mediating function of latent inflammatory proteins in this process, a two-step MR study was used. Inverse-variance weighting was the primary analytical technique used; sensitivity, heterogeneity, and horizontal multiplicity studies were also carried out. Our MR analysis revealed potential associations between 11 bacterial genera in the gut microbiota and GC in Europeans, with 3 genera suggesting an increased risk and 8 a protective role. Among the inflammatory proteins, 3 demonstrated potential associations with GC, with 1 indicating a higher risk and 2 a protective effect. A two-step MR analysis indicated that genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 may mediate an increased risk of GC through the downregulation of C-X-C motif chemokine 1, with a mediation ratio of 7.79%. The current research offers evidence that intestinal flora influences the risk of GC development through inflammatory proteins by means of MR analysis. The results of the MR analysis indicated that in European populations, certain microbial taxa may influence GC risk through C-X-C motif chemokine 1.

1. Introduction

The global epidemiologic status of gastric cancer (GC) and the research advances made over the past few years have revealed several key trends and findings. Despite a decline in global incidence and mortality rates, GC remains the fourth greatest cause of cancer-related fatalities.[1] As indicated in Global Cancer Statistics 2020,[2] the number of new cases of GC reached approximately 1.09 million in 2020, while the number of deaths was as high as 769,000.[3] This underscores the significant global public health burden associated with this disease and highlights the formidable challenge in preventing and treating it effectively.

The intestinal microbial community in the human body is a complex ecosystem comprising a multitude of microorganisms, with more than a thousand species, as evidenced by recent studies. The potential correlation between disrupted microbial balance and the advancement of GC has garnered significant interest within the academic community.[4] Studies have demonstrated that the intestinal flora of GC patients exhibit a higher abundance of specific bacteria, including Haemophilus parahaemolyticus and H. sputorum. The increased abundance of Fusobacterium mortiferum has been postulated to be potentially associated with inflammatory processes and tumor progression. Conversely, the higher abundance of Lactococcus lactis may be linked to disease states. In addition, Methylobacterium adhaesivum has been suggested to be associated with the tumor microenvironment.[5] Furthermore, Lactobacillus spp. and Megasphaera spp. demonstrated high accuracy in distinguishing between GC patients and healthy individuals, suggesting potential as markers for predicting GC.[6] While some associations between intestinal flora and GC have been identified, the precise mechanisms of interaction and their roles in disease progression remain to be elucidated.

Chronic gastritis, particularly in conjunction with Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, represents a pivotal contributing factor in the pathogenesis of GC. Inflammatory proteins such as tumor necrosis factor α, vascular endothelial growth factor C, and vascular endothelial growth factor D have been found to be higher in patients with gastrointestinal tract cancers,[7] the causal link among other inflammatory proteins and GC is uncertain. The goal of this study is to discover if 91 inflammatory proteins have a potential causative involvement in GC development, particularly in the European population, through the use of Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the intestinal flora can influence the host immune responses through the production of inflammatory proteins. Certain intestinal bacteria have been shown to produce metabolites that activate the NLRP6 inflammasome, which in turn regulates the secretion of interleukin-18, affecting intestinal health or participating in tumor immunity.[4] The gut microbiota can influence the host immune response and inflammatory state by affecting, for instance, interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor α, which may, in turn, impact gastric carcinogenesis.[8] In conclusion, inflammatory proteins, as mediators of gut flora-host interactions, may be pivotal in the pathophysiological process of GC.

MR analysis is a sophisticated epidemiological tool that utilizes genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV). It helps to assess the potential causal relationship between exposure factors and disease while reducing the confounding of confounders and reverse causality inherent in traditional observational studies.[9] This provides clues about the influence of intestinal flora on the risk of GC through inflammatory proteins.[10] The objective of this study is to investigate the potential mechanisms through which intestinal flora may influence GC through inflammatory proteins. To this end, a two-step MR analysis of genome-wide association study (GWAS) pooled data will be conducted to assess the causal relationship of this influence. This will facilitate the elucidation of the mechanism of the interaction between intestinal flora and inflammatory proteins in the development of GC. Furthermore, this study will provide insights that could inform the development of early diagnostic techniques and personalized therapies for GC.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources

The dataset utilized in this analysis consists of 633 individuals diagnosed with GC and a control group of 174,006 individuals of European descent. The data were sourced from the GWAS summary statistics (GWAS ID: finn-b-c3_gastro_exallc).[11] Genetic summary statistics pertaining to the human gut microbiome were derived from a meta-analytical study conducted by the MiBioGen consortium, encompassing 211 distinct taxonomic units across 131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 classes, and 9 phyla[12] (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). Jing et al described a genome-wide protein quantitative trait locus analysis that assessed 91 different inflammatory proteins (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2). The study used the Olink Target platform and included 14,824 participants with European ancestry.[13] As this research was based on publicly accessible aggregated data, no further ethical review or participant consent was deemed necessary.

2.2. IV selection

In this study, we investigated the causal link between exposure and outcome at the genetic level using single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs.[14] To determine IVs for gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins, we used a significance criterion of P < 1 × 10−5.[15] To ensure that the selected IVs were not specific to any particular genetic locus, we employed the “TwoSampleMR” software package.[16] Linkage disequilibrium analysis was performed with the European reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project.[17] All IVs were clustered within a ±10,000 kb distance with an linkage disequilibrium threshold of R2 < 0.001.[18] Furthermore, we collected detailed information for each SNP, including the effect allele, effect size (β value), standard deviation, and P value. The forward-strand alleles of palindromic SNPs were identified using allele frequency data. In the end, F-statistics were computed for each SNP under analysis; larger F values (>10) suggested a lower probability of weak instrument bias.[19,20]

2.3. Statistical analysis

In order for an MR analysis design to be considered valid, it must fulfill 3 assumptions: there is an association between genetics and exposure; genetics are independent of potential confounders; and genetics variables affect outcomes only through exposure.[21] In this study, a two-sample MR analysis approach was employed to investigate the potential association between the exposure variable, namely the gut microbiota, and the outcome of interest, specifically the risk of GC. In addition, to evaluate the potential mediating impact of inflammatory proteins in the connection between the gut microbiota and the development of GC, a two-step MR study was carried out. The comprehensive study design is illustrated in Figure 1.

MR process diagram. GC = gastric cancer, GWAS = genome-wide association study, MR = Mendelian randomization.

In this investigation, a suite of MR analytical techniques was employed, encompassing inverse-variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and both weighted and simple models. This study employed the IVW method as the primary analytical approach, which exhibited the greatest capacity to assess causality in MR analyses, unless evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was present.[22] The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < .05 for the IVW method. Only those results that met this criterion were advanced to subsequent stages of the study.[23]

A series of MR analyses was conducted. Initially, a two-sample MR analysis was performed to investigate the potential causal relationship between the gut microbiota and GC. Secondly, we conducted a MR analysis between inflammatory proteins and GC. Thirdly, we performed an MR analysis of the gut flora and inflammatory proteins that were causally related to GC, as obtained in the first and second steps, with the aim of identifying inflammatory proteins that may have a mediating role. Furthermore, we undertaken a reverse MR study on the gut flora that was causally related to GC. Finally, we assessed the mediating effect of inflammatory proteins.

In order to assess the stability between pooled estimates, we employed Cochran Q statistic to examine the heterogeneity of results (indicating the presence of heterogeneity), as well as MR-Egger and MR of multiple residuals and outliers of effectiveness (MR-PRESSO) to assess gene pleiotropy (P < .05 indicating the presence of horizontal pleiotropy). Statistical significance was defined at P < .05. In order to correct for the false discovery rate, each MR analysis was subjected to a false discovery rate threshold of q < 0.1. The results were deemed to be statistically significant when P < .05 and q ≥ 0.1.[24] All analyses were run using R (version 4.3.1, R Core Team), TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.5; MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit, University of Bristol), MR (version 0.5.0; MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit, University of Bristol), Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (developed by Verbanck et al., 2018), and Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression (developed by Bulik Sullivan et al., 2015 at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard).[25–27]

2.4. Mediating effect analysis

The intermediate assessment was performed utilizing a two-step MR approach, which permits the splitting of the overall effect into direct and indirect portions.[28] The first coefficient, β1, represents the MR effect of intestinal flora on inflammatory proteins, while the second coefficient, β2, represents the MR effect of inflammatory proteins on GC. The indirect effect is given by β1 × β2. β3 represents the MR effect of intestinal flora on GC, which is also referred to as the “overall effect.” The direct effect is β3 − β1 × β2. The percentage of effects mediated by inflammatory proteins was calculated by dividing the indirect effects by the overall effect (β1 × β2/β3). The bootstrap was used to estimate the standard errors, while the two-sample MR analysis was used to estimate the effect sizes.

3. Results

3.1. Overall effect of gut microbiota on GC

The results are presented in Figure 2. Eleven bacterial genera were identified as potentially associated with GC. Among these, genus Prevotella 7 (OR = 1.39, 95% CI = 1.02–1.90, P = .036), genus Roseburia (OR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.03–3.53, P = .040), and genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 (OR = 1.84, 95% CI = 1.07–3.16, P = .028) were found to be significantly correlated with an increased risk of GC. In addition, family Enterobacteriaceae (OR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.49–0.93, P = .018), order Enterobacteriales (OR = 0.35, 95% CI = 0.16–0.77, P = .009), genus Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group (OR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.30–0.89, P = .018), genus Allisonella (OR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.49–0.93, P = .018), genus Ruminococcaceae UCG009 (OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.42–0.95, P = .026), genus Ruminococcaceae UCG004 (OR = 0.57, 95% CI = 0.35–0.94, P = .027), family Bacteroidaceae (OR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.17–0.91, P = .030), and genus Bacteroides (OR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.17–0.91, P = .030) were found to confer protection against GC. P > .05 for both the heterogeneity and multiplicity tests confirmed the consistency of our findings (Tables S3 and S4, Supplemental Digital Content 3). Sensitivity analyses reinforced the robustness and consistency of the results.

MR analysis of the gut microbiota-gastric cancer causal connection. CI = confidence interval, MR = Mendelian randomization, OR = odds ratio, SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism.

3.2. Causal effect of inflammatory proteins on GC

The MR analysis was performed on 91 inflammatory proteins linked with GC (Fig. 3). Among the identified proteins, 3 were expected to have a genetic association with GC in the European population (P < .05). In addition, transforming growth factor-alpha (TGF-alpha; OR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.01–2.11, P = .042) levels may be linked to a higher risk of GC, while C-C motif chemokine 19 (CCL19; OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.54–0.90, P = .006) and C-X-C motif chemokine 1 (CXCL1; OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.54–0.98, P = .038) levels may have a protective effect against GC. TGF-alpha exhibited pleiotropy in sensitivity analyses but remained stable in all heterogeneity analyses (Tables S5 and S6, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

MR analysis of the causal relationship between inflammatory proteins and gastric cancer. CI = confidence interval, MR = Mendelian randomization, OR = odds ratio, SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism, TGF = transforming growth factor.

3.3. Influence of gut microbiota on inflammatory proteins

MR analysis of the 11 intestinal flora and 3 inflammatory proteins from the aforementioned results indicated that genus Ruminococcaceae UCG009 may serve as a protective factor for CXCL1 (OR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.81–0.97, P = .009; Fig. 4A). Similarly, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 may also act as a protective factor for CXCL1 (OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.76–0.97, P = .014; Fig. 4B). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis yielded the same result.

(A) MR analysis of CXCL1 as a mediator between genus Ruminococcaceae UCG009 and GC. (B) MR analysis of CXCL1 as a mediator between genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 and GC. CI = confidence interval, CXCL1 = C-X-C motif chemokine 1, GC = gastric cancer, MR = Mendelian randomization, OR = odds ratio, SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism.

In accordance with the principles of MR analysis, the outcome is deemed to be an immutable factor with respect to exposure. Subsequently, we conducted an inverse MR analysis to assess the potential inverse association between bacterial traits and GC. The application of the conventional significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8 in MR analyses from GC to intestinal flora may impede the feasibility of the study. Accordingly, a more lenient criterion of P < 1 × 10−5 was uniformly employed when extracting IVs for reverse MR analysis, with the objective of increasing the number of SNPs accessible for analysis.[29] Subsequently, the F-statistics of the selected SNPs were also calculated to ensure their statistical robustness, as previously described. The IVW model of GC-Ruminococcaceae UCG014 demonstrates heterogeneity (P < .05), while other sensitivity analyses yield negative results (Tables S7 and S8, Supplemental Digital Content 7).

3.4. Mediation effect of inflammatory proteins

The two-step MR analysis conducted in this study identified CXCL1 as a pivotal mediator influencing the relationship between intestinal flora and GC. Ruminococcaceae UCG009 (β1 = −0.117, P = .009) and CXCL1 (β2 = −0.315, P = .038) demonstrated a mediating effect of β1 × β2 = 0.037, with a total effect of β3 = −0.467. However, this total effect was not established since the reverse direction of the β values. Ruminococcaceae UCG014 (β′1 = −0.150, P = .014) and CXCL1 (β′2 = −0.315, P = .038) showed a mediation effect of β′1 × β′2 = 0.047. The total effect was β′3 = 0.609, and the mediation ratio was β′1 × β′2/β′3 = 7.79% (Fig. 5).

4. Discussion

H. pylori is a bacterial pathogen that is intimately linked with the onset of GC in humans, and is regarded as a pivotal risk factor for this disease.[30] It has been demonstrated that this bacterium can trigger chronic inflammation, which results in the destruction of the gastric epithelium’s function and structure. This inflammatory environment has been shown to promote the development of GC.[31] While the eradication of H. pylori has been demonstrated to markedly diminish the likelihood of GC, it is notable that not all infected individuals ultimately develop this disease. Indeed, only approximately 1% of infected individuals are at risk of developing GC.[32] This indicates that additional factors beyond H. pylori may be implicated in the pathogenesis of GC. It has been postulated that the pathway for the formation of Short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs) may be impaired in GC patients, which may be associated with inflammation and dysregulation of bacterial communities in GC.[30] However, the sample size of the current study was restricted, and more longitudinal research is needed to confirm these results.

The aim of this study was to use genetic prediction approaches in a European population to explore the causal link between gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and GC risk. The study included 633 GC cases and 174,006 controls. The MR analysis indicated that genus Prevotella7, genus Roseburia, and genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 may be associated with an increased risk of GC in the intestinal flora of the European population. Conversely, family Enterobacteriaceae, order Enterobacteriales, genus Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group, genus Allisonella, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG009, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG004, family Bacteroidaceae, and genus Bacteroides were identified as protective against GC. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the inflammatory protein TGF-alpha may be associated with an increased risk of GC, whereas CCL19 and CXCL1 may confer protection against this disease. Ultimately, we predicted by mediated MR analysis that the increased risk of GC due to genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 might be mediated by reducing the expression of CXCL1. We underscored the importance of inflammatory proteins in the host microcephaly associated with GC, giving fresh perspectives into the link between intestinal flora and GC as well as new therapeutic methods.

Ruminococcaceae UCG014 is an unclassified group of bacteria in the family Ruminococcaceae. Existing studies indicate that Ruminococcaceae UCG014 in the gut microbial community may be associated with a range of health states, including inflammatory responses and metabolic functions.[33] Family Ruminococcaceae is typically monitored in conjunction with the family Lachnospiraceae, and their proportions and activities within the gut microbial community may serve as indicators of gut health status.[34] In patients with inflammatory bowel disease, the number of bacteria belonging to the family Ruminococcaceae may be reduced, which is connected with disease activity and impaired function of the gastrointestinal barrier.[35] A recent study indicated that chronic stress-induced microbiome perturbation significantly increased the abundance of Ruminococcaceae UCG014, which may promote colorectal cancer growth and metastasis.[36] This indicates that an imbalance in Ruminococcaceae UCG014 is closely linked to inflammatory responses, neurotransmission, and microbial metabolism, suggesting a specific role for this bacterial group in colorectal cancer growth and metastasis.

Nevertheless, P = .040 (P < .05) in the heterogeneity analysis of the IVW model for GC-Ruminococcaceae UCG014 indicated a notable discrepancy between effect sizes estimated using disparate genetic IVs. This may imply that some IVs may have disparate associations with GC and intestinal flora compared to others for various reasons, including differing biological mechanisms, confounding variables, or measurement errors. No significant heterogeneity was identified when employing alternative MR techniques, including MR-Egger regression and the weighted median method. This suggests that the observed heterogeneity may be specific to the IVW model and not a general phenomenon affecting all genetic IVs. The absence of significant results in tests of multiplicity indicates that there is insufficient proof to imply that the selected genetic IVs exert influence on the association between GC and intestinal flora via alternative pathways, thereby enhancing the confidence in the use of these IVs for causal inference.

Genus Ruminococcaceae and genus Roseburia are integral to the intestinal microbial community, facilitating a multitude of metabolic processes. Of particular significance is their role in promoting the production of SCFAs.[37] These are the primary metabolites produced by the intestinal flora through the fermentation of dietary fibers, including acetic, propionic, and butyric acids.[38] They play a significant role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, regulating immune responses, and influencing host metabolism.[39] In GC, SCFAs may act through a variety of mechanisms, including the inhibition of inflammation, the maintenance of equilibrium within the intestinal microbiota, and the promotion of intestinal health.[40] When SCFAs act on mouse neutrophils, they may play an anti-inflammatory role by activating GPR43, which in turn promotes the release of reactive oxygen species, a potent bactericidal factor for removing pathogens.[41] These findings suggest that the promotion of SCFAs production by intestinal flora such as genus Ruminococcaceae and genus Roseburia may contribute to a reduction in the risk of GC.

The results of this research suggest that the flora of Ruminococcaceae UCG009 and Ruminococcaceae UCG004 genera exert a protective effect against GC. Conversely, the flora of Ruminococcaceae UCG014 and genus Roseburia may contribute to an increased risk of GC. It can be reasonably inferred that not all flora in genus Ruminococcaceae play the same role. Consequently, the roles of these unclassified flora in the development of the disease require further investigation. It is possible that their role in GC is related to other factors, such as a chronic inflammatory state, which may increase the risk of GC. We subsequently investigated the correlation between inflammatory proteins and GC, as well as the relationship between intestinal flora and inflammatory proteins.

Our findings suggest that TGF-alpha may be associated with an elevated risk of GC, which is consistent with previous research.[42] Although the results of the sensitivity analyses showed that TGF-alpha-GC had pleiotropic but not heterogeneous, which may indicate that TGF-alpha indirectly affects GC risk by influencing biological pathways other than GC. However, this effect was consistent and did not show significant variability among different genetic IVs, suggesting that the observed association is robust. These findings lend support to the hypothesis of a potential causal relationship between TGF-alpha and GC risk. However, further research is necessary to elucidate the mechanisms through which TGF-alpha affects GC risk via alternative pathways.

The relationship between TGF-alpha and GC is intricate and may involve multiple mechanisms, including the stimulation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition, the procedure by which epithelial cells take on mesenchymal characteristics that improve their ability to invade and migrate through surrounding tissues.[43,44] In the initial stages of gastric carcinogenesis, TGF-alpha may serve a protective function by facilitating cell differentiation and suppressing cell proliferation.[45] However, as the cancer progresses, TGF-alpha can be converted to a pro-cancer role by promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and inducing angiogenesis, which are hallmarks of cancer progression. This conversion may be due to alterations in the tumor microenvironment or alterations in signaling pathways within the cancer cells.[46,47]

Our study also revealed that CCL19 and CXCL1 exhibited protective effects against GC. In the context of the mediated MR analysis, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 demonstrated a negative correlation with CXCL1 (β = −0.150, P = .014), suggesting that the higher the abundance of genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014, the lower the CXCL1 expression level is. Therefore, we propose that the increased risk of GC caused by genus Ruminococcaceae UCG014 may be mediated by a reduction in CXCL1 expression. As a chemotactic agent for neutrophils, the growth factor CXCL1 interacts with the G-protein-coupled receptor CXCR2.[48] It is a key player in the inflammatory process.[49] In the field of oncology, CXCL1 is an important figure in the development of numerous cancers. In the context of GC, CXCL1 is frequently regarded as a risk factor.[50] Studies have demonstrated that elevated CXCL1 expression in GC cells promotes lymphangiogenesis and metastasis in GC,[51] and is associated with advanced TNM stage, tumor size, and poor prognosis in GC patients.[52,53] In bladder cancer, CXCL1 is also implicated in immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment, attracting tumor-associated macrophages and cancer-associated fibroblasts to the tumor region. These cells further promote tumor growth and invasion through the secretion of growth factors, chemokines, and matrix metalloproteinases.[54] Moreover, Miyake demonstrated that CXCL1 can also facilitate vascular endothelial growth factor expression via stimulating the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway, which in turn encourages angiogenesis and tumor development.[55]

Drawing from the findings of multiple studies, we postulated that CXCL1 might not function as a protective factor but rather act as a risk factor in the context of GC, although the underlying mechanism remains elusive. Our own data, derived from MR analysis, indicated that CXCL1 exhibited a protective effect against GC (OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.54–0.98, P = .038), contradicting previous research. CXCL1 predicted genetically in our study reflects the long-term basal expression level of individuals, while most previous studies detected acute local expression after GC occurrence or in the tumor microenvironment, and these differences may lead to distinct functions of CXCL1. This discrepancy led us to consider the possibility of alternative mechanisms by which CXCL1 could influence GC progression. CXCL1 has functionally heterogeneous variants, but due to the limitations of the existing GWAS dataset, subgroup analysis cannot be performed temporarily, which has been listed as a future research direction. CXCL1 has tissue-specific functions – it may exert a protective effect in the intestinal microenvironment and promote tumor progression in the gastric tumor microenvironment, which is one of the key reasons for the discrepancy. However, our study did not include the validation of these findings through extensive cellular or animal model experiments. We look forward to future research that can delve deeper into this area, aiming to elucidate the role of CXCL1 in GC development.

This study effectively reduced the impact of confounding variables by using MR analysis, establishing a tentative causal connection between the gut microbiota and GC. It also shed light on the intermediary role of inflammatory proteins, paving the way for new avenues of research. However, our research is not without its limitations; the population and data analyzed were exclusively of European ancestry, which may restrict the applicability of our results to other ethnic groups. The small gastric-cancer GWAS sample, lack of Helicobacter pylori adjustment, and neglect of temporal dynamics in the gut microbiome. Furthermore, the analysis was primarily focused on genetic variations, with the dynamic shifts and complex interactions within the gut microbiota’s diverse and uncharted microbial communities not fully accounted for. Thus, a degree of prudence is warranted when interpreting these findings. Due to the unavailability of the original analysis environment following platform updates, advanced analyses such as nonlinear MR and colocalization studies could not be performed in the current study. Future research endeavors should therefore incorporate these methods, along with multiethnic replication and in vivo functional experiments, to clarify the specific targets, routes, and processes in play. This will make it possible to comprehend the relationship between GC and the gut microbiota in more detail, leading to the development of more efficacious preventative and therapeutic strategies.

5. Conclusion

The current research offers evidence that intestinal flora influences the risk of GC development through inflammatory proteins by means of MR analysis. The results of the MR analysis indicated that in European populations, certain microbial taxa may influence GC risk through CXCL1.
