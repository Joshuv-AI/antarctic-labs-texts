An improved facial emotion recognition system using convolutional neural network for the optimization of human robot interaction
Authors: Ravi Raj, Ilker Demirkol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) has been effectively augmenting the features of robotics applications, including surveillance, medical support, aid services for the elderly or disabled, and many more uses. Most robotics applications need a variety of human-robot interactions (HRI) for effective and accurate use. Computer vision plays a vital role in improved and precise HRI. The human facial emotion recognition (FER) technique is one of the important types of computer vision that is crucial for the enhancement of HRI. This paper presents a study that uses algorithms based on computer vision and machine learning (ML) to identify the emotional states of humans in photographs and videos while users interact with various visual objects. Using ML techniques and a digital image processing pipeline, this study aims to reveal the development of software that can recognize emotions in human gestures. This research shows that software creation and training based on emotional expressions, together with the use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for emotional identification, are feasible. The main objective of this study is to find the essential facial gestures that the CNN framework with FER emphasizes. Finally, the proposed model is comparatively analyzed against FER2013, the Real-world Affective Faces Database (RAF-DB), and CK+ datasets. The rate of accuracy is highest for the CK+ dataset, which is around 95% and lowest for the FER2013 dataset, which is around 64%. The findings of this research will advance our knowledge about neural networks and help enhance the efficiency of computer vision.

Introduction

An interdisciplinary topic called “affective computing” studies and develops computers that can understand and analyze human emotions1. Facial expressions are an important source of non-verbal cues in human interaction that might lend depth and significance to spoken words. According to certain research, 60–80% of communication takes place nonverbally2. These non-verbal cues include physical distance, hand gestures, quality of voice, eye contact, and facial expressions. In the domain of human-robot interaction (HRI), facial emotion recognition (FER) is used in surveillance, robotic navigation, education, human-activity recognition (HAR), medical care, psychological care, autopilot, and behavioral research in computer vision3,4. Mobile robots are autonomous devices that use preprogramming to see, identify, work together, and perform a variety of duties, including manufacturing activities, medical support, customer service to consumers, security and civil surveillance, and more5,6. The ability to simulate empathy for various controllers is required7, that is, to give robots the ability to identify, understand, and therefore produce adaptable actions using recognized information, which is a major driving force behind studies in this domain.

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have made a significant contribution to developing human awareness, consciousness, actions, behavior, and learning8. Future advancements of AI depend on human-AI cooperation. In addition to fully autonomous or manually controlled equipment, robots can cooperate with a user companion with differing levels of automation and assistance9. Emotions are basic human characteristics that influence decisions and behavior10,11. They are vital to interaction, and effective relationships depend on emotional intelligence, that is, the capacity to recognize, utilize, and control the way one feels12. Human-like observation, determination, and expressive emotion skills are desired for robots in the HRI framework13,14. The ability of the robotic device to display recognized emotional expressions has a significant influence on social behavior that results in the application of complicated communication situations, including assistive, industrial, educational, and interpersonal robotics15,16. Several studies investigated how individuals perceive and identify states of consciousness, as well as which modalities, including human pose estimation17,18, gesture recognition19, HAR20–22, and many more, can transmit psychological data from robots to humans. The feature extraction techniques that use static images are the Haar wavelet transform25, Gabor wavelet transform26, and Active Appearance Models (AAM)27. On the other hand, dynamic methods presume a temporal correlation in the sequencing of the expression of the input face throughout consecutive frames28,29. Several prevalent methods for recognizing emotions include artificial neural networks (ANN), AdaBoost, hidden Markov models, and support vector machines (SVM)30. Deep learning has made significant developments, and CNN’s use has shown great promise31,32. However, a significant problem with deep learning is that usually developing good models requires a lot of data.

The algorithm developed by CNN has experienced considerable advancements in the identification of facial expressions, but there are still some shortcomings, such as excessively long training cycles and poor recognition rates in complicated environments. Two issues with ML breakthroughs in FER approaches have been noted in datasets that are now in use: (1) limited images and (2) images acquired during highly controlled settings. These issues led to the development of FER methods that prioritize the collection of web images33. The development of an intelligent, multimodal HRI framework that performs in a real-time environment is the primary goal of this present work. The purpose of the present study is to improve the efficiency of the FER system by developing an automatic FER system that uses CNN.

There are many stages involved in facial recognition: image detection, image pre-processing, feature retrieval, image alignment, and image recognition34. Geometric characteristic extraction and total statistics characteristics-focused techniques are the two main methods for extracting features. For the purpose of characterizing the locations of the face organs as classification characteristics, the geometric feature-based technique is often employed35. The goal of this research is to provide a technique for building an FER scheme using CNN. Using a camera, the given model might be utilized in real time for FER. Examining and comprehending the benefits of deep CNN models over alternative deep learning models are analyzed.

Related work

Automatic FER is still an extremely challenging and fascinating task in computer vision. DNNs are being used more and more to build discriminatory models for automatic FER as a result of the evolution of FER beyond controlled research environments, too difficult in wild situations, and the present popularity of ML techniques in a variety of domains. In recent decades, various researchers have focused on facial gesture recognition to enhance HRI.

Mollahosseini et al.36 present a deep neural network (DNN) framework that can be used to solve the FER issue in several popular standard facial data sets. In particular, this network is composed of four Inception layers, two convolutional layers, and max pooling. The network, which consists of a single component design, classifies input photos of faces into either the neutral or the six fundamental expressions. In terms of accuracy and training time, the recommended design performs as well as or more effectively than state-of-the-art techniques and conventional convolutional neural networks (CNN).

Jung et al.37 present a collaborative fine-tuning in DNNs for FER. The properties of temporal information are helpful in identifying facial expressions. However, it takes a lot of work to manually develop features that are beneficial. In this study, the deep learning approach is utilized, which is thought of as a method to automatically obtain important characteristics from unprocessed data. In order to minimize this effort. Two distinct models form the foundation of this deep network. One deep network uses imagery sequences to acquire temporal aspect features, and another uses temporal face landmark points to derive temporal geometrical information. Compared to conventional techniques such as feature synthesis and weighted summation, this new integrated technique yields more precise findings.

Kotsia et al.38 present two new techniques for FER in face picture sequences. There are landmarks in the picture series that the user must personally configure part of the Candide grid’s points to face in the initial frame. Using flexible models, the grid-tracking and deformed technique employed follows the grid in a series of video frames as the emotion changes continuously unless it reaches the frame with the strongest emotion. A unique multiple-class Support Vector Machine (SVM) framework of classifiers that operates to identify both the six fundamental facial gestures and an array of picked facial Action Units (FAUs) uses the geometrical movement of some chosen Candide nodes, which can be expressed as the variation among the node coordinates among the initial and highest emotion intensity frames. According to FAU recognition, the findings in the Cohn-Kanade database provide a recognition precision of 95.1% for FER and 99.7% for facial emotion identification utilizing the suggested multiclass SVMs.

Oh et al.39 create a deep learning (DL) model for emotion classification using facial expressions that are resistant to distortion within the picture data. 53 participants were asked to express four distinct reactions for 15 seconds at a time; then, feelings were classified as positive or negative. After extracting facial emotions from the captured photographs and storing them as image data, noise was added in five stages to analyze the data set. Furthermore, when the noise was applied, its predictability was verified. This work verified that emotions might be identified from facial expressions using a DL model that is resistant to noise in a given data set.

Liu et al.40 study the multimodal emotion integration process and companion robot empathic reactions. Natural HRI requires humanoid robots to be able to understand sympathetic facial reactions and respond accordingly. In order to create a multimodal emotion classification mechanism, this work integrates the GPT-3.5 system with a facial emotion-recognizing system. This work also tackles the problem of accurately replicating human facial expressions through the mechanical structure of a humanoid robot. The linked GPT-based multimodal emotion identification system employing text and images works better than existing approaches, according to studies conducted on benchmark databases, and has unique empathic reaction capabilities.

Kim et al.41 suggest a new FER system design that depends on layered DL. In a system with hierarchy, the geometrical feature and the feature taken from the looks of the feature-based network are merged. The visual feature-based model uses the preprocessed picture to identify the holistic elements of the face, while the geometric feature-based network uses action unit landmarks to learn the position shift of a muscular system that moves primarily during facial emotions. With the help of this method, the extraction of variable facial characteristics is possible without the need for sequential information among the neutral and emotional photos.

Filippini et al.42 suggest a highly efficient convolutional neural network (CNN)-based FER system that can be used to improve the understanding of the NAO robot of human facial reactions while providing it with the ability to determine the level of arousal of an interlocutor. In fact, the model evaluated in HRI was less successful in identifying neutral and furious emotions and 75% accurate in identifying shocked and afraid expressions. It was also 90% accurate in identifying happy and distressed facial expressions, respectively.

Martinez et al.43 provide a facial emotion recognition technique that relies on the most recent ML techniques released for the state of the art and was built using the Robot Operating System (ROS), Noetic version. The system can identify emotions including rage, happiness, sadness, or shock. Using an inexpensive multipurpose board, the Raspberry Pi 4 Model B, comprehensive tests were carried out in a real-world setting to make these approaches more effective and to demonstrate that they could be implemented in real time using an inexpensive board. The whole FER system described in this paper can perhaps operate in real time with higher accuracy.

Alonso-Martín et al.44 provide a multimodal approach for social robots to recognize users’ emotions. The purpose of this framework is to facilitate HRI. It is a component of the Robotics Dialog System (RDS), which is the robot’s whole communication system. The Gender and Emotion Facial Analysis (GEFA) technique was subsequently created to detect emotions in facial expressions. To establish the ultimate decision rule and evaluate the accuracy of every aspect, many tests were conducted with actual users. The results of the trials using this decision rule demonstrate a high accuracy rate in automatically identifying the emotions of the user, outperforming the results provided by the two sources of information (image and audio) separately.

Yan et al.45 offer a multilayered CNN model utilizing a cross-attention mechanism (MCNN-CA) for precise emotion recognition. The proposed paradigm facilitates the automatic extraction of pertinent features from multimodal data and the integration of feature maps from various sources as modules for future emotion recognition. During the feature extraction phase, several CNNs were developed to extract essential information from multidimensional characteristics. The feature fusion module was employed to improve the intercorrelation among features via a channel-efficient attention method. This innovation demonstrates efficacy in integrating unique characteristics inside a singular mode and across other modes. The model was evaluated by EEG emotion recognition studies utilizing the SEED and SEED-IV datasets. Multimodal emotion studies, using EEG and textual data from the ZuCo dataset, assessed the efficacy of the proposed model. A comparative examination with contemporary studies demonstrates that the proposed model surpasses others in accuracy, F1, precision, and recall scores.

Aguang et al.46 examine the application of deep learning for FER utilizing the Emognition dataset, which has ten target emotions: amusement, wonder, enthusiasm, liking, surprise, anger, contempt, fear, sorrow, and neutrality. A sequence of data preparation steps was executed to transform video data into photos and enhance the dataset. This paper presents CNN models developed via two methodologies: transfer learning (fine-tuning) utilizing previously trained models Inception-V3 and MobileNet-V2, and constructing models from the ground up employing the Taguchi method to identify an efficient combination of hyperparameter settings. The suggested model exhibited commendable performance across various testing procedures, achieving an accuracy of 96% and a mean F1-score of 0.95 on the simulated data.

The suggested CNN-based system presents a competitive and lightweight alternative, demonstrating robust performance on controlled datasets. Although leading models in the literature frequently utilize ensemble learning or transformer-based techniques to achieve accuracy exceeding 90% on datasets such as RAF-DB or AffectNet, these approaches incur heightened computational complexity and inference duration, rendering them less appropriate for real-time human–robot interaction contexts. Conversely, our model attains an accuracy of up to 95% on CK+ and has commendable performance (about 64%) on FER2013 without extensive preprocessing or ensemble methods. This equilibrium between precision and efficiency underscores the appropriateness of our methodology for implementation in real-time robots and assistive technologies. Moreover, in contrast to numerous opaque deep models, our research prioritizes interpretability by pinpointing the facial motions that significantly impact the CNN’s decision-making, hence enhancing transparency in emotion inference. Background information

FER may be applied in several domains, including interpersonal and linguistic communication, psychological clinics, psychology, neuroscience, pain assessment, detection of deception, intelligent environments, and multisensory HCI. As shown in Fig. 1, facial recognition can be performed using a variety of techniques, including feature-based techniques, neighboring graph techniques, expression-based techniques, and framework approaches47. The form and texture of human faces are identical; therefore, a model acquired from an adequate number of faces could be used to recognize the other faces, which are not included in the training of the network process, with good generalization. A complex set of steps must be taken to develop the whole FER framework because the efficiency of the trained model is dependent on numerous factors, including the number of images in the training data set, the addition of data, the structure of CNN, hyperparameter adjustments, loss function, fine-tuning, transfer learning, and measurement metrics.

CNNs are an established class of deep learning algorithms utilized by computer vision, including automatic identification of emotions48. CNNs are made to autonomously recognize and identify features from pictures that might be used to predict the emotions of a person based on the picture49,50. CNNs might be trained using huge databases of pictures with matching emotional labeling in the framework of emotion identification to discover patterns and features linked to various emotions. Once trained, CNN might be utilized to predict new, unlabeled images by feeding them via the network and choosing the label that has the best chance of prediction. Next, a trained CNN might be used to predict the emotional impact of new images51. There are several benefits in using CNNs for emotion identification over more conventional techniques for image processing. For instance, CNNs might be trained to autonomously identify relevant characteristics from pictures instead of needing the tedious and mistake-susceptible process of human feature extraction. Furthermore, advances in DL have made it possible to handle different poses, brightness, and other environmental aspects that can alter the look of pictures, especially with the incorporation of approaches, including CNN and other supportive approaches52.

CNNs are highly effective at solving complicated issues such as classification of images and analysis of medical images because they do away with the requirement to manually extract features. Initially, we observe a basic CNN template comprising many components that we may readily comprehend and associate with the suggested CNN model. A fundamental CNN has three sorts of layer: input, hidden, and output. After entering CNN along its input layers, the data pass through several hidden layers and eventually arrive at the output layer53,54. The output layer represents the network forecast. The output of the network is contrasted with the real labels to detect any error or loss. A foundational component of data processing is provided by the network’s hidden layers. It is possible to break down each layer into its four sub-functions: convolutional layers, pooling layers, normalization, and activation. Multiple layers of neurons make up CNNs, and the most significant layers are the convolution layers and the pooling layers. Convolutional layers

In a CNN, convolutional layers are primarily responsible for extracting features from the input images. Each convolutional layer contains a set of filters (or kernels) which are trainable parameters learned during the training process. Typically, the filters’ size is less than the picture’s real size. Every filter produces an activation map by convolving using the picture. A mathematical process known as convolution integrates two functions to explain their overlap. The filter is slid over the image’s width and height during convolution, and at all geographical points, the product of dots among each filter component and the source data is computed. When two functions are combined, one is “slid” over the other, multiplied at all points of overlap, and the results are added to produce a novel function. Pooling layers

The pooling layer is a crucial element of CNNs. It is generally used in conjunction with the convolutional layer to decrease the spatial extent of feature maps and minimize both the quantity of data and the variable value. The pooling layer functions by dividing every feature map into predetermined areas and performing actions, including identifying the largest value (max pooling) or computing the mean value (average pooling) inside each area57. The two primary categories of pooling procedures are average pooling and max pooling. The max pooling is the largest score found in each zone, while the average pooling is the average score computed for each zone. Figure 5 shows the estimate of the average and max pooling. By exhibiting a certain level of translation invariance and aiding in the preservation of important properties, these processes strengthen the resistance of the network to even a small amount of changes in the input.Fig. 5Illustration of average pooling and max pooling calculation.

In CNNs, the pooling layer has two main functions. First, it shortens the learning process and minimizes computing complexity by decreasing the physical dimensions of the feature maps. Secondly, it improves the generality and interpretability of the framework by extracting important features and eliminating unnecessary information58. A stochastic method known as pooling enables the aggregation of the outputs at a designated location into a singular value. The pooling is an essential phase in convolutional methods to reduce the dimensionality of the extracted features. The pooling approach reduces the computational costs of the top layers and provides supplementary spatially altered data by removing the imperfect interconnection present between the convolutional layers59. Fully-connected layers

A fully connected layer denotes a neural network configuration in which each neuron performs a linear transformation on the given input vector via a weight matrix. Consequently, all potential layer-to-layer linkages exist, indicating that each element on the input vector affects each component of the output vector. In classification tasks, fully connected layers convert high-level information into scores, which are usually processed using a softmax function to obtain probabilistic class forecasts.

ReLU activation function

A well-liked activation function that is commonly utilized in deep learning models is the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. The ReLU activation function can be used to give the framework as a whole non-linearity without affecting the reception fields of the convolutional layer. The function is a simple but powerful tool for modeling neural networks since it yields a result that is exactly proportionate to the input. Whenever the input is positive, the ReLU function provides the value directly; if not, the result is zero. Because it solves the problem of disappearing gradients and enables models to train more quickly and efficiently, it has been the standard activation function in a variety of neural networks61. Challenges and future research trends

The most widely used method for image analysis is CNN. Future endeavors should concentrate on achieving enhanced precision in the classification of a user’s facial expressions. Providing information on observed facial expressions is essential for an accurate categorization of emotions. Research and design in HRI require much increased involvement from the human factors community compared to previous instances, with the exception of certain domains like commercial aviation and defense systems, where human factors specialists have historically participated. Modern advances in autonomous vehicles and drones have substantial obstacles in terms of safety and social acceptance76. All of the modalities analyzed offer potential sources of knowledge for future research and development. For example, the quick development of emotionally aware robots is expected to result from the combination of ML advancements with accessible and inventive technology, including depth sensors and smart gadgets. However, the demand for reliable findings, a trustworthy relationship, and the time limitations necessary to incorporate the observed emotion within the modification of robotic behavior makes the identification of emotions with robots a challenge today. The goal of future research might be to categorize facial reactions of users with greater accuracy. True emotion classification requires an understanding of the recognized facial gestures; it is suggested that larger, higher-resolution, higher-dimensional images be used and that more users participate in the video evaluation to see whether improved outcomes in FER might be achieved. A temporal series of facial expressions might be used to train a new emotion classifier that uses long-short-term memory (LSTM) networks77. A time series can deliver the categorization with some context, since it can be determined by an array of images that define a gesture of an emotion rather than just one specific facial expression. Future advancements will use many inputs, including infrared photos, audio models, and physiological information and depth information collected from 3D facial models, to increase the accuracy of methodologies78. The HRI model created in this study will be improved for use in medicine, primarily to help elderly patients suffering from dementia and to improve the interpersonal skills of kids diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder. Through interaction with a humanoid robot, medical input from psychologists, doctors, instructors, and therapists might be used in the research to alleviate anxiety and dissatisfaction.

Conclusions

This paper presents an image-driven computer vision methodology for advancing CNN techniques for performing FER using different datasets. The proposed framework is suitable for real-time recognition of emotions in many applications, including augmented reality, HRI, and robots. Advanced approaches, including data augmentation, transfer learning, and optimizing hyperparameters, can help improve the FER framework even more. This work proposes an implementation of a framework capable of predicting and identifying facial emotion via the use of the CNN technique and methods to extract features. The proposed approach has great potential for FER and can make major contributions to AI and computer vision. A significant problem in previous studies was their reliance on static pictures that do not evolve over time, rendering them time-consuming and unsuitable for real-time applications. In contrast, our method can immediately forecast people’s emotions using different FER datasets, including FER2013, RAF-DB, and CK+. These findings have substantial implications for FER since they illustrate the ability of ML algorithms to analyze facial expressions with precision and efficiency in real time. Employing advanced methodologies, including transfer learning, data augmentation, and hyperparameter tuning, can further enhance the model. The suggested model exhibits substantial potential for emotion recognition and could significantly advance the fields of computer vision and machine learning.
