Stereotactic radiosurgery using a self-shielded gyroscopic radiosurgery system for meningiomas: technical aspects and dosimetric results
Authors: Yongsook C. Lee, Rupesh Kotecha, Robert H. Press, Noah S. Kalman, Matthew D. Hall, D. Jay Wieczorek, Ranjini Tolakanahalli, Minesh P. Mehta, Michael W. McDermott, Alonso N. Gutierrez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ZAP-X® Gyroscopic Radiosurgery® platform (ZAP Surgical Systems, Inc., San Carlos, CA, USA) is a novel self-shielded image-guided radiotherapy device developed for frameless stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and fractionated SRS (FSRS) of intracranial and cervical spine lesions. This article presents technical aspects and dosimetric results for SRS and FSRS using the ZAP-X system for meningioma patients.MethodsUnique technical aspects of our treatment workflow using the ZAP-X system encompassing computed tomography simulation, treatment planning, independent dose calculations, patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) and treatment delivery were described. Eighteen single-lesion meningioma patients, who were treated following this workflow, were selected and divided into two groups (Group A [thirteen patients]: 25 Gy in five fractions, FSRS); Group B [five patients]: 15 Gy in a single fraction, SRS). Plan quality metrics, independent dose calculations and PSQA for each group were reviewed and analyzed.ResultsFor Group A, mean age (range) was 60 years (35 years − 80 years) and mean planning target volume (PTV) (range) was 4.78 cc (2.10 cc − 9.38 cc). Mean (range) PTV coverage, prescribed isodose line, Paddick conformity index, gradient index, number of isocenters, number of beams, delivery time estimate per fraction and PSQA gamma passing rate were 99.5% (99.5% − 99.6%), 53.5% (50.1% − 61.3%), 0.89 (0.83–0.93), 2.64 (2.54–2.88), 11.8 (5–19), 208.5 (91–332), 43.6 min (24.5 –61.0 min) and 95.0% (91.5% − 98.4%), respectively. For Group B, corresponding values were 70 years (62 years − 75 years), 1.12 cc (0.77 cc − 1.99 cc), 99.5% (99.5% − 99.5%), 56.4% (50.6% − 62.8%), 0.89 (0.85–0.93), 2.95 (2.63–3.22), 7.2 (5–9), 102.4 (81–132), 33.9 min (27.3 –38.9 min) and 95.3% (91.1% − 100.0%). For all beams in both groups, independent dose calculations were < ± 0.02% of treatment planning system dose calculations.ConclusionsOur experience with SRS and FSRS using the ZAP-X system for meningioma patients demonstrated that a typical external beam radiotherapy workflow was applicable with unique technical aspects, and treatment plans met our institutional planning goals and were concordant with independent dose calculations and PSQA.

Background

The ZAP-X® Gyroscopic Radiosurgery® platform (ZAP Surgical Systems, Inc., San Carlos, CA, USA) is a novel self-shieled image-guided radiotherapy device developed for frameless stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and fractionated SRS (FSRS) of intracranial and cervical spine lesions [1]. Prior dosimetry/physics characterization work and the first two-year machine performance have demonstrated safety and accuracy of the system for SRS [2–13]. The unique features of this system and its dosimetric properties have been previously described [1–6].

Since early 2019, ZAP-X has been in clinical operation worldwide [7]. Despite the brief history of the system, various intracranial conditions, including malignant tumors (e.g., brain metastases), benign tumors (e.g., meningioma, pituitary adenoma, neurocytoma, and hemangioma), functional disorders (e.g., trigeminal neuralgia) and vascular disorders (e.g., arteriovenous malformation) have been treated using the ZAP-X [14–21]. Dosimetric performance, patient satisfaction, and short- or long-term outcomes reported by the users have suggested the suitability of this device for SRS [16–19, 21].

In 2024, ZAP-X was installed, commissioned, and the first patient was treated at our institution. Here, we report our experience of SRS and FSRS for meningiomas treated on this unit, specifically focusing on technical aspects of our treatment workflow encompassing computed tomography (CT) simulation, treatment planning, independent dose calculations, patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) and treatment delivery as well as dosimetric results. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to cover these technical aspects and to present dosimetric results for a homogeneous patient population treated using this novel delivery system.

Methods

ZAP-X system

Our ZAP-X treatment unit and operator console are shown in Fig. 1. The treatment unit is comprised of a shielded rotating spherical chamber, a pneumatic door with a rotary shell, a treatment table, a linear accelerator (linac), a kilovoltage (kV) imaging system, a megavoltage (MV) detector, and safety systems [1]. The spherical chamber, shell and door create shelf shielding during treatment delivery, eliminating the necessity for a shielded vault (Fig. 1(a)) [7]. The table has unique motions (yaw: left-right; longitudinal: superior-inferior; pitch: anterior-posterior) to move each isocenter of a treatment plan to the machine isocenter (45 cm from the source, isocentric delivery) [8]. The linac, mounted on coupled gimbals, rotates around a common isocenter along an axial axis or multiple oblique planes and produces 3 MV unflattened static non-coplanar beams (dmax ~ 7 mm) at a nominal dose rate of 1500 monitor unit (MU)/min (Fig. 1(b)) [10]. The tungsten-shielded collimator wheel under the linac has eight openings to achieve circular collimation (4, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, 20 and 25 mm in diameter) and rotates to align the center of the collimators to the machine isocenter (Fig. 1(c)) [4]. The kV imaging system, consisting of a single x-ray tube and a single detector (41 cm × 41 cm), is used for pretreatment alignment and intrafraction motion monitoring (Fig. 1(b)) [8]. The MV detector monitors exit dose during treatment (Fig. 1(b)) [7]. Safety systems include multiple cameras inside the unit, a collimator collision sensor near the linac (Fig. 1(b)), proximity sensors in the unit (Fig. 1(a)) and other safety features in user interface (UI) on the console (Fig. 1(d)).

ZAP-X releases

Table 1 compares ZAP-X releases among DP-1008, DP-1010 and DP-1011. The initial release at the system installation was DP-1008 and it was later upgraded to DP-1010 (after DP-1009) and DP-1011 sequentially. The major hardware upgrade to DP-1009/1010 entailed a new treatment table with CT simulation overlay and a new mask system dedicated to the new table. The new table has allowed for treatment of lower cervical spine lesions (5.2 cm longer), more solid angles for beams (15–25% increased) and greater weight limit (from 300 lbs to 460 lbs). The DP-1009/1010 software upgrade encompassed slight changes in UI on the console and additional module (Advanced workspace) added to the existing treatment planning system (TPS) (Legacy TPS). The Advanced workspace, which is embedded in the Legacy TPS with a small icon, has the same dose calculation and optimization algorithms (Analytic Ray Tracing using linear convex) but uses a more powerful dose calculation engine to handle more beams. Uniquely, an automated isocenter placement tool is available in this workspace. DP-1011, limited to a software upgrade only, has introduced a new feature, called gyroscopic corrections for table rotations (pitch, yaw and roll) by physical adjustment of gantry angles after setup verification (initial and between isocenters) and during treatment delivery.

Patient cohort

Eighteen (n = 18) single-lesion meningioma patients, who were treated using our treatment workflow (Fig. 2) and were enrolled onto an institutional clinical/biorepository registry, were included in this report. While thirteen patients received FSRS to a planning target volume (PTV) (= GTV [gross tumor volume] + 1-mm setup margin), with a regimen of 25 Gy in five fractions (Group A), the rest received SRS to the PTV to 15 Gy in a single fraction (Group B). These regimens were specifically selected using institutional prescription guidelines. Of eighteen patients, the first six (Group A: 5; Group B: 1) were treated with DP-1008, and five (Group A: 4; Group B: 1) and seven patients (Group A: 4; Group B: 3) were treated after the upgrades to DP-1010 and DP-1011, respectively (Table 2). This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board.

CT simulation

In our CT simulator, all patients underwent CT simulation using the ZAP-X CT overlay. The CT overlay simulates the treatment table during CT simulation. The CT overlay, consisting of a CT head support (superior part) and an adaptor (inferior part), was positioned on a 2-pin index bar locked on the CT simulator table (Fig. 3(a)). The ZAP-X cushion was added on the overlay. For DP-1008, a head rest (Silverman Head Support, Klarity, Heath, OH, USA) with a head cushion (AccuForm™ Cushions, CQ Medical, Avondale, PA, USA) was placed on the CT overlay and an SRS mask (Qfix, Gamma Gurus Pty Ltd, Sydney, Australia) was created on a patient. For DP-1010/1011, a head cushion (AccuForm) only and a dedicated mask (Orfit Industries, Wijnegem, Belgium) were used (Fig. 3(b)). Using our CT scan protocol for SRS treatment (orientation: head-first supine; field of view: 350 mm; matrix size: 512 × 512; slice thickness: 1.0 mm (contiguous); metal artifact reduction turned on), CT scans without and with contrast agent were sequentially acquired. CT scout images (axial and lateral views) assured that the CT table was not included in the CT scans and the CT overlay was not cut off superiorly and posteriorly (DP-1008 and DP-1010/1011) (Fig. 3(c)). Additional imaging for all patients included T1 post-contrast magnetic resonance (MR) images (MPRAGE and SPACE) and gallium-68 dotatate positron emission tomography (PET) images.

Treatment planning

Since the current release (DP-1011 or before) of ZAP-X TPS does not have sophisticated contouring tools (e.g., margin expansion), contouring was performed in a third-party TPS. CT, MR, and PET images were imported in RayStation (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) and image fusion was performed. The GTV was contoured on CT images without contrast (planning CT images) fused to CT images with contrast, MR images and dotatate PET images [22]. No clinical target volume margin expansion was utilized. A 1-mm setup margin was used to derive a PTV from the GTV. Organs-at-risk (OARs) such as the brainstem, optic chiasm, and optic nerves were also contoured. After the planning CT images, and structure set were imported into ZAP-X TPS, the table origin (green cross in Fig. 3(c)), which is automatically determined by the detection of the indexing in the CT overlay, was confirmed before treatment planning.

Thirteen patients (Group A: nine; Group B: four) were planned in Legacy TPS and the rest (Group A: four; Group B: one) in Advanced workspace (Fig. 4(a)-(b)) following our institutional planning guidelines (PTV coverage (COV) ≥ 99.5%, prescribed isodose line (Rx IDL) ≥ 50%, Paddick conformity index (PCI) as close to 1.0 as possible, and gradient index (GI) ≤ 3.0) [23, 24]. Planning in Legacy TPS was performed in the following sequence. First, isocenters were manually placed within the PTV and collimator size (eight collimators: 4, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, 20 and 25 mm) for each isocenter was chosen (Fig. 4(c)). Second, path density (five options: 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8) for each isocenter was selected. Within a collision clearance zone for each patient, a higher path density has more beam angle options. Third, prescription dose and the number of fractions were entered. Fourth, maximum and minimum doses for the PTV, maximum dose for OAR(s) (if applicable), maximum dose for points at 1 mm, 2 mm, 5 mm and/or 10 mm from the PTV and the maximum number of MU per beam (typically 200 MU, limit of 500 MU) were set in the optimizer (Fig. 4(d)) and the optimizer was run with a dose grid size of 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm. After inverse planning optimization, Rx IDL (%), delivery time estimate per fraction (min), beam angles, the number of beams per isocenter and MU per beam were determined. In Advanced workspace, the same steps were followed except that the position and the number of the isocenters and collimator size were automatically determined using the automated isocenter placement tool. In DP-1011, gyroscopic corrections were turned on during planning.

Independent dose calculations for an approved clinical plan were performed using a web-based in-house tool developed with Python. After treatment plan approval by the treating physician, an Excel file (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) containing beam parameters (beam number, isocenter coordinate, collimator size, MU, effective depth to target, dose at isocenter, and so on) generated in the TPS, was exported to a local drive. The in-house tool, which accepts patient information and the file as input parameters (Fig. 5), was then run to calculate dose for each beam using an equation of MU × output factor × a tissue phantom ratio (TPR) at an effective depth (isocentric delivery) and the results were compared with TPS calculated dose. A report in a PDF format was automatically created in the same location as the Excel file.

PSQA

A PSQA plan for each approved clinical plan was created in Legacy TPS. Each clinical plan was copied onto CT images of a QA phantom (StereoPHAN and SRS MapCHECK, Sun Nuclear, Melbourne, FL, USA) such that the center of the target in each plan was automatically aligned to the center (i.e., intersection of X, Y and Z axes) of SRS MapCHECK (Fig. 6(a)). Each plan was then re-calculated on the StereoPHAN containing SRS MapCHECK and buildup spacers with a dose grid size of 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm. Following the recommendations of the Sun Nuclear SRS MapCHECK user guide, the average CT number and the density (1.16 g/cm3) of the StereoPHAN were included in the CT density table during commissioning of TPS. Planar dose of a composite plan for five fractions (Group A) or for a single fraction (Group B) was exported for PSQA.

PSQA was performed following American Association of Physicists in Medicine Task Group (AAPM TG)-218 recommendations [25]. After a ZAP-X flat plate adapter for DP-1010/1011 was placed on the table, the SterePHAN with SRS MapCHECK was attached to the adapter (Fig. 6(b)). With array calibration and dose calibration of the SRS MapCHECK selected, one (or single) fraction of each QA plan was delivered and recorded in Sun Nuclear SRS MapCHECK software (version 8.6.0.76). Measurement was then compared to calculation with a dose scaling factor of 0.2 (one fraction, Group A) or 1.0 (single fraction, Group B) applied. For the comparison, gamma criteria of dose difference/distance-to-agreement of 2%/1 mm were applied. Absolute dose mode, global normalization and a threshold of 10% were used. Our tolerance limit was set to gamma passing rate ≥ 90%.

Treatment delivery

For every fraction, the coincidence of the radiation, mechanical and imaging isocenters of the ZAP-X system was verified. An anthropomorphic head phantom with a steel ball cube inside was positioned on the table (Fig. 7(a)). The Steel Ball test under Daily QA in the UI of the console was executed and automated analysis was displayed in the UI when it was completed. The Steel Ball test is a pre-programed Winston-Lutz test consisting of six beams with the 15 mm collimator at different gantry (axial/oblique) angles. At each gantry angle, kV imaging is taken for setup verification and the MV imager captures an image while a beam is delivered. After the upgrade to DP-1011, a three dimensionally (3D) printed mount for the steel ball cube was devised and used for more reproducible setup (Fig. 7(b)). Other daily QA tests were also performed following the QA protocol in Lee et al. [1].

Setup for the Steel Ball test (Winston-Lutz test) using (a) a head phantom and (b) a 3D printed mount (blue)

Treatment was delivered with intrafraction motion monitoring. After each patient was positioned on the treatment table, three kV images were taken at three pre-determined gantry angles (axial/oblique: 90°/180°, 180°/180° and 240°/180°) and automated alignments between kV images and digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs) were performed for initial setup verification (Fig. 8). Two kV images were acquired at two treatment beam angles separated by ≥ 30.0 degrees in the axial angle for every isocenter, and automated alignments were performed. All automated image registrations were visually reviewed before treatment. During treatment, patient motion was monitored by a single kV image taken every 45 s (default, adjustable between 10 s and 120 s) at a treatment beam angle. In DP-1008 and DP-1010, when table rotations approached 3.0 degrees in any axis, the patient was coached to adjust their head accordingly before imaging at the next isocenter. If the rotations exceeded 3.0 degrees, an alert appeared in the UI and initial setup verification was repeated at the user’s discretion. After imaging, only translational offsets (left-right, superior-inferior, and anterior-posterior) were automatically corrected by table shifts. In DP-1011, gyroscopic corrections were turned on during treatment delivery in the UI and rotational offsets (yaw, pitch and roll, ≤3 degrees) were automatically corrected by gantry angles in addition to the corrections of translational offsets.

Results

Patient characteristics and plan quality metrics of treatment plans for Group A (five fractions) are summarized in Table 2. Most patients were female (ten patients, 76.9%). The mean age (range) at the time of treatment was 60 years (35 years − 80 years). The mean PTV (range) was 4.78 cc (2.10 cc − 9.38 cc). Mean values (ranges) of PTV COV, Rx IDL, PCI, and GI were 99.5% (99.5% − 99.6%), 53.5% (50.1% − 61.3%), 0.89 (0.83–0.93) and 2.64 (2.54–2.88), respectively. Corresponding values for the number of isocenters, the number of beams, total MUs, and delivery time estimate per fraction were 11.8 (5–19), 208.5 (91–332), 30640.8 (12971.7–52749.0) and 43.6 min (24.5 –61.0 min), respectively. The delivery time estimate includes imaging times for pretreatment alignment and at each isocenter and intrafraction motion monitoring. Discussion

The upgrades to DP-1010 and DP-1011 were seamless but required commissioning and/or testing before clinical use. Commissioning for the upgrade to DP-1010 included mechanical QA tests for the new treatment table, all dosimetry and imaging QA tests involving the table described in Lee et al. [1] as well as TPS testing (treatment planning and delivery) for the Advanced workspace. Safety training for the manual shell release valve was also performed with the vendor. Validation of gyroscopic corrections was completed after the upgrade to DP-1011. After upgrades, slight modifications to routine machine QA procedures were made along with updated internal procedure documents.

A similar workflow for any other external beam radiotherapy using a linac was applied to ZAP-X treatment (Fig. 2) but there are multiple unique technical aspects dedicated to ZAP-X workflow. (1) CT simulation requires CT overlay for the treatment table and the definition of stereotactic coordinates. (2) Two different TPS modules (Legacy and Advanced workspace) in the releases of DP-1010/1011 are available for treatment planning. (3) Due to TPS DICOM incompatibility, an in-house tool or equivalent is needed for verification of TPS dose calculations. (4) The Winston-Lutz test and analysis are fully automated in the UI of the console. (5) During setup verification, fusion between kV images and DRRs is automatically performed without any manual adjustment option. (6) After imaging, translational offsets (DP-1011 or before) and/or rotational offsets (DP-1011) are automatically applied before treatment and/or during treatment and intervention of the user is limited. Of these, DICOM incompatibility is expected to be improved in next TPS version(s).

For ZAP-X treatment planning, structured training and hand-on experience were essential to enhance plan quality and reduce planning time as the program matured. Planning for the first few patients in Legacy TPS was time intensive requiring 4–5 h per patient. With increased familiarity, the average planning time was reduced to 2–3 h per patient. This process included manual isocenter placement, optimization, and iterative fine adjustments. Among these, manual isocenter placement with adjustments of isocenter position and collimator selection to improve plan quality were most tedious. The introduction of the automated isocenter placement tool in the Advanced workspace substantially reduced this effort lowering the time for isocenter placement (and manual adjustment if necessary) to less than 5–10 min. As planners gain additional experience, further improvements in plan quality are anticipated.

While all treatment plans for Group A (five fractions) met our institutional planning goals, two of the five plans for Group B (single fraction) had GI greater than 3.0. Both of these plans (GI > 3.0) had PTV < 1.0 cc. Ehret et al. conducted a prospective study for SRS treatments using the ZAP-X system and reported analysis for the first 100 patients with 155 lesions [17]. Their study included brain metastases, vestibular schwannomas and meningiomas treated in a single fraction but analysis was performed for the three diseases combined [17]. For PTV of 0.34 cc − 2.69 cc (25% − 75% interquartile range), PCI and GI ranged from 0.78 to 0.84 and from 2.88 to 3.41, respectively, with PTV COV of 96.7% − 99.1% [17]. In contrast, for Group B (PTV of 0.77 cc − 1.99 cc) in our patient population, PCI and GI were 0.85–0.93 and 2.63–3.22 respectively, with a higher PTV COV ≥ 99.5% (Table 2). Smaller PCI and greater GI in Ehret et al.’s study could be attributed to irregular shape of PTV (vestibular schwannoma) and smaller PTV (brain metastases). Rx IDL in their study (52.0% − 62.0%) was comparable to that in our cohort (50.6% − 62.8%) (Table 2) [17]. It is noted that there were only five patients in Group B and PTVs in Group A were too large (2.10 cc − 9.38 cc) to compare analysis for those (25% − 75% interquartile range) in Ehret et al.

There are several studies published reporting SRS treatment plan quality metrics for meningiomas. Lee et al. performed a retrospective analysis for meningioma patients who were treated using Gamma Knife (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) and had > 10-year follow up [26]. For the treatment success group (n = 52), mean ± SD (range) was 4.10 cc ± 3.52 cc (0.20 cc − 13.97 cc) for GTV, was 92.8% ± 4.6% (76.8% − 99.6%) for target COV, and was 0.75 ± 0.12 (0.43–0.89) for PCI [26]. GI was not presented in their study. Singh et al. also analyzed 71 meningioma patients treated with Gamma Knife and reported the mean target volume of 8.21 cc, the mean target COV (range) of 94.0% (79.0% − 100%), the mean PCI (range) of 0.74 (0.52–0.96), and the mean GI (range) of 3.02 (2.47–3.96) [27]. Kaul et al. re-planned ten meningioma patients, who were treated using CyberKnife (Accuray, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), for Gamma Knife and a linac (Novalis, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) [28]. In their study, for the mean ± SD PTV value of 6.26 cc ± 3.43 cc, plan quality metrics for Gamma Knife, CyberKnife and Novalis in sequence were 99.4% ± 1.29%, 99.3% ± 1.29%, 99.3% ± 0.69% for PTV COV, 0.77 ± 0.06, 0.76 ± 0.07, 0.66 ± 0.07 for PCI, and 2.71 ± 0.18, 3.38 ± 0.06, 3.51 ± 0.85 for GI [28]. Their study mentioned Rx IDL (%) of ~ 50% for Gamma Knife, ~ 70% for CyberKnife and ~ 70% for Novalis [28]. Compared with these previous studies, plan quality metrics for Group B in our study seem improved or comparable. TPS dose calculations were verified using our in-house tool. The in-house tool was developed because the plan DICOM format from ZAP-X TPS (DP-1011 or before) was not compatible with any commercial software. The ZAP-X TPS uses an Analytic Ray Tracing algorithm which is based on the effective depth as determined by the density variation along the beam path accounting for tissue heterogeneities [29]. In the ZAP-X system, machine output (1 cGy/MU) is calibrated for the 25 mm collimator at a source to axis distance (SAD) of 45 cm at dmax (~ 7 mm). Since beam delivery is isocentric, SAD is always 45 cm and off-center ratio (OCR) is 1.0 at isocenter for each beam. Therefore, dose for each beam is simply calculated using MU, output factor (OF) and TPR at an effective depth using Eq. (1) below. The ZAP-X TPS provides MU and an effective depth for each beam after planning is completed. Using the information, the in-house tool, in which OF and TPR are embedded, calculated dose at isocenter for each beam. PSQA for clinical plans of all patients in both groups met the QA passing rate set in our clinic (≥ 90% with gamma criteria of 2%/1 mm). For PSQA, the AAPM TG-218 recommendations were followed [25]: (1) QA was performed using the true composite delivery method, that is, all beams were delivered to a measurement device placed on the table using the actual treatment beam geometry for the patient (Fig. 6). (2) Applied tolerances (gamma criteria of 2%/1 mm, 10% threshold, global normalization) were tighter than the recommendations for intensity modulated radiation therapy (gamma passing rate ≥ 90% with 3%/2 mm and a 10% dose threshold with global normalization). Of eighteen patients, eight patients (six in Group A; two in Group B) achieved gamma passing rates > 95% and the rest had lower passing rates. For the plans which had lower passing rates, failing points typically occurred in low dose regions and/or high gradient regions (Fig. 15) or when daily output was lower or higher than usual. Overall, PSQA implementation using SRS MapCHECK was successful and no issue was encountered.

Switching to the 3D printed mount for the Steel Ball (Winston-Lutz) test improved setup reproducibility (Fig. 7(b)) and consequently, better and more consistent results (mean target offsets < ± 0.25 mm in each direction). The head phantom with a mask was susceptible to inconsistent setup (Fig. 7(a)). When large table rotations exist, they are not automatically corrected and can lead to greater target offsets (Fig. 13(b)). The 3D printed mount was designed such that it was securely docked on the table in the same position daily. The results over several months were fairly consistent (Fig. 13(b)), demonstrating the degree of accuracy and precision for the isocenter alignment of the ZAP-X system.

All eighteen patients tolerated the treatment well and completed the course of treatment. During planning, delivery time per fraction was limited to 60 min or shorter after experience with the very first patient. For the first patient, limited planning experience led to the use of excessive number of isocenters (19) which unnecessarily prolonged the delivery time per fraction to 61 min (Fig. 9(e) and (h)). For Group A (five fractions), three patients (23.1%) required a brief treatment interruption (i.e., got off the table for 10–15 min) (Fig. 16(a)): the first patient had an extended delivery time (61 min), the second had a panic attack, and the third reported mask discomfort leading to excessive motion. For Group B (single fraction), none of the patients needed a break (Fig. 16(b)) because of shorter delivery time (mean: 33.9 min (Group B) vs. 43.6 min (Group A)). Our overall experience with meningioma and non-meningioma patients (data not shown in this report) is that patients would typically need a break midway through the treatment if delivery time is longer than 45 min. Even though our overall experience with ZAP-X was favorable, there are some limitations to this study. First, the dataset for this study was comprised of small patient cohort (thirteen for five fractions and five for a single fraction) from a single institution. Second, short-term or long-term follow-up outcomes were not included. Further research and investigation for larger patient cohorts will be warranted.

Conclusions

Our experience with SRS and FSRS using the ZAP-X system for the treatment of single-lesion meningioma patients demonstrated that a typical external beam radiotherapy workflow was applicable with unique technical aspects, and treatment plans met our institutional planning goals for other radiosurgery systems and were concordant with independent dose calculations and PSQA.
