Women in chemistry: Q&A with Dr. Stefanie Flohr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDr. Stefanie Flohr serves as an Associate Director at Novartis Biomedical Research in Basel, Switzerland.

Alexander Smith

With over two decades of experience as a medicinal chemist and team leader, she has contributed to various fields including immunology, ophthalmology, neuroscience, oncology, and rare diseases. This year, Dr. Flohr began leading the team that utilizes AI-enabled medicinal chemistry expertise to support programs across the Novartis portfolio in Basel.

Dr. Flohr contributed to the development of Fabhalta® (iptacopan), Novartis 1st in class Factor B inhibitor. In recognition of her accomplishments, she received the Friedrich Stolz Award in 2024 from the German Chemical Society and German Pharmaceutical Society alongside her colleagues. Dr. Flohr earned her Ph.D. from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) in 1998 under the supervision of Prof. Herbert Waldmann followed by postdoctoral research in biocatalysis with Prof. Donald Hilvert at ETH, Zürich, Switzerland. Prior to joining Novartis, she worked as a medicinal chemist and chemistry project leader at Aventis in Frankfurt, Germany from 1999 to 2002.

Why did you choose to be a scientist?

Chemistry was one of my favorite subjects in high school. The turning point for me was reading a biography of Marie Curie; her scientific curiosity and perseverance were truly inspiring.

What scientific development are you currently most excited about?

I’m fascinated by the possibilities that AI/ML methods and generative chemistry offer. Although it’s still early days, there’s so much we need to learn to use these tools effectively. This aligns perfectly with my scientific curiosity, as I love working in a field that opens new, unexplored trajectories.

What direction do you think your research field should go in?

On top of the new developments such as oral peptides and generative chemistry to name just two, I think, we should always strive to develop new synthetic methods. For example, photochemistry has revolutionized the ease with which we can prepare certain compounds, and biocatalysis has enabled us to access many molecules with defined however challenging stereochemistry. I look forward to seeing more advancements that will make our work in the lab even easier.

What aspects of your research do you find most exciting or most rewarding?

My initial dream was to contribute to a compound that would make it to the pharmacy. With the launch of Fabhalta® (iptacopan) end of last year, that dream has come true. Over the past few years, I’ve developed a new passion for mentoring and developing colleagues. It’s incredibly rewarding to witness the growth of those around you!

Are you or have you been supported by a mentor? What was the best advice you received?

Early in my career, I didn’t seek out mentorship, as it wasn’t as common then. However, a management course with female colleagues inspired me to change that. One memorable moment in my mentorship journey was when a senior leader suggested to proactively and early-on prepare a plan to organize and delegate my work for the four months I would be off work for the birth of my second daughter. The plan allowed me to retain the project team leadership, which was a crucial learning experience in my professional life. Most importantly, this experience taught me that I could take control of my career path.

Do you have any advice you would like to share with women starting out in chemical research?

At first, I thought I needed to be 110% ready for a job and wait to be asked. This mindset, which I believe is more common among women, has evolved for me. I’ve realized that being 90% ready is perfectly fine, and even less can work. Embracing learning, seeking advice from experienced colleagues, experimenting, and asking for feedback are essential. You learn the fastest when you’re willing to take leaps! A standout moment for me was when I became the team leader for the early preclinical development team of our complement inhibitors, despite lacking experience in those phases. It felt like being thrown into cold water, but I had a senior leader to turn to for advice and great colleagues from whom I learned a lot. That year marked my steepest learning curve.

Where do you hope to see women in chemistry in 20 years?

When I started my industry career over 20 years ago, I was the second female lab head in the Medicinal Chemistry department in Germany. Thankfully, this changed rapidly, and now almost 50% of our new hires are women. Ideally, I’d love to see this representation extend to all levels, whether in industry or in academia, and hopefully much sooner than in another 20 years. From what I observe, we’re making great strides in this area.
