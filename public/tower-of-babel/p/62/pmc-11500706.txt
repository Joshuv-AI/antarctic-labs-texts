Effects of In-Air Post Deposition Annealing Process on the Oxygen Vacancy Content in Sputtered GDC Thin Films Probed via Operando XAS and Raman Spectroscopy
Authors: Nunzia Coppola, Sami Ur Rehman, Giovanni Carapella, Luca Braglia, Vincenzo Vaiano, Dario Montinaro, Veronica Granata, Sandeep Kumar Chaluvadi, Pasquale Orgiani, Piero Torelli, Luigi Maritato, Carmela Aruta, Alice Galdi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigate the ionic mobility in room-temperature RF-sputtered gadolinium doped ceria (GDC) thin films grown on industrial solid oxide fuel cell substrates as a function of the air-annealing at 800 and 1000 °C. The combination of X-ray diffraction, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy allows us to study the different Ce3+/ Ce4+ ratios induced by the post growth annealing procedure, together with the Ce valence changes induced by different gas atmosphere exposure. Our results give evidence of different kinetics as a function of the annealing temperature, with the sample annealed at 800 °C showing marked changes of the Ce oxidation state when exposed to both reducing and oxidizing gas atmospheres at moderate temperature (300 °C), while the Ce valence is weakly affected for the 1000 °C annealed sample. Raman spectra measurements allow us to trace the responses of the investigated samples to different gas atmospheres on the basis of the presence of different Gd–O bond strengths inside the lattice. These findings provide insight into the microscopic origin of the best performances already observed in SOFCs with a sputtered GDC barrier layer annealed at 800 °C and are fundamental to further improve sputtered GDC thin film performance in energy devices.Keywords: doped ceria, thin films, solid oxide cells, operando XAS, Raman spectroscopy, defect chemistry engineering, annealing temperature

Introduction

Cerium oxides doped with trivalent rare earths have attracted interest in the past years due to their structural and chemical stability, ionic conductivity, and catalytic properties that make them suitable for a wide range of applications.1−4 The hydrogen oxidation reaction used to generate electricity in solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) relies on the ionic transport through the electrolyte and the barrier layer that usually is a gadolinium doped ceria (GDC) layer.5−7 This layer is the one directly at the interface with the cathode and the gas phase, i.e., it is part of the triple-phase boundaries where the oxygen reduction reaction and the incorporation of O2– ions in the solid electrolyte mainly take place. As a general rule, ionic conductivity σ can be directly related to ionic mobility μ with the following equation: σ = nqμ, where n is the density of charge carrier ions and q is the ion’s charge.8,9 Concerning doped cerias, an increase in the ionic conductivity can be related to both dopant type and dopant concentration:10 when changing the dopant type, the ion migration barrier changes and, therefore, so does the ionic mobility μ; at the same time, the concentration of the dopant affects the oxygen diffusion coefficient through vacancy formation.

It is well-known that, in GDC, the ionic conductivity is related to the hopping of oxygen ions through oxygen vacancy sites in the lattice and that the oxygen vacancy formation mechanism is associated with the valence change in cerium (from Ce4+ to Ce3+) induced by Gd doping.3,11 Furthermore, once the Gd doping percentage is fixed, the ionic conductivity of the GDC layer can vary depending upon the oxygen vacancy concentration, structure, granularity, and defect incorporation.

Physical vapor deposition (PVD) techniques have been successfully used for dense GDC barrier layer thin film growth and constitute a promising alternative to standard SOFC fabrication processes; moreover, PVD techniques usually imply the use of lower fabrication temperatures when compared to the industrially used sintering process.12−17 The use of a post growth annealing treatment at moderate temperatures also turns out to be beneficial to reduce interdiffusion and increase the overall SOFC performance.14,16,18−20

Previous works9−12 demonstrated a huge increment of output current (up to +78%) and the simultaneous decrement of the ohmic resistance (up to −42%) in SOFCs in which the barrier layer is a room-temperature sputtered GDC thin film with respect to industrial screen-printed GDC. Among the SOFCs embedding sputtered layers, the best performances were attained by the ones postannealed in air at 800 °C, which showed the lowest ohmic resistance (ca. −13% in the ones annealed at 800 °C with respect to the ones annealed at 1000 °C). Nonetheless, in these studies, the microscopic mechanisms related to the activity of oxygen vacancies in the thin film were not investigated. Element and valence sensitive probes, such as quantitative X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), allow one to achieve characterization at the atomic level of nanostructured polycrystalline GDC layers deposited on granular anode/electrolyte bilayer substrates (industrial half-cells), giving hints of the interplay between morphology and stoichiometry in determining the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio that rules their ionic and electronic conductivity.11,21,22 In particular, in ref (11) Schmitt et al. give a detailed and updated review of defect structure in ceria and solid solutions and relate them to their catalytic and redox activity, reporting a detailed bibliography of XAFS, XAS, and Raman experiments. In ref (21), Pryds and Esposito point out the possibility of determining and modulating defects in 2D oxide heterostructures to obtain superior ionic (or electronic) properties. In ref (22), Yang and co-workers systematically explore the effect of trivalent rare earth dopants on the cerium oxidation state, making use of in situ XPS and coupling the ionic radius of the dopant to Ce3+ formation in epitaxial doped ceria thin films.

In this work, we study the results obtained on three GDC room-temperature radio frequency (RF)-sputtered GDC thin films, annealed with the same heating ramp rate but different dwell temperatures, making use of X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements. Moreover, operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) has been employed to investigate the changes in the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio in different reactive atmospheres (namely neutral, oxidizing, and reducing). The measurements have been conducted by making use of the ambient pressure cell available at the APE-HE beamline (Trieste, Italy). These measurements have allowed us to determine the role of the annealing temperature in the density of oxygen vacancies in the GDC sample, highlighting the changes induced in the nanostructured layer in terms of the availability and reversibility of the oxygen ions hopping sites and, thus, the final efficiency of the cells. Raman spectroscopy measurements have been performed to highlight microstructural changes (amount of oxygen vacancies and intrinsic and extrinsic defects) among the samples both before and after the XAS experiment.

Our results are particularly relevant to understand the post growth annealing induced microscopic mechanisms occurring in room-temperature sputtered GDC thin films and to optimize them in both reducing and oxidizing atmospheres in terms of oxygen storage, release, and transport. In particular, our findings can explain the better performances already observed in sputtered GDC barrier layers embedded in industrial SOFC matrices when annealed at 800 °C23 in comparison to the performances obtained with 1000 °C annealing. These findings can be extended to all other devices that use GDC layers to improve ionic conductivity and catalyzing properties such as gas sensors,24,25 solar to fuel convertors,26,27 catalysts,28,29 and oxygen storage devices.30

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation

Gadolinium doped ceria Gd0.1Ce0.9O1.95−δ (GDC) thin films were grown on approximately 5 × 5 mm2 SolydEra S.p.A. industrial anode-supported half-cells (NiO/YSZ bilayer), making use of a radio frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering instrument equipped with a 15 cm diameter and 99.999% purity target. The power discharge used was 400 W with the typical RF frequency of 13.6 MHz to set up the Ar plasma (2.3 Torr pressure); the deposition rate was 30 nm/min and was stable during the target lifetime.23,31,32 Three 300 nm thick films were fabricated and then thermally treated in air using an optimized ramp profile23 (the so-called “slow ramp” in ref (23)) consisting of a slow increase from room temperature up to 300 °C (1 °C/min, 300 °C maintained for 1 h), followed by a faster increase up to different plateau temperatures detailed in Table 1.

X-ray Diffraction

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to preliminarily investigate the crystal structure and stoichiometry of all the samples both before and after the annealing procedure. A D2-Phaser instrument (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) with a Cu–Kα radiation source characterized by a wavelength of λ = 1.541 Å was employed, setting 2θ = 0.01° as the angular resolution and 0.4 s as the acquisition time.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy

XPS was performed on all the samples using a twin cathode TX400 X-ray source equipped with an Al cathode (1486.7 eV), available as an offline facility of the APE-HE beamline of the Elettra Synchrotron (Trieste, Italy).33 The emission current was 10 mA, and the source voltage was 11 kV. The distance between the anode and sample was kept at 40 mm. The measurements were carried out at 10–9 mbar pressure and a pass energy of 50 eV, using an electrostatic hemispherical analyzer with a mean radius of 200 mm.

For all the samples measured, both a survey scan (from 0 to 1400 eV) and elemental specific scans (cerium 3d and oxygen 1s bands) were performed. After each sampling run, a 4f Au spectrum was acquired in order to use it as a reference spectrum.

Operando X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy

The ambient pressure reactor cell34 present at the APE-HE beamline of Elettra Synchrotron in Trieste (Italy) was used to perform operando XAS on all the samples. Total electron yield XAS spectra were acquired by applying a bias of −100 V to the SiN membrane, located on the cap of the reactor, and acquiring the drain current from the sample.

Spectra were acquired in three different atmospheres in flux condition, namely neutral (1 bar He gas, concentration 100%), reducing (1 bar H2 gas, concentration 100%) and oxidizing (1 bar O2 gas, with 4% concentration in O2/He mixture) atmospheres, and temperatures ranging from room temperature to approximately 300 °C. The elemental edges explored were Ce M4,5, and spectra analysis was conducted normalizing to the M4 unit edge jump.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The morphology of the samples was examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (Fe-SEM), specifically the model INSPECT F from FEI. The images were captured utilizing a secondary electron (SE) detector, with operational parameters set at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV, a working distance of 10 mm, a spot size of 3, and a nominal resolution of 1.3 nm at 20 kV.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra of the samples were acquired at room temperature with a dispersive MicroRaman spectrometer (Invia, Renishaw) utilizing a monochromatic laser emitting at 514 nm. The laser power was set at 25 mW, and the samples were exposed to the laser beam with an accumulation time of 30 s.

Results and Discussion

Crystalline Structure and Surface Analysis: XRD, XPS, and SEM

A preliminary study of the crystal structure was performed, making use of XRD measurements on all the samples (GDC1000, GDC800, and GDC-as-grown). As expected, the as-grown sample is characterized by the highest amount of Ce3+ among all of the examined samples (24%) due to room-temperature deposition, which makes oxygen incorporation more difficult. The GDC800 sample is characterized by an intermediate amount of Ce3+ (17%), while GDC1000 is characterized by the lowest Ce3+ content (13%) due to the higher annealing temperature that favors oxygen incorporation.10 We observe here that the cerium valence percentage change between samples GDC800 and GDC1000 is small but significant, although no c-axis variation is observed from the XRD spectra (Figure 1a). However, the surface properties are extremely important in determining the rates of the relevant reactions in SOFCs, which occur at the interfaces.

Operando XAS Experiment

Samples GDC800, GDC1000, and GDC-as-grown were measured by means of operando XAS in order to investigate changes in the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio in oxidizing, reducing, and neutral atmospheres at 300 °C in order to have a better comprehension of the microscopical processes that could explain the different performance of the sputtered GDC barrier annealed at different temperatures (800 and 1000 °C) when inserted in the industrial SOFC matrix.23,31,32,37,45

Before starting the experiment, the samples were heated up to 300 °C in a He (neutral) atmosphere to remove surface contaminants (for example, adsorbed water) while monitoring the evolution of the Ce M4,5 and O K edges until the spectra reached a steady state.

We start by discussing the results obtained for sample GDC800, which are summarized in Figure 2.

Ce M5 edge evolution with time exposure in (a) hydrogen up to 75 min (dashed line is the spectrum acquired after 60 min of continuous exposure in 100% H2), (b) oxygen (up to 30 min, red line), and (c) helium (up to 220 min, red line). All of the spectra were acquired in isothermal conditions at 300 °C and were normalized to the M4 edge area.

The operando XAS experiment on GDC800 started by heating the sample to 300 °C in neutral atmosphere (He); once this temperature was reached, the next step consisted of exposing the sample to a reducing atmosphere (100% H2) for 15 min and then acquiring the resulting spectrum in 100% He. The same procedure was repeated for a total exposure time of 75 min, interrupting the H2 flux every 15 min to acquire the spectra. The spectra obtained via this procedure are shown in Figure 2a, where the features associated with the different valence states of Ce are clearly indicated with light blue arrows; in particular, the features at ca. 881 and 883 eV are associated with Ce3+, while the feature visible at ca. 884 is commonly associated with Ce4+. A clear trend is visible with increasing H2 atmosphere exposure time. In particular, as expected, we observe a significant Ce4+ reduction to the Ce3+ valence state with increasing exposure time. The dashed green spectrum in Figure 2a, on the other hand, refers to the spectrum acquired in He after continuous H2 atmosphere exposure without any interruption: the Ce3+ content is, in this case, higher than that observed at the end of the intermittent 75 min exposure, indicating that exposing the sample to neutral atmosphere can alter the sample surface oxidation state, reducing the final Ce3+ amount in the intermittent case. Therefore, we exposed the sample to a neutral atmosphere (100% He) in order to observe the changes induced in the XAS spectrum. In Figure 2c, the result of the experiment is shown: starting from the light green spectrum in Figure 2a, in which the sample GDC800 resulted in being highly reduced, we observe a decrease in the Ce3+ features with a simultaneous increase in the Ce4+ feature, with the final spectrum (red line, acquired after ca. 220 min) being almost completely oxidized with Ce3+ minimized.

After a re-reduction of the GDC thin film, obtained by exposing it for 60 min in a pure hydrogen atmosphere, the last step of the experiment consisted of exposing the sample to an oxidizing atmosphere (2 sccm of O2 diluted in 55 sccm of He) for a total time of about 33 min. Results of this last step are shown in Figure 2b. Here we observe that oxidizing atmosphere exposure of the GDC800 sample results, as in a neutral atmosphere, in Ce oxidation with the last spectrum (red line) characterized by Ce3+ features almost disappearing. We point out that, while in a neutral atmosphere, complete oxidation occurred in almost 4 h (ca. 220 min), here the complete oxidation was faster and occurred in about 30 min.

A similar operando XAS experiment was performed on sample GDC1000 in H2 and O2 atmospheres.

Sample GDC1000 was first heated to 300 °C in 100% He atmosphere and then exposed to a reducing atmosphere, following the same procedure as that used for GDC800 (15 min in 100% H2, followed by the acquisition of spectra in He). The total time of H2 exposure was 75 min, as for GDC800. Looking at Figure 3a, we can notice that there is an almost negligible evolution of the Ce3+ edge with H2 exposure time compared to the previously analyzed sample. Indeed, here we can observe just a hinted change in the shape of the Ce3+ feature at about 881 eV.

Ce M5 edge exposure time evolution of sample GDC1000 in (a) reducing atmosphere (H2) and (b) oxidizing atmosphere (O2). All of the spectra were normalized to the M4 area.

Starting from last spectrum acquired in pure hydrogen, namely the yellow line in Figure 3b, sample GDC1000 was exposed to an oxidizing atmosphere (2 sccm of O2 diluted in 55 sccm of He) for a total amount of 30 min, with the spectrum reaching again the initial shape with Ce3+ almost disappearing.

The measurements of the GDC-as-grown sample are reported in the Supporting Information, and the results reveal nonreversible dynamics of the Ce M5 edge in He and O2/He atmospheres. The measurements after heat cleaning at 300 °C in a He atmosphere show a strong Ce3+ component, as expected for an oxygen deficient sample. Once exposed to an oxidizing atmosphere, the Ce3+ concentration drops and stays the same when the sample is exposed again to He for an extended period of time (∼80 min). This behavior indicates that the as-grown sample structure is not stable even at temperatures as low as 300 °C and that oxidizing atmosphere exposure acts as a “mild annealing”, changing the structure and composition of the sample for good.

In Figure 4a,b, the estimated quantitative amount of Ce4+ and Ce3+ in samples GDC800 and GDC1000, respectively, are reported as a function of time exposure in pure H2 and O2. This quantitative comparison allows us to obtain clearer evidence of the higher ionic mobility inside the GDC800 lattice, as Ce4+ ranges from 100% to ∼73%, while Ce3+ ranges from 0% to 27% when compared to GDC1000, which is characterized by an almost constant Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio in both environments. Moreover, the same fit analysis performed on a Ce M5 spectrum acquired after 60 min of continuous H2 exposure (dashed violet line in Figure 3b), gave the following results: Ce4+ amount decreases to ∼73%, while Ce3+ increases to ∼27% (highest Ce3+ amount observed during the whole experiment in sample GDC800), giving proof of the availability and reversibility of the oxygen hopping sites induced by the post growth annealing temperature in sample GDC800. Indeed, the enhanced kinetics observed in this sample relies not only on the higher ionic mobility (see Figure 4a,b) but also on its higher capability to adsorb oxygen (in the presence of an oxidizing environment) and more easily dissociate these oxygen bonds and release electrons when the nanostructured GDC layer is exposed to reducing environments. This observation can give hints on the microscopic mechanism responsible for the better performance of sputtered GDC 800 °C annealed in SOFCs, in particular for what concerns the further lowering of the ohmic resistance.23

It is also interesting to observe that the initial spectrum fitting of GDC800 in H2 gives a very high average oxidation state for Ce (estimated Ce4+ amount is 99.2%, while Ce3+ is only 0.8%). The discrepancy with the same analysis performed by XPS spectra before the XAS experiment can be ascribed to a concurrence of the surface cleaning effect given by heating in a neutral atmosphere, the ionic mobility from inner layers (the probing depth of XPS is only a few nanometers, while that of XAS is tens of nanometers), and the “estimative” nature of the fitting procedure applied to absorption spectra.

Raman Spectra Analysis

To gain a better understanding of the XPS and operando XAS measurements, Raman spectra of GDC800 and GDC1000 were acquired both before and after the absorption spectroscopy measurements, together with the spectrum of the industrial substrate. In Figure 5, Raman spectra of all the analyzed samples are displayed for Raman shifts ranging from ∼200 to ∼900 cm–1.

Raman spectra acquired for samples GDC800 and GDC1000 before the XAS experiment (top panel, blue and red lines, respectively) and after the XAS experiment (bottom panel, green and burgundy lines, respectively). All data are normalized at the F2g band intensity. In the insets, details of the F2g vibrational bands are reported.

Following the nomenclature used by Rupp et al. in ref (10), we call F2g the band visible at ∼465 ± 1 cm–1, α the band between 550 and 680 cm–1, and β the band visible at around 250 cm–1.

The F2g band is commonly associated with first-order scattering of a triply degenerate Raman-active phonon of the CeO2 cubic fluorite phase (symmetric breathing mode of the oxygen ions around each cation),10,11,46 while the α and β bands are associated with second-order phonon scattering on oxygen vacancies.10,47

The insets in Figure 5 highlight the presence of a shift of the F2g peak among all the analyzed samples. Focusing on the before XAS samples (top panel), while the F2g mode of the GDC1000 is at 464 ± 1 cm–1 (close to the value of the full oxidated ceria F2g peak in the literature),48 the main peak of GDC800 before the XAS experiment is found at a lower value, namely 463 cm–1. A lowering of the F2g peak in ceria compounds is usually associated with a change in the lattice parameter and/or a cerium reduction from Ce4+ to Ce3+.10,11,48 Since XRD does not show any change in the lattice parameter induced by different annealing temperatures, this first result can be mainly attributed to the different amount of reduced Ce in the two samples, in agreement with the results found during the preliminary XPS analysis.

On the other hand, looking at the samples after the XAS experiment (bottom panel of Figure 5; for both samples, the last experimental step was an oxidizing one), we notice that both samples are characterized by a lowering of the F2g peak wavenumber position and that, while the F2g peak of GDC800 lowers to 461 cm–1, the F2g peak of GDC1000 reaches 463 cm–1.

Since lower Raman shift values in the F2g peak are also associated with a lowering of the anion–cation bond strength and the fact that this depends upon the lowering of the annealing temperature (once the doping concentration is fixed),10 we can give a first explanation of the oxygen ion mobility in GDC800 since this is characterized by the lowest peak position and, thus, the lowest anion–cation bond strength.

We now focus on the α peak to obtain information on the oxygen vacancy content. In Figure 6, data acquired for Raman shifts ranging from 520 to 680 cm–1 are reported for both GDC800 and GDC1000 before and after the XAS experiment, together with the Raman spectrum of the industrial NiO/YSZ bilayer substrate.

Curves are vertically shifted for clarity.

In accordance with the literature, in the GDC thin film Raman spectra, it is possible to individuate three main bands10,46,49 (see Figure 6a,b) associated with three different kinds of defects; indeed, if we compare the spectra of the GDC samples with the substrate’s spectrum, both before and after the XAS measurements, the sputtered GDC thin film on the industrial YSZ substrate leads to the formation of three different peaks in which the substrate main peak is convoluted. In refs (46) and (49), the origin of these three peaks in doped ceria is extensively discussed. In particular, it is inferred that the second-order peak α is a disorder band related to phonon scattering on oxygen vacancies.

In more detail, the band visible at 585–620 cm–1, indicated with D1, refers to Frenkel anion pairs, in which an oxygen moves in an octahedral interstitial position, generating a vacancy46,49,50 and giving rise to the following complexes: (2Gd′Ce:VO••)X or (Gd′Ce:VO••)•. On the other side, the D2 band (630–660 cm–1) is ascribed to cation doping of pure CeO2, generating “extrinsic” defects that do not incorporate any oxygen vacancy, such as the formation of Gd cations 8-fold coordinated with oxygen ions. Furthermore, at lower Raman shifts (530–575 cm–1), we observe the formation of a third band, typically found in doped cerias,46,49 that can be associated with the presence of oxygen vacancies coupled with Ce3+. Looking at Figures 5 and 6 and Table 4, it is clear that the samples GDC800 and GDC1000 before the XAS experiment are very similar, with no big changes in the characteristic features. Indeed, apart from the already mentioned F2g peak shift, the other features appear to be nearly superposed, as confirmed by the ratios reported in Table 4. Little changes can be detected in D2 and D3 with sample GDC1000, characterized by a slightly higher amount of GdO8 complexes and slightly fewer oxygen vacancies. This is in accordance with the XRD data shown in Figure 1a, which does not allow us to appreciate any difference in the structure of the two samples, and also with the XPS spectra that once again are characterized by just little changes in intensity of the peaks associated with Ce3+ or adsorbed species.

The Raman spectra obtained with the same samples after the XAS experiment in the reactor cell, in which they were subsequently exposed to both reducing and oxidizing atmospheres, show, on the other side, appreciable differences between the two samples: indeed, while sample GDC1000 shows a partial lowering of the features associated with Frenkel defects (D1 band) and “extrinsic” defects (D2) and the band associated with oxygen vacancies associated with trivalent Ce ions remains unchanged, sample GDC800 shows, on the contrary, a remarkable change in the same features, with D1 and D2 bands that disappear and a slight increase in the amount of oxygen vacancies associated with the D3 band.

This is also confirmed by the β peak (associated with CeO2 vacancies)47 evolution reported in Table 4 and Figure 5. While the initial intensities are almost comparable in both samples before the XAS experiment, after the experiment it is evident that the number of oxygen vacancies available for ion transport through the lattice remains higher in sample GDC800, while it is ∼20% lower in GDC1000.

A tentative explanation of the evolution of α and β peaks can be the following: the D2 band initially observed in both samples disappears after the operando XAS experiment only in GDC800, while it only lowers in GDC1000. As this peak is associated with Gd defects, we can infer that a higher annealing temperature makes the oxygen–gadolinium bonds stronger, preventing the mobility of these oxygen ions. On the other side, a lowering of the annealing temperature creates weaker bonds in the extrinsic defects more prone to breaking during the experiment, allowing oxygen migration inside the GDC lattice and permitting the further creation and mobility of oxygen vacancies inside the GDC800 thin film during the XAS experiment.

Conclusions

In this work, we investigated the reactivity and ionic mobility in room-temperature RF-sputtered GDC thin films annealed at different temperatures (800 and 1000 °C) grown on industrial substrates. Structural measurements do not evidence any changes in the lattice parameters induced by the change in the annealing plateau temperature, while XPS measurements evidence differences in the Ce3+ and Ce4+ content on the sample surface. Exposing the samples at different atmospheres (neutral, reducing, and oxidizing) and monitoring the changes in the samples’ surfaces by means of operando XAS spectra acquisition at the Ce M4,5 edge, it was possible to point out a different evolution of the Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio as a function of the annealing temperature. The sample annealed at 800 °C is indeed characterized by large exchanges of Ce valence between 4+ and 3+ valence as a function of the gas exposure in both reducing and oxidizing atmospheres. Moreover, when the reduced sample is exposed to a neutral atmosphere (Figure 2c), the Ce4+ concentration slowly increases back to the initial value, indicating that oxygen ions migrate from the bulk of the sample toward the reduced surface as a consequence of the good ionic conductivity. On the other hand, the thin film annealed at 1000 °C is characterized by a poor valence dynamic when exposed to different atmospheres, resulting in being almost stuck at the initial stage.

Raman spectra measurements performed on both samples before and after the operando XAS experiment hinted that the different observed responses to gas atmospheres with different Ce3+ and Ce4+ evolutions measured on the investigated samples can be ascribed to a different Gd–O bond strength inside the lattice: lowering the annealing temperature to 800 °C is indeed responsible for a lowering of the cation–anion bond strength, together with the formation of extrinsic defects with a lower bond strength that can be more easily broken during gas exposure at 300 °C. On the other side, an annealing temperature of 1000 °C increases the bond strength of all the defects in the α band, conferring a “frozen” character to the structure of the sample and resulting in a less responsive sensitivity to gas exposure when compared to the 800 °C annealed sample.

It is worth noting that the operando measurements were obtained at a much lower temperature (300 °C) than what is typically used during intermediate temperature SOFC operation (700–800 °C); however, the 800 °C annealed sample demonstrates good reactivity and ionic conductivity. This observation is in agreement with previously reported23,32 current–voltage characteristics and electrochemical impedance measurements showing that SOFCs embedding 800 °C annealed GDC layers better perform at the lowest operation temperature (600 °C) compared to 1000 °C annealed ones.

Our methodology allows one to identify the signatures of efficient ionic conductivity and catalytic properties in GDC layers and can be extended to other elements and materials used in SOFCs, with the aim of guiding the optimization of SOFCs toward operation in the intermediate temperature range6,13,51 (500–700 °C), with important consequences on the durability, cost, and diffusion of these electrochemical devices.
