Cementless one-stage hip revision arthroplasty with an injectable antibiotic bone graft substitute
Authors: Thilo Khakzad, Sebastian Meller, Sebastian Hardt, Vincent J Leopold, Lukas Mödl, Carsten Perka, Michael Mueller, Tobias Winkler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe classic, widely accepted approach for one-stage hip revision arthroplasty in patients with periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is the cemented exchange. This approach provides stable implant anchoring despite bone defects after removal of infected components, and facilitates local antibiotic delivery. This study aims to investigate the efficacy of cementless one-stage hip revision arthroplasty using a gentamicin-eluting bone graft substitute (GBGS) to address both bone defect filling and antibiotic elution.MethodsWe conducted a prospective analysis of 20 patients with confirmed PJI undergoing cementless one-stage hip revision arthroplasty using GBGS. The GBGS was used to fill femoral and acetabular defects during implantation. Clinical outcomes, radiographs, adverse events, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) including Harris Hip Score (HHS) and EuroQol five-dimension five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) were assessed. Patients received 12 weeks of systemic antibiotics and were followed up for at least 24 months.ResultsThe mean age of the cohort was 66.3 years (SD 8.4; 46 to 80), with ten female and ten male patients. On average, 13.2 ml (SD 3.9; 5 to 17) of GBGS was applied to bone defects. No reinfections occurred during the follow-up period of 3.3 years (SD 0.92; 2.1 to 4.8). We observed 15 serious adverse events (SAEs), none of which were associated with the product. All cases showed good bony consolidation and prosthesis integration at 12 months. Significant improvements were seen in HHS (preoperative mean: 47.7; final visit mean: 80.1; p < 0.001) and EQ-5D-5L score (preoperative mean: 0.43; 12-month mean: 0.88; p < 0.001).ConclusionThis prospective pilot study is the first to demonstrate the safety and feasibility of single-stage cementless hip exchange arthroplasty using GBGS in managing PJI and associated bone defects. The technique resulted in significant improvements in functional outcomes and quality of life, with a good safety profile. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(9):1146–1155.Keywords: Cementless hip arthroplasty, Periprosthetic joint infection, Hip revision, revision arthroplasty, antibiotics, bone graft, hip, Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), bone defects, Gentamicin, functional outcome scores, EQ-5D-5L, Harris Hip Score

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a serious complication following total hip arthroplasty (THA), accounting for about 15% of revision surgeries, with an incidence of around 1% of all hip arthroplasties.1-3 The increasing global prevalence of THA highlights the challenge that PJI presents internationally.

The optimal therapeutic strategy is controversial, depending on infection duration, microbial spectrum, and patient factors. Prosthesis-preserving therapies, like debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR), are reserved for acute infections.4 For chronic PJI, two-stage revision is the most established method, involving a temporary spacer or resection arthroplasty, limiting patient mobility during treatment.5

Single-stage revision is gaining popularity in case of absence of ‘difficult-to-treat’ pathogens,6 as it avoids the prosthesis-free interval, allowing quicker return to mobility and reducing risks of contractures, immobilization, and muscle atrophy.7 However, effective local antibiotic therapy and addressing osteolysis and bone defects are critical, making the cemented procedure the gold standard. Here, non-resorbable polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) is used to fix implants, deliver high antibiotic concentrations, and fill bony defects.8 Nevertheless, advancements in biological fixation, cementless antibiotic carriers, and implant designs have led to an increased use of cementless components in revision THA, including single-stage hip revision arthroplasty.9,10

The use of resorbable, antibiotic-eluting bone graft substitutes could address this challenge by allowing bone regrowth and potentially benefiting subsequent revisions. Resorbable antibiotic-loaded bone substitutes are already routinely employed in osteomyelitis therapy, enabling a single-stage approach in many scenarios where a two-stage approach would have traditionally been deemed necessary.11-13 Since PJI involves osteomyelitis therapy, these substitutes might be valuable in single-stage revision surgery.

In this prospective case series, we aimed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of a new methodology of cementless single-stage revision in hip PJI patients using a gentamicin-eluting bone graft substitute (GBGS) for filling bony defects. The primary outcome of the study was the percentage of patients classified as infection-free at a defined timepoint of two years, based on Delphi criteria.14

Methods

Ethical approval was obtained from the local Ethics Committee (EA2/102/18) and the study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00024071). Data were prospectively collected and pseudonymized using an electronic case report form.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: patients aged ≥ 18 years; confirmed PJI of the hip (European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS) 2018 or International Consensus Meeting (ICM) 2018);15,16 scheduled single-stage revision arthroplasty by treating physician; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade ≤ III17 and informed consent for surgery and study participation.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were:

1) Fistula (sinus tract), purulence around the prosthesis, and/or severe bone and soft-tissue infection precluding sufficient debridement; excluded based on clinical assessment by two experienced orthopaedic surgeons (SH, TW), as a two-stage procedure was recommended.

2) Evidence of difficult-to-treat bacteria, defined as the growth of microorganism(s) resistant to all available biofilm-active antibiotics, as well as the presence of fungi.18

3) Drug or alcohol abuse in the last 12 months.

4) Moderate to severe dementia or serious psychiatric disorder.

5) Treatment for malignant disease.

6) Prior PMMA spacer placement (two-stage procedure).

7) Cerament G (Bonesupport, Sweden) is contraindicated as per instructions for use (hypersensitivity to aminoglycoside antibiotics, myasthenia gravis, severe renal dysfunction, pre-existing calcium metabolism disorders, pregnancy, breastfeeding).19

8) Gentamicin allergies.

During the study recruitment period, a total of 106 patients underwent revision surgery at our institution. Due to the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, 82 patients were excluded, as they required a two-stage procedure based on standard indications. Only four patients who met the study criteria declined participation, leaving 20 patients for the final analysis (Figure 1).

Study execution and outcome measures

Patients completed ten visits over a minimum of 24 months: screening, five visits during hospital stay (day (D) of operation (D0), D1, D3, D5, D7, discharge day), and three outpatient visits (week (W)6, W26, W52). At least 24 months after enrolment, patients were contacted again to confirm they remained free of infection according to the Delphi criteria.14

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of the study was to evaluate whether this new method achieves infection-free status in the treated study participants. The Delphi criteria were used to define infection-free status. These criteria include the following success parameters: absence of infection, healed and unremarkable wound/scar conditions, no recurrence by the same organism, no subsequent surgical intervention due to infection, and no PJI-related mortality, within a minimum follow-up period of two years.

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcome was to evaluate the safety of the procedure through a structured safety analysis. All serious adverse events (SAEs) and adverse events (AEs) were systematically recorded, categorized, and documented. Regular clinical examinations, laboratory assessments, and imaging were performed to ensure comprehensive detection of complications. Additionally, functional outcome scores, including the Harris Hip Score (HHS)20 and the EuroQol-five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D),21,22 were collected to assess the participants’ physical recovery and overall treatment impact.

Patient characteristics

A total of 20 patients were included, with ten females and ten males. The mean age was 66.3 years (SD 8.4; 46 to 80). The mean BMI was 26.2 kg/m2 (SD 4.2; 18.4 to 37.0), and the mean ASA grade was 2.1 (SD 0.7; 1 to 3). The patient cohort had mean CRP levels of 12.1 mg/l (SD 24.1; 0 to 102.5) at the screening visit, decreasing to 2.3 (SD 2.8; 0.3 to 11.3) at the 12-month follow-up. The demographic characteristics of each patient are listed in Table I. The leucocyte count was 7.2/nl (SD 1.2; 4.9 to 10.9) at inclusion and 7.1/ nl (SD 1.8; 5.1 to 11.5) at the 12-month follow-up. Preoperative microbial detection was successful in 15 out of 20 patients (75%), with nine patients showing monobacterial and six polybacterial findings. None of the detected organisms were resistant to gentamicin or belonged to the ‘difficult to treat’ organism group.

Cerament G

Cerament G (BoneSupport, Sweden) is a resorbable, injectable bone graft substitute composed of 60% calcium sulphate and 40% hydroxyapatite, impregnated with gentamicin sulphate (17.5 mg per 1 ml of Cerament G). It serves both as a structural bone defect filler and a local antibiotic carrier, releasing high local concentrations of gentamicin while minimizing systemic exposure.

Surgical procedure

Three experienced orthopaedic surgeons (SH, SM, MM) performed the surgeries on 20 patients. After prosthetic component removal, thorough debridement, and irrigation with pulsatile saline and antiseptic solution containing polyhexanide were performed to reduce the bacterial load. Bone defects were injected with GBGS (Cerament G), classified by the Paprosky classification of bone defects.23,24 Acetabular reconstruction involved injecting GBGS followed by immediate placement of a cementless component. Femoral defects were addressed with GBGS after component insertion or by additional component augmentation. Implantation followed standard procedures, and wound closure was performed as per institutional standards.

Antimicrobial therapy

Post-surgery, all patients received intravenous biofilm-active antibiotics as empirical therapy to target sessile bacteria embedded in biofilms, which are highly resistant to conventional antibiotics. Biofilm-active agents were chosen to enhance bacterial eradication and reduce the risk of persistent infection. After obtaining culture results, antimicrobial therapy was individualized based on pathogen susceptibility, initially administered intravenously during the hospital stay and then continued orally after discharge for a total duration of 12 weeks (Table II).

Radiological assessment

Postoperative radiographs were evaluated at 12 months by three independent orthopaedic surgeons (TK, CP, TW) (blinded to surgical details). Implant position was assessed based on standard alignment parameters (femoral component alignment, cup inclination/anteversion). Loosening was defined as progressive radiolucency > 2 mm, migration, or subsidence. Osteolysis was recorded if radiologically visible to the evaluating surgeons. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS v. 25 (IBM, USA). For documentation of the collected data, Excel (Microsoft,USA) was used. Improvements in HHS and EQ-5D scores were assessed by comparing preoperative values with those at the follow-up, using a non-parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA) with an LD-F1 design for longitudinal data. All calculations were performed in R Studio v. 4.3.3 (R Studio, USA), with a p-value < 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

Results

The average operating time was 151 mins (SD 23.33; 117 to 208). Among the 20 participants, 13 received a Zweymüller-type cementless femoral component (Smith & Nephew Orthopaedics, Switzerland), while six required a cementless revision femoral component (Smith & Nephew Orthopaedics), and one patient received a modular revision femoral component (Zimmer Biomet, Switzerland). The Zweymüller-type femoral component features a quadrangular, tapered design, allowing metaphyseal press-fit fixation with additional diaphyseal support, making it particularly suitable for cases with compromised proximal bone stock. All patients underwent acetabular revision with a cementless press-fit component. In 16 cases, screw fixation was used, and in three cases, additional acetabular augments were implanted. The mean volume of GBGS applied to bone defects was 13.2 ml (SD 3.9 5 to 17) (Table III).

Regarding the primary outcome, all 20 patients were infection-free after a mean follow-up of 3.3 years (SD 0.92; 1.3 to 4.8), according to Delphi consensus criteria. One patient, who was infection-free at the 12-month visit, had passed away after 1.3 years due to progression of a malignant condition.

Overall, we observed 15 SAEs. All AEs and SAEs were thoroughly evaluated by the multidisciplinary team. Only two SAEs pertained to the operated hip joint: one early cup dislocation and one haematoma evacuation. The other SAEs were general illnesses or injuries leading to hospitalization (Table IV). None of the SAEs were associated with the investigated product. The early cup dislocation was attributed to a surgical error with a newly introduced cup design. Additionally, 54 adverse events (AEs) were recorded, mainly expected and inconsequential changes in blood counts, as well as musculoskeletal and systemic comorbidities.

Discussion

This pilot study introduces a novel one-stage exchange strategy for managing chronic hip PJI. We demonstrated that using GBGS is a safe procedure, addressing both infection eradication and reconstruction of bone defects during single-stage revision. Utilizing the globally recognized Delphi criteria we observed no reinfections during our observation period. Although our cohort of 20 patients with a mean follow-up of 3.3 years is small, we consider these results promising. While the two-stage procedure remains the most frequently applied therapy for PJI, evidence is mounting that a one-stage approach is favourable in a selected group of patients.9,10,25,26 Assuming its non-inferiority compared to two-stage procedures, the benefits are reduced treatment morbidity, improved functional outcomes, and economic advantages.27-29

Heterogeneous patient populations and varying definitions of a successfully treated infection make direct comparisons challenging. However, meta-analyses, such as Lange et al,30 reported reinfection rates of 13.1% for single-stage revisions of the hip. Recent meta-analyses indicate a positive trend over time, with reinfection rates decreasing. Goud et al31 compared 14 studies on single-stage hip revisions, revealing a pooled reinfection rate of 5.7%. In contrast, recent registry studies from Germany indicate that the risk of revision after septic hip revision is significantly higher than previously thought, with rates up to 30%.32

Limited research exists on one-stage revision without cement in hip joint PJI. For many years, cemented single-stage septic exchange was considered the gold standard.33 However, recent publications show promising success rates for cementless one-stage revision. In 2014, Zeller et al34 published a cohort study involving 157 patients, with 45% undergoing cementless procedures, and a five-year reinfection rate of 5%. Winkler et al29 reported a 92% success rate using antibiotic impregnated allograft bone. Lange et al9 published similar results in 2018, showing a 91% infection control in chronic PJI using the Cementless One-stage Revision of Infected Hip Arthroplasty protocol (CORIHA), even with fistulation and/or preoperative unknown microorganisms. In this protocol, a fleece was used as an antibiotic (gentamicin) carrier. While reinfection rates for single-stage revisions vary, recent findings suggest that even in cases of unexpected positive cultures, appropriate antibiotic therapy can lead to excellent infection-free survival.35

Ilchmann et al27 reported 100% infection freedom following single-stage hip revision for PJI over a median observation period of 6.6 years, using the guidelines of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA).36 According to these, a large bone defect would exclude single-stage arthroplasty. However, GBGS can directly address bone defects. This method could be particularly advantageous for revisions of cemented prostheses, as these revisions often entail larger bone defects.37,38 From our perspective, in addition to defect reconstruction, dead space management is also of significant importance, especially in femoral revision. The cross-sectional diameter of the femoral component often changes, creating a dead space that can be filled with the bone void filler, GBGS, instead of cement. A recent study also demonstrates the sufficiency of gentamicin as a local antibiotic, without the need for other antibiotics such as vancomycin.39

Procedural safety is paramount. Septic revision surgeries have a high complication rate. Boniello et al40 demonstrated that 10% of patients experience major complications following septic revision THA within 30 days, with 47.7% requiring transfusion and 7% suffering from sepsis. We recorded 15 SAEs, of which two affected the operated hip joint: one early cup dislocation and one haematoma evacuation. The other SAEs included distal radius fractures, deep vein thrombosis, or other hospitalizations (Table III). One patient experienced acetabular component loosening on the fourth postoperative day due to a newly introduced implant, not attributed to GBGS use. No procedural complications were observed in other patients.

This study has several limitations. The cohort of 20 participants was small and we did not have a control group compared to results from the literature. Furthermore, we followed our patients only for two years for infection control. Of course, several other factors also influence the results, such as the type and severity of infections and patient-specific factors like age, comorbidities, and adherence to postoperative care instructions. These variables may contribute to differences in outcomes and limit the generalizability of our findings. One limitation is that bone reconstitution was assessed using 12-month postoperative radiographs, which showed vital bone around the implants, but as summation images, they do not allow definitive evaluation.

However, this pilot study aims to pave the way for further, larger studies on this innovative surgical technique for managing chronic hip PJI, thus we consider the cohort suitable for the initial procedure assessment.

In conclusion, this pilot study introduces a novel one-stage therapeutic strategy for managing chronic hip PJI. Our data indicate that the use of GBGS is a safe procedure addressing both infection eradication and cementless reconstruction of bone defects.

Take home message

- This study highlights the potential of a one-stage exchange strategy using gentamicin-eluting bone graft substitute (GBGS) for the treatment of chronic hip periprosthetic joint infections.

- The approach combines reliable infection control with cementless bone defect reconstruction, thereby reducing morbidity and preserving bone stock.

- These findings support further clinical evaluation of GBGS as a valuable option in selected patients.
