Medullary breast cancer and germline BRCA1 mutations: a possible criterion for genetic testing
Authors: Adriana I Apostol, David Lim, Steven A Narod
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedullary breast cancer is a rare subtype of invasive breast cancer, representing from 0.2% to 6% of all breast carcinomas, with a higher proportion among women with triple-negative breast cancer and among those with a BRCA1 mutation. This review article aims to investigate the frequency of medullary breast cancer among all breast cancers and to assess its association with BRCA1 mutations. We surveyed studies involving patients diagnosed with breast cancer that report both the histology of the breast cancer as well as the presence of BRCA1 mutations. Among women with medullary breast cancer, the proportion of cases that carry a BRCA1 mutation ranges from 3% up to 35.3%, depending on the study. Among BRCA1-mutated breast cancers, the proportion that are medullary ranges from 8 to 20%. Given the notable association between medullary breast cancer and BRCA1 mutations, we propose to consider medullary breast cancer as a criterion for genetic testing in order to improve the identification of a larger number of carriers, thereby enhancing screening and prevention strategies.Keywords: BRCA mutation, Medullary breast cancer, Genetic testing

Background

Medullary breast carcinoma (MBC) is a rare histological form of invasive breast cancer, characterized by poorly differentiated cells arranged in large sheets with a mononuclear stromal infiltrate with the absence of an intraductal component or areas of microglandular differentiation [1]. Medullary tumors typically display a well-circumscribed border and expansive margins [2]. A diagnosis of atypical medullary carcinoma is considered when less than 25% of the tumor contains classical medullary features, when the lymphoid infiltrate is moderate, or when the circumscription is incomplete [3]. MBC is typically triple-negative breast cancer, in contrast with invasive ductal carcinoma (56.0% vs. 13.1%) [4, 5]. In the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Breast Tumors (2018), MBC has been reclassified within the category of invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), with the designation of IBC-NST with a medullary pattern or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)-rich IBC-NST [6].

Despite its cytological features and its triple-negative phenotype, MBC patients have a more favorable prognosis than patients with invasive ductal carcinoma [7]. Although MBC represents only a small percentage of all breast carcinomas, it is a clinically significant subclass, in part due to its contribution to hereditary cancers, in particular its association with BRCA1 pathogenic variants/mutations (‘mutation hereinafter’). The aim of this review is to investigate MBC as a predictor of BRCA1 mutation status. Specifically, we aim to assess what proportion of BRCA1-associated breast cancers are of medullary histology and what proportion of MBC cases carry a BRCA1 mutation.

In the current narrative review, we have searched the PubMed database for paper using the terms medullary breast cancer and BRCA1. From these we have selected the largest and most informative studies and have not included studies with small numbers of patients or poorly defined histology.

What proportion of breast cancers are medullary?

In a review of the literature, the proportion of breast cancers that are MBC varies significantly according to the population examined, and the age of diagnosis, immuno-chemical marker status, and family history, ranging from fewer than 1% to 6% of all breast carcinomas, with a higher percentage in women with triple-negative breast cancer.

In the study by Millis et al., among 729,920 patients from the USA, MBC was identified in 1,509 cases and accounted for only 0.2% of the total. Notably, 60.6% of the MBC cases were triple-negative (p < 0.001) [8]. Chen et al., reported a similar proportion of 0.3% among 179,613 breast cancer patients from the SEER database [7]. In a study by Zhang et al., of 4,748 breast cancer patients, 0.4% were diagnosed with MBC [9]. However, within the triple-negative subgroup (12.3% of the total cohort), the proportion was 3.4%. A similar percentage was observed in an Italian population of triple-negative patients, where MBC accounted for 4.1% of all cases [10].

The prevalence of this histological subtype appears to be higher in certain geographical regions such as Poland and Hungary, where it reaches approximately 5% of all breast tumors. Notably, in the study by Lubinski et al., conducted on 3,472 Polish patients, 5% were affected with MBC [2]. A similar finding was reported in Hungary, with a proportion of MBC of 4.6% [11]. In a multicenter international setting involving 3,260 patients from many countries, a prevalence of MBC was 2.8% was observed [12]. To some extent the large variation observed may also reflect variations in the pathologist’s interpretation of the specimen(s) reviewed.

What proportion of breast cancers among BRCA1 carriers are medullary

The relationship between medullary breast cancer and BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations have been evaluated in 11 studies (Table 1) [2, 11–20]. Marcus et al. studied 99 BRCA-related breast cancer cases with known histology. 20.0% of breast cancers in BRCA1 carriers were medullary, compared to 4.8% in a comparison series (p < 0.0001) [13].

Fountzilas et al. analyzed a series of 385 BRCA-mutated breast cancer cases. Among those with known histology, 24 cases were medullary (10.3%). All were associated with BRCA1 mutations, with no case linked to BRCA2 [19].

Verhoog et al. compared 196 sporadic breast cancer cases with 49 BRCA1-mutated breast cancers. Among the 49 BRCA1 positive cases, 4 were medullary (8.2%). No statistically significant association with BRCA1 and medullary cancer was observed (p = 0.23), but this study was small [14].

Mavaddat et al. studied a large a cohort of BRCA mutation carriers diagnosed with invasive breast cancer (2,993 BRCA1 and 1,824 BRCA2) with known histological data. MBC was diagnosed in 9.4% of 281 BRCA1-related cancers and in 2.2% of 40 BRCA2-related cancers [18].

A more recent study by Mavaddat et al. (2022) involved 42,680 breast cancer cases with known germline mutational status, including CHEK2, BARD1 and RAD51C (Table 1). MBC was identified in 5.2% of 523 BRCA1 carriers and in only 1.1% of 717 BRCA2 carriers (1.1%). The frequency of MBC in BRCA1-mutated breast cancers was considerably higher than that observed in non-carriers (0.7% of 40,108). Notably, MBC was also detected in 4.7% of 43 RAD51C and 3.6% of 56 BARD1 mutation carriers [20].

BARD1 mutations confer a 17–30% absolute risk of breast cancer [21] and may also have prognostic implications. High BARD1 expression has been associated with shorter recurrence-free and distant metastasis-free survival, suggesting poorer outcomes [22]. Its potential association with MBC has been explored. Suszynska et al. analyzed approximately 14,000 Polish and Belarusian breast cancer patients, identifying the pathogenic p.Q564X variant in only two MBC cases (0.7%), while the variants of unknown significance, p.R658C and p.R659R, were found in three additional MBC cases [23]. These limited findings may reflect the small number of pathogenic variants assessed.

What proportion of medullary breast cancer carry a BRCA1 mutations

This question is important when considering who to offer genetic testing. A higher than expected frequency of BRCA1 mutations has been seen among MBC patients compared to other histological subtypes. However, the magnitude of this association has not been precisely established, mainly due to the small sample sizes in various studies and the rarity of this tumor subtype. Also in many studies, over-representation of triple-negative patients in the sample studied predicts a higher than average BRCA1 mutation rate.

It is of clinical interest to estimate what proportion of invasive medullary cancers carry a mutation in BRCA1 (or another gene) as this will help to decide whether medullary histology should be a criterion for genetic testing, regardless of other features (such as triple-negative status). In some centers, genetic testing is offered to all breast cancer cases, but more often testing is restricted to women with a prior probability of 5% of more of carrying a mutation, based on e.g., age of diagnosis, Jewish ancestry, triple negative status, family history or bilaterality.

In a study by Van der Looij et al. two of 23 MBC cases had a BRCA1 mutation (8.7%) and one had a BRCA2 mutation [11]. Both BRCA1-positive patients carried the 300 T > G mutation, one of the common mutations in Hungarian patients [24].

Iau et al. studied 42 patients with (typical and atypical) MBC cases and three had a BRCA1 mutation (7.1%). All three mutations were detected in patients with a family history of breast or ovarian cancer [16]. In a Polish population-based study, Lubiński et al. analyzed 3,472 breast cancer cases, including 173 MBC [2]. All patients were tested for the three BRCA1 founder mutations (5382insC, C61G, and 4153delC), which represent 90% of BRCA1 mutations in Polish families. The study found that 35.3% of the 173 MBC cases were BRCA1-positive. Mateju et al. confirmed this association in a study of 679 unselected breast cancer cases. Of the 19 cases of MBC (3.3% of all breast cancers with known histology), four (21.1%) were BRCA1-positive, whereas no association was observed with BRCA2 [17].

Three large studies were published in 2012 [12, 18, 20]. These were multicentric international studies, based on the Breast Cancer Family Registry (BCFR), the Consortium of Investigators of Modifiers of BRCA1/2 (CIMBA), and the Breast Cancer Association Consortium, respectively. Work et al. compared histological subtypes between BRCA1 mutation and controls. Among 92 MBC cases: 21% were BRCA1-positive (p < 0.001). No association was seen for other histologic subtypes [12].

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be considered. The studies included here exhibit heterogeneous designs and a wide variability, both in number of cases and in terms of geographical origin. Notably, most studies represented European populations, with limited representation of those of Asian and African origin.

The genetic analysis techniques used across studies were heterogeneous, reflecting the technological evolution over the years. Methods such as multiplex allele specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay and linkage analysis, used in the first studies, have been replaced by direct sequencing analysis or multigene panels testing in more recent studies. These differences could have affected the accuracy and completeness of mutation identification. In addition, there was a variability in the definition and classification of mutations. This is partly attributable to the temporal range of the included studies (1996–2022), considering that the standardized classification of genetic variants was introduced by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics in 2015, implying that earlier studies may have applied non-uniform or outdated classification systems [25].

The histological evaluation of MBC has not been standardized across the various centers, potentially resulting in misclassification or diagnostic variability. Furthermore, several studies predominantly focused on triple-negative breast cancers, introducing a possible selection bias.

Clinical significance and future directions

The identification of germline mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 in breast cancer patients has important clinical implications, as it can influence treatment decisions and interventions such as prophylactic surgery, oophorectomy and targeted therapies, including poly (ADP–ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. Current guidelines have defined criteria to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from genetic testing [26–29]. However, none of these guidelines explicitly recommend testing for women with MBC. The data from this confirms an association between medullary breast carcinoma (MBC) and the presence of a germline BRCA1 mutation, and a potential association with BARD1 and RAD51C mutations. This suggests these three genes be included within the testing panel. An excess of medullary cancers has not been observed with the BRCA2 gene or other susceptibility genes.

We recommend that criteria be updated to include medullary carcinoma as a criterion for genetic testing. Most of these cancers are also of the triple-negative subtype, which in itself is often a criterion for genetic testing. Identifying a BRCA1 mutation in these patients would provide early access to personalized surveillance and prevention pathways.
