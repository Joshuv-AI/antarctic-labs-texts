Extended static preservation of donor lungs at 10°C and early post-transplant outcomes: Experience from a center in the US
Authors: Yub Raj Sedhai, Christopher S. King, Silpa D. Kilaru, Zein Kattih, Adriana Kochi, Vikramjit Khangoora, Jared D. Wilkinson, Alan S. Nyquist, Anju Singhal, Junad M. Chowdhury, Christopher A. Thomas, Oksana A. Shlobin, Steven D. Nathan, Daniel Tang, Shambhu Aryal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAn emerging body of evidence suggests that extended static preservation of donor lung allografts at 10°C yields clinical outcomes comparable to standard storage, while enabling transplantation to be performed in a semi-elective manner. We conducted a retrospective analysis to determine whether a mid-sized lung transplant program could replicate outcomes reported in a recent clinical trial and larger transplant centers, while assessing the feasibility and real-world applicability of extended static cold storage.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single-center study of lung transplants performed between January 1 and December 31, 2025. Recipients were categorized by preservation strategy into standard storage or extended static cold storage at 10°C. Clinical and demographic data were obtained from the electronic health record. The primary outcome was the incidence of primary graft dysfunction (PGD) grades 1 to 3 at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours following transplantation. Secondary outcomes included duration of mechanical ventilation, length of hospitalization, length of ICU stay, postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) requirement, and 30-day survival.ResultsAmong 59 lung transplants, 40 underwent extended static cold storage at 10°C and 19 underwent standard preservation. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis was the most common indication; 55 of 59 recipients received bilateral lung transplantation. Four cases utilized ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) (Lung Bioengineering Inc., Silver Spring, MD, USA). Mean ischemia times were longer in the extended group (right 573.9±237.8 vs 343.5±168.1 min; left 613.8±237.3 vs 364.1±151.2 min). There were no significant between-group differences in PGD at 0, 24, 48, or 72 hours. Length of hospitalization, mechanical ventilation duration, and ICU stay were similar. ECMO was required in 4 (10%) of extended storage cases, but none in standard preservation group. There was no difference in the incidence of PGD based on the donor type (DCD vs DBD), regardless of the extended storage at 10°C.ConclusionExtended static lung preservation at 10 °C was not associated with a statistically significant increase in PGD or adverse short-term clinical outcomes, despite significantly prolonged ischemia times in our cohort. These findings support the feasibility of extended 10 °C preservation without compromising early post-transplant outcomes.

Introduction

Conventional lung preservation techniques involve flushing the donor organ with a low-potassium dextran preservation solution, maintaining the lungs in a partially inflated state, and traditionally storing them on ice with an expected temperature of 4 °C.1 The safe preservation window for donor lungs with this approach is generally limited to approximately 6 to 8 hours.2 This restricted preservation time contributes to geographic limitations in organ allocation and reinforces the time-sensitive, often semi-emergent nature of lung transplantation. Prolonging donor lung preservation beyond the conventional 6-to-8-hour window has been a subject of interest in transplantation. An extended preservation period may allow improved donor-recipient immunologic matching, facilitate semi-elective rather than urgent transplantation, and reduce geographical constraints in organ allocation. By enabling safer transport over longer distances, it may also expand the organ pool and improve logistical coordination and recipient optimization. The use of 10 °C as an optimal temperature for static lung preservation was explored more than 3 decades ago.3, 4, 5 This concept was later re-examined in a preclinical study using large animal models.6 Lungs stored at 10 °C demonstrated a more favorable inflammatory profile and better preservation of cell membrane integrity compared with those stored at 4 °C.6, 7 Extended storage at 10 °C demonstrated comparable clinical outcomes and improved logistical flexibility compared with conventional cold storage in a recent controlled trial.8 Similar findings have been reproduced in subsequent retrospective analyses.9, 10 In this single-center retrospective study, we sought to evaluate our contemporary experience with 10 °C donor lung preservation and to examine its impact on short-term clinical outcomes at a moderate size program able to replicate the results of prior cohort studies at larger institutions.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective analysis of all patients who underwent lung transplantation at Inova Fairfax Hospital from January 1, 2025, through December 31, 2025. Inova Lung Transplant Program is a moderate volume program based at a large community teaching hospital in Northern Virginia and is the only lung transplant program in the Washington DC metro area. Data on donor and recipient characteristics, including demographics, type of transplantation (unilateral vs bilateral), use of ex vivo lung perfusion, and total cold ischemia time, were abstracted from the electronic health record. Subgroups were defined according to donor type: donation after brain death (DBD) and donation after circulatory death (DCD). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Inova Fairfax Hospital (IRB number U23–03–5032).

Donor lungs procurement and preservation procedures

Donor lungs were procured from DBD or after DCD using standardized procurement protocols described in the literature.11, 12 Following retrieval, lungs were subjected to antegrade pulmonary arterial flush with a low-potassium dextran based preservation solution, followed by retrograde flushing via the pulmonary veins. The lungs were mildly inflated with 50% oxygen prior to storage. Lungs were stored and transported in a cooler with a thin layer of ice with expected temperature of 4 °C. Upon arrival at the recipient center, donor lungs were either implanted or transferred to a temperature-controlled incubator (MYTEMP 65HC, Benchmark Scientific, NJ, USA) designed to maintain a uniform temperature of 10 °C until implantation. For study purposes, patients who underwent transplantation following extended static storage at 10 °C were defined as the study group. Patients who underwent transplantation immediately upon organ arrival without extended 10 °C storage comprised the control group. The institutional adoption of extended static preservation at 10 °C was based on available clinical and preclinical evidence.6, 8 In practice, the decision to use extended preservation was made on a case-by-case basis, based on the logistical considerations, including but not limited to patient travel, staff logistics, surgical timing and operating room availability.Figure 1Standard and extended methods of lung allograft transport and static cold storage

Surgical considerations

All transplant surgeries were performed under cardiopulmonary bypass, in accordance with institutional protocol and the preference of the surgical team; no procedures utilized intraoperative ECMO support. All surgeries were performed via a midline sternotomy approach.

Outcome of interest

Patient charts in the electronic health record were retrospectively reviewed by an advanced lung disease and transplant fellow. Primary graft dysfunction (PGD) was adjudicated into grades 0, 1, 2, and 3 according to current International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines (2016 consensus update).13 Time points of interest for PGD assessment were defined at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours following transplantation. PGD grading performed by the fellow was independently verified by the medical director of the lung transplant program. Secondary outcomes included short-term clinical measures such as 30-day survival, duration of mechanical ventilation, length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, length of index hospitalization, and the need for postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, along with predefined clinical outcomes, were compared between the study and control groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate based on distribution. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Statistical significance was assessed using the independent samples t-test for continuous variables and the Pearson chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate) for categorical variables. Unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) were computed at each time point (0, 24, 48, and 72 hours) and for the pooled dataset to compare the odds of PGD between the 10 °C and standard preservation groups. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were derived using the Woolf logit method. A 0.5 continuity correction was applied to zero cells to maintain computational stability. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the statistical significance (p value) of differences in PGD incidence between groups at predefined clinical time points.

Results

Demographic summary

A total of 59 lung transplants were performed during the study period. Of these, 40 transplants utilized extended static storage at 10 °C (study group), while 19 were performed without extended storage (control group). The mean recipient age was 56.18 (SD = 13.45) years in the study group compared with 56.74 (15.00) years in the control group. Among the transplant recipients, 69.5% were male and 30.5% were female. IPF was the most common indication for transplantation (39%). Bilateral transplantation was performed in 55 cases (93%). Four cases utilized ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) (Lung Bioengineering Inc., Silver Spring, MD, USA). Thirteen transplants were performed using lungs from DCD donors. Among the DCD donors, 9 were preserved at 10 °C (study group), while the remaining 4 were classified in the control group. Mean ischemia times were longer in the study group (right 573.9±237.8 vs 343.5±168.1 min; left 613.8±237.3 vs 364.1±151.2 min). Extended 10°C utilization

Among the 59 lung transplantations, 40 donor lungs were stored for an extended duration at 10°C. The median duration of extended 10°C storage was 224.5 min (IQR, 117.25 to 508.25 min). Mean duration of 10°C storage was 286.33 (SD=231.70) min. Mean total ischemia time in the 10°C group was 573.90 (SD=237.79) and 613.77 (237.296) for the right and left lungs, respectively. Since the conventional storage group did not utilize extended 10°C storage, the mean total ischemia time was shorter, measuring 343.5 (168.08) min for the right lung and 364.11 (151.22) min for the left lung.

Primary graft dysfunction

PGD was assessed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours following transplantation. The time of transportation from the operating room to the cardiovascular intensive care unit was designated as time zero. There were no statistically significant differences in the distribution of PGD grades (0 to 3) between the 10 °C and conventional storage groups at 0, 24, 48, or 72 hours following lung transplantation. PGD grade 0 was observed in 25.0% vs 21.1% of patients at 0 hours and increased to 40.0% vs 47.4% by 72 hours in the 10 °C and conventional storage groups, respectively, without significant intergroup differences. Across all time points, most patients demonstrated PGD grades 0 to 1, while severe PGD (grade 3) remained infrequent in both groups. A comparison of PGD between the study and control groups is presented in Table 2, and Figure 2. The unadjusted odds of PGD grades 1 to 3 in the 10 °C group compared with the standard storage group were not significantly different at any time point: 0 hour (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.21 to 2.98), 24 hours (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.12 to 3.56), 48 hours (OR 1.54, 95% CI 0.45 to 5.22), and 72 hours (OR 1.35, 95% CI 0.45 to 4.06). None of these associations reached statistical significance. Regression analysis of PGD within 72-hours

On univariate donor analysis, higher donor age (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.10; p = 0.023) and donor BMI (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.17; p = 0.023) were associated with increased odds of PGD grades 1 to 3 at 0 hour, while donor PaO₂ and reported aspiration were not significantly associated with PGD at any time point. (e-Table C) Recipient factors including age, BMI, male sex, and diagnosis of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis were not significantly associated with PGD grades 1 to 3 at 0, 24, 48, or 72 hours following transplantation. Similarly, storage technique (10 °C vs conventional storage) was not independently associated with PGD grades 1 to 3 at any assessed time point, with odds ratios ranging from 0.65 to 1.54 and all comparisons remaining statistically non-significant. (e-Table D) Firth’s penalized logistic regression analysis demonstrated no independent association between 10 °C storage and PGD grades 1 to 3 within 72 hours following transplantation (OR 2.22, 95% CI 0.52 to 9.40; p = 0.279). Similarly, donor and recipient demographic variables, diagnosis of IPF, and donation after circulatory death (DCD) status were not significantly associated with PGD in the adjusted model. Postoperative ECMO support

Four patients (10%) in the study group required postoperative ECMO support. Among the 3 patients who required venovenous (VV) ECMO support, all developed grade 3 primary graft dysfunction (PGD). One patient had a complicated surgical course, including hemothorax requiring multiple thoracotomies for hemostasis and evacuation.

30-day survival

One patient in the 10 °C extended preservation group died within 30 days of lung transplantation. The death occurred in a patient undergoing redo transplantation for chronic lung allograft dysfunction. The intraoperative course was complicated by significant bleeding related to dense adhesions from prior surgery. Postoperatively, the patient developed irreversible neurological injury, confirmed by imaging and clinical examination. The patient’s family elected for compassionate withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies considering the poor neurologic prognosis and anticipated quality of life.

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was performed among recipients of lungs from DCD donors. Of the 59 transplants, 13 were performed using DCD lungs, of which 9 were in the study group and 4 in the control group. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in the incidence of PGD or secondary clinical outcomes within the DCD and DBD subgroups. These results are summarized in e-Table E, F and G.

Timing of transplant procedures

Surgical start times were abstracted from anesthesia records and categorized into four 6-hour quarters (Q1: 00:00 to 06:00; Q2: 06:01 to 12:00; Q3: 12:01 to 18:00; Q4: 18:01 to 24:00). In regard to the timing of surgery, 29 (72.5%) among 40 in the 10 °C group and 13 (68.4%) among 19 in the control group occurred during Q2 and Q3. Only 4 surgeries were performed between midnight and 06:00. (e-Figure A).

Discussion

Historically, lung transplantation has been limited by the relatively short duration for which the organ remains viable after retrieval. A 2013 multicenter analysis suggested that graft ischemic times exceeding 5.5 hours were associated with worse early gas exchange and diminished long-term survival after transplantation.14 Given this limited preservation window from the time of aortic cross-clamping, historically donor lungs could not be transported over long geographic distances, thereby restricting the potential donor pool. For the same reason, lung transplant surgeries have traditionally been performed as an urgent procedure during nighttime hours to minimize total ischemic time.15 In a 10-year single-center retrospective analysis, Yang et al. observed an association between nighttime lung transplantation and increased major postoperative complications, as well as lower 5-year overall and bronchiolitis obliterans-free survival, compared with daytime procedures.15 However, these findings were challenged by Choi et al., in a large UNOS-based analysis of 30,404 cases, who found no significant difference in short- or long-term survival between daytime and nighttime procedures.16 Interestingly, improved survival was associated with donor clamp times between 8:00 AM and 1:00 PM and reperfusion between 1:00 PM and 6:00 PM.16 Thus, semi-elective daytime lung transplantation has garnered increasing interest, as scheduled daytime procedures may optimize operating room logistics, improve staffing availability, and enhance patient safety, with potential implications for postoperative outcomes. A major barrier to the widespread adoption of semi-elective lung transplantation has been the lack of a safe and reliable donor lung preservation strategy.

A recent prospective, multicenter, single-arm clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04616365) evaluated extended lung preservation at 10 °C in 70 transplant recipients and compared outcomes with 140 matched contemporaneous controls whose grafts were preserved using conventional 4 °C storage. Donor lungs retrieved overnight were preserved at 10°C for up to 12 hours from cross-clamp to recipient anesthesia (median 12 hours 28 min for the first lung, 14 hours 8 min for the second lungs). PGD grade 3 at 72 hours occurred in 5.7% of the 10°C group vs 9.3% of matched controls, with no significant differences in ECMO use, mechanical ventilation duration, ICU or hospital length of stay, 30-day survival (100%), or 1-year survival.8

A multicenter retrospective study by Hoetzenecker et al. (n = 181 in the study group) replicated similar findings, demonstrating that prolonged static preservation of donor lungs at 10 °C for up to 24 hours did not increase primary graft dysfunction, ventilation duration, ICU or hospital length of stay, airway complications, or mortality compared with shorter preservation times or on-ice controls, thereby supporting the safety of extended 10 °C storage and the feasibility of semi-elective lung transplantation.10 Retrospective studies have replicated these findings, demonstrating that prolonged 10 °C storage is safe and associated with comparable clinical outcomes,9, 17, 18 with additional emerging evidence from ex-vivo lung perfusion further supporting the feasibility of extended preservation.19, 20

In our study, we did not observe significant differences in the incidence of PGD at 0, 24, 48, or 72 hours between lungs preserved with extended storage at 10 °C and those preserved conventionally. Furthermore, after adjustment for total cold ischemia time and donor type (DCD vs DBD), extended static storage at 10 °C was not independently associated with the development of PGD. There was one 30-day mortality in our study cohort, partly attributable to intraoperative bleeding and hypotension resulting in irreversible neurological injury. While the clinical trial by Ali et al. and the largest retrospective study by Hoetzenecker et al. which included 181 patients in the 10 °C arm primarily evaluated the incidence of grade 3 PGD at 72 hours, our analysis assessed the temporal evolution of PGD across prespecified postoperative time points, thereby providing additional insight into early graft function dynamics. Our study also examined the timing of transplant surgery. Although prior studies have reported mixed findings regarding outcomes after nighttime transplantation,15, 16 daytime surgery may offer advantages in staffing, resource availability, and logistical support. In our cohort, the majority of procedures were performed between 6 AM and 6 PM, which may help mitigate provider fatigue and reduce burnout among transplant teams.21, 22

Our cohort extends prior observations on the feasibility and relative safety of extended cold storage to recipient populations underrepresented in earlier studies, including patients with cystic fibrosis (5.1%) and systemic sclerosis (6.7%). Specifically, the inclusion of 6.7% lungs undergoing EVLP, and 22% DCD donors in our cohort adds important granularity and broadens the applicability of cold storage. However, given the single-center retrospective design, our findings may have limited generalizability Lungs were initially stored on ice and subsequently transferred to 10°C; given the lack of detailed preservation time data, this may limit the ability to fully characterize the effect of 10°C preservation. While ice storage is commonly considered to approximate 4°C, prior studies have demonstrated that temperatures may drop below 0°C.23 The wide confidence intervals, especially with respect to PGD occurrence across time points, may be prone to type II error. Further, our findings are also limited by small sample size with limited statistical power, and the lack of randomization. Importantly, our study, conducted at a mid-sized lung transplant program, successfully replicated outcomes reported by larger centers, further supporting the feasibility of extended static cold storage.

Collectively, emerging evidence supports extended donor lung preservation as a safe strategy, demonstrating comparable rates of PGD and short-term clinical outcomes relative to conventional storage. The biological plausibility may be explained by the promotion of mitochondrial-protective biomolecules including itaconate, glutamine, and N-acetylglutamine with 10 °C representing an optimal temperature for generating cytoprotective metabolites that support graft viability, as demonstrated in preclinical animal studies.24 Early post-implementation analyses and registry data suggest that implementation of the Composite Allocation Score (CAS) has been associated with increased donor lung travel distances and broader geographic allocation patterns.25 In this context, safe organ preservation strategies that maintain comparable clinical outcomes may help facilitate wider organ sharing. A multicenter randomized controlled trial is currently underway to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of 10 °C preservation in lung transplantation (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05898776). Additional evidence from this study is anticipated to further clarify the role of extended preservation.

Conclusion

Based on our findings, extended static preservation at 10 °C appears safe and is associated with clinical outcomes comparable to conventional storage. The approach is also logistically feasible within existing transplant workflows, without requiring substantial additional resources. If larger, multicenter studies replicate these results, 10 °C extended preservation may facilitate semi-elective daytime transplantation and broaden geographic organ allocation, ultimately expanding the donor pool and improving system-level equity and efficiency.
