Workplace violence and associated factors among healthcare professionals in public hospitals of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bemnet Fekre Butta, Natnael Atle Benti, Bethelhem Tamiru Tasew, Bemnet Yacobe Sayid, Huda Mohammed Fetwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorkplace violence has emerged as a global problem that affects all nations, workplaces, and occupational categories. The health sector accounts for more than one-third of all workplace violence worldwide. There is very little information available in Ethiopia about workplace violence affecting healthcare professionals. This study aims to identify key associated factors of workplace violence to inform future evidence-based interventions and policy development. Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of workplace violence and associated factors among healthcare professionals working in selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsAn analytic cross-sectional study was conducted from May 1 to June 30, 2023, among 599 randomly selected healthcare professionals working at four referral hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia via a multistage sampling technique. Data was collected via a pretested, structured, self-administered questionnaire adapted from a standard questionnaire. The data were coded and entered into EPI Information version 7 and exported to SPSS V.20.0 software for analysis. A frequency table was used to summarize the data. To identify factors associated with workplace violence, a binary logistic regression model in which the degree of association for variables was assessed via adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs and p values ≤ 0.05 was used.ResultThe prevalence of experiencing at least one type of workplace violence (physical, verbal, bullying, or sexual) in the previous year was 59.4% (95% CI = 55.6–63.1). The study revealed a statistically significant relationship between workplace violence and female sex (AOR = 1.56, 95% CI = 1.04–2.34, p = 0.033), pharmacist profession (AOR = 2.9, 95% CI = 1.15–7.33, p = 0.025), routine direct physical contact with patients (AOR = 2.19, 95% CI = 1.12–4.29, p = 0.022), emergency work starting (AOR = 2.50, 95% CI = 1.02–6.15, p = 0.045), and witnessing incidents of physical violence (AOR = 10.1, 95% CI = 5.75–17.59, p < 0.0001).ConclusionThis study revealed a high prevalence of workplace violence among health care professionals working in government hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Healthcare facilities should prioritize the establishment of comprehensive health and safety programs focused on the prevention and management of workplace violence, with particular attention given to the most vulnerable groups.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15126-5.

Background

Workplace violence (WPV) is any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation, or other disruptive behavior that occurs in the workplace. It ranges from threats and verbal abuse to physical assault and even homicide. This phenomenon can affect and involve employees, clients, customers, and visitors [1]. It is the leading cause of occupational death for workers worldwide, disproportionately affecting women [2]. Also a significant contributor to occupational morbidity and mortality worldwide, with the healthcare sector being one of the most hazardous environments [3–7].

WPV has severe physical and psychological consequences for healthcare workers. It impacts well-being, job performance, and patient care, contributing to stress, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and job dissatisfaction [8]. Victims may experience fear, anxiety, and even burnout, leading to decreased productivity and increased turnover. The ripple effect also disrupts communication between patients and providers, fueling “patient-avoiding behaviors” that compromise care quality [9].

These individual-level effects escalate to system-level challenges, especially in low-resource settings. In Africa, which has some of the lowest health indicators globally, it contributes to staff dissatisfaction, poor healthcare quality, and retention challenges [10]. Ethiopia’s health system already struggles with workforce shortages and underreporting of workplace violence, compounding the challenge. Empirical evidence estimates that the total cost of violence to society may reach 30% of GDP [11].

While prevention is essential, strategies to reduce WPV—such as improved facility policies, staff training, and environmental safety measures—remain largely unimplemented in many developing countries [12–17]. Risk factors for this phenomenon include patient-related factors (e.g., substance use, agitation, and poor prognosis), environmental factors (e.g., overcrowded spaces, poor design), and organizational issues (e.g., lack of reporting systems or support structures).

Despite this, WPV remains underrecognized and underreported in Ethiopia. Prior studies have shown a prevalence of 26.7–58.2% among healthcare workers [18–22], but many of these focused only on nurses or single facilities. Addis Ababa was purposefully selected for this study due to its dense concentration of referral hospitals, heavy patient loads, and workforce diversity. These factors contribute to a high-risk environment for violence. This study aimed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the prevalence and factors associated with this phenomenon among healthcare professionals in Addis Ababa, filling a critical knowledge gap and informing targeted interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Addis Ababa city from May 1– June 30, 2023. On the basis of 2012 Ethiopian Fiscal Year international la(EFY) figures from the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia, the Addis Ababa Region has an estimated total population of 3,686,068, consisting of 1,389,817 males and 1,527,478 females. Addis Ababa has 13 hospitals and 98 health centers [23]. According to the Ministry of Health data, approximately 17,546 health care professionals work in Addis Ababa. These include approximately 1867 physicians, 10,205 nurses, 2252 health officers, and 2222 midwives.

Sample size and sampling

The study was conducted in four hospitals in Addis Ababa, which were chosen by simple random sampling. They are St. Peter specialized hospital, which has 850 health professionals, Alert hospital 924, Menelik II referral hospital 815, and Aabet hospital 412, on the basis of data taken from their respective human resource offices during the study period. The study included all active staff at the facility. We excluded staff members with less than one year of work experience to ensure they had sufficient exposure to the work environment. Additionally, staff on long-term leave, such as maternity leave, were excluded from the study. A single population proportion formula with a confidence interval of 95%, a 5% margin of error, and a proportion of 58.2 [12] from a previous study was used and computed for the first objective, adding a 10% nonresponse rate. A 1.5 design effect resulted in a total sample size of 617 subjects. For the second objective, associated factors such as department, profession, shift work, and experience were taken since they had a statistically significant association with WPV in a previous study. Using the Epi Info software program for double population proportions with a confidence level of 95%, a margin of error of 5%, and a power of 80%, the sample size was calculated for those selected variables that were all less than 231. Since 617 is the largest sample size, it was used for this study.

A multistage sampling technique was used for this study. First, all the government hospitals of Addis Ababa were selected, and four hospitals (St. Peter specialized hospital, Alert hospital, Menelik II referral hospital, and Aabet hospital) were selected via a simple random sampling technique. Health professionals were subsequently sampled from each hospital using the same sampling technique. The sample size was allocated to each hospital proportionally 25% of the sample size.

Data collection and quality assurance

Data were collected via a standardized self-administered questionnaire via Google Forms that was written in English and Amharic. It was adapted from a standard questionnaire by the International Labor Office, International Council of Nurses, World Health Organization, and Public Services International (ILO/ICN/WHO/PSI) [24] Contextualized to fit with this research objective. It was pretested on 5% of the sample size in a different hospital from the sample. The data collection team consisted of nurses and doctors who participated in a one-day training session covering the study’s objectives and the orientation process. These collectors were responsible for distributing the digital form links to the sampled individuals. To ensure ethical compliance, the first page of the Google Form contained an informed consent statement; participants were required to click ‘Yes’ to provide consent before being allowed to proceed to the survey items. The questionnaire was divided into five parts. The first part focused on sociodemographic and occupational characteristics such as gender, age, profession, working shifts, and marital status. The second, third, fourth, and fifth sections contain physical violence, verbal abuse, bullying, and sexual harassment, respectively, with related questions.

Data processing and analysis

After the data were collected through Google Forms, they were exported to Microsoft Excel, checked for completeness, cleared, and exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 25 for analysis. Categorical variables are presented via frequency tables, graphs, and charts. Marital status was dichotomized to improve model parsimony. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). The model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test. The associations between WPV and the independent factors were assessed via a binary logistic regression model to determine the odds ratios (ORs). Univariate analysis at the 25% level of significance was run to select the variables to be fit into the multivariable binary logistic regression model, which was then run through a multivariable binary logistic regression model. A p-value threshold of 25% was chosen at the univariate stage to avoid excluding potentially important confounding variables that may not appear significant in unadjusted analyses but could show meaningful associations after adjustment in the multivariable model. Variables with p values < 0.05 were considered to have a statistically significant association with the outcome variable.

Results

Sociodemographic statistics

The total study population included 599 healthcare workers. The response rate was 97%, with nurses (47.1%), physicians (33.4%), pharmacists (7.3%), midwives (5.8%), and others (6.3%). Furthermore, 308 (51.4%) were females. The majority of healthcare professionals (270; 45.1%) were between the ages of 25 and 29, and the majority were single or divorced (337; 56.3%).

Workplace characteristics

The majority of the study participants, 558 (93.2%), interact with patients at work; additionally, 505 (84.3%) have routine direct physical contact with patients, such as washing, turning, lifting, and examining, regular hands-on interactions with patients; and 522 (87.1%) work shifts or duty hours. Most of the health professionals (345, 57.6%) had work experience ranging from one to five years, and the majority of the participants (112, 18.7%) spent most of their time in the emergency room. The number of staff present in the same work setting for the majority of their working hours is between 6 and 10. According to many of the participants (433 (72.3%)), there are no procedures for reporting WPV that they are aware of. Approximately one-third (188, 31.4%) had witnessed physical violence against colleagues in their working environment. (Table 1). Statistical assumptions for the logistic regression were met. The VIF values were checked and showed no evidence of multicollinearity among the independent variables. Furthermore, the Hosmer and Lemeshow test indicated that the model is a good fit for the data (p > 0.05).

Prevalence of workplace violence

More than half (59.4%) [95% CI: (55.6, 63.1)] of the health care professionals reported experiencing at least one form of WPV (physical, verbal, bullying, or sexual) in the previous year. Verbal abuse was the most common, 323 (53.9%), followed by bullying, 100 (16.7%), physical violence, 81 (13.5%), and sexual violence, 27 (4.5%). In the present study, 93 (15.5%) health care workers(HCW) were victims of two types of violence, whereas only 1% of participants reported experiencing all forms of violence. (Table 2)

Factors associated with workplace violence

Univariate analysis at the 25% level of significance was run to select the variables to be fit into the multivariable binary logistic regression model, from which age, sex, profession, years of working experience, working shifts, interacting with patients, routine direct physical contact, number of staff, work setting and witnessed incidents of physical violence were found to have statistically significant relationships with it. However, in the multivariable binary logistic regression model, after adjusting for other covariates, sex, profession, routine direct physical contact with patients, work setting, and witnessed incidents of physical violence were found to have a statistically significant relationship with WPV at the 5% level of significance.

Accordingly, after adjustments are made for other covariates, the odds of violence occurring are 1.56 times greater in females than in males (AOR 1.56 95% CI = 1.04, 2.34, p value = 0.033), and the odds of pharmacists being violated are approximately three times greater than those of physicians (AOR 2.9 95% CI = 1.15,7.33, p value = 0.025). Similarly, the odds that health professionals who have routine direct physical contact with patients encounter violence are approximately two times greater than those who do not have routine direct physical contact (AOR 2.19, 95%CI = 1.12, 4.299; p value = 0.022). The odds of health care professionals who spend the majority of their time in the emergency room experiencing violence were 2.5 times greater than those who spend their time at the outpatient clinic (AOR 2.5, 95% CI = 1.02,6.15; p value = 0.045). In addition, the odds of WPV are 10 times greater for health care professionals who have witnessed incidents of violence than for those who have not (AOR 10.05, 95% CI = 5.75, 17.59; p value = 0.000) (Table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first comprehensive study of WPV in health care that includes multiple professions in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The study revealed that 59.4% of the respondents encountered it. This number is higher than what was reported by the International Labor Organization (ILO), which states that violence affects one of every two health workers worldwide [25]. The overall 12-month prevalence of WPV among healthcare workers is in line with findings in northern Ethiopia [19], Sudan [26], and European Union member states [4]. This may be due to the similarity of the professionals who participated. These rates are lower than those reported in studies in eastern Ethiopia, which were 64.0% (95% CI: 60.2–67.7%) [14], Sidama, 74.5% (95% CI: 70.1–78.5) [22], and Addis Ababa, 67.6% [27]. This could be due to studies focusing mainly on nurses, which are an associated factor in multiple studies.

Female sex was significantly associated with WPV in health professionals in this study, and the most common type of violence they experienced was verbal violence (55.8%), followed by bullying (21.1%), physical violence (12.7%), and sexual violence (7.1%). The ILO states that women are more vulnerable to WPV [25]. than men are. This is also supported by studies performed in southern Ethiopia [20, 28] and Turkey [16] and a systematic review performed in Africa [10]. One possible explanation for this association is rooted in gender-based power dynamics and societal norms. In many societies, including Ethiopia, women are often perceived as more vulnerable and submissive, making them potential targets for aggression and violence. Furthermore, cultural beliefs and stereotypes may play a role, as some individuals may hold biased views that undermine the authority and competence of female healthcare professionals, leading to increased incidents of WPV [18, 28].

Unlike most studies, this study revealed that being a pharmacist was strongly associated with Violence. Verbal violence (47.7%) and bullying (15.9%) were the only forms they experienced. Although our findings indicate that pharmacists reported higher rates of workplace violence compared to other healthcare professionals, the underlying reasons remain unclear. One possible explanation relates to their frequent interactions with patients and caregivers during medication dispensing, which can involve misunderstandings, prescription issues, or delays that trigger frustration. Additionally, pharmacies in Addis Ababa government hospitals are often overcrowded with high demand for medications amid drug scarcity, creating a stressful environment for pharmacists who face constant pressure from patients seeking limited supplies. These factors may lead to uncomfortable conversations and even violent situations. However, this interpretation is speculative and warrants further investigation. Future studies should explore the specific workplace dynamics and risk factors unique to pharmacists that may contribute to their heightened exposure to violence. It is also possible that other studies did not observe this result because they did not include pharmacists in their samples.

According to this study, healthcare workers in Addis Ababa, who have regular direct physical contact with patients, are more vulnerable to WPV. The most common type of violence they experienced was verbal (58.2%), followed by bullying (17.8%), physical violence (15.4%), and sexual violence (5.1%). This result is supported by another study performed at Amanuel Hospital in Addis Ababa [19] and China [29]. This is mostly due to the close nature of their contacts, in which physical touch can occasionally elicit hostile or violent conduct from patients or their accompanying families. Violence is more likely to occur in healthcare environments where healthcare personnel are responsible for administering injections, conducting physical examinations, or providing hands-on care. Language barriers, cultural differences, and limited resources all contribute to the potential for patient irritation and agitation, which can lead to violent occurrences.

On the basis of this study, working in emergency departments is associated with a greater risk of WPV. Those who work in emergencies are more than two times more likely to face this phenomenon than those who work in outpatient clinics. Professionals working in emergencies have experienced verbal violence (71.4%), physical violence (40.2%), bullying (24.1%), and sexual violence (5.4%). The Occupational Safety and Health (OSHA) Act states that working in high-volume urban emergency departments has one of the highest risks for violence. This finding is consistent with research conducted in northern Ethiopia [19], eastern Ethiopia [21], and southern Ethiopia [28]. The comparability of outcomes may be explained by a similar emergency service delivery system, violence handling, and security conditions. Furthermore, despite differences in sociodemographic and work setting factors, similar findings have been reported in Italy [15], the United States [1, 3], and Egypt [30].

Witnessing physical violence incidents was significantly associated with WPV. These factors are consistent with studies conducted in Eastern Ethiopia [21] and Brazil [31]. This might indicate that those who were witnessing violence were working in a clinically predisposed work environment, which increased their risk of experiencing it themselves. Additionally, witnessing physical violence can normalize aggressive behavior and perpetuate a culture of violence within the workplace. This can lead to a cycle of violence, where witnessing such incidents increases the likelihood of experiencing violence in the future.

To address these risks, public hospitals should implement multi-component violence prevention programs. This includes the establishment of formal, user-friendly reporting systems; our data showed that 72.3% of staff were unaware of reporting procedures, highlighting a significant gap in organizational support. Clear protocols would allow for systematic tracking and response to incidents.

Additionally, targeted training in de-escalation techniques is essential, particularly for staff in high-contact areas like the emergency room and pharmacy. Strengthening environmental security—such as controlled access points and increased security presence during night shifts- can serve as a physical deterrent to violence while fostering a safer atmosphere for both staff and patients.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of this study are having wider study subject coverage by profession that included multiple government hospitals in Addis Ababa, and being the first of its kind in the capital. However, there are some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of a causal relationship between the identified factors and WPV. Second, the study is subject to recall bias, as participants were asked to report incidents over the preceding 12 months. Third, social desirability bias may have occurred, leading to the underreporting of sensitive incidents like sexual harassment. Finally, the study focused on healthcare workers and did not assess perpetrator-related factors, which are vital for a holistic understanding of workplace aggression.

Conclusions

Workplace violence is a common occupational challenge among healthcare professionals working in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, with nearly six in ten participants reporting exposure to at least one form of violence in the previous year. Verbal abuse was the most frequently reported form of violence, followed by bullying, physical violence, and sexual harassment. Female healthcare professionals, pharmacists, staff with routine direct physical contact with patients, those working in emergency departments, and those who had witnessed workplace violence were at increased risk of experiencing workplace violence.

These findings highlight the need for comprehensive workplace violence prevention and management strategies within healthcare facilities. Strengthening reporting and response mechanisms, increasing awareness of available reporting procedures, providing training on communication and de-escalation techniques, and enhancing safety measures in high-risk departments such as emergency units may help reduce the occurrence of workplace violence. Creating a safe and supportive work environment for healthcare professionals should be recognized as an essential component of healthcare quality, workforce retention, and patient safety.
