Investigation of university students’ oral health attitudes, behaviors, and habits: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Muhammet Fidan, Mustafa Fidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeveloping positive attitudes toward oral health is critical for improving general health and hygiene. This study aimed to examine the relationship between university students’ oral health attitudes, behaviors, and habits.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Turkey using convenience sampling. The Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S) was applied alongside questions on socio-demographic factors and oral health attitudes and behaviors, including oral hygiene habits such as tooth brushing, flossing, dental visits and and toothbrush replacement frequencies. The data were analyzed using independent sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA (p < .05).ResultsDuring the 2023–2024 academic year, a total of 596 university students (407 females and 189 males) participated in the study. The mean age of university students was 20.2 ± 1.67. The mean total OHA-S scores ranged from 151 to 174 according to categories, within the theoretical range of 41–205. Higher OHA-S scores were associated with frequent tooth brushing (≥ 2 times/day), regular flossing, routine dental visits, and timely toothbrush replacement (all p < .05), while smoking was linked to significantly lower scores (p < .001).ConclusionUniversity students with more positive oral health attitudes demonstrated healthier oral hygiene practices, whereas smoking negatively affected attitudes. These findings highlight the importance of preventive oral health education and the value of multidimensional assessment tools such as the OHA-S.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07990-8.Keywords: Attitude, Dental care, Oral hygiene, Smoking, Students

Background

An individual’s overall health is influenced by a combination of personal attributes, health behaviors, and cognitive perceptions [1]. Overall well-being is a multidimensional construct that significantly impacts both physical and psychosocial quality of life. Within this context, oral health is considered one of the fundamental components of well-being [2, 3]. Numerous factors including behavioral patterns, educational attainment, environmental conditions, and sociodemographic characteristics play significant roles in shaping oral health outcomes. Research consistently highlights the pivotal role of education in promoting positive health behaviors and reducing the risk of dental health problems [4, 5]. In this regard, attitudes toward oral health—formed through educational, emotional, and socio-environmental influences—serve as key predictors of preventive behaviors and long-term oral hygiene maintenance [6, 7].

Attitudes are influenced by cognitive, emotional, and behavioral characteristics, which collectively guide how individuals interpret and act upon oral health information [8]. These characteristics, whether positive or negative, underscore the importance of identifying and addressing underlying tendencies. Attitudes, as learned dispositions, can be modified over time; thus, addressing them presents an opportunity to foster healthier behaviors [9]. Although early childhood represents a critical stage in the development of health-related knowledge and habits, it remains essential to support and guide attitudes toward oral health throughout the life course [10, 11].

Good oral health significantly impacts an individual’s ability to speak, smile, and engage creatively within society [12]. Numerous factors contribute to oral health, underscoring the importance of developing and consistently practicing healthy oral habits for improvement [13]. One of the key factors in achieving optimal oral health is the adoption of effective and efficient dental health behaviors. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses confirm that oral health behaviors are directly linked to clinical outcomes [14]. A positive attitude toward oral and dental health is associated with improvements in both general health and oral hygiene [15]. A previous study noted the scarcity of validated and multidimensional scales specifically developed to assess oral health attitudes and behaviors in university students or adult populations [11]. However, there remains a lack of valid and reliable instruments tailored to university students [11, 16, 17]. A recent study has revealed a discrepancy between university students’ oral health attitudes and their actual behaviors [18]. However, the use of unidimensional scales in existing studies limits the ability to gather comprehensive data on the various sub-dimensions of oral health attitudes [19, 20]. A previous study reported that university students exhibited lower levels of protective oral health behaviors compared to their peers in industrialized countries, highlighting the need for the application of multidimensional scale studies in this context [16].

University students represent a population in a transitional life stage where the development and reinforcement of healthy habits are especially important. However, research indicates that many students in this group demonstrate limited awareness and insufficient knowledge regarding oral health and related attitudes [18]. Although oral health behaviors among university students have been explored in numerous studies, the majority have focused on dental students or relied on unidimensional tools, limiting their ability to capture the multifaceted nature of oral health attitudes. Additionally, there is a noticeable lack of validated, multidimensional instruments specifically designed for non-dental student populations, which restricts a comprehensive understanding of behavioral determinants and the design of effective interventions [5, 11, 21].

Although various instruments are available to measure attitudes toward oral health, the literature on valid and reliable scales based on comprehensive theoretical frameworks remains insufficient. To address this gap, the present study employed the Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S) to provide a more holistic assessment of oral health attitudes [18]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between university students’ oral health attitudes, as measured by the OHA-S, and their oral hygiene behaviors.

Methods

Participants for the study

This cross-sectional study was conducted during the 2023–2024 academic year, with ethical approval obtained from the ethics committee (Protocol Number: 2023-SBB-0731). To estimate the required sample size, a pilot study was performed with 30 individuals, consistent with recommendations from previous research [22]. Several independent variables such as gender, smoking status, parental education, and income level were considered to assess potential differences in oral health attitudes, measured by the OHA-S total score. Gender was emphasized in the power analysis since it served as the reference variable for calculating the minimum required sample size. The total Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S) score, ranging from 41 to 205, reflects each student’s overall attitude toward oral health and serves as a continuous outcome variable for power analysis and subsequent between-group comparisons. Based on these findings, the power analysis (G*Power 3.1, Düsseldorf, Germany) indicated that a minimum sample size of 334 university students was required, with a 95% confidence level (1-α), 85% test power (1-β), and a minimum effect size of d = 0.294.

Participants were recruited from a public university located in the Western Black Sea Region of Türkiye, specifically in Bartın. This region is characterized by a semi-rural environment with a mix of urban and rural populations, moderate socioeconomic diversity, and limited access to specialized oral healthcare services. Students enrolled in fields such as medicine, dentistry, nursing, pharmacy, and other health-related programs were excluded from the study to minimize potential bias related to prior knowledge and attitudes about oral health. They were recruited using a convenience sampling method from the faculty of education (62%) and literature (38%). An online form was created for data collection and shared with the participants. In addition, reminders were posted twice in faculty groups over a two-week period. Prior to data collection, detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, and scope was provided to all participants through an informed consent form. Written consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring their voluntary participation in the study.

Data collection process

Structured questionnaire form

A structured questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic characteristics [age, gender, parental education (mother and father), income, smoking status (yes/no)] and oral hygiene behaviors including habits such as frequency of dental visits, frequency of tooth brushing, frequency of flossing, and frequency of toothbrush replacement. Importantly, the form was structured to ensure the anonymity of participants by excluding any personally identifiable information. The smoking question was asked as a dichotomous variable: ‘Do you smoke?’ with the response options Yes/No. In addition, participants who reported smoking were asked to indicate the number of cigarettes smoked per day. Minimum wage refers to the legally mandated lowest level of compensation that employers are required to pay their employees. It is determined in line with a country’s economic conditions, living costs, and social standards, and serves as a baseline to secure workers’ fundamental livelihood. Thus, minimum wage is not only an economic indicator but also a reflection of social welfare and labor standards [23]. During the period when this study was conducted, the legal minimum wage in Türkiye was approximately 11,402 TL (Turkish Lira, the official currency of Türkiye) per month (about 420 USD), which was used as the reference value to categorize participants’ income levels. In this study, the term behavior refers to observable oral hygiene actions such as brushing, flossing, or visiting the dentist, whereas habit denotes the repetitive and automatic nature of these actions. This distinction was considered throughout the interpretation of the results. To avoid redundancy, the specific answering options and coding of these variables are not repeated here, as they are clearly presented in Tables 2 and 3. These tables outline both the response categories and the related comparisons used in the statistical analyses.

Oral health attitude scale (OHA-S)

The Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S), developed by Fidan et al. [18] was utilized to assess university students’ tendencies toward oral health. The OHA-S comprises six sub-dimensions with a total of 41 items (see Appendix A for the English version).

For clarity, the six sub-dimensions of the Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S) are referred to throughout the text as follows:

Factor 1 (F1)-Sensitivity (12 items): Personal awareness of oral health needs.

Factor 1 (F1)-Sensitivity (12 items): Personal awareness of oral health needs.

Factor 2 (F2)-Importance (6 items): The perceived importance of maintaining oral health.

Factor 2 (F2)-Importance (6 items): The perceived importance of maintaining oral health.

Factor 3 (F3)-Avoidance of Harmful Elements (7 items): Tendency to avoid behaviors detrimental to oral health.

Factor 3 (F3)-Avoidance of Harmful Elements (7 items): Tendency to avoid behaviors detrimental to oral health.

Factor 4 (F4)-Tendency Toward Products and Activities (6 items): Interest in oral health–related products and preventive actions.

Factor 4 (F4)-Tendency Toward Products and Activities (6 items): Interest in oral health–related products and preventive actions.

Factor 5 (F5)-Awareness (6 items): General cognitive awareness and knowledge regarding oral health.

Factor 5 (F5)-Awareness (6 items): General cognitive awareness and knowledge regarding oral health.

Factor 6 (F6)-Social Impact (4 items): How oral health influences social interactions and daily life.

Factor 6 (F6)-Social Impact (4 items): How oral health influences social interactions and daily life.

The scale employs a 5-point Likert format, where responses are rated as “strongly disagree”, “disagree”, “somewhat agree”, “agree”, and “strongly agree”. The scale has a minimum score of 41 and a maximum score of 205, with no reverse-scored items. Score ranges were defined to categorize attitudes into five levels: “very low " (41–101 points), “low” (102–144 points), “improvable” (145–160 points), “high” (161–184 points), and “very high” (185–205 points). The OHA-S score ranges are as follows [18]. Higher scores in each sub-dimension indicate more positive attitudes toward oral health, while lower scores reflect weaker attitudes or less inclination toward the behaviors represented by that dimension.

To assess the validity and reliability of the Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S) within the current sample, a series of statistical analyses were performed. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS version 23 to evaluate the construct validity of the scale. The CFA results indicated that the model had a good fit to the data, with the following fit indices: χ²/df = 3.79; p < .001; GFI (Goodness of Fit Index) = 0.90; CFI (Comparative Fit Index) = 0.92; NNFI (Non-Normed Fit Index) = 0.91; SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual) = 0.030; and RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation) = 0.042. These values fall within the acceptable thresholds for model fit, suggesting that the six-factor structure of the OHA-S is supported in the current university student sample. To evaluate convergent validity, Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were calculated for each sub-dimension, ranging from 0.53 to 0.72, all of which exceed the recommended minimum threshold of 0.50. Composite Reliability (CR) coefficients were also computed and ranged from 0.82 to 0.89 across the six sub-factors, indicating a high level of internal consistency. The internal reliability of the OHA-S was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. The subscales demonstrated acceptable to high reliability: sensitivity (α = 0.82), importance (α = 0.73), avoidance of harmful elements (α = 0.76), tendency toward products and activities (α = 0.80), awareness (α = 0.76), and social impact (α = 0.75). The Cronbach’s alpha for the total OHA-S scale was 0.87, indicating overall high internal consistency. The OHA-S demonstrated either high or acceptable internal consistency, as assessed by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, which were reported in the original validation study by Fidan et al. [18]. These findings provide strong evidence for the construct validity, convergent validity, and internal reliability of the OHA-S when applied to the university student population in this study.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the data using SPSS version 27 and AMOS 23 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The model’s fit to the data was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis. Initially, all data were assessed for normality, with skewness and kurtosis values considered acceptable within the range of -1.5 to + 1.5. Differences in oral health attitude scores between groups with two categories were analyzed using the independent samples t-test. For groups with more than two categories, differences in oral health attitude scores were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with statistical significance set at p < .05. Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was applied to examine the relationship between the OHA-S score and the number of cigarettes smoked.

Table 2 shows the comparisons of Oral Health Attitude Scale (OHA-S) scores across gender, age, smoking status, and parental education. While overall attitudes mostly fell into the “could be improved” or “high” categories - none reached “very high” - we observed statistically significant differences in OHA-S scores between the different hygiene behaviors. In contrast, comparisons by gender showed no significant differences in either total scores or any of the scale’s dimensions (Table 2). Participants who did not smoke had a significantly higher total score on the OHA-S (p < .001), F1 (p = .002), F2 (p = .016), F3 (p < .001), F5 (p = .001), and F6 (p < .001) compared to participants who smoked (Table 2). There was no significant difference between the parental education level and OHA-S total score (p > .05) (Table2).

Table 3 illustrates the significant differences between oral hygiene habits and OHA-S scores. Participants who went to the dentist once every six months and once a year had a significantly higher total score for the OHA-S (p < .001), F1 (p < .001), F2 (p < .001), F3 (p < .001), F4 (p = .001), and F5 (p < .001), and compared to participants who went to the dentist whenever there was a problem (Table 3). Participants who had two or more daily tooth brushing habits had significantly higher total scores on the OHA-S (p < .001), F1 (p < .001), F2 (p < .001), F3 (p < .001), F4 (p < .001), F5 (p < .001), and F6 (p < .001) than participants who had less than two daily brushing habits (Table 3). Participants who regularly flossed their teeth had significantly higher total OHA-S scores (p < .001), F1 (p < .001), F2 (p < .001), F3 (p < .001), F4 (p < .001), F5 (p = .001), F6 (p = .001) than participants who rarely and never flossed (Table 3). Participants who changed their toothbrushes every three months had significantly higher total scores of OHA-S (p = .027), F1 (p < .001), F2 (p < .001), F3 (p < .001), F4 (p < .001), F5 (p < .001), and F6 (p = .027) than participants who changed their toothbrushes every six months and participants who rarely changed their toothbrushes (Table 3). On the other hand, there was a negative correlation between the amount of cigarette smoking and the total score of the OHA-S (r = − .218, p < .001).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a clear relationship between university students’ oral health attitudes and their oral hygiene behaviors, underscoring the importance of strengthening preventive practices within this population. The findings indicated that attitudes were influenced by both socio-demographic factors and daily oral hygiene routines. Overall, OHA-S scores were most commonly categorized as ‘improvable’ or ‘high,’ reflecting a moderately positive outlook toward oral health while also highlighting key areas for further improvement.

While female students demonstrated slightly better total OHA-S scores, the difference was not statistically significant. This result was consistent with previous studies, which also reported no significant differences in oral health attitudes between male and female students [24, 25]. On the other hand, Mekhemar et al. [21] reported that female dental students exhibited significantly better oral health attitudes than their male peers. These differences may be related to differences in the study populations or methodologies. Furthermore, because our participants were young adults recruited from a single university setting, their relatively homogeneous living conditions and shared social environment may have reduced the likelihood of detecting gender-based differences in oral health attitudes [26]. While the study by Mekhemar et al. [21] focused on a specific academic group (dental students), our study, conducted on general student groups, collected data from a larger and more heterogeneous sample.

Oral diseases have multifactorial causes, with socio-behavioral and environmental factors playing a significant role in oral health. Previous studies indicated that socioeconomic status and parental education levels were associated with improved health outcomes in children [27]. In the present study, no significant relationship was found between parental education level and OHA-S scores, whereas previous studies have reported a positive association between parental education and oral health attitudes in adolescents and young adults [11, 27]. As individuals enter adulthood and gain autonomy, the influence of parental education on health-related attitudes may diminish. This could explain the lack of a significant association observed in our study and underscores the need for further research in more diverse populations.

Participants who practiced regular oral hygiene behaviors—such as brushing at least twice daily, flossing consistently, attending routine dental visits, and replacing their toothbrush every three months—demonstrated significantly higher OHA-S scores. These findings support the notion of a bidirectional relationship between oral health attitudes and behaviors, whereby positive attitudes encourage healthier practices, and sustained engagement in these behaviors further reinforces those attitudes [28, 29]. This aligns with the conceptual framework proposed by Edwards et al. [30] who emphasized the predictive role of oral health values and attitudes in determining behavior. University-based educational programs can play a critical role in promoting these behaviors by providing practical training on proper oral hygiene techniques and encouraging preventive care routines. In our study, individuals who brushed and flossed regularly scored higher across multiple sub-dimensions of the OHA-S, indicating that attitude is a key driver of positive oral health behavior. This finding was consistent with prior research suggesting that individuals with greater oral health knowledge and awareness especially those who maintain regular brushing and flossing habits are more likely to exhibit stronger oral health attitudes [6, 31]. Although oral diseases are preventable, their prevalence has continued to rise despite efforts to maintain oral health [32]. To enhance prevention, it is essential to standardize current dental care practices and educational frameworks, implement specific regulations, and consider potential sanctions [31].

Given the long-term consequences of dental caries and periodontal disease—as well as the associated financial burden—preventive interventions are essential at both individual and systemic levels. Regular dental visits not only support early detection and management of oral health issues but may also contribute to the identification of broader health conditions [33]. In our study, over half of the participants reported visiting the dentist only when experiencing a problem, whereas only 23.6% attended routine check-ups every six months. This behavioral pattern was clearly reflected in OHA-S scores, as those who visited the dentist regularly exhibited more positive oral health attitudes. These results suggest that the education and awareness gained during dental visits may positively influence individuals’ attitudes. In contrast, seeking dental care only when problems arise, rather than through routine check-ups, may increase the prevalence of oral diseases and lead to higher treatment cost [34]. These findings highlight the importance of ensuring access to oral health services and expanding educational programs that promote the value of preventive care.

Smoking is associated with poor oral hygiene, periodontal disease, and a decreased likelihood of engaging in preventive behaviors. A previous study [35] reported a significant association between smoking and irregular dental care among adults. In our study, the significantly lower OHA-S scores among smokers align with existing literature that highlights the detrimental impact of smoking on both oral health attitudes and practices [36]. Preventing the negative effects of smoking on oral health should therefore be a key focus in enhancing the effectiveness of educational programs. In this regard, oral health education should adopt a comprehensive approach that promotes behavioral changes in addition to increasing individuals’ knowledge. The harmful effects of smoking on oral health can lead to serious consequences, such as oral cancers and tooth loss [36]. The lower level of awareness about oral health among smokers may explain the negative impact smoking has on their attitudes. The positive impact of oral health education on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of university students supports its effectiveness as a strategy. The findings of our study, consistent with the existing literature [37] revealed significant differences in oral health attitudes and behaviors. One study suggested that dental curricula should focus on the effectiveness of anti-smoking initiatives in both theoretical and practical courses, as knowledge about the harmful effects of smoking alone may not be sufficient to enhance students’ attitudes [38]. This highlights the broader role of dentists not only in promoting oral hygiene behaviors and the formation of healthy habits but also in shaping general health-related beliefs and attitudes—such as encouraging healthy eating, physical activity, smoking cessation, and preventive care—through both education and clinical interaction.

While our study provides valuable information, it is important to acknowledge some limitations. This study included a high number of female students, which may have affected the overall attitude scores. Gender differences in health-related attitudes and behaviors are well documented, and therefore this imbalance may skew the results and limit their generalizability. The recent study was also conducted using convenience sampling and included students from a single state university in Turkey. This situation may create selection bias, as data collection relied on voluntary participation, and individuals with a greater interest in oral health were more likely to participate. Furthermore, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader student populations or different sociocultural contexts. Future studies involving random sampling from multiple universities and different regions will help to increase external validity and provide a more comprehensive understanding of university students’ attitudes toward oral health. Another limitation of this study is that all oral hygiene behaviors are based on participants’ self-reports. Self-reported measurements may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, as participants may misremember their actual behaviors or exaggerate desirable habits such as frequent tooth brushing or flossing. These factors may have led to an overestimation of positive behaviors and should be considered when interpreting the results. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Future longitudinal studies may offer a clearer understanding of the dynamics between oral health attitudes and oral hygiene habits over time. Expanding the study to include diverse populations across different cultural and socioeconomic contexts would also enhance the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study’s focus on a specific geographical region may not fully reflect broader demographics. Further research utilizing larger, more diverse populations and longitudinal designs is necessary to validate these findings and explore potential causal relationships. Moreover, the study did not consider several factors that may influence oral health, such as dietary habits and access to dental treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study showed that university students with more positive oral health attitudes participated more frequently in preventive behaviors such as regular tooth brushing, flossing, and dental visits, while smoking was associated with significantly lower attitude scores. These findings highlight the importance of implementing targeted oral health education and awareness programs in universities to strengthen preventive practices, discourage risky behaviors such as smoking, and promote sustainable improvements in oral health at both the individual and population levels.
