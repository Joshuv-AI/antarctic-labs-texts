Genetic diversity and virulence profiling of Streptococcus suis isolates from swine lesions in South Korea: a longitudinal study
Authors: Sung-Hyun Moon, Da-Yun Bae, Umma Habiba, Young-Seung Ko, Taek Geun Lee, Yun-Chae Cho, Zoya Afzal, Won-Il Kim, Yeonsu Oh, Ho-Seong Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ImportanceStreptococcus suis is a zoonotic pathogen that poses a threat to human and animal health. In pigs, it causes arthritis, meningitis, and septicemia, while in humans it leads to meningitis, endocarditis, pneumonia, septicemia, and septic shock. Effective control of this pathogen is important for both public and animal health.ObjectiveThis study characterized S. suis isolates from swine lesions in the Republic of Korea to provide insights into their epidemiological features.MethodsSeventy-four isolates were collected from diverse tissues. Serotypes were determined using multiplex PCR targeting capsular polysaccharide genes, and multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted with seven housekeeping genes. The virulence-associated genes epf, mrp, and sly were screened by multiplex PCR.ResultsSixteen serotypes were identified, with serotype 2 most prevalent (27.0%), followed by serotypes 3, 8, 7, 13, and 4. MLST revealed 41 sequence types, including 26 novel STs. Among virulence genes, sly was most frequently detected, but nearly half of the isolates were negative for all three genes. The serotype and sequence type distribution also showed notable year-to-year variations, indicating ongoing genetic diversification in Korea.Conclusions and RelevanceThese findings underscore the need for continuous surveillance to track serotype shifts and the emergence of novel lineages. Molecular tools such as MLST and virulence gene profiling enhance epidemiological understanding and support risk assessment for this zoonotic bacterium.

INTRODUCTION

Streptococcus suis is a significant zoonotic pathogen that poses a considerable threat to public health and the global swine industry. This bacterium is responsible for a wide range of diseases in pigs, including meningitis, arthritis, septicemia, and endocarditis, resulting in substantial economic losses due to mortality, morbidity, and reduced productivity [1]. S. suis is also an emerging zoonotic agent, capable of causing severe infections in humans, such as meningitis, septicemia, and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome [1]. Although S. suis infections are a global concern, the dynamics of infection, particularly regarding serotype variability, genetic diversity, and their correlation with virulence and epidemiological patterns, are not well understood.

Moreover, despite S. suis being primarily a swine-associated pathogen, its zoonotic potential warrants broader consideration under the One Health framework. Although direct evidence of transmission from wildlife to humans is limited, a single documented case involved a Eurasian river otter (Lutra lutra) in the Republic of Korea that developed septicemia caused by S. suis serotype 2. Genetic analysis suggested that the infection may have originated from domestic pigs through environmental exposure [2]. Although this is the only known case of a S. suis infection in wildlife with potential zoonotic relevance, it underscores the importance of continuous environmental surveillance and cross-species monitoring.

The paucity of comprehensive knowledge poses a significant challenge to disease control and prevention efforts. The different serotypes and genetic profiles of S. suis exhibit varying degrees of pathogenicity and geographical distribution, highlighting the need for a detailed characterization of the circulating strains [1]. Understanding the prevalence of specific serotypes and the presence of virulence-associated genes is crucial for developing effective diagnostic tools, vaccines, and targeted intervention strategies.

This longitudinal study, which was conducted at the Jeonbuk National University Animal Disease Diagnosis Center, Republic of Korea, from March 2013 to December 2017 and January 2022 to December 2023, examined the genetic and serological diversity of S. suis strains isolated from lesion tissues of diseased pigs. Multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used for serotyping, targeting capsular polysaccharide (cps) genes, which play a crucial role in the virulence of S. suis. Furthermore, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was performed to determine the genetic relatedness of the isolates by analyzing seven key housekeeping genes (aroA, cpn60, dpr, gki, mutS, recA, and thrA), which were selected for their conserved nature and suitability for strain differentiation. Each isolate was assigned a sequence type (ST) based on its allelic profile. In addition, this study examined the presence of three major virulence-associated genes (epf, mrp, and sly) to characterize the pathogenic potential of the isolates [34].

This study aimed to enhance the understanding of the epidemiology of this important pathogen by providing a comprehensive overview of the genetic and serological diversity of S. suis circulating in Korean swine populations. These findings will be valuable for developing disease surveillance programs, improved diagnostic methods, and effective control strategies to mitigate the impact of S. suis infections in veterinary and public health settings.

METHODS

Ethical statement

The current study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jeonbuk National University (Approved number: NON2024-186).

Isolation and identification

From March 2013 to December 2017 and January 2022 to December 2023, lesion tissue samples from pigs were submitted to the Veterinary Diagnostic Center of Jeonbuk National University as a part of routine diagnostic investigations. The samples were obtained from farms located across various regions in South Korea, not limited to Jeonbuk Province. Seventy-four S. suis isolates were recovered, each from a different pig. In many cases, multiple tissue samples (e.g., lung and brain) were submitted from a single pig, but only one isolate of S. suis per pig was obtained based on the tissue in which the bacterium was cultured.

The tissue from which isolates were recovered included lung (65), brain (4), genital tract (2), joint (1), nasal cavity (1), and pericardium (1). All samples were processed aseptically upon arrival. The samples were cultured on tryptic soy agar at 37°C for 24 h, followed by the re-culturing of single colonies in tryptic soy broth medium for an additional 24 h. Microbial DNA was extracted from the culture supernatant using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Germany), quantified using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer, and stored at −20°C until further confirmation.

Species identity was confirmed by PCR targeting the gdh gene, a housekeeping gene specific to S. suis, using the primers described by Kerdsin et al. [3]: GDH-F (5´-TTCTGCAGCGTATTCTGTCAAACG-3´) and GDH-R (5´-TGTTCCATGGACAGATAAAGATGG-3´), which amplify a 695 bp product.

The 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene sequences were amplified and matched against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Nucleotide database via BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) for subspecies identification. PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene and subsequent sequence alignment with known ribosomal sequences in the NCBI database is a reliable method for taxonomic identification at the genus, species, and subspecies levels [5].

Multiplex PCR for serotyping

The serotypes of S. suis were determined using multiplex PCR [3], segmented into four sets targeting specific serotype groups. The first set contained the primers for serotypes 1 (and 14), 2 (and 1/2), 3, 7, 9, 11, 14, and 16. The second set contained the primers for serotypes 4, 5, 8, 12, 18, 19, 24, and 25. The third set contained the primers for serotypes 6, 10, 13, 15, 17, 23, and 31, and the fourth set contained the primers for serotypes 21, 27, 28, 29, and 30. The PCR products were resolved by gel electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels stained with Redsafe (iNtRON Biotechnology, Korea) and visualized under UV light. The product sizes were compared against a 100 bp Plus DNA Ladder (Bioneer, Korea).

MLST and phylogenetic analysis

MLST was conducted on seven housekeeping genes: a 5-enolpyruvylshikimate 3-phosphate synthase (aroA), a 60 kDa chaperonine (cpn60), a peroxide resistance protein (dpr), a glucose kinase (gki), a DNA mismatch repair protein (mutS), a homologous recombination factor (recA), and an aspartokinase (thrA). The PCR amplification involved 30 cycles, with the annealing temperatures adjusted according to gene-specific requirements: 55°C (aroA and gki), 52°C (cpn and thrA), and 50°C (dpr, mutS, and recA). The amplified PCR products were purified using a QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen, USA) and sequenced at the Macrogen service center, Korea. The sequences were compared with those in the PubMLST database to determine the STs, and the new allele sequences were uploaded [4]. The integrated analysis of serotype, ST, and virulence gene distribution was performed by comparing the individual typing results using spreadsheet-based alignment. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted based on the concatenated nucleotide sequences of the seven MLST housekeeping genes, generated using the MLST database tool (http://ssuis.mlst.net/sql/concatenate/default.asp). The concatenated sequences were used to construct a neighbor-joining tree in MEGA11 software. The robustness of the phylogenetic groupings was evaluated using a bootstrap test with 1,000 replicates.

Virulence-associated genes

The presence of the following virulence genes was assessed using established multiplex PCR protocols: extracellular protein factor (epf), muramidase-released protein (mrp), and suilysin (sly) [67]. The PCR amplification process included an initial denaturation step followed by 35 cycles of temperature-specific denaturation, annealing, and extension phases. The products were electrophoresed on 2% agarose gels in TBE buffer, stained with RedSafe, and analyzed under UV light.

RESULTS

Isolation

Seventy-four isolates underwent 16s rRNA analysis for identification, with BLAST analysis showing 99%–100% identity and 100% query coverage for S. suis in all samples.

Of the 74 S. suis isolates analyzed, 70 were classified into 16 distinct serotypes using multiplex PCR targeting cps genes (Supplementary Table 1). PCR targeting the species-specific gdh gene confirmed that the remaining four isolates were S. suis, but they could not be assigned to any known serotype, likely due to the lack of reactivity with the available serotype-specific primers. MLST

Forty-one STs were identified, including 26 novel STs. New sequences for housekeeping genes were submitted and validated in PubMLST, with the allele profiles registered. ST25 (n = 10) was the most common ST, followed by ST27 (n = 9), ST28 (n = 7), ST87 (n = 3), ST108 (n = 3), and ST373 (n = 3). Fig. 2B shows the main ST groups. ST25 and ST1218 were single-locus variants of ST29, and several other STs were single- or double-locus variants of various groups. Of the 26 newly assigned STs, 17 were classified as singletons, belonging to no known group (Fig. 2A). Of the isolates with newly assigned STs, 68.23% (n = 18) were strains isolated between 2013 and 2015.

Presence of virulence genes

The virulence-related genes epf, mrp, and sly were present in 13.5%, 18.9%, and 41.8% of isolates, respectively (Table 2). The sly gene was detected each year except for 2022, and 10 of the 12 isolates from 2014 carried epf, mrp, and sly. The genotype epf+/mrp+/sly+ was observed in isolates from 2014 (n = 3) and 2017 (n = 1). The most common genotype was epf-/mrp-/sly+ (n = 15, 20.2%), while epf+/mrp−/sly− was not detected in any isolate. Thirty-five isolates exhibited the genotype profile epf−/mrp−/sly− (47.2%).

Serotypes and Sequence Types

The distribution of Sequence Types (STs) across serotypes revealed the diversity of the S. suis strains. The 16 identified serotypes included multiple STs, ranging from two to six per serotype (Fig. 3). The predominant combination was serotype 2 with ST25 (n = 10). The sequence types associated with serotype 2 included ST1, ST28 (n = 7), ST779, and ST781. ST27 was found to be associated with serotypes 3 (n = 5) and 13 (n = 4). Serotype 8 exhibited the highest diversity, with unique STs, including ST87, ST783, ST840, ST844, ST849, and ST1217, of which five were newly identified. Serotype 7 included ST29, ST373 (n = 2), ST839, ST1218, and ST2651, with three newly assigned STs. ST27, ST117, and ST627 associated with serotype 13 were last identified in 2013. The serotype 4 isolates identified in 2017 included ST17 (n = 2), ST485 (n = 2), and ST977. Serotypes 11, 18, 19, 20, 23, and 24 included only one isolate, with each assigned a new ST number.

Associations among serotypes, STs, and the virulence gene profiles

Phylogenetic analysis based on the MLST allele sequences explored the potential associations among the genotype, serotype, and virulence-related genes (Fig. 4). The virulence genotype profile epf+/mrp+/sly+ was found in five isolates, spanning serotypes 2 (ST779), 3 (ST27), 4 (ST17), and 8 (ST87). A serotype 2 isolate from 2023 with ST1 was identified as epf+/mrp+/sly−. Most serotype 3 isolates with ST27 (n = 5) had a virulence profile of epf−/mrp−/sly−. ST373, ST839, and ST2651 isolates (n = 5) from serotype 7 contained the sly gene, except for one strain. The serotype 4 isolates were all sly+, with profiles epf+/mrp+/sly+ (n = 1, 2017) and epf−/mrp+/sly+ (n = 1, 2023). Serotype 8 had six of its eight isolates as sly+, and none were identified post-2017. Two serotype-unspecified isolates with ST94 and ST17 had the virulence profiles of epf−/mrp+/sly+ (n = 1, 2014) and epf+/mrp−/sly+ (n = 1, 2016), respectively.

DISCUSSION

The data presented in this study were obtained from a detailed evaluation of the genetic characteristics of S. suis isolates collected from the lesion tissues of pigs in Korea during the periods 2013–2018 and 2022–2023. The 74 S. suis isolates analyzed in this study were classified into 16 different serotypes and 41 STs. Among these, 26 STs were novel, accounting for more than half of the total STs identified. These serotypes and ST profiles were further analyzed in combination with their respective virulence gene patterns. The diversity in the S. suis serotype distribution has been reported within individual countries, and the temporal changes in serotype prevalence have been described in Canada and the United States [89]. Among the 16 S. suis serotypes identified in this study, serotype 2 (n = 20) was the most common, followed by serotypes 3 (n = 9), 8 (n = 8), and 7 (n = 7). A survey conducted in Korea from 2009 to 2010 revealed serotypes 3 (n = 17, 17.3%) and 2 (n = 15, 15.3%) to be the most prevalent serotypes observed in isolates recovered from diseased pigs [10]. Other domestic surveys conducted from 2010 to 2013 commonly reported serotypes 7 (n = 7, 12.5%) and 2 or 1/2 (n = 6, 10.7%) [11]. The serotyping results of this study are similar to those in previous reports, but clear differences were observed when analyzed by year. Of the 12 strains isolated in 2013, six were identified as serotype 13, which had not been isolated in Korea before or since that year. Serotype 8 was first identified in 2014 and was isolated most frequently in 2015 (n = 4, 33.3%), but only one isolate was detected in 2016 and 2017. A previous study identified serotypes 3 and 4 as commonly occurring among S. suis isolates from cases of porcine polymicrobial enteritis, suggesting that these serotypes may be associated with enteric involvement in some regions [12]. In the present study, serotype 4 was identified in three isolates from lung samples and two from brain samples. These findings suggest that the serotype distribution of S. suis may vary depending on the clinical presentation. Serotype 9, which is the most common serotype in some European countries [13], was identified in only three isolates in the present study. These results support the need for ongoing surveillance of S. suis serotypes in Korea.

A comparison of the MLST results according to the serotype revealed serotype 2 to be primarily associated with ST25 (n = 10) and ST28 (n = 7), along with ST1, ST779, and ST781 (Fig. 3). ST27 was identified in serotype 3 (n = 5) and serotype 13 (n = 4). For serotype 8, six different STs were detected, including ST87, ST783, ST840, ST844, ST849, and ST1217—five of which were newly assigned. Serotype 7 included ST29, ST373 (n = 2), ST839, ST1218, and ST2651, with three novel STs. The serotype 4 isolates from 2017 were classified as ST17 (n = 2), ST485 (n = 2), and ST977. Serotypes 11, 18, 19, 20, 23, and 24 each yielded a single isolate with a unique ST. These findings reflect the genetic diversity of S. suis across serotypes. Consistent with previous studies, ST25 was reported as predominant in Canada, while ST28 was found more frequently in the United States [14].

Oh et al. [10] also identified most serotype 2 isolates as ST25, consistent with the present findings. These STs have been associated with human cases in North America, Hong Kong, and Thailand [15]. ST25 (n = 10), ST27 (n = 9), and ST28 (n = 7) were mostly isolated from pulmonary lesions. ST27 and ST28 of serotype 2 may be associated with respiratory tract infections [16], a finding consistent with studies from China, Japan, and the U.S., but further investigation will be needed to confirm their pathogenicity [1517]. Serotypes 3 and 8 have been previously associated with pneumonia [18]. In the present study, all nine serotype 3 isolates, comprising ST27 (n = 5), ST31, and ST108 (n = 3), were recovered from pulmonary lesions. One serotype 2 isolate was identified as ST1, which was notable considering that ST1 is typically associated with highly virulent strains and potential co-infections [1316]. This isolate was recovered from a brain lesion and harbored the epf+/mrp+/sly− virulence gene profile. Although most isolates in this study were derived from lung samples, S. suis was also recovered from other organs, including the brain, joint, genital tract, nasal cavity, and pericardium. In particular, virulent genotypes, such as epf+/mrp+/sly− and epf+/mrp+/sly+, were identified in the isolates from brain lesions, highlighting the importance of non-pulmonary samples in understanding the pathogenicity of S. suis. Despite the smaller number of such samples, their clinical relevance warrants attention in surveillance and diagnostic efforts.

The classical virulence markers epf, mrp, and sly showed interesting associations with different serotypes and STs. Among the 20 serotype 2 isolates, one strain recovered from a brain lesion was identified as ST1, carrying the virulence gene profile epf+/mrp+/sly−. Previous studies reported that the serotype 2 strains with the epf+/mrp+/sly− genotype can be highly virulent in pigs [151819]. All five isolates of serotype 4 carried virulence genes, with the sly gene detected in all of them. Two of these isolates were identified as ST17, exhibiting either epf+/mrp+/sly+ or epf−/mrp+/sly+ genotypes. These profiles have previously been associated with increased virulence potential in S. suis [13]. These findings are consistent with those of earlier reports, further supporting the notion that serotype 4 ST17 strains may carry enhanced pathogenic characteristics. This reinforces the importance of monitoring less common serotypes, such as serotype 4, particularly when they harbor recognized virulence markers [20].

In conclusion, serotype 2 was the most prevalent serotype among the 74 domestic S. suis isolates in this study. MLST analysis revealed ST25, ST27, and ST28 to be the most common sequence types, with 26 novel STs identified. Three and one epf+/mrp+/sly+ profiles were found in the isolates from 2014 and 2017, respectively. One ST1 strain corresponding to serotype 2 was isolated from a brain lesion and harbored the epf+/mrp+/sly+ profile. Serotype 4 contained the virulence genes in all isolates, and most isolates of serotypes 3, 7, and 8 also carried the virulence markers. On the other hand, even within the same serotype, different STs were identified depending on the year of isolation, indicating variations in the virulence gene profiles.

The virulence profiling in this study was limited to only three well-characterized virulence-associated genes (epf, mrp, and sly), which may not fully reflect the overall virulence potential of the isolates. This limitation should be addressed in future studies incorporating broader genomic or transcriptomic analyses.

This study confirmed the genetic diversity of S. suis found in the lesions of domestic pigs, but a consistent correlation between the serotypes and sequence types over time could not be established. Nevertheless, continuous and broad surveillance of S. suis serotypes, virulence-associated genotypes, and sequence types will be needed to generate up-to-date information and guide effective clinical management and control of S. suis infections in pigs.
