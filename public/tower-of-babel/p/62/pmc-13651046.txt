ACL injury reprograms quadriceps myonuclear epigenetic and transcriptional signatures in mice
Authors: Nicholas T. Thomas, Camille R. Brightwell, Thomas Carter, Alexander R. Keeble, Sara Gonzalez-Velez, Christine M. Latham, Allison M. Owen, Yuan Wen, Kevin A. Murach, Christopher S. Fry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundACL injury is a debilitating sport-related injury that induces prolonged deficits in muscle size and strength. Using a time-course of in vivo fluorescent metabolic labeling of nascent RNA in an ACL injury mouse model, we identified upregulated myonuclear transcriptional output early following the injury.MethodsTo define altered myonuclear transcription, we bred HSArtTA/rtTA:TetO-H2B-GFP mice to allow for stable in vivo fluorescent labeling and sorting of resident (non-satellite cell-derived) myonuclei 3-days post ACL transection surgery. We performed myonucleus-specific RNA sequencing (RNAseq) and reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) to capture altered myonuclear transcriptional and epigenetic signatures following ACL injury.ResultsIntegration of these datasets revealed transcriptional reprogramming of quadriceps myonuclei following ACL injury toward upregulation of fibrosis related genes and downregulation of metabolism related genes. When integrating bulk tissue RRBS with bulk tissue RNAseq, we demonstrate a similar, but more robust pattern of transcriptional reprogramming, suggesting the altered epigenetic landscape following ACL injury extends beyond myonuclei.ConclusionsOur results support a model of epigenetically-encoded muscle maladaptation following orthopedic injury, with implications for understanding a “muscle memory” of persistent dysfunction.

Background

Musculoskeletal injuries represent a significant source of disability (Murray et al. 2013; Wallace et al. 2017), and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are among the most common within the United States (Griffin et al. 2006; McLean and Beaulieu 2010). Persistent muscle atrophy and weakness limit functional recovery following ACL injury and strongly predict poor patient outcomes (Wyngaarden et al. 2021). Recent work underscores quadriceps myocellular pathology that likely antagonizes recovery (Fry et al. 2016; Peck et al. 2019; Brightwell et al. 2023; Latham et al. 2024; Noehren et al. 2016; Tourville et al. 2022; Mendias et al. 2013). Myonuclear methylation status is a critical regulatory step that strongly influences muscle gene expression (Turner et al. 2026; Ismaeel et al. 2023; 2024; Cabrera et al. 2025). Epigenetic modifications precede phenotypic adaptation and are responsive to loading cues within muscle (2024; Seaborne et al. 2018; Turner et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2021; Dungan et al. 2022). While existing work in skeletal muscle epigenetics has been on exercise training responses, emerging evidence suggests epigenetic modifications also accompany muscle pathology (Turner et al. 2026; Cabrera et al. 2025). Despite the persistent nature of ACL injury-induced muscle deficits, the epigenetic response to the injury is unknown and could support the development of novel therapeutic interventions targeting injury-induced modifications.

The purpose was to define the myonuclear epigenetic and transcriptomic responses to ACL injury. Our pre-clinical ACL transection rodent model enabled time course detection of the myonuclear transcriptional response using in-vivo fluorescent metabolic labeling of nascent transcripts. We then assessed concurrent resident myonuclear DNA methylation and transcription. Our multi-omic integration analysis revealed a rapidly-emergent epigenetic signature in myonuclei induced by ACL injury. Furthermore, these modifications conferred gene-level changes within critical functional properties of skeletal muscle that could directly limit functional recovery.

Methods

Mouse model and ethical approval

Animal care and experimentation was administered under the University of Kentucky Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #2019–3241). All mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room on a 14:10 light:dark cycle and provided chow and water ad libitum. C57BL/6 J mice (Jax #000664; 4–5 months) were allocated to quadriceps muscle tissue collection 1-day (n = 4; 2 M/2F), 3-days (n = 7; 3 M/4F), 7-days (n = 7; 4 M/3F), or 10-days (n = 4; 2 M/2F) following ACL Transection (ACLT). Following ACLT, all mice were treated with 20 mg/mL 5-ethynyl-2’-uridine (EU, #E10345, Thermo Scientific) 5 h before tissue collection as previously reported (Kirby et al. 2016). Quadriceps muscles were collected for immunohistochemical analysis.

HSArtTA/rtTA:TetO-H2B-GFP mice (N = 12; 8 M/4F) were created by crossing HSArtTA/rtTA mice (Iwata et al. 2018) with mice harboring a histone core H2B-GFP fusion gene (H2B-GFP) under transcriptional control of a tetracycline response element (TRE; Jax #005104) (Tumbar et al. 2004). This genetic strategy allows for doxycycline-inducible expression of H2B-GFP, enabling highly specific and stable fluorescent labeling of myonuclei (Borowik et al. 2022; Mobley et al. 2020). Mice were studied between 4 and 6 months of age. Mice were treated with doxycycline (#D9891, Sigma Aldrich; 0.5 mg/mL in 2% sucrose enriched drinking water) for five days followed by a seven-day washout period prior to ACLT. Quadriceps muscles were collected from HSArtTA/rtTA:TetO-H2B-GFP mice 3-days post-ACLT for downstream DNA and RNA isolation. Tissue allocated for immunohistochemistry was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 4 h, equilibrated in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C, embedded in OCT, and frozen in nitrogen-chilled 2-methylbutane. Remaining tissue in the 3-day post-ACLT cohort was allocated for fluorescence activated nuclear sorting (FANS).

ACL transection (ACLT) surgery

Surgical ACLT was performed on a single limb for all mice, with the contralateral hind limb serving as an internal healthy control, similar to our prior work (Brightwell et al. 2023; Keeble et al. 2023). Briefly, anesthetized mice had their surgical limb shaved and prepared with povidone-iodide scrub. A 3 mm longitudinal incision was made over the distal patella to proximal tibial plateau. The joint capsule was opened by incision. The infrapatellar fat pad over the intercondylar area was dissected and the patellar tendon gently dislocated laterally to expose the femoral-tibial joint, allowing visualization of the ACL. The ACL was completely transected via scalpel under a stereo dissecting microscope. Damage to the posterior cruciate ligament was avoided. The incision was closed with a suture, and the mouse was placed in a clean cage over a heat pad and monitored for recovery. Transection was confirmed with a positive anterior drawer. Mice were monitored regularly throughout the post-surgical time course for signs of pain and inflammation and to confirm proper healing of the incision site.

Immunohistochemistry

7 µm thick sections were cut on a cryostat (HM525 NX, ThermoFisher) and mounted on glass slides. For laminin-EU-DAPI staining, tissue sections were incubated in 4% PFA for seven minutes prior to overnight incubation in rabbit anti-laminin primary antibody (1:100 in PBS, Sigma, cat: L9393). EU staining was performed using Click-It chemistry protocol according to manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher). Sections were then incubated in 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 10 nM, #D3571, LifeTechnologies).

For identification of GFP + myonuclei, unfixed sections were blocked in 1% bovine serum albumin and 10% mouse on mouse blocking reagent, and then incubated overnight in pericentriolar material 1 (PCM1, 1:100, # HPA023370, Sigma) and dystrophin (#VPD505, 1:50, Vector Labs) primary antibodies. The next day, slides were incubated for one hour in secondary antibodies diluted 1:500 in PBS: goat anti-mouse IgG1 AF647 (#A21240, LifeTechnologies) and goat anti-rabbit AF555 (#A21428, LifeTechnologies). Lastly, sections were incubated for 10 min DAPI, washed in PBS, and mounted using Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, #H-1000, Vector).

RNA fluorescent in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH)

Postn and Lox mRNA in-situ localization 7 d post-ACLT was determined with RNA fluorescent in situ hybridization as previously described (Toth et al. 2025; Keeble et al. 2026). Seven µm thick sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, ethanol dehydrated (50%, 70%, 100% for 5 min each) and incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide for ten minutes to quench endogenous peroxidases (ACDBio, Newark, CA). Antigen retrieval was performed using Protease IV (#322,336, ACDBio). Target mRNA were hybridized with customized mouse Postn (#418,581-C1, ACDBio) and Lox probes (#425,311-C3, ACDBio) which were detected using the TSA vivid 570 (#323,272, ACDBio) and 650 (#323,273, ACDBio) fluorophores, respectively. Fluorescent signals were quenched using an HRP blocker. Samples were incubated overnight in a laminin primary antibody (#L9393, Sigma) followed by incubation goat anti-rabbit AF488 (#A-11034, ThermoFisher) for one hour and co-staining with DAPI. Slides were mounted with fluorescent mounting media.

Image acquisition and analysis

Immunofluorescence was visualized at 10 × or 20 × magnification (Plan-Apochromat 10x, NA: 0.45, air; Plan-Apochromat 20x, NA: 0.8, air) using a Zeiss M2 Axioimager with an automatic stage (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Image analysis for EU intensity and RNA-FISH was performed by a single assessor (TC or SGV) using Zeiss Zen software (v3.1), blinded to group allocation and injury status of the muscle. For EU analysis, an automated script in Zen was created to identify all DAPI + nuclei followed by manual annotation of myonuclei (identified as having > 50% of the nucleus area residing within the laminin border (Bruusgaard and Gundersen 2008)). Integrated myonuclear EU intensity was captured from manual annotation (n = 30 myonuclei). Postn and Lox RNA-FISH signal were assessed as mean fluorescent signal intensity within a region of interest (ROI) encompassing myonuclei (DAPI + nuclei residing completely within the laminin border) or Postn +/DAPI + interstitial cells (n = 30 myonuclei and n = 30 interstitial cells per limb per animal). Fluorescent signal intensity for Postn and Lox are presented as an average value for both myonuclei and interstitial cells for each animal. Myofiber cross-sectional area (CSA) was determined via MyoVision (Wen et al. 2018).

Myonuclear isolation and fluorescence activated cell (Nucleus) sorting for low-input sequencing

HSA-rtTA+/-;H2B-GFP+/- (HSA-GFP) mice (Iwata et al. 2018; Dungan et al. 2022; Wen et al. 2021) underwent an overnight fast at 3-days post-ACLT. Myonuclei from the ACLT and healthy contralateral quadriceps muscles were processed via Dounce homogenization in a physiological buffer (5 mM PIPES, 85 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 5% sucrose, 0.5 × HALT Protease Inhibitor [ThermoFisher], 0.5% NP-40), purified via fluorescent activated myonuclear (GFP +/DAPI +) sorting (Walden et al. 2020; Koopmans et al. 2026). Immediately prior to fluorescent activated myonuclear sorting, samples were filtered through a 40 µm filter (Fisher Scientific), DAPI was added as a viability marker (4 µg/ml), then myonuclei were sorted directly into TRIzol LS Reagent (ThermoFisher), omitting any centrifugation steps to maximize myonuclear yield, as previously described (Murach et al. 2022). Myonuclear RNA was extracted using the Zymo Direct-zol RNA Microprep Kit (#R2061, Irvine, CA) in accordance with manufacturer guidelines (RNA integrity number [RIN] > 6.0). RNA content and integrity were quantified using the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) at the University of Kentucky Genomics Core. RNA samples were pooled (n = 3 mouse muscles/pooled sample for both the ACLT and Healthy limb; each pool was comprised of the following sexes: 3 M, 2 M/1F, 2 M/1F) to maximize RNA yield (Murach et al. 2022). This resulted in n = 3 ACLT and n = 3 Healthy distinct pooled samples (e.g. n = 3 biological replicates) that were sequenced. RNA pools are similar to averages of individuals that comprise the pool (Kendziorski et al. 2003).

Three hundred nanograms of total RNA per pooled sample was sent to Novogene Corporation for low-input (rRNA removal) library construction and sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 using a paired-end 150–base pair dual-indexing protocol. Raw FASTQ files underwent prealignment quality control, aligned to GRCm38/mm10 using STAR, and count quantification with HTSeq. After gene filtering (> 50 counts), DESeq2 was used for differential expression testing (Dungan et al. 2022; Brightwell et al. 2021). EnrichR was used for pathway enrichment analysis (Kuleshov et al. 2016). RNA-sequencing data are deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE211682. Additionally, bulk mouse muscle RNA-seq following ACLT was analyzed; raw count matrices from ACLT and Healthy limbs were obtained from GSE230708 (Brightwell et al. 2023).

DNA isolation and reduced representation bisulfite sequencing

For myonuclear DNA isolation at 3-days post-ACLT (N = 3; 1 M/2F), GFP +/DAPI + myonuclei were sorted directly into buffer ATL plus proteinase K per manufacturer instructions (DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit #69,504, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and myonuclear DNA was isolated according to manufacturer’s protocol. Concurrently, interstitial nuclei (GFP-/DAPI +) were also sorted and DNA isolated similar to myonuclear DNA. Approximately 3 µg of genomic DNA (myonuclear and interstitial) was sent to Zymo Research (Tustin, CA) for bisulfite conversion and Reduced Representation Bisulfite Sequencing (RRBS). Quality control and adapter trimming were performed using FastQC and Cutadapt, within the Trim Galore wrapper and base read Phred scores < 20 were removed. FASTQ files were to the bisulfite-converted GRCm39 genome using Bismark aligner. Coverage files were used for data analysis in the methylKit R package (Akalin et al. 2012). Samples were pooled into their respective groups to minimize sparsity with site coverage across a minimum of 1 sample per group and > 10 reads per site per sample required for site inclusion in analysis. Pooled groups were independently compared to Healthy limb samples using Fisher’s Exact Test with sliding linear model adjustment for false discovery (SLIM) (Wang et al. 2011). Annotation for intragenic regions was performed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information mm39.bed file.

Binding and expression target analysis

We applied Binding and Expression Target Analysis (BETA) (Wang et al. 2013) to integrate DNA methylation and RNA-sequencing data from subject-matched muscle samples as we have done previously (Ismaeel et al. 2023).

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± SD with individual data points overlaid on bar charts. No data points were excluded from any sample, and there were no missing data. For EU intensity, RNA-FISH, and fiber CSA, paired-sample t-tests were performed between ACLT and Healthy limbs to compare each dependent variable, with significance set at P < 0.05. Comparisons between time points were not performed as our primary purpose was to define limb-specific differences that occur following ACLT. Data met assumptions for parametric statistical testing: approximate normal distribution and equal variance. All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.2.0.

Results and discussion

Following ACL injury, the myocellular molecular response precedes prolonged functional deficits and poor recovery (Fry et al. 2016; Peck et al. 2019; Brightwell et al. 2023; Latham et al. 2024; Noehren et al. 2016). The rapidity of ACL injury-induced gene expression changes is not established. Insight into this time course could enable more timely intervention. To determine the myocellular etiology of deficits following ACL injury, we treated C57BL/6 J mice with 5-ethynyl-uridine (EU) 5 h before tissue collection at 1-, 3-, 7-, or 10-days post-ACLT (Fig. 1A). ACLT increased quadriceps myonuclear EU fluorescent intensity (transcriptional output) at 3- and 7-days post-ACLT versus Healthy, preceding fiber atrophy at 7- and 10-days post-ACLT (Fig. 1B-E). We validated myonuclear specificity of RNA sequencing data using markers for FAPs and immune cells, showing low normalized transcript abundance for non-myogenic biomarker genes (Fig. 2A). We observed distinct cartesian spatial separation of Healthy and ACLT limb muscle samples across principal component 2 of myonucleus specific RNA-sequencing which revealed differential expression of genes primarily associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling (Postn, Lox, Col3a1, Col1a1; Fig. 2B-G) and downregulation of genes critical for skeletal muscle metabolism (Slc2a4/Glut4, Phkg1/phosphorylase kinase, Pfkm/phosphofructokinase, and Eno3/skeletal muscle-specific enolase; Fig. 2H-L). Interestingly, the elevated expression of ECM components shows similarity to acute myonuclear adaptation to mechanical loading (Murach et al. 2022), suggesting a shared signature despite disparate loading patterns. We performed RNA-FISH for Postn and Lox which showed elevated myonuclear abundance in the ACLT limb for both transcripts (Fig. 3A-C), consistent in magnitude as gene counts in our myonucleus specific RNA-sequencing (Fig. 2D-E). ACL injury-responsive modifications to the myonuclear DNA methylome included a bias toward hypomethylation in the ACLT limb across promoter-proximal, exon-resident, and intron-resident sites as compared to hypermethylation (Fig. 4A-D). We observed notable promoter-proximal hypomethylation of several genes including Mycbp2 (Fig. 4E), an upstream effector of synaptic remodeling (Babetto et al. 2013; Xiong et al. 2012; Desbois et al. 2018), a key feature of ACL injury. MYCBP2 upregulation is also associated with muscle atrophy in humans following spinal cord injury (Petrie et al. 2014), and we posit that transcriptionally permissive promoter-proximal hypomethylation of Mycbp2 early post-injury may contribute to neurogenic muscle atrophy at later post-injury timepoints. We also observed promoter-proximal hypomethylation of Stat3 (Fig. 4F) and Ago2 (Fig. 4G), which may increase transcription via Stat3 (Trenerry et al. 2007; Tierney et al. 2014; Bonetto et al. 2011) and translational control of nascent transcripts via Ago2-RISC mediated miRNA activity (MacRae et al. 2008). In addition to hypomethylation, we also observed transcriptionally repressive promoter-proximal hypermethylation of several genes that may underlie poor recovery following ACL injury. Hypermethylated genes included the nuclear encoded mitochondrial DNA polymerase, Polg which maintains mitochondrial genomic stability (Fig. 4J) along with genes associated with mechanosensitivity (Piezo1, Fig. 4K), myofibrillar contractile machinery (Actn3, Fig. 4L; Myh7, Fig. 4M) and atrophy resistance (Klf13, Fig. 4N) (Yang et al. 2024). Multi-omics integration (BETA) revealed activation of a myonuclear ECM remodeling process that may underlie deficits in muscle quality. Prior work from our lab and others has shown an accumulation of collagenous ECM components after knee injury (Fry et al. 2016; Peck et al. 2019; Brightwell et al. 2023; Noehren et al. 2016; Mendias et al. 2013; Keeble et al. 2024; Wurtzel et al. 2017), and BETA integration revealed a myonuclear DNA methylation-dependent elevated expression of fibrosis-related genes (Fig. 5A-C). BETA-identified sites were generally hypomethylated compared to Healthy with some notable hypermethylation (Fig. 5B). Specifically, fibrillar collagens Col1a1 and Col3a1, as well as Tgfbi were BETA-identified myonuclear DNA methylation gene targets. Concomitantly, metabolism genes like Pfkm, Pgam2, and Eno3 were hypermethylated and transcriptionally downregulated (Fig. 5C). Integration of DNA methylation from interstitial and myonuclear DNA 3-days post-ACLT with a bulk muscle transcriptome dataset at 7-days post-ACLT identified a similarly themed but heightened fibrotic transcriptional response to ACL injury (Fig. 5D). Similar to myonucleus-specific DNA methylation, there was an overrepresentation of hypomethylated genes in the ACLT limb (Fig. 5E). The gene target bias of hypomethylation was also oriented toward fibrosis-related genes (Fig. 5F), which shows minimal responsiveness to current treatment strategies (Brightwell et al. 2023; Erickson et al. 2025). Recent evidence supports an active role for muscle fibers in ECM remodeling during muscle adaptation (Murach et al. 2022; Petrosino et al. 2022; Noviello et al. 2022). Myonuclei have an underappreciated role governing ECM adaptation (Brightwell et al. 2022), and our results refine our understanding of ECM dysregulation accompanying ACL injury. Interestingly, downregulation of metabolism-related genes appears to be restricted to myonuclei. Our study is not without limitations. While guided by a discovery time course, our data are cross-sectional, limiting direct illustration of the permanence of DNA methylation changes. Additionally, our surgical ACL injury model may influence muscle adaptation, though prior work from our lab supports minimal influence of a sham surgery on muscle phenotype (Brightwell et al. 2023; Keeble et al. 2023), and we report strong congruency between our rodent model and patient tissue samples (Brightwell et al. 2023; Latham et al. 2024; Keeble et al. 2023). Finally, while our reported DNA methylation changes were largely inversely associated with gene expression in key pathways, the functional consequences of these epigenetic modifications remain to be fully defined.

Conclusion

Our data show elevated myonuclear transcriptional output that is supported by a broadly hypomethylated genomic signature that precedes muscle atrophy following ACL injury. Critical processes influenced by alterations in myonuclear DNA methylation include 1) a fibrotic transcriptional program and 2) a downregulation of contractile- and metabolism-related genes, all of which likely contribute to phenotypic deficits in patient muscle (Brightwell et al. 2023; Latham et al. 2024; Tourville et al. 2022). Given recent support for an epigenetically-encoded ‘memory’ of disuse within skeletal muscle (Turner et al. 2026), our results provide evidence for a novel model of protracted muscle recovery following orthopaedic injury. Skeletal muscle fibrotic transformation and a decline in metabolic flexibility are common pathological features that extend beyond ACL injury (Alnaqeeb et al. 1984; Tezze et al. 2017; Rebolledo et al. 2019; Langer et al. 2020). Here, we show in ACL injury that elevated myonuclear expression of fibrosis genes and downregulation of metabolic genes are epigenetically driven in direct response to the injury. This provides targeted evidence for therapeutic strategies such as directed exercise which may alleviate the imposed epigenetic signature of pathology (Voisin et al. 2024).
