Impact of sex differences on revascularization and long-term clinical outcomes in patients with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction: a multicentre study from China
Authors: Chongyou Rao, Qin Zhong, Wuhong Zhou, Zongren Li, Liqiang Fu, Wei Liu, Wei Qin, Jixian Gao, Bo Liu, Kunlun He, Feihu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe influence of sex differences on the clinical prognosis of patients with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) remains controversial. This multicenter, retrospective study analyzed NSTEMI patients from five institutions. The primary endpoints were major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and major adverse cardiac events (MACE). Multivariate logistic regression was used to assess sex differences in revascularization. Multivariate Cox regression with stepwise selection identified independent prognostic predictors. Stratified analyses were performed by revascularization status. To strengthen the analytical robustness, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed as a sensitivity analysis, balancing age, comorbidities, and key treatment factors. A total of 4,911 NSTEMI patients (3,506 men; 1,405 women) completed follow-up. Women were less likely to undergo revascularization (adjusted odds ratio 0.68, 95%CI 0.58–0.79). Over a median follow-up of 527 days, women had significantly lower cumulative survival free from MACCE and MACE (both P < 0.001). After multivariate adjustment, women remained to have an increased risk of MACCE (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.03–1.40) and MACE (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.09–1.55) in the overall cohort, particularly among those not undergoing revascularization (MACCE: HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.05–1.60; MACE: HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.27–2.10). However, no significant sex-based differences were observed in the revascularized subgroup (P > 0.05). These findings were consistent in the PSM-based sensitivity analysis. The sex-based prognosis difference in NSTEMI is linked to underutilization of revascularization in women. Introduction

In recent years, the mortality rate of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) has significantly decreased with the widespread application of novel antiplatelet agents, next-generation drug-eluting stents, and reperfusion therapy strategies1,2. However, previous studies, including meta-analyses, have shown that the in-hospital mortality rate of women with myocardial infarction is significantly higher than that of men.3,4 Female sex has been identified as an independent risk factor for an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) within 5 years after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI),5 and this sex-based prognostic disparity persists even after controlling for confounding factors, such as patient age and comorbidities.3–5 Compared with men, women with AMI appear to derive less benefit from current therapeutic management strategies.6,7

Previous studies have demonstrated significant disparities between women and men in demographic characteristics, comorbidity profiles, clinical symptoms, and AMI treatment strategies, partly owing to differences in baseline physiological hormone levels.8–12 However, it is controversial whether poorer outcomes in women with AMI are due to sex itself or are attributable to their typically older age, higher comorbidity burden, and disparities in treatment compared to men. While some investigations indicated that women face a higher risk of an adverse prognosis than men,3–5 others reported no significant sex-based difference in short- or long-term mortality after adjusting for baseline cardiovascular risk factors and clinical variations.13,14 Conversely, among patients with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), evidence suggested that risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality is lower in women than in men.15 Furthermore, women with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) have demonstrated a more favorable long-term prognosis than men16.

Therefore, the impact of sex differences on the clinical prognosis of patients with AMI remains controversial. Most previous studies have focused on the overall AMI population or patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), with limited studies involving patients with NSTEMI. Furthermore, the assessment of clinical outcomes has primarily centred on adverse events during hospitalization, while investigations into long-term prognosis are still scarce. Therefore, this multicentre study aims to evaluate the impact of sex differences on revascularization and long-term clinical prognosis in patients with NSTEMI based on real-world clinical data. The primary objective is to generate evidence that informs more precise and effective long-term clinical management of women with NSTEMI.

Methods

Study population

This study involved retrospective screening of patients who visited the Chinese PLA General Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical University, Cangzhou People’s Hospital, Xuzhou Central Hospital, and Yuncheng Central Hospital from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2022.

The primary inclusion criteria were patients aged > 18 years with a discharge diagnosis of NSTEMI. Patients who died during hospitalization, with end-stage renal failure requiring long-term renal replacement therapy, or with a history of active malignancy were excluded. This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2023-107–01). As this was a retrospective study and patient-identifying information was anonymized during data analysis, the Chinese PLA General Hospital Ethics Committee waived the requirement for written informed consent.

Procedural characteristics and definitions

The patients were administered oral loading doses of aspirin (300 mg) combined with clopidogrel (300–600 mg) or ticagrelor (180 mg) before coronary angiography (CAG) examination. Myocardial infarction with no obstructive coronary artery disease (MINOCA) was defined as 1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for AMI; 2) coronary stenosis of major epicardial vessels < 50%; and 3) no other clinically apparent specific cause that could serve as an alternative aetiology for AMI.17 Acute coronary occlusion (ACO) was defined as occlusion involving at least one of the major coronary arteries or their significant branches in the culprit vessel, accompanied by a Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grade of 0. Chronic total occlusion (CTO) was defined as a TIMI flow grade of 0 with an estimated duration of vessel occlusion exceeding 3 months. The primary difference between CTO and ACO lesions on angiography is that CTO lesions lack acute thrombosis, acute plaque rupture, or plaque erosion, and they usually have an ipsilateral or contralateral collateral circulation at the lesion site.18Multivessel disease was defined as stenosis ≥ 70% in two or more major epicardial vessels or stenosis ≥ 50% in the left main coronary artery.19 Coronary artery stenosis > 70% on CAG was considered an indication for revascularization, and the choice of revascularization procedure was determined by experienced interventional cardiologists and/or cardiac surgeons. Patients who did not meet the criteria for revascularization or did not undergo revascularization received guideline-directed optimal medical therapy in accordance with contemporary international guidelines17. This regimen consistently included: dual antiplatelet therapy, statins, beta-blockers, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers. Emergency revascularization was defined as revascularization within 2 h of presentation, while early revascularization was defined as revascularization within 24 h of presentation. Complete revascularization was defined as treating severe stenosis in each major epicardial vessel and its major branch vessels with a diameter of ≥ 2.0 mm in multivessel disease (left main coronary artery stenosis > 50%, other major epicardial vessel stenosis > 70%).19.

Study endpoints and definitions

The primary endpoints were major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE; defined as a composite of all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and unplanned repeat revascularization [URR]) and MACE (defined as a composite of cardiac death, recurrent myocardial infarction, heart failure, and URR).

The secondary endpoints were all-cause death and cardiac death. Cardiac death was defined as any death attributable to cardiac causes, including acute myocardial infarction, fatal arrhythmias, decompensated heart failure, or unwitnessed sudden death without a clear non-cardiac cause. All events were evaluated through a standardized process involving a detailed review of medical records, death certificates, and autopsy reports (if applicable) to confirm the underlying cause of death. Recurrent myocardial infarction was defined as the occurrence of STEMI or NSTEMI during follow-up.20 Heart failure was defined as resting dyspnoea with progressive fluid retention requiring oral or intravenous diuretic therapy. Stroke was defined as haemorrhagic or ischaemic stroke confirmed by imaging and assessed by a neurologist. URR was defined as revascularization, either PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) of any coronary vessel with significant stenosis, driven by symptoms of myocardial ischaemia.21 Follow-up was completed between April 2023 and September 2023. Loss to follow-up was defined as failure to contact the patient or their family members on three separate occasions on different days, and the inability to access the patient’s medical records.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as percentages. The Mann–Whitney U test or t-test was used to compare the continuous variables between the groups, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the categorical variables. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences in cumulative survival rates between the groups were identified using the log-rank test. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate sex differences in revascularization. Multivariate Cox regression models were used to assess the association between sex and adverse outcomes after discharge, while controlling for key clinical characteristics. To evaluate whether revascularization influenced the association between sex and adverse outcomes, stratified analyses were conducted according to revascularization status. As a sensitivity analysis, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to strengthen the robustness of the findings. The matching covariates included age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, prior revascularization, current smoking, the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score, Killip class, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), serum albumin, TIMI class, urgent revascularization, in-hospital revascularization, in-hospital major bleeding event, and discharge medication (ticagrelor, nitrate, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEI/ARBs), diuretics, and β-blocker). A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width of 0.2 was applied. The balance between sexes was assessed using standardized mean differences. Variables with > 20% missing values were excluded, and for those with < 20% missing values, data imputation was performed using the “mice” package of R software. The proportion of missing data for each covariate was presented in Supplemental Table 1. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.1.3), and a two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, 5,583 consecutive patients with NSTEMI who attended any of five medical institutions met the inclusion criteria for this study (Chinese PLA General Hospital: n = 767; Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical University: n = 893; Cangzhou People’s Hospital: n = 1,371; Xuzhou Central Hospital: n = 1,338; Yuncheng Central Hospital: n = 1,214). After excluding 60 patients with end-stage renal failure, 178 with in-hospital death, 35 with active cancer, and 399 who were lost to follow-up, a total of 4,911 patients with NSTEMI were included in the final analysis, among whom 3,506 were men and 1,405 were women (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart. NSTEMI: non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, women were older (median age 70 vs. 63 years) and had a higher likelihood of having hypertension (69.4% vs. 56.4%), diabetes mellitus (43.3% vs. 31.1%), and stroke (19.9% vs. 16.3%). Conversely, women were less likely to smoke (7.8% vs. 51.5%) or to have dyslipidaemia (45.2% vs. 56.2%) or prior myocardial infarction (9.7% vs. 12.5%). Additionally, women had higher eGFR (89.5 vs. 74.2 mL/min/1.73 m2), total cholesterol (4.38 vs. 4.06 mmol/L), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (1.13 vs. 0.98 mmol/L) values, but lower LVEF (55% vs. 57%) and higher GRACE score (147 vs. 131). Despite being significantly less likely to undergo CAG (76.9% vs. 86.2%) and revascularization (60.1% vs. 70.2%) during hospitalization, women who did receive CAG had a higher prevalence of multivessel disease than men (66% vs. 58.4%). In terms of medication use at discharge, compared with men, women were less likely to receive aspirin (91.5% vs. 95.2%), ticagrelor (33.6% vs. 43.0%), and ACEI/ARBs (42.0% vs. 48.3%). Still, they were more likely to receive clopidogrel (60.6% vs. 54.4%) and diuretics (27.5% vs. 17.4%). Consistent with these baseline disparities, women demonstrated significantly worse unadjusted clinical outcomes, with higher rates of MACCE, MACE, all-cause death, and cardiac death (all P < 0.001). Following PSM, baseline characteristics—including age, comorbidities, procedural features, and discharge medications—were well-balanced between the matched cohorts of women and men. Importantly, in this balanced cohort, the risks of MACCE and MACE remained significantly higher in women than in men (both P < 0.05). In the revascularization subgroup, several sex-specific differences in procedural characteristics and disease severity were observed, in addition to the baseline disparities described in the overall cohort (Table 3). Women had a more complex coronary disease profile, with a lower prevalence of single-vessel disease (28.6% vs. 37.7%) and a higher prevalence of multivessel disease (70.9% vs. 61.8%) compared to men. They were also less likely to undergo urgent revascularization (15.2% vs. 19.8%) and experienced a longer median time from admission to revascularization therapy (1.97 vs. 1.65 days). Discharge medication patterns also differed in this subgroup, with women more frequently prescribed clopidogrel and β-blockers. In the non-revascularization subgroup, the sex-based differences in baseline characteristics were less pronounced than in the overall cohort (Table 4). Following PSM in both the revascularization and non-revascularization subgroups, baseline characteristics were effectively balanced between women and men. Despite this balance, the risks of MACCE and MACE remained elevated in women compared with men in the non-revascularization subgroup (both P < 0.05; Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

Through retrospective analysis of multicenter real-world data, this study investigated the influence of sex differences on long-term clinical outcomes in NSTEMI patients within contemporary practice. After adjustment for potential confounders, our findings revealed that despite being older, having more comorbidities, and receiving fewer revascularizations and suboptimal medical therapy compared to men, women with NSTEMI demonstrated a significantly higher long-term risk of combined ischemic events, particularly among those who did not undergo revascularization. However, among patients who underwent revascularization, the sex-based difference in long-term adverse clinical outcomes was no longer statistically significant. These findings suggest that a more equitable application of revascularization may represent a feasible strategy to mitigate the sex-based disparity in long-term outcomes among patients with NSTEMI.

Sex differences in cardiovascular disease have been an area of research interest in recent decades. Numerous studies3–5,8–11,13–15,19 have shown that compared with men, women tend to be older, less likely to smoke, and more likely to have complications, such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Women with AMI typically exhibit more atypical symptoms, with delays in symptom recognition and presentation. Consistent with previous research, the median age of women in the present study was approximately 7 years older than men, with a higher likelihood of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and a higher GRACE risk score than men. This significant sex difference persisted in the revascularization and non-revascularization subgroups. The differences in these clinical characteristics may be explained as follows. First, oestrogens exert a cardioprotective effect in young women, necessitating a higher burden of cardiovascular disease risk factors to induce AMI in this population.22 Second, the decline in oestrogen levels during menopause leads to endothelial dysfunction and lipid deposition in the blood vessels, thereby increasing the risk of AMI, resulting in an older age at AMI presentation in women.23.

In addition to the significant differences between the clinical characteristics of men and women, previous studies have shown that women are less likely to receive clinical guideline-recommended therapeutic agents and invasive interventions, such as CAG, PCI, or CABG.10–12 The reasons for this disparity may be various. First, clinicians’ treatment selection bias could be a significant factor, as the known higher incidence of procedure-related complications (such as contrast-induced nephropathy or vascular complications) may mean that the risks of revascularization in women outweigh the potential benefits.24,25 Second, analyses from early randomized controlled trials indicated that routine early invasive strategies were associated with a reduced risk of death or non-fatal myocardial infarction in men but not women.6,7 Additionally, the higher incidence of MINOCA in women with NSTEMI may contribute to the lower rate of PCI.15 Finally, although invasive revascularization is generally beneficial, some studies have shown that women have a higher risk of bleeding-related complications.24,26 However, it is important to note that demographic characteristics and comorbidities do not fully explain the lower rate of cardiovascular surgery in women compared with men.27,28 Moreover, the higher incidence of MINOCA in women does not explain the lower incidence of CAG examination, as CAG examination is a prerequisite for visualization of the coronary anatomy. If patient-related factors are not the primary drivers of the difference in revascularization rate, it is crucial to consider other potential causes. However, stepwise risk adjustment for confounding factors, including race, socioeconomic status, and hospital characteristics, did not significantly change the association between sex and risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality.15 The analysis of socioeconomic and health system factors versus the choice of invasive strategies for ischaemic heart disease in China similarly showed no significant treatment disparities between men and women.29 The specific reasons for the differences in revascularization treatment choices between men and women remain unclear. Although recent observational studies from single countries have indicated a narrowing gap in the use of invasive treatment strategies between men and women in recent years,27,28 after adjusting for confounding factors, such as age and comorbidities, women were still significantly less likely to receive revascularization treatment than men (adjusted OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.58–0.79), which is consistent with previous cross-national research findings.30 Further research is needed to evaluate how to reduce this disparity.

Previous studies evaluating the impact of sex differences on the clinical outcomes of patients with coronary heart disease have yielded conflicting results. An analysis of 16,861 patients with NSTEMI showed that women had a higher unadjusted in-hospital mortality rate than men (4.8% vs. 3.9%, P < 0.001), and this difference persisted after controlling for age, comorbidities, and hospitalization factors.3 A meta-analysis incorporating 21 randomized PCI trials found that women had a significantly increased risk of MACE at 5 years after PCI (adjusted HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.30).4 A study of more than 6.5 million PCI discharges in the United States demonstrated that even after adjusting for a range of confounding factors, including age and comorbidities, differences in in-hospital mortality and major in-hospital complications between men and women persisted.5 However, another meta-analysis incorporating 30 studies found that, after adjusting for baseline cardiovascular risk factors and clinical differences, no significant sex differences in short-term and long-term mortality were observed between men and women with non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome.13 A prospective multicentre observational study involving 1,020 patients showed that, in the adjusted analysis, there were no differences in in-hospital cardiovascular events between men and women with NSTEMI.14 Furthermore, although women with NSTEMI had a higher unadjusted in-hospital mortality rate than men, after adjusting for age and comorbidities, women with NSTEMI had a lower risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality rate (adjusted OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.89–0.92). The most important and unresolved issue remains the persistent sex-related difference in prognosis. Previous studies have been inconclusive, but overall, women have significantly higher unadjusted adverse event rates.15.

In the present study, within the median follow-up period of 527 days, women with NSTEMI had lower unadjusted cumulative survival rates for MACCE, MACE, all-cause death, and cardiac death than men (all P < 0.001). After adjusting for confounding variables, such as demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and procedural characteristics, this significant sex-related prognostic difference persisted in the subgroup of patients who did not undergo revascularization therapy, but it was no longer significant in those who did. Therefore, the risk of sex-specific adverse clinical outcomes may be influenced by differences in invasive treatment strategies, which is consistent with previous studies showing that invasive therapy significantly narrows the difference in in-hospital mortality between women and men.31,32 The present study further extends this conclusion beyond the hospital setting, suggesting that the benefits of invasive strategies become more apparent over time.33 In addition, the present study showed no association between sex and the risk of all-cause death correction in the cohort of patients treated with revascularization. This is consistent with previous findings showing that all-cause death is more frequently affected by age and comorbidity burden, while it is not significantly associated with sex.4 The present study has the following strengths. First, by including consecutive patients with NSTEMI from five medical institutions in different regions, selection bias was minimized, ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the findings. Second, the inclusion of patients in the context of current new diagnostic methods and therapeutic methods (such as high-sensitivity troponin detection and drug balloon therapy) ensured that the results could accurately reveal the impact of sex differences on the prognosis of patients with NSTEMI.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, as a retrospective study, the study is susceptible to potential unmeasured confounding factors, such as frailty, which may be sex-related and could have affected the study results. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to validate our findings. Second, data on differences in outpatient management (such as medication adherence and outpatient visit frequency) between men and women were not collected. Therefore, future studies are required to assess the impact of these sex-specific outpatient management differences on the prognosis of patients with NSTEMI. Third, the confounding factors included in our analysis were limited to the patient’s baseline data at admission. Considering that dynamic changes in lifestyle, medications, and relevant examination indicators during follow-up may influence the results, future research should incorporate these dynamic changes into the analysis. Fourth, data on perioperative surgical complications or outpatient bleeding outcomes were not collected; therefore, it was not possible to assess the impact of bleeding-related complications on the revascularization strategies and clinical prognosis of patients with NSTEMI. Finally, not all patients underwent CAG examination and coronary lumen measurement, so it was not possible to evaluate the impact of the type of NSTEMI and vessel diameter on the treatment decisions and clinical prognosis.

Conclusion

This multicenter real-world study demonstrates that sex-based differences in long-term outcomes among patients with NSTEMI are closely linked to the underutilization of guideline-recommended revascularization therapy in women. Crucially, when revascularization was performed, these sex-based disparities were eliminated. Given the lower rate of revascularization observed in women, ensuring equitable application of guideline-directed revascularization in women may represent a readily achievable strategy to improve long-term outcomes in women with NSTEMI.
