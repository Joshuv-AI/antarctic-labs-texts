Assessing polylactic acid nanofibers with cellulose and chitosan nanocapsules loaded with chamomile extract for treating gram-negative infections
Authors: Fatemeh Asadzadeh, Sadegh Ghorbanzadeh, Ahmad Poursattar Marjani, Reza Gholami, Faezeh Asadzadeh, Lida Lotfollahi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the development and characterization of a novel nanocomposite wound dressing material based on polylactic acid (PLA) nanofibers incorporating chitosan nanocapsules loaded with chamomile extract and cellulose nanoparticles. The nanofibers were fabricated using a three-step synthesis and electrospinning techniques, resulting in uniform, bead-free fibers with an average diameter of 186 ± 56 nm. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed the successful incorporation of all components, while tensile strength tests demonstrated improved mechanical properties by adding nanoparticles. Water contact angle measurements revealed enhanced surface wettability of the PLA-Cellulose-Chitosan complex compared to pure PLA nanofibers. In vitro biocompatibility assessments using MTT assays showed excellent cell viability and proliferation, with the optimized composite exhibiting the best performance. Scanning electron microscopy imaging confirmed robust cell adhesion and interaction with the nanofibers. The nanocomposite demonstrated significant antimicrobial activity against Escherichia coli, with a 20 mm inhibition zone observed for chamomile extract-loaded samples. Additionally, the material showed superior hemostatic ability compared to commercial gauze and high hemocompatibility. These comprehensive results indicate that the developed nanocomposite is a promising candidate for advanced wound management applications, offering a multifunctional approach to wound healing by combining antimicrobial activity, cell compatibility, and hemostatic properties.Keywords: Wound dressings, Polylactic acid, Nanofibers, Cellulose, Chitosan, Chamomile extractSubject terms: Materials chemistry, Polymer chemistry, Biomaterials, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

Bioactive wound dressings are fabricated from various biopolymers, including polyethylene glycol, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, alginate, and cellulose 1–3. Recent advances have incorporated antimicrobial and antibiotic agents into biopolymer matrices to mitigate wound infection and contamination, such as employing the antibiotic gentamicin in natural polymer matrices and chitosan-based wound dressings with intrinsic antibacterial activity for sustained tetracycline release to prevent bacterial infections 4–6. Wound dressing selection is contingent on the specific wound type and healing mechanism. NFs are commonly utilized to expedite wound healing due to their high surface area-to-volume ratio; consequently, their usage as modern wound dressings has increased substantially 7. As the largest organ and protective barrier interfacing the body and external environment in mammals, the skin safeguards internal organs from environmental threats like bacterial/viral infections and contaminants. The skin encounters potential chemical, microbial, and thermal stressors, necessitating compatible wound dressings aligning with bodily environments and wound characteristics for proper physiological function 1,8,9. Due to controlled drug delivery mechanisms, therapeutic agents can be released to attain desired pharmacological effects. Conventional drug formulations (tablets, capsules, syrups, ointments, etc.) cannot sustain drug release and exhibit low bioavailability with fluctuating plasma drug levels. An ideal wound dressing requires antibacterial functionality to prevent infection and accelerate healing for effective wound management 10,11. This is particularly crucial for diabetic and immunocompromised patients 12,13. Chamomile is due to effective compounds such as lipophilic substances, camazolin, terpenoid, α-bisabolol, and its oxides, esters, and apsenin compounds, which can be used to prevent infection and treat wounds 14,15. With rising global antibiotic resistance, natural antibacterial agents like plant extracts have gained traction to circumvent bacterial resistance and biofilm formation 16. Chamomile extracts are particularly promising as effective antibacterial and anti-inflammatory agents for treating various skin wounds 17,18. Nanomaterials that allow slow, stable, and controlled release of plant extracts can be important carriers for effectively transferring plant extracts to cells at the wound site. Chitosan nanoparticles exhibit enhanced physico-chemical, antibacterial, and biological properties compared to bulk counterparts owing to their diminutive size and high surface area-to-volume ratio. Chitosan-based nanocomposites have garnered substantial interest as drug delivery carriers 19–21. Chitosan is an effective wound-healing agent, and its chemical structure is similar to glycosaminoglycans in the cell's external matrix, which helps the growth and proliferation of cells 22. However, chitosan has disadvantages of electrospinning due to poor mechanical properties. Chitosan, as a drug carrier, is often mixed with other polymers 23. Polylactic acid (PLA) is among industry and medicine's most widely utilized biocompatible polymers due to its non-toxicity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and suitable mechanical properties 24,25. PLA polymer is one of the suitable polymers for increasing the properties of chitosan and a suitable polymer for electrospinning. Wound dressing combines the properties of nanomaterials and PLA polymer as a composite substrate by overcoming the polymer's shortcomings and improving the material's mechanical and biological properties. The biggest drawback of PLA is its hydrophobicity and lack of moisture absorption 26. In wound dressing, hydrophilicity is important for proper drug release and maintaining wound moisture for proper attachment of cells to the wound dressing. In the present work, to overcome this limitation, PLA, and increase the biodegradability properties and synthesis of safer wound dressing, nanocellulose was used in the polymer substrate. Nanocelluloses (NCs) are attractive emerging nanomaterials, rapidly advancing in recent years with immense potential for biomedical applications due to their diverse biological/physical traits and amenability to tailoring for specific purposes, including cartilage tissue engineering 27, vascular grafting 28, drug delivery 29, tissue engineering 30–32, wound dressings 33–35, biosensing and bioimaging 36, and hemostatic sponges 37. A key attribute of cellulose nanoparticles for skin wound treatment is their high exudate absorption capacity and ability to retain moisture around wounds via water absorption, concurrently enhancing the wound dressing's mechanical integrity 38,39. Electrospinning facilitates fiber composition/property modulation, rendering it an advanced pivotal NF fabrication technique compared to alternatives 40. NFs can recapitulate extracellular matrix-mimicking scaffolds with a high surface area conducive to enhanced cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation 41. Electrospun nanofibrous wound dressings exhibit desirable permeability and rapid hemostasis and can form protective barriers against external infectious agent 42–44.

This project aims to create a biocompatible wound dressing that promotes rapid healing. The approach involves synthesizing electrospun PLA nanofibers that incorporate chitosan nanoparticles. These nanoparticles act as nanocapsules, serving as carriers for an antibacterial chamomile extract. The dressing is designed to deliver the chamomile extract and cellulose nanoparticles to the wound site, enhancing the healing process. As far as we know, this effect considers several essential factors in accelerating wound healing, such as antibacterial, antioxidant, biocompatibility, moisture absorption, and a suitable scaffold for cell adhesion and growth. The emergence and spread of bacterial species with multiple antibiotic resistance means paying attention to the need to produce new antibacterial products to prevent infection caused by prolonging the treatment process. Chamomile was used as a natural antibacterial agent. Chitosan nanoparticles were used to promote plant extracts' effective and gradual release. The plant extract was added to the solution during the formation of nanoparticles. In the present work, nanocellulose and nano chitosan were used in a polymer substrate to overcome the limitation of PLA, increase the biodegradability properties, and synthesize a safer wound dressing. NFs were synthesized via electrospinning and characterized for fiber properties, including FT-IR, tensile strength, cell compatibility (MTT assay), and antibacterial activity. This research aims to explain a new approach based on nanomaterials and investigate its effect on wound healing (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Polylactic acid (PLA), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), Sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP), Trypsin–EDTA (0.25%), Hemocytometer, Trypan blue, and MTT solution were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Sterile cotton (Panberes Co., Iran), Chitosan (300–1000 cps) medium molecular weight (poly-D-glucosamine, M.W: 1,250,000 (avg.), Deacetylation: 92.0%, Iranchitosan, Iran), Chamomile extract (Zardband Pharmaceuticals Co., Iran), Chloroform (CHCl3), Dimethylformamide (DMF), Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%) and Acetic acid (glacial 99%) were prepared from Merck Germany, L-929 cell (mouse cell, Motamed Cancer Institute and Pasteur Institute of Iran) Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS, bioidea Co., Iran).

Preparation of cellulose particles

1 Gram of sterile cotton is placed in 10 mL of 70% sulfuric acid. It is allowed to dissolve for 24 h for the cotton in the acid completely. After the cotton has dissolved in the acid, NaOH solution (5 M) is slowly added to the solution until the pH of the solution reaches 10. The resulting solution is then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 3 min. The obtained cellulose nanoparticles are washed three times with distilled water and dried at room temperature (Fig. 2A).

Average nanofiber diameter: 186 ± 56 nm. Images demonstrate uniform, bead-free fibers with a nonwoven, mesh-like structure. Preparation of chitosan nanoparticles containing chamomile-extract

0.16 Grams of chitosan is dissolved in 2% acetic acid solution (10 mL) on a stirrer for 45 min. 2 mL of chamomile extract diluted with ethanol is added to the chitosan solution and stirred for 60 min. Chitosan-chamomile solution is slowly dripped into a 0.1 molar sodium tripolyphosphate solution on the stirrer to form chitosan nanoparticles containing chamomile extract. The resulting gel is separated by centrifugation and dried in an oven (Fig. 2A).

Preparation of electrospinning solution

1.25 Grams of polylactic acid is dissolved in chloroform (6 mL) and dimethylformamide (4 mL) with a 60:40 volume ratio on a stirrer hotplate. The obtained cellulose nanoparticles (1%wt based on PLA) and chitosan nanoparticles (7%wt based on PLA) are added to the solution, and 1 mL of chamomile is added in a volume of 1 mL of ethanol to dilute the plant extract and disperse it nicely in the chitosan synthesis solution and stirred on the hotplate until the final solution is dispersed (Fig. 2A).

Electrospinning process

The prepared solution is loaded into a syringe specific to the dual-pump electrospinning device (Electroris, ESDP30, Fanavaran Nano-Meghyas, Iran) to produce fibers, and an aluminum foil is placed on the device's collecting drum. The distance between the syringe nozzle and drum is set to 120 mm. The device's voltage is set to 18.3 kilovolts, and electrospinning is carried out for 4 h.

Infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy

In order to identify the functional groups and determine the type of reaction or links established, all analyses were performed in the range of 400–4000 nm. The test was performed for six samples: nanocomposite with and without the Nanoparticles and the final nanocomposite and nanoparticles. Quantities of each sample were prepared and ground with potassium bromide powder. Thin tablets were made from these components and placed in a special chamber for spectroscopy devices (Thermo-Nicolet Nexus 670, USA).

Tensile strength test

In this analysis, a tensile strength tool (Universal Testing Machine STM-50, Santam Co., Iran) determined the mechanical properties of NFs. Measurements were carried out at a tensile speed of 1 mm/s with the two holders set 20 mm apart. The ultimate tensile strength and elongation were determined based on test results.

Cell culture

The L-929 cell line was used for cell culture assays. Cells were cultured in T-75 flasks in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2, and 70% humidity). After reaching about 80% confluence, the cells were detached using trypsin–EDTA (0.25%). Then, trypsin–EDTA was neutralized with a complete culture medium (DMEM + 10% FBS, Gibco). The supernatant solution was removed after centrifugation (1200 rpm, 5 min, Sigma). Cells were suspended in 5 mL of complete medium. Then, counting was done with the help of a hemocytometer and trypan blue, and the viability of the cells was determined.

MTT assay

An MTT assay was performed in two ways, direct and indirect, to check the cytotoxicity of the sample.

Direct MTT assay

A 96-well plate was used to perform the MTT assay. Samples were first punched. Then, they were sterilized using an autoclave and placed in the wells of the plate. 10,000 Cells were added to each sample and control well, and the plate was placed in the incubator. After 24 and 48 h, MTT solution was added to each well so that its concentration reached 0.5 mg/mL. After 4 h, the contents of the wells were carefully removed so as not to remove the formazan crystals. Then 100 μL of DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well and placed in a shaker incubator for 30 min. At this stage, the samples were carefully removed from the wells. Then, readings were taken at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA reader.

Extract preparation

Extracts were prepared according to the protocol provided in the ISO-10993–12 standard related to sample preparation and reference materials. Based on this, 150 mg of the sterile samples were placed in 1 mL of DMEM culture medium without FBS and placed in an incubator at 37 °C for 1, 4, and 7 days. Finally, the samples were removed from the extracts and used for less than 12 h without purification.

Indirect MTT assay

10,000 Cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate and placed in the incubator. After 24 h., the contents of each well were removed. Then, 200 μL of the complete medium was added to the negative control wells. Positive control wells were treated with 200 μL of DMSO. 200 μL of 1-, 4-, and 7-day extracts were added to the appropriate wells, and the plate was placed in the incubator. After 24 and 48 h., MTT solution was added to each well so that its concentration reached 0.5 mg/mL. After 4 h., the contents of the wells were carefully removed. Then, 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well and placed in a shaker incubator for 30 min. Then, readings were taken at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA reader.

Cell adhesion assay

In order to check the cell adhesion of the prepared sample, three samples with a diameter of 1 cm were punched and sterilized with the help of an autoclave. Then, the samples were placed in the wells of a 24-well plate. L-929 cells (a type of mouse fibroblast cell line) with a density of 50,000 cells/well were placed on each sample, and the plate was placed in the incubator. After 30 min, 1 mL of complete medium was added to each well, and the plate was placed in the incubator again. After 48 h, the medium of each well was removed, and fresh medium was added to it. After 72 h, the contents of the wells were removed, and the cells were fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde for 30 min. Then, the cells were washed three times with PBS. In the next step, the cells were dehydrated with 50, 70, 90, 96, and 100 percent ethanol for 10 min each. Finally, imaging of the surface of the samples was done with the help of an FESEM microscope.

Hemolysis rate assay

For the hemolysis assay, the reported method was followed 45. Centrifugation separated the supernatant (plasma) from stabilized fresh blood. To remove lysed hemoglobin (pH 7.4) from the pellets (human red blood cells), the pellets were rinsed 3 times with PBS. It was observed that the pellets were placed in PBS diluted ten times, mixed with one mL of PBS-containing samples, and incubated for one hour at 37 °C. A positive control with 100% hemolysis was created using DI water, while a negative control with 0% hemolysis was created using PBS solution. The samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 3500 rpm after incubation. In the 96-well plates, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured by a spectrophotometer set at 545 nm. Blood clotting

Previously reported procedures were followed for the blood clotting analysis 45. Each 10 mm-diameter-slice sample was put in the well plate. The plate was then placed at 37 °C for 15 min to prewarm the samples. The NF samples and control group were slowly soaked in 200 L of stabilized blood, followed by 40 L of CaCl2 solution (0.2 M) to begin the blood clotting process. Following 15 min at 37 °C incubation, the clot traps red blood cells. 6 mL of DI water was added to each well to hemolysis the blood cells. After gently shaking for 2 min, 2 mL of media (1 mL) was removed and centrifuged at 1500 rpm. A 540 nm ELISA-reader (BioTech) was used to measure the supernatant absorbance in water. Chamomile release

The presence and release of chamomile extract in chitosan nanocapsules was investigated by incubating samples of equal size (5 cm × 5 cm) in PBS buffer for 72 h at 37 degrees. Absorbance measurements were then conducted using a PerkinElmer UV–Vis spectrophotometer lambda 1050+ in the 600 to 200 nm range. The batch of 1 w/w% solution of chamomile extract in PBS buffer was prepared as a standard sample and evaluated alongside other samples.

Water angle test

Contact angle measurements were performed using a CAG-20 SE system (JIKAN-IRAN) equipped with a CCD camera for droplet imaging. The system's software analyzed the contact angle between the droplet (approximately 4 µl) and the surface of interest.

Scanning electron microscopy

The surface morphology and microstructure of the samples were examined using two different scanning electron microscopes. For standard SEM imaging, a MIRA3 TESCAN microscope was employed. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was conducted using a JEOL JM7610F microscope for higher-resolution imaging and more detailed surface analysis. Before imaging, samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and prevent charging. The SEM and FESEM were operated under high vacuum conditions. For the MIRA3 TESCAN, an accelerating voltage of 5 kV was used, while the JEOL JM7610F FESEM was operated at 10 kV.

Investigating the antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of electrospun nanofibers was studied using the agar method with gram-negative bacteria. First, the culture medium was prepared according to the manufacturer's protocols. After preparing the agar nutrient medium, the nanofibers were placed on the surface of the plate. The sample plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the growth inhibition halos were examined.

Results and discussion

Determination of nanofiber morphology and diameter by scanning electron microscopy

The wound dressing material under investigation in this study was synthesized using a three-step method depicted in Fig. 2A. The products from the two synthesis steps involving the production of cellulose particles (Fig. 2B) and Chitosan nanoparticles loaded with chamomile extract (Fig. 2C), as well as the morphology and diameter of the electrospun NFs, were examined by scanning electron microscopy. Figure 2F–D display scanning electron micrographs of the polylactic acid NFs containing cellulose particles and chamomile extract-loaded chitosan nanoparticles. As observed, the NFs have a diameter of approximately 186 ± 56 nm (Fig. 2F), and the structure of the produced fibers comprises a nonwoven, bead-free fibrous mesh network. These results indicate that the electrospinning parameters could generate a suitable scaffold morphology for the wound dressing application. Analysis of the obtained data revealed that uniform, bead-free fibers were formed under the electrospinning voltage utilized in this work. These display acceptable characteristics regarding NF diameter, uniformity, and mesh-like networking. The presence of the cellulosic and chitosan nanoparticles is discernable in the microscopic images. Owing to their nanoscale dimensions, the fabricated fibers provide a substantial surface area-to-volume ratio, rendering them appropriate for development as a wound dressing material. Figure 2F shows no trace of additional particles can be detected on the synthesized network in the pure PLA sample. However, in Fig. 2D, E, The chitosan and cellulose particles added to the background material can be observed. In several locations, nanofibers impregnated with these particles can be discerned. These observations suggest the successful incorporation of the additives into the nanofiber matrix. To further investigate and confirm the incorporation of cellulose and chitosan particles within the PLA nanofiber substrate, we examined the sample's morphology using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM). Figure 2G, H reveal the presence of cellulose and chitosan particles, respectively. These particles' observed morphology and characteristics are consistent with previous literature reports 46,47, corroborating their successful integration into the composite substrate. In Fig. 2G, it can be observed that the PLA fibers have been impregnated with cellulose. However, larger cellulose particles are also visible in certain areas. In contrast, Fig. 2H shows that chitosan is predominantly present in nanoparticle clusters, with some instances of larger agglomerates.

FT-IR Spectroscopy to investigate the bonds established in composite nanofibers

Infrared transform spectroscopy was performed to identify the functional groups and to determine the type of bonds established between the components of the electrospun NFs. Figure 3A shows the spectra of composite NFs. According to the spectrum of nanocomposite containing cellulose and chitosan nanoparticles, OH frequency vibrations at 3431 cm−1, aliphatic CH absorption bands at 2997 cm−1, amide I (O=C–NH2) belonging to the carbonyl group (C=O) at 1618 cm−1 and amide II at 1533 cm−1, Stretching frequency at 1384 cm−1 for the C–O group and neutral –CH vibrations in 704 and 869 cm−1 were observed. According to the FTIR spectra for cellulose and cellulose with polylactic acid, there is a broad peak at 3344 cm−1 due to the stretching vibrations of the hydrogen bond of cellulose hydroxyl groups. The O–H stretching peak is vast because the hydrogen bond strength is different 48. Vibrations 2900 and 1638 cm−1 appeared due to moisture absorption, and vibrations 1430 and 1163 cm−1 are related to the vibration of polysaccharide and glycosidic bonds, respectively 49. In the spectra of chitosan, a characteristic band at 3447 cm−1 shows that it is related to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl and overlaps with the peak of the N–H stretching vibration of chitosan. The visible bands at 1577 and 1637 cm−1 correspond to C–O stretching and N–H bending vibrations, respectively. FTIR peaks of PLA in the following vibrational groups: O=C–O in ester groups at 1758 cm−1, and CH3 at 1457 cm−1, C–O–C at 1184 cm−1, O=C–O stretch in ester groups at 1130 cm−1, and C–O stretch at 1089 cm−1 50. As mentioned above, the result corresponded well with all the essential chemical groups found along the backbone of PLA. The existence of broad peaks in nanofibers containing chitosan and cellulose in the region of 3400 cm−1 can be attributed to the vibrations of hydroxyl and N–H groups in the structure of these compounds. The slightly lower peak value for nanofibers most likely indicates the formation of hydrogen bonds. The peaks of composite nanofibers show no difference from those of pure PLA, suggesting that little interaction occurs between nanoparticles and PLA.

Tensile strength test

In this analysis, the tensile strength tool determined the mechanical properties of NFs. Measurements were made at a tensile speed of 1 mm/s with two holders at a distance of 20 mm. The final tensile strength and elongation were determined according to the test results. Figure 3B shows the stress–strain curve of pure polylactic acid nanocomposite with 46% strain, nanocomposite containing cellulose nanoparticles with 48% strain, and nanocomposite containing cellulose nanoparticles and chitosan nanoparticles containing chamomile extract with 50% strain. According to the graphs, the tensile strength has increased by adding nanoparticles to the composite. Previous studies showed that synthesizing polylactic acid NFs, cellulose nanoparticles, and chitosan nanoparticles improves the tensile strength and physical properties 51,52. This may be attributed to cellulose and chitosan nanoparticles' high inherent strength and stiffness. According to these results, using nanoparticles in the nanocomposite substrate improves the mechanical resistance of NFs. As indicated by the results of the tensile strength test, the stress–strain curve, and the results of previous research in this field, the new nanocomposite wound dressing possesses adequate tensile strength and performance.

Water contact angle

The water contact angle (WCA) measurement was conducted to evaluate the surface wettability of PLA (Fig. 3C) and PLA-Cellulose-Chitosan complex (Fig. 3D), as this property is known to affect cell attachability. As illustrated in Fig. 3C, D, WCA values of 129 ± 2.5° and 109 ± 1.5° were observed for the pure PLA nanofibers and PLA-Cellulose-Chitosan complex, respectively. A significant decrease in the WCA value was noted for the complex compared to pure PLA nanofibers. This reduction in surface hydrophobicity can be attributed to the water absorbance of cellulose and chitosan due to their hydrophilic nature. Based on these results, it can be inferred that the surface of the complex offers improved conditions for cell absorption and adaptation to wound surfaces. Thus, the potential of this complex as a wound dressing material is demonstrated.

Cytotoxicity and biocompatibility tests

MTT assay

MTT assay assessed the cell viability and proliferation on wound dressing materials. In wound dressings, the MTT assay helps to determine how the dressing affects the cells critical to the healing process. The assay measures cells' metabolic activity, which indicates cell health and viability 53. The biocompatibility and cytotoxicity evidence of the developed composites were first evaluated using the MTT assay (Fig. 4A). In this cell viability test, the polylactic acid NF composites, the polylactic acid NF composite with chitosan nanocapsules containing chamomile extract, and the polylactic acid NF composite with chitosan nanocapsules containing chamomile extract and cellulose nanoparticles were examined at both 24 and 48 h. The graph shows that all the evaluated composite formulations exhibited adaptable behavior within 24 to 48 h. Interestingly, the best performance was demonstrated by the polylactic acid NF composite with chitosan nanocapsules containing chamomile extract and cellulose nanoparticles. This composite provided excellent cell viability and maintained high adaptability over the 48 h study period.

To further confirm the favorable biocompatibility profile of this optimized composite, the direct cell response data were supplemented with an indirect cytotoxicity assessment. In the indirect method, the composite sample was extracted at 1, 3, and 7 days, and the MTT assay was performed in the extracted media. The results from this indirect evaluation (Fig. 4B) fully corroborated the direct response behavior, indicating the stable and biocompatible nature of this nanocomposite formulation. In this figure, Ctrl− (negative control) represents untreated cells, and Ctrl+ (positive control) represents cells treated with cytotoxic agents. A noteworthy observation in this test is the decrease in cell viability during the first 24 h, followed by an increase at 48 h. The behavior observed in the initial 24 h can be attributed to factors that potentially disrupt cell stabilization on the surface (non-ideal wettability of the composite surface), resulting in cellular shock and subsequent cell death. However, after cell fixation on the surface, it is observed that in the following 24 h, cell proliferation appears to have occurred. This proliferation indicates a positive effect of the designed complex for cellular compatibility. The initial decline followed by recovery and growth suggests that while there may be initial challenges to cell attachment, the complex provides a supportive environment for cell survival and multiplication. These comprehensive in vitro biocompatibility assessments highlight the suitability of the polylactic acid NF composite with chitosan nanocapsules containing chamomile extract and cellulose nanoparticles for potential wound management applications, where maintaining a favorable cellular environment is crucial for promoting tissue regeneration and wound healing.

Cell adhesion test

Wound dressing's ability to promote rapid wound healing is influenced by cell adhesion, which is crucial to wound dressing research. A wound dressing material's ability to adhere to cells, such as mesenchymal stem cells, fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), is crucial for healing. It is essential to know the cell adhesion properties of the wound dressing material because they affect the proliferation, differentiation, and migration of wound cells 54,55. This study used the L929 cell line to represent fibroblast cells to assess cell adhesion. Then, using SEM images, the extent and manner of cell adhesion on the surface of the designed composite were evaluated, and the results are shown in Fig. 4F, G. In Fig. 4F, the extensive spreading of cells on the wound dressing surface, indicative of adequate cell adhesion, is observed. Additionally, in Fig. 4G, the interaction of the cells with the PLA NFs is visible with greater clarity. These observations demonstrate the surface's suitability for cell growth and suggest significant surface-cell interactions that will facilitate wound healing through the uptake of chamomile extract and the exchange of intracellular materials.

Antimicrobial efficacy testing

In wound management, bacterial infections are a significant problem, and aerobic or facultative pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and beta-hemolytic streptococci are the most common infections reported. In addition, anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, and Peptostreptococcus spp. Contribute to wound colonization and infection, especially in human and animal bites 56. The antibacterial test with Escherichia coli (E. coli) is essential in evaluating the effectiveness of wound dressings in preventing bacterial colonization and promoting wound healing. This bacterium represents a pathogenic species that can lead to infection and is commonly found in wound environments. The antibacterial test results are analyzed by assessing the ability of the wound dressing to inhibit the growth of these bacteria, typically by measuring the reduction in the bacterial population over time. The choice of E. coli for this test is based on their prevalence in wound infections, which represents gram-negative bacteria commonly found in wounds, and their clinical relevance in real-world scenarios. The test results provide valuable information on the wound dressing's effectiveness in preventing bacterial growth, promoting healing, and reducing infection risk 57,58.

As mentioned, the gram-negative bacteria E. coli was used in this study, along with the agar diffusion method. The E. coli test is shown In Fig. 4C. In the Petri dish, the notations A to D, respectively, indicate samples containing polylactic acid NFs, polylactic acid NFs and cellulose particles, polylactic acid NFs and chitosan nanocapsules loaded with chamomile extract, and polylactic acid NFs and chitosan nanocapsules loaded with chamomile extract plus cellulose particles.

As shown in Fig. 4C, the growth inhibitory effects of the nanocomposites were assessed by observing the clear zones surrounding the nanocomposite samples. The measured inhibition zones (mm) for the nanofibers in the antimicrobial tests are listed in Table 1. The prepared nanocomposites containing chamomile extract exhibited distinct inhibition zones against bacterial strain. For the gram-negative bacterium E. coli, the diameter of the inhibition zone was 20 mm around the NFs loaded with chitosan nanocapsules containing chamomile extract. In contrast, no inhibition zone was observed around the disc of NFs without the extract. Chamomile flowers contain over 120 secondary metabolites, making them rich in active compounds. These phytochemicals fall into several categories: phenolic acids, flavonoids, terpenoids, coumarins, and glucosides. The flowers also yield essential oils, comprising 0.24–2% of their composition. Known as " blue oil " due to its color, chamomile oil contains mainly sesquiterpenes and their derivatives (75–90%), with small amounts of monoterpenes. Essential compounds include β-farnesene, farnesol, chamazulene, α-bisabolol, and α-bisabolol oxides A and B. Notably, chamazulene and (-)-α-bisabolol contribute significantly to chamomile's therapeutic properties, including healing, anti-inflammatory, anti-spasmodic, antimicrobial, and ulcer-protective effects 59,60. These results demonstrate the potent antimicrobial activity imparted by incorporating chamomile extract into the nanocomposite structures, which was effective against Gram-negative bacterial pathogens.

Blood clotting and hemolysis studies

In vitro, hemostatic ability was calculated using a dynamic whole-blood clotting model. Theoretically, the coagulation index (BCI value) is inversely related to the blood coagulation capacity (BCI value) 45. The results showed (Fig. 4D) that the PLA-Cellulose-Chitosan complex clots more efficiently than commercial Gauze. This proved the complex was hemostatic enough to be applied as a wound dressing. Hemocompatibility studies, essential for hemostatic drugs, can be conducted using hemolysis. In normal human blood, the number of erythrocytes is around 50%, so the interaction between these cells and blood-contacting surfaces is vital to understanding hemoglobin release 45. Figure 4E shows the hemolysis ratio of the prepared complex. According to the inset image, the preparation complex and PBS (negative control) hues were the same. They were translucent and had a faint yellow tint. In contrast, the positive control was opaque and crimson, demonstrating that the PLA-Cellulose-Chitosan complex was suitable as a wound dressing candidate due to its high hemocompatibility.

Chamomile release

A release test was conducted on various samples using UV–Vis spectroscopy to investigate the presence of chamomile extract in chitosan structures. According to literature reports 61, chamomile extract exhibits an absorption spectrum in the 200 to 400 nm wavelength range. The results of the test, performed according to the protocol, are presented in Fig. 4H. The results reveal that samples without chitosan display minimal absorption, while PLA-Chitosan and PLA-Cellulose-Chitosan samples exhibit higher and relatively similar absorption profiles. The absorption spectrum of a 1% w/w chamomile extract solution in PBS buffer is also plotted for comparison. Based on these observations, we can confirm the presence of chamomile extract in the composite structure. The higher absorption of chitosan-containing samples suggests successful incorporation and potential controlled release of the chamomile extract from these matrices.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of polylactic acid-based composite nanofibers (NFs) for wound management applications. Incorporating chitosan nanocapsules loaded with chamomile extract and cellulose nanoparticles resulted in a multifunctional wound dressing material with excellent hemocompatibility and biocompatibility properties. The optimized composite NFs exhibited antimicrobial activity against Gram-negative bacteria, crucial for preventing wound infections. In vitro assessments revealed the NFs' lack of cytotoxicity and ability to support fibroblast cell proliferation, indicating their potential to promote wound healing. These findings suggest that the developed nanocomposite formulation is a promising candidate for practical wound management applications. Future research should focus on optimizing the material properties, evaluating in vivo performance, and assessing long-term stability for clinical use.
