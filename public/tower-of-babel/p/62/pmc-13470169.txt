Reconstruction of chronic distal biceps tendon rupture using dual hamstring autograft in a professional athlete: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ulas Can Kolac, Ahmet Kagan Tuncez, Mert Morkan, Melih Oral, Akin Uzumcugil, Egemen Turhan
Published: 2026

Introduction

Distal biceps tendon rupture is a functionally significant injury that results in substantial loss of forearm supination and elbow flexion strength and endurance, particularly affecting individuals with high physical demands.24,34 Acute ruptures treated with anatomic repair to the radial tuberosity generally yield excellent functional outcomes.21,38 In contrast, chronic ruptures, most commonly defined as those presenting beyond 3 to 4 weeks after injury, are characterized by factors that influence surgical management.4,13,24 Progressive tendon retraction, fibrotic changes, and degeneration of the distal tendon stump often compromise tissue quality and excursion.4,14,16,24 Although selected retracted ruptures can be managed with primary repair in high elbow flexion, reconstruction is often required when the tendon cannot be advanced to the radial tuberosity without excessive tension or when tissue quality is inadequate.24

When direct repair cannot be achieved, reconstruction using tendon grafts has been advocated to restore the length and tension relationship of the biceps muscle tendon unit.22 Both allograft and autograft options have been described, including Achilles tendon allograft and autologous semitendinosus, gracilis, fascia lata, or palmaris longus tendons.10,31,33,36 A variety of fixation strategies have been used, such as cortical button fixation, interference screws, transosseous tunnels, and hybrid techniques, reflecting the lack of consensus regarding an optimal reconstructive method.20,29,33 Although most reported series demonstrate satisfactory pain relief and strength recovery, the existing literature is largely composed of small case series, and evidence specifically addressing outcomes in elite or professional athletes remains limited.28,29 Moreover, return to competitive sport at a high performance level is infrequently reported, despite its critical importance in this population.34

Professional athletes with chronic distal biceps ruptures have unique demands, requiring durable fixation, reliable biological healing, and restoration of strength sufficient to permit return to elite competition. The purpose of this report is to describe the surgical management of a chronic distal biceps tendon rupture in a professional athlete using combined semitendinosus and gracilis autograft reconstruction, to review the current literature regarding graft selection and reconstructive outcomes.

Case report

A 25-year-old right hand dominant male professional kickboxer and national team athlete presented with pain, weakness, and functional limitation of the right elbow following an acute injury sustained during a championship competition. The injury occurred during a forceful punching maneuver, resulting in a sudden eccentric load applied to the flexed elbow, Figure 1A. The patient reported immediate sharp pain accompanied by a visible change in arm contour. Initial presentation to our institution was delayed by approximately one month due to mandatory military medical examinations and concerns regarding the presence of a visible surgical scar prior to their completion. On physical examination, a pronounced Reverse Popeye deformity was observed, consistent with proximal migration of the biceps muscle belly. The distal biceps insertion region demonstrated localized tenderness and residual ecchymosis. The hook test was negative, indicating complete disruption of the distal biceps tendon. Active elbow range of motion (ROM) revealed a pain limited 10° loss of terminal extension, while flexion was preserved. Manual muscle testing demonstrated reduced strength during resisted elbow flexion (4/5) and more notable weakness during forearm supination (4-/5) compared with the contralateral side. Sensory and motor examinations of the radial, median, and ulnar nerves were normal, and distal vascular examination was normal.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the right elbow confirmed a complete rupture of the distal biceps tendon with marked proximal retraction measuring approximately 8.5 cm from the radial tuberosity, Figure 1B. A surrounding hematoma measuring approximately 2.5 cm in depth was noted adjacent to the retracted tendon, along with mild elbow joint effusion. The distal tendon stump demonstrated signal changes consistent with chronic degeneration and fibrotic transformation, findings that were considered unfavorable for direct primary repair due to insufficient tendon length and compromised tissue quality.

Given the patient's young age, professional athletic status, and high functional demands, surgical treatment was recommended. Due to the chronic nature of the rupture, degree of tendon retraction, and degenerative changes observed on imaging and anticipated intraoperatively, primary anatomic repair was considered unlikely to restore the native length and tension of the biceps muscle tendon unit. The treatment goal was to achieve durable reconstruction capable of restoring strength and permitting return to high-level competitive sport. After discussion of available reconstructive options, surgical reconstruction using autologous hamstring tendon grafts was selected. Autologous semitendinosus and gracilis tendons were chosen to provide sufficient graft length to bridge the tendon defect and to increase graft bulk when combined. Given the patient's status as a professional kickboxer, the potential risk of lower-extremity donor-site morbidity was carefully considered and discussed pre-operatively.

Surgical technique

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with the patient positioned supine and the operative arm placed on an arm board. A sterile tourniquet was applied to the upper arm, and the surgical field was prepared and draped in a standard sterile fashion.

A longitudinal incision was first made over the anterior aspect of the distal arm to identify the retracted distal biceps tendon. Careful dissection was carried through the subcutaneous tissue to expose the biceps muscle belly. The distal tendon stump was found to be proximally retracted and densely adherent to surrounding tissues, with marked fibrotic and degenerative changes, Figure 2A. Although extensive mobilization of the muscle–tendon unit was performed, the native tendon could not be advanced to the radial tuberosity without excessive tension. Based on these intraoperative findings, primary anatomic repair was considered not feasible, and reconstruction was decided.Figure 2Intraoperative findings during chronic distal biceps tendon reconstruction. Autologous semitendinosus and gracilis tendons were subsequently harvested from the ipsilateral lower extremity through a standard anteromedial approach at the knee. The grafts were prepared on the back table. The prepared grafts were then used to augment the proximal biceps muscle tendon unit. Proximal fixation was performed using a Pulvertaft weave technique, with multiple passes through the residual tendon and muscle belly to maximize surface contact and load distribution, thereby creating composite tendon construct, Figure 2B.

Because the residual distal biceps tendon stump was markedly degenerative and proximally retracted, the graft was first incorporated into the native biceps muscle–tendon unit to create a secure proximal construct before distal passage. The graft limbs were intentionally left longer than required during proximal incorporation.

After proximal graft incorporation was completed, attention was turned to the distal aspect of the reconstruction. A second longitudinal incision was made over the antecubital fossa, centered just ulnar to the brachioradialis muscle. Blunt dissection was performed with meticulous identification and protection of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. The interval between the brachioradialis and pronator teres was developed, and the forearm was maintained in supination during deep dissection to minimize the risk to the posterior interosseous nerve. The anatomic footprint of the distal biceps tendon on the radial tuberosity was identified and prepared by decortication to create a bleeding bone surface, Figure 3. A unicortical tunnel was created at the center of the native insertion site under fluoroscopic guidance.Figure 3Distal fixation of the reconstructed distal biceps tendon. Distal fixation was achieved using a hybrid technique. The prepared graft was passed into the radial tuberosity tunnel, and cortical suspension fixation was obtained using a cortical button deployed on the far cortex. Final tensioning was performed with the elbow positioned at 90° of flexion to restore an appropriate length and tension relationship comparable to the contralateral side. Aperture fixation was then completed using an interference screw to improve rotational stability and compress the graft within the tunnel.

After confirmation of graft tension and elbow ROM, the surgical sites were thoroughly irrigated. Hemostasis was achieved, and the wounds were closed in layers. A sterile dressing was applied, and the arm was placed in a hinged elbow brace locked at 90° of flexion. No intraoperative complications were encountered.

Phase I: immobilization and early motion (0–2 weeks)

The operated limb was immobilized in a hinged elbow brace with the elbow maintained at 90° of flexion. Passive and assisted ROM exercises were initiated within the limits of the brace, focusing on controlled elbow flexion and extension. Forearm rotation was restricted. Hand, wrist, and shoulder ROM exercises were encouraged to prevent stiffness. No active elbow flexion or resisted forearm supination was permitted.

Phase II: progressive range of motion (3–6 weeks)

At 3 weeks post-operatively, active-assisted elbow ROM exercises were introduced with gradual progression toward full extension. Forearm pronation and supination were initiated in a controlled manner with the elbow flexed to reduce stress on the reconstruction. The brace was adjusted incrementally to allow increased extension. Light isometric exercises for the shoulder and scapular stabilizers were incorporated. Resisted elbow flexion and supination remained restricted.

Phase III: strengthening phase (7–12 weeks)

By 6 weeks, full elbow ROM was achieved. Progressive strengthening exercises were initiated at approximately 8 weeks post-operatively, beginning with isometric and low-resistance isotonic exercises for elbow flexion and forearm supination. Emphasis was placed on proper technique and gradual load progression. Proprioceptive and neuromuscular control exercises were added as tolerated.

Phase IV: advanced strengthening and sport-specific training (3–6 months)

From 3 months post-operatively, strengthening exercises were advanced with progressive resistance training. Plyometric exercises and sport-specific drills were gradually introduced, with careful monitoring for pain or fatigue. Emphasis was placed on restoring endurance, coordination, and power relevant to the patient's sport.

Phase V: return to sport (after 6 months)

Return to unrestricted training was permitted at approximately 6 months post-operatively following confirmation of symmetrical elbow flexion and forearm supination strength on objective testing. Gradual return to full competitive activity was allowed thereafter, based on clinical assessment and functional performance. Following completion of the rehabilitation program, the patient gradually returned to sport-specific training.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were assessed pre-operatively and at 3, 6, and 12 months post-operatively using the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS), the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) questionnaire, and visual analog scale (VAS) for pain.5,11,23 Pre-operatively, the MEPS was 55, the QuickDASH score was 54.5, and the VAS pain score was 5/10. At 3 months, the MEPS improved to 85 and the QuickDASH score decreased to 18.2, with a VAS pain score of 1/10. Further improvement was observed at 6 months, with an MEPS of 95, a QuickDASH score of 6.8, and no reported pain, Figure 5. At 12 months, the patient demonstrated full, pain-free elbow ROM with symmetrical strength compared with the contralateral limb, an MEPS of 100, a QuickDASH score of 0, and a VAS pain score of 0. Isokinetic testing at 6 and 12 months confirmed restoration of elbow flexion and forearm supination strength.Figure 5Changes in clinical outcomes over time, demonstrating improvement in MEPS, QuickDASH, and VAS pain scores from the pre-operative period through 12 months of follow-up. The patient subsequently returned to competitive kickboxing at the professional level. During follow-up, he participated in national and international tournaments without pain, functional limitation, or recurrence of symptoms, and achieved competitive success. At final follow-up, he remained active at the elite level and reported no limitations during training or competition.

Discussion

In the chronic setting, distal biceps tendon rupture is associated with tendon retraction that influences surgical decision-making.24,39 Compared with acute repair, management of chronic ruptures more often requires reconstructive strategies to address limited tendon excursion.16,39 In physically active patients, particularly athletes, reduced forearm supination and elbow flexion may affect performance and return to sport.34

Primary anatomic repair remains the preferred treatment for distal biceps tendon rupture when adequate tendon length and tissue quality permit tension free reinsertion.16,22,38 In chronic ruptures, however, advancement of the tendon to the radial tuberosity may be required, which can compromise restoration of native tension and affect post-operative motion or repair durability. Accordingly, reconstructive techniques are more commonly selected when marked retraction or degenerative changes are present. In the present case, pre-operative imaging and intraoperative findings demonstrated substantial proximal retraction and reduced tendon quality, supporting reconstruction rather than primary repair.

Several nonanatomic treatment options have been described for irreparable distal biceps tendon ruptures, including tenodesis to the brachialis muscle or other soft tissue attachment sites.8,14 These approaches may provide pain relief and restore a degree of elbow flexion; however, they do not reliably reestablish the physiological insertion.8,14 As a consequence, nonanatomic strategies are generally considered for low-demand patients.

When primary repair is not suitable, anatomic reconstruction using tendon grafts allows restoration of the distal biceps insertion at the radial tuberosity.29 Both autograft and allograft techniques have been described with a variety of fixation strategies. In a systematic review of chronic distal biceps reconstructions, Litowski et al29 reported improvement in post-operative ROM, strength, and patient-reported outcome measures across studies, with no statistically significant differences between graft sources or fixation methods. Allograft reconstruction, most commonly using Achilles tendon, offers the advantage of avoiding donor-site morbidity and providing grafts of sufficient size to bridge large defects, and was associated with lower overall complication rates, largely due to the absence of harvest related morbidity.27,29 Autograft reconstruction, in contrast, provides predictable biological incorporation and avoids disease transmission concerns, which may be relevant in young and high demand patients.29,39 However, donor site morbidity accounted for a substantial proportion of complications reported with autograft use.29 Consequently, selection between autograft and allograft reconstruction should be individualized, taking into account patient age, activity level, and tolerance for potential donor site effects rather than clear differences in functional outcomes.39

Among the autograft options described, hamstring tendons are among the most frequently reported.1,29 Semitendinosus and gracilis grafts provide sufficient length to bridge large tendon defects and allow flexibility in graft diameter, which may be advantageous in cases with substantial retraction.1,30,44 In contrast, palmaris longus grafts may be absent or insufficient in diameter, and fascia lata autografts require more extensive harvest with a higher potential for donor-site morbidity.32,42

In the present case, a combined semitendinosus and gracilis autograft was selected to address a substantial tendon gap resulting from marked proximal retraction. Use of a dual hamstring construct allowed augmentation of graft diameter and bulk, facilitating reconstruction of a composite tendon capable of spanning the defect while permitting secure fixation. Although single hamstring autografts have been reported for chronic distal biceps reconstruction, descriptions of combined semitendinosus–gracilis constructs remain limited, particularly in athletic populations.29,34,35 The absence of donor site morbidity and the favorable functional outcome observed in this case suggest that a dual hamstring autograft may represent a feasible reconstructive option when greater graft volume is required, although broader conclusions cannot be drawn from a single case.

Multiple fixation strategies have been described for distal biceps tendon reconstruction, including suture anchors, cortical buttons, interference screws, bone tunnels, and hybrid constructs.9,21,25,41 Biomechanical studies have demonstrated high initial fixation strength with cortical button fixation, while interference screws provide aperture fixation and may enhance rotational stability.9,25,41 In the systematic review, no fixation technique demonstrated superior post-operative ROM, strength, or patient-reported outcomes, although weave constructs were associated with higher complication rates.29 In athlete-specific cohorts, cortical button fixation was associated with earlier return to sport, without an increase in complication rates.34 The hybrid fixation strategy used in the present case aligns with these principles by combining stable cortical fixation with aperture compression, providing a construct consistent with existing reconstructive approaches.

Post-operative rehabilitation following distal biceps tendon reconstruction is variable, and no standardized protocol has been established. In their systematic review of athletes, Pitsilos et al34 found no association between rehabilitation parameters, including duration of immobilization or timing of strengthening, and time to return to sport. These findings suggest that, when adequate fixation stability is achieved, rehabilitation can be individualized without adversely affecting return-to-sport outcomes. In the same review, a return-to-sport rate of 97.5% with a mean return time of approximately 6 months was reported, although most athletes underwent acute repair and only a limited number of chronic cases required graft augmentation. Consistent with these findings, a recent systematic review by Boufadel et al7 reported an overall return-to-sport rate of 91.5%, with 85.2% of patients returning to their preinjury level or higher at a mean of 6.3 months after distal biceps tendon repair, regardless of incision approach or fixation method. In this context, the present case demonstrates that reconstruction using a dual hamstring autograft can permit return to elite-level competition within a timeframe comparable to that reported after acute repair.

Complications following distal biceps tendon reconstruction have been reported more frequently than those following acute repair. The overall complication rate after reconstruction was approximately 26%, with higher rates observed in autograft reconstructions, largely attributable to donor-site morbidity.29 Nerve-related complications and heterotopic ossification were among the most commonly reported adverse events. In contrast, complication rates in athlete cohorts were not associated with chronicity of injury, fixation technique, or time to return to sport.34 In the present case, no complications, including donor-site morbidity, neurovascular injury, heterotopic ossification, or graft failure, were observed during follow-up. While this favorable outcome cannot be generalized, it suggests that careful patient selection and surgical technique may help decrease known risks associated with reconstruction.

This report has several limitations. As a single-patient case report, the findings cannot be generalized, and no conclusions can be drawn regarding the comparative effectiveness of graft choice or fixation strategy. Follow-up was limited to one year, and longer-term durability of the reconstruction cannot be assessed. Additionally, comparison with alternative reconstructive approaches or rehabilitation strategies was not possible. These limitations are inherent to case-based reports and underscore the need for further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up in athletic populations.

Conclusion

Chronic distal biceps tendon rupture may require reconstructive treatment when primary anatomic repair is not feasible. In this case, reconstruction using a combined semitendinosus and gracilis autograft allowed restoration of function and return to elite level athletic competition, supporting this approach as a viable option in selected high demand patients.
