Lappaconitine hydrobromide prevents NLRP3-GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis to alleviate sepsis-induced acute lung injury by enhancing NR1H3
Authors: Yuejia Lan, Qingrui Zhong, Hongkai Huang, Xiao Deng, Lu Yang, Jiayi Sun, Mingyang Liu, Xianli Meng, Jiasi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence indicates that nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 3 (NR1H3) activation mitigates sepsis-induced acute lung injury (SALI) by reducing inflammatory response. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying NR1H3-inflammation interactions in addressing SALI remain unclear. Moreover, limited phytochemicals have been documented to ameliorate SALI via this pathway. Here, we discovered that NR1H3 activation directly suppresses the clustering of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) to lipid rafts, which acts as a part of the priming of NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. This process results in the suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation, consequently alleviating gasdermin-D (GSDMD)-mediated pyroptosis. Furthermore, through dual-luciferase reporter gene testing, drug binding assays, gene expression analysis, and biochemical evaluation, we determined lappaconitine hydrobromide (LAH) as a novel NR1H3 agonist that effectively suppresses TLR4-NLRP3 signaling pathway. Our results showed LAH attenuates inflammatory lung injury in both lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and cecal slurry (CS)-induced SALI mouse models. In vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that LAH suppresses NLRP3 signaling activation and pyroptosis. Additionally, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis demonstrated that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) linked to interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) production, modulation of inflammatory response, and control of innate immune response were markedly enriched in LAH, and core genes amongst the DEGs included NR1H3. Mechanistically, LAH reduces inflammation and pyroptosis through modulation of NR1H3-NLRP3-GSDMD axis in mice. This finding was further substantiated by the suppression of NR1H3 in vitro. Our findings offer novel perspectives on the NR1H3-inflammation interaction in SALI regulation and indicate the therapeutic promise of LAH as an NR1H3 agonist for SALI management via NLRP3 suppression.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) represents a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) characterized by acute respiratory distress (ARDS), pulmonary edema, and neutrophil accumulation in the lung [1,2]. There are numerous factors that can result in ALI, with sepsis-induced acute lung injury (SALI) being the most prevalent type of ALI [3]. Furthermore, the relevant epidemiological data indicated that ALI incidence in sepsis patients reached 68.2%, and the 90-day mortality rate in patients with concurrent ALI was as elevated as 35.5%, which seriously threatens the life safety of patients [4]. Nevertheless, the exact mechanism underpinning SALI remains obscure, and the practical agents available are exceedingly limited. Intratracheal surfactant instillation, for example, has been shown to enhance oxygenation in individuals with ALI; however, it does not exert a substantial impact on mortality rates [5]. Although dexamethasone (DEX) has been demonstrated to reduce the mortality rate of patients due to its immunomodulatory properties, the routine use of corticosteroids for broad-spectrum immunosuppression in non-severe or severe influenza pneumonia is not recommended [6]. Consequently, further research into the pathogenesis and related treatment of SALI remains an urgent necessity.

Nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 3 (NR1H3) (also referred to as LXRα) represents a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily functioning as a ligand-activated transcription factor within the liver X receptor family. This receptor demonstrates broad expression patterns across the liver, kidney, adipose tissue, and macrophages [7]. NR1H3 activation triggers cholesterol efflux gene expression, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), and ATP binding cassette subfamily G member 1 (ABCG1), while inhibiting inflammation through cholesterol homeostasis modulation or inflammatory factor reduction [8]. Modern research has revealed that NR1H3 signaling pathway displays an organ-protective effect in diverse inflammatory diseases, like septic liver injury, atherosclerosis, ALI, and septic myocardial injury [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. This multi-organ protective role may be primarily attributed to the activation of the NR1H3 pathway to attenuate inflammation and infection [7]. In addition, certain studies demonstrated that the pathogenesis and progression of SALI is attributed to NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, whereas NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition has been shown to alleviate lung injury [13,14]. Remarkably, a recent study showed that a natural plant extract, silibinin, exerts protective effects against septic myocardial injury through modulation of NR1H3 pathway activation and various sepsis-related signaling pathways, such as NLRP3 inflammasome [15]. These observations indicate that modulating the NR1H3 signaling pathway and its downstream NLRP3 inflammasome exhibits potential as a therapeutic strategy for SALI.

Lappaconitine hydrobromide (LAH), the hydrobromide hydrate of lappaconitine extracted from the root of Aconitum sinomontanum Nakai in Ranunculaceae, exhibits multiple pharmacological properties, encompassing anti-inflammatory, antinociceptive, antiarrhythmic, and antitumor activities [16]. Over the past several years, LAH has been intensively studied for its anti-inflammatory effects. For example, LAH has been reported to restrain the inflammatory signaling transduction in Freund's adjuvant-induced rat foot pads and alleviate the swelling symptoms of rat foot joints [17]. Additionally, the LAH derivative was found to mitigate inflammatory responses by reducing NO, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) production via negative regulation of nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways [18]. Of note, a clinical investigation revealed that the combination of LAH and continuous renal replacement treatment improved the cellular immune function and meanwhile reduced the inflammatory response in ICU sepsis patients [19]. However, the protective potential of LAH against sepsis-induced lung injury and the underlying key molecular mechanisms involved in this process remain unexplored. The present study focused on whether LAH demonstrated protective effects in SALI and sought to elucidate the potential mechanism.

Animals

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade C57BL/6 male mice (aged 6–8 weeks, weight 22–25 g) were procured from GemPharmatech LLC. (Nanjing, China). All animals were maintained under standard SPF conditions with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle and unrestricted access to food and water. After acclimatization for one week, only healthy mice (confirmed by normal activity, food/water intake, and absence of overt pathology) were included and scheduled to the following investigation. The Ethics Committee of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine sanctioned all animal experiments executed in this investigation (License No.: SCXK [Chuan] 2020-0034, ethical approval No.: 2022-79, ethical approval time: June 26, 2023). All animal experiment protocols followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 2011).

The sample size for each experimental group (n = 8–12 mice) was determined based on our pilot studies and previous literature [20]. A SALI model was created through intraperitoneal administration of LPS into C57BL/6 mice according to a previously published protocol [21]. The mice were arbitrarily split into Control, Model, LPS + DEX (50 μg/kg), and LPS + LAH (1, 2, 4 mg/kg) groups based on body weight [17,22]. Furthermore, daily intraperitoneal injection of LAH and DEX was given one week before LPS administration. Mice in the Control group received intraperitoneal injections of normal saline (NS), while mice in the remaining groups received intraperitoneal injections of LPS (12 mg/kg). 24 h after the LPS and NS injection, mice were euthanized by pentobarbital sodium anesthesia (50 mg/kg) [17]. The researcher responsible for drug injection and animal monitoring remained blinded to the group identities until the end of data collection. Serum samples, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples, and lung tissues were gathered and preserved at −80 °C for subsequent investigations.

Cell culture

THP-1 human monocytes were procured from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China). THP-1 cells (passage number of cell lines ranging from 3 to 15) were cultivated on dishes in an incubator (37 °C and 5% CO2). The medium consisted of RPMI-1640, FBS (10%), and penicillin/streptomycin (1%).

Measurement of wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio of the lungs

After mouse euthanasia, the left lungs were promptly weighed to determine the “wet” weight, and subsequently placed in a 60 °C oven for 48 h to establish the “dry” weight. The W/D weight ratio was computed to evaluate lung edema.

BALF collection

Briefly, the lungs were lavaged through the intravenous infusion needle using the instillation of three washes with 0.8 mL of cold PBS. BALF specimens were collected for total protein concentration, flow cytometry, and cytokine measurements.

Total protein concentration of BALF

The total protein concentration of BALF was determined by the kit of BCA assay (Boster, Wuhan, China).

Histopathologic evaluation of the lung tissues

Lung tissues underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (Boster, Wuhan, China), paraffin embedding, sectioning at 4 μm thickness, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. We utilized the designated scoring system to assess the extent of histological lung injury in mice [23]. Inflammatory scores ranging from 0 to 4 were employed to assess alterations in pulmonary vascular edema, alveolar septal thickness, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Each category was graded as shown below: 0: no damage, 1: minor damage, 2: moderate damage, 3: serious damage, and 4: significant serious histological changes.

Flow cytometry

Proportion of macrophages and neutrophils in BALF and peripheral blood (PB)

Based on the determination that 4 mg/kg was the optimally effective dose in ameliorating lung injury and inflammation, this dosage of LAH and DEX was selected for flow cytometric analysis of immune cell infiltration. The 100 μL of BALF and PB of mice were stained with 5 μL PE anti-mouse F4/80 and 5 μL Alexa Fluor 647 anti-mouse Ly-6G/Ly-6C (Beijing 4A Biotech, Beijing, China) for 30 min at 4 °C and washed for three times with PBS. The samples were resuspended with 500 μL PBS and measured by BD FACSCanto Ⅱ (BD Biotech, Lake Franklin, NJ, USA).

Proportion of Treg cells in PB

Antibodies against PE Anti-Mouse CD45, PerCP-Cy5.5 Anti-Mouse CD3, FITC Anti-Mouse CD4, and PerCP-Cy7 Anti-Mouse CD25 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) were used to surface-stain the blood of mice. Following fixation and permeabilization with Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD Biotech, Lake Franklin, NA, USA), the cells that had been previously stained for membrane proteins were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 Anti-Mouse Foxp3 (Biolegend, Santiago, CA, USA). The BD FACSCanto Ⅱ (BD Biotech, Lake Franklin, NJ, USA) was utilized for collecting data.

THP-1 cells pyroptosis examination

After treatment, the THP-1 cells were made into a single-cell suspension (100 μL). Per the Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Apoptosis Kit (Univ Biotech, Shanghai, China) manufacturer's protocol, each tube received 5 μL Annexin V-PE and 5 μL 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD), followed by dark incubation at 4 °C for 15 min, then underwent three wash cycles. Following this, the cells were resuspended by adding 500 μL PBS. The specimens were then assessed via flow cytometry utilizing BD FACSCanto Ⅱ (BD Biotech, Lake Franklin, NJ, USA).

The flow cytometry examination data were subsequently examined employing Flowjo software (BD Biotech, Lake Franklin, NJ, USA).

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity assay

Lung tissue specimens were rinsed with cold PBS, subsequently homogenized in MPO assay buffer and subjected to centrifugation at 1200 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was evaluated for MPO activity utilizing MPO Detection Kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). All experimental procedures were executed per the supplier's protocols.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis of transcriptomes

Metabo-Profile (Shanghai, China) performed the transcriptome sequencing and analysis. RNA extraction, purification, and library construction were applied to the samples. The sequencing library then underwent paired-end (PE) sequencing using next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology on the Illumina HiSeq platform. Cufflinks computed the fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) value for each gene, while HTSeq-count determined the read counts for each gene. The DESeq R package functions estimateSizeFactors and nbinomTest identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The threshold for significantly differential expression was established as P < 0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 2. A web-based Gene Ontology (GO) analysis tool mapped all DEGs to terms within the GO database, seeking markedly enriched GO terms. The Reactome pathway database constructed pathways to identify differential networks and core regulators. The biological function of all genes from the LAH 4 mg/kg vs. Model groups was analyzed through Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) utilizing the GSEA software (v.4.3.2). Gene sets with nominal P < 0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, and normalized enrichment score (NES) ≥ 1 were deemed markedly enriched. Additionally, leading-edge analysis was executed to identify the core subset of genes that primarily drive the enrichment signal.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of interleukin-18 (IL-18), IL-1β, IL-1α, and the alarmin high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) in BALF, serum, and the supernatants derived from the THP-1 cell culture medium were ascertained using ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotech, Wuhan, China).

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining

Lung tissues

IF staining of lung tissues was carried out. Briefly, the frozen lung tissue sections underwent fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Boster, Wuhan, China) for 10 min, and succeeded by antigen repair using antigen repair buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Subsequently, the sections underwent sealing with a 5% BSA solution for 30 min and incubated with 5% BSA solution-containing GSDMD antibody (1:200, Cat. THP-1 cells

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining of lung tissues and THP-1 cells

Paraffin sections of lung tissues were deparaffinized and hydrated. THP-1 cells were plated onto 20-mm-diameter coverslips and treated with drugs. The sections and cells underwent staining using a One-step TUNEL In Situ Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience Biotech, Wuhan, China). Fixation was performed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by permeabilization through incubation with 2% Triton X-100 (Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 5 min. TdT Equilibration Buffer was added to sections and cells to equilibrate for 15 min at 37 °C. They were stained with Labeling Solution for 60 min without light, then stained with DAPI, and observed with a Leica TSC SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Following the introduction of THP-1 cells into 96-well plates, a seeding density of 4 × 105 cells per well was employed. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to treatment with different concentrations of LAH (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) for 24 h. Subsequently, THP-1 cells underwent stimulation and treatment with various drugs. Cell viability was assessed using the cell counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) assay

The differentiated THP-1 cells underwent collection and three washes with cold PBS. Following this, cell lysis occurred using IP lysis buffer on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant underwent careful collection and overnight incubation with 3 mg ASC antibody at 4 °C. On the following day, the mixtures received further cultivation with Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotech, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) for another 4 h at ambient conditions. After centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the agarose beads underwent resuspension with 1× loading buffer and 10-min boiling. WB analysis was performed on the samples.

Hoechst 33342/propidium iodide (PI) staining

The cell death rate of THP-1 cells was measured using Hoechst 33342/PI Double Stain Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). After treatment with target compounds, THP-1 cells were incubated with cell staining buffer, Hoechst staining solution, and PI staining solution at 4 °C in darkness for 30 min per the supplier's protocol. The stained cells were then examined using a Leica SP8 SR STED confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Monochromatic fluorescence images were merged and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Lactatedehydrogenase (LDH) assay

Upon treatment, supernatants from various THP-1 cell treatment groups were carefully collected and transferred to a fresh 96-well plate (60 μL per well), followed by the addition of 60 μL LDH detection fluid to each well (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After 30 min incubation at ambient conditions, absorbance at 490 nm was ascertained using the SpectraMax iD5 instrument (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Scanning electron microscope

After the treatments, THP-1 cells underwent fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C, followed by triple rinsing with 0.1% PBS, subsequent dehydration using graded ethanol concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%), and drying. Subsequently, samples were examined using a scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA extraction was executed using the Total RNA Isolation Kit (Foregene Biotech, Chengdu, China). The extracted RNA underwent reverse transcription with ABScript III RT Master Mix (ABclonal Biotech, Wuhan, China) per the supplier's protocols. RT-qPCR analysis was carried out using Genious 2X SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal Biotech, Wuhan, China) per the supplier's protocols. GAPDH served as an internal reference, with relative messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels determined via the 2−ΔΔCT methodology. All primer sequences are provided in Tables S1 and S2.

RNA interference (RNAi) experiments

Human NR1H3-small interfering RNA (siRNA) along with a negative control siRNA were produced GenePharma (Suzhou, China). THP-1 cells comprising PMA were plated in six-well plates at 2 × 105 cells/well and maintained at 37 °C. After 24 h incubation, human NR1H3-siRNA and negative control siRNA were combined with Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at a 1:1 ratio using OPTI-MEM dilution, with mixtures incubated at ambient conditions for 5 min prior to transfection. These siRNAs (50 nM) were subsequently introduced into THP-1 cells at 70% confluence by adding transfection complexes to each well. Following 48 h transfection, knockdown efficiency was confirmed via WB analysis. RNAi experiments involved three independent replicates with GAPDH serving as the internal control. Corresponding gene expression data were normalized to negative si-control group means. siRNA sequences are presented in Table S3.

Cell transfection and luciferase assay

Luciferase reporter vector (NR1H3-pGL3-basic) was prepared by Genecreate Biotech. (Wuhan, China), and the NR1H3 promoter sequence was detailed in Table S4. 293T cells were transfected using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) per the supplier's protocols. Luciferase activities were assessed in cell lysate utilizing the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Abbkine, Wuhan, China). Firefly luciferase expression levels were normalized to Renilla activity serving as a transfection efficiency control, and presented as fold stimulation relative to control cells (transfected and treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Isolation of lipid rafts

Briefly, THP-1 cells underwent lysis in 2.1 mL ice-cold modified barth’s saline (MBS) buffer (25 mM MES, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and 1% protease inhibitors). Lysates were combined with 2.1 mL of 80% sucrose and layered with 4.2 mL of 30% sucrose and 2.1 mL of 5% sucrose in MBS buffer. Samples underwent ultracentrifugation at 40,000 rpm for 20 h at 4 °C and were separated into 12 subfractions, followed by quantification utilizing the BCA assay kit (Boster, Wuhan, China). Subsequently, protein samples were concentrated via Trichloroacetic Acid-Acetone method for WB analysis.

Cholesterol content measurement in lipid rafts

Lipid rafts were extracted following the methodology outlined above. Cholesterol levels were examined by using Amplite™ Cholesterol Quantitation Kit (AAT Bioquest Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to the instruction manual.

Human NR1H3 mRNA expression

An analysis of NR1H3 gene was performed using the publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) dataset GSE32707. We selected normal, sepsis, sepsis plus ARDS (Se/ARDS), and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) samples from this dataset to compare the levels of NR1H3 mRNA expression.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The 3-dimensional structure (MOL2 format) of LAH was procured from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 3-dimensional structure of NR1H3 was procured from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org). Molecular docking between LAH and NR1H3 was realized by AutoDock (The Scripps Research Institute, San Diego, CA, USA). Pymol (Schrödinger, New York, NY, USA) and Discovery Studio (BIOVIA, Omaha, NE, USA) visualized these docking structures in cartoon or surface forms.

After docking, MD simulation was executed employing Gromacs2022.3 software. Briefly, the MDs simulation workflow encompassed four steps: energy minimization, heating, equilibrium, and production dynamics simulation. Upon simulation completion, the software's built-in tool was employed to analyze the trajectory, calculating root mean square variance (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg) value, and solvent accessible surface area (SASA), alongside the free binding energy of complexation through the Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) method.

Localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) assay

The binding between NR1H3 and LAH was assessed utilizing an OpenSPR LSPR biosensor (Nicoya Lifesciences, Waterloo, Canada). Briefly, NR1H3 protein was covalently attached to COOH-sensor chips (Nicoya, Kitchener, Canada) through 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC)/N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) chemistry. Subsequently, LAH was serially diluted to various concentrations (500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.25, and 0 μM) in analyte buffer and introduced to the chip in ascending concentration order at 20 μL/min flow rate for 240 s association phase, then 360 s dissociation. The affinity constant was determined and evaluated using Trace Drawer software (Ridgeview Instruments AB, Uppsala, Sweden).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) and isothermal dose-response fingerprintCETSA (ITDRFCETSA)

For CETSA experiments, THP-1 cells with treated DMSO (for control) or LAH (100 μM) were harvested and subjected to heating across a temperature gradient from 40 °C to 60 °C. For ITDRFCETSA experiments, THP-1 cells treated with LAH (0, 10−3, 10−2, 10−1, 100, 101, 102, 103 μM) were collected and heated at a temperature of 58.2 °C. Then, all samples underwent centrifugation at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm for 20 min, after which the supernatants were harvested for WB analysis.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was utilized for statistical evaluation and graphical representation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was executed to assess group differences. Sepsis score data at each time point underwent analysis using a two-way ANOVA (mixed model) and Sidak's multiple comparisons test. All data are denoted as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

LAH alleviated SALI in LPS-induced and CS-induced mice

The chemical structure of LAH was demonstrated in Fig. 1A. To examine the pharmacological effects of LAH on lung injury, we established LPS-induced SALI in mice according to previous studies (Fig. 1B). As shown in Figs. 1C, 24 h survival rate of LPS-induced mice was around 58%. As expected, LAH administration increased the survival rate in a dose-dependent manner. Additionally, LAH treatment in mice led to a significant decrease in LPS-induced inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar septal thickening, and pulmonary vascular edema (Fig. 1D). The semiquantitative score of lung pathological injury indicated that administration of LAH and DEX both effectively diminished LPS-induced lung injury (Fig. 1E). As illustrated in Figs. 1F and G, both the W/D ratio and total protein concentration in BALF were potently increased in the Model group, while markedly decreased by LAH and DEX administration versus the Control group, indicating that pulmonary edema was relieved in mice treated with LAH and DEX. Also, LPS increased the percentages of macrophages and neutrophils in BALF, while both LAH and DEX prevented the infiltration of these cells into lung tissues (Figs. 1H and I). We then evaluated MPO activity to further evaluate neutrophil accumulation within lung tissues. LPS administration induced a significant increase in MPO activity in model mice, which was ameliorated by LAH and DEX treatment (Fig. 1J). Of interest, the percentage of PB macrophages and neutrophils was elevated, whilst lymphocyte percentage was lowered in the mice in LPS-induced SALI, which was negated by medication with LAH and DEX (Figs. 1H−M), manifesting that LAH blocked the systemic inflammatory response in ALI mice. Furthermore, Treg cells are reported to be a special subset of CD4+ T lymphocytes with an immunosuppressive function. We observed that LPS lowered the percentages of CD4+ CD25+ Foxp3+-positive regulatory T cells (Tregs) in PB of the mice, which were drastically reversed by LAH at 4 mg/kg and DEX at 50 μg/kg (Fig. 1N), suggesting that LAH ameliorated LPS-induced ALI through immune function modulation in mice.

The whole ceca obtained from female mice was demonstrated in Fig. S1A. To further investigate the pharmacological effects of LAH on lung injury, a cecal slurry (CS)-induced SALI model in mice was established referring to previous studies (Fig. S1B). As shown in Fig. S1C, the 24 h survival rate of CS-induced mice was around 43%. As expected, LAH and DEX administration increased the survival rate. Moreover, LAH and DEX pretreatment enhanced illness severity (median sepsis score 7 [IQR 6, 10] for the model vs. 10 [IQR 8, 11] for the DEX, P < 0.01; median sepsis score 7 [IQR 6, 10] for the model vs. 8 [IQR 6.25, 10] for the LAH 4 mg/kg, P < 0.05) (Fig. S1D). Supplementation with LAH markedly alleviated pulmonary edema and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, thickening of alveolar septa, as well as pulmonary vascular edema (Figs. S1E−H). Additionally, CS-induced elevations in IL-18 and IL-1β levels in lung tissue, serum, and BALF were attenuated by LAH and DEX intervention (Figs. S1I−K).

Taken together, these data indicated that LAH may effectively restore lung tissue damage caused by LPS and CS.

LAH restrained the over-activation of NLRP3 pathway in LPS-induced SALI mice

NLRP3, belongs to the NOD-like receptor family, is the core of the pathogenesis of SALI [24]. To elucidate the pharmacological mechanism of LAH against SALI, we examined LAH's impact on the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway in LPS-induced mice. Relative to the control group, LPS stimulated the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, alterations include elevated NLRP3 expression, ASC, Pro/Cleaved-Caspase-1, Pro/Cleaved-IL-1β, and GSDMD-full length (FL)/N-terminal fragment (NT), whereas pronounced repression was observed in the LAH and DEX groups (Figs. 2A and B). Proinflammatory cytokines are critical factors with the function of triggering the inflammatory cascade and subsequent tissue damage, so we thereafter examined IL-1β, IL-18, IL-1α, and HMGB1 levels in mice serum and BALF. The results demonstrated a marked decrease in the concentrations of these inflammatory mediators within the LAH + LPS group versus the LPS group (Figs. 2C and D). IHC analyses presented that LAH and DEX drastically diminished NLRP3 level in the lung tissues of the mice challenged with LPS, which was consistent with WB analyses (Figs. 2E and F). Another set of IHC results also exhibited that LAH 4 mg/kg and DEX notably decreased NLRP3 level in the lung tissues of the mice challenged with CS (Fig. S2). Likewise, LAH obviously restrained the up-regulation of GSDMD protein levels in the lung tissues of LPS-induced mice (Figs. 2G and H). TUNEL findings revealed an up-regulated number of cells undergoing apoptosis in the pulmonary region due to LPS exposure. However, apoptotic cell proportions in lung tissues decreased dose-dependently following LAH or DEX administration (Figs. 2I and J). LAH restrained NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and pyroptosis in vitro

Macrophages serve as primary effector cells in NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, functioning centrally in inflammatory diseases including SALI. To validate the in vivo observations from SALI mice, we employed a well-established classical approach (LPS plus ATP) for triggering NLRP3 inflammasome activation in THP-1 cells, which can well simulate the inflammatory response processes involving macrophages in vivo [25]. We initially used the CCK-8 assay to detect cytotoxicity and determined the LAH concentration for in vitro experiments. The results demonstrated that in 24 h, within the concentrations range from 2.5 μM to 80 μM, LAH exhibited no significant cytotoxic effects on THP-1 cells (Fig. 3A). Thus, 10 μM, 20 μM, and 40 μM LAH were chosen in the following assays. The results of IF staining showed that LPS plus ATP obviously promoted NLRP3 expression versus the Control group, while the extra supplementation of LAH markedly weakened NLRP3 expression in a concentration-dependent manner (Figs. 3B and C). Figs. 3D and E demonstrated that LAH and MCC950 both maredly decreased NLRP3, Cleaved-Caspase-1, and Cleaved-IL-1β protein levels, while showing no impact on NLRP3, Pro-Caspase-1, Pro-IL-1β, and ASC expression versus the LPS group. Upon NLRP3 inflammasome activation, the adaptor protein ASC forms a large protein complex termed an ASC speck or pyroptosome, which represents a characteristic indicator of inflammasome activation [26]. Our findings demonstrated that LAH did not alter ASC protein expression but decreased the quantity of ASC specks induced by LPS and ATP (Fig. S3). Notably, unlike Sen A, a natural product previously identified by our group to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, LAH suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome assembly via blocking the interaction between ASC and NLRP3 while did not affect the binding between Pro-Caspase-1 and ASC (Figs. 3F and G) [27]. The results of immunofluorescence further verified that LAH markedly inhibited the interaction between NLRP3 and ASC, while demonstrating no effect on colocalisation of Pro-Caspase-1 and ASC (Fig. S4). Moreover, relative to the normal control, THP-1 cells stimulated by LPS plus ATP demonstrated enhanced production of inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-18, IL-1α, and HMGB1. Afterward, we investigated whether LAH protects THP-1 cells from LPS + ATP via pyroptosis inhibition. As displayed in Figs. 3J and K, MCC950 and LAH treatment at 10–40 μM dose-dependently rescued pyroptotic cell death induced by LPS + ATP in THP-1 cells, which was further confirmed by CCK-8 and LDH assays. Additionally, the dual staining with Annexin V and 7-AAD revealed the induction of cell death by LPS plus ATP (Annexin V+, 7-AAD+) (Figs. 3L and M). Treatment with LAH and MCC950 effectively prevented cell death in THP-1 cells. We executed TUNEL and PI staining to evaluate DNA fragmentation extent and random degradation in LPS + ATP-induced THP-1 cells, which represent pyroptosis characteristics. Consistent with the results of the above experiments, the outcomes showed that MCC950 and LAH notably downregulated fluorescence intensity of TUNEL and PI positive cell numbers versus the LPS + ATP group (Figs. 3N−Q). The SEM results further confirmed that LAH treatment alleviated pyroptosis. As illustrated in Fig. 3R, relative to the Control group, the membrane surface of LPS + ATP-stimulated THP-1 cells exhibited severe damage with extensive pyroptosis pore formation. Following MCC950 or LAH treatment, cell morphology was restored, and pyroptosis pore numbers were diminished. Moreover, Figs. 3U and V demonstrated that GSDMD fluorescence intensity exhibited an observable elevation following LPS + ATP treatment, and LAH notably decreased GSDMD fluorescence intensity.

Collectively, the above data suggested that LAH suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis protein overexpression, thus blocking the pyroptosis occurrence.

LAH activated NR1H3 signaling pathway in LPS-induced SALI mice

To further ascertain the key pathways underlying LAH's protective effect in LPS-induced SALI, we conducted RNA-Seq experiments to examine DEGs across Control, Model, and LAH 4 mg/kg groups. As demonstrated in Fig. 4A, principal component analysis (PCA) of the identified genes showed a distinct separation between Model group and Control group, with some reversal of the gene pattern observed after treatment with LAH. To be specific, 1364 DEGs (763 up-regulated and 601 down-regulated) were discovered in Model group vs. Control group, while 400 DEGs (177 up-regulated and 223 down-regulated) were found in LAH 4 mg/kg group vs. Model group (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, a total of 136 DEGs were overlapped between Model vs. Control and LAH 4 mg/kg vs. Model. It is worth noting that only 127 of these genes showed an upregulation or downregulation in Model group, while after LAH administration, they showed a downregulation or upregulation (Fig. 4C). Based on the change tendency, these 127 genes were identified as LAH-rescued or -responsive genes whose expression demonstrated in Fig. 4D by heatmaps and volcano plots. The GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 4E) demonstrated that genes rescued by LAH exhibited marked links to IL-1β production, biotic stimulus response regulation, and natural killer cell-mediated immunity regulation. Furthermore, by utilizing the String database to examine the interactions among these rescued genes linked to IL-1β production, inflammatory response regulation, and innate immune response regulation, we noticed that core genes amongst the DEGs included Nr1h3, Nlrp3, Casp1, and Igtp (Figs. 4F and G). Nlrp3 and Casp1 are the main genes involved in the inflammasome signaling pathway; Igtp is the main gene involved in the IFN-γ-mediated immune regulatory pathway. Notably, NR1H3 is a pivotal transcription factor related to the modulation of inflammatory response during SALI [8]. Nlrp3, Casp1, and Igtp were expressed at a higher level in Model group, and after treatment with LAH, the expression level decreased. Nr1h3 was expressed at lower levels in Model group, and after treatment with LAH, the expression levels increased (Fig. 4G). GSEA enrichment analysis was subsequently executed to examine the associated signaling pathways utilizing gene expression data from the LAH 4 mg/kg vs. Model groups as the analytical parameter. The data suggested that inflammatory response, pyroptosis, and cytokine activity were enriched and restrained, as well as negative regulation of inflammatory response and ABC type transporter activity were enriched and up-regulated following LAH treatment (Fig. 4H). Not surprisingly, as shown in Figs. 4J and K, LAH profoundly elevated NR1H3 protein levels in lung tissues from ALI mice. Additional IHC findings similarly demonstrated that LAH 4 mg/kg and DEX markedly enhanced NR1H3 expression within lung tissues of CS-challenged mice (Fig. S6). As documented previously, LXR activation triggers the expression of genes linked to cholesterol efflux, including PPARγ, ABCA1, and ABCG1 [28]. Lipid rafts represent specialized membrane microdomains enriched with cholesterol and sphingomyelin that serve as platforms for ligand recognition and complex assembly. Consequently, enhanced cholesterol efflux substantially reduces lipid raft abundance, thereby preventing the activation of inflammatory cascades mediated by raft-associated ligands like TLR4, and subsequently attenuates the inflammatory response [29]. In recent years, the PPARγ-NR1H3-ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway has been regarded as a pivotal signaling pathway that exerts anti-inflammatory effects by regulating cholesterol homeostasis [30]. Hence, further immunoblotting assay was arranged, which showed a remarkable reduction in the NR1H3, ABCA1, ABCG1, as well as PPARγ in the Model group, while both LAH and DEX treatment led to a dose-dependent up-regulation (Figs. 4L and M). Overall, LAH may mitigate LPS-incurred SALI of the mice by up-regulation of NR1H3 signaling pathway.

NR1H3 is activated by LAH and is associated with inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome priming in vitro

NR1H3 is a critical transcription factor that regulates lipid homeostasis and inflammatory responses. As demonstrated in Fig. 5A, NR1H3 transcriptional activity was assessed using dual-luciferase reporter assay. As expected, the results demonstrated that LPS markedly diminished NR1H3 promoter activity, while T0901317 (an agonist of NR1H3) and LAH dose-dependently enhanced NR1H3 luciferase reporter gene expression (Fig. 5B). Further study confirmed that LAH increased the gene expression of NR1H3 (Fig. 5C). In Figs. 5D and E, a downregulated expression of NR1H3, ABCA1, ABCG1, and PPARγ proteins in LPS + ATP stimulated THP-1 cells was observed. Nevertheless, both T0901317 and LAH treatment accomplished a significant increase in NR1H3 signaling pathway-related protein expression. Consistent with WB results, IF staining indicated that NR1H3 expression was markedly decreased following LPS stimulation compared to the Control group, while administration of T0901317 and LAH effectively up-regulated the expression level of NR1H3 (Figs. 5F and G). Furthermore, we conducted a more detailed study evaluating the effects of LAH on other nuclear receptors. The data demonstrated that when applying identical LAH concentrations to enhance NR1H3 expression, no notable alterations were detected in mRNA and protein levels of nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 2 (NR1H2), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), and retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα) (Fig. S7). It has been proven by previous studies that the enhancement of the NR1H3 pathway transduction accelerates cholesterol efflux to decrease lipid raft abundance, which results in the inhibition of TLR4-mediated signal transduction. As demonstrated in Fig. 5H, both NR1H3 agonists T0901317 and LAH disrupted lipid rafts through cholesterol removal from these membrane domains. Furthermore, we isolated raft fractions and examined the translocation of TLR4 by WB. The findings demonstrated that LPS promoted TLR4 clustering within lipid rafts, whereas pretreatment with T0901317 or LAH counteracted this phenomenon (Figs. 5I and J). Notably, Figs. 5K and L revealed that LPS markedly enhanced TLR4 expression, while LAH and T0901317 demonstrated no obvious effect on TLR4 up-regulation mediated by LPS. WB analysis further demonstrated that LAH reduced nuclear p65 protein levels in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating that LAH inhibited p65 nuclear translocation. Additionally, LAH and T0901317 treatment suppressed the LPS-induced overexpression of NLRP3 mRNA (Fig. 5M). LAH displayed a direct interaction with NR1H3

Molecular docking analysis was subsequently utilized to ascertain the precise binding sites between LAH and NR1H3. The predicted binding energy of LAH with NR1H3 is −9.81 kcal/mol. Analysis indicated that LAH interacts with several residues of NR1H3 (Figs. 6A and B). MD simulation was subsequently executed to further examine the stability of the NR1H3-LAH complex. The mean RMSD values for unbound NR1H3 and the NR1H3-LAH complex were 0.38 and 0.32 nm, respectively. RMSD data indicated that LAH binding stabilized NR1H3 and reduced structural deviations from its native conformation (Fig. 6C). In the RMSF value, NR1H3-LAH system was less than that of NR1H3, further suggesting the overall stability of NR1H3-LAH system was greater than that of NR1H3 (Fig. 6D). In addition, the Rg values or SASA values of the NR1H3-LAH complex and NR1H3 showed similar patterns, indicating that these complexes maintained structural stability throughout the 100 ns simulation (Figs. 6E and F). Moreover, there were several hydrogen bonds in the equilibrium process of the system (Fig. 6G). As shown in Fig. 6H, the computed binding free energy revealed van der Waals (−40.011 kcal/mol) and electrostatic (−30.430 kcal/mol) contributed primarily to this protein-ligand interaction. The above analysis indicated that LAH interacts with several residues of NR1H3, a finding further verified by the LSPR assay. The estimated equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) for the binding of LAH to the NR1H3 protein was 1.83 × 10−4 M, with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI) of 1.57 × 10−4−2.12 × 10−4 M (Fig. 6I). Similarly, the substantial shifts in CETSA experiments confirmed the interaction between LAH and target proteins. As demonstrated in Figs. 6J and K, protein damage in the DMSO group escalated with rising temperature. After LAH binding to NR1H3, the thermal stability of NR1H3 was enhanced, particularly at 56.4 °C, with this alteration being more pronounced versus the DMSO group (DMSO Tm50 = 55.93 °C vs. LAH Tm50 = 61.40 °C). The thermal stabilization between LAH and NR1H3 was dose-dependent from ITDRFCETSA (Figs. 6L and M). As shown in Figs. 7A and B, when NR1H3 expression was restrained by GSK2033, the up-regulatory effect of LAH on the expression of NR1H3, ABCA1, ABCG1, and PPARγ was markedly attenuated. Discussion

The lung represents one of the most susceptible organs in sepsis, with septic patients experiencing ALI demonstrating elevated mortality rates and worse prognosis [4]. As far as we know, there is still no satisfactory strategy to suppress SALI. The latest research shows that psoralen exerts its therapeutic effects on ALI by covalently targeting HMGB1 in macrophages and modulating the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway [31]. Chrysophanol A has also been recently shown to suppress LPS-induced ALI in mice through PPARγ/RXRα regulation and TLR4/MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway inhibition [32]. Therefore, alleviating excessive inflammatory response is key to treating ALI [3]. In the present study, we found that LAH could alleviate pathological injury of lung tissues and pneumonia symptoms in both LPS and CS induced-SALI mice, and maintain immune homeostasis, the underlying mechanism relies on NR1H3 pathway.

Lappaconitine, a natural compound with a novel C18-diterpenoid alkaloid skeleton, displays an extensive biological profile, and LAH is its hydrogen bromide salt form. Previous research on LAH is focused mainly on its anti-tumor and analgesic effects. In recent years, the potential role of LAH to ameliorate inflammatory responses such as sepsis has also been gradually discovered. Liu and colleagues confirmed the anti-inflammatory effect of LAH by utilizing inflammatory pain models, including adjuvant arthritis, carrageenan-induced inflammation, and formalin-induced inflammation in rats [33]. Furthermore, LAH demonstrated a significant inhibitory impact on LPS-induced NO generation in RAW 264.7 cells, and the underlying mechanism may be linked to suppressing COX-2 activity [34]. Recently, Xiao et al. [35] discovered that LA derivatives markedly suppressed inflammatory mediator secretion and neutrophil activation through inhibiting phosphorylation of NF-κB p65, inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B alpha (IκBα), extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 (ERK1/2), c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNK), and p38 MAPKs in ALI mice. As a whole, studies on the biological function of LAH in SALI are very limited.

Accumulating evidence has shown that the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome plays a key role in SALI pathogenesis [24,36]. Currently, the suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome to initiate pyroptosis is the main strategy employed to alleviate SALI induced by LPS [37,38]. Our results demonstrated that the protein expression of NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis-related molecules was dramatically decreased by LAH treatment in lung tissues of SALI mice. Moreover, LAH down-regulated the levels of IL-1β, IL-18, IL-1α, and HMGB1 in serum and BALF, which is crucial for recruiting and activating immune cells to augment inflammation at infection or tissue damage locations [39]. TUNEL assay showed that the SALI model induced cell death in the lung tissues of C57BL/6J mice, which was mitigated by LAH intervention, suggesting that LAH inhibited sepsis-induced pyroptosis to relieve SALI. Further in vitro investigations showed that LAH interfered with multiple stages of NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Specifically, it not only blocked the priming phase but also impaired inflammasome assembly, as evidenced by suppressed ASC speck formation, disrupted NLRP3-ASC interaction, reduced Caspase-1 activation, and diminished secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. In agreement with the in vivo results, LAH pretreatment substantially downregulated the degree of pyroptosis in LPS plus ATP-stimulated THP-1 cells, reduced LDH release by 46.8%, decreased programmed cell death by 70.7%, restrained membrane pore formation, and inhibited the activation of GSDMD by 60.7%. Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro data indicated that LAH exerted protective effects against SALI by attenuating NLRP3-GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis.

RNA-seq represents a robust high-throughput approach for elucidating drug mechanisms and identifying potential therapeutic targets [40]. In the present study, transcriptomic analysis revealed that LAH predominantly modulated genes associated with IL-1β production, regulation of inflammatory response, and regulation of innate immune response. Then, the PPI construction and analysis highlighted the involvement of NR1H3 signaling pathways in inflammation and immunity. In recent years, abundant evidence has indicated that NR1H3 serves as a protective target for various natural compounds in ALI. For example, dihydrotanshinone isolated from the root of Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge attenuated LPS-induced ALI in mice by enhancing NR1H3 expression and suppressing NF-κB activity, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects [41]. Similarly, a bioactive compound, Chikusetsusaponin V, was reported to ameliorate LPS-induced lung histopathological changes [11]. In addition, Cyanidin-3-O-β-glucoside, a typical dietary anthocyanin, facilitated LPS-induced ALI via upregulating the NR1H3-ABCG1 pathway, which subsequently inhibited TLR4-mediated inflammatory response [42]. In this study, LAH preadministration effectively promoted NR1H3, ABCA1, ABCG1, and PPARγ protein expression in lung tissues, which may play an anti-inflammatory role in SALI mice.

NR1H3 is a well-established regulator of inflammatory gene expression and cholesterol homeostasis, with its agonists showing therapeutic potential in various diseases [15]. Our results showed that LAH, like NR1H3 agonists T0901317, could promote NR1H3 transcription factor activity and gene expression. Notably, LAH did not significantly alter mRNA or protein levels of NR1H2, PPARα, or RXRα, suggesting that LAH exhibits a certain degree of selectivity in its action on these nuclear receptors, and its regulation of NR1H3 does not involve a non-specific, generalized effect on members of the nuclear receptor family. Consistent with in vivo observations, LAH restored the LPS-suppressed expression of NR1H3 pathway-related proteins in THP-1 cells. A previous study reported that platycodin D protects against LPS-induced lung inflammation through two mechanisms. One involves up-regulation of the NR1H3-ABCA1 axis to promote cholesterol efflux, and the other involves the reduction of lipid raft cholesterol levels to suppress LPS-triggered NF-κB and IRF3 activation [43]. Consistent with their findings, we found that LAH also attenuated LPS-induced cholesterol accumulation in lipid rafts—specialized membrane microdomains essential for TLR4 signal transduction [44]. In our study, the results demonstrated that LAH inhibited recruitment of TLR4 to lipid rafts by 30.4%. While neither LAH nor T0901317 affected TLR4 protein expression, both effectively suppressed nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and NLRP3 gene expression. Therefore, we announce for the first time that LAH facilitated NR1H3 pathway, which led to disrupting lipid rafts by depleting cholesterol and decreasing the translocation of TLR4 to lipid rafts, thereby restraining LPS-triggered inflammatory response in THP-1 cells.

Molecular simulation, SPR, and CETSA were employed to investigate the potential of LAH as an NR1H3 activator. Molecular docking results indicated a binding energy of −9.81 kcal/mol between LAH and NR1H3. Notably, LAH formed hydrogen bonds with key residues (LEU-441, GLY-443, LYS-431, SER-427) of NR1H3, indicating that the “NR1H3-LAH” complex has binding stability. Furthermore, the MD simulation results over a 100 ns duration confirmed the stable positioning of LAH within the ligand-binding pocket of NR1H3. According to the SPRi parameter KD = 1.83 × 10−4 (M), a strong affinity exists between NR1H3 and LAH. The analysis of CETSA demonstrated that the relative expression of NR1H3 protein increased after binding to LAH at temperatures of 40–60 °C, and the binding showed a dose-dependent increasing trend. These results imply that LAH is a potential NR1H3 activator.

Mounting evidence indicates that there is a close relationship between NR1H3 and NLRP3 in various diseases. Lei et al. [45] found that NR1H3 agonist T0901317 alleviated amyloid β-induced retinal inflammation by suppressing NF-κB signaling and the expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, and IL-1β. Terasaka et al. [46] reported that T0901317 and GW3695 inhibited IL-1β production, Caspase-1 cleavage, ASC oligomerization, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby improving alum-induced peritonitis in mice. Additionally, NR1H3 directly repressed NLRP3 activity to ameliorate sepsis-induced heart failure [9]. These studies suggested that NR1H3 could potentially act as a therapeutic target for NLRP3-driven disorders. To further investigate the mechanism of NR1H3 involved in the anti-inflammatory effect of LAH, we applied NR1H3 inhibitor GSK2033 or si-NR1H3 to pretreat cells before establishing the model. Our data demonstrated that the stimulative impact of LAH on the expression of PPARγ, NR1H3, ABCA1, and ABCG1 was partially attenuated after inhibition of NR1H3. Moreover, the inhibitory effects of LAH on LPS + ATP-triggered NLRP3 pathway activation and pyroptosis were partially abolished in vitro by NR1H3 inhibition. In depth, NLRP3 inflammasome participated in the protective mechanism of NR1H3 in SALI, and the anti-inflammatory properties of LAH targeting NR1H3 for activation may be partially ascribed to its ability to repress NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Furthermore, consistent with the findings from our study, Liu et al. [47] also revealed that the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome could be inhibited by damaging the integrity of lipid rafts via NR1H3 activation. Therefore, based on prior research, a functionally relevant crosstalk may exist between NR1H3 and NLRP3 signaling, meriting continued investigation.

Our research demonstrates that LAH, a novel NR1H3 agonist, protects against SALI by blocking the recruitment and signal transduction of TLR4 by disrupting the integrity of lipid rafts, thereby suppressing NLRP3-GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, but there are still several limitations that warrant further investigation. Firstly, we acknowledge that while intraperitoneal LPS injection is a well-established and reproducible model for investigating SALI, it does not fully replicate the complexity of polymicrobial sepsis, involving slower and more sustained release of pathogens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [48]. Although a CS-induced SALI model was also conducted to investigate the pharmacological effects of LAH and preliminarily explore its mechanism of action, further experiments are needed to validate these findings. Furthermore, THP-1 cells are widely used in inflammation and pyroptosis research. However, as they are a modified cell line, they may not fully replicate the phenotypic and functional characteristics of primary human monocytes or alveolar macrophages (key effector cells in lung injury). The “LPS plus ATP” stimulation protocol specifically activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, but clinical sepsis may simultaneously activate other inflammasomes (e.g., AIM2 and NLRC4), which were not ascertained in the present study [49]. While our in vitro assays suggest that LAH positively influences SALI effects and inhibits the NLRP3-GSDMD pyroptosis pathway via its NR1H3 agonism, experiments using NR1H3 knockout mice are required in future investigations to rule out potential off-target effects in vivo. Moreover, LAH's unique effects on NLRP3 transcription and subsequent pyroptotic pathways compared to other NR1H3 agonists, such as T0901317, warrant further exploration. Transcriptional patterns may vary significantly among NR1H3 agonists with different structures. Future studies should synthesise and screen LAH derivatives to enhance solubility, reduce toxicity, and improve selectivity for NR1H3. Last but not least, comprehensive studies on the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity of LAH in the context of sepsis are essential prerequisites for clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study shows that LAH attenuates SALI, Furthermore, we found that LAH inhibits pneumonia and pyroptosis by directly activating NR1H3 and suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Fig. 9).
