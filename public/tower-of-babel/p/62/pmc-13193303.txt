The “No Man’s Land” of Nasal Subunits: A Case Series and Algorithm for Reconstruction
Authors: Alexa De la Fuente Hagopian, Sebastian Guadarrama-Sistos Vazquez, Souha Farhat, Rodger H. Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The area in the lower third of the nose between the tip and the ala is a small concavity between 2 convex subunits. It lies adjacent to the soft triangle and the nasal sidewall. The senior author termed this region the “no man’s land” of nasal reconstruction, as it does not fall within any of the subunits. This region, at the junction of 4 nasal subunits, has not been widely discussed, despite being one of the most challenging areas to achieve optimal aesthetic outcomes.Methods:A retrospective review of medical records was conducted for patients who underwent nasal reconstruction of wounds involving the no man’s land region between 2012 and 2023. Fifty patients underwent nasal reconstruction. Cases were categorized based on defect size, involved nasal subunits, distance from the alar rim, reconstructive technique, and need for cartilage grafts.Results:Five surgical techniques were used for reconstruction, the most common being the paramedian forehead flap (25 cases), followed by nasolabial flaps (11 cases), bilobed flaps (9 cases), full-thickness skin graft (4 cases), and V-Y flaps (1 case). Ear and septal cartilage grafts were required in 25 cases and 1 case, respectively. An algorithm was developed to assist in choosing the best reconstructive technique based on defect characteristics, surgical experience, and findings from this case series.Conclusions:We describe the no man’s land of nasal reconstruction as the junction of the nasal tip, ala, soft triangle, and sidewall. We discuss the challenges this area poses and present an algorithm to guide surgeon decision-making in managing defects in this area.

Takeaways

Question: What is the optimal approach to reconstruct the challenging concave area between the nasal tip and ala, where multiple nasal subunits converge?

Findings: We present a case series and an algorithm addressing the unique challenges of reconstructing this “no man’s land” region of the nose. Our findings emphasize that achieving superior results in this complex region relies on sound decision-making, meticulous attention to detail, and precise technical execution, providing a guide for optimal patient outcomes.

Meaning: Reconstructing the nasal no man’s land requires specialized strategies for the best aesthetic and functional results.

INTRODUCTION

The concept of nasal aesthetic subunits, and the emphasis on the placement of incisions along the borders of aesthetic regions, is a central guiding principle in all nasal reconstruction cases.1,2 There are 9 aesthetic subunits of the nose identified by convex and concave surfaces separated from one another by ridges and valleys,3 which include the nasal tip, dorsum, paired sidewalls, paired alae, paired soft triangles, and the columella. The subunit principle described by Burget and Menick4 explains that scars that fall along the boundaries of nasal subunits are less conspicuous. This is of foremost importance, especially in convex subunits, such as the tip and ala, due to the tendency for flap reconstructions in these areas to develop pincushioning. They propose that if any wound involves more than 50% of a convex subunit, then the remainder of the subunit should be excised to allow reconstruction of the entire subunit as a single aesthetic unit.4,5

The area in the lower third of the nose that lies between the tip and ala is a small concavity between 2 convex subunits. It also lies at the confluence of the tip, ala, soft triangle, and sidewall subunits. Although there have been many descriptions of the use of the subunit principle, and many articles have discussed potential reconstruction algorithms for individual subunits, this area at the junction of these 4 subunits has not been widely discussed, despite being one of the most challenging areas to obtain an optimal aesthetic outcome. Through years of practice performing nasal reconstruction, the senior author has found this to be an area that can be challenging both from a decision-making standpoint, regarding which reconstructive technique would be best, and from a technical standpoint in achieving optimal aesthetic outcomes. He has termed this area the “no man’s land” of nasal reconstruction, as it does not fall within any of the standard 9 subunits (Fig. 1). We define it as the area at the junction between the ala, tip, soft triangle, and sidewall. We present a case series of patients with nasal wounds involving the no man’s land area requiring reconstruction by the senior author and discuss the insights gained and a potential algorithm for reconstruction in this area.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

Data were collected retrospectively from the medical records of patients who underwent nasal reconstruction of wounds involving the junction between the ala, tip, soft triangle, and sidewall, designated by the authors as no man’s land, by the senior surgeon between 2012 and 2023. Procedure data were then linked to hospital-encoded encounter data to obtain other metrics and patient demographics. The study was approved by the institutional review board at Houston Methodist Hospital. The following variables were collected for each patient: defect size, distance from the alar rim, number of subunits involved, specific subunits involved, reconstruction technique, and cartilage graft use.

Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 18 years of age and patients with wounds in other areas not involving the no man’s land. Data analyses were performed using Excel Version 16.44 (Redmond, WA).

RESULTS

A total of 50 patients underwent nasal reconstruction of defects involving the no man’s land, performed by the senior surgeon. Defect sizes averaged 1.69 cm ± 0.75 mm, using the lesion’s biggest measure. The alar rim was involved in 18 cases, and for the remainder, the average distance from the lesion to the rim was 2.29 ± 1.16 mm (Table 1). The average number of subunits involved was 3, with the ala being involved in all cases, followed by the tip in 47 cases (94%) and the sidewall in 36 cases (72%). Other subunits were affected less frequently, with the soft triangle affected in 12 cases (24%), the dorsum affected in 6 cases (12%), and the columella remaining unaffected in all cases (Table 2).

This assessment includes defect characteristics (size, depth, subunits involved, and distance from the alar rim), as well as individual patient factors (previous surgery, scars, donor site suitability, and comorbidities that may influence the decision to proceed with larger staged reconstructions or general anesthesia). Our Approach to No Man’s Land of Nasal Reconstruction

Algorithm for no man’s land reconstruction.

Full-thickness skin graft: For small (≤1 cm) and superficial defects, a color-matched full-thickness skin graft is the primary consideration. Given that the no man’s land is a unique area of concavity, a skin graft may be the best option to maintain the natural thinness and overall nasal shape while limiting donor site morbidity. Donor sites include the lateral forehead and the preauricular, postauricular, and supraclavicular areas, with the lateral forehead and preauricular sites typically providing the best texture and color match. Patients with thinner, less sebaceous skin tend to yield better results. Postoperative dermabrasion and meticulous scar care are used to address potential graft edge irregularities and color mismatch (Fig. 3).

Local nasal flaps (1-stage): Local nasal flaps, specifically the bilobed flap or a V-Y advancement flap along the alar groove, may provide a reasonable 1-stage reconstruction option for small wounds (<1.3 cm in size) in the more superior aspect of the no man’s land. This approach is limited to smaller defects to minimize the risk of nasal shape distortion and asymmetry. Meticulous care in the design and execution of these flaps is essential to prevent irreversible distortion of nasal shape.6

Two-stage flap reconstruction: A 2-stage flap reconstruction is required for larger wounds (>1–1.3 cm), deep defects, and/or those within 2 mm of the nostril rim. This staged approach is necessary to avoid distortion, such as depression or retraction/notching, of the alar rim. The choice between a superiorly based nasolabial flap (Fig. 4) or a paramedian forehead flap (Fig. 5) is based on the wound size, patient anatomy, and comorbidities. Due to the high risk of contracture in this area, these defects usually require a conchal cartilage graft placed along the nostril rim for structural bolster. Furthermore, when a 2-stage flap reconstruction is needed, the subunit principle is liberally applied to convex subunits, routinely excising the remainder of the ala or tip if more than 30%–40% of the subunit is involved in the wound.7

DISCUSSION

The nose is the central feature of the face, and even minor irregularities and asymmetries are easily noticed. The nose is a common location for skin cancers, and treatment of these skin cancers, usually through Mohs surgery, can leave patients with wounds of various sizes, shapes, and locations on the nose that require skilled reconstruction. Patients expect to look “normal” after their reconstruction. Many books and articles have been written about nasal reconstruction, including many different techniques and algorithms for different nasal subunits.8 Different surgical techniques to obtain cosmetically appealing and functional results, while also considering factors such as postoperative pain and tissue scarring, have been widely established. However, these are all outcome-based and do not provide insight into preoperative decision-making.9,10

The lower third of the nose, made up of the nasal tip, alae, and soft triangles, has a unique 3-dimensional shape and no excess skin laxity, making reconstruction in this area particularly challenging.11 Previous literature reports outstanding results using nasolabial local flaps when single-area lesions are identified in the nasal tip or ala.12 However, in the senior author’s practice, referrals for nasal reconstruction were frequently in the area at the junction of the tip and ala—a small concavity between 2 convex subunits.

This area can often involve multiple subunits, even with a small defect, making an optimal aesthetic reconstruction difficult. We have termed this zone the no man’s land of nasal reconstruction, as it lies at the confluence of the tip, ala, soft triangle, and sidewall subunits, and has not been widely discussed in the literature despite its challenges. The findings from this case series have been consolidated into an algorithm discussed in the Results section (Fig. 2) to provide a structured approach to technique selection for defects in this area.

The chief challenge with flap reconstruction in this no man’s land is managing the transition between the naturally thin, concave subunit and the thicker, convex surrounding subunits. Flaps, by their nature, can be thicker and prone to pincushioning, which creates an unsightly convexity in an area that should be concave. It is therefore imperative to recreate the thinner concavity when performing flap reconstruction.

Minimizing pincushioning requires several surgical considerations. First, accurate template design and flap sizing are crucial, as oversized flaps are more prone to this complication.13 Second, aggressive flap thinning is often necessary because the skin of a forehead or cheek flap is frequently thicker than the native skin, especially if a nonanatomical cartilage graft for alar rim support has been placed. Third, strategic suture placement is immensely helpful during the division and inset stage; specifically, using quilting sutures that originate intranasally and tack the undersurface of the flap down into the concavity. Finally, insetting the flap under slight tension and using mattress sutures promotes tissue eversion and minimizes the risk of pincushioning.

Although the presented algorithm is a solid guide, it must be applied with flexibility, as multiple patient-specific factors influence the final decision. These include the patient’s nasal skin thickness, donor site suitability, comorbidities, and their preference regarding staged reconstruction. For instance, in patients with very thin, less sebaceous skin, the indications for a full-thickness skin graft may be expanded to deeper or larger defects. The choice between a paramedian forehead flap and a nasolabial flap can be dictated by the patient’s anatomy (eg, short forehead favoring a nasolabial flap) or preference (eg, preference or need for local anesthesia favoring a nasolabial flap).14

It is crucial to set realistic patient expectations, as reconstruction in this challenging area often requires revisions. For skin grafts, dermabrasion is typically performed at 4–6 weeks postoperatively. For flap reconstructions, reevaluation for potential revision (eg, flap thinning or scar revision) is performed after the patient has healed for at least 3 months.

LIMITATIONS

The primary limitation is the retrospective, descriptive case series design, which precludes the use of statistical analysis to validate the outcomes. Consequently, the proposed algorithm is based on the senior author’s extensive clinical experience.

Furthermore, the generalizability of these findings may be limited, as the patient cohort is drawn from a single institution. A prospective study would be the ideal method for validating this algorithm and its utility; however, such research is inherently challenging in reconstructive surgery due to the heterogeneous nature of defects and the significant influence of patient preferences on surgical decision-making. Future prospective research is necessary.

CONCLUSIONS

Optimal results in nasal reconstruction are subject to patient evaluation; however, attention to detail and accurate technical execution are crucial for decision-making. The specific challenges that the no man’s land area poses have been described, and the authors present an algorithm derived from the senior surgeon’s experience with this patient cohort; this algorithm is a structured guide designed to assist the reader in sound decision-making and accurate technical execution required to achieve superior results in nasal reconstruction for this challenging junction.
