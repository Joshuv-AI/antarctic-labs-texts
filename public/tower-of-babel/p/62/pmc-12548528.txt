Two attempts at transcatheter device closure of an acquired Gerbode defect following mitral valve replacement: a case report

Introduction

A Gerbode defect is defined as an abnormal communication between the left ventricle (LV) and right atrium (RA). This defect, first described by Frank Gerbode in 1958,1 is a rare congenital or acquired anomaly.2 Previous cardiac surgery, particularly valve replacement procedures, is the most common cause of acquired Gerbode defects.3–5 This malformation allows direct shunting of blood from the LV to the RA, which leads to right ventricular volume overload, pulmonary artery hypertension, and heart failure. As a first-line imaging modality, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is frequently used to diagnose Gerbode defects.6 Although surgical correction remains the gold standard of treatment, transcatheter closure is a viable alternative for high-risk surgical candidates. Herein, we present a case of an acquired Gerbode defect secondary to mitral valve replacement, which was successfully treated with percutaneous device closure.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old female patient with rheumatic heart disease underwent a second mechanical mitral valve replacement in March 2024. She had a history of subdural haematoma because of over-anticoagulation in June 2024. On admission, she presented with persistent dyspnoea and a sensation of heaviness at the right subcostal region. Clinical examination revealed signs of right heart failure. The mechanical valve sound was clear on auscultation, with a grade III/VI systolic murmur at the lower left parasternal area and a high-pitched grade III/VI murmur at the third intercostal space.

TTE confirmed that the mechanical mitral valve was functioning normally. However, severe tricuspid regurgitation was noted, with a systolic pressure gradient of 70 mmHg and dilated right heart chambers. A high-velocity jet from the LV to the RA was suspected on the apical five-chamber view. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a 14-mm left-to-right shunt in the atrioventricular membranous septum, with a pressure gradient of 60 mmHg. The TEE findings were corroborated by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging (Figure 1).

(A) Doppler demonstrate a large left-to-right shunt from the LV to the RA. The left upper and right upper arrows indicate the defect and shunt flow, respectively. (B) CMR imaging, four-chamber view showing shunt flow from the LV to the RA (center lower arrow).

Subsequently, the patient underwent transcatheter device closure. During the procedure, elevated pressures were recorded in the inferior vena cava and RA (mean pressure: 42 mmHg) (normal mean RA pressure ∼4 mmHg), a moderate elevation in the mean pulmonary artery pressure to 46 mmHg was noted, and a 16-mm LV-to-RA shunt was identified (Figure 2). The defect was closed using a 20 × 18-mm duct occluder (Cocoon Duct Occluder, Vascular Innovations Co.), and appropriate device placement with minimal residual shunt was confirmed via TTE (Figure 3).

Left ventricular angiography showing contrast passage from the left ventricle to the right atrium through a defect (diameter: ∼16 mm).

Closure of the Gerbode defect (yellow arrow) with minimal residual shunt. The contrast agent can be seen passing through the device into the right atrium.

Following closure of the defect, the patient developed severe, acute left heart failure, with a reduced ejection fraction (EF) of 30%. Her pro-B-type natriuretic peptide level was markedly elevated (5500 pg/mL) (normal value < 125 pg/mL), and her troponin T level was 540 ng/L (normal value < 14 ng/L). Her heart failure was managed with intensive medical therapy for over one week, which resulted in clinical improvement and EF recovery. However, haematuria developed after EF recovery, likely because of residual flow through the device. Over the following two weeks, conservative management, including temporary withdrawal of anticoagulants, fluid infusion, red blood cell transfusions, and intravenous corticosteroids, failed to resolve the haematuria. Repeat TEE revealed a 2.8-mm high-velocity para-device residual shunt and moderate tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 4).

TEE biplane with colour Doppler demonstrating a para-device residual shunt. (A) 125° (left arrow, shunt flow; right arrow, device in position). (B) 45° (left and upper right arrows, residual shunt flow; middle left arrow, device in position; lower right arrow, tricuspid regurgitation). TEE, transoesophageal echocardiography.

After thoroughly discussing the risks and benefits of the available treatment options, we decided to proceed with transcatheter reintervention. In the catheterization laboratory, the original device was retrieved using a snare (Figure 5A, Video 1). Paroxysmal atrial tachycardia occurred during the procedure, which was converted to sinus rhythm with intravenous amiodarone infusion. Subsequently, a larger 20 × 22-mm Cera PDA Occluder (Lifetech Scientific Corporation) was deployed (Figure 5B, Videos 2, 3). TTE confirmed the proper placement of the new device, with no residual shunt or interference with surrounding structures. After the second intervention, the patient’s haematuria resolved, and her heart failure symptoms markedly improved. Anticoagulation with warfarin was resumed for the mechanical mitral valve. The patient was discharged 10 days later. Five months post-discharge, follow-up revealed that the patient remained stable with no signs of heart failure, reflecting the positive outcomes of the ongoing recovery process.

Discussion

Our patient underwent a bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement 13 years ago and mechanical replacement 6 months ago. After the second surgery, she experienced persistent right heart failure. Initially, we suspected severe tricuspid regurgitation, which is typically less amenable to conservative management during open-heart surgery. However, only mild pulmonary regurgitation (PR) and slightly elevated peak PR velocity were noted on continuous-wave Doppler, helping to rule out pulmonary hypertension. Moreover, TEE clearly demonstrated LV-to-RA communication, and CMR imaging objectively confirmed the shunt and precisely localized the defect in three dimensions along with the adjacent structures. Gerbode defects can be misdiagnosed as severe tricuspid regurgitation.7 Given that the patient had undergone mitral valve replacements twice and did not have current or previous infections, an acquired surgery-related Gerbode defect was suspected. The patient’s recent surgical history and evidence of right heart failure raised significant risks for a third open-heart surgery. Previous studies have demonstrated successful percutaneous closures of such defects. This minimally invasive approach avoids the need for repeated surgery and enables a faster recovery.8,9 Given our centre’s expertise in complex interventional cardiology, we chose a transcatheter device closure approach. Initially, we selected the largest available duct occluder device to close the defect. The duct occluder was chosen because it has only one disc at the left side of the device. This allows the device to shift along its longitudinal axis, minimizing the risk of complete atrioventricular block and decreasing compression of the surrounding structures.10 Unfortunately, the chosen device appeared to be slightly smaller than the defect size, resulting in a small residual shunt. The reduced EF of the LV after device closure masked haemolysis complications, which became evident as the LV’s function recovered. As haemolysis was not self-limiting, conservative treatment was deemed inadequate. There were several alternatives to manage the residual shunt, e.g. surgical removal of the device followed by defect closure with a patch, placing an additional small device at the residual shunt, or replacing the original device with a larger one.11 Ultimately, we proceeded with a second transcatheter device closure. Based on thorough echocardiographic and angiographic evaluation, we estimated that a device one size larger than the previous one could resolve the issue. Moreover, open-heart surgery would be avoided assuming that we could remove and replace the device with a larger one. Therefore, we ordered a 20/22-mm duct occluder device. To ensure the success of the procedure, we chose the Cera PDA Occluder (Lifetech Scientific Corporation), which features a polytetrafluoroethylene membrane securely sewn into the device to lessen the risk of intradevice residual shunt caused by high-pressure blood flow. This approach yielded excellent results.

Gerbode defects are rare but have been increasingly reported, particularly in patients with cardiac surgery or intervention history. TEE is valuable for obtaining a definitive diagnosis. Initially, the diagnosis was not obvious but was later identified based on clinical suspicion and the combined use of two- and three-dimensional TTE and TEE. Although surgical repair remains the traditional treatment approach, advancements in interventional cardiology have made transcatheter closure a viable alternative, particularly in cases of Gerbode defects acquired after heart surgery. To the best of our knowledge, no similar cases have been reported in the domestic medical literature.
