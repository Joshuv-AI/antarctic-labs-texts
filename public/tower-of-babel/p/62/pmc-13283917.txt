Heritability of Alzheimer's disease–related plasma biomarkers in the Amish population

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPlasma biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease (AD) hold promise for disease diagnosis and prediction, yet their genetic underpinnings remain under explored.METHODSWe measured plasma amyloid beta (Aβ)40, Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40, total tau (t‐tau), phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181), Aβ42/t‐tau, Aβ42/p‐tau181, neurofilament light chain, and glial fibrillary acidic protein in the Midwestern Amish. Pedigree‐based heritability (hped2) was estimated from multigenerational pedigrees, and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)–based heritability (hSNP2) was derived from SNPs.RESULTSAmong all Amish individuals, additive genetic effects (hped2) explained 11.1% to 36.6% of biomarker variances. hSNP2 estimates were consistently lower, ranging from 6.7% to 28.7%. The heritability of these biomarkers in subgroups of cognitively normal individuals and apolipoprotein E ε4 non‐carriers yielded similar results.DISCUSSIONPlasma biomarkers such as Aβ, t‐tau, and p‐tau181 are moderately heritable in the Amish, underscoring the impact of genetic determinants of plasma biomarkers associated with AD.Keywords: Amish, amyloid beta, glial fibrillary acidic protein, heritability, neurofilament light chain, plasma biomarkers, tau

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia. 1 The complexity of AD arises from the interplay between genetic and environmental influences on biological and lifestyle risk factors. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have identified > 70 loci associated with late‐onset AD (LOAD), 2 , 3 yet the full genetic architecture remains unclear. Twin studies estimated AD heritability at 37% to 79%; 4 , 5 , 6 however, common variations explain only 12% to 57%, with apolipoprotein E (APOE) contributing up to 27%. 7 This gap, coupled with diagnostic misclassification rates up to 30% 8 and the limitations of binary disease classifications, 2 , 3 , 9 highlights the need for quantitative endophenotypes to advance understanding of AD genetics.

In AD, the primary pathological features are the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and tau tangles in the brain. 10 , 11 Recently, neurofilament light chain (NfL) has emerged as a general biomarker for neuroaxonal damage, 12 and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) reflects neuroinflammation and astrocytic activation, 13 both central to AD development. Although these biomarkers can be quantified in vivo using neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, plasma biomarkers offer scalable, non‐invasive measures across all AD stages. Establishing their heritability is essential to identify biomarkers with sufficient genetic signal for GWAS locus discovery, prioritize them as genetically informed endophenotypes, and support the integration of polygenic risk scores with biomarker data. Despite their potential, heritability estimates remain limited and variable: early work in LOAD families reported 40.7% to 56.1% for plasma Aβ species, 14 while recent twin studies reported heritability ranging from 16% for the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio to 60% for GFAP, 15 , 16 with key biomarkers like phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)181 underexplored. Robust heritability estimates are critical for developing individualized approaches that integrate biomarker and genetic data to stratify risk and guide interventions.

We estimated the heritability of plasma Aβ40, Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40, total tau (t‐tau), p‐tau181, Aβ42/t‐tau, Aβ42/p‐tau181, NfL, and GFAP levels in the Midwestern Amish, using their extensive pedigree and genotype data. We hypothesized that these plasma biomarkers are heritable, and that pedigree‐based estimates would exceed single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‐based estimates due to contributions from rare variants and non‐additive genetic effects. The extended multigenerational families and relatively uniform lifestyles in the Amish provide a powerful tool to estimate the heritability with less influence from the variability in the shared environment. 17

METHODS

Study population

We analyzed data from the Collaborative Amish Aging and Memory Project (CAAMP), a prospective study investigating successful aging and dementia within the Amish communities of Ohio (OH) and Indiana (IN). The study protocol for CAAMP has been previously described. 18 , 19 Briefly, a door‐to‐door interview was used to gather demographic information, medical histories, blood samples, and cognitive assessments. An all‐connecting pedigree was generated using records from the Anabaptist Genealogy Database. 20

Clinical phenotypes

Upon enrollment, all participants underwent cognitive screening. Based on established cutoffs, 19 a clinical adjudication team of neurologists and neuropsychologists determined a consensus diagnosis for each Amish participant. Individuals were categorized as cognitively unimpaired (CU), having mild cognitive impairments (MCI), cognitively impaired but not AD (CINAD), or cognitively impaired related to AD (AD). A few individuals requiring additional follow‐up could not be initially characterized and were excluded from the analysis.

Plasma biomarkers and quality control

Plasma biomarkers were measured from frozen samples stored in 500 µL aliquots at −80°C, using the Quanterix HD‐X platform. Specifically, Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels were initially measured using the Simoa Neurology 3‐Plex A (N3PA) assay, then transitioned to the Neurology 4‐Plex E (N4PE) assay after its release in 2017. Notably, Aβ40 and Aβ42 were measured with the same assays (both N3PA or both N4PE) for given samples, ensuring internal consistency of the Aβ42/40 ratio. T‐tau levels were assessed using the N3PA assay, while NfL and GFAP levels were measured with the N4PE assay, and p‐tau181 was assessed using the Simoa P‐tau181 Advantage V2 assay. All results were above the detection limits.

Quality control (QC) procedures were conducted at the individual biomarker level (Figure S1 in supporting information). Note that the outlier cut‐offs were defined by the median absolute deviation, which is more robust in detecting outliers for skewed distributions given its reliance on non‐parametric measures of central tendency and variation. 21 Only one to three samples fell beyond the outlier cut‐off per biomarker. The analysis focused on the most recent measurements from biomarkers that passed QC.

Genotyping, imputation, and QC

Genotyping was conducted using the Illumina Expanded Multi‐Ethnic Genotyping Array (MEGAEX) with custom content 22 or the Illumina Global Screening Array (GSA). 23 The MEGAEX chip includes > 2 million markers, while the GSA has 660,000 markers, with an additional 6000 novel variants specific to the Amish population included. 24 , 25

Imputation was carried out using the Michigan Imputation Server and the Trans‐Omics for Precision Medicine panel. 26 The MEGAEX and GSA array data were imputed separately, using the GRCh38 build for autosomes and hg19 for the X chromosome. QC procedures were conducted using PLINK 2.0 to assess family relationships, sex discrepancies, and batch effects. SNPs were filtered based on low call rates (missing > 5%), minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.05), and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE < 1e‐6). Ultimately, 4,403,280 SNPs passed the QC across both genotyping platforms. This QC process was repeated for biomarker‐specific datasets before heritability estimation.

Statistical analysis

To compare continuous variables with non‐normal distributions, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐squared test. We applied the Bonferroni method to correct for multiple comparisons at the threshold of α = 0.0056 (0.05 divided by 9 biomarkers).

A prior analysis of Amish genealogy established a large 14‐generation pedigree encompassing all Amish individuals. 27 Individuals in this study were organized into smaller pedigrees based on shared grandparental lineage. Biomarkers were standardized using a rank‐based inverse normal transformation. Pedigree‐based heritability (hped2) was estimated using a variance component model in the Sequential Oligogenic Linkage Analysis Routines (SOLAR) packages, 28 adjusting for age, sex, diagnosis, study center, biomarker batches, and the presence or absence of one or more APOE ε4 alleles (coded as 0 = non‐carrier, 1 = carrier) to account for the strong known genetic effect of APOE on AD‐related traits. These covariates were applied consistently across all models, including those for composite ratio outcomes. The estimates therefore represent covariate‐adjusted h 2, capturing residual genetic variance beyond APOE, rather than total heritability inclusive of APOE’s contribution. The statistical significance of hped2 was determined by a likelihood ratio test. SOLAR uses a maximum likelihood estimate to analyze pedigrees of arbitrary size and is designed to handle discrete and quantitative traits with covariate adjustments. To estimate SNP‐based heritability (hSNP2), individual‐level genotype data were used to derive empirical kinship matrices using KING (version 2.7). hSNP2 was then calculated using the QuantHer method in LDAK (version 4.9), 29 with the same covariates as above.

We first estimated the heritability of biomarkers in the entire Amish cohort (All‐Amish group). For sensitivity analyses, we repeated the calculation in two subgroups: cognitively unimpaired individuals (CU‐only group) and individuals without APOE ε4 alleles (non‐APOE ε4 group). These subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of heritability estimates when the potential influence of cognitive status or APOE ε4 genotype was minimized.

hped2 and hSNP2 among all Amish individuals

As shown in Table 2, the heritability analysis in the All‐Amish group revealed notable genetic influences across various measures, with pedigree‐based heritability generally exceeding SNP‐based estimates. Among all evaluated biomarkers, p‐tau181 exhibited the highest heritability, with both pedigree‐based (36.6%) and SNP‐based (28.7%) estimates reaching statistical significance (p < 0.001). The heritability for other tau‐related biomarkers (t‐tau, Aβ42/t‐tau, and Aβ42/p‐tau181) was less pronounced. Aβ biomarkers (Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ42/Aβ40) showed moderate pedigree‐based heritability ranging from 22.7% to 30.0% (p ≤ 0.007), with slightly lower but significant SNP‐based heritability estimates (17.9%–22.4%, p ≤ 0.034). In contrast, plasma NfL and GFAP presented lower, non‐significant heritability estimates in both pedigree‐based (11.1%–21.1%, p ≥ 0.207) and SNP‐based analyses (6.7%–16.3%, p ≥ 0.337).

DISCUSSION

We assessed the heritability of plasma Aβ40, Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40, t‐tau, p‐tau181, Aβ42/t‐tau, Aβ42/p‐tau181, NfL, and GFAP levels in the Midwestern Amish. For each biomarker, we calculated both the pedigree‐based heritability and SNP‐based heritability. In the All‐Amish group, p‐tau181 showed the highest heritability (hped2 = 36.6%; hSNP2 = 28.7%), while GFAP exhibited the lowest (hped2 = 11.1%; hSNP2 = 6.7%). Overall, our findings are consistent with heritability estimates in previous studies, 14 , 15 , 16 highlighting the substantial genetic contribution to the variation of AD‐related plasma biomarkers. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively estimate both pedigree‐based and SNP‐based heritability of AD‐related plasma biomarkers within a family‐based design.

Pedigree‐based versus SNP‐based heritability

We observed consistently higher hped2 than hSNP2, which likely reflects the contribution of more complex genetic models and rare variants not detected by SNP‐based approaches. 30 The pedigree‐based approach considers the overall genetic relatedness and is more likely to capture the influence of rare variants as well as other inherited genomic features, such as structural variants and epistatic interactions, leading to higher heritability estimates. 31 Moreover, the Amish had relatively uniform shared environmental factors such as education, diet, and health behaviors. 32 This homogeneity may reduce environmental variance, making variation explained by genetic components more apparent in pedigree‐based analysis.

Heritability of plasma Aβ biomarkers

The hped2 of Aβ biomarkers (Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ42/Aβ40) ranged from 22.7% to 30.0%, and the hSNP2 estimates were between 17.9% and 22.4%. Overall, our heritability estimates of individual Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels were lower than previously reported values. 14 , 16 Specifically, Ertekin‐Taner et al. reported estimates of 40.7% to 56.1% in LOAD families, likely reflecting ascertainment of families with higher genetic loading, younger cohort age, differences in biomarker arrays, and additive‐only variance component modeling. 14 Notably, the plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was significantly heritable, in contrast to recent studies that found much lower heritability for this ratio. 15 , 16 The higher heritability observed for Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio may be due to the advanced age of individuals in this study (mean > 80 years) and relatively homogeneous lifestyle in the Amish, reducing environmental variance. The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio shares many of the same loci that affect individual Aβ40 or Aβ42 levels. 33 , 34 However, some variants may preferentially alter the relative production or clearance of Aβ40 versus Aβ42, 33 causing this ratio to have partially distinct genetic architecture compared to either peptide alone. Such complexity underscores the need for further investigation into the genetic pathways underlying Aβ biomarkers.

Heritability of plasma tau biomarkers

Plasma p‐tau181 levels were significantly higher in the AD group compared to the CU group, and p‐tau181 demonstrated the strongest heritability with a hped2 of 36.6% and hSNP2 of 28.7%. Elevated p‐tau181 levels are strongly associated with the disease onset, diagnosis, and progression, 35 and the substantial heritable component of p‐tau181 makes this marker a key candidate for identifying genetic loci linked to AD risk. A recent GWAS has shown that AD risk loci outside of APOE are primarily affecting plasma p‐tau181. 34 The hped2 and hSNP2 for plasma t‐tau levels were comparable between the All‐Amish and non‐APOE ε4 groups. However, plasma t‐tau did not appear to be heritable in the CU‐only group, with a hped2 of 1.9% and a hSNP2 of 0.9%. This could be due to the constrained phenotypic variance in this subgroup. Moreover, pathological changes in t‐tau are not specific to AD, and previous studies have demonstrated plasma t‐tau levels remain relatively low in cognitively normal individuals, and tau elevation is more pronounced in later stages of AD. 36

Heritability of composite biomarkers

Beyond single‐analyte concentrations, we examined Aβ42/t‐tau and Aβ42/p‐tau181 ratios that reflect the relative balance between amyloid and tau pathways rather than their absolute levels. These composite measures have been shown to improve discrimination between AD and control individuals in both CSF and plasma studies, and may better capture coordinated biological processes underlying AD pathophysiology. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Plasma Aβ42/t‐tau appears to be a heritable trait with heritability estimates comparable to or exceeding those of individual Aβ42 and t‐tau levels. It is also worth noting that the Aβ42/p‐tau181 ratio may be a superior measure in classifying AD in the Amish, as the AD group had a significantly lower Aβ42/p‐tau181 ratio compared to individuals with CINAD and CU individuals. However, the heritability of the Aβ42/p‐tau181 ratio was relatively low and not statistically significant. The reduced heritability likely reflects multiple factors. First, unlike Aβ42/Aβ40 for which both components were measured on the same type of assay, Aβ/tau ratios combine biomarkers from different assays, introducing additional technical variance and potentially increasing measurement error. Additionally, the ratio constructs may be under less direct genetic control—Aβ42 and p‐tau181 are regulated by distinct genetic pathways that may not synergize, leading to attenuated heritability for the composite measure. 34 , 41 While these ratios show clinical utility, they may be suboptimal genetic endophenotypes compared to individual biomarkers or same‐platform ratios.

Heritability of plasma NfL and GFAP

This study is among the first to estimate the heritability of plasma NfL and GFAP using a family‐based design. We found significantly elevated levels of plasma NfL and GFAP in Amish individuals with AD compared to CU individuals. Gillespie et al. 16 and Rousset et al. 15 reported NfL heritability of 42% to 43% and GFAP heritability up to 60%, exceeding our estimates. Several factors likely contribute to these discrepancies. First, the small sample sizes of NfL and GFAP in the Amish reduced the power to detect genetic effects. Post hoc analysis only showed ≈ 10% to 15% for h 2 = 0.20 and 20% to 25% for h 2 = 0.30 at Bonferroni‐corrected α level (α = 0.0056). Second, while twin studies can account for both additive and non‐additive genetic effects, they often do not consider the impact of shared environmental factors, which may lead to an overestimation of heritability. 42 , 43 In contrast, pedigree‐based and SNP‐based methods focus on additive effects, resulting in lower estimates if non‐additive effects contribute significantly. Finally, NfL and GFAP are general markers of neuronal injury and astrocytic activation that respond to diverse pathological processes. In our elderly cohort, accumulation of age‐related non‐genetic factors affecting neurodegeneration, inflammation, and vascular disease markers may contribute to lower heritability estimates. 44

Role of age in heritability analyses

Age consistently emerged as a significant covariate in all heritability models, highlighting its critical role in biomarker variability. Aging influences multiple biological systems, including the blood–brain barrier and neuroinflammatory pathways, affecting the production, clearance, and stability of fluid biomarkers. 45 Additionally, genetic influences on biomarker levels may vary across the lifespan, with some effects becoming more pronounced or diminishing over time. 46 It is essential for future studies to adjust for age in the genetic analysis to disentangle genetic contributions from age‐related changes and ensure more accurate heritability estimates.

Study limitations

This study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, pedigree‐based estimates assume negligible shared environmental effects; however, because informative pairs are predominantly first‐degree relatives, our h 2 estimates may partially reflect unmodeled familial environmental variance. Future models explicitly partitioning genetic and environmental variance would provide more precise estimates. Second, SNP‐based heritability relies on common variants and the underlying reference population used for imputation. Although Amish may have an enrichment of rare genetic variants due to the founder effect and limited gene flow, 25 rare variants contributing to biomarker variability may remain undetected. Third, some of the biomarker data in this study were obtained from historically collected plasma samples and measured using a mix of Simoa assays. To control for non‐biological variability, batch identifiers were included as covariates in all heritability models; however, it's important to note that composite biomarkers, especially Aβ/tau ratios, may amplify technical errors. Fourth, the AD diagnosis relies mostly on medical history and detailed cognitive measurements, as the Amish do not have imaging or CSF data available. We adjusted for non‐genetic factors, but detailed data on medications, vascular comorbidities, and other chronic conditions were not uniformly available, which may have introduced residual confounding of biomarker levels.

CONCLUSION

We leveraged both pedigree and genotype data in a family‐based design to estimate the heritability of core AD‐related biomarkers, and our findings confirmed that plasma Aβ and tau biomarkers have substantial heritable components. This study also emphasizes the importance of subgroup analyses in heritability estimation, as genetic influences on biomarkers may vary significantly across different study groups and disease stages. Future studies are needed to incorporate additional genetic and environmental factors to provide deeper insights into the determinants of plasma biomarkers associated with AD.
