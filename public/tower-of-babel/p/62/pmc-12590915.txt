Application of high-flow nasal oxygen during anesthesia induction for frame-based stereoelectroencephalography: a randomized controlled non-inferiority trial
Authors: Xiaofen Sun, Mingming Han, Zefeng Fan, Xiang Huang, Fang Kang, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) is increasingly used for preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation; however, its efficacy during anesthesia induction for frame-based stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) remains uncertain. This study evaluated whether HFNO is non-inferior to facemask ventilation in maintaining oxygenation during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG.MethodsIn this randomized, controlled, non-inferiority trial, adult patients with refractory epilepsy undergoing frame-based SEEG were randomized to either the HFNO or facemask group. The primary endpoint was the lowest peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2). Secondary endpoints included arterial blood gas analyses, hemodynamic measures, airway adjuncts use, first-pass intubation success (FPS), time to secure the airway, anesthesiologist satisfaction, patient comfort, and perioperative complications.ResultsAmong 46 patients, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) lowest SpO2 was 98 (97–99) % in the HFNO group vs. 96 (95–98) % in the facemask group, with a median difference of 1% (95 confidence interval [CI], 0–2%), confirming non-inferiority within a 2% margin. Post-intubation partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) was significantly higher in the HFNO group than that in the facemask group, with median (IQR) of 437.8 (411.1–474.4) mmHg and 400.6 (365.6–435.2) mmHg, respectively. HFNO reduced the airway adjuncts use (0/23 vs. 6/23 patients; p = 0.022), shortened the time to secure the airway (p = 0.015), and improved anesthesiologist satisfaction and patient comfort (both p < 0.0001). Hemodynamic parameters and FPS were similar between groups.ConclusionHFNO is non-inferior to facemask ventilation in maintaining oxygenation during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG. Moreover, HFNO reduces the need for airway adjuncts, shortens airway securement time, and enhances both patient and anesthesiologist satisfaction.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100044239; first registration date: March 12, 2021; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=119780).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-025-00619-4.

Introduction

Approximately 30% of the 70 million people with epilepsy worldwide have drug-resistant epilepsy, often requiring frame-based stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) for diagnosis and management (Thijs et al. 2019). This procedure poses significant anesthetic challenges to ensure patient safety and procedural success (Rajkalyan et al. 2019). While tracheal intubation prior to headframe placement is desirable, it is often precluded by factors such as high-risk transport of intubated patients (e.g., potential airway dislodgement), prolonged anesthesia, and limited resources (e.g., lack of anesthesia equipment in imaging centers) (Baker & Isert 1997; Cardinale et al. 2013). Consequently, headframe fixation under regional anesthesia followed by electrode implantation under general anesthesia is commonly adopted (van der Loo et al. 2017; Yao et al. 2023). This strategy, however, demands meticulous airway management because the headframe restricts access to the airway.

Effective preoxygenation is essential to maximize oxygen reserves for tracheal intubation, as inadequate reserves or prolonged intubation can lead to hypoxemia and related complications. Patients with epilepsy frequently exhibit emotional comorbidities that reduce their tolerance for tightly sealed facemasks, thereby impairing preoxygenation efficacy (Wei et al. 2021). Moreover, the headframe further complicates airway management by restricting facemask placement, compromising the facemask seal, and limiting cervical extension, all of which can impair ventilation and intubation performance (Bhutani et al. 2017; George et al. 2012; Liu et al., 2022) . Mannequin studies have demonstrated that the presence of a headframe significantly increases intubation failure rates and prolongs the time required to secure the airway (Brockerville et al. 2018; Dalby et al. 2022). In clinical scenarios, repeated intubation attempts are frequently associated with hypoxemia, airway trauma, headframe displacement, and neurological deterioration (Ferson et al. 2001; Kurnutala et al. 2015; York et al. 2009). Therefore, optimized airway management strategies that ensure patient comfort, adequate oxygenation, and headframe stability are critical for patients with refractory epilepsy undergoing frame-based SEEG.

High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) delivers heated, humidified oxygen at flow rates up to 70 L/min via a specialized nasal cannula, providing a stable fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), flushing of anatomical dead space, and generating mild positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) (Gustafsson et al. 2017; Hermez et al. 2019). Unlike traditional mask-based oxygen therapy, which depends on a tightly sealed facemask and often causes discomfort and airway dryness, HFNO provides efficient oxygenation while markedly improving patient comfort (Baldomero et al. 2021). In addition, HFNO can sustain apneic oxygenation and extend the safe apnea duration in anaesthetized patients (Kim et al. 2025; Langer et al. 2024). In the context of frame-based SEEG, the flexible nasal cannula of HFNO is fully compatible with the headframe. Its combination of superior comfort, reliable oxygenation, and compatibility with the headframe facilitates a controlled and unhurried induction of anesthesia.

We hypothesized that HFNO is non-inferior to facemask oxygenation in maintaining oxygen saturation during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG. This randomized controlled non-inferiority trial compared HFNO for preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation with facemask for preoxygenation and bag-mask ventilation (BMV).

Materials and methods

This prospective, randomized, controlled, non-inferiority study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (No. 2021KY088; May 10, 2021) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100044239; March 12, 2021) before patient enrollment (May 20, 2021). The study adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent, including for image publication, was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Adults (≥18 years) with refractory epilepsy scheduled to undergo frame-based SEEG between May 2021 and December 2023 were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status > III, severe psychiatric disorders, or a planned rapid sequence intubation (RSI).

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomized (1:1) to either the HFNO group or the facemask group using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened immediately before preoxygenation. Due to the nature of the interventions, anesthesia providers and patients were not blinded; however, data collectors and outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation to minimize bias.

Anesthetic management

Pre-anesthetic assessments were done the day before surgery, during which the anesthetic plan was explained in detail to alleviate patient anxiety. Baseline characteristics, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), ASA physical status, and Mallampati scores, were recorded.

On the day of surgery, a Leksell headframe with a curved anterior bar (Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden) was fixed under scalp nerve block in the operating theatre (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The patient was then escorted to the computed tomography (CT) room by the neurosurgeon and anesthesiologist for thin-slice CT imaging, which was co-registered to the pre-implantation dataset. The patient was subsequently returned to the operating theatre. After the implantation trajectory was finalized, general anesthesia was induced (Supplementary Fig. S2). Standard monitoring included electrocardiogram, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), axillary temperature, end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2), and bispectral index (BIS). An arterial line was inserted for invasive blood pressure monitoring and arterial blood gas analysis.

In the facemask group, oxygen was delivered via a size-3 or −4 anesthetic facemask (Intersurgical Ltd., Wokingham, UK) connected to a Primus anesthesia workstation (Drager Medical, Lübeck, Germany) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). In the HFNO group, the AIRVO-2 system (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, Auckland, New Zealand) was used. The nasal cannula size (medium or large) was selected to occlude approximately two-thirds of the nostrils (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Impossible mask ventilation (IMV) was defined as the inability to achieve adequate ventilation via facemask, as previously described (Kheterpal et al. 2009). In cases of IMV, the study protocol was terminated, and the patient was excluded (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

Preoxygenation was administered for 3 min. In the facemask group, oxygen (10 L/min, FiO2 100%) was delivered via a facemask gently held by the anesthesiologist to ensure an adequate seal. In the HFNO group, the device was turned on before preoxygenation to ensure the oxygen humidified sufficiently (30 L/min, 31 °C, FiO2 100%), and patients were instructed to breathe nasally with their mouths closed.

General anesthesia was induced according to a standardized protocol. Propofol (2.0–2.5 mg/kg) and sufentanil (0.5–1.0 μg/kg) were administered intravenously to achieve a BIS between 40 and 60. In the facemask group, BMV was initiated to maintain an ETCO2 of 35–45 mmHg, whereas in the HFNO group, the flow rate was increased to 60 L/min. Rocuronium (0.6–1.0 mg/kg) was then administered. Airway maneuvers (chin lift and jaw thrust) and adjuncts (oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways) were used at the discretion of the anesthesiologist. A nerve stimulator was calibrated, and train-of-four (TOF) counts were measured at 20-s intervals. Tracheal intubation was performed using a video laryngoscope (UE Co.Ltd., Zhejiang, China) once the TOF count reached zero. The facemask was removed before laryngoscopy, while HFNO was maintained. Successful intubation was confirmed by quantitative ETCO2 monitoring upon initiation of mechanical ventilation. After surgery, the headframe was removed, and patients were monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) until fully awake. A postoperative CT scan was conducted to verify electrode positioning and exclude intracranial hemorrhage.

Airway rescue equipment (e.g., laryngeal mask airways, fiberoptic bronchoscopes, cricothyrotomy kits) and a headframe removal screwdriver were readily available throughout the procedure. Failed intubation was defined as more than three intubation attempts or a total duration exceeding 120 s. In the event of failed intubation, the study protocol was terminated, and airway management proceeded in accordance with the ASA difficult airway guidelines (Apfelbaum et al. 2021).

Measurements and data collection

Time points were defined as follows: T0, baseline; T1, 3 min after preoxygenation; T2, pre-intubation, immediately before intubation; and T3, post-intubation, immediately after intubation. Arterial blood gas analyses and hemodynamic parameters were recorded at each point. Patient comfort was evaluated at the end of preoxygenation using a 10-cm visual numerical scale (0, extreme discomfort; 10, most comfortable), adapted from previously published methods (Supplementary Fig. S3) (Lee et al. 2014). Anesthesiologist satisfaction was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1, not at all; 5, very satisfied) shortly after intubation. Time taken for complete paralysis was defined as the interval from the initiation of propofol injection to the insertion of the laryngoscope. Time taken for intubation was defined as the interval from laryngoscope insertion to successful tracheal intubation. Time to secure the airway was defined as the interval from the initiation of propofol injection to successful intubation, corresponding to the sum of the two preceding times. Perioperative complications, including seizures, desaturation (SpO2 ≤ 92%), airway dryness, gastric insufflation, and intracranial hemorrhage, were documented.

Study endpoints and sample size calculation

The primary endpoint was the lowest SpO2 during anesthesia induction. Secondary endpoints included arterial blood gas analyses and hemodynamic parameters at predefined time points, the use of airway adjuncts, anesthesiologist satisfaction, patient comfort, time taken for complete paralysis, time taken for intubation, time to secure the airway, first-pass success (FPS), and perioperative complications.

A pilot study involving 15 SEEG patients found that the mean lowest SpO2 during anesthesia induction with facemask ventilation was 96.75% (2.23%). For non-inferiority of HFNO versus facemask ventilation, a sample size of 21 patients per group was required, based on a non-inferiority margin of 2% (considered clinically acceptable), a one-sided α level of 0.025 and power of 80%. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, 50 patients were enrolled.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were reported as the mean (standard deviation [SD]) or the median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Within-group comparisons of hemodynamic and arterial blood gas parameters across time points were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) (normally distributed data, assuming sphericity) or Friedman tests (non-normally distributed data), with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Between-group and time-related effects were further evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model, which included group, time, and their interaction as fixed effects, and subject as a random effect. Model residuals were examined for normality using histograms and quantile–quantile plots, and residual versus fitted value plots were inspected to verify that errors were centered around zero and exhibited constant variance. If the interaction between time and group was significant, between-group comparisons were conducted at each time point using independent t-tests for normally distributed data or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data, with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Primary outcome

For the primary endpoint of lowest SpO2 during anesthesia induction, HFNO was non-inferior to facemask ventilation. The median (IQR) lowest SpO2 was 98 (97–99) % in the HFNO group and 96 (95–98) % in the facemask group, with a median difference of 1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 0–2%). As the lower bound of the CI exceeded the predefined non-inferiority margin of −2%, non-inferiority was confirmed.

Gas exchange

Following preoxygenation, PaO2 increased significantly from baseline in both groups (Supplementary Table S1). At pre-intubation, PaO2 further increased compared with the end of preoxygenation. Post-intubation, PaO2 decreased significantly in the facemask group but remained stable in the HFNO group relative to pre-intubation values (Supplementary Table S1). Post-intubation PaO2 was significantly higher in the HFNO group than in the facemask group: 437.8 (411.1–474.4) mmHg versus 400.6 (365.6–435.2) mmHg, with a median difference of 37.2 mmHg (95% CI, 20.8–53.6; p = 0.002) (Fig. 2A). After anesthesia induction, PaCO2 increased and pH decreased significantly from baseline in both groups, with no significant differences between groups at corresponding time points (Fig. 2B, C, and Supplementary Table S1). Other secondary endpoints

After anesthesia induction, heart rate (HR) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) decreased significantly from baseline in both groups, with no significant intergroup differences at corresponding time points (Supplementary Table S1). The HFNO group required fewer airway adjuncts (risk difference, −26.1%; 95% CI, −44.0% to −8.2%; p = 0.022), had a shorter time to secure the airway (median difference, –16 s; 95% CI –27 to –4; p = 0.015), and demonstrated higher anesthesiologist satisfaction and patient comfort scores (median difference, 1; 95% CI 0–1; p < 0.0001 and median difference, 3; 95% CI, 2–3; p < 0.0001, respectively) (Table 2). The time taken for complete paralysis, time taken for intubation, and FPS rates were comparable between the two groups, and no perioperative complications were reported in either group (Table 2). Discussion

This randomized controlled non-inferiority trial demonstrated that HFNO is non-inferior to facemask ventilation in maintaining oxygenation during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG. HFNO resulted in higher post-intubation PaO2, reduced the time required to secure the airway, eliminated the need for airway adjuncts, and was associated with greater acceptance by both anesthesiologists and patients. These findings support the seamless integration of HFNO into preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation protocols for frame-based SEEG, offering a practical alternative for airway management in this challenging setting.

Despite several potentially physiological advantages of HFNO, evidence regarding its efficacy during tracheal intubation remains inconsistent. Some studies report that HFNO significantly improves the lowest SpO2 and reduces the incidence of hypoxemia, whereas others find no significant benefit (Chua et al. 2022; George et al. 2025; Lodenius et al. 2018; F. Mir et al. 2017a; Vourc’h et al. 2023; Wong et al. 2019). These discrepancies may reflect heterogeneity in individual physiological factors (e.g., age, obesity, comorbidities, and airway patency), intubation techniques (rapid sequence or conventional intubation), and oxygenation protocols (flow rate and duration of therapy). This study compared HFNO preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation with facemask preoxygenation and BMV during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG. HFNO was non-inferior to facemask ventilation in maintaining the lowest SpO2 and provided several distinct advantages. First, in awake patients with stereotactic headframes, tightly sealed facemask often causing significant discomfort, whereas HFNO markedly improved patient comfort, enhancing preoxygenation efficiency. Second, HFNO eliminated the need for airway adjuncts or positional adjustments, minimizing air dilution and maintaining a consistently high FiO2. Finally, HFNO provided continuous apneic oxygenation, sustaining PaO2 during laryngoscopy.

Although several studies have reported higher PaO2 with HFNO during preoxygenation (Ng et al. 2018; Osman & Abd El-Raof 2021), we observed no significant difference in median PaO2 at the end of preoxygenation between HFNO and facemask groups. This may reflect the lower flow rate and shorter preoxygenation duration used in our protocol (30 L/min for 3 min) compared with prior studies (50 L/min for 5 min). The 30 L/min flow rate was chosen to align with standard practice for awake patients at our institution, ensuring procedural consistency while maximizing tolerability in patients with epilepsy. Recent evidence suggests that HFNO at 30 L/min and 31 °C achieves adequate oxygenation with optimal patient comfort (Basile et al. 2020). This regimen was well-tolerated in our cohort, achieving a practical balance between oxygenation and patient experience.

Another advantage of HFNO is its ability to remain in place during intubation attempts, continuously supporting gas exchange throughout the apneic phase. Prior studies comparing HFNO with facemasks during RSI have reported comparable blood gas profiles, with HFNO supporting longer apnea times (F Mir et al. 2017a; Sjöblom et al. 2021). In our non-RSI setting, we further evaluated the role of HFNO in providing apneic oxygenation from the onset of neuromuscular blockade until successful intubation. Increasing the HFNO flow rate in anaesthetized patients significantly elevated pre-intubation PaO2 to levels comparable with BMV. Unlike the facemask group, in which BMV was discontinued upon laryngoscope insertion, HFNO was maintained throughout intubation. Despite similar intubation times between groups, HFNO achieved higher post-intubation PaO2, underscoring its advantage in providing continuous apneic oxygenation. Although HFNO can maintain oxygenation during prolonged apnea, its efficacy is limited by CO2 accumulation (Booth et al. 2021). Ng et al. reported that, in non-RSI procedures, HFNO was less effective than BMV at clearing CO2 (Ng et al. 2019). In our study, PaCO2 increased after induction, with a rise of 8.6 mmHg in the HFNO group (to 47.5 mmHg) and 7.6 mmHg in the facemask group (to 46.9 mmHg); this difference was not statistically significant. The difference between the two studies may be attributable to the timing of PaCO2 measurement. Ng et al. measured PaCO2 immediately before intubation, whereas in our study it was measured after intubation. During laryngoscopy in our study, BMV was interrupted, thereby diminishing its advantage in CO2 clearance. Given the shorter time to secure the airway in the HFNO group, we further analyzed the rate of PaCO2 increase. The HFNO group exhibited a PaCO2 rise of 3.75 mmHg/min, which was comparable to the 3.06 mmHg/min reported by Ng et al., but significantly higher than the 2.67 mmHg/min observed in the facemask group. Taken together with previous studies, these findings confirm that HFNO effectively maintains oxygenation during apnea; however, careful monitoring of CO₂ accumulation is warranted during prolonged apneic periods (Piosik et al. 2021).

Conventional facemask ventilation requires a two-handed technique to achieve an adequate seal, whereas HFNO is secured with adjustable over-ear straps, eliminating the need for additional pressure. Previous studies have shown that preoxygenation with HFNO is easier for anesthesiologists to manage and improves patient comfort (Merry et al., 2022). In our study, awake patients in the facemask group experienced significant discomfort due to the need for a tightly sealed facemask. After induction of anesthesia, airway adjuncts were often required to maintain airway patency. Under the constraints of the head frame, removal of the facemask prior to intubation carried a risk of displacement. In contrast, HFNO allowed a “hands-free” induction, simplifying airway management and improving both patient comfort and anesthesiologist satisfaction. FPS rates were comparable between groups, lower than in patients without difficult airway predictors but consistent with those wearing cervical orthoses (Liu et al., 2022; Zhang et al. 2022). Reduced FPS likely reflected anatomical constraints imposed by the head frame rather than the oxygenation technique.

HFNO has a small spatial footprint and is advantageous in situations with limited airway access (Bennett et al. 2014; Gusti & Vaghadia 2020; Ng et al. 2019). In the facemask group, two patients with deep-seated epileptic foci required low headframe fixation, positioning the anterior bar at or below the nasal tip, obstructing the nostrils and rendering IMV. In contrast, the nasal cannula of HFNO placed below the nostrils was unaffected by the bar. Following informed consent, HFNO was successfully used for preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation in these patients, enabling safe intubation. Both patients underwent electrode implantation followed by radiofrequency ablation, achieving effective seizure control.

This study has several limitations. First, the predefined non-inferiority margin of 2% was narrow. Preliminary experiments indicated a lowest SpO2 of 97% during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG, suggesting that the non-inferiority comparison may reflect two states of hyperoxia, potentially limiting clinical relevance. However, HFNO may offer practical benefits for patients unable to tolerate facemask ventilation due to headframe constraints. Second, patients with IMV were excluded, leaving the efficacy of HFNO in this high-risk subgroup unevaluated. Further research should include such patients to explore HFNO in managing these complex airways. Third, the small, single-center sample size may have limited detection of significant PaCO2 differences despite the higher rates of increase with HFNO. Fourth, exclusion of pediatric patients limits generalizability to this population, which represents a substantial proportion of SEEG candidates; a multicenter study including pediatric patients is ongoing. Fifth, the unblinded design may have introduced bias in subjective outcomes. Finally, as our cohort was relatively lean, caution is warranted in extrapolating findings to obese patients.

Conclusion

HFNO is non-inferior to facemask ventilation for maintaining oxygenation during anesthesia induction for frame-based SEEG, offering reduced airway adjunct use, shorter time to secure the airway, and improved patient comfort and anesthesiologist satisfaction. By addressing the limitations of traditional facemask ventilation, HFNO enhances anesthesia care in this complex setting. Further research is warranted to validate HFNO in other stereotactic procedures, including deep brain stimulation surgery, brain biopsies, catheter insertion, brain aspirations, and stereotactic radiosurgery.
