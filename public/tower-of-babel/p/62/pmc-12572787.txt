Effect of neoadjuvant therapy on survival outcomes in patients with melanoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fan Yuan, Mingyi Jing, Xinlong Chen, Xufan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMelanoma is the most aggressive type of skin cancer, and its advanced cases often have a poor prognosis due to early metastasis. In recent years, neoadjuvant therapy (NT) has demonstrated potential benefits in treating solid tumours. However, its efficacy and safety in treating melanoma remain inconclusive. This study aimed to evaluate the complete response (CR), survival outcomes, and safety of NT in patients with melanoma.MethodsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis, Cochrane, Web of Science, Embase, and PubMed were searched up to 24 February 2025, and the Clinical Trial Registry was also search to identify additional eligible studies. Studies were included if they involved patients with melanoma receiving NT, with or without a comparator group. Both randomised controlled trials and non-randomised studies were eligible. In comparative studies, only those with adjuvant therapy or surgery alone as the comparator were included. Regardless of study design, all analyses were centred on the NT groups. Two reviewers separately screened the literature, extracted data, and evaluated the risk of bias. The quality of the included studies was appraised using National Institutes of Health study quality assessment tool (NIH-QAT). Data analysis was conducted through Stata 15.0 and R 4.4.1. After heterogeneity analysis of Cochrane’s Q test and I2 statistics, single-arm and pairwise meta-analysis was separately conducted. Sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, meta-regression were conducted to explore the source and influence of heterogeneity. Publication bias was identified by Egger’s test, funnel plot and explored by trim-and-fill analysis. The primary outcome was pathological complete response (pCR) and radiological complete response (rCR), and the secondary outcomes included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and adverse events (AEs). The study is registered with PROSPERO, CRD420251021368.FindingsA total of 43 studies involving 2821 patients with melanoma who received NT were included. Based on the NIH-QAT, the overall quality of the included studies was considered good. Meta-analysis showed that the pooled pCR was 33% (95% CI: 27%–39%, I2 = 77.0%), the rCR was 11% (95% CI: 5%, 16%, I2 = 68.0%), and the incidence of grade ≥3 AEs was 33% (95% CI: 23%–44%, I2 = 95.5%). Regarding survival outcomes, the pooled OS rate (OSR) was 81% (95% CI: 76%, 86%, I2 = 85.3%), and the PFS rate (PFSR) was 60% (95% CI: 52%–68%, I2 = 91.7%). Subgroup analyses indicated that follow-up duration, type of intervention and study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Compared with adjuvant-only therapy (AT), NT was associated with significantly improved PFS (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.87, I2 = 72.5%) and OS (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.41–0.99, I2 = 0%), with no significant difference in the risk of severe AEs (RR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.93–1.43, I2 = 0%).InterpretationCurrent evidence suggests that NT in patients with melanoma is associated with a favourable CR and promising survival benefits without increasing the risk of severe AEs. Compared to AT, NT demonstrates potential advantages in reducing the risks of disease progression and death. However, the results of this study s should be considered exploratory since the majority of included studies are single-arm or observational studies. Further high-quality randomised trials are needed to confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of NT.FundingNone.Keywords: Neoadjuvant therapy, Melanoma, Survival outcomes, Complete response

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Before the initiation of the study and during the submission process, we searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science for existing systematic reviews or meta-analyses related to this topic, with the last search conducted on February 25, 2025. Our search strategy was melanoma AND neoadjuvant AND (“systematic review” OR meta-analysis), with no restrictions on language, time, or other factors. We found that there is currently a lack of relevant studies evaluating and synthesising existing evidence on neoadjuvant therapy in melanoma. A Cochrane systematic review published in 2023 has provided the most comprehensive discussion on this topic to date. Although it included 8 randomised controlled trials, the study failed to yield meaningful results due to significant differences between interventions and a relatively sparse evidence base.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this study provides the most up-to-date and comprehensive synthesis of evidence on the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant therapy (NT) in melanoma, based on data from 43 studies involving 2821 patients. Through quantitative meta-analysis, we demonstrate that NT is associated with a meaningful pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 33% and a pooled overall survival rate of 81%, with an acceptable safety profile. Notably, in head-to-head comparisons with adjuvant-only therapy (AT), NT significantly reduced the risk of disease progression and death, without increasing the risk of severe adverse events. These findings offer robust evidence supporting the clinical value of NT and suggest that its earlier use may lead to improved long-term outcomes in melanoma patients. Furthermore, subgroup analyses highlight the influence of follow-up duration and intervention type on heterogeneity, offering important insights for individualised treatment strategies.

Implications of all the available evidence

Based on the currently limited evidence, this study provides preliminary support that NT may represent a potential therapeutic approach for melanoma. However, its specific application scenarios and protocols remain undefined. Until further high-quality studies provide robust evidence, NT should still be regarded as an exploratory strategy. Several limitations of this study should be noted: the heterogeneity in NT approaches for melanoma (e.g., Radiation Therapy (RT), immunotherapy) with unstandardised drugs and dosages restricted exploration of survival outcomes tied to specific interventions, limiting analyses to therapeutic category-based subgroups; inclusion criteria focused solely on pre-surgery NT administration, ignoring other potential factors, with study design identified as a key heterogeneity source, and the inclusion of single-arm studies—valuable but flawed by lack of controls and bias risk—highlighting the need for high-quality RCTs to validate findings and clarify NT efficacy/safety; additionally, the use of a random-effects model demands cautious interpretation, and future research should optimise individualised strategies, identify effective drugs/dosages, and incorporate novel long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Melanoma is a malignancy originating from epidermal melanocytes. Its pathogenesis is closely linked to ultraviolet radiation (UVR) exposure, and intermittent high-intensity UVR exposure is identified as the predominant risk factor for melanoma.1,2 The incidence of melanoma has steadily increased worldwide over the past 30 years at an annual rate of 3–7%. Although it makes up only 1% of all skin cancer cases, it accounts for over 80% of skin cancer-related deaths. Thus, it has emerged as a major challenge in clinical management.3 Patients with early-stage melanoma, when treated with surgical resection, typically achieve a five-year survival rate exceeding 90%.4 Nevertheless, for individuals with metastatic melanoma, particularly those in stages III and IV, the prognosis remains poor, with a median survival of less than 12 months. Even more concerning is that over 60% of cases experience recurrence within 18 months after surgery, further intensifying pressure on existing healthcare systems. Current melanoma treatment primarily includes multimodal therapies. Surgical resection remains the standard therapy for localized lesions, and it is especially effective in treating early-stage patients. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy serve as the primary therapeutic strategies for advanced or metastatic melanoma.5 Regarding immunotherapy, programmed death (PD)-1 and PD-ligand 1 inhibitors (such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab) have become the main therapies. Immune checkpoint blockade can enhance anti-tumour immune responses by activating the host immune system.6,7 Targeted therapy focuses on patients with BRAF V600 E/K mutations, and it involves the combination of BRAF and MEK inhibitors (such as dabrafenib + trametinib). These therapeutic approaches have notably improved the SR in individuals with advanced melanoma, increasing the three-year SR from less than 10% in 2011 to 40%–50% today. Nonetheless, current therapies still face several challenges. First, approximately 35–45% of cases exhibit primary resistance to immunotherapy, making the treatment ineffective. Second, acquired resistance is a common challenge in most patients receiving targeted therapy,8,9 with nearly 80% experiencing disease progression within 12–18 months. Additionally, AT exhibits considerable variability in efficacy across individuals. Phase III trials indicated that only 54% of high-risk patients achieved relapse-free survival (RFS) with postoperative adjuvant immunotherapy, while immune-related adverse events (AEs) occurred in 41.6% of cases, further limiting its widespread application.10 NT an emerging treatment strategy, has garnered increasing attention.11 By administering systemic therapy before surgery, NT enhances tumour immunogenicity by inducing the release of tumour antigens. It also provides measurable lesions for real-time assessment of drug sensitivity and offers biomarkers for treatment planning after surgery. Recent phase II studies revealed that preoperative combination immunotherapy (such as nivolumab + ipilimumab) exhibited significant efficacy in treating stage IIIB-C patients: approximately 72% of cases achieved pathological complete response (pCR), with a 24-month event-free survival rate of 94%, far surpassing the 64% observed in traditional postoperative AT group.12 Moreover, studies that focus on BRAF-mutant patients also indicated encouraging results. For instance, in a phase II trial (NCT02231775), patients with stage III or resectable oligometastatic stage IV melanoma who received neoadjuvant dabrafenib plus trametinib for 8 weeks before surgery, followed by 44 weeks of adjuvant therapy, achieved markedly improved outcomes: at a median follow-up of 18.6 months, 71% of patients in the neoadjuvant group were alive without disease progression, compared with 0% in the standard-of-care group.13

Based on the results of the current cohorts, this study seeks to comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic effects of NT in melanoma patients.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This study was carried out in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered and updated in PROSPERO (CRD420251021368).

Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane, and PubMed databases were searched up to February 2025, with no language or country limits. The search strategy was designed by combining medical subject headings terms and free-text terms. The search strategy incorporated controlled vocabulary terms such as “melanoma” and “neoadjuvant therapy”. To ensure comprehensive coverage and minimise the risk of missing relevant studies, citation tracking was performed. Furthermore, clinical trial registries were searched to identify related registered trials, with particular emphasis on trials with published results, which were subsequently included in the analysis.

All retrieved studies were imported into EndNote 21, and duplicates were excluded. Two researchers (F.Y. and M.J.) separately reviewed the titles and abstracts of the studies based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. After initial screening, the remaining studies underwent a full-text review. Finally, the selected studies were cross-verified by the two reviewers, and any disagreements were addressed through discussion.

Inclusion criteria were defined by the PICOS model: (i) study population: patients histologically or cytologically diagnosed with melanoma, presenting with the clinically detectable and measurable disease as defined by the Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumours, and staged based on the Cancer Staging Manual; (ii) intervention: NT administered prior to surgical resection, including immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (such as PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4 inhibitors), targeted therapy for BRAF-mutant melanoma (such as BRAF/MEK inhibitor combinations), and, in specific clinical scenarios, radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (iii) treatment response: outcomes included complete response (CR) as assessed by either pCR or rCR, PFS, OS, and grade ≥3 AEs; (iv) study design: eligible studies included randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, single-arm studies, registered clinical trials with publicly available outcome data but without a peer-reviewed publication, and studies available only in abstract form (such as conference abstracts) that reported relevant clinical outcomes.

Although single-arm studies are generally considered to provide a lower level of evidence compared to randomised controlled trials or cohort studies, they represent a substantial portion of the current research on neoadjuvant therapy in melanoma. Given that this treatment approach remains in an exploratory stage, including eligible single-arm studies allows for a more comprehensive integration of existing data. This strategy facilitates the quantitative synthesis of rate-based outcomes and offers preliminary evidence that may inform the design and prioritisation of future prospective studies.

Exclusion criteria were: (i) commentaries, editorials, and review articles; (ii) non-English studies; (iii) animal studies; (iv) studies involving patients who received systemic therapy after definitive surgical resection of the primary tumour.

Data analysis

Two researchers (F.Y. and X.L.C.) separately extracted data from the included studies. The collected data included: country, publication year, first author, sample size, patient details (disease stage, age, sex), interventions (drug type, dose, administration cycle, surgery timing), the control groups (surgery timing, drug type, dose, treatment duration), and outcome measures. Study design was categorised as single-arm studies (SA), multi-arm studies with a control group (MAc), and multi-arm studies without a control group (MAnc). Treatment type was classified into four groups according to drug class: immunotherapy (IO), targeted therapy (TT), combined immunotherapy and targeted therapy (IO + TT), and other treatments (OT). Follow-up duration was recorded as reported in the original studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on these three variables. Data were imported into Excel and cross-verified by both researchers to ensure consistency. For studies with two or more treatment arms, pairwise comparisons were extracted when applicable. The primary outcomes included rCR and pCR, while secondary outcomes included PFS, OS, and AEs.

Treatment response included both pCR and rCR. pCR was defined according to the criteria of the International Neoadjuvant Melanoma Consortium (INMC) as the absence of any viable tumour cells in the surgical specimen (0% viable tumour).14 rCR was defined based on RECIST version 1.1 criteria as the disappearance of all target lesions without the appearance of new lesions.15

Hazard ratios (HR) for OS and PFS were defined as the relative risk of events (death or progression) during the follow-up period in the neoadjuvant group compared with the adjuvant group, and were extracted only from studies with direct treatment comparisons. These HR were derived from multivariate time-to-event models as reported in the original studies, reflecting the relative treatment effect.

The OS rate (OSR) was defined as the proportion of patients alive at the longest follow-up time point reported in each included study. Similarly, the PFS rate (PFSR) referred to the proportion of patients who remained free from disease progression or death at that same final follow-up point. For the meta-analysis of PFSR, studies that reported event-free survival (EFS) or RFS were also included, as these endpoints are conceptually and clinically analogous to PFS. To enhance data completeness and comparability, these outcomes were pooled accordingly. To avoid data duplication, only one survival endpoint per study was included; when multiple endpoints were reported, priority was given to PFS, followed by EFS, and then RFS. Importantly, this pooling strategy was applied exclusively to the analysis of PFSR.

Two types of survival data were analysed separately: survival rates (OSR and PFSR), and hazard ratios (OS and PFS). Survival rate analysis included both single-arm and multi-arm studies that reported the number of events and corresponding total sample sizes. In contrast, HR analysis was limited to comparative studies of neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy. All survival data were extracted based on the most recent follow-up time points as reported in the original articles, with no standardised time cutoff imposed. These two analytical approaches were treated as methodologically independent and were not merged during synthesis.

AEs were extracted when explicitly reported as grade 3 or higher (Grade ≥ 3), regardless of the specific grading criteria employed by individual studies. No uniform toxicity assessment tool was imposed, and data were recorded as reported in the original articles.

The quality of the eligible studies was assessed via the National Institutes of Health (NIH) tools.16 RCTs were evaluated using the Quality Assessment of Controlled Intervention Studies, and cohort studies were assessed using the Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies. Both tools consisted of 14 items, with each item rated as “yes”, “no”, or “other” based on study design and methodological rigour. For single-arm studies, the Quality Assessment Tool for Before-After Studies with No Control Group was used, which included 12 items rated in the same manner. Each item was rated as “yes”, “no”, or “other” based on the study design and methodological rigour. The assessment was conducted independently by two researchers (M.Y.J. and X.L.C.), with any disagreements addressed by a third reviewer (X.Z.) to ensure fairness and consistency. According to the predefined scoring criteria of each tool, studies were categorised by their risk of bias as follows: for RCTs and cohort studies, scores of 0–5 indicated high risk of bias (poor), 6–10 indicated moderate risk (fair), and 11–14 indicated low risk (good); for single-arm studies, scores of 0–4 were considered high risk, 5–8 moderate risk, and 9–12 low risk.

Most analysis were conducted in STATA 15.0 with meta package. Heterogeneity was first analysed with Cochrane’s Q test and I2 statistics. When the I2 > 50% and relative p < 0.05, heterogeneity were seemed as substantial and a random-effects model was utilised for analysis; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied.

For CR (rCR or pCR) AND survival rates (OSR, PFSR, AEs), the rates of event incidence were pooled along with their 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Data were collected simultaneously from single-arm studies and multiple-arm studies to expand the evidence base, but a subgroup analysis of study design of data sources was also conducted to determine the influence of such combination. rCR, pCR, OSR, PFSR, and AEs, which were treated as proportions at prespecified follow-up time points, were pooled with a random-effects inverse-variance model in STATA. Specifically, studies with zero events were not excluded. Since STATA could not handle such conditions properly, we utilised “meta”package in R 4.4.1 to solve this. Function “metaprop” with sm = “PRAW” and method = “Inverse” could implement appropriate continuity corrections when zero events occur, ensuring these studies contribute to the pooled estimates without introducing computational bias. For survival data, in the form of HR, extracted from Kaplan–Meier Curve or Cox-regression (OS and PFS), log-transformation was first performed to obtain log (HR) and corresponding SElog (HR) (SE means standard error), then meta-analyses of generic data were performed. When output, the effect size was set as “HR” to automatically re-calculate HR value and its 95%CI.

Sensitivity analysis by “leave-one-out” method, subgroup analysis and meta-regression (simple linear regression in STATA or R with meta package.) based on intervention type and follow-up time were utilised to explore the stability of pooling estimations and identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias tests with funnel plots and Egger’s test were conducted when 10 or more studies were included in the pooling process of an outcome. If asymmetry was observed or Egger’p < 0.05, publication bias was considered significant, “trim and fill” method would be used to identify its influence on results’ stability and explore its source.

Results

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 3460 relevant studies were selected from the four databases. After automatically removing 790 duplicates, 2670 studies underwent title and abstract screening. The remaining 165 studies then underwent a full-text review. A total of 13 studies with inaccessible full texts were excluded. Additionally, 33 studies were excluded due to an ineligible study design, including review articles and basic science studies (such as cellular, pathological, or molecular-level research) (Appendix Table 2). Moreover, 45 studies were excluded for mismatched interventions, such as the absence of NT, unclear reporting on whether the intervention was neoadjuvant, or insufficient information to determine whether patients had undergone surgical resection. Furthermore, 32 studies with incompatible comparison groups were removed, including studies that only reported protocols or trial registrations without results, or those that did not report the key outcomes of interest, such as pathological response or survival endpoints. Quality assessment

The overall quality of included studies was high, with most achieving good quality in terms of randomisation and outcome assessment. In addition, many studies employed intention-to-treat analysis, with follow-up loss or withdrawal rates below 20%. Due to the specificity of melanoma, some studies did not meet sample size requirements for the evaluation criteria. The results are illustrated in Appendix Table 3.

Primary outcomes

Twenty-three studies that investigated the efficacy of NT in treating melanoma were included. Among these, 7 studies reported data on rCR, and 22 studies provided data on pCR. The pooled analysis of pCR revealed a pCR rate of 33% (95% CI: 27%–39%, p < 0.050), but significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 77.0%, Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis further confirmed the reliability of the findings (Appendix Fig. 1). Subgroup analysis by intervention type showed a pCR rate of 36% (95% CI: 29%–43%) for IO, 36% (95% CI: 18%–53%) for targeted therapy (TT), 27% (95% CI: 10%–43%) for IO + TT, and 22% (95%CI:11%–32%) for OT (Appendix Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis by study design showed that the pooled pCR rate was 23% (95% CI: 15%–31%) in SA studies, 38% (95% CI: 31%–45%) in MAnc studies, and 38% (95% CI: 19%–57%) in MAc studies. The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.02), indicating that study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity (Appendix Fig. 3). Meta-regression indicated that treatment type was not a significant source of heterogeneity (r = −0.047, p = 0.12). Egger’s test suggested no evidence of publication bias for pCR (p = 0.86) (Appendix Fig. 4).

Secondary outcome

OS

In studies on NT (n = 26), the pooled OSR was 81% (95% CI: 76%–86%), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 85.3%) (Appendix Fig. 7). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the results (Appendix Fig. 8). Subgroup analysis based on follow-up time demonstrated a 24-month OSR of 86% (95% CI: 81%–92%), 75% (95% CI: 60%–91%) at 36 months, which decreased to 77% (95% CI: 76%–86%) at 60 months. The difference was statistically significant (p < 0.0001), indicating that study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity (Appendix Fig. 9). When stratified by treatment type, the OSR was 82% (95% CI: 75%–88%) in the IO group, 80% (95% CI: 68%–92%) in the TT group, 77% (95% CI: 49%–104%) in the combination therapy group, and 81% (95% CI: 75%–88%) in the other treatment (OT) group (Appendix Fig. 10). When stratified by study design, the OSR was 75% (95% CI: 68%–83%) in the SA group, 89% (95% CI: 84%–94%) in the MAnc group, and 78% (95% CI: 59%–97%) in the MAc group. The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.01), indicating that study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity (Appendix Fig. 11). Meta-regression analysis indicated that follow-up duration was a significant source of heterogeneity (r = −0.003, p = 0.021). Publication bias was detected according to Egger’s test (p = 0.001; Appendix Fig. 12). However, the pooled estimate remained unchanged after adjustment using the trim-and-fill method, suggesting that the impact of potential publication bias on the overall results was limited.

In the comparative analysis of neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy, three studies were included. In the comparison analysis between NT and AT (n = 3), NT showed notable survival benefits (HR = 0.641, 95% CI: 0.41–0.99), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, Appendix Fig. 13).

PFS

The PFSR analysis in the NT group, based on 24 studies and two registered trials, demonstrated a PFSR of 60% (95% CI: 52%–68%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 91.7%, Appendix Fig. 14). Sensitivity analysis (Appendix Fig. 15) confirmed the robustness of the results. Subgroup analysis by follow-up duration showed PFSR of 67% (95% CI: 47%–86%) at 12 months, 61% (95% CI: 42%–80%) at 24 months, 59% (95% CI: 46%–72%) at 36 months, and 35% (95% CI: 19%–52%) at 60 months. The difference was statistically significant (p < 0.0001), indicating that study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity (Appendix Fig. 16). When stratified by intervention type, the PFSr was 65% (95% CI: 55%–74%) in the IO group, 52% (95% CI: 33%–71%) in the TT group, 46% (95% CI: 16%–77%) in the combination therapy group, and 54% (95% CI: 28%–79%) in the OT group (Appendix Fig. 17). When stratified by study design, the PFSR was 54% (95% CI: 29%–79%) in the MAc group, 71% (95% CI: 63%–79%) in the MAnc group, and 49% (95% CI: 37%–61%) in the SA group. The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.01), indicating that study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity (Appendix Fig. 18). Meta-regression analysis indicated that follow-up time (r = −0.044, p = 0.01) and intervention type (r = −0.11, p = 0.027) were significant contributors to heterogeneity. Egger’s test (p = 0.067; Appendix Fig. 19) indicated no publication bias, supporting the robustness of the results.

In the comparison analysis between NT and AT, NT substantially reduced the risk of disease progression (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.87), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 72.5%, Appendix Fig. 20). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the results (Appendix Fig. 21).

AEs

The incidence of grade ≥3 AEs in the NT group was 33% (95% CI: 23%–44%), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 95.5%, Appendix Fig. 22). Sensitivity analysis (Appendix Fig. 23) confirmed the robustness of the results. Subgroup analysis by intervention type showed that the incidence was 35% (95% CI: 22%–47%) in the IO group, 29% (95% CI: 14%–44%) in the TT group, and 29% (95% CI: 6%–53%) in the IO + TT combination group (Appendix Fig. 24). When stratified by study design, the incidence was 26% (95% CI: −15% to 67%) in the MAc group, 38% (95% CI: 26%–49%) in the MAnc group, and 16% (95% CI: 6%–26%) in the SA group. The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.03), indicating that study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity (Appendix Fig. 25). Meta-regression analysis did not identify intervention type (r = −0.004, p = 0.93) as significant sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias assessment (Egger’s test, p = 0.63, Appendix Fig. 26) showed no evidence of publication bias, confirming the robustness of the results.

In the comparison of severe AEs between NT and AT groups, there was no significant difference in AE risk (RR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.93–1.43), with negligible heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, Appendix Fig. 27). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the results (Appendix Fig. 28).

Discussion

This meta-analysis covered 43 studies comprising 2821 patients with melanoma who received NT. The pooled analysis showed that the pCR was 33%, the rCR was 11%, and the incidence of grade 3 or higher AEs was 33%. In terms of survival outcomes, the pooled OSR was 81%, and the PFSR was 60%. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses indicated that follow-up duration, type of intervention and study design may contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Compared with AT, NT demonstrated a potential survival benefit, with significantly reduced risks of disease progression (HR = 0.58) and death (HR = 0.64), while showing no statistically significant difference in the risk of severe AEs between the two groups.

A study demonstrated that NT for melanoma not only improved survival outcomes but also played a role in early intervention in tumour biology and remodelling the immune microenvironment.23 Compared to traditional AT, NT, through preoperative systemic treatment and surgery, can inhibit micrometastasis and prevent recurrence, as well as improve surgical outcomes and long-term immune response.58,59 Furthermore, NT may contribute to tumour downstaging by reducing tumour burden, thereby limiting the extent of melanoma resection. In certain cases, it may even convert initially unresectable tumours into resectable ones, thus enabling surgical intervention.47,53

The core mechanism of NT lies in triggering an immune response in the early stages by activating the immune system and conducting targeted or combination therapies before surgery. Moreover, it utilises the release of tumour antigens to intervene in tumour evolution and improve the immune microenvironment.60,61 In this process, ICIs (such as anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4) activate tumour-specific T cells and form immune memory by blocking the relevant signalling pathways, thereby facilitating the release of tumour antigens.62 Existing studies confirm that early immune activation not only enhances the elimination of primary tumours but also suppresses potential micrometastases through a bystander effect, thus significantly reducing the risk of postoperative recurrence.63 In contrast to AT, which is administered after surgery, NT can intervene when tumour antigen load is higher, allowing for more complete and sustained activation of the immune system. Emerging evidence suggests that this early window of antigen exposure may play a critical role in priming the immune system, a process that may be less effective when treatment is initiated only after surgery.64 Especially in patients with BRAF-mutant melanoma, NT has demonstrated higher efficacy and safety. Targeted therapy (such as BRAF-MEK inhibitors) can rapidly inhibit tumour proliferation signals, leading to a quick reduction in the primary tumour, and ultimately optimising the surgical resection margins and reducing the risk of intraoperative tumour dissemination.65 In the present study, the observed pCR and rCR rates following NT were comparable to those reported in real-world clinical settings,66 further supporting the reliability of our findings. Moreover, accumulating clinical evidence, such as from the SWOG-1801 trial, indicates that combining NT with AT offers superior therapeutic benefits compared to AT alone, possibly due to enhanced immune priming.67,68 However, this intensified approach may be associated with a higher incidence of immune-related AEs.

Additionally, the multifaceted synergistic effects of NT further enhance its therapeutic potential. The combination of immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and Radiation Therapy (RT) can establish a dynamic therapeutic network by concurrently targeting local lesions and systemic micrometastases, thus providing more comprehensive disease control.14 This strategy helps control the tumour and provides dual intervention on tumour heterogeneity and sources of recurrence during the early phase of immune system activation. Our results demonstrated that the survival and PFS rates in the NT group exceeded 50%, with a lower risk ratio compared to the AT group. The potential mechanism is that preoperative systemic treatment effectively clears circulating tumour cells and hidden metastases, thereby decreasing the risk of postoperative recurrence. Moreover, the formation of immune memory helps maintain the suppression of residual tumour cells after surgery.65

Regarding safety, the incidence of AEs in the NT group was slightly higher, but no significant difference was found compared to AT. However, the treatment cycle of NT is shorter, typically lasting four to eight weeks, and can be personalised based on individual patient conditions. Thus, it is more feasible for clinical application.48 For patients, the preoperative treatment allows for earlier identification and management of immune-related toxicities (such as pneumonitis and colitis), thus reducing the risk of complications during the postoperative recovery. In contrast, AT often requires long-term medication, potentially leading to an increased cumulative toxic burden.

Therefore, NT demonstrates multidimensional advantages in melanoma treatment. Although its AE rate is slightly higher, its shorter treatment cycle and greater potential for personalised adjustments make it an important strategy in the comprehensive management of melanoma. However, several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, since the interventions of NT for melanoma involved various approaches (such as RT, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and tumour-lysing therapy), there was no standardisation of drugs and dosages. This heterogeneity limited further exploration of survival outcomes associated with specific interventions or dosing regimens. Consequently, only subgroup analyses based on therapeutic categories were conducted. Second, the inclusion criteria of this study focused solely on whether NT was administered prior to surgery, without accounting for other potentially influential inclusion factors. Importantly, subgroup analysis revealed that study design was a significant source of heterogeneity. In order to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of neoadjuvant therapy in melanoma, this meta-analysis included a number of single-arm studies. While these studies provide valuable preliminary data, they are inherently limited by the lack of a control group and increased risk of bias. Therefore, high-quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are urgently needed to validate these findings and to further elucidate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of NT. Lastly, as a random-effects model was employed for analysis, the findings should be interpreted carefully. Future research should focus on optimising individualised treatment strategies, and additional clinical studies are warranted to identify the most effective drugs and dosing protocols, along with the incorporation of novel long-term follow-up outcomes.

Moreover, this study observed that some clinical trials on NT for melanoma were in the active exploratory phase. Several key trials completed patient enrolment and entered the efficacy follow-up period, but the final analysis results were not published. These trials included various NT approaches, such as immune modulation, targeted therapy, and combination therapy, and involved patients across different disease stages in resectable melanoma. In terms of immunotherapy, the NCT04197882 study applied the combination of oncolytic viruses and ICIs as a biological treatment approach. The induction efficacy of this combination therapy in patients with completely resectable stage III and stage IV (M1a) melanoma was evaluated by intratumourally injecting GM-CSF gene-carrying engineered herpes simplex virus (OrienX010) and intravenously administering anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (toripalimab). Regarding targeted therapy, the NCT04949113 study compared the efficacy of neoadjuvant dual immunotherapy (ipilimumab 80 mg combined with nivolumab 240 mg) versus standard surgery plus adjuvant nivolumab (480 mg, q4w) in patients with stage III skin melanoma or melanoma of unknown primary. The NCT01972347 and NCT02231775 phase II studies focused on BRAF V600-mutant melanoma patients. The former evaluated the pathological response rate in patients with AJCC stage IIIB-C melanoma who received neoadjuvant dabrafenib (a BRAF inhibitor) combined with trametinib (a MEK inhibitor). The latter adopted a neoadjuvant-adjuvant sequential approach to explore the effects of continuous administration of these targeted combinations during the perioperative period on tumour downstaging and postoperative RFS. Additionally, the NCT02977052 study, using a multi-arm dose-optimization design, systematically compared the safety and efficacy of different doses of ipilimumab (1 mg/kg or 3 mg/kg) and nivolumab (3 mg/kg or 1 mg/kg) in treating clinically detectable stage III melanoma, and it innovatively included the PRADO expanded cohort to dynamically adjust therapy based on the pCR. This study hopes that the results of these trials, once published, will further validate our findings. Moreover, further exploration of the optimal NT regimen is needed to guide personalised treatment options for patients.

In conclusion, NT demonstrated substantial clinical utility in treating melanoma. It can lower the risks of disease progression or death. Therefore, given its promising efficacy and manageable safety, NT may serve as a key treatment option for individuals with locally advanced or high-risk melanoma.
