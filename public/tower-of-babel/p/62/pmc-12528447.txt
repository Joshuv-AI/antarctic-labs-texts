Performance optimization of ethanol blends in diesel model using Taguchi and grey relational approach
Authors: Chaithanya Kalangi, Nikhil Janardan Rathod, Kolli Sudha Madhuri, Sudheer Kumar, Vikas Pandey, Manoj Sahni, Ernesto Leon-Castro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incorporation of biofuels, such as ethanol and biodiesel, offers a viable alternative, but it requires meticulous tuning of engine characteristics. Given their emissions of nitrogen oxides (NOx), and particulate matter (PM), diesel engines are crucial for many applications but also pose substantial environmental and health concerns, the following important selections of alternatives: renewable sources, lower environmental impact, carbon-neutral potential, and circular economy support. This work assesses the influence of fuel injection pressure, fuel percentage (specifically ethanol-biodiesel-diesel mixtures), and engine load on the efficiency and atmospheric emissions of a direct injection diesel engine designed for light-duty applications. Variations in fuel injection pressures (220, 240, 260 bar), ethanol and waste cooking oil biodiesel fractions (10%, 20%, 30% by volume), and load circumstances (20%, 50%, 80%) were evaluated using the Taguchi L9 orthogonal array. An analysis was conducted on key performance indicators, the desirability function established the ideal parameters as 220 bar fuel injection pressure, 40% ethanol-biodiesel blend, and 80% engine load, reduced brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) by 7.5%, carbon monoxide (CO) emissions by 15%, and smoke density by 18% when compared to traditional diesel performance. Furthermore, the levels of NOx emissions rose by 12%, therefore emphasizing the inherent trade-off associated with emissions. Under these ideal conditions, the brake thermal efficiency (BTE) reached its maximum value of 32%. Thereby achieving a balance between performance and emissions. The present work showcases the efficacy of the Taguchi technique, and its desired function in the optimization of diesel engines, therefore promoting environmental sustainability and operational efficiency.Keywords: Diesel engine, Biodiesel, Emissions, Taguchi optimization, Grey relational analysis, Bioenergy, Injection pressureSubject terms: Ecology, Climate-change ecology, Environmental economics

Introduction

Diesel engines serve as critical power sources across several subdivisions of the global economy, including industries, transportation, and farming. Their widespread use stems from their efficiency, durability, and high torque output. But when these engines burn fossil fuels quickly, they release a lot of pollutant into the air. These pollutants, such as NOx, HC, CO, and PM are very bed for people’s health and the environment. Small particles called fine PM, which are found in large amounts in diesel fumes, can get deep into the lungs and make asthma and bronchitis worse. Long term exposure to PM2.5 particles, which are the smallest and most dangerous, has been linked to heart disease, and lung cancer1,2.

The damage that diesel emissions due to the earth is just as scary. NOx emissions show a large part in creating pulverized equal ozone, which is a main part of smog. Ozone is bed for people’s lungs and forests, and ecosystems. NOx emissions also cause acid rain, which makes lakes and streams more acidic, which is bad for marine life. PM, especially black carbon, soaks up sunshine, which warms the planet and makes climate change worse. Also, diesel emissions include greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide (CO2), which make the climate problem even worse. India relies a lot on diesel-powered irrigation pump sets because it has a large farming sector. It is expected that 1.5 million of these fuel pump sets will be made each year, and that number will rise by 7% each year. About 14.42 million diesel-powered pump sets are in use across the country right now3. In this regard’s biofuels, especially ethanol and biodiesel, are becoming more popular as diesel fuel replacements because they can cut down on greenhouse gas pollution and make energy more secure. Biofuels are renewable, biodegradable, and animal fats. Numerous studies have shown that ethanol and biodiesel improve diesel engine emissions and reducing HC, PM, and CO emissions. However, biodiesel has a superior cetane number and lubricity than diesel, improving combustion efficiency and engine wear. However, biofuels in diesel engines present complications. Biofuel’s physical and chemical qualities differ from diesel, affecting engine performance and emissions. Due to its lower energy content and higher volatility, ethanol might reduce power output and cause vapor lock. Biodiesels increased viscosity and density may influence fuel atomization and spray, causing incomplete combustion and higher NOx emissions. Thus, optimizing engine characteristics to account for these variances is essential to maximize biofuel benefits and minimize negatives. This study examines how fuel injection pressure, fuel fraction, and load affect ethanol, bioenergy, and diesel blend-powered light duty engine presentation and discharges4. Over the past few years, researchers have made promising strides in optimizing biofuels for cleaner and more efficient combustion. A study conducted elsewhere examined jojoba-based biodiesel, where the research was focused on practical potential of these blends for maritime use, and further complete life cycle of blends5. Another similar study used biodiesel blends, added with magnesium carbonate nanoparticles, the nanoparticles addition was done to enhance the performance characteristics as same time reduction emissions was sought after. In another investigation MgCO₃ nanoparticle doping was done in an attempt to improve the blend characteristics. Further RSM was used for optimization task6,7.

Khana and Anand8 examined injection timing and pressure in used cooking oil-diesel fuel blend powered diesel engine. There were big changes in cylinder pressure, heat release rate (HRR) and BTE when they used 280 bar of IFP and 25.5° b TDC injection time. Nox and smoke emissions went down in these situations. Jaichander and Annamalai9 found that toroidal ignition chambers improve ignition effectiveness and reduce BSFC and emissions of HC and CO in a DI engine using 20% by volume Pongamia biofuel blend. This study group later showed that a toroidal combustion chamber geometry, comparable biodiesel blend, and 23° b TDC injection timing increased performance and emissions10. Numerous research has optimized engine characteristics to improve performance and reduce pollution. ANOVA was used by Saravanan et al.11 to determine how injection parameters, and exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) affect NOx discharges from an engine running on crude methyl ester of rice bran oil. They determined that emission control requires EGR and injection timings.

The piston and combustion chamber shapes have also been studied. Li et al.12 explored how piston bowl shape affects biodiesel-powered diesel engine combustion and emissions. Omega, shallow depth, and hemispherical designs were examined. Their findings revealed that geometrical bowls with a smaller area are better for lower engine speeds and those with greater squish for higher speeds. This emphasizes the importance of combustion chamber design matching engine operating. Fuel characteristics and engine design parameters must combine to optimize presentation and discharges. For engine using Kapok methyl ester, Vedharaj et al. 13 investigated combustion bowl shapes. With 25% biodiesel by volume, a hemispherical chamber performed best. Their investigation found that the toroidal chamber balanced performance and emissions well across operating circumstances.

Re-entrant combustion chambers with a low injection pressure of 230 bar performed well in an engine using dual fuels and port injection of production gas into Honge methyl ester, according to Yaliwal et al.14. This emphasizes the need of fuel specific engine setting. Viswanathan and Pasupathy15 investigated toroidal, hemispherical, and trapezoidal cylinders in an engine fueled with neem, pumpkin seed, and orange alternative. They found that the toroidal bowl geometry engine with orange alternative fuel performed best in combustion, discharges, and performance. Soni and Gupta16 for different spray directions and torques, it was found that a hemispherical worked better that re-entrant bowl piston geometry. Engine performance and emissions depend on injection timing and pressure. Saravanan et al.17 used ANOVA to assess how injection parameters, and EGR affected an engine running on iso-butanol diesel mixes. They observed that injection timing affected NOx emissions second after EGR. Variations in injection time greatly affected BTE and smoke emissions, but injection pressure had slight result on engine discharges. The smoke, NOx trade-off is a perennial diesel engine development difficulty. Due to opposing formation pathways, reducing both simultaneously is difficult. High temperatures and excess oxygen favor NOx generation, which improves combustion and reduces smoke emissions. However, reducing combustion temperature or oxygen supply to prevent NOx generations, engine characteristics must be optimized. Given the many factors in modern diesel engine operating, choosing the best settings for efficiency and emissions can be difficult and time consuming. Traditional engine testing frequently takes too long and costs too much to explore all parameter combinations. This is when statistical design of experiments (DoE) methods like Taguchi and GRA come in handy18.

These systematic methods optimize parameters with fewer experiments, saving time and money compared to full factorial experimental designs19. These approaches organize process parameters and their appropriate levels using orthogonal arrays. These methods reduce trial count and provide solid optimization outcomes through methodical experimentation and analysis. The optimization of diesel engine settings and fuel blends has improved performance and emissions in several studies20–22. Multiple injections, EGR, and intake boost pressure reduced smoke and NOx emissions in a diesel engine, according to Thobois et al.23 shown different ratios of propane, methane and inert gases and compared to combustion characteristics using integrated CFD Computation and optimization methods. An artificial neural network (ANN) model by Parlak et al.24 predicted BSFC and exhaust temperature for various injection timings in a diesel engine with a 2% error rate. A neuro-fuzzy interface system was used by AI- Al-Hinti et al.25 to properly anticipate engine characteristics after boost pressure. Kegl et al.26 optimized diesel engines fuel injection system for biodiesel and diesel. Their optimization of nozzle design, cam profile shape and injection control parameters improved injection independent of fuel type. Bunce et al.27 optimization fuel rail pressure, SOI, EGR and air-fuel ratio to minimize NOx emissions from a diesel engine using neat soybean biodiesel. They reduced NOx emissions by 5–42% and PM emissions by 50% with minimum BSFC.

Karnwal et al.28 employed Taguchi and GRA to establish optimal compression ratio (CR), nozzle pressure, injection timing, and thumba biodiesel-diesel blends. Their experiments indicated that a 30.0% thumba biodiesel blend, 20° b TDC injection timing and 250 bar nozzle pressure produced the highest performance and lowest emissions. Ganapathy et al.29 used RSM to study how injection timing, load toque, and engine speed affect diesel engine emissions and performance on Jatropha methyl ester. Their genetic algorithm optimized predictions were within 2.0% inaccuracy when experimentally tested. Lee et al.30 developed a high efficiency, low emission diesel engine for cogeneration using a three-way catalyst system, attaining higher efficiency by 6% than similar system. AI-Dawody et al.31 optimized and found that 20% soybean methyl ester in fuel was best. They used rosenbrock biased multi parametric optimization to reduce NOx emissions by 50%, which was empirically validated. Beatrice et al.32 optimized injection parameters via DoE for a light-duty diesel engine that burns bioethanol, rapeseed biodiesel and diesel fuel blended to engine run better and clean up the fumes. These works demonstrate how statistical and computational methods streamline engine optimization. Many research has focused on reducing emissions or increasing performance measures, but there’s a rising need for compressive investigations that incorporate numerous elements. Due to the intricate relationship between fuel characteristics, engine design, and operating circumstances, optimizing one parameter may affect others.

This Taguchi L9 orthogonal array study of ethanol-biofuel-diesel mixes on diesel engine presentation and release is unique. This study addresses the synergistic effects of combining ethanol and biofuel, unlike previous research. Using the want function and GRA to adjust engine parameters including FIP, percentage of fuel, and load is novel way to balance performance and pollution savings. Biofuels may be efficiently added to diesel engines using this multi parameter optimization method to promote environmental sustainability. This study has four main goals, (i) Combining contradictory items into a single metric: the desirability function combines several, occasionally conflicting replies rather than maximizing emission and performance in a superlative manner, (ii) Holistic system: evaluation balances emissions and performance trade-offs by evaluating the engine as a whole system rather than merely at the individual level, (iii) Understanding of parameter interaction: it provided a multifaceted view of the interaction between engine design factors and fuel characteristics and (iv) Framework for practical decision-making: the approach can direct optimization in the actual world where decision-makers require a single, unambiguous performance.

The goal of this study is to help make the future more sustainable by shedding light on how to make diesel engines cleaner and more efficient. The present work showcases the efficacy of the Taguchi technique, and desire function in the optimization of diesel engines, therefore promoting environmental sustainability and operational efficiency.

Materials and methods

Biodiesel production from waste oil step by step: First step, feedstock collection from hostels, restaurant, hotel, household, and local markets like vegetable oil, waste oils, and non-edible oils. Second step: transesterification reaction of oil and alcohol in the presence of a catalyst (KOH). Third step: separation of biodiesel and glycerol and then purified biodiesel. Thereafter, test the quality of biodiesel to meet the criteria of the ASTM D6751 or EN 14,214 standard using different instruments like a viscometer, a fire and flash point instrument, and a bomb calorimeter.

A methodical methodology using early experiments and optimization developed testing parameters. Based on literature and early testing, FIP, percentage of fuel, and load were examined. These preliminary testing determined the final settings, which covered a wide range of operational situations and balanced presentation and discharge reductions. The FIP were 220, 240, and 260 bar; ethanol and biodiesel fractions were 10, 20, and 30% by volume; and engine loads were 20%, 50%, and 80%. We picked these settings to study their influence on engine presentation and discharges. Ethanol was blended with diesel at incremental concentrations of 10%, 20%, and 30%. This systematic increase was selected to monitor both linear and nonlinear variations in engine performance metrics. The use of progressively higher ethanol content facilitated a more transparent evaluation of trends across performance and emission behavior. Similar type of incremental variation was selected by Shadidi et al.33 in their research, and it was made clear that incremental increase allows a better viewpoint for trend analysis for performance and emission matrices.

The engine employed in this study can handle (maximum load value, e.g., 80%). Full engine load trials are technically possible, however sub-maximal loads (20, 50, and 80%) were chosen intentionally. This approach was chosen to reflect a more realistic range of engine operating conditions in practical applications. Operating under full load continually may distort data and not provide a complete picture of engine function under more usual operating settings. Testing under full load may also increase the danger of heat stress, excessive wear, or engine component failure, which might affect experimental results. Thus, the load levels balance engine performance and emissions under different operational settings, providing real-world information.

Taguchi method

Design by Taguchi is an effective technique that pinpoints the ideal variable locations with a least number of trials, dropping testing time and money36,37. The Taguchi method measures noise’s effect on performance using a single-to-noise (S/N) ratio. Products with higher S/N are more noise-resistant. In this study, the larger-the better S/N ratio is chosen to maximize system efficiency. S/N ratio calculation as shown in Eq. (2)26.

Figure 2 depicts the process flowchart for this design. High levels of BSFC, CO, HC, NOx, and BTE are frequently observed in the exhaust of engines using biodiesel fuel. The parameters that influence the engine’s BSFC, CO, HC, smoke density, NOx, and BTE levels include the peak combustion temperature, the length of time gas from combustion spends within such a high-temperature combustion chamber, and the oxygen. The factors for the current target must be those that have an impact on these criteria. By raising the combustion temperature, fuel-bound oxygen in biodiesels enhances combustion quality by boosting even in fuel-rich areas, there is still oxygen available and decreasing BSFC, CO, HC, smoke density, and NOx levels while increasing BTE levels. The main fuel injection time, which controls the temperature of the airflow when injecting fuel, and FIP, which alters the fuel particle size distribution, both have an impact on the combustion temperature. The ratio of the premix and the time of injection change the heat produced by combustion in mixed combustion mode, which changes the combustion temperature.

Grey relational analysis

Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) was purposefully selected in light of the experimental scope and the nature of the dataset. Given the relatively compact design and limited number of experimental runs, we prioritized a technique that could extract meaningful insights without requiring complex model structures or large computational requirements. GRA unlike approaches such as RSM or Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS), avoids iterative tuning and elaborate regression modelling, making it especially suited for studies where clarity and resource efficiency are essential. Similar studies were conducted elsewhere using GRA as optimization tools engine parameters28,29.

Because Methley ester and ethanol/diesel mixtures are oxygenated in difference to diesel, the amount of fuel also has an impact on how well an engine operates42,44. The oxygen rich fuels such as used for current study have tendency to fasten the combustion kinetics leading higher in-cylinder pressure and temperature which would contribute to higher NOx formation as per extended Zeldovich Mechanism. Similar findings were demonstrated by Dhairiyasamy et al.44. The percentage impact of engine parameters on NOx is exposed in Fig. 7b. High cylinder temperature during combustion, fuel oxygen concentration, and combustion time all have an important influence on NOx emissions. Conclusions

The impartial of this work was to study the complex interconnections among fuel characteristics, engine design factors, and their collective influence on the efficiency and emissions of a light-duty diesel engine powered by ethanol-biodiesel-diesel mixtures. An investigation was conducted using a Taguchi L9 orthogonal array experimental design to systematically analyze. The following conclusions drawn:

Higher injection pressures typically enhance fuel atomization and mixing, leading to reduced BSFC and smoke emissions. Nevertheless, increasing injection pressures can also give rise to heightened NOx emissions as a result of elevated peak temperatures.

Higher injection pressures typically enhance fuel atomization and mixing, leading to reduced BSFC and smoke emissions. Nevertheless, increasing injection pressures can also give rise to heightened NOx emissions as a result of elevated peak temperatures.

The fuel fraction is essential in defining the overall combustion kinetics and emissions profile. Augmented combustion and increased oxygen availability resulting from higher proportions of biodiesel and ethanol in the blend typically lead to a reduction in CO, HC, and smoke emissions. Notwithstanding, the levels of NOx emissions tend to rise as the biodiesel content increases, therefore emphasizing the ongoing difficulty of achieving a balance between these conflicting pollutants.

At a fuel injection pressure of 220 bar, a fuel fraction of 40% by volume, and a load of 80%, the optimal equilibrium between performance and emissions was achieved. Such a combination resulted in substantial decreases in NOx and smoke density, together with enhancements in BTE.

The load on an engine has a big effect on its working point, which in turn affects its emissions and economy. Higher loads usually mean higher temperatures and pressures during burning, which means more NOx is released into the air. bigger loads, on the other hand, usually mean bigger BTE because they are more thermodynamically efficient.

Future scope

To enhance the scalability and successful application of these findings, future research should give priority to five essential areas. (1) Studies on long-term durability, (2) Advanced techniques for combustion such as incorporating in-cylinder combustion properties such as ignition delay and heat release rate, using high-speed pressure transducers and thermodynamic modeling. (3) Technologies for controlling emissions during combustion. (4) Life cycle analysis and (5) Scalability and potential for commercialization. Engaging in this research avenues will help to deepen the comprehension and improve the scalability and wider applicability of the study’s results, thereby contributing to the advancement of cleaner and more efficient diesel engine technology.
