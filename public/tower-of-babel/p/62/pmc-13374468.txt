Evaluation of physical activity intervention studies among children and adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa using the RE-AIM framework: a scoping review
Authors: Patrick Mkhanyiseli Zimu, Zethembiso Mabaso, Avela Mathe, Jeanne Martin Grace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhysical inactivity among children and adolescents is an increasing public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), contributing to the growing burden of non-communicable diseases and poor psychosocial outcomes. Although several physical activity (PA) interventions have been implemented across SSA, limited evidence exists regarding their implementation quality, sustainability and real-world applicability. This scoping review evaluated PA intervention studies among children and adolescents in SSA using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance) framework.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, EBSCOhost (SportDiscus and CINAHL) and Google Scholar were searched for peer-reviewed and grey literature published between January 2010 and July 2025. Studies were included if they reported on PA interventions targeting children and adolescents aged 6–18 years in SSA and addressed at least one RE-AIM dimension. Three reviewers independently conducted screening and data extraction using Covidence and a standardised RE-AIM framework. Data were synthesised using descriptive statistics and thematic analysis.Results27 studies met the inclusion criteria. All studies reported on Reach, including participant populations and intervention settings; however, only 33.3% described recruitment strategies and 25.9% addressed participant representativeness. Effectiveness outcomes were mainly physiological (85.2%), while psychosocial (66.7%) and academic outcomes (22.2%) were less frequently reported. Only 14.8% reported adverse events. Adoption was reported in 88.9% of studies, although representativeness and reasons for adoption were poorly described. Implementation fidelity (25.9%), intervention dose (37.0%) and programme cost (11.1%) were inconsistently reported. Maintenance was the least reported dimension (22.2%).ConclusionsPA interventions in SSA frequently report reach and effectiveness outcomes, but implementation fidelity, cost and sustainability remain underreported. Improved use of implementation frameworks such as RE-AIM is needed to strengthen the scalability, sustainability and policy relevance of PA interventions in SSA.

Introduction

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) faces a growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) due to increasing physical inactivity, urbanisation and lifestyle changes.1 Physical activity (PA) is a crucial preventive strategy; however, the SSA region faces unique barriers that limit the effectiveness and scalability of PA interventions. Factors such as limited infrastructure, socioeconomic constraints, varying cultural attitudes towards PA and inconsistent access to resources often hinder implementing and adopting health-promoting programmes.2 For example, environmental factors, including unsafe public spaces and a lack of recreational facilities, discourage active lifestyles in many urban and rural settings.3 4 Additionally, the cultural diversity across SSA countries requires interventions to be tailored to specific communities. What works in one context may not be suitable in another due to differences in social norms, values and daily living patterns.5 6 Given these diverse health challenges and environmental conditions, generalising PA interventions from other regions (eg, Europe or North America) may not be feasible without careful evaluation and adaptation to the SSA context.7

Despite the rising need for effective PA interventions in SSA, there is a notable gap in the reporting of external validity in regional studies.8 9 While many interventions report efficacy (ie, showing positive results in controlled settings), few studies address the challenges of translating these results into real-world applications across varied populations and settings.10 This lack of external validity reporting creates a significant barrier for practitioners and policymakers who must assess whether an intervention could be feasible, acceptable and effective outside controlled research environments.

Previous reviews on PA in SSA have largely focused on individual health outcomes and intervention efficacy but have not systematically evaluated the broader applicability of these interventions.11 12 Furthermore, a lack of data on the representativeness, cultural adaptability and sustainability of interventions across different countries and communities within SSA means that successful pilot studies often fail to scale effectively.13 Therefore, this review addresses an important gap by assessing how these interventions report on key aspects of external validity, which is essential for guiding real-world implementation and enhancing the scalability of effective PA programmes in the region.

External validity refers to the extent to which a study’s or intervention’s findings can be generalised to other settings, populations, times and situations beyond the study’s specific context. Therefore, when external validity is not reported, it becomes difficult for practitioners and researchers to adopt and implement the intervention in another setting, as this information is pertinent in understanding how and why the research intervention worked.14 There are various evaluation strategies that have been used to assess the external validity of an intervention, which includes a RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance) framework that evaluates an intervention trial’s internal and external validity through its five components.15 16

Additionally, the RE-AIM framework guides the evaluation, offering a structured approach to assess each study’s design, impact and sustainability within the unique context of SSA. The first component of the RE-AIM is ‘Reach’, which describes the representativeness and the proportion of participants, namely the number of those who participated in the intervention and those who were eligible but did not participate.17 18 ‘Efficacy’ or ‘effectiveness’ describes an intervention’s impact on important outcomes, for example, potential adverse effects, quality of life and economic outcomes.15 19 Adoption, the third component, entails the percentage and representativeness of settings willing to adopt or try a health promotion programme. ‘Implementation’ refers to how institutions willing to adopt the programme consistently deliver various elements of a programme as intended, as well as the intervention’s time and cost requirements.15 The RE-AIM framework’s final component is Maintenance, or the extent to which a programme or policy becomes institutionalised or part of an organisation’s routine practices and policies.19

Given the context described above, this study will contribute to the existing body of literature by providing a comprehensive review of PA interventions in SSA, using the RE-AIM framework to assess internal and external validity. By focusing on how RE-AIM are reported, this review systematically examines the factors that influence the generalisability of interventions in real-world settings. Specifically, it highlights the successes and challenges of achieving external validity, thereby informing practitioners and researchers on best practices for designing and implementing PA programmes that can be sustained and scaled across diverse African communities.

Moreover, this study seeks to identify patterns and trends in the reporting practices of SSA interventions, highlighting gaps where further research is needed to strengthen intervention scalability and adaptability. The findings will enhance understanding of the practical applicability of PA interventions and provide evidence-based recommendations to improve reporting standards in future studies, potentially guiding policy efforts to integrate effective, culturally appropriate PA programmes into broader public health initiatives across SSA. The scoping review aims to evaluate PA interventions implemented in SSA from 2010 to 2025 using the RE-AIM framework, and to identify trends, gaps and opportunities to enhance external validity reporting.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study followed a scoping review methodology, guided by the Arksey and O’Malley framework,20 with enhancements from Levac’s scoping studies methodology21 and the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist for reporting.22 The completed PRISMA-ScR Checklist is available in online supplemental appendix A. The review aimed to map the extent, range, and nature of research on PA interventions conducted in SSA between 2010 and 2025, with a specific focus on reporting internal and external validity using the RE-AIM framework. The review protocol was not registered.

Eligibility criteria

A comprehensive and systematic search strategy was developed in consultation with a University librarian to ensure methodological rigour and database appropriateness. The literature search targeted peer-reviewed publications and relevant grey literature published between January 2010 and 2025 based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria:

Inclusion criteria

Empirical studies (quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods) conducted in SSA involving children and adolescents aged 6–18, primarily focusing on PA. Studies must report at least one RE-AIM framework dimension and be published in peer-reviewed journals or grey literature with full-text access and sufficient methodological detail.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they did not align with the scope and objectives of this review. Specifically, studies were excluded if the population was not children and adolescents (6–18 years) or if participants were not based in SSA. Studies that did not focus on a PA intervention were excluded. Non-empirical publications, including reviews, commentaries, editorials, protocols and conference abstracts without sufficient data, were also excluded. Studies were excluded if they were published before 2010. In addition, studies were excluded if they did not report any component of the RE-AIM framework or if full-text articles were unavailable or lacked sufficient methodological detail to allow for data extraction. Only studies published in English were included.

Data sources and search strategy

The following electronic databases were systematically searched: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, EBSCOhost (including SPORTDiscus, CINAHL) and Google Scholar. In addition, Google Scholar was used to identify grey literature and supplement peer-reviewed sources that may not be indexed in traditional databases. The search strategy incorporated a combination of keywords and Boolean operators tailored to each platform, focusing on PA interventions involving children and adolescents in SSA and incorporating outcomes aligned with the RE-AIM framework. The complete search strategies for all databases, including search terms, Boolean operators and limits applied, are provided in online supplemental file 1. Study selection

All identified records retrieved from the database searches were imported into Covidence systematic review software for screening, de-duplication and management. Covidence automatically removed duplicate records prior to the screening process. Three reviewers conducted the title and abstract screening independently using Covidence’s blinded screening feature. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or, where necessary, by a fourth reviewer. Studies deemed potentially eligible were then retrieved in full and subjected to a full-text review to determine final inclusion, based on the pre-defined eligibility criteria. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded within Covidence to ensure transparency and replicability.

A structured data charting process was used to systematically extract and organise information from the studies included. Data were charted using Covidence systematic review software, using the customised Data Extraction 1 template. Based on the RE-AIM framework, the charting form was developed to ensure comprehensive and theory-driven data capture.

The data charting form was piloted on a small subset of studies by two independent reviewers to ensure consistency and clarity in interpretation. Based on this pilot, minor refinements were made to improve usability. The final charting template included the following domains:

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers for all included studies, while screening at both title/abstract and full-text levels was conducted independently by three reviewers. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion, with a fourth reviewer consulted where consensus could not be reached. The extracted data were exported from Covidence for analysis. Quantitative data were summarised using descriptive statistics, while qualitative findings were synthesised thematically according to the RE-AIM dimensions.

Synthesis of results

The results of this scoping review were collated and summarised using both quantitative and qualitative synthesis methods, guided by the five dimensions of the RE-AIM framework as described in online supplemental appendix C. Data extracted from Covidence was first exported into Microsoft Excel for organisation and descriptive analysis. Frequencies and proportions were used to summarise key study characteristics, such as country of origin, study design, intervention setting and target population.

A narrative synthesis of the RE-AIM dimensions was conducted to highlight how each component (RE-AIM) was reported across studies. Patterns, gaps and contextual variations were identified to provide insight into the feasibility, uptake and sustainability of PA interventions for children and adolescents in SSA. Where studies did not explicitly mention RE-AIM terms, proxy indicators were identified and mapped accordingly (eg, participation rate as a proxy for reach).

Qualitative data from included studies were analysed thematically, enabling a deeper understanding of implementation processes, cultural adaptations and contextual barriers and enablers. The synthesis also considered equity-related issues, including geographic disparities, population-specific adaptations and health system constraints.

Findings are presented in tabular and narrative formats to enhance clarity and accessibility. A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram outlines the study selection process, and summary tables visualise the distribution and characteristics of included studies across the RE-AIM dimensions.

Characteristics of sources of evidence

A total of 27 studies published between 2010 and 2025 were included in this scoping review. Most interventions were conducted in school settings and targeted children and adolescents aged 6–18 years. South Africa accounted for the largest proportion of included studies, followed by Nigeria and other SSA countries. Study designs included cluster-randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies and mixed-methods approaches. Sample sizes ranged from fewer than 100 participants to more than 1200 participants, while intervention durations varied from 2 weeks to 12 months. Interventions primarily included aerobic exercise, sport-based activities and structured PA programmes.

Synthesis of results

Reach

All studies reported their target population and setting, often focusing on low-resource schools or youth at risk of non-communicable diseases. Reach in this review was assessed as the number and proportion of the target population impacted by one or both components of the intervention programme and how representative these participants were of the broader at-risk population.15 Although all 27 studies reported some level of Reach, few comprehensively described how recruitment was conducted or whether proactive strategies were used to ensure inclusion of the most vulnerable groups.

According to the RE-AIM framework,15 recruitment strategies should improve access, awareness and the appropriateness of interventions to meet the needs of the intended population. However, the majority of studies lacked detail on these strategies. Proactive recruitment, a strategy in which participants are actively approached, has been shown to improve participation rates and inclusiveness, particularly among newly diagnosed or marginalised individuals.23 In contrast, many reviewed studies appeared to rely on reactive or convenience sampling methods, which may limit generalisability and exclude higher-risk individuals. Only a minority of studies included disaggregated data by sex, socioeconomic status or geography which are key factors in assessing the representativeness of those reached. As a result, while reach was universally reported, its depth and inclusivity were inconsistently documented across the studies.

Effectiveness

85% of studies reported on PA or physiological outcomes, such as increased MVPA (moderate-to-vigorous PA), body mass index (BMI) reduction or improved cardiovascular endurance. However, outcome measures and timing varied widely, limiting direct comparability. Only a few studies used objective measures such as accelerometers.

Effectiveness in the context of this review was understood not only as improvements in physiological outcomes but also as anticipated and unanticipated effects at both the individual and institutional levels. According to King et al,24 effectiveness is a measure of a health behaviour intervention’s impact, including both positive and negative effects assessed through quantitative and qualitative methods. Studies varied significantly in how comprehensively they reported these multidimensional outcomes. While most highlighted improvements in fitness levels, some reported secondary benefits such as enhanced academic performance and improved emotional well-being, although these were often not the primary focus.

Moreover, adverse or unanticipated effects were rarely reported, such as increased drop-out rates due to programme intensity or schedule conflicts with school curricula. This limits the field’s understanding of potential unintended consequences of PA programming. At the institutional level, few studies documented how the interventions influenced school-wide policies or teaching practices, missing an opportunity to assess broader systemic impact. Overall, while most studies effectively measured individual-level change, the omission of institutional effectiveness and qualitative reporting leaves gaps in understanding the full scope of intervention impact.

Adoption

Nearly 89% of studies detailed the number and type of schools or community settings that adopted the intervention. Adoption was defined as the percentage and representativeness of settings, such as schools, clinics or community centres, that were willing to initiate or support a health promotion programme. Despite broad reporting on where interventions occurred, most studies did not elaborate on how these sites were selected, adopting institutions’ representativeness or why some settings declined to participate.

According to King et al,24 adoption is critical for determining an intervention’s potential scalability, as interventions must be accepted and operationalised by institutions that can deliver them to broader populations. Most studies failed to explore organisational readiness, infrastructure or policy support mechanisms, all of which are vital to ensuring intervention success across diverse contexts. Additionally, a few studies examined adoption at multiple levels, for example, between school administrators, physical education teachers and external facilitators, which would allow for a more nuanced understanding of institutional uptake.

While adoption rates were numerically high, the lack of detail limits insight into the conditions and characteristics that support or hinder programme initiation across different settings. Without such contextual information, it becomes difficult to determine how generalisable or adaptable the interventions are for other institutions seeking to adopt similar initiatives.

Implementation

Implementation reporting was fragmented across the studies. While many studies described basic intervention components, such as programme duration and session frequency, detailed reporting on fidelity, adherence to protocol and delivery consistency was often lacking. Implementation involves evaluating whether intervention components are delivered as intended, especially when executed by non-research staff in applied settings.25 Furthermore, the RE-AIM framework emphasises that implementation includes both the consistency of programme delivery and the associated cost or resource implications.15

Across the 27 studies, fidelity assessment documentation was limited, such as observations, checklists or structured fidelity tools used to ensure interventions were being delivered per protocol. This omission threatens internal validity and raises questions about replicability and scalability. Few studies discussed who delivered the interventions and whether they were trained personnel, educators or health professionals. This detail is particularly important for scaling interventions in community or school-based settings.

Additionally, intervention delivery’s time and cost implications were almost universally under-reported. This represents a major limitation for real-world uptake, where schools and organisations require a clear understanding of resource demands. While some studies referred to the use of volunteers or external facilitators, cost-effectiveness analyses were virtually absent. Thus, although many interventions were successfully implemented in the short term, a lack of detailed implementation metrics restricts the ability to assess their feasibility and sustainability in routine practice.

Maintenance

Fewer than half of the studies addressed maintenance. Of those that did, most focused on short-term follow-up (6–12 months). Maintenance was defined in this review as the extent to which an intervention became institutionalised within routine practices or policies at the setting level, and whether the positive individual outcomes were sustained over time.15 19 Despite its importance, long-term sustainability and integration into local infrastructure were rarely reported.

At the individual level, few studies assessed whether participants maintained improved PA behaviours or health outcomes beyond the immediate postintervention period. Most lacked longer-term data collection points or behavioural tracking. At the organisational level, even fewer studies documented the extent to which interventions were absorbed into school policies, schedules or routines.

The absence of planning for programme continuation, such as through teacher training, local budget allocation or institutional policy development indicates a disconnect between short-term efficacy and sustainable public health impact. This gap limits opportunities for replication and scale-up, particularly in resource-constrained environments where long-term integration is essential to maintaining impact. Future interventions must explicitly incorporate and measure sustainability strategies from the outset to support evidence-based practice.

Discussion

This scoping review of 27 PA intervention studies conducted between 2010 and 2025 highlights significant progress and persistent gaps in the application of the RE-AIM framework in SSA. The universal reporting of Reach (100%) indicates that researchers consistently identify and describe their target populations, predominantly children and adolescents in school settings, often within low-resource environments. However, the depth of reach reporting is limited, with few studies detailing recruitment strategies or providing disaggregated data by sex, socioeconomic status or geographic location. Such omissions restrict insights into the representativeness and inclusivity of interventions, which are critical for tailoring effective public health strategies.24 25

Effectiveness was reported in most studies (85.2%), primarily through PA and physiological outcomes such as MVPA, BMI and cardiovascular fitness. Yet, variability in outcome measures and timing limits cross-study comparability. Additionally, psychosocial outcomes were less consistently assessed (66.7%), reflecting a need for broader evaluation beyond physiological markers. Importantly, few studies captured unanticipated or adverse effects, nor did many evaluate institutional or systemic changes such as key aspects that inform holistic intervention impact.15 19

Adoption reporting was relatively strong (88.9%), focusing on the proportion of settings initiating interventions. Nonetheless, details about setting selection, institutional readiness and multi-level adoption processes were largely absent, hindering understanding of scalability and contextual adaptability.25 Similarly, Implementation was extensively documented in terms of frequency and duration, but critical elements such as fidelity, delivery consistency, training of facilitators and cost were frequently underreported. This lack compromises internal validity and challenges replication or scale-up efforts in real-world conditions.15

Maintenance emerged as the least consistently addressed dimension, with fewer than half of the studies including follow-up data, mostly limited to short-term (6–12 months). The scarcity of long-term behavioural and institutional sustainability data highlights a significant gap, especially in resource-constrained settings where sustained integration into routine practice is essential for lasting health impact. While interventions showed promising short-term outcomes, their durability and systemic embedding remain uncertain.

Trends and gaps in RE-AIM reporting in PA interventions in SSA

The findings of this review reveal several consistent trends in the reporting of RE-AIM dimensions within PA interventions targeting children and adolescents in SSA. Most notably, there is a strong emphasis on Reach and Effectiveness, with nearly all studies reporting basic participant characteristics and intervention outcomes. This aligns with global patterns in PA intervention research, where internal validity and outcome-based measures are prioritised over implementation and external validity dimensions.15

However, beyond these commonly reported dimensions, substantial gaps remain. Reporting on Adoption was limited, with few studies providing information on organisational uptake, setting representativeness or implementer characteristics. This is particularly concerning in SSA, where interventions are often delivered through diverse and resource-constrained systems such as schools, community programmes and non-governmental organisations. Without such information, it becomes difficult to determine whether interventions can be feasibly adopted across similar contexts.

Similarly, Implementation reporting was inconsistent. While some studies described intervention components and delivery processes, critical elements such as fidelity, adaptations and cost were frequently absent. This reflects broader findings in implementation science literature, which highlight cost and fidelity as among the least reported yet most important components for scale-up and sustainability.15 25 In SSA settings, where resources are limited and adaptations are often necessary, the absence of such information further limits the practical applicability of interventions.

The most pronounced gap was observed in Maintenance, with very few studies assessing long-term outcomes, sustainability or institutionalisation of interventions. This trend is consistent with international evidence, where maintenance remains the least reported RE-AIM dimension despite being critical for understanding long-term impact.25 In SSA, this gap is particularly problematic, as interventions that are not sustained beyond the study period offer limited value for policy and practice.

Collectively, these trends highlight a broader structural limitation in the SSA evidence base. While there is growing evidence of intervention effectiveness, insufficient reporting exists on the conditions required for replication, adaptation and scale-up. As a result, even promising interventions cannot be confidently translated across different SSA contexts. This reinforces the need for future studies to adopt more comprehensive reporting frameworks that systematically address all RE-AIM dimensions.

Limitations

Language restrictions may have excluded non-English studies. The review period (2010–2025) may have missed earlier or ongoing unpublished interventions. Inconsistent reporting across studies limited the application of the RE-AIM framework, especially for Maintenance and organisational-level Adoption. As per scoping review methodology, no formal quality appraisal was conducted, and narrative synthesis was used to address methodological diversity.

Conclusions

The findings underscore that, while PA interventions in SSA are robustly reported on in terms of Reach, Effectiveness and Adoption, critical dimensions of Implementation fidelity and long-term Maintenance are inadequately documented. This uneven reporting limits the capacity to fully assess intervention quality, scalability and sustainability. To bridge this gap, future research must prioritise comprehensive and standardised measurement across all RE-AIM dimensions, particularly emphasising fidelity monitoring, cost evaluation and long-term follow-up to ensure that successful interventions translate into sustained public health benefits.

Recommendations

To strengthen the quality and impact of PA interventions, researchers should incorporate detailed recruitment methodologies and provide disaggregated reporting to enhance understanding of intervention reach and representativeness, particularly among vulnerable subpopulations. Effectiveness evaluations must be expanded to systematically include psychosocial outcomes, adverse events and institutional impacts, employing quantitative and qualitative methods. Adoption studies should investigate multilevel organisational readiness, barriers and facilitators to develop scalable and contextually appropriate interventions. Implementation reporting can be improved through routine fidelity assessments, clear documentation of the qualifications of those delivering interventions and comprehensive cost analyses to ensure real-world feasibility. Maintenance must become a core focus, with longer-term follow-up that includes behavioural tracking and evaluation of policy or routine integration to ensure sustainable health outcomes. Lastly, funding agencies and policymakers should incentivise thorough RE-AIM reporting and support capacity-building efforts to address these gaps, thereby facilitating the translation of research findings into practical and sustainable applications within resource-limited settings.
