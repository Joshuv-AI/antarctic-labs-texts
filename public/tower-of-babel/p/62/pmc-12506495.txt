Decreased Mitochondrial DNA Integrity and Elevated Inflammatory Markers in Late-Life Depression: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Ana Paula Mendes-Silva, Perla El-Ahmad, Ana Paula Costa, Lloyd Cenon Balbuena, Yuliya Stoycheva Nikolova, Tarek Rajji, James Lowery Kennedy, Erica Leandro Vieira, Vanessa Faria Gonçalves, Breno Satler Diniz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate-life depression (LLD) is a prevalent and severe mental disorder. The biological mechanisms underlying LLD are not fully understood, but increasing evidence suggests that mitochondria play a significant role. Impaired mitochondrial function leads to excessive production of reactive oxygen species and the release of circulating cell-free mitochondrial DNA (ccf-mtDNA). The ccf-mtDNA activates the toll-like receptor system, which triggers a systemic proinflammatory response. However, there is limited understanding of the impact of ccf-mtDNA integrity, such as deletions, on LLD pathological conditions.MethodsWe included 90 older individuals (50 LLDs, 40 nondepressed healthy control participants [HCs]), with a subset of individuals followed up at 30 months (13 LLDs, 13 HCs). Plasma was separated from blood, and DNA was extracted. Mitochondrial genes MT-ND2 and MT-ND4 were targeted to evaluate ccf-mtDNA levels and deletion using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Plasma interleukins (ILs) 1β, 5, and 6 were quantified via multiplex immunoassay.ResultsLLD was linked to increased ccf-mtDNA instability at baseline (deletion: F88,1 = 7.105, p = .009; levels: F88,1 = 6.885, p = .01), which was associated with more severe depressive symptoms and greater medical comorbidity burden. Longitudinal analysis revealed significant effects of diagnosis and time on ccf-mtDNA levels and the deletion rate. Additionally, higher deletion rates at baseline predicted IL-5 and IL-6 levels at 30 months (padjusted = .13, padjusted = .12, respectively).ConclusionsIncreased ccf-mtDNA instability may heighten vulnerability to emotional dysregulation and medical burden in individuals with LLD. Further research is needed to validate our findings and elucidate the mechanisms that connect mitochondrial instability and inflammation in LLD.

Late-life depression (LLD) impacts between 1% and 5% of older adults globally and ranks among the leading causes of disability; it is often associated with higher rates of comorbidity and temporary cognitive impairment (1,2). Several studies have reported the interaction of abnormalities in multiple biological mechanisms involved in LLD (3, 4, 5, 6), including telomere shortening (7,8), molecular senescence (9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17), cell cycle dysregulation (18,19), heightened proinflammatory status (20, 21, 22, 23), and oxidative stress (24, 25, 26, 27). Notably, mitochondria play a significant role in all these processes (28, 29, 30, 31, 32). However, the mechanism by which abnormal mitochondrial function exacerbates increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, such as increased risk of comorbid conditions and frailty, in older adults with depression is unclear.

Dysfunctional mitochondria disrupt the cellular energy balance and metabolism, leading to excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (33,34). Increased ROS can damage macromolecules within mitochondria, including DNA (35), leading to defects in the function of complexes I and III (36,37). An imbalance between ROS production and cellular antioxidant capacity can lead to metabolic oxidative stress, genomic instability (e.g., mutations and deletions), decreased energy production, and cellular injury (37, 38, 39, 40). Deletions are particularly common in the major arc of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), encompassing genes such as MT-ND4 (mitochondrial-encoded NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit 4), while deletions are rare in the minor arc, which harbors genes such as MT-ND2 (mitochondrial-encoded NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit 2) (41, 42, 43). The instability of mtDNA increases vulnerability to fragmentation, thereby facilitating its release from the mitochondria into the cytosol and body fluids as a cell-free material, such as circulating cell-free mtDNA (ccf-mtDNA) (44). Elevated levels of ccf-mtDNA can act as damage-associated molecular patterns (45), eliciting an inflammatory response by inducing the production of several cytokines, including interleukins 1β, 5, and 6 (IL-1β, IL-5, and IL-6, respectively) (45, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50).

The role of the ccf-mtDNA in depression is still not fully understood. Previous studies suggest a potential association between elevated ccf-mtDNA levels and major depressive disorder (MDD). Higher levels of ccf-mtDNA have been associated with a worse response to treatment (51), more severe depressive symptoms (51), the presence of suicidal behaviors (52), and concurrent physical frailty (53). On the other hand, few studies have reported lower plasma levels of ccf-mtDNA in adults with MDD than in control participants (47,54). DNA integrity is an important yet underexplored feature of ccf-mtDNA that can explain some of the mixed findings that have been observed in depression studies. The features, such as high copy number of mtDNA and the accumulation of mtDNA mutations and deletions, arise before cellular dysfunction is evident and chronic inflammation is established (49,55,56). This phenomenon shows a progressive increase with age (57) and contributes to the pathogenesis of LLD (58). Despite its potential significance, there is limited understanding of how damage in circulating mtDNA (i.e., ccf-mtDNA), such as deletions, contributes to the worsening of pathological conditions.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to include a longitudinal evaluation of deletion rates and levels of ccf-mtDNA in individuals with LLD. We hypothesized that higher levels of ccf-mtDNA and deletions (indicative of lower mtDNA integrity) would be increased in LLDs and lead to systemic inflammation. We also hypothesized that these abnormalities would be associated with more severe depressive symptoms, poorer health status, and worse cognitive performance in individuals with LLD compared with nondepressed healthy control participants (HCs). Moreover, a subset of individuals from both groups were followed up at 30 months, which offered insights into how diagnosis, time, and these 2 factors together affected the trajectory of ccf-mtDNA. We also investigated whether baseline ccf-mtDNA markers could predict future levels of proinflammatory markers (IL-1β, IL-5, and IL-6).

Methods and Materials

Population Assessment

Our team previously published preliminary cross-sectional studies based on samples collected from the SenDep cohort in 2018 and 2019 (N = 53, including 32 individuals with LLD) (51,53). Here, we expanded our analysis and added a longitudinal component to understand how levels of ccf-mtDNA and measures of instability (deletion burden) influence disease outcomes. Following 2019, recruitment and patient retention were affected by challenges related to COVID-19, which resulted in reduced participation rates. For the current study, we enrolled 90 older adults at baseline (50 LLDs and 40 HCs), which included individuals from the earlier 2018 to 2019 collection. Ultimately, 26 individuals (13 LLDs and 13 HCs) completed the 30-month follow-up assessment. All participant assessments were conducted at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, Canada, with approval from the local ethics board. All participants provided written informed consent for their participation.

Individuals with LLD were diagnosed according to DSM-5. Older adults ≥60 years without a history of major depressive episodes, other psychiatric disorders, or substance abuse were included in the HCs group. We used the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview to confirm the diagnosis of a major depressive episode or to exclude a lifetime history of psychiatric disorders. The exclusion criteria for all participants included a previous diagnosis of dementia; major neurological disorders (e.g., bipolar disorder, posttraumatic disorder, schizophrenia), history of stroke, inflammatory disorders, use of immunomodulators, current unstable medical illness, recent cancer history (except nonmelanoma skin cancer), and a history of alcohol or drug abuse in the past 6 months. Individuals with coexisting anxiety disorders (such as phobias) were not excluded from the study due to substantial overlap with MDD. Individuals with LLD had not received antidepressant treatment or immunomodulators for at least 3 months before the research baseline assessment and blood draw.

Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) (59). Global cognitive performance was assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (60), which evaluates multiple cognitive domains, including attention, memory, language, visuospatial abilities, executive functions, and orientation. Additionally, the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale-Geriatric (CIRS-G) (61) was used to evaluate the overall health status of the participants. The CIRS-G considers a wide array of medical comorbidities commonly encountered in older adults, ranging from cardiac and respiratory disorders to neurological and psychiatric conditions.

Biological Specimens

Peripheral blood was collected from all participants by venipuncture in EDTA tubes after they had fasted overnight and was processed within 3 hours of collection. EDTA tubes containing blood were centrifuged at 3000g for 10 minutes at 4 °C to separate the plasma. The plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until laboratory analysis.

DNA Extraction From Plasma

We initially thawed the aliquoted plasma at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 10,000g for 10 minutes to remove cells and cellular debris. Subsequently, we used the QIAmp 96 DNA Blood Kit (Qiagen) to extract DNA from 200 μL of plasma following the manufacturer’s protocol. The total DNA was then eluted in 50 μL of elution buffer and quantified via spectrophotometric analysis at 260/280 nm in a NanoPhotometer P-Class (Implen).

Assessment of ccf-mtDNA Levels and Integrity

Quantitative analysis of the ccf-mtDNA was conducted using real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), using a modified version of a previously published method (43). To assess the integrity and abundance of ccf-mtDNA, we targeted the 2 mitochondrial genes MT-ND2 and MT-ND4. The choice of the MT-ND2 gene, which is located in the minor arc where large deletions are rare, allowed us to assess ccf-mtDNA levels without the influence of deletions. Conversely, choosing the MT-ND4 gene, which is located in the major arc where approximately 84% of large deletions are common, enabled us to estimate the occurrence of deletions within the mtDNA (62,63). Therefore, MT-ND4 levels may reflect an increased burden of mtDNA deletions rather than an overall reduction in circulating mtDNA content.

The primers used were as follows: MT-ND2, forward 5′-CACACTCATCACAGCGCTAA-3′ and reverse 5′-GGATTATGGATGCGGTTGCT-3′ (Life Technology); MT-ND4, forward 5′-ACCTTGGCTATCATCACCCGAT-3′ and reverse 5′-AGTGCGATGAGTAGGGGAAGG-3′ (Life Technology).

Quantitative PCR was conducted using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Each reaction, with a total volume of 20 μL, contained 50 ng of DNA template, 1 μL of each primer (10 μM), 10 μL of SYBR MIX (2× Sensifast no-ROX; Bioline), and nuclease-free water. We generated an 8-point standard curve for each primer pair using the same pooled DNA sample (combined plasma DNA samples). All the reactions were performed in triplicate. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 minutes, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C in 5 seconds (melting), annealing at 60 °C to 65 °C for 10 seconds, and extension at 72 °C for 10 seconds (extension). The program concluded with a melting curve analysis, with fluorescence measured continuously from 60 °C to 95 °C.

The relative concentration of the ccf-mtDNA was determined by comparing crossing-point values from the test samples with the standard curve. For the MT-ND2 gene, the number of ccf-mtDNA units per sample was calculated using the following formula: DNA amount (ng/μL) divided by the size of the PCR fragment (161 bp) and the molar mass per base pair (g/mol) and then multiplied by Avogadro’s constant. Similarly, for the MT-ND4 gene, the same formula was applied, with the only difference being the amplicon size (107 bp). The ccf-mtDNA levels are reported individually for MT-ND2 and MT-ND4. After the relative levels were calculated, we confirmed a positive correlation between the ccf-mtDNA MT-ND2 and MT-ND4 levels in the cohort (r = 0.507, p < .001). Subsequently, the estimated deletion rate was calculated by dividing the copy number of MT-ND2 by that of MT-ND4 (43). As the ratio between the minor and major arcs should be consistent in the absence of deletions, utilizing this ratio allows us to estimate deletions within the major arc in the samples. The ccf-mtDNA levels and deletion rate were reported on a logarithmic scale of units per microliter.

Plasma Inflammatory Marker Levels

Plasma samples were analyzed using a customized multiplex immunoassay containing IL-1β, IL-5, and IL-6 analytes from R&D/Biotechne and conducted on a LUMINEX 100/200 (Luminex Corp.). The laboratory procedures strictly followed the manufacturer’s instructions and were conducted in 2 distinct batches, with samples from individuals with LLD and HCs samples randomly distributed across these batches.

Statistical Analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the data. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests. Prior to subsequent analysis, the estimated deletion rate and levels of ccf-mtDNA, IL-1β, IL-5, and IL-6 were log transformed. We carried out Student's t tests or analyses of variance (ANOVAs) to examine differences between individuals with LLD and control participants for continuous variables with non-normal or normal distributions, respectively. ANOVAs were adjusted for covariates such as age, sex, and tobacco use (packs per year). A linear mixed-effects model for repeated measures adjusting for age and sex was used to assess the effects of diagnosis, time, and their interaction on ccf-mtDNA levels and deletion rate. In each model, the main effects of time and diagnosis and their interaction were estimated without imposing a linear time trend. A general linear model (glm) was generated in R (version 4.2.3) to test the association between the baseline levels and deletion rates of ccf-mtDNA and the levels of inflammatory markers IL-1β, IL-5, and IL-6 at the 30-month follow-up. A Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons, and adjusted p values (padj) were reported. Cohen’s d effect sizes were also calculated in R using the effsize package (version 0.8.1). Specifically, the cohen.d() function was used to quantify the magnitude of group differences for continuous variables between the LLD and control groups. For each comparison, effect sizes were interpreted according to conventional thresholds: negligible (|d| < 0.2), small (0.2 ≥ |d| < 0.5), medium (0.5 ≥ |d| < 0.8), and large (|d| ≥ 0.8). Correlations between ccf-mtDNA status or inflammatory marker levels and demographic and clinical features were determined using Spearman’s rho or Pearson’s tests. All the statistical analyses, except for those for glm and Cohen’s d, were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp.). All tests were 2 tailed with a 95% CI, and a p value ≤ .05 indicated statistical significance.

Additionally, we performed 2-way ANOVA models including sex, diagnosis, and their interaction as predictors for ccf-mtDNA MT-ND2 levels, ccf-mtDNA MT-ND4 levels, and estimated deletion ratios to investigate potential sex effects. We observed significant main effects of diagnosis for ccf-mtDNA MT-ND2 (p = .044) and estimated deletion ratios (p = .025), while no significant result was found for MT-ND4 (p = .99). Longitudinal Evaluation of ccf-mtDNA Levels and Deletion Rates Reveals Main Effects of Diagnosis and Time

Post hoc pairwise comparisons examining the effect of time within each diagnosis group revealed a significant increase in cc-mtDNA MT-ND2 levels from baseline to 30 months in the control group (estimate = 0.72, SE = 0.28, t71.2 = 2.55, p = .013). Although the change in the LLDs group was not statistically significant, there was a trend toward an increase over time (estimate = 0.50, SE = 0.28, t84.5 = 1.75, p = .084). Similarly, there were significant effects of diagnosis (F51,1 = 10.523, p = .002) and time (F102,1 = 29.184, p < .001) on the estimated deletion rate, but no significant interaction between diagnosis and time was observed (F102,1 = 0.375, p = .54) (Figure 3B). Baseline ccf-mtDNA Deletion Rates Predict Long-Term IL-5 and IL-6 Levels

We observed significantly elevated levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β (F56,1 = 14.757, p = .001) and IL-5 (F56,1 = 4.594, p = .036) in individuals with LLD compared with HCs at baseline (Table 1). In contrast, the difference in IL-6 levels between groups did not reach statistical significance (F58,1 = 3.368, p = .07).

We further investigated whether baseline levels and deletion rates of ccf-mtDNA could predict the levels of inflammatory markers (IL-1β, IL-5, and IL-6) at the 30-month follow-up using the whole cohort. Our findings from the glm revealed that baseline deletion rates significantly predicted IL-5 and IL-6 levels at 30 months (β = −2.31; 95% CI, −4.45 to −0.17; p = .045 and β = −1.42; 95% CI, −2.70 to −0.14; p = .040, respectively). Discussion

There is accumulating evidence of mitochondrial involvement in the pathophysiology of MDD across the lifespan. Our findings add new insight to previous research by revealing the potential role of both the level and integrity of ccf-mtDNA in individuals with LLD and their relationship with depressive symptoms and medical comorbidity burden. At baseline, we observed significantly higher levels of the ccf-mtDNA MT-ND2 in individuals with LLD compared with HCs, with a trend toward statistical significance observed for the estimated deletion rate after adjusting for covariates. Furthermore, increased levels of ccf-mtDNA MT-ND2 and deletion rates were both linked to more severe depressive symptoms and a greater burden of medical comorbidity. Longitudinal analysis demonstrated significant effects of diagnosis and time in the levels of ccf-mtDNA MT-ND2 and deletion. However, no significant interaction between diagnosis and time was observed, suggesting that these effects are independent. Notably, we found nominally significant associations between baseline estimated deletion rates and IL-5 and IL-6 levels at the 30-month follow-up, indicating a potential role for ccf-mtDNA integrity in shaping future inflammatory responses.

Our findings underscore the importance of investigating the biological implications of alterations in the levels and integrity of ccf-mtDNA and their potential relevance for the pathology and outcomes of LLD. Studies examining the association between ccf-mtDNA levels and major depression across the lifespan have had mixed results (47,51, 52, 53,64,65) and many have significant limitations. Furthermore, not all the studies adjusted for important covariates, such as somatic health and lifestyle factors, and the vast majority have focused on the cross-sectional relationship between depression and ccf-mtDNA status. Studies suggest that ccf-mtDNA levels are influenced by cellular stress, apoptosis, or inefficiency in the clearance of circulating ccf-mtDNA. Moreover, the number of extracellular mtDNA copies (e.g., ccf-mtDNA) may reflect the ability of a cell to produce adenosine triphosphate through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, indirectly helping to assess mitochondrial function (66). Elevated ccf-mtDNA levels may indicate a compensatory upregulation of mtDNA copies due to poor mitochondrial health or stress. This compensatory mechanism may partially explain the increased health risks observed in individuals with depression (67).

mtDNA represents a critical target for oxidative damage (35,68), with mutations and deletions becoming more frequent with age (69) and contributing to age-related disorders (70,71). Damaged mtDNA can further increase oxidative stress by impairing the expression of key proteins involved in electron transport (72,73), leading to a vicious cycle of ROS and organelle dysregulation that eventually triggers apoptosis (11,72, 73, 74). Our group recently found that greater presence of nonsynonymous mutations in the mtDNA, where there is an elevated functional impact score, is related to premature epigenetic as well as biological aging in young adulthood (75). In older adults, alterations in mtDNA have also been associated with altered cognitive abilities (76,77).

Postmortem brain studies have reported higher rates of mtDNA deletions, particularly within the MT-ND4 gene, in individuals diagnosed with MDD (78,79) and bipolar disorder (78,80) compared with control participants. Furthermore, mouse models harboring mtDNA deletions have shown that the loss of integrity of mtDNA translates into intracellular calcium deregulation and depression-like symptoms (81,82). In contrast, a study conducted with individuals with bipolar disorder found no differences in plasma levels of ccf-mtDNA carrying the common 4977-bp deletion (which encompasses the mitochondrial gene MT-ND4) between depressed and remitted states or any significant association between the ccf-mtDNA deletion rate and symptom severity (83). Despite these findings, the role of mtDNA integrity—particularly deletions in its ccf form (ccf-mtDNA)—in the development and progression of psychiatric disorders such as depression remains poorly understood (84). Clarifying its contribution to the clinical presentation of depression could offer valuable insights into the previously inconsistent findings.

In our study, individuals with LLD exhibited higher estimated ccf-mtDNA deletion rates compared with HCs at baseline. These elevated ccf-mtDNA deletions were associated with more severe depressive symptoms and a greater medical comorbidity burden. Considering the essential role of the mitochondrial gene MT-ND4 in maintaining the proper function of complex I of the electron transport chain (85), which is vital for energy metabolism, higher deletion rates may indicate compromised mitochondrial function in individuals with LLD. Mitochondrial dysfunction can in turn lead to the increased oxidative stress observed in individuals with LLD, which contributes to accelerated aging (28) and poorer health outcomes in individuals with mood disorders (24,25,86, 87, 88). Supporting the relevance of our findings, our group recently reported that higher mtDNA deletion rates within exosomes are associated with poorer cognitive performance in individuals with LLD compared with healthy control participants (89). This previous work provides complementary evidence and supports the validity of the estimated deletion rate metric using a distinct and less degradation-prone source of mtDNA. Future studies that isolate exosomes derived from specific brain cell types will help determine whether peripheral mtDNA deletion markers may reflect brain-specific mitochondrial dysfunction. Growing evidence indicates that both central and peripheral cells and tissues contribute to circulating mtDNA, which is often associated with inflammatory responses (90, 91, 92). Although ccf-mtDNA can originate from multiple sources, distinguishing between central and peripheral contributions remains a challenge (93). However, independent of the cell origin, mtDNA can be transported to and from the central nervous system (e.g., via exosomes) and play a significant role in neuropsychiatric conditions (93, 94).

Mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress can trigger mtDNA release, potentially leading to inflammation (95). This process can result in persistent immune system activation, characterized by the recruitment of circulating immune cells and the production of proinflammatory cytokines. In turn, this sustained immune activation can cause further mtDNA damage, resulting in a vicious cycle of immune activation and additional mitochondrial dysfunction (96). Our findings suggest that a higher percentage of deletions could influence the innate immune response via toll-like receptors, particularly associated with the cytokines IL-5 and IL-6. A previous study showed that mitochondrial complex I serves as a critical hub for ROS production in microglia and plays a crucial role in sustaining neuroinflammation (97). Based on this, we hypothesize that elevated mitochondrial genomic instability, reflected by increased mtDNA deletion levels, may lead to deficiencies in complex I activity in individuals with LLD, potentially contributing to long-term dampening of inflammatory responses. This may reflect adaptive or pathological changes in immune-metabolic regulation.

Our study has several important limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may restrict the generalizability of our results to broader populations of individuals with LLD. Additionally, the small number of individuals in the longitudinal analysis limits the breadth of our analysis of the changes in the ccf-mtDNA and deletion rates in patients with LLD over time. We also did not have a direct measure of mitochondrial function in this sample. Furthermore, because measurements were made in plasma, it is likely that the DNA originated from peripheral cells rather than the central nervous system. Another limitation is that the estimated deletion rate metric (MT-ND2/MT-ND4 ratio) was not benchmarked against gold-standard methods such as digital droplet PCR in this or other cohorts. While previous work from our group using exosomal mtDNA supports the relevance of this metric, future studies with direct validation are warranted. Although we appropriately excluded participants who received antidepressants or immunomodulators within 3 months of baseline assessment, potential residual confounding from prior treatments, lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, physical activity), and sleep quality were not addressed. Despite these limitations, the robust characterization of the LLD group and comparison individuals and the fact that all participants were not receiving antidepressant treatment during blood collection are major strengths of our study.

Conclusions

Our study sheds light on the potential significance of ccf-mtDNA levels and deletions in individuals with LLD. We identified a notable association between elevated ccf-mtDNA levels and deletion, indicative of mitochondrial damage, as well as poorer clinical outcomes and increased inflammation in individuals with LLD. Future research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms that drive these specific changes in levels and deletions. Additionally, exploring ccf-mtDNA methylation patterns, fragmentation profiles, and topological properties in patients with LLD could provide valuable insights (98). These investigations may ultimately inform the development of personalized therapeutic interventions and deepen our understanding of the complex heterogeneous nature of LLD (99).
