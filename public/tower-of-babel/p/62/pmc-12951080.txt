EpGAT: integrating epigenetics and 3D genome structure to predict alternative splicing and polyadenylation
Authors: Sudipto Baul, Naima Ahmed Fahmi, Guangyu Wang, Hao Zheng, Ahmed Louri, Jeongsik Yong, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how the 3D structure of the genome influences gene regulation is a growing area of interest, particularly in the context of alternative post-transcriptional regulatory events such as alternative splicing (AS) and alternative polyadenylation (APA). These processes are essential for generating transcript and protein diversity, and they are tightly coordinated with transcription. However, despite their biological importance, the relationship between chromatin interactions and alternative pre-messenger RNA regulation remains poorly understood. This gap largely stems from a lack of computational tools capable of integrating structural genomic data with RNA processing dynamics. Exploring how chromatin interactions and epigenetic landscapes shape these events is essential for uncovering the multilayered regulation of gene expression. To bridge this gap, we present EpGAT, a graph attention network-based model that integrates epigenetic read coverage and chromatin interaction data to predict and quantify AS and APA events. By explicitly modeling the spatial organization of the genome, EpGAT captures the regulatory influence of chromatin looping and long-range genomic interactions on RNA processing. The model’s predictions are validated through rigorous cross-cell line and cross-chromosome evaluations, affirming its generalizability and reliability. Beyond prediction, EpGAT offers interpretability by tracing learned parameters back to genomic features, enabling the identification of active enhancers, mapping promoter–enhancer connectivity, and pinpointing the epigenetic factors most critical to specific RNA processing events. These capabilities make EpGAT a powerful tool for dissecting the complex interplay between genome architecture and transcriptomic regulation. More broadly, it provides a generalizable framework for multiple tasks to study the link between 3D genome organization, epigenetic signals, and RNA processing.Keywords: alternative splicing, alternative polyadenylation, epigenetics, chromatin interaction, promoter–enhancer interaction, graph attention network, attention coefficients

Introduction

Gene regulation is determined not only by the linear sequence of DNA but also by the folding and organization of the genome within the 3D space of the nucleus [1]. This spatial arrangement enables distant regulatory elements, such as enhancers and promoters, to physically interact with the genes they regulate, even when separated by large genomic distances [2]. Such long-range interactions are fundamental for controlling where within the 3D nucleus and at which stage of the cell cycle genes are expressed. The 3D genome architecture operates in concert with epigenetic modifications—chemical marks on DNA or histone proteins that influence whether a gene is active or repressed [3]. Together, spatial genome organization and epigenetic landscapes establish a tightly coordinated regulatory framework that governs gene expression, particularly through the processing of RNA transcripts. These transcript processing steps can vary, giving rise to alternative splicing (AS) and alternative polyadenylation (APA)—mechanisms that enable a single gene to produce multiple messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein isoforms [4–6]. Such events greatly expand the functional capacity of the genome. For example, APA occurring within an intron can produce truncated mRNA transcripts that give rise to distinct protein isoforms, potentially bypassing regulatory domains and altering cellular behavior [7–11]. Similarly, AS within coding regions can alter functional domains of proteins, thereby influencing nearly all aspects of protein function [12]. It is estimated that >95% of multi-exon pre-mRNAs in humans undergo AS, generating over 250 000 protein isoforms from 20 000 genes [12–14]. Thus, these regulatory events allow cells to generate diverse protein isoforms from a limited number of genes, expanding the genome’s functional repertoire and supporting its multifaceted roles in biology.

Technological advances such as Hi-C [15] and ChIP-seq [16] have enabled genome-wide mapping of chromatin interactions and regulatory protein binding sites, respectively, providing new insights into how genome structure influences transcriptional regulation. Large-scale consortium efforts [17–20] have further generated extensive datasets, including Hi-C maps and chromatin profiles across diverse histone marks and transcription factors. However, how this 3D regulatory landscape shapes downstream processes, particularly post-transcriptional regulation, remains largely unexplored. Despite their central roles in gene regulation, chromatin interactions and epigenetic features have not been systematically investigated in the context of AS and APA. While individual studies have reported specific cases where epigenetic modifications affect alternative post-transcriptional regulatory events [21–29], a broader, integrative understanding is still lacking. Elucidating these complex regulatory relationships would provide critical insights into how gene regulation contributes to transcriptome diversity.

Graph-based learning models [30–32] have emerged as powerful tools for analyzing and visualizing the complex architecture of chromatin interactions captured by Hi-C data [33–37]. In these models, genomic regions, typically binned at resolutions such as 5 kilobases (kb; 1kb = 1000 base pairs) or 10kb, are represented as nodes, while edges denote statistically significant contact frequencies between loci, thereby encoding the 3D conformation of the genome as a graph structure. These approaches have been instrumental in uncovering topological features of genome organization and, in some cases, have been extended to integrate epigenetic information for predicting gene expression levels [38]. However, none of these models were designed to learn the impact of 3D genome organization or epigenetic signals on alternative post-transcriptional regulation. Although post-transcriptional regulatory events can be detected using large-scale RNA-seq data, their relationship to upstream 3D genome features (e.g. enhancer–promoter interactions) and epigenetic markers (e.g. histone modifications and transcription factors) remains poorly studied. Consequently, the regulatory logic linking spatial genome features and chromatin context to transcript isoform diversity is still not well understood. To address this gap, there is a pressing need for integrative frameworks that combine chromatin conformation, epigenetic landscapes, and RNA processing data to explicitly model how the 3D genome and its regulatory elements influence alternative post-transcriptional events.

Therefore, we propose a graph attention-based model, EpGAT, which uses epigenomic profiles as input and quantified post-transcriptional regulatory events as output to learn key chromatin features and epigenetic signatures for predicting AS or APA events. In addition, we investigate the crosstalk between 3D genome organization and alternative post-transcriptional regulation by extracting and analyzing the learned weight parameters of the model, tracing predictions back to identify enhancer positions and epigenetic features with significant impact. The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows. In Section Methodology, we describe the model architecture, data preprocessing steps, and training procedure. Section Predicting post-transcriptional regulatory events evaluates the predictive performance of EpGAT, while Section Estimation of enhancer–promoter interactions and Section Estimation of important epigenetic features detail how enhancer locations and critical epigenetic signatures are identified. We then assess the contribution of each model component and input modality to predictive performance through an ablation study in Section Ablation Study. Finally, Section Discussion examines the broader applicability of EpGAT, including its performance under data-scarce conditions, transferability across species, considerations in epigenetic feature selection, and the overall robustness of the model.

Methodology

Chromatin interactions, measurable through Hi-C profiling technologies, together with epigenomic features such as histone modifications and transcription factor binding, provide critical insights into the regulatory landscape. In contrast, true AS and APA events can be experimentally validated using short-read RNA-seq data. The graph attention network (GAT)-based framework (EpGAT) is designed to predict and quantify these AS and APA events by integrating chromatin interaction data with epigenomic profiles. EpGAT employs three modules for this purpose: a Pooling module, an Attention module, and a Linear module. Its primary objective is to trace predictions back to their biological significance by linking them to epigenetic features and chromatin interactions. During prediction, EpGAT learns attention weight matrices that quantify the importance of chromatin interactions between genomic regions. These attention scores can then be analyzed to identify the epigenetic features most relevant to particular AS or APA events. Furthermore, when mapped to chromosomal coordinates, the attention scores may reveal the positions of promoters and enhancers associated with specific genes. This capability enables EpGAT not only to predict and quantify post-transcriptional regulatory events, but also to provide mechanistic insights into how chromatin architecture, epigenetic modifications, and gene regulatory elements influence these processes.

In the following sections, we first describe how data from different domains are integrated into a unified context, and then present the details of the EpGAT model architecture and training procedure.

Data preparation

Three types of data are required to train the EpGAT model: epigenetic data (e.g. ChIP-seq), Hi-C data, and quantified AS or APA events derived from RNA-seq. To ensure compatibility with the EpGAT framework, all data types are divided into genomic bins along the chromosomal length at a resolution of 200 base pairs (). For convenient model application, each chromosome is partitioned into regions of 6 million base pairs (), with each region serving as a distinct sample. For example, Chromosome 1, which spans , is divided into samples. Within a region, there are bins of each, where every bin corresponds to a node in the chromatin interaction network constructed from Hi-C data.

Because the three datasets are typically available at different resolutions and in diverse formats, preprocessing is necessary to unify them into a consistent format and resolution. The preprocessing steps, described below, ensure the seamless integration of epigenetic signals, chromatin interactions, and transcriptomic data into a coherent framework, thereby enabling their effective use within the EpGAT model.

ChIP-seq data (epigenomic profiles)

ChIP-seq data, which capture histone modifications and transcription factor binding, are essential for defining the epigenomic profiles of genomic bins in the EpGAT model. These data provide critical insights into the regulatory landscape by recording specific epigenetic signals across the genome. Normalized read coverage from transcription factor ChIP-seq and histone ChIP-seq experiments, comprising 32 distinct epigenetic signals (listed in the Supplementary Document), were obtained from the ENCODE project [17]. The processed datasets, available for multiple cell lines, are used to assign input features to each genomic bin, as illustrated in the right panel of Fig. 1. The data are provided in bigWig file format, where the signal intensity is expressed as a P-value representing the statistical significance of enrichment at a given genomic location compared with a control, thereby enabling precise identification of regions with significant epigenetic activity. To preserve the fine details of read coverage, the data are first divided into bins of resolution, followed by log normalization of the P-values within each bin to standardize the input. This high-resolution binning retains the distinct properties of the sequencing data while allowing subsequent aggregation into bins in the Pooling module of EpGAT. Finally, the processed values from all 32 epigenetic signals are concatenated to form a 32D feature vector for each bin.

RNA-seq data (post-transcriptional regulatory events)

Splicing and polyadenylation events were identified and quantified using our custom pipelines, AS-Quant [39] and APA-Scan [40], applied to RNA-seq cell line data. Splicing events were quantified using Percent Spliced-In (PSI), which measures the proportion of reads supporting an intron inclusion event relative to the total reads in the corresponding intron group (Fig. 2A). In this study, we focus specifically on exon-skipping splicing events, as they are more straightforward to quantify and the most frequently occurring AS type. Similarly, polyadenylation events were quantified using Truncation Ratio (TR), which represents the expression of a specific APA isoform as a fraction of the total transcript abundance from a gene (Fig. 2B). EpGAT predicts and quantifies splicing and polyadenylation events for genes located within each genomic bin. It is important to note that individual events may span multiple bins. In such cases, all bins overlapping a given event are assigned the same PSI or TR values.

Hi-C data (chromatin interactions)

The Hi-C chromatin conformation capture data, shown in the left panel of Fig. 1, were downloaded from the ENCODE project [17] after filtering out low-quality reads (detailed data sources are provided in the Supplementary document). These data are provided as contact matrices, where each entry represents the number of observed contacts between two loci partitioned from the linear genome. For this study, we extracted the processed contact matrices at a resolution of , meaning that each value in the Hi-C matrix corresponds to the interaction between two genomic bins of length on the respective row and column. This resolution was chosen because the length of many genes is smaller than or close to , and using a larger bin size would mix multiple genes within the same bin. To properly distinguish these genes in our experiments, a higher resolution is needed. However, using a bin size smaller than may result in overly sparse matrices with limited predictive power. From the Hi-C contact matrices, a weighted adjacency matrix is generated separately for each genomic sample. In this step, only neighbors corresponding to bins with available prediction data (i.e. post-transcriptional regulation events) are retained, resulting in an asymmetric adjacency matrix. Note that, while the bins void of any AS or APA event were removed from one axis (rows) of the adjacency matrix, all the bins remained present in the other axis (columns) providing necessary long range interactions needed for the prediction. EpGAT model architecture

The proposed model extracts biological signatures embedded in epigenetic and Hi-C data to predict and quantify AS or APA events through three key modules (Fig. 1). The Pooling module is designed to extract and quantify the most informative signals from epigenetic data along the chromosome. The Attention module then integrates the processed epigenetic features from the Pooling module with the structural information provided by Hi-C data in the form of an adjacency matrix. This module also learns attention coefficients, which are essential not only for accurate event prediction but also for uncovering the intricate relationships between distinct biological phenomena. Finally, the Linear module transforms the integrated representation into predicted AS (PSI) or APA (TR) values. Each module is described in detail in the following sections.

The purpose of the Pooling module is to extract and aggregate the biological context captured from high-resolution genomic bins of the epigenetic data. Attention module

The Attention module, shown in the middle panel of Fig. 1, receives the output of the Pooling module and integrates it with structural information derived from the Hi-C adjacency matrix. By applying a GAT layer [30], this module computes the attention that each target node receives from its neighboring nodes. Specifically, it learns weight matrices that produce the embedding of a target node by integrating both its own features and those of its neighbors, as provided by the Pooling module. Objective function

The AS PSI and APA TR ground truth values often fluctuate sharply between their maxima and minima across adjacent genomic bins. Traditional loss functions, such as mean squared error (MSE), tend to oversmooth these variations by biasing predictions toward the mean. Backtracking the results

After training EpGAT on epigenetic and Hi-C data, we sought to interpret the biological meaning and significance of the results, focusing on enhancer–promoter connectivity and the identification of key features (genes) involved in the process. To this end, attention scores were extracted from the trained EpGAT model. Each head of the Attention module records the attention assigned to a target node from its neighbors using an attention matrix, , constructed by concatenating the neighbor attention coefficients of all target nodes, i.e. . It is important to note that a single gene may span multiple target nodes. To obtain a global representation, an overall attention matrix is then computed by taking the maximum value at each position across all attention heads.

We hypothesized that locations with large neighbor attention coefficients corresponding to the target nodes covered by a given gene may coincide with enhancer positions. To test this, we plotted the neighbor attention coefficients from the attention matrix, which revealed putative enhancer locations on the chromosome. These results were validated by comparison with enhancer predictions from the widely used Activity-by-Contact (ABC) model [43]. Considering the transcription start site of each gene as its promoter region further allowed us to investigate the corresponding enhancer–promoter connectivity.

To identify the most relevant epigenetic features for a given event, the attention coefficients of neighboring nodes corresponding to the target bins covered by the gene are aggregated into an attention vector. This vector is then normalized by dividing by the number of covered bins. From the normalized vector, the five neighboring nodes with the highest attention scores are selected, as they are expected to exert the greatest influence on the predicted event. The epigenetic features with higher values in these top neighbors are highlighted, and the feature supported by the largest proportion of neighboring nodes is considered to have the most impact on the prediction results for that gene.

Baseline model

To benchmark the performance of the EpGAT model across various experimental setups, two state-of-the-art baseline models were leveraged. First, a recurrent neural network (RNN)-based model named ESPRNN [44], consisting of three RNN layers and a linear layer, was applied to the epigenetic bins. Although this model was originally developed for binary prediction of splicing event occurrence from epigenetic signals, it was adapted and modified for the regression task of predicting quantified AS and APA events. Since a recurrent layer operates over time steps, each bin of size was treated as a single time step, and four time steps were combined to generate an embedding at the resolution. This ensures the same epigenetic input resolution for ESPRNN as for EpGAT.

Next, due to the scarcity of computational models for predicting quantified post-transcriptional events from epigenetic data, another baseline model was constructed based on a traditional convolutional neural network (CNN), which has been widely applied and shown to excel in image prediction tasks. In this context, epigenetic data can be treated as a 1D image, making 1D convolutional blocks suitable for extracting informative features relevant to post-transcriptional regulatory event prediction. This baseline model, referred to as CNN, consists of three 1D convolutional layers followed by a linear layer. Unlike EpGAT, this model does not include pooling layers to reconcile resolutions. Therefore, the input to the CNN baseline is directly partitioned into bins of size , rather than first processing bins and then mapping them to resolution.

Experiments

To evaluate the performance of EpGAT in predicting post-transcriptional regulatory events and its ability to capture complex biological signatures from epigenetic signals, we conducted a series of comprehensive experiments. First, we assessed its predictive power by comparing the true and predicted values of AS PSI and APA TR. Next, we extracted biological insights from the trained model to reveal meaningful relationships between the predicted outputs and the input data. Finally, we performed an ablation study to examine the contribution of each component of the proposed model, input features, as well as the impact of the objective function.

Predicting post-transcriptional regulatory events

To verify the credibility of the predicted AS and APA values, we designed three comprehensive experiments. In the first, referred to as Cross cell line, the model was trained and validated on a specific chromosome from the MCF7 cell line and then tested on the corresponding chromosome from the K562 cell line. In the second, termed Cross chromosome, the model was trained on 10 chromosomes, validated on two chromosomes, and tested on two additional chromosomes within the same cell line. Finally, in the third setting, termed Cross cell line and cross chromosome, the model was trained and validated on 10 and two chromosomes of the MCF7 cell line, respectively, and tested on two different chromosomes of the K562 cell line. We compared EpGAT with ESPRNN and CNN baselines by computing the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) between the true and predicted AS PSI and APA TR values for the three models, showcased using box plots in Figs 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7. Each value on a box plot corresponds to the correlation coefficient for a single genomic window of length . PSI and TR both being continuous variables, makes PCC well-suited for this task since it quantifies linear relationships between predicted and true values. It focuses on co-variation rather than exact magnitudes, rewarding models that capture correct trends even if the absolute scales differ. This makes it biologically meaningful, as it reflects consistent directional regulation across genes or samples rather than exact numeric matches.

Cross cell line

In this experiment, for each chromosome, EpGAT was trained and validated on MCF7 cell line data and then tested on K562 for predicting post-transcriptional regulatory events. The box plots in Figs 3 and 4 demonstrate that EpGAT consistently outperforms the baselines for both AS and APA prediction. Each plot shows results across all chromosomes, along with the mean correlation value at the end. The low -value from the ANOVA test between the models’ correlation results further highlights the significant performance improvement achieved by EpGAT. Supplementary Figure S1 provides additional support, showing AS prediction results in more detail for four representative windows from four different chromosomes, where the plots compare the true AS PSI values against the EpGAT-predicted values.

Although EpGAT and the baseline models rely on epigenetic data, EpGAT is uniquely capable of incorporating chromatin interaction information through Hi-C data. This integration enables EpGAT to achieve superior performance across both tasks. Specifically, the Pooling module captures key features by summarizing epigenetic profiles through the MaxPool and AveragePool layers. Meanwhile, the attention mechanism in the GAT layer extracts neighborhood information from high-resolution Hi-C data across genomic windows, assigning attention weights to neighbors based on their relevance to computing AS or APA values for a target node. By jointly leveraging epigenetic signals and chromatin interaction patterns, EpGAT exhibits strong predictive ability. Notably, the correlation for AS events is slightly higher than that for APA events, as shown in the box plots. This difference may be attributed to the greater complexity of APA, which involves regulation at the 3’ end of mRNA that can be shared across multiple transcripts from the same gene, whereas AS primarily reflects the cassette type exons.

Cross chromosome

Building on the compelling results from the cross cell line experiments, we extended the evaluation of EpGAT to cross-chromosome cases. To further strengthen the reliability of the reported results, we conducted this experiment on two cell lines (MCF7 and K562) for AS and APA events separately. For each cell line, EpGAT and the baselines were trained on 10 chromosomes (1–5, 8–12), validated on two chromosomes (6, 13), and tested on two chromosomes (7, 14). Figures 5 and 6 present box plots showing the correlation coefficients between true and predicted AS PSI and APA TR values, respectively, for the test chromosomes. EpGAT consistently outperforms the baselines with a clear margin in this setup as well.

To further assess the impact of chromosome selection, we evaluated EpGAT’s performance using four different randomly selected chromosome sets, which are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The results shown in Supplementary Figure S2 demonstrate that EpGAT’s overall prediction accuracy remains consistent across these experimental setups, indicating the robustness of the model and reaffirming its applicability across diverse scenarios. For reproducibility, the training, validation, and testing chromosome sets are also provided as configurable hyperparameters in the publicly available GitHub code.

Cross cell line and cross chromosome

Motivated by the strong performance of EpGAT in the preceding experimental settings, we further evaluated the model under a more stringent cross cell line, cross-chromosome scenario. The models were trained and validated on Chromosomes 1–6 and 8–13 of the MCF7 cell line and tested on Chromosomes 7,14 of the K562 cell line. Figure 7 presents box plots illustrating the correlation between true and predicted AS PSI and APA TR values for all three models on the held-out test chromosomes. Under this challenging evaluation setting, EpGAT consistently outperforms the baseline models, demonstrating its strong generalization capability across both cell lines and chromosomal contexts. These findings further substantiate the superior predictive performance of EpGAT and provide a foundation for subsequent analyses aimed at elucidating the biological relevance of the learned representations.

Estimation of enhancer–promoter interaction

Promoters and enhancers play crucial roles in gene regulation. Promoters serve as binding sites for transcription initiation, whereas enhancers, often located far from the promoter, regulate the likelihood of transcription occurring. Despite decades of research, accurately identifying the location and function of enhancers remains a challenging problem [45, 46]. In this work, we explored whether the learned weight parameters of EpGAT model could estimate enhancer positions for genes. By associating predicted enhancer locations with transcription start sites representing promoter positions, we sought to infer potential enhancer–promoter interactions.

As shown in Fig. 8, four genes located on different chromosomes were used to demonstrate the ability of EpGAT’s learned parameters to suggest enhancer positions. All four genes are associated with various cancer types, as listed in Table 1. The figure displays the gene transcripts obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser [53]. Notably, in most cases, the candidate enhancer regions identified by EpGAT closely align with those predicted by the widely used ABC model [43]. For instance, in the case of MATR3, both models point to enhancer sites coinciding with the start of two transcripts. Interestingly, for ARID4A, EpGAT suggests an additional candidate enhancer near the start of a transcript that the ABC model does not detect. Furthermore, for CALM2 and MATR3, EpGAT highlights upstream regions not predicted by ABC, which may represent promising candidates for further biological investigation. Taken together, these results suggest that EpGAT has the potential to uncover enhancer–promoter interactions, thereby providing biological insights that may help explain its superior predictive performance.

Histone H3K36me3, mediated by the SETD2 methyltransferase [54, 55], regulates AS events and influences nonsense-mediated mRNA decay of the BRCA1-associated RING domain protein 1 (BARD1) [56, 57]. Reduced levels of H3K36me3 also result in alternative exon usage during RNA splicing in TP53 [58]. Furthermore, PALB2 associates with actively transcribed genes through MRG15, which recognizes H3K36me3 [61, 62]. Similarly, RUNX1 and NPM1 are implicated in acute myeloid leukemia [75, 76]. H3K27me3 has been shown to transcriptionally repress RUNX1 [63, 64], whereas NPM1 has been identified as a direct target of H3K36me3 [65].

EpGAT was trained on Chromosome 1 of MCF7 cell line and tested on the same chromosome of K562 cell line for all the experiments in this ablation study. In this setting, the degree centrality of each node in the adjacency matrix derived from Hi-C data is used as the sole node feature. Similarly, EpGAT-pool and EpGAT-GAT denote variants of EpGAT without the Pooling module and Attention module, respectively. The Pooling module aggregates and concatenates features from bins to generate representations at a lower resolution of . When this module is removed, bins of size are directly provided as input to the GAT layer. Furthermore, removing the Attention module effectively excludes Hi-C information from the model, as chromatin interaction data are incorporated exclusively through this module. In this case, the pooled epigenetic features are directly passed to the Linear module for AS or APA prediction.

As demonstrated in Fig. 9, the prediction accuracy of the original EpGAT framework coupled with the customized objective function surpasses the other four variants by a substantial margin. The objective function formulated in Equation (6) clearly shows advantage over the traditional MSE loss. While the epigenetic signals and the Pooling module demonstrate more impact on EpGAT’s performance, the attention mechanism unequivocally has the most significant influence on the prediction because it integrates the pooled features with the network information from Hi-C data along with assigning attention weights properly. To further examine the contribution of individual loss components, we extended the ablation study by training EpGAT using each loss term separately. The corresponding results, shown in Supplementary Figure S3, indicate that jointly optimizing all loss components yields the highest prediction accuracy.

Discussion

Epigenetic features such as histone modifications and transcription factors play a vital role in gene regulation impacting post-transcriptional events such as AS, APA. On the other hand, Hi-C data provide genome-wide maps of chromatin conformation capture at various resolutions, capturing the 3D organization of the genome. By integrating these two complementary layers of information, our proposed model, EpGAT, leverages both the molecular signatures encoded in epigenetic signals and the structural context derived from Hi-C data to more effectively predict regulatory events. This integrative design highlights the importance of jointly considering biochemical and spatial dimensions of genome regulation when modeling complex transcriptomic processes.

Moreover, as demonstrated in the Estimation of enhancer–promoter interaction Section, the learned weight parameters of the EpGAT framework can be traced back to reveal putative connections between enhancers and promoters. These findings have the potential to narrow the search space for costly wet-lab experiments, thereby reducing experimental burden. While highly promising, these results are derived purely from computational analyses and require future experimental validation, such as CRISPR-based perturbation or reporter assays, for definitive confirmation.

Although our evaluation focuses on high-quality ENCODE datasets from the MCF-7 and K562 cell lines, the broader applicability of the model extends to scenarios in which data may be limited or noisier. In settings such as rare cell types, non-model organisms, or patient samples with lower sequencing depth, the framework can leverage transfer-learning strategies, where pretrained representations from large public datasets are fine-tuned on smaller, task-specific collections. In addition, several publicly available low-coverage datasets have been shown to yield biologically meaningful peak calls [77–80], indicating that informative regulatory signals can still be recovered under suboptimal sequencing conditions. Because our model learns from these same processed representations, it can similarly extract useful patterns from data that are shallow, heterogeneous, or generated in less-controlled environments. With respect to cross-species applications, the model architecture is largely species-agnostic and can be retrained or adapted given a reference genome and minimal annotation; for more distantly related organisms, additional fine-tuning may be required to capture species-specific regulatory characteristics. Collectively, these considerations outline both the limitations and feasible pathways for deploying EpGAT in data-sparse, low-quality, or cross-species contexts.

In designing the input feature set, we selected 32 epigenetic signals that capture major histone modifications and transcription-associated chromatin states with well-established regulatory roles. These features were chosen because they are (i) consistently available for both MCF-7 and K562 cell lines in the ENCODE repository and (ii) among the most deeply sequenced and comprehensively annotated datasets for these cell lines, providing a robust foundation for model training. Although chromatin accessibility assays such as ATAC-seq and DNase-seq provide valuable insights into open chromatin, they measure accessibility rather than modification-specific chromatin states, and accessibility peaks often do not overlap with histone mark ChIP-seq peaks. Moreover, such accessibility datasets were not uniformly available across both cell lines at the time of data collection, and incorporating uneven modalities could bias model training. Nevertheless, the EpGAT framework is flexible and can readily incorporate accessibility assays or additional epigenetic layers when they are consistently profiled across conditions of interest. Accordingly, the number of epigenetic signals is treated as a configurable hyperparameter in the released software.

All experiments were implemented using Pytorch [41] and executed on a server with an AMD Ryzen 9 processor, 124Gigabyes RAM, and an Nvidia RTX A4500 GPU (CUDA 11.3), running Ubuntu operating system. The EpGAT model is very robust with only 401k parameters and compact model size of 1.6MB. The model achieves an inference time of 20–30 s, making it practical for use on local machines. This efficiency allows researchers to rapidly prototype and test hypotheses, thereby accelerating discovery before scaling up to more computationally demanding analyses of complex regulatory mechanisms.

Conclusion

Although alternative post-transcriptional regulatory events play critical role in diversifying transcriptome and proteome, a comprehensive framework to connect the genome organization and epigenetic signals to those events is largely missing. Therefore in this study, we proposed a graph attention-based model to predict and quantify AS and APA events by analyzing epigenetic features and chromatin interactions. The model’s performance was evaluated through extensive experiments on different cell lines and chromosomes. The learned weight parameters of the model were further explored to reveal location of associated enhancers and establish the impact of specific epigenetic signatures behind a particular event. These results can be further utilized to narrow down the search area for biological investigations before conducting costly and time-consuming wet-lab experiments. Moreover, the broad applicability of EpGAT makes it a suitable framework for in-depth analyses of the relationship between 3D genome organization, epigenetic regulation, and post-transcriptional events.
