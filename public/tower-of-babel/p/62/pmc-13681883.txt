Bladder cancer in Africa, 2010–2023: burden, geographic heterogeneity, demographic drivers, pandemic-era temporal changes, and projections to 2030
Authors: Yongli Jin, Yan Jin, Yan Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo characterize the burden, temporal trends, geographic heterogeneity, demographic components, pandemic-era deviations, and projected age-standardized rates of bladder cancer across Africa.MethodsWe conducted an ecological secondary analysis of modeled Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 estimates for the GBD aggregate labeled “African Union” and 54 African country locations from 2010 to 2023. Incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) were assessed as counts and age-standardized rates (ASRs) per 100,000 population for both sexes combined. Trends were summarized using estimated annual percentage changes. Incidence trajectories were descriptively grouped using dynamic time warping, and changes in counts were partitioned into population-size, age-structure, and age-specific rate components using Das Gupta decomposition. Exploratory autoregressive integrated moving average models compared GBD-estimated rates in 2020–2023 with trajectories extrapolated from 2010 to 2019. Country-specific ASRs were projected through 2030 using log-linear models.ResultsFor the GBD aggregate labeled “African Union,” incident cases increased from 16,357 in 2010 to 31,503 in 2023, prevalent cases from 73,806 to 147,599, deaths from 9,261 to 16,467, and DALYs from 232,414 to 415,746. Corresponding ASRs increased from 3.5 to 4.4 for incidence, 13.9 to 18.3 for prevalence, 2.2 to 2.6 for deaths, and 48.1 to 56.1 for DALYs. The highest estimated incidence ASRs in 2023 occurred in Tunisia, Malawi, Libya, Egypt, and Mali, while several countries with low estimated baseline rates had rapid relative increases. Population-size change was the largest decomposition component, accounting for 39.8%–58.8% of the increase across outcomes, with additional contributions from age structure and age-specific rates. GBD-estimated incidence, mortality, and DALY rates during 2020–2023 were above the extrapolated point forecasts; however, this exploratory analysis did not establish a causal pandemic effect. The primary projection models yielded higher fitted 2030 ASRs in a majority of countries, although results varied across training windows.ConclusionGBD 2023 estimates indicate an increasing but geographically heterogeneous bladder cancer burden across Africa. Population growth was the largest contributor to increasing counts, while age-structure and age-specific rate changes also contributed. Regionally tailored prevention, diagnostic and treatment capacity, and improved cancer surveillance are needed. Pandemic-era deviations and 2030 projections should be interpreted as model-dependent rather than causal or definitive forecasts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13027-026-00782-3.

Introduction

Bladder cancer remains a substantial global health problem. GLOBOCAN 2024 estimated 635,264 new cases and 227,626 deaths worldwide, ranking bladder cancer eighth in cancer incidence and thirteenth in cancer mortality [1]. The burden is strongly patterned by sex and age, occurring predominantly in men and older adults, and varies markedly across regions [1; 2]. Recent analyses of population-based cancer-registry and vital-statistics data indicate that incidence has stabilized or declined in many high-income populations but remains high or is increasing in some settings; elevated rates have also been reported in parts of Northern Africa [2; 3]. These geographic and temporal differences probably reflect the combined influence of population aging, tobacco exposure, occupational carcinogens, infection-related disease, diagnostic activity, and variation in cancer-registration practices [3; 4].

Risk-factor distributions are heterogeneous across Africa. Tobacco smoking is the leading established modifiable risk factor for bladder cancer, while occupational exposure to aromatic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons represents another important external risk [4]. A GBD-based analysis estimated that, in 2019, the proportions of people aged 15 years or older who smoked were 18.1% in North Africa, 17.8% in Southern Africa, 10.5% in Central Africa, 8.8% in Eastern Africa, and 7.0% in Western Africa. Although these model-based proportions declined across all five subregions between 1990 and 2019, the estimated number of smokers in Africa increased from approximately 50 million to 90 million because of population growth [5]. Occupationally relevant exposures may occur in dye, rubber, painting, printing, and other industries involving aromatic amines or combustion-derived compounds [4]. Evidence specific to African settings remains limited; for example, a multicentre case–control study in Egypt found an association between agricultural work and bladder cancer among men, but agricultural work represented a composite exposure category rather than a specific identified carcinogen [6]. Consequently, occupational patterns across other African subregions remain insufficiently characterized.

Urogenital schistosomiasis constitutes a distinct, geographically focal pathway to bladder carcinogenesis. A GBD 2021 analysis estimated that the age-standardized prevalence of schistosomiasis from all species in Africa was 9,461.8 per 100,000 population in 2021 and identified a particularly high burden in West Africa [7]. However, this all-species estimate should not be equated directly with exposure to Schistosoma haematobium or with bladder cancer risk. Chronic S. haematobium infection is considered carcinogenic and is associated particularly with squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder [8]. Historical case–control evidence from Alexandria, Egypt, associated previous urinary schistosomiasis with increased bladder cancer risk after adjustment for smoking and occupational factors [8]. In a more recent hospital-based pathology series of 481 patients from Tanzania’s Lake Zone, squamous cell carcinoma accounted for 57.0% of bladder cancers, and S. haematobium eggs were detected in 25.2% of specimens and were associated with squamous histology [9]. These studies illustrate the continuing relevance of schistosomiasis in selected endemic freshwater communities but should not be extrapolated to contemporary national incidence or to all countries within a subregion.

Comparable epidemiological evidence across Africa nevertheless remains incomplete. An Africa-wide systematic review identified only 22 eligible incidence studies from 15 countries and reported very high between-study heterogeneity, underscoring the limited representativeness of available empirical data [10]. Cancer-surveillance capacity also varies substantially across sub-Saharan Africa and the Middle East and North Africa, with many settings lacking recent, nationally representative population-based cancer registration or complete vital-registration data [11; 12]. Low reported or modeled rates may therefore reflect genuinely lower disease occurrence, incomplete diagnosis or registration, greater reliance on modeled relationships, or a combination of these factors. The COVID-19 pandemic introduced an additional interpretive challenge. A systematic review documented interruptions and delays in cancer screening, diagnosis, and treatment in sub-Saharan Africa [13]; however, GBD 2023 cancer estimates did not comprehensively incorporate pandemic-related effects because sufficiently robust data were unavailable across locations and outcomes [14]. Analyses of 2020–2023 patterns should therefore be regarded as exploratory assessments of pandemic-era temporal deviations rather than causal estimates of COVID-19 effects.

To address these gaps, we analyzed modeled estimates from GBD 2023 for Africa overall and 54 African country locations from 2010 to 2023, with projections to 2030. We assessed counts and age-standardized rates of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life-years; characterized geographic and temporal heterogeneity; identified country trajectory groups using dynamic time warping–based clustering; and decomposed changes in counts into contributions from population growth, population aging, and changes in age-specific outcome rates. We also explored whether GBD-estimated rates during 2020–2023 deviated from trajectories extrapolated from 2010 - 2019, without attributing such deviations causally to the COVID-19 pandemic, and projected age-standardized rates to 2030 under alternative modeling windows. Because the analysis did not include country-level measurements of smoking, schistosomiasis, occupational exposure, or health-system capacity, these factors are considered contextual explanations rather than causal mechanisms tested in the present study.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted an ecological secondary analysis of modeled population-level estimates from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study 2023 (GBD 2023) to characterize bladder cancer burden across Africa. Annual estimates from 2010 through 2023 were analyzed, and study-specific projections were generated through 2030. The endpoint of 2023 was selected because it was the latest year available in GBD 2023. The starting year of 2010 was chosen to focus on contemporary burden patterns while providing 10 annual prepandemic estimates (2010–2019) and four pandemic-era estimates (2020–2023) for the exploratory time-series analysis.

We extracted estimates for bladder cancer incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), including all-age counts and age-standardized rates (ASRs) per 100,000 population for both sexes combined. Corresponding GBD population estimates were used in the decomposition analysis. The continent-level analysis used the aggregate location labeled “African Union” in the GBD dataset; this series was obtained directly from GBD and was not calculated by averaging country estimates.

GBD 2023 synthesizes population-based cancer-registry, vital-registration, and verbal-autopsy data using standardized statistical models. Cancer mortality is estimated using Cause of Death Ensemble modeling and mortality-to-incidence ratios, from which incidence is derived. Prevalence is modeled using incidence and survival, while DALYs combine years lived with disability and years of life lost [14]. Accordingly, the analyzed values are GBD-modeled estimates rather than directly reported national registry data

Geographic scope and outcomes

Country-level analyses included 54 prespecified African countries: Algeria, Angola, Benin, Botswana, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cabo Verde, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Comoros, Congo, Côte d’Ivoire, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Djibouti, Egypt, Equatorial Guinea, Eritrea, Eswatini, Ethiopia, Gabon, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Kenya, Lesotho, Liberia, Libya, Madagascar, Malawi, Mali, Mauritania, Mauritius, Morocco, Mozambique, Namibia, Niger, Nigeria, Rwanda, Sao Tome and Principe, Senegal, Seychelles, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Africa, South Sudan, Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Tunisia, Uganda, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. No separate country-level estimate was analyzed for Western Sahara/Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic.

For presentation, countries were grouped into North Africa and Central, Eastern, Southern, and Western sub-Saharan Africa. These groupings were used only to organize descriptive comparisons and were not explanatory variables in the statistical models. The study outcomes were incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs. For each outcome, we analyzed counts and ASRs, with GBD 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) reported for descriptive estimates. Downstream analyses used GBD mean point estimates; draw-level uncertainty was not propagated through the secondary models.

Descriptive and trend analyses

We summarized continent- and country-level burden in 2010 and 2023. Country-specific ASRs were displayed using choropleth maps. For each outcome and year separately, countries were grouped into up to five categories based on empirical quintiles of the ASR distribution. Because category boundaries were year-specific, the maps were interpreted as showing relative geographic patterns within each year rather than absolute temporal changes based on color categories alone.

Temporal trends were summarized using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). and calculated: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$EAPC = 100\left\{ {\exp \left( \beta \right) - 1} \right\}$$\end{document}

The 95% confidence interval (CI) was obtained by transforming the corresponding confidence limits for β [15]. A trend was classified as increasing when the lower CI limit was greater than zero, decreasing when the upper limit was less than zero, and showing no clear change when the CI included zero.

Trajectory clustering

Dynamic time warping (DTW)–based partitional clustering was used to identify descriptive groups of country-level incidence trajectories. Annual incidence ASRs from 2010 through 2023 were analyzed, and only countries with complete 14-year series were eligible. Clustering was performed on the original ASR scale without within-country standardization; therefore, the groups reflected both incidence level and trajectory shape. We used dtw_basic distance, DTW barycenter averaging, three prespecified clusters, and a random seed of 20,260,307. The three-cluster solution was treated as a descriptive summary rather than a statistically unique or etiologically defined classification. Groups were ordered by their mean 2023 ASR and labeled lower-, intermediate-, and higher-burden trajectory groups.

Decomposition analysis

A symmetric Das Gupta decomposition was used to partition changes in counts between 2010 and 2023 into contributions from total population size, population age structure, and age-specific rates. Age-specific population and outcome-rate files were matched by country, year, and harmonized age group. For country c and year t, the reconstructed count was: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_{c,t}} = \mathop \sum \limits_a {{{P_{c,t}}{A_{c,a,t}}{R_{c,a,t}}} \over {100000}}$$\end{document}

where P denotes total population, A the age-specific population proportion, and R the age-specific outcome rate. Contributions were calculated for all six possible replacement orders of the three components and averaged across orders. Country-level contributions were summed to obtain the 54-country continental summary. The age-specific rate-change component is a mathematical component and was not interpreted as a causal effect; it may reflect changes in exposure, detection, registration, treatment, survival, or other factors.\

Exploratory analysis of pandemic-era deviations

We conducted an exploratory, non-causal analysis of whether GBD-estimated ASRs during 2020–2023 deviated from trajectories extrapolated from 2010 to 2019. For each outcome, the 10 prepandemic ASRs for the GBD aggregate labeled “African Union” were log-transformed, and an autoregressive integrated moving average model was selected using auto.arima(), with stepwise search and approximation disabled. The model generated forecasts and pointwise 95% prediction intervals for 2020–2023, which were back-transformed by exponentiation. Residual autocorrelation was assessed using the Ljung–Box test at lag 9.

Annual deviations were calculated as the GBD-estimated ASR minus the forecasted ASR. Positive and negative deviations were summed separately across 2020–2023. These summaries have units of ASR-years per 100,000 and do not represent excess cases, deaths, or DALYs. Given the short training series and modeled input estimates, this analysis does not establish a causal effect of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Projections through 2030

Country-specific ASRs were projected using ordinary least-squares log-linear models: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ln\left( {ASRt} \right) = \alpha + \beta t + \varepsilon t.$$\end{document}

The primary model used 2010–2023 data; sensitivity models used 2010–2019 and 2010–2018. Parameter uncertainty was assessed using 2,000 coefficient vectors sampled from a multivariate normal distribution defined by the fitted coefficients and variance–covariance matrix. The random seed was 20,260,307. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the back-transformed simulated values defined coefficient-based 95% CIs.

Residual innovations and GBD draw-level uncertainty were not included; these intervals should therefore not be interpreted as prediction intervals or GBD UIs. The model-fitted 2023 ASR was used as the within-model reference for change through 2030. Projections were limited to ASRs and did not incorporate future population changes, risk-factor trajectories, diagnostic capacity, or interventions.

Statistical software and ethics

Analyses were performed in R using dplyr, ggplot2, broom, sf, dtwclust, forecast, and MASS. The study used publicly available, deidentified aggregate estimates and did not involve individual-level data; institutional review board approval and informed consent were therefore not applicable.

Discussion

In this ecological secondary analysis of GBD 2023 modeled estimates, the Africa-wide burden of bladder cancer increased between 2010 and 2023 in both counts and ASRs. In 2023, the highest estimated incidence rates occurred in Tunisia, Malawi, Libya, Egypt, and Mali, while Malawi had the highest mortality and DALY rates. Several countries with comparatively low estimated baseline rates, including Botswana and Gambia, nevertheless had some of the largest positive EAPCs, indicating rapid relative change from a low baseline. Population size was the largest contributor to the increase in counts, although age-structure and age-specific rate changes also contributed. These findings show that the African pattern is heterogeneous rather than a single uniform transition and are broadly consistent with previously reported international variation in bladder cancer burden [2].

Regional variation is compatible with, but cannot be attributed from our aggregate data to, heterogeneous exposure profiles. Smoking and selected occupational carcinogens are established bladder cancer risk factors [16]. A GBD 2019 analysis estimated smoking prevalence among people aged 15 years or older at 18.1% in North Africa and 17.8% in Southern Africa, compared with 10.5%, 8.8%, and 7.0% in Central, Eastern, and Western Africa, respectively [5]; Tunisia was among the countries exceeding 20%. This is compatible with, but does not explain, its high bladder cancer ASR. Infection-related disease also remains relevant in selected endemic settings. In Egypt, schistosomiasis control has coincided historically with declining schistosomiasis-associated squamous carcinoma and a relative shift toward urothelial carcinoma [17]. A hospital series from the Tanzanian Lake Zone reported squamous carcinoma in 57.0% of bladder cancers and Schistosoma haematobium eggs in 25.2% of specimens [9], but these findings cannot be extrapolated to all of Eastern Africa. International evidence also links bladder cancer to o-toluidine and selected occupations, but comparable African exposure data are sparse [18]. Our study therefore cannot determine whether smoking, schistosomiasis, or occupational exposures caused the observed country differences.

Lower estimated ASRs should not automatically be interpreted as lower underlying risk. Cancer surveillance varies substantially across Africa. For GLOBOCAN 2020, relatively recent registry data directly informed estimates in only 25 of 48 sub-Saharan African countries, while estimates for 16 countries without such data used information from neighbouring populations [11]. A later appraisal identified 15 sub-Saharan African countries without established cancer registries [19]. Mortality surveillance is similarly uneven: historical studies estimated death-registration completeness at approximately 10% in Nigeria in 2017 but approximately 96% overall in Egypt, with lower completeness in some rural areas [20; 21]. Although these studies did not evaluate the exact GBD 2023 bladder cancer inputs, they demonstrate the heterogeneous data environment. GBD addresses gaps through statistical modeling; estimates from data-sparse settings nevertheless depend more heavily on indirect inputs and assumptions [14]. Thus, low estimates may reflect true differences, incomplete ascertainment, or both.

The decomposition adds interpretation without establishing causation. Population-size change accounted for 43.2% of the increase in incidence, 39.8% in prevalence, 58.8% in deaths, and 50.3% in DALYs. The remaining changes were partitioned between age structure and age-specific rates. The latter should be termed the “age-specific rate-change component,” not “adverse epidemiological change.” For incidence, it may reflect exposure, detection, registration, or modeling changes; for prevalence, it may also reflect survival; and for deaths and DALYs, it may incorporate treatment, survival, and cause-of-death ascertainment. Its negative value in selected countries and outcomes further shows that it is a mathematical component rather than evidence of worsening risk.

DTW clustering likewise provided a descriptive, rather than etiological, summary. The lower-, intermediate-, and higher-burden trajectory groups reflected both ASR level and temporal shape because clustering used unstandardized rates. Because three clusters were prespecified and stability across alternative solutions was not tested, they should not be described as distinct biological phenotypes. They nevertheless illustrate why prevention, surveillance, and care planning should be adapted to country context rather than applied uniformly [22].

The pandemic-era results require particular restraint. GBD-estimated incidence, mortality, and DALY rates during 2020–2023 were above the ARIMA point forecasts, while prevalence changed from below to above its forecast trajectory. These differences are not observed excess cases or proof of a COVID-19 effect. The models used only 10 prepandemic observations, compared estimates primarily with point forecasts, and included no variables describing service disruption. Although a systematic review documented interruptions in cancer screening, diagnosis, and treatment in sub-Saharan Africa, the GBD 2023 cancer study explicitly stated that its estimates did not comprehensively reflect the pandemic’s potential effect because suitable data were unavailable across locations and measures. Our findings therefore represent exploratory pandemic-era deviations, not causal estimates of COVID-19 disruption.

The main log-linear models projected higher fitted ASRs in a majority of countries by 2030, but results varied with the fitting window. These projections are conditional extrapolations, not precise forecasts, and do not incorporate future population change, risk-factor trends, schistosomiasis control, registration, diagnosis, or treatment. Because they predict ASRs rather than absolute counts, they do not directly quantify future service demand. Nevertheless, considered together with population growth, they support forward planning for prevention, diagnostic evaluation, pathology, treatment, and surveillance, with tobacco control and schistosomiasis prevention emphasized according to local exposure patterns [22].

A strength of this study is its consistent comparison of four burden outcomes across 54 countries combined with trend, trajectory, decomposition, pandemic-era, and projection analyses. Several limitations remain. First, GBD estimates depend on heterogeneous source data and modeling, and country UIs cannot eliminate possible systematic error from incomplete ascertainment. Second, downstream analyses used GBD point estimates; draw-level uncertainty was not propagated into EAPC, clustering, decomposition, ARIMA, or projection results. Third, the ecological design and absence of country-year exposure data prevent causal attribution. Fourth, histology, stage, treatment, recurrence, and survival were unavailable. Fifth, clustering used a prespecified three-group solution, and decomposition components do not identify mechanisms. Finally, the short pandemic model and log-linear projections are assumption-dependent; projection intervals reflected coefficient uncertainty without incorporating GBD draw uncertainty or future random variation. These limitations are especially important for countries with sparse surveillance data.

In conclusion, GBD 2023 estimates indicate increasing and geographically heterogeneous bladder cancer burden across Africa. Population growth was the largest driver of increasing counts, while age structure and age-specific rates also contributed. Strengthening locally appropriate prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and cancer-registration systems is essential, but country differences and pandemic-era deviations should not be interpreted causally from these aggregate modeled data.
