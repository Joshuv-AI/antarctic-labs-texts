Cadaveric analysis of a safe trail for surgical exposure of the anterior cervico-thoracic junction
Authors: Tuna Aras, Reza Vahdad, Tobias Hilje, Julia Khabyuk, Margarethe Draga, Frank Eifinger, Grigore Cernaianu, Wael Ahmad, Rabi Raj Datta, Payman Majd, Martin Scaal, Martin Dübbers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical access to the cervico-thoracic junction in pediatric tumors presents substantial challenges due to complex anatomy and the proximity of critical neurovascular structures. To improve interdisciplinary training and minimize iatrogenic risks, we optimized a stepwise dissection protocol—referred to as the “Trail of Safety”—using formalin-fixed cadavers.MethodsSixteen bilateral cervico-thoracic dissections were conducted on eight formalin-fixed adult cadavers by a team of vascular and pediatric surgeons. A structured protocol involving supraclavicular incision, median sternotomy, and anterolateral thoracotomy was employed to sequentially expose neurovascular structures, including the common carotid, subclavian and vertebral arteries, as well as the vagus, phrenic and recurrent laryngeal nerves. Each anatomical landmark was scored for difficulty of exposure using a four-point scale.ResultsAll targeted neurovascular structures were successfully exposed. Supraclavicular and midline approaches provided reliable access to anterior mediastinal structures without necessitating lateral thoracotomy. The vertebral artery and recurrent laryngeal nerves were consistently the most challenging to expose. The rigid lungs of formalin-fixed cadavers impeded visualization of posterior mediastinal structures, which became accessible only after lung removal. Bidirectional dissection (from both cervical and thoracic fields) improved safety and exposure, particularly for nerves obscured beneath the clavicle.ConclusionsThe formalin-fixed cadaveric model proved effective for iterative interdisciplinary training and for refining a reproducible “Trail of Safety” protocol. This approach enhances anatomical familiarity and may contribute to safer surgical strategies for rare but complex pediatric cervico-thoracic pathologies.

Introduction

Pediatric tumors involving the cervico-thoracic junction are rare and present substantial surgical challenges owing to the complex regional anatomy and the close proximity of critical neurovascular and mediastinal structures [1, 2]. Achieving adequate exposure often requires combined surgical approaches, including cervical incisions, median sternotomy, and thoracotomy, tailored to tumor extent and location [3–14]. For pediatric malignancies such as rhabdomyosarcoma [15] and high-risk neuroblastoma [16–18], survival outcomes correlate strongly with the completeness of surgical resection. Consequently, developing surgical proficiency in the cervico-thoracic region—particularly outside high-stakes environments such as trauma settings [19–22] —is essential. Such expertise holds relevance across multiple disciplines, including vascular, pediatric, neurosurgical, thoracic, and esophageal surgery. Interdisciplinary training not only enhances anatomical familiarity and operative confidence but also promotes structured knowledge exchange between specialties. Importantly, the involvement of vascular surgeons in complex tumor resections has been associated with improved oncologic outcomes and enhanced procedural safety [23–25].

In this context, the concept of a structured “Trail of Safety”—as outlined in trauma surgery training manuals such as Top Knife [26]—offers a strategic framework for systematically exposing anatomical landmarks while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic injury.

Cadaveric dissection labs provide a unique platform for surgeons to rehearse complex exposure techniques in a controlled environment, particularly when dealing with rare anatomical scenarios that are difficult to simulate in live surgical training [27–33]. Formalin-fixed cadavers are especially practical, as they are widely available, preserve anatomical structures over extended periods, and allow for staged dissection across multiple sessions [34]. These features make them well suited for interdisciplinary collaboration and iterative learning.

Motivated by several pediatric cases involving large cervico-thoracic tumors (Fig. 1a and b), our team used a formalin-fixed cadaver model for surgical rehearsal. Adult cadavers were used, because of ethical concerns and the rare availability of pediatric donors. Our objective was to evaluate whether a cadaveric dissection protocol with flexible working times could be established for board-certified surgeons from different specialties—particularly vascular and pediatric surgery—to collaboratively explore and refine access to the cervico-thoracic region.

In this study, a structured and reproducible dissection protocol—referred to as the “Trail of Safety”—was applied to sequentially expose the critical neurovascular structures of the cervico-thoracic junction. The feasibility, advantages, and limitations of this formalin-fixed cadaveric model for interdisciplinary surgical training and anatomical analysis were evaluated.

Methods

No cadavers were obtained from other institutions or from non-voluntary sources. All data generated or analyzed during this study are included in this published article. The manuscript does not contain any individual person’s identifiable data in any form; therefore, consent for publication was not required.

Sixteen cervico-thoracic dissections were performed bilaterally on eight formalin-fixed adult cadavers (five males and three females). All dissections were performed by a vascular and a pediatric surgeon, assisted by medical students.

A standardized, stepwise dissection protocol was used to systematically expose neurovascular structures extending from the cervical region into the upper thorax. The initial plan included the exposure of key anatomical landmarks in the following sequential order:

Supraclavicular exposure

A transverse supraclavicular incision was made 1.5 cm superior to the clavicle, extending from the medial border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM) to the acromion (Figs. 2A and 3A).

Midline sternotomy

The supraclavicular incision was extended inferiorly through the suprasternal notch and continued along the midline to the level of the xiphoid process (Fig. 2A).

One cm above the jugulum, the anterior fascial layer overlying the strap muscles (sternohyoid and sternothyroid muscles) was identified and incised (Fig. 6A and B).

Following blunt retraction of the muscle laterally, the posterior fascial layer was incised (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, the posterior side of the manubrium was mobilized by blunt dissection with the tip of the finger. The ipsilateral strap muscles were divided one cm from their sternal insertions. A midline sternotomy was then performed using an oscillating saw, with deliberate preservation of the clavicles to maintain sternoclavicular joint (SCJ) integrity. A Finochietto retractor was inserted to retract the sternum. Directly under the split manubrium, the thymus became visible, as the most ventral mediastinal anatomical landmark (Figs. 6C and D and 7A).

Lateral thoracotomy

An anterolateral thoracotomy was performed through the fifth intercostal space and extended medially to the lateral margin of the sternum. The internal thoracic artery (ITA) was transected, and a partial lateral sternal division was carried out to complete the trap-door configuration. The following posterior mediastinal structures were subsequently assessed for dissection difficulty: the dorsal aspect of the LH, azygos vein (AV), thoracic segment of the esophagus (TE), and the descending thoracic aorta (DTA). In two cadavers, a posterolateral thoracotomy was performed through the fifth intercostal space on both the left and right sides, resulting in four additional exposures. This approach was undertaken to assess whether a posterior incision would enhance visualization of the dorsal aspect of the LH, AV, TE, and the DTA.

Following each dissection, the surgical team jointly assessed the difficulty of exposing each anatomical landmark using a standardized four-point scale: (1) indicated an easy exposure (readily identifiable and easily dissected structure); (2) indicated average difficulty (moderate anatomical variability requiring careful dissection of several anatomical landmarks prior identification); (3) denoted difficult exposure (limited visibility or extensive dissection required); and (4) represented very difficult exposure (associated with a high risk of iatrogenic injury). Danger points encountered during dissection, as well as specific maneuvers that facilitated exposure, were systematically documented. This scoring system was applied to all cervical and thoracic landmarks. To achieve continuous anatomical access, dissections were performed in a bidirectional manner—alternating between cervical and thoracic fields—which enabled comprehensive exposure of critical neurovascular structures, including the PNs and VNs, RLNs, and the SAs and CCAs, from both superior and inferior vantage points.

Results

A total of sixteen cervico-thoracic exposures were performed bilaterally on eight formalin-fixed cadavers. All targeted neurovascular structures at the cervico-thoracic junction and within the anterior mediastinum were successfully identified on each side.

The combination of supraclavicular exposure and midline sternotomy provided continuous and unobstructed access to neurovascular structures at the cervico-thoracic junction and within the anterior mediastinum, without the need for additional incisions.

Supraclavicular exposure

The CCA and IJV were consistently rated as easy to expose on both sides (Table 1). The cervical segment of the VN was rated as simple in seven left-sided and six right-sided cases. Dissection was scored as average in one left-sided and in one right-sided cervical segment of the VN. One right-sided case was rated as difficult. In this latter case, the nerve could not be easily identified in the space between IJV and CCA, because of its intimate adherence with the medio-dorsal side of the CCA.

The thoracic segment of the left CCA was rated as easy to expose in all but one case, which was scored as average. In contrast, exposure of the thoracic segment of the right CCA proved more challenging, as it required initial dissection of the BT. Five right-sided exposures were rated as average and three as difficult.

Access to the thoracic segments of the SAs showed side-dependent variation. On the left side, the first dissection was rated as very difficult due to inadvertent injury to the adjacent VN (Fig. 8A). Following this, the dissection strategy was modified to first isolate the VN on the right and left side, before proceeding to the thoracic segment of the SA. With this adjusted approach, the subsequent left-sided dissections were rated as average in six and easy in one case. On the right side, the thoracic SA was more difficult to expose due to its deep course beneath the SCJ. Three dissections were rated as difficult and five as average.

The degree of difficulty for dissection of the thoracic segments of the VN was also side-dependent. On the left side, the first case was scored as very difficult, due to the above-mentioned injury of the VN, while exposing the ventral side of the thoracic segment of the left SA. The dissection of the origin of the vessel had been easy and proceeded cranially along the vessel. The thoracic segment of the VN had not been yet exposed and its injury occurred inadvertently, on the ventral side of the SA, where it passed under the clavicle, subclavius muscle and subclavian vein (SV). In the subsequent cases, the thoracic segment of the left VN was exposed before approaching the artery. Exposing scores ranged from average in four cases to difficult in the other three, particularly in early cases, where identification of the nerve was started in the thorax. The difficulty of exposing the thoracic VN was down scored to average, when the technique was refined by including tracing of the cervical segment of the VN into the thorax. On the right side, dissection was consistently challenging due to the nerve’s location behind the BV and in front of the SA. Four dissections were rated as difficult and four as average.

The thoracic segments of the RLNs were among the most difficult structures to expose and revealed danger points of dissection (Fig. 8B). On the left, all seven dissections were rated as difficult due to the nerve’s deep trajectory beneath the AA. On the right, where the nerve loops around the SA, three dissections were rated as average and five as difficult.

In contrast, the thoracic PNs were easier to expose. The left PN was rated as easy in five cases and average in three, while the right PN was rated as easy in four and average in four cases.

Exposure of the dorsal LH on either side, as well as the AV, TE and DTA through the midline sternotomy, was uniformly rated as very difficult due to poor visibility caused by limited retraction of the rigidly expanded formalin-fixed lungs, regardless of side.

Lateral thoracotomy

Despite the addition of the anterolateral thoracotomy and partial sternotomy (Fig. 2B), the rigidity of the formalin-fixed lungs continued to impair visualization of the dorsal LH, AV, TE, and DTA. These structures continued to be rated as very difficult to expose. Even in the cases where a posterior thoracotomy was supplemented, visualization of these landmarks remained consistently very difficult, as long as the lungs remained in situ.

Adequate exposure of these posterior mediastinal landmarks was only achieved after the lungs were surgically removed, at which point the procedure transitioned from a surgical simulation to an anatomical preparation. Although this precluded assessment of surgical technique under realistic operative conditions, the resulting anatomical dissection proved valuable for gaining topographical familiarity with deep mediastinal structures.

Discussion

Cervico-thoracic exposure techniques

The need to improve exposure techniques in the cervico-thoracic region originates from the historically poor resection rates of apical lung tumors (Pancoast tumors), with incomplete excision in nearly one-third of cases associated with poor outcomes [36, 37]. In these tumors, traditional approaches such as the Paulson operation—an extended posterolateral thoracotomy—provide good access to posterior but insufficient control over anterior vascular structures [38].

To address this, anterior approaches were developed. These included transcervical-thoracic approaches, combining cervical incisions with clavicle resection, sometimes extended via thoracotomy [38–40]. Although these techniques provide excellent intraoperative exposure—including improved control of the SA and SV and safer dissection of the brachial plexus—they are associated with significant postoperative morbidity, such as shoulder dysfunction, cervical postural changes, and respiratory insufficiency [37, 39, 41].

The trap-door approach (supraclavicular incision, partial sternotomy, anterolateral thoracotomy) was designed to avoid clavicle resection or SCJ opening to minimize functional impairment. It has gained popularity in adult and pediatric surgery for improved cervico-thoracic access [3, 4, 13, 14, 42, 43]. Despite its advantages, the addition of anterolateral thoracotomy and partial lateral sternotomy to complete this exposure requires ligation of the ITA, thus limiting future bypass options.

Cadaveric models

Cadaveric models offer a unique opportunity to rehearse interdisciplinary techniques in a controlled and reproducible setting, particularly for rare but technically demanding scenarios that are infrequently encountered in live surgical practice. They also support analysis of anatomy and anatomical variations in the cervico-thoracic region [44–47].

Their long preservation time of formalin-fixed cadavers and the ability to reuse them across multiple sessions makes this model especially valuable in this context [27, 29, 30, 32–34]. These advantages also help overcome a key logistical challenge: coordinating time-intensive dissections among multiple surgical specialists. Consequently, formalin-preserved adult cadavers—already widely used in undergraduate medical education —are ideally suited for structured, multi-session surgical training. Until now, no cadaveric study has quantitatively evaluated the specific anatomical exposure of the separated elements of the trap-door technique. Our investigation addressed this gap, using the “Trail of Safety” strategy—first described by Hirshberg and Mattox in Top Knife [26] as a stepwise, structured dissection to minimize neurovascular injury.

Accordingly, this study was designed to evaluate the formalin-fixed cadaveric model as a training and analysis platform for interdisciplinary surgical exposure performed by vascular and pediatric surgeons.

The combination of a supraclavicular incision and a median sternotomy provided sufficient exposure of all anatomical landmarks of the cervico-thoracic junction and the anterior mediastinum. These structures could be well exposed without the need of performing a lateral thoracotomy, as also previously stated by other authors [26]. Besides preserving the ITA, avoidance of this incision might help to facilitate recovery. The formalin-fixed cadaveric model was valuable for refining the sequence of surgical steps, allowing interruptions after complex stages to review the literature and seek expert input. The team took advantage of the possibility to interrupt the dissection after difficult steps, refocus the literature search and consult other experts. The long period of conservation of the cadavers proved very suitable to accommodate the clinical schedules of the surgeons in order to allow interdisciplinary dissections. In particular, the exposure of individual anatomical landmarks could be divided into several shorter and more convenient sessions.

Trail of Safety

This approach allowed modification of the exposure sequence until a final, optimized “Trail of Safety” was deemed satisfactory by all team members.

Step 1- The first step of dissection during this trail remained the initial identification of the central cervical landmarks IJV and CCA by supraclavicular exposure. This procedure was straight-forward and simplified the following steps of dissection.

Step 2- The second step was to expose the cervical segment of the VN between CCA and IJV. This was in most cases also an easy procedure. In a case where the nerve could not be readily identified between the internal jugular vein and the common carotid artery, identification was found to be easier posterior to the common carotid artery.

The operative workflow and landmark exposure strategy were optimized with increasing experience and interdisciplinary discussion.

The superficial part of both the supraclavicular dissection and the median sternotomy was found to be easier to perform at the beginning of the procedure.

Step 3- Consequently, we continued with the median sternotomy directly after supraclavicular exposure of the IJV, the CCA and the cervical segment of the VN, before exposing deeper cervical landmarks. This procedure was further simplified by starting exposure with incision of the anterior fascial layer overlying the strap muscles one centimeter above the jugulum. This allowed to bluntly retract the muscles and incise their posterior fascial layer. Subsequently, creating a working space behind the manubrium by blunt digital dissection was simplified and offered a very good control for the following midline sternotomy. In addition, we found it much easier to begin the midline sternotomy from caudally and cut the bone towards the jugulum.

Step 4- The fourth step constituted the “descent” into the anterior mediastinum by sequentially exposing the thymus, left BV, BT and the AA, with the aim to identify and expose the thoracic segments of the PNs on the anterior LH. The easier to expose cervical segment of the VN and thoracic segment of the PN were secured first before proceeding to the more difficult dissection in the region under the clavicle. Before modification of the tactic to bidirectional cervical and thoracic exposure, we found the course of the VNs and PNs difficult to identify in this “no man´s land”, where the clavicle and subclavius muscle obscure the view on the underlying SA and SV. Usually, both nerves are hidden here between SA and SV and complicate dissection in the depth of the operative field. In addition, aberrant intramuscular passage of the PN through the anterior scalene muscle increases transection risk of the cervical segment [48]. Accessory PNs, present in up to 75% of individuals, may pass anterior or posterior to the SV, with venous loops in 45% of cases and sometimes traverse venous annuli [49–51].

Beginning exposure of the PN at its thoracic segment and continuing it until the portion beneath the clavicle particularly proved to be safe during dissection in a case where the left PN ran on the ventral side of the SV (Fig. 8B). Similarly, performing identification of the left VN at its cervical segment and then proceeding to its more difficult thoracic segment, before exposing the left cervico-thoracic SA, also proved to make subsequent dissection safer. This step prevented any inadvertent transsections at the danger point on the ventral side of the cervico-thoracic arteria subclavia, as encountered when this vessel was exposed before securing the nerve. A helpful aid during bilateral dissection of the PNs and VNs was to pass a dissection clamp next to the more easily identified segment of the nerve, then palpate the tip of the instrument from the opposite side of the region underlying the clavicle. This manoeuver facilitated dissection. These tricks might prove very useful, when these vessels or nerves are encased or obscured by tumors in the clinical setting.

Step 5- Only after these preparations, the deeper cervical anatomic landmarks were approached as the fifth step. After returning to the supraclavicular region, dissection of the cervical PN was initiated by dissecting laterally of the previously exposed IJV. Here, the fat pad covering the anterior scalene muscle was identified and lifted starting on its medial side. We found it easier to identify the medial side of the anterior scalene muscle first and then continue with the search for the PN by dissecting towards the lateral side of this muscle. In the formalin-fixed cadaver, the fat pad covering the anterior scalene muscle was rigidly transformed in most cases, making exposure of the underlying PN sometimes challenging. There where the underlying cervical segment of the PN could not be readily exposed, continuing dissection along the previously identified thoracic segment was very helpful to avoid injury.

Step 6- In the sixth step, the anterior scalene muscle was transected and the underlying supraclavicular segment of the SA could be exposed.

Step 7- The seventh step, exposure of the deeply located VA, was demanding. The VA may originate from the AA (2–8%) [52, 53] share origin with the inferior thyroid artery, or enter the transverse foramen at atypical levels—posing bleeding risks. A frequent problem—misidentifying another supraclavicular branch as the VA—led to the maxim: “Before you believe it is the VA, ensure there is not a more proximal vessel exiting the SA.”

We analyzed two approaches described for exposure of the VA in a standard textbook of vascular surgery [54]. In the first cases, we started exposure laterally of the IJV, using the supraclavicular segment of the SA as continuous guide, starting laterally of the thyrocervical trunk (TT) towards the medially located origin of the VA. This approach was found to be technically demanding due to limited visibility. The procedure was therefore modified by initiating the exposure medially from the internal jugular vein. Despite giving up direct contact to the SA at the TT, the approach medially from the IJV and laterally from CCA created a greater working space at the first segment of the SA, distally from the origin of the VA. Having previously exposed the cervical segment of the VN aided to increase the safety of this dissection. We found it then easier to develop the exposure of the VA distally from its origin.

Step 8- The eighth step, dissection of the cervical segment of the RLN, was likewise difficult. On the right, a non-RLN—associated with an aberrant SA—is present in ~ 1% of cases [55]. On the left, variations include a high ligamentum arteriosum and dual branches [56]. Our approach to dissect the nerve starting medially from the previously identified CCA proved to be a helpful navigational aid.

Despite progress, the VA and both segments of the RLN remained the most technically challenging structures and deserve special attention during dissection.

The view on posterior mediastinal structures at and below the posterior LH was impaired in the formalin-fixed cadaveric model because of rigidity of the lungs.

Step 9- Exposure of this region was attempted first by performing an anterolateral thoracotomy and partial lateral sternotomy.

Step 10- The exposure was continuated by extending the incision to a posterior thoracotomy. In the formalin-fixed cadaver model, however, the visualization of the posterior aspect of the LH, the TE, and the DTA was very difficult and unsatisfactory. These posterior mediastinal structures became only readily accessible after removal of the lungs. Thus, concerning exposure of the posterior mediastinum at and caudally from the posterior aspect of the LH, the formalin-fixed cadaveric model is rather useful for anatomy training than surgical simulation.

The formalin-fixed cadaveric model demonstrated feasibility for iterative interdisciplinary training and for developing a reproducible “Trail of Safety” protocol. This approach enhances anatomical familiarity and may support the refinement of safer surgical strategies in simulated settings.

This study has several limitations. First, the model was based exclusively on a small series of eight formalin-fixed adult cadavers. Formalin fixation alters tissue elasticity and lung compliance, which restricts the realism of surgical exposure. Second, the use of adult cadavers limits generalizability to pediatric anatomy, which was the motivating clinical context. Third, the feasibility ratings for exposure difficulty were based on subjective assessment by the surgical team rather than a validated scoring system. Finally, cadaveric models cannot replicate hemodynamic or physiological conditions, such as bleeding, tissue perfusion, or operative risk. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as preliminary anatomical observations that support training and protocol development, but not as evidence of clinical outcomes. Further work, including unfixed cadaver models and clinical validation, is needed to confirm applicability to pediatric surgery.
