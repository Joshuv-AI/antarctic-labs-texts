Clinical utility of runs of homozygosity in the identification of genetic causes in individuals with rare diseases
Authors: Theru A Sivakumaran, Bilal Azab, Emily Hershman, Ananditha Sivakumaran, Neil R Friedman, Dannah Raz, Derek Neilson, Theresa A Grebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the clinical utility of runs of homozygosity (ROH) in identifying the genetic causes of rare diseases in our patient population.MethodsAll unrelated patients (N = 2787), presenting with neurodevelopmental disorders and/or congenital anomalies, who underwent chromosomal microarray testing in our laboratory between July 2019 and December 2023, and publicly available single-nucleotide polymorphism microarray data from 3488 unrelated individuals in the general population were included in this study. Electronic medical records of all 2787 patients were reviewed to obtain information on follow-up genetic testing to identify the genetic causes.ResultsA total of 947 patients had at least 1 ROH > 5 Mb, and the majority of these (75%) were recurrent. One of the recurrent ROH, located at 16p11.2p11.1, was significantly increased in our patient population, especially in the Hispanic/Latino and Native Americans, compared with the general population. Approximately 1% (26/2787) of patients had possible uniparental disomy (UPD) and follow-up genetic testing on 2 patients revealed paternal UPD(14) in 1 patient and possible paternal UPD(6) in the other. Deleterious homozygous variants were found in 14 of 78 patients with nonrecurrent ROH who underwent follow-up sequencing studies. Of these 14 patients, causal genes were found to be within the ROH in 12 (15.4%) with causal genes within ROH smaller than 5 Mb in 5 patients, and 4 of these 5 patients were Native Americans. Deleterious homozygous variants were detected in 2 patients without nonrecurrent ROH; however, these were not located within ROH 1 Mb and larger.ConclusionOur results highlight the importance of investigating ROH 1 Mb and larger for deleterious homozygous variants in patients with nonrecurrent ROH 5 Mb and larger to identify the genetic basis of the presenting features.Keywords: Consanguinity, Recessive disorder, Runs of homozygosity, SNP microarray, Uniparental disomy

Introduction

Rare diseases are caused by different types of alterations in the genome, including genomic imbalances or copy-number variants (CNVs). Traditionally G-banded chromosomal analysis has been the gold standard for detecting these genomic imbalances. G-banded karyotype can detect microscopically visible chromosomal changes (>5-7 Mb in size depending on the band resolution) in up to 5% of patients with neurodevelopmental disorders.1 Chromosomal microarray (CMA), developed in early 2000s, has been shown to detect these alterations at a much higher resolution than G-banded chromosomal analysis, offering a diagnostic yield of 10% to 15% in patients with developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorders, and multiple congenital anomalies. Thus, in 2010, CMA was recommended as a first-line diagnostic test for patients with these conditions.2 Although exome sequencing (ES) has been recommended as a first-tier test for these conditions,3 most patients still undergo CMA testing initially.

Besides CNVs, the CMA platform with single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers enables the detection of runs of homozygosity (ROH), which is also referred to as long contiguous stretches of homozygosity or absence of heterozygosity.4 ROH is defined as an uninterrupted region of homozygous genotypes with copy-number neutral state and is one of the most common types of genomic characteristics in humans.5 ROH can occur when 2 copies of an ancestral haplotype are brought together in an individual. ROH from recent common ancestors are long (>5 Mb), whereas short ROH are from distant ancestors due to background relatedness. Studies on individuals from the general population show considerable variation in the number and total length of ROH per individual across individuals and populations. Additionally, frequencies of ROH across the genome are correlated with local genomic variables, such as recombination rate, and with signals of recent positive selection.6

The presence of ROH on multiple chromosomes suggests parental consanguinity.4 Consanguinity confers increased homozygosity and the sum of the size of autosomal ROH > 5 Mb is directly proportional to the level of parental relatedness, ranging from ∼1.5% in children born to parents with fifth-degree relationship to ∼25% in the case of parents with a first-degree relationship.4,7 Long ROH are disproportionately enriched for homozygous deleterious variants compared with what would be expected based on the total ROH coverage of the genome and the genomic distribution of nondeleterious homozygotes.8,9 A higher proportion of deleterious homozygous alleles increases the frequency of recessive disorders in inbred individuals. Therefore, the presence of ROH is not diagnostic of a specific condition but increases the risk of recessive disorders caused by homozygous pathogenic variants in genes located within the ROH.4,7,10,11 In silico analysis of recessive genes in the ROH can indicate possible candidate genes that can explain the patients’ clinical features because of pathogenic variants. Follow-up studies through targeted, ES or genome sequencing (GS), can be performed to understand the clinical significance by identifying the pathogenic variants causing autosomal recessive conditions.

ROH can also indicate uniparental disomy (UPD), which occurs when both copies of a chromosome or a chromosomal segment are inherited from one parent either because of trisomy rescue or monosomy rescue. In the case of trisomy rescue, UPD occurs when 2 retained chromosomes are from the same parent; both chromosomes can be isodisomic or completely heterodisomic when meiotic nondisjunction occurred in meiosis II or partially isodisomic/heterodisomic (called mixed UPD) when the meiotic error occurred in meiosis I. Whole chromosome isodisomy is indicated by the absence of heterozygosity along the entire chromosome, whereas partial isodisomy/heterodisomy is indicated by 1 or more large ROH on a single chromosome. Another class of UPD is segmental UPD, which is defined as UPD of a part of one chromosome together with biparental inheritance of the rest of the chromosome.12 It occurs because of postzygotic recombination between parental chromosomes or because of trisomy rescue. Although whole chromosome isodisomy is known to occur less frequently compared with whole chromosome heterodisomy, the frequencies of whole chromosome UPD and segmental UPD are not well understood. A few studies determining the prevalence of UPD in the general and clinical populations estimated it to be 0.05% to 0.6%.13, 14, 15, 16

The presence of large ROH (>10 Mb) on a single chromosome is indicative of UPD. A study examining the patterns of homozygosity in patients with UPD showed that the average size of terminal ROH and interstitial ROH were 11 and 24 Mb, respectively.17 UPD is not pathogenic, but it is an important cause of imprinting disorders that are caused by silencing of one of the alleles of imprinted genes in a gender-specific manner with genetic alteration on the other allele. In cases of imprinting disorders caused by UPD, both copies of imprinted genes inherited from one parent are silenced. UPD has been reported in all known imprinting disorders, such as transient neonatal diabetes mellitus (UPD(6)pat), Silver-Russell syndrome (UPD(7)mat), Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (UPD(11)pat), Temple syndrome (UPD(14)mat), Kagami-Ogata syndrome (UPD(14)pat), Angelman syndrome (UPD(15)pat), Prader-Willi syndrome (UPD(15)mat), etc.18 Frequencies of imprinting disorders caused by UPD are widely variable. For example, whole chromosome or segmental UPD of paternal chromosome 15 accounts for 3 to 7% of Angelman syndrome, whereas UPD(15)mat accounts for up to 30% of patients with Prader-Willi syndrome. UPD(6)pat and UPD(11)pat account for approximately 40% of transient neonatal diabetes mellitus and approximately 20% of Beckwith-Wiedeman syndrome, respectively.19,20 In addition, ROH resulting from UPD can also uncover homozygous pathogenic variants causing autosomal recessive disorder for which one of parents is heterozygous.21, 22, 23 Follow-up studies are warranted to confirm the imprinting disorders and to identify the pathogenic variants underlying recessive disorders. Published studies have demonstrated the detection of pathogenic variants for underlying autosomal recessive and UPD-related disorders in 1 to 11% of patients with ROH.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29

Our patient population is unique in which approximately 40% are Hispanic/Latino and Native Americans. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the clinical utility of ROH in identifying the cause of rare genetic diseases in our patient population.

Materials and Methods

Patient samples

All individuals, irrespective of the presenting symptoms, who underwent constitutional chromosomal microarray testing at the Phoenix Children’s Clinical Genomics Laboratory from July 2019 to December 2023 were considered for this retrospective study. A total of 2787 unrelated patients from this cohort were included in this study, and all relatives were excluded. Approximately 71% of these patients presented with neurodevelopmental conditions, such as developmental delay, intellectual disability, and autism spectrum disorders with or without congenital anomalies. Another 23% of patients had isolated congenital heart defects or multiple congenital anomalies. The remaining patients presented with isolated symptoms, including failure to thrive, short stature, intrauterine growth retardation, childhood obesity, etc. This study was granted a waiver of consent by the Phoenix Children’s Institutional Review Board.

Microarray analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood, saliva, and buccal swab samples using QIAsymphony DSP DNA Midi kit on the QIAsymphony instrument (Qiagen Inc). Then, the DNA was digested, amplified, labeled, and hybridized to CytoScan HD microarray using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc). The resulting raw data were analyzed to identify CNVs larger than 50 Kb and ROH 5 Mb and larger using Chromosomal Analysis Suite (ChAS) software versions 4.0 and 4.3. Both recurrent and nonrecurrent ROH were reported based on the physicians’ preference. Genomic coordinates were defined using the human genome reference sequence GRCh37/hg19. Auto-generated percentage of homozygosity by ChAS software was used to determine possible parental consanguinity. In addition, ROH of 1 Mb and larger were regenerated in a subset of patients (n = 9), in which the deleterious homozygous variants were not found within ROH of 5 Mb and larger.

Data from general population and data analysis

To study the patterns of ROH in different populations, we used 2 publicly available SNP microarray data sets: the Ontario Population Genomics Platform (OPGP) and the 1000 genome project (1KGP). The OPGP data set includes raw data (CEL files) from 873 individuals previously genotyped using CytoScan HD microarray. The vast majority (94%) of these individuals were Whites, 3.11% were South Americans, 1.91% were Asians, and the remaining were from admixed populations.30 We downloaded the raw microarray data from gene expression omnibus (accession #GSE59150). The data were analyzed and ROH 5 Mb and larger were generated using ChAS software version 4.0. One of the samples that did not meet the quality metrics was excluded from downstream analysis.

The 1KGP data set includes raw data from 3432 individuals from 26 different populations belonging to 5 different regions: Africa, America, East Asia, Europe, and South Asia. These individuals were genotyped using the Affymetrix Human SNP 6.0 array. The raw microarray data were downloaded from the 1KGP data portal and analyzed using Genotype Console software version 4.0. Sixty samples that did not meet the array quality metrics threshold were excluded from further analysis. After excluding all relatives, a total of 2616 unrelated individuals were included in the downstream analysis (Supplemental Table 1). ROH of 5 Mb were generated using ChAS software version 4.0.

Electronic medical record review

We reviewed electronic medical records of all 2787 patients to obtain information on the follow-up sequencing analysis performed to identify the genetic causes of diseases in these patients (Figure 1). All laboratories that performed follow-up sequencing used GRCh37 for analysis.

Data/statistical analysis

Frequencies of ROH were calculated using SAS version 9.4. Overlapping ROH that was present in at least 1% of all patients were defined as recurrent ROH. For ROH on chromosome X, only females were considered for this purpose. The frequencies of recurrent ROH between patients and the OPGP data set were compared, and odds ratio and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the MedCalc online tool https://www.medcalc.org/calc/odds_ratio.php.

Results

Of the 2787 unrelated patients included in this study, 1725 (62%) were males, and 1062 (38%) were females. The vast majority were <18 years of age and presented with variable phenotypes, including developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, dysmorphic facial features, congenital heart defect, and/or other congenital anomalies, such duodenal atresia, kidney malformation, etc. At least 1 ROH was detected in 947 patients (34%). Of these, 714 patients (75%) carried recurrent ROH only, whereas the remaining 233 had nonrecurrent ROH with or without recurrent ROH. Out of 1902 ROH detected in 947 patients, 1058 ROH were present at 7 overlapping regions with each present in at least 1% of patients. Three of these 7 recurrent overlapping ROH were present on autosomes (3p21.31p21.1, 11p11.2p11.12, and 16p11.2p11.1), accounting for approximately 15% of all recurrent ROH (Table 1). The remaining 4 recurrent overlapping ROH were present on chromosome X (Xp11.23p11.1, Xq11.2q13.1, Xq13.1q21.1, and Xq22.1q23). Frequencies of all recurrent ROH in the patient population were comparable to those in the OPGP data set, except ROH at 16p11.2p11.1 (Supplemental Figure 1). Further analysis showed that ROH at 16p11.2p11.1 was significantly enriched in the patients compared with the OPGP data set that predominantly include Whites (P = .02; OR = 2.34; 95% CI:1.11-4.91). But, approximately 39% of patients were Hispanic/Latino and Native Americans, and only approximately 46% were Whites (Supplemental Table 2). ROH at 16p11.2p11.1 was present in 1% of Whites, comparable to that of OPGP data set, whereas it was present in approximately 3.5% and 5% of Hispanic/Latino and Native Americans, respectively. Frequencies of these recurrent ROHs varied among different subpopulations in the 1KGP data set. ROH at 16p11.2p11.1 was present in approximately 1% of Caucasians (CEU), Colombians (CLM), Puerto Ricans (PUR), Han Chinese (CHB), Japanese (JPT), and Indian Telugu (ITU). It was not present in several other populations including Africans, African Americans, Mexicans, Finnish, British, etc. (Figure 2). It was more prevalent in the Peruvian population (PEL = 4.4%) followed by Chinese Dai (CDX = 2.0%).

Of the 233 patients with nonrecurrent ROH with or without recurrent ROH, 18 had clinically significant CNVs (Supplemental Table 3), 50 had variants of uncertain clinical significance, and the remaining patients had normal CMA results.

Possible uniparental disomy

After excluding recurrent ROH in 233 patients with nonrecurrent ROH with or without recurrent ROH, 121 patients (52%) carried at least 1 nonrecurrent ROH on a single chromosome ranging in size from 5 to 47.7 Mb. In the remaining 112 patients (48%), 2 or more nonrecurrent ROH were detected on more than 1 chromosome. Of all 233 patients, the vast majority (77%) had a percentage of autosomal homozygosity < 1.5%. Three patients had ROH on multiple chromosomes encompassing >22% of the autosomal genome indicative of parents being first-degree relatives, and the remaining 51 patients had percentage of autosomal homozygosity 1.5% to 13%, suggesting that they were the products of parents with second to fifth degree relationship. We classified the presence of one nonrecurrent ROH of >10 Mb or 2 nonrecurrent ROH of >5 Mb on a single chromosome as possible uniparental disomy (UPD), as described by Wang et al,27 Of the 121 patients with one or more nonrecurrent ROH on a single chromosome, 26 were classified as having possible UPD (Table 2).

Patients with possible UPD

CNV, copy-number variants; ROH, runs of homozygosity.

Pathogenic CNVs were also detected in 5 of these 26 patients with possible UPD (CR-0564, CR-0759, CR-1931, CR-2469, and CR-2746) and trisomy 21 was detected in 1 patient (CR-0609). Three patients had CNVs of uncertain clinical significance, and the rest had normal CMA results. In 4 of these 26 patients, ROH overlapped the regions associated with imprinted disorders on chromosomes 6, 7, 11, and 14. Follow-up studies in 2 of these 4 patients confirmed paternal UPD(14) in one (Figure 1)- a 5-day old male (CR-0544) with a prenatal history of polyhydramnios, ear malformations, musculoskeletal contractures, and scoliosis. CMA did not reveal any clinically significant CNVs but revealed 2 ROH of ∼10.7 and ∼34.7 Mb on the long arm of chromosome 14 involving imprinting genes associated with Temple syndrome and Kagami-Ogata syndrome. The second patient was a 5-week-old male (CR-0374) with transient elevated direct bilirubin, glycemic dysregulation, macroglossia, micrognathia, and failure to thrive. CMA showed a single large ROH of approximately 37.5 Mb spanning 6q23.2q27 that included an imprinted locus associated with transient neonatal diabetes mellitus. Follow-up studies showed no contribution of maternal chromosome 6, suggesting the possible paternal UPD(6) in this child. The father was not available for confirmation.

One of the 2 other patients with possible UPD involving imprinted loci was a 3-week-old female (CR-2469) with intrauterine growth restriction, respiratory distress, failure to thrive, and laryngomalacia. CMA revealed a mosaic ROH of ∼48 Mb involving an imprinted locus on 11p.15.5 in addition to 2 pathogenic deletions on the short arm of chromosome 5 involving the Cri-du-chat syndrome locus (Table 2, Supplemental Table 3). The fourth patient was a 6-month-old male (CR-2746) with dysmorphic facial features. CMA showed a pathogenic 457 Kb duplication at Xq28. In addition, this child had a ROH of ∼26 Mb on the long arm of chromosome 7 that included an imprinting locus at 7q32. Follow-up studies were not performed on these 2 patients to confirm UPD.

In addition, CMA revealed 3 ROH ranging in size from 16 to 29 Mb on chromosome 4 in a 10-week-old female (CR-1803) with intrauterine growth restriction, prematurity, feeding difficulties, failure to thrive, and dysmorphic facial features. Follow-up trio ES analysis showed 2 copies of maternal chromosome 4 and no paternal chromosome 4, confirming UPD(4)mat, which is not associated with any imprinting disorder. Furthermore, ES analysis did not reveal any homozygous pathogenic variants.

Autosomal recessive disorders

Electronic medical record review showed sequencing results from individual gene sequencing, panels or exome in 78 (33.5%) of the 233 patients. Two patients (CR-0759 and CR-0845) had pathogenic copy-number loss (Supplemental Table 3), whereas the third had UPD(14)pat and the fourth patient had possible UPD(6)pat (Table 2). Sequencing studies did not reveal any clinically significant homozygous alterations in these 4 patients (Figure 1). Of the remaining 74 patients, 16 patients had heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, 21 patients had heterozygous variants of uncertain significance (VUS), 19 patients had homozygous variants, and the remaining 18 patients had negative results. Of the 19 patients with homozygous variants, 13 had pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, and the remaining 6 had VUS in one or more genes (Table 3). None of the homozygous variants detected in these 19 patients were present within the 7 recurrent overlapping ROH. The causal genes with pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in 6 patients were within the nonrecurrent ROH > 5 Mb (Figure 1).

Discussion

The primary goal of CMA analysis is to identify disease-causing CNVs. The advantage of genotyping polymorphic markers in SNP arrays allows simultaneous detection of copy-neutral ROH, which can indicate uniparental disomy, parental consanguinity or background homozygosity. Recent consanguineous events and ancestral population events influence the number and size of ROH. The detection of ROH is not diagnostic of any specific disease but provides valuable diagnostic information critical to the clinical evaluation of patients with constitutional genetic disorders, especially imprinting and autosomal recessive disorders.

In this study, recurrent ROH were predominant among all ROH detected, with ROH at 7 overlapping regions accounting for ∼56% of all ROH. Three of these recurrent ROH were located on chromosomes 3, 11, and 16, and the rest were on chromosome X. Recurrent ROH at these regions were also reported by other studies in the literature besides the recurrent ROH on the other chromosomes.27,31,32 Nonrecurrent ROH > 5 Mb on 1 or more chromosomes were identified in approximately 8% (233/2787) of our patients. Wang et al27 identified 1 ROH > 10 Mb or at least 2 ROH > 5 Mb in each region in approximately 6% of 14,547 patients. Pajusalu et al32 observed nonrecurrent ROH in approximately 6% of their patients. Correia-Costa et al33 identified ROH > 10 Mb on one or multiple chromosomes exceeding 1% of the total autosomal genome in approximately 7% of Brazilian patients with neurodevelopmental disorders. The increase in the frequency of nonrecurrent ROH in our patients could be due to the lower cutoff used in this study (5 vs 10 Mb). One of the recurrent ROH, located at 16p11.2p11.1, was significantly increased in our patient population compared with individuals in the OPGP data set. Because the 16p11.2p11.1 recurrent ROH region overlaps the 16p11.2 recurrent deletion/duplication region, we further assessed the frequency of this recurrent ROH in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders. Of the 1989 patients with neurodevelopmental disorders, recurrent 16p11.2p11.1 ROH was present in 41 patients, which was statistically significant compared with the OPGP data set, as expected, (2.11% Vs 1%; P = .035; OR = 2.27; 95% CI 1.06-4.87). Because only approximately 46% of our patients were Whites compared with 94% in the OPGP data set, we cannot rule out type 1 error. Approximately 1% of White patients with neurodevelopmental disorder carried ROH at 16p11.2p11.1 (Supplemental Table 2), which is comparable to its frequency among Whites in the OPGP data sets (1%). Interestingly, ROH at 16p11.2p.11.1 was observed in 4% and 6% of Hispanic/Latino and Native American patients with neurodevelopmental disorder, respectively. This ROH was not observed in Mexicans and observed in approximately 1% of Puerto Ricans in the 1KGP dataset (Figure 2). However, the frequency of this ROH in Native Americans is not available for comparison. Therefore, additional studies are needed to confirm this association. To the best of our knowledge, no other study has reported an association of the 16p11.2p11.1 ROH with neurodevelopmental disorder or any other conditions. Our results add further evidence to the significance of genes in this region in neurodevelopment.

The presence of 1 or 2 ROH > 10 Mb on a single chromosome is suggestive of possible isodisomy due to UPD, either whole chromosome or segmental UPD. One of the aims of this study was to identify the frequency of possible UPD in our patient population. Previously, Wang et al27 and Bruno et al34 identified possible UPD in 1.2% and 0.7%, respectively, of their patients. In alignment with these reports, we also observed possible UPD in approximately 1% (26/2787) of our patients, which was significantly lower (P = .0001) compared with that of in the Brazilian patients (2.8%) with neurodevelopmental disorders.31,35 However, these numbers are still within the range (0%-8.18%) found in individuals from different populations in the 1KGP data sets. (Supplemental Table 4). The frequency of possible UPD in the OPGP data set was approximately 1.61%, which was higher than that of our patient population although statistically not significant (P > .05). Not all possible UPDs are considered pathogenic, but they can cause disease when UPD involves imprinted genes, in which one of the alleles is silenced in a gender-specific manner. When UPD does not involve imprinted genes, it can still uncover variants associated with recessive disorders. UPD involving imprinting disorder loci on chromosomes 6, 7, 11, and 15 are more frequently detected in patients by CMA.27 Our analysis of OPGP and 1GKP data sets did not show possible UPD involving any imprinting disorder loci. However, we identified possible UPDs involving imprinting disorder loci on chromosomes 6, 7, 11, and 14 in 4 patients. Follow-up studies confirmed paternal UPD(14) in 1 of these patients and suggested possible paternal UPD(6) in another patient. The UPD(6) testing in this patient showed no maternal contribution of chromosome 6, but a paternal sample was not available to confirm paternal UPD(6). One of the patients with possible UPD at 11q15.5 included imprinting locus associated with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) and Silver-Russell syndrome (SRS). BWS is associated with paternal UPD(11) and is characterized by overgrowth, macroglossia, abdominal wall abnormalities, and increased risk for certain childhood cancers, such as Wilms tumors, hepatoblastoma, etc. On the other hand, maternal UPD(11) in SRS is mainly characterized by intrauterine and postnatal growth restriction. Our patient presented with intrauterine growth restriction rather than overgrowth, suggesting the possibility of maternal UPD(11). Maternal mosaic UPD(11) has been previously reported in patients with SRS.36,37 This patient also carried pathogenic deletions involving the Cri-du-chat syndrome region and to the best of our knowledge, 5p deletions and UPD(11) have not been reported in any patient.

Ancestral population effects and recent consanguineous effects influence the number and size of ROH. Ancestral or population-specific ROH are generally smaller in size, whereas the ROHs resulting from recent consanguinity tend to be multiple megabases and likely to harbor disease-causing variants.6,38 Medical records review of all 233 patients with nonrecurrent ROH revealed follow-up sequencing studies in 78 patients. Of these, homozygous deleterious variants in 13 patients explained the phenotype (Figure 1). The deleterious homozygous variants were within ROH > 5 Mb in 6 patients (7.7%). Most systematic follow-up sequencing studies in literature considered ROH > 5 Mb threshold to identify disease-causing variants.27,28 In this study, we investigated if any homozygous pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in the remaining 7 patients were within the ROH < 5 Mb. Our analysis showed that ROH smaller than 5 Mb contained homozygous disease-causing variants in 5 of the remaining 7 patients, including 2 patients with ROH of 1.5 Mb. Interestingly, 4 of these 5 patients were Native Americans. Pajusalu et al32 identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication in KPTN within the shared ROH ∼1.5 Mb in an adult man and his sister with intellectual disability and macrocephaly. Recently, Manunath et al39 identified a homozygous disease-causing variant (p.Thr182pro) in approximately 98% of the limb-girdle muscular dystrophy patients with SGCB variants. Follow-up microarray analysis performed in 68 patients with this homozygous variant to understand the genetic structure showed a shared ROH of approximately 1 Mb in all patients, suggesting a possible founder event. These findings suggest that the size of ROH cannot be used as a reliable indicator for identifying homozygous disease-causing variants. Therefore, if there is a strong indication for a recessive condition and distant consanguinity or common ancestry, lowering the threshold of ROH size could aid in the identification of disease-causing variants.

Of the 6 patients with homozygous VUS, a 2-day old female (CR-1514) with congenital heart disease showed autosomal ROH totaling approximately 13%, which is consistent with a second-degree parental relationship. Follow-up ES showed homozygous variants in 5 genes, including TMEM260 (HGNC: 20185), AHCY (HGNC: 343), FANCI (HGNC: 25568), PCCA (HGNC: 8653), and SLC19A1 (HGNC: 10937), and all genes except SLC19A1 were located within ROH > 5 Mb. Homozygous or compound heterozygous truncating variants in TMEM260 (HGNC: 20185) are reported in several patients with structural heart defects, with or without renal anomalies and neurodevelopmental delay,40, 41, 42, 43 whereas variants in other genes were not reported to be associated with congenital heart defects. Our patient had a frameshift homozygous variant (NM_017799.3:c.332delT p.(Phe111SerfsTer25)), which was predicted to result in loss of function of the TMEM260 protein, and this variant was absent in GnomAD 4.0. In addition, this variant was within ROH, indicating the possibility of this variant being present on both copies of chromosome 14. The mother was heterozygous for this variant, and the father was unavailable for testing. These findings suggest that the patient’s phenotype was likely to be caused by this loss of function variant (Figure 1). Because of limited evidence, homozygous variants in the remaining 5 patients were classified as VUS.

Follow-up sequencing studies revealed homozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants underlying autosomal recessive diseases in a total of 12 out of 78 patients (15.4%), which is higher compared with other reports in the literature.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Of these 12 patients, 5 were Native Americans. Two of these 5 patients had homozygous deleterious variant in HOXA1 (HGNC: 5099) (NM_005522.4:c.76C>T p.(Arg26Ter)) that is commonly associated with HOXA1 deficiency in Navajo and Apache populations. One of these 2 patients had an autosomal homozygosity of approximately 3%, with HOXA1 gene located within a ROH of ∼27 Mb (Table 3). The other patient had autosomal homozygosity of approximately 0.6%, with HOXA1 within a ROH of ∼3.8 Mb. The deleterious variants found in TAF6 (HGNC: 11540), CWC27 (HGNC: 10664) and FAM20C (HGNC: 22140) in the Native Americans were not reported to be common among the Southwestern Athabaskans.44 Additional studies are needed to confirm if these deleterious variants or ROH are common among Native Americans in the Southwestern United States. The detection rate of homozygous variants underlying autosomal recessive diseases in patients with ROH in those studies ranged from 2% to 11%,24, 25, 26,28,29 and this number in consanguineous patients can be as high as 78%.45 The increased detection rate in our patient population could be attributed to disease-causing variants found within the ROH < 5 Mb. All 6 patients with homozygous pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants within ROH > 5Mb were the products of possible consanguineous marriages as indicated by the autosomal homozygosity percentage of 3% to 13%. One of the 5 patients with disease-causing variants within ROH < 5 Mb also had homozygosity of approximately 10% in the autosomal genome. The remaining 4 patients were Native Americans with percentage of autosomal homozygosity < 1%, indicating parental relationship of sixth degree and above, and deleterious variants in the population-specific ROH might be contributing to the disease in these patients. This aligns with the social practice of the Southwest Athabaskans, including Navajo and Apache Indians, in whom marriage within a clan is prohibited and matrilineal system is practiced.44,46 These Native American populations went through a number of population bottlenecks. As a result of population explosion after these bottlenecks, members of these groups may share ROH with deleterious variants causing autosomal recessive conditions.

None of the deleterious homozygous variants detected in our patients without nonrecurrent ROH were within ROH of 1 Mb and larger, whereas approximately 86% (12/14) of the deleterious homozygous variants were found within ROH in patients with nonrecurrent ROH. These findings suggest that the ROH of 1 Mb and larger in patients with nonrecurrent ROH more commonly harbor deleterious homozygous variants than in patients without nonrecurrent ROH. The results of this study highlight the importance of investigating patients with nonrecurrent ROH using follow-up sequencing studies to identify genetic causes of the disease. Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. This study is not a prospective one, in which all ROH are investigated to identify deleterious homozygous variants. Instead, we took a reverse approach in which we investigated, using data from medical records, if deleterious homozygous variants detected in a subset of patients with nonrecurrent ROH were within ROH or not. Therefore, additional systematic, prospective studies are needed to confirm our findings. Instead of using CMA and follow-up sequencing, ES/GS is now often used as a first-tier test to detect ROH, UPD, and deleterious homozygous variants simultaneously. Although the professional guidelines recommend ES/GS as first-tier test for patients with neurodevelopmental disorders or congenital anomalies,3,47 because of limitations of insurance coverage and lack of universally available clinical genetic services, CMA is still used as a first-tier test in more than half of the patients.

Given this situation, based on our findings in this study, we recommend performing follow-up UPD testing for confirmation in patients with at least 1 ROH > 10 Mb or 2 ROH > 5 Mb on a single chromosome on CMA. All 4 patients with possible UPDs involving imprinting loci in this study had 1 or 2 ROH > 10 Mb on single chromosome. It is also possible that UPD can be detected in patients with 1 or more nonrecurrent ROH (ie, partial isodisomy) and possible uniparental heterodisomy for rest of the chromosome that includes imprinted loci (ie, mixed UPD). CMA cannot detect possible uniparental heterodisomy. Therefore, UPD or trio ES/GS studies after the detection of 1 or more nonrecurrent ROH on a single chromosome, especially the one known to harbor imprinted loci, will confirm imprinting disorders. Because smaller ROH are common even in outbred populations, ROH threshold of >3-5 Mb is widely used in the clinical laboratories to report parental consanguinity.4,48 We recommend performing follow-up sequencing studies in patients with 1 or more nonrecurrent ROH of >3-5 Mb to identify deleterious homozygous variants that are within ROH. In this study, disease-associated genes were found to be within ROH in ∼15% of patients with nonrecurrent ROH who underwent follow-up sequencing studies. In approximately 40% (5/12) of these patients, the disease-associated genes were within ROH < 5 Mb. Therefore, in patients with at least one ROH of >3-5 Mb, we recommend lowering ROH threshold to 1 Mb as a second step to capture all ROH 1 Mb and larger and screening all potential candidate genes within the ROH for deleterious homozygous variants. Follow-up studies using ES/GS would be ideal for this purpose because this approach not only identifies deleterious homozygous variants within ROH but also identifies deleterious variants in the regions outside of ROH in patients without deleterious homozygous variants within ROH.

In conclusion, we present the utility of ROH in detecting causes of rare diseases in our patient population. One of the 7 recurrent ROH, located at 16p11.2p11.1, was significantly increased in our patients, particularly in Hispanic/Latino and Native American populations compared with the general population. Follow-up genetic testing in patients with nonrecurrent ROH showed imprinting disorders in 2 patients and autosomal recessive disorders caused by deleterious homozygous variants in 12 patients. Five of these 12 patients were Native Americans, with 2 carrying a deleterious variant associated with HOXA1 deficiency common in the Southwestern Athabaskans. Of the 5 patients with deleterious homozygous variants within ROH < 5 Mb, 4 were Native Americans. Thus, these findings highlight the importance of investigating ROH 1 Mb and larger for deleterious homozygous variants via follow-up sequencing studies in patients with nonrecurrent ROH of 5 Mb and larger.
