Determinants of human papillomavirus vaccination uptake in Nigeria: a mixed-methods systematic review
Authors: Kafayat Aminu, Emeka Benjamin Okeke, Yovanthi Anurangi Jayasinghe, Chiamaka Norah Maduabuchi, Adetayo Olorunlana, Temitope Akinyemiju, Charles Adetokunbo Olomo, Ugochukwu Eze, Abhishek Banerjee, Koshala Devi Manyapura Ganesh, Afeez Abolarinwa Salami, Oluwatobi Emmanuel Adegbile, Veena Madavanathara Narayanan, Kehinde Kazeem Kanmodi, Precious Chika Nnannah, Rita Amarachi Nwebo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination coverage in Nigeria is hindered by multiple barriers, including limited awareness, socioeconomic constraints, cultural beliefs, systemic challenges, and policy gaps. As a result, the burden of cervical cancer and other HPV-associated malignancies continues to rise despite public health interventions. The current mixed-methods systematic review (SR) synthesizes evidence on the determinants of HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria to inform context-specific strategies for improving coverage.MethodsThe methodological procedures of this SR adhered to the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) guideline for systematic reviews and reported using PRISMA. A comprehensive systematic search of nine electronic databases was undertaken to identify peer-reviewed articles published from January 2015 to August 2025. After deduplication and screening of the retrieved literature, the methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool. Relevant data were extracted from all included articles and synthesized using a convergent integrated approach.ResultsAmong the 481 records identified, 16 studies involving 10,764 participants met the eligibility criteria. Most studies were conducted in southern Nigeria and focused on adolescents, students, parents, and caregivers. HPV vaccine acceptance ranged from 32.7% to 97.3%, yet actual uptake remained low at 1% to 15% in most studies. Limited knowledge and poor awareness were the most consistent determinants of low uptake. Other barriers included fear of side effects, misconceptions about infertility or sexual behavior, limited healthcare engagement, and lack of provider recommendation. Sociocultural influences such as parental approval, religious beliefs, and cultural norms also shaped vaccination decisions, while vaccine cost and limited availability further restricted uptake.ConclusionA significant gap exists between HPV vaccine acceptance and actual uptake in Nigeria. Improving coverage requires coordinated strategies that strengthen public education, address vaccine affordability, enhance provider communication, and respond to sociocultural concerns influencing vaccination decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27195-6.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination represents a key preventive intervention for reducing the burden of several cancers, including anal, cervical, oropharyngeal, penile, vaginal, and vulvar cancers [1–5]. Cervical cancer, the majority of which is caused by persistent HPV infection, remains a major public health challenge in Nigeria, ranking as the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy in women and a major cause of cancer-related mortality among those aged 15–44 years [6–8]. Recent national estimates indicate that approximately 12,075 women are diagnosed annually, with nearly 7,968 deaths each year [8, 9]. This reflects one of the highest burdens in sub-Saharan Africa, with unadjusted incidence rates of 11.9 and mortality rates of 7.84 per 100,000 women [8, 9]. In addition to the burden among women, men are also affected by HPV-associated cancers, including penile, anal and head and neck cancers, with unadjusted new cases in Nigeria estimated at 0.02 per 100,000 for penile cancer and 0.65 per 100,000 for anal cancer [2]. The risk is particularly elevated in men who have sex with men (MSM) and people living with HIV (PLHIV) due to higher prevalence of anal HPV infection [10, 11].

Despite the availability of effective preventive interventions, including the prophylactic HPV vaccination and cervical screening, uptake remains alarmingly low [12, 13]. Reported HPV vaccine coverage in Nigeria is estimated at 2.6–5.1%, with slightly higher rates in structured school-based campaigns [13]. Globally, cervical cancer ranks fourth among cancers affecting women, accounting for an estimated 0.66 million new cases and 0.35 million deaths in 2022 [14–17]. Approximately 90% of cervical cancer-related deaths are reported in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where preventive services remain largely inaccessible [18, 19]. In sub-Saharan Africa, cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women [20, 21]. Globally, nearly 70% of cervical cancer cases are attributable to persistent infection with high-risk HPV types, particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18 [1, 20, 22].

Nigeria’s vaccination uptake lags behind global and regional targets, as barriers such as socioeconomic constraints, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, cultural norms, erroneous ideas, and limited awareness continue to impede progress toward the WHO 90–70–90 target for eliminating cervical cancer, including achieving 90% HPV vaccination uptake in 2030 [23–27]. Although current efforts focus primarily on vaccinating girls aged 9–14 years in line with WHO recommendations [15, 28], extending coverage to boys and older age cohorts, as endorsed internationally, would create a more inclusive approach to curb HPV transmission and reduce the overall cancer burden [29]. Several studies have identified the determinants of HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria, including socio-demographic characteristics, cultural beliefs, healthcare accessibility, and policy gaps [30–33]. However, the evidence is fragmented, often limited to single-method approaches and specific regions [34–38]. The lack of comprehensive synthesis restricts the development of effective, context-specific interventions. Consequently, there is a pressing need for integrated evidence that captures the magnitude of uptake, the sociocultural milieu and systemic factors influencing vaccination behavior using a mixed-methods approach.

This mixed-methods systematic review (SR) seeks to offer a comprehensive understanding of the contributing factors of HPV vaccine coverage in Nigeria, thereby informing context-specific strategies to strengthen HPV vaccination uptake and minimize the health impact of cervical cancer and other HPV-associated malignancies.

Methods

The review adhered to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for mixed-methods systematic reviews, based on the 2015 and 2020 editions [38, 39]. A mixed-method systematic review provides a valuable and multifaceted understanding of the obstacles encountered in healthcare service provision [39]. This approach was therefore selected to generate comprehensive evidence, combining quantitative relationships with in-depth insights into the determinants of HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria.

The protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (Ref. No: CRD420251113431). The review had no deviations from the protocol, it was performed and reported in alignment with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [40]. (Supplementary File, Page 1).

Search strategy

To identify studies on the barriers, facilitators, and determinants of HPV vaccination in Nigeria, a systematic literature search was led on August 8, 2025. The following research question was addressed in this SR: What are the determinants (barriers and/or facilitators) of HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria? Study selection

All the identified records were collated and imported into the Rayyan web-based application, where duplicate entries were removed prior to screening. Titles and abstracts were independently screened against the predefined eligibility criteria by three reviewers (RAN, PCN, and CAO). Following the initial screening, two review team members (RAN, PCN) independently reviewed the full texts of retrieved studies to verify their suitability for the review. Any disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion within the review team until consensus was achieved. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (KKK) resolved the decision. Studies failing to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, with reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage recorded. The overall process of study identification, deduplication, screening, and inclusion is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Data extraction was initially performed by one reviewer (KA), who independently extracted both qualitative and quantitative data from all studies included in this systematic review. The extracted data were subsequently reviewed by three additional reviewers (RAN, PCN, and KKK) to ensure accuracy, appropriateness, and completeness. The extracted information was documented in a uniquely designed data extraction sheet, jointly developed by all authors, to facilitate quality appraisal and evidence synthesis. The data extraction form was piloted prior to full data extraction to ensure consistency, clarity, and reliability among reviewers. Extracted details included study context, year of publication, methodological design, study aims, participant demographics, features of HPV vaccination programmes or interventions, and outcomes relating to uptake, acceptability, feasibility, as well as reported barriers/facilitators. The extracted outcomes for quantitative studies were descriptive and/or inferential statistical results, whereas qualitative studies yielded thematic or sub-thematic findings supported by representative quotations or examples. Any discrepancies among reviewers were resolved through consensus-based discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (KKK).

Risk of bias assessment

To systematically appraise the methodological quality of the studies included in this SR, the 2018 version of the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was selected by the reviewers (YAJ and EBO) [41]. The MMAT is specifically designed to evaluate diverse study designs within a single review, such as observational studies and experimental studies. This tool includes two generic screening questions applicable to all study types. Subsequently, each study was categorized according to its design, and five methodological quality criteria specific to that design were applied.

For each criterion, studies were rated as “Yes” if the criterion was met, “No” if the criterion was not met, or “Can’t tell” if insufficient information was reported, following MMAT guidance. Two reviewers (YAJ and EBO) jointly and independently assessed the methodological quality of each study, and a third reviewer (UAE) was consulted to settle any disagreements occurred during the appraisal process. The detailed quality appraisal outcomes were summarized in the supplementary file [Tables S5-S9, Pages 7–12]. In accordance with MMAT guidelines, no studies were excluded from the systematic review based on their methodological quality.

Data analysis and synthesis

Data analysis was conducted separately for quantitative and qualitative studies, followed by an integrated synthesis to offer an in-depth insight into the factors influencing HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria.

The extracted numerical data from quantitative studies, encompassing both descriptive and inferential results, were organized in tables and narratively synthesized due to variations in study design, participant characteristics, and measured outcomes. Owing to the variability in methodologies, outcomes, and measurement of vaccination uptake determinants a meta-analysis was not feasible. For qualitative studies, a thematic synthesis approach was employed. Extracted themes and subthemes related to determinants of HPV vaccination were systematically coded and organized to identify patterns across studies. Illustrative quotations or examples were included to support each theme.

Quantitative and qualitative evidence were subsequently integrated using a convergent approach, guided by the JBI methodology for mixed-methods systematic reviews [42]. The quantitative data were “qualitized” by transforming numerical findings into descriptive narratives. These narratives were then combined with the qualitative data and read through thoroughly to identify categories based on similarities in meaning. Each category included data of qualitative, qualitized quantitative, or a blend of both. Finally, the categories were combined to synthesize integrated findings that addressed the review question and provided a holistic understanding of barriers, facilitators, and determinants of HPV vaccination in Nigeria. All decisions regarding data interpretation and categorization were discussed among the review team members to verify consistency and reliability of the synthesis.

Results

In total, 481 records were identified from initial search of nine databases (PubMed = 122; SCOPUS = 233; MEDLINE = 92; CINAHL Ultimate = 26; SPORTDiscus with Full Text = 0; APA PsycArticles = 0; Psychology and Behavioral Sciences Collection = 2; and APA PsycInfo = 6; APA PsycTest = 0). Two hundred and fifty-four (254) articles were removed due to duplication, two hundred and thirty (230) were excluded due to ineligible title and abstract, and eight (8) were excluded due to inappropriate study outcomes. Following this, full texts of the 16 included studies were appraised for quality, and all were scored above average using the mixed-methods approach. Study designs

The included studies comprised both qualitative and quantitative designs. The majority (n = 10; 62.5%) were quantitative non-randomized studies, all adopting a cross-sectional analytical design [33, 43–51]. Four studies (25%) used qualitative design [52–55]. The remaining two studies (12.5%) were quantitative descriptive, both adopting a cross-sectional descriptive design [56, 57].

Outcomes of mixed-method appraisal tool

According to the MMAT criteria, all the studies included in this systematic review demonstrated varying levels of methodological rigor across qualitative and quantitative designs. The four qualitative studies included in the review satisfied all MMAT criteria. These studies demonstrated clear research questions, appropriate qualitative approaches, and adequate data collection methods. Furthermore, the findings were effectively derived from the data, with strong coherence between the qualitative data sources, analysis, and interpretation [42–45]. Among the ten quantitative non-randomized studies, all met the MMAT criteria for having clear research questions and utilizing appropriate measurements for both outcomes and exposures [23, 33–41]. However, participant representativeness emerged as a notable limitation; four studies did not meet the representativeness requirement [34–36, 39], while four others were rated as “Can’t tell” for this criterion [33, 37, 38, 40]. Additionally, three studies failed to adequately account for confounders in their design and analysis [33, 37, 40]. Outcome completeness was also identified as a concern in two studies [36, 41]. The remaining two quantitative descriptive studies demonstrated high methodological quality regarding clear research questions, relevant sampling strategies, and representative samples of the target population [46, 47]. While both studies used appropriate measurements, one study was rated as “No” for the appropriateness of the statistical analysis [47], and the other was rated as “Can’t tell” for the same criterion [46].

No studies were excluded on the basis of the quality appraisal. These quality appraisal findings were utilized to support a cautious interpretation of the evidence regarding the determinants of HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria.

Characteristics of studies on determinants of HPV vaccination in Nigeria

Majority (12/16) of the included studies were single-location studies [33, 43, 47–53, 55–57]. Others were conducted in multiple locations [44–46, 54], with 2 spreading across all the 6 regions or geopolitical zones of Nigeria [44, 54]. Locations from the South West were Ibadan [43, 47, 52, 53], , Lagos [49, 51]. Those from the South-East were conducted in Anambra [48, 55], , Enugu [56] while Kaduna [57] and Kano [33] were from the North-West and Ilorin from the North-Central [50]. This indicates that majority of the studies were clustered in Southern Nigeria (Fig. 2).

Individual-level determinants of HPV vaccine coverage in Nigeria include low knowledge and awareness, misperceptions of risk, safety concerns, and limited healthcare engagement. Knowledge and awareness of HPV infection and vaccine

Across the reviewed studies, individual-level factors emerged as the most direct determinant of HPV vaccine uptake. Low knowledge and awareness was a recurring determinant in nearly all studies [33, 43, 46–57]. Many participants had never heard of HPV, for instance, Ndikom and Oboh reported poor awareness of up to 88.2% [47]. In another study, most participants misunderstood mode of HPV transmission as only 15% linked it to sexual intercourse [43]. Even among healthcare workers, knowledge gaps persisted, especially about male vaccination [57].

Perception of risk and safety of HPV vaccine

Perceived risks of HPV infection, fear of cancer and death, motivated acceptance in some studies [56]. Also concerns about HPV vaccine side effects and safety were major barriers [33, 43, 49]. Likewise, mistrust in vaccine manufacturers [56] and fear of infertility [55] also shaped decisions.

Level of education and information sources

Education was positively associated with uptake in some study [49], although another study noted an inverse trend where uneducated caregivers showed higher uptake, possibly reflecting community program targeting [45]. Consultation with doctors/gynaecologists improved acceptance among some community members [43, 46], while lack of doctor recommendation reduced uptake among parents and caregivers of adolescents [33].

Age and demographic factors

Younger adolescents had higher uptake (71.8%) [45]. Among university students, age (20–25), sexual activity, having multiple partners, and history of sexually transmitted infections were linked to HPV vaccine uptake [50].

Motivation and prior health behaviors

Caregiver motivation and prior vaccination history (e.g., COVID-19 vaccination) increased likelihood of HPV vaccine uptake [55]. Ability to navigate health services also contributed to HPV vaccine uptake in some studies [45, 54].

Gender bias and recommendation patterns

Some studies found patterns of gender bias, with vaccines recommended more for girls than boys, reinforcing gendered perceptions of HPV risk [57].

Socio-cultural determinants of HPV vaccine uptake

Similarly, socio-cultural determinants of HPV vaccine uptake in Nigeria such as parental approval, religious and cultural beliefs, and community norms were identified in the current review. Parental influence was the strongest driver of adolescent vaccination intention, followed by peer influence. This was a consistent determinant observed across adolescent-focused studies [43, 47, 55]. Between 30.1% and 52% of adolescents intended to vaccinate if approved by parents [43, 47], while parental opposition was cited as a major barrier in another study [55].

Peer and social network influence had less influence on vaccine uptake compared to parental input. About 26.2% reported willingness to take HPV vaccine if peers were vaccinated [43]. In another study, peer and friend support encouraged uptake, but had weaker influence than family influence [47].

Religious and cultural beliefs regarding HPV vaccination were strong barriers to vaccine uptake. There were fears and religious beliefs that vaccination would encourage promiscuity [42, 43, 45], cause infertility or increase sexual activity [45]. These cultural and religious views fueled hesitancy [23]. In another study, many respondents were unsure about religious/cultural positions regarding vaccine uptake [37]. It was reported that about 55.4% were unsure whether their religious beliefs permits them to receive the HPV vaccination while 49.7% did not know if it is against their cultural beliefs; reflecting uncertainty rather than outright rejection [37]. Influence of traditional and religious leaders were also reported [43] as they held strong beliefs that cervical cancer is caused by promiscuity and lifestyle changes due to civilization, rather than HPV [43]. Cultural insensitivity among healthcare providers and public mistrust were likewise linked to perceptions of poor leadership [44].

Similarly, social norms and approval also shaped caregiver willingness, as discussions with friends/family boosted acceptance [44, 45]. Regional differences in community-level norms further influenced uptake as seen in higher acceptance in Nasarawa compared to Abuja and Adamawa [45].

Financial factors influencing HPV vaccine coverage

Vaccine cost and affordability were the main financial factors which influence HPV vaccine coverage in Nigeria. The single most cited economic barrier across almost all studies was the cost of vaccine as it influenced willingness to vaccinate [33, 47, 49, 51–53, 55]. About 29% of parents would allow vaccination if the cost was subsidized [43], while 67.3% to 72% would accept HPV vaccine if it was free [33, 51]. However, vaccine hesitancy persisted even with subsidies, showing that financial access interacts with cultural concerns [51]. Even when participants expressed high willingness, actual uptake was low due to prohibitive cost [46, 55].

Another study revealed that poor availability (25.9%) of vaccines was a significant barrier [49]. Also, systemic barriers like scarcity of health insurance, insufficient government financing, and improper storage affected affordability and access [54].

Discussion

This mixed-methods systematic review focused on the determinants of HPV vaccine uptake in Nigeria. Majority of the included studies in this review were carried out in the southern part of the country with young adolescents/students and their parents/caregivers constituting the largest study group. Across studies, the willingness to get vaccinated was relatively high. However, the vaccine coverage remained low, especially with young adolescents/students. This disparity between acceptance of the vaccine and its actual uptake has been found to be consistent with findings from other studies where insufficient knowledge, inadequate funding, socio-cultural and gender beliefs implicated in the low uptake of HPV vaccination in LMICs and global indigenous communities ​ [58–60]. Notwithstanding, factors such as increased level of education, young age, motivation from caregivers and sexual history were found to have increased uptake of HPV vaccines in this study.

The inadequate knowledge of HPV, its vaccination and role has been identified as the key factor influencing the uptake of the vaccine in Nigeria. Almost all the studies reported a low level of knowledge about HPV among adolescents, parents and healthcare workers except the study by ​Onalu et al., [55] where the participants had a good understanding of the vaccine and its preventive role. However, misconceptions of the role of the vaccine in preventing cervical cancer were prevalent across most of the studies. These findings are consistent with those from other African countries, where limited health literacy and erroneous ideas contributed significantly to poor vaccine uptake [25, 26, 61, 62]​.

Healthcare workers are instrumental in influencing HPV vaccine acceptance and uptake as they are considered to be one of the most credible sources of information on HPV vaccination, however, notable vaccine hesitancy because of inadequate knowledge, parental hesitancy and low vaccine confidence have been reported among them [71, 72]. Osaghae et al., [73] in their study stated the importance of providing training targeted on HPV vaccine hesitancy counselling to healthcare workers with a small number of years in practice and nurses to significantly improve the uptake of the vaccine. Thus, it becomes imperative to focus on health education to improve the knowledge level on HPV vaccination and eliminate misconceptions surrounding it. Research evidence has shown that educational strategies such as face-to-face education at schools, simplification of information and engagement between public health nurses and individuals are effective in improving the knowledge level of HPV vaccination [63]​.

In this study, we identified socio-cultural and religious factors that influence the uptake of HPV vaccines. Most of the studies showed that the willingness of adolescents to take HPV vaccine was significantly based on parental consent with up to half of the adolescents reported that they are ready to take the vaccines if approved to do so by their parents ​ [43, 47]​. In some communities in Northern and Southern Nigeria mothers did not want their daughters to take the vaccine at all due to cultural and religious concerns about increased promiscuity and infertility among their daughters ​ [33, 52, 53, 55] which is similar to results from other studies conducted in some African countries where cultural norms and religious beliefs have negatively impacted the HPV vaccine uptake [64]. Thus, interventions must be focused on engaging and educating religious leaders and community stakeholders to dispel misconceptions and promote acceptance by presenting HPV vaccination as a tool to safeguard the health of adolescents instead of a threat to religious and cultural values.

Additionally, nearly all the studies documented increased cost of the vaccine as the main economic factor impacting the uptake of HPV vaccine in Nigeria. Inconsistent government funding, scarcity of health insurance and unavailability of the vaccines were other barriers reported [49, 54]​. We found out that willingness to vaccinate significantly increased when the vaccine was subsidized or offered free. However, actual uptake remained low where the vaccination cost a fortune [33, 46, 51]. According to Murewanhema et al., [65]​ the cost of HPV vaccination is expensive in sub-Saharan Africa and without global partnership and funding, HPV vaccination will likely remain an unaddressed priority.

Furthermore, another important determinant we found was gender bias in HPV vaccination program. Olajide et al., [57]​ reported that healthcare workers considered the male gender as low-risk and that the vaccine was mainly recommended for the female gender. Research evidence has shown that HPV can affect up to 21% of any male site and can cause penile, head and neck, anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers ​ [66–68]. Thus, ​Han et al., [69]​ recommended the adoption of gender-neutral vaccination to address this issue. Involving the male gender in HPV vaccination will indirectly benefit the female gender through herd immunity and offers significant decline in anogenital diseases amidst the male population [74]. Focusing on education and expansion of HPV vaccination to boys and men is crucial in achieving public health goals and preventing the spread of the disease in the general population irrespective of gender [28]. We therefore recommend that government should formulate policies that will address the low knowledge level and misconceptions through the adoption of regular health education awareness programs that will focus on improving HPV vaccination awareness by engaging parents, caregivers, healthcare workers religious and community leaders.

While we acknowledge that the government of Nigeria has integrated HPV vaccination into the routine national immunization programme to protect about 7.7 million girls [70], we also encourage the government to embrace gender neutral HPV immunization programmes in the best interest of the public.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This review possesses several strengths that enhance its validity and relevance. The authors employed a transparent and systematic methodology consistent with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for mixed-methods systematic reviews [38, 39]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO, and the study was conducted and reported in line with the PRISMA guidelines [40]. In addition, a comprehensive search strategy was applied across nine electronic databases using tailored search strings adapted to each database. This approach improved the likelihood of identifying relevant literature and strengthened the transparency of the review process.

Another strength of this review is the inclusion of methodologically sound original studies. The Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies [41]. The MMAT enables the appraisal of different study designs within a single framework, allowing for consistent quality assessment across qualitative and quantitative studies. Overall, the included studies demonstrated acceptable methodological quality across the relevant appraisal domains, supporting the reliability of the findings generated through the synthesis.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the review attempted to capture studies conducted across Nigeria, many of the included studies were concentrated in Southern Nigeria, particularly in Ibadan and Lagos. This regional clustering may limit the generalisability of the findings to the entire country, especially to northern regions where sociocultural contexts influencing vaccination behaviour may differ.

Second, the evidence base was dominated by quantitative cross-sectional studies, with 13 of the 16 included studies adopting this design. Only a small number of qualitative studies were available. This imbalance may limit deeper exploration of contextual and behavioural factors influencing HPV vaccination decisions that qualitative research could provide.

Another limitation relates to the heterogeneity of the included studies. The studies differed in their design, methodologies, participant characteristics, and outcome measurements. Because of these variations, it was not feasible to conduct a quantitative meta-analysis, and the findings were therefore synthesised narratively.

The review may also be affected by publication and reporting bias. Only peer-reviewed studies published in English were included, which may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant research available in other languages or in grey literature.

Another limitation relates to the time span and population focus of the included studies. The studies included in this review were published between 2015 and 2025, a period during which HPV vaccination programmes, policies, and public awareness initiatives in Nigeria have continued to evolve. Consequently, findings from earlier studies may not fully reflect the current landscape of HPV vaccination uptake in the country. In addition, most of the available evidence focused primarily on girls, adolescent females, and their caregivers, with relatively limited research examining HPV vaccination among boys and men. This imbalance in the evidence base may restrict a comprehensive understanding of gender-inclusive vaccination perspectives in Nigeria.

The review also identified an important gap between the relatively high willingness to accept HPV vaccination and the consistently low levels of actual vaccine uptake reported across the included studies. Similar discrepancies between expressed willingness and real-world vaccination behaviour have been reported in studies examining HPV vaccination barriers in low- and middle-income countries [25, 26, 65]. One possible explanation for this gap is the influence of social desirability bias. Many of the included studies relied on self-reported measures of willingness or intention to vaccinate. In such situations, participants may provide responses that they believe are socially acceptable rather than reflecting their true intentions. Studies examining HPV vaccine hesitancy in Nigeria suggest that reported willingness does not always translate into actual uptake because of underlying social, cultural, and structural barriers [30, 33]. Consequently, the reported acceptance of HPV vaccination in some studies may overestimate real-world willingness.

Future research directions

Future research on HPV vaccination in Nigeria needs to build on the strengths of the existing quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies, while expanding the areas that have not yet been fully explored. One important direction is to generate evidence from communities that have not been well represented in the current literature. Most available studies were drawn from a few southern regions and expanding research to more diverse cultural and religious settings will help deepen understanding of how families across the country make decisions about HPV vaccination [33, 45, 56].

There is also a need for research that follows adolescents, parents and caregivers over time. As Nigeria continues to roll out HPV vaccination nationally, understanding how awareness, confidence and uptake change with increased exposure to the vaccine will be valuable for programme refinement [70]. Studies that explore real-life vaccination experiences, including how families access services and how they respond to communication from schools, health workers and community groups, would provide practical insights for strengthening delivery.

Another important direction is to explore sociocultural influences in more depth. Several studies showed that parental approval, religious beliefs and cultural expectations strongly influence vaccination decisions, but little is known about how these views are formed or how they vary within households and communities [43, 47, 52, 53]. Engaging families, community leaders and adolescents directly in research will help uncover the factors that build trust or create hesitation.

Research on boys and male adolescents is also needed. Since HPV affects both males and females, exploring how male vaccination is understood and accepted can support broader discussions about equitable vaccination strategies [2, 57]. Understanding the perspectives of parents of boys and the attitudes of young men themselves will help shape future policy options.

Finally, future research should explore practical factors that influence everyday access to HPV vaccination. This includes understanding how families perceive costs, how they navigate service availability and what conditions make vaccination easier or more difficult to obtain [33, 49, 55]. Gathering this type of evidence will support decisions about how to design programmes that are convenient, affordable and aligned with community expectations.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings of this SR point to several areas where policy action and practical programme design can help improve HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria. One clear implication is the need for stronger, more consistent public education. Many adolescents, caregivers and healthcare workers still have limited knowledge of HPV and the purpose of the vaccine, which affects confidence and willingness to seek vaccination [43, 47, 57]. National and community-level communication efforts that use simple, culturally sensitive messages delivered through teachers, nurses, faith leaders and trusted community figures could help address misconceptions and reduce ongoing fears about vaccine safety, sexuality and fertility [52, 53, 55].

Improving awareness must go hand in hand with making vaccination financially accessible. Cost was one of the most frequent barriers across the studies, and many families were only willing to vaccinate if the vaccine was subsidised or free [33, 49, 51]. Ensuring reliable government funding, regular vaccine supply and clear information on where and when vaccines are available can reduce missed opportunities and help families plan for vaccination without financial strain.

Strong engagement with parents and caregivers is another practical area for action. Across the studies, adolescents’ decisions were heavily influenced by parental approval [43, 47]. This means that effective HPV vaccination programmes need to involve families at every stage. Community discussions, parent–teacher meetings and targeted information sessions may help parents understand the benefits of vaccination and feel confident supporting their children’s decisions. As the national rollout continues, linking school-based vaccination with local primary healthcare centres can also support better communication, follow-up and record-keeping.

The evidence also highlights the value of including both boys and girls in HPV education campaigns. While current guidelines focus on girls aged 9 to 14 years, research shows that HPV affects men as well as women, which means that gender-neutral messages can help reduce stigma and support long-term acceptance of broader vaccination strategies when appropriate [2, 57].

Together, these findings show that improving HPV vaccine uptake in Nigeria will require coordinated efforts from government agencies, schools, health workers and community leaders. Policies that strengthen public education, improve affordability, increase access and involve families directly will be essential for closing the gap between high levels of willingness and low levels of actual uptake.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods systematic review shows that HPV vaccination uptake in Nigeria remains low despite high levels of willingness among adolescents, caregivers and women. Across studies, low knowledge, safety concerns, sociocultural beliefs, and the cost of vaccination emerged as the most consistent determinants shaping real-world uptake. These findings highlight the gap between intention and action, and they reinforce the need for targeted approaches that improve awareness, reduce erroneous ideas, and make vaccination more affordable and accessible.

Sociocultural influences, including parental approval, religious beliefs and gender norms, continue to play a central role in shaping decisions. Addressing these influences through community engagement and trusted information channels is essential for strengthening confidence in HPV vaccination. Economic barriers, especially vaccine cost and irregular availability, remain major obstacles, underscoring the need for sustained government support and reliable supply systems. The evidence also points to the importance of inclusive approaches that consider both girls and boys, given the burden of HPV-related diseases across genders.

As Nigeria expands routine HPV vaccination nationwide, ensuring equitable uptake will require coordinated efforts from government agencies, community leaders, schools and healthcare providers. Strengthening health education, promoting gender-neutral communication, and improving service accessibility can help close the gap between acceptance and actual uptake. By implementing context-specific strategies that respond to the realities identified in this review, Nigeria can make meaningful progress toward reducing cervical cancer and other HPV-related diseases, and toward meeting global targets for HPV elimination.
