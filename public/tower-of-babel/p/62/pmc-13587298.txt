Temporal deep learning for 28-day mortality prediction in critically ill ovarian cancer patients: a multicenter development and validation study using hourly vital signs
Authors: Chengling Wang, Li Liu, Jianguo Hu, Xingshu Li, Xingchuan Li, Qikun Zhu, Liang Yang, Ke Pu, Renlan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission face particularly grave prognosis, yet current prognostic models rely on static baseline characteristics and generic severity scores, neglecting the rich temporal dynamics of vital signs that may better capture physiological deterioration patterns.ObjectivesTo develop and validate an interpretable Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network integrating hourly vital signs and static features for predicting 28-day mortality in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study utilized MIMIC-IV (2008–2022) and SICdb (2013–2021) databases. Adult ovarian cancer patients with first ICU admission, length of stay ≥ 24 h, and complete hourly measurements of six vital signs (blood pressures, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation) were included. MIMIC-IV was split into training (n = 269) and internal validation (n = 115) sets, while SICdb (n = 154) served as external validation. A dual-pathway LSTM network integrated temporal and static features (age, BMI, SOFA score). Model performance was assessed using AUROC, calibration metrics, and compared against traditional ICU scores and five static-only machine learning baselines. Five-fold cross-validation and 100 repeated temporal ablation trials assessed robustness. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis quantified feature importance and temporal dynamics.ResultsThe LSTM model achieved AUROC of 0.845 (95%CI: 0.789–0.901) in training, 0.785 (95%CI: 0.655–0.915) in internal validation, and 0.767 (95%CI: 0.617–0.917) in external validation, significantly outperforming SOFA (AUC: 0.683–0.733), SAPS II (AUC: 0.743), SAPS 3 (AUC: 0.678), and OASIS (AUC: 0.659 − 0.569). Five-fold cross-validation confirmed stability (mean AUROC: 0.785 ± 0.018). Negative predictive values exceeded 94% across all cohorts. The LSTM achieved AUC improvements of 0.10–0.17 over static baselines, with repeated permutation trials confirming significant temporal contributions (all P < 0.001). Temporal ablation identified the 12–24 h window as most critical (ΔAUC = 0.062). SHAP analysis revealed static features dominated prediction, while respiratory rate and diastolic blood pressure were leading temporal contributors.ConclusionsThis interpretable LSTM model accurately predicts mortality in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients by capturing temporal vital sign dynamics, significantly outperforming traditional severity scores and enabling personalized risk stratification for clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13048-026-02174-y.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer remains one of the most lethal gynecological malignancies worldwide, ranking as the eighth leading cause of cancer-related death among women, with over 70% of patients ultimately experiencing disease progression despite aggressive multimodal treatment [1, 2]. When ovarian cancer patients require intensive care unit (ICU) admission due to severe complications, treatment-related toxicity, or acute organ dysfunction, their prognosis becomes particularly grave, yet this critically ill population remains understudied. Current prognostic assessment tools for ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients rely primarily on generic critical illness scores such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS II), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), which fail to incorporate cancer-specific factors and dynamic physiological parameters [3].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have revolutionized prognostic modeling in ovarian cancer. Machine learning models integrating clinical, laboratory, and imaging data have achieved superior diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional biomarkers such as CA125 and HE4, with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.90 [4, 5]. Random survival forest algorithms have demonstrated utility in forecasting progression-free survival in high-grade serous ovarian cancer, with concordance indices exceeding 0.75 [1, 6]. Li et al. developed a Support Vector Machine model for ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients achieving AUC 0.857, identifying red cell distribution width, bicarbonate, and chloride as critical predictors [7]. However, these studies predominantly utilized static baseline characteristics collected at a single time point, neglecting the rich prognostic information contained in temporal physiological dynamics during the critical first 24 h of ICU admission.

Significant knowledge gaps persist regarding prognostic factors for ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients. Traditional prognostic determinants include FIGO stage, residual disease after cytoreductive surgery, performance status, and BRCA mutation status, yet these represent static characteristics that fail to capture dynamic physiological deterioration during critical illness [8, 9]. Emerging evidence suggests that temporal patterns in vital signs during the first 24 h of ICU admission contain rich prognostic information, with time-series analysis of hemodynamic parameters significantly improving mortality prediction in other disease contexts [10]. Moreover, inflammation and immunonutritional biomarkers such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and prognostic nutritional index have shown independent prognostic value in ovarian cancer patients, yet their utility in ICU settings remains unexplored [11, 12]. The lack of studies integrating temporal vital sign dynamics with static clinical features represents a critical gap, as ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients experience rapid physiological changes that static models cannot adequately capture.

To address these limitations, we conducted a multicenter retrospective study utilizing MIMIC-IV and SICdb databases to develop and validate an interpretable machine learning model for predicting 28-day mortality in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients. We employed Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks to capture temporal dependencies in six vital signs measured hourly over the first 24 h of ICU admission, while simultaneously incorporating static baseline features including age, body mass index, gender, and SOFA score. To enhance clinical applicability, we utilized SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to identify critical time windows, quantify individual feature contributions, and reveal nonlinear relationships between physiological parameters and mortality risk. Our study is the first to comprehensively analyze temporal feature importance in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients using hourly-resolution time-series data and to develop an explainable deep learning framework specifically tailored to this high-risk population, potentially enabling personalized risk stratification and timely clinical interventions to improve survival outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective cohort study utilized two publicly available critical care databases: MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV) version 3.1 and SICdb (Salzburg Intensive Care database) version 1.0.8. MIMIC-IV encompasses de-identified electronic health records of over 360,000 patients from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston between 2008 and 2022 [13]. SICdb contains over 27,000 ICU admissions from Salzburg University Hospital, Austria, between 2013 and 2021 [14]. Both databases are fully de-identified and compliant with GDPR and HIPAA regulations. The collection of MIMIC-IV patient information was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, who granted a waiver of informed consent. The SICdb study received ethical approval from the Salzburg Local Ethics Committee with waiver of individual consent due to retrospective design and data de-identification. This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and reported following the TRIPOD guidelines.

Study population and inclusion criteria

Adult patients aged 18 years or older with primary ovarian cancer diagnosis were identified using International Classification of Diseases codes (ICD-9: 183.0; ICD-10: C56). Inclusion criteria required: (1) first ICU admission only; (2) ICU length of stay of 24 h or longer; and (3) complete hourly measurements of six vital signs (systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation) throughout the first 24 h following ICU admission (hours 0 through 24). The 24-hour observation window was uniformly defined as the first 24 h after ICU admission for all patients; no alternative sampling windows were used, and only patients who remained alive throughout this window contributed data, thereby ensuring that the observation period did not overlap with or include the time of death. Given that this study specifically aimed to develop a time-series deep learning model for temporal pattern recognition, complete hourly vital sign data were essential to preserve temporal integrity and sequential dependencies inherent in LSTM neural network architectures. Patients with missing vital sign measurements at any hourly time point were excluded from the primary analysis to ensure consistent temporal resolution, as irregular intervals or missing sequences would compromise the recurrent neural network’s ability to learn meaningful temporal dynamics. To evaluate whether this exclusion introduced selection bias, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by re-including excluded patients after applying last observation carried forward (LOCF) imputation for missing hourly values. The final cohort comprised 384 patients from MIMIC-IV and 154 patients from SICdb (Fig. 1). The MIMIC-IV cohort was randomly split into training (70%, n = 269) and internal validation (30%, n = 115) sets, while the SICdb cohort served as external validation to assess generalizability across healthcare systems and geographic regions.

Data extraction and preprocessing

Clinical data extraction encompassed time-series vital signs recorded hourly during the first 24 h following ICU admission (25 time points: hours 0 through 24) and baseline static features including age, body mass index, gender, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score measured at ICU admission. Time-series data were structured as three-dimensional tensors representing patients, time points, and features. Static features were standardized using z-score normalization fitted on the training set and applied to validation sets. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, defined as death within 28 days of ICU admission. Each patient was assigned a binary label: 1 for death within 28 days (non-survivors) and 0 for survival beyond 28 days (survivors). This label was paired with the corresponding 24-hour vital sign tensor and static feature vector to form each training instance. To characterize distributional differences between the development and external validation cohorts, standardized mean differences (SMD), Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were computed for all static and temporal input features. Features with |SMD| > 0.1 were flagged as exhibiting meaningful distributional shift.

Model architecture and training

A dual-pathway Long Short-Term Memory neural network was developed integrating temporal and static information. The architecture comprised two parallel branches: a sequential branch processing time-series data through two stacked LSTM layers with dropout, followed by a dense layer; and a static branch processing baseline features through two dense layers with dropout. The specific layer widths and dropout rates were determined by the grid search optimization described above, with the optimal configuration reported in Sect. 3.7. To assess sensitivity to architectural choices, alternative dense layer width configurations (narrow: 8 units; wide: 32 units) were additionally evaluated (Table S8). Outputs from both branches were concatenated and passed through a fusion layer (16 units) before final binary classification with sigmoid activation. LSTM architecture was selected over traditional machine learning models such as logistic regression or random forest because these conventional methods require aggregated static features and cannot capture temporal dynamics and sequential patterns fundamental to time-series modeling.

Model training employed the Adam optimizer with initial learning rate of 0.001 and binary cross-entropy loss. Class weights were calculated as: weight = n samples / (n classes × n samples per class). Early stopping (patience = 20 epochs) and learning rate reduction (factor = 0.5, patience = 8 epochs) were implemented. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted through exhaustive grid search over 54 discrete combinations: LSTM units ∈ {32, 64, 128}, dropout rate ∈ {0.1, 0.3}, learning rate ∈ {0.0001, 0.001, 0.01}, and batch size ∈ {16, 32, 64}. The optimization objective was validation AUROC evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation on the MIMIC-IV cohort. All 54 configurations and their corresponding validation AUROC values are reported in Table S7, and pairwise hyperparameter interaction heatmaps are presented in Figure S11.

Model evaluation and interpretation

Performance metrics included area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score. Calibration was evaluated using calibration curves and Brier scores. To assess the stability of performance estimates beyond a single train–validation split, 5-fold cross-validation was performed on the full MIMIC-IV cohort.

To quantify the incremental predictive contribution of the temporal component, the full dual-pathway LSTM was compared against five static-only baseline models (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, support vector machine, and multilayer perceptron) trained using baseline static variables (age, BMI, SOFA score) combined with the first recorded value (hour 0) of each vital sign. Pairwise AUROC comparisons between the LSTM and each static baseline were evaluated using DeLong tests.

Temporal feature importance was quantified through ablation studies with systematic time window masking. To evaluate the statistical robustness of ablation findings, temporal feature permutation experiments were repeated 100 times with different random seeds, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess whether observed AUC degradation was significantly different from zero. Model interpretability was enhanced using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework, calculating SHAP values across temporal and static features.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range; categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Distributional comparisons between cohorts were quantified using standardized mean differences (SMD), Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. DeLong tests were used for pairwise AUROC comparisons between models.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final cohort comprised 538 ovarian cancer patients: 384 from MIMIC-IV (training: n = 269; internal validation: n = 115) and 154 from SICdb as the external validation cohort (Fig. 1). In MIMIC-IV, exclusions included 31 patients for non-first admissions or multiple ICU stays, 5 for age ≤ 18 years, 64 for ICU length of stay < 24 h, and 121 for incomplete hourly vital signs during the 0–24 h observation window. In SICdb, 36 patients were excluded for non-first admissions, 12 for age criteria, 32 for insufficient ICU duration, and 63 for incomplete vital signs. The overall 28-day mortality rate was 13.2% (71/538), with 14.8% (57/384) in MIMIC-IV and 9.1% (14/154) in SICdb (P = 0.204; SMD = 0.119).

Baseline characteristics of study cohorts

Systematic distributional comparison of all input features between the two cohorts was performed using standardized mean difference (SMD), Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Table S1, Figure S1). Among static features, BMI exhibited the largest distributional shift (SMD = 0.681; KS P < 0.001; Wilcoxon P < 0.001), followed by SOFA score (SMD = 0.408; KS P < 0.001; Wilcoxon P = 0.037), while age showed a modest difference that did not reach the conventional threshold for meaningful imbalance (SMD = 0.144; KS P = 0.005; Wilcoxon P = 0.147). Among temporal features aggregated across all hourly measurements, respiratory rate demonstrated the most pronounced distributional divergence between cohorts (SMD = 0.994; KS P < 0.001; Wilcoxon P < 0.001), followed by diastolic blood pressure (SMD = 0.612), heart rate (SMD = 0.573), systolic blood pressure (SMD = 0.454), and mean arterial pressure (SMD = 0.332), all with statistically significant distributional differences (all KS and Wilcoxon P < 0.001). Oxygen saturation was the only temporal feature with negligible distributional shift (SMD = 0.038; Wilcoxon P < 0.001).

Temporal dynamics of vital signs

Temporal analysis of vital signs during the first 24 h post-ICU admission revealed broadly overlapping trajectories between survivors and non-survivors in both cohorts, with group separation varying by parameter and time point (Fig. 2A–B, Table S2–S3). In the MIMIC-IV cohort (327 survivors, 57 non-survivors), systolic blood pressure was consistently lower in non-survivors throughout the 24-hour window. The greatest separation was observed during hours 5–9 (e.g., hour 5: non-survivors 108.9 ± 17.7 mmHg vs. survivors 114.6 ± 20.0 mmHg; hour 9: 108.2 ± 16.3 vs. 115.4 ± 19.4 mmHg), though standard deviations overlapped substantially at all time points. Heart rate was persistently elevated in non-survivors (range: 93.4–97.8 bpm) compared with survivors (range: 90.4–94.5 bpm), with the most pronounced separation during the first 3 h (e.g., hour 0: 97.6 ± 20.7 vs. 94.5 ± 20.9 bpm; hour 2: 97.8 ± 19.7 vs. 92.5 ± 20.8 bpm). Respiratory rate showed modest but consistent elevation in non-survivors across the entire observation period (non-survivors: approximately 20.3–22.9 breaths/min; survivors: approximately 19.3–20.4 breaths/min), with the widest separation at hour 11 (22.7 ± 6.2 vs. 20.1 ± 6.1 breaths/min) and hour 17 (22.9 ± 6.0 vs. 20.0 ± 5.2 breaths/min). Diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and oxygen saturation exhibited minimal separation between groups, with nearly complete overlap of the mean ± standard deviation bands throughout the 24-hour window (e.g., DBP at hour 0: non-survivors 65.1 ± 13.2 mmHg vs. survivors 67.4 ± 14.5 mmHg; SpO2 at hour 0: 97.0 ± 2.6% vs. 97.3 ± 2.4%).

In the SICdb cohort, the non-survivor subgroup was notably small (n = 14 at hour 0, decreasing to n = 10–11 at subsequent hours), resulting in wider variability and limiting the precision of between-group comparisons. Heart rate demonstrated the most pronounced separation, with non-survivors maintaining consistently higher values (range: 85.3–96.4 bpm) than survivors (range: 71.3–85.5 bpm) across all 25 time points (e.g., hour 2: 92.0 ± 18.0 vs. 74.4 ± 17.0 bpm; hour 8: 96.4 ± 13.4 vs. 85.5 ± 16.3 bpm). Diastolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure trended lower in non-survivors, with the widest separation during hours 11–14 (DBP at hour 13: 48.2 ± 7.7 vs. 54.8 ± 9.5 mmHg; MAP at hour 13: 64.5 ± 11.4 vs. 72.1 ± 11.0 mmHg). Systolic blood pressure showed a variable pattern, with non-survivors exhibiting higher values during hours 0–2 (e.g., hour 0: 115.9 ± 16.1 vs. 112.7 ± 19.4 mmHg) but lower values during hours 11–14 (e.g., hour 13: 99.1 ± 24.0 vs. 104.9 ± 16.8 mmHg). Oxygen saturation was slightly lower in non-survivors throughout most of the observation period (non-survivors: approximately 95.5–97.7%; survivors: approximately 96.2–98.0%), with the largest absolute difference observed at hour 22 (95.5 ± 2.4% vs. 96.4 ± 1.8%). Respiratory rate trajectories largely overlapped between groups in SICdb, in contrast to the more consistent separation observed in MIMIC-IV.

Model performance and cross-validation stability

The LSTM model demonstrated robust predictive performance across all three cohorts (Fig. 3A, Table S4). In the MIMIC-IV training set, the model achieved an AUROC of 0.845 (95% CI: 0.789–0.901), with accuracy of 76.9%, sensitivity of 78.7%, specificity of 76.6%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 95.2%. Performance was maintained in internal validation, with AUROC of 0.785 (95% CI: 0.655–0.915), accuracy of 72.4%, sensitivity of 60.0%, specificity of 74.2%, and NPV of 92.5%. External validation in the SICdb cohort yielded an AUROC of 0.767 (95% CI: 0.617–0.917) with sensitivity of 71.4% and NPV of 94.3%, confirming cross-database transferability. AUPRC values were 0.504, 0.359, and 0.351 for training, validation, and SICdb cohorts, respectively, reflecting the influence of class imbalance on precision-based metrics. Calibration analysis showed Brier scores of 0.104, 0.104, and 0.080 for training, validation, and SICdb cohorts, respectively (Figure S2).

To assess the stability and robustness of performance estimates beyond a single train–validation split, five-fold cross-validation was performed on the full MIMIC-IV cohort (Table S5). The mean AUROC across five folds was 0.785 ± 0.018 (range: 0.762–0.806), with mean AUPRC of 0.359 ± 0.022, mean accuracy of 72.5 ± 1.7%, and mean sensitivity of 59.8 ± 3.2%. The narrow standard deviations across all metrics indicated stable model performance with limited sensitivity to data partitioning. The cross-validated mean AUROC (0.785) was consistent with the AUROC obtained from the original 70/30 internal validation split (0.785), supporting the reliability of the primary performance estimates.

Comparative analysis against traditional severity scoring systems demonstrated the superior discriminative ability of the LSTM model (Fig. 3B–D). In the MIMIC-IV training set, the LSTM (AUC: 0.845) outperformed SAPS II (AUC: 0.743, 95% CI: 0.666–0.821), Charlson Comorbidity Index (AUC: 0.693, 95% CI: 0.619–0.766), SOFA (AUC: 0.683, 95% CI: 0.589–0.778), and OASIS (AUC: 0.659, 95% CI: 0.566–0.753). This advantage was maintained in internal validation, where the LSTM (AUC: 0.785) exceeded SAPS II (AUC: 0.743, 95% CI: 0.616–0.871), Charlson (AUC: 0.738, 95% CI: 0.620–0.856), SOFA (AUC: 0.670, 95% CI: 0.548–0.791), and OASIS (AUC: 0.569, 95% CI: 0.444–0.695). In SICdb external validation, the LSTM (AUC: 0.767, 95% CI: 0.617–0.917) also outperformed SOFA (AUC: 0.733, 95% CI: 0.566–0.900) and SAPS 3 (AUC: 0.678, 95% CI: 0.504–0.852).

Incremental contribution of temporal features and critical prediction windows

To quantify the incremental predictive value of the temporal component, the full dual-pathway LSTM model was compared against five static-only baseline models (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, SVM, and MLP) using baseline static variables and the first recorded vital sign values as inputs (Figure S3, Table S6). In the MIMIC-IV training set, the LSTM (AUC: 0.845) significantly outperformed all static baselines (AUC range: 0.672–0.698; all DeLong P < 0.01). In internal validation, the LSTM (AUC: 0.785) maintained numerically higher discrimination than all static models (AUC range: 0.648–0.683), though pairwise comparisons did not reach significance (all P > 0.05), likely due to limited sample size (n = 115). In SICdb external validation, the LSTM (AUC: 0.767) similarly exceeded all static models (AUC range: 0.639–0.666; all P > 0.05). Across cohorts, the LSTM demonstrated an absolute AUC improvement of 0.10–0.17 over the best-performing static model. The statistical robustness of the temporal contribution was further confirmed through 100 repeated permutation trials (Figure S5): mean ΔAUC upon temporal feature ablation was 0.034 ± 0.012 in training, 0.047 ± 0.018 in validation, and 0.017 ± 0.020 in SICdb, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirming significance in all three cohorts (all P < 0.001).

Systematic time-window ablation studies identified the mid-to-late observation period as most critical for prediction (Figure S4A–B). In the MIMIC-IV training set, masking the 12–24 h window produced the largest AUC decline (ΔAUC = 0.062), followed by 18–24 h (ΔAUC = 0.036) and 12–18 h (ΔAUC = 0.027), while early windows showed minimal impact (0–6 h: ΔAUC = − 0.003; 6–12 h: ΔAUC = 0.004). This pattern was replicated in internal validation (12–24 h ΔAUC = 0.067). In the SICdb cohort, masking later windows paradoxically improved performance (12–24 h: ΔAUC = − 0.105), consistent with the substantial distributional shifts between cohorts documented in Sect. 3.1 (Table S1). Hourly ablation identified hours 15–19 as the most discriminative interval, with hour 19 (mean ΔAUC = 0.012 ± 0.003) and hour 16 (mean ΔAUC = 0.010 ± 0.006) exhibiting the largest degradation upon masking.

Feature-specific temporal importance analysis revealed dynamic evolution of vital sign contributions over 24 h (Fig. 4A–C). In the MIMIC-IV training set, diastolic blood pressure importance increased 40-fold from hour 0 (0.0005 ± 0.0005) to hour 21 (0.020 ± 0.011), and systolic blood pressure followed a similar trajectory peaking at hour 22 (0.017 ± 0.010). Heart rate showed a biphasic pattern with peak importance at hour 17 (0.013 ± 0.007), while respiratory rate maintained consistently high importance across all time points (range: 0.004–0.013). Oxygen saturation importance increased 16-fold from hour 0 to hour 11 (0.008 ± 0.005). These patterns were reproducible in both the validation and SICdb cohorts, though SICdb exhibited higher absolute importance values, particularly for diastolic blood pressure (peak: 0.031 ± 0.009) and respiratory rate (peak: 0.020 ± 0.006).

Model interpretability through SHAP analysis

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed to quantify both static baseline characteristics and dynamic temporal features. Global feature importance analysis revealed that static clinical features dominated the prediction hierarchy (Figure S6–S8). In the MIMIC-IV training set, SOFA score exhibited the highest mean absolute SHAP value (0.125 ± 0.156), followed by age (0.056 ± 0.069), BMI (0.049 ± 0.064), and gender (0.014 ± 0.039). Among temporal features, respiratory rate demonstrated the greatest contribution (0.009 ± 0.013), followed by heart rate (0.007 ± 0.009), SpO2 (0.006 ± 0.011), MAP (0.004 ± 0.006), DBP (0.002 ± 0.004), and SBP (0.001 ± 0.002). This hierarchical pattern was preserved in internal validation. Notably, the SICdb cohort exhibited a distinctive profile, with gender emerging as the predominant predictor (0.194 ± 0.035), substantially exceeding SOFA (0.070 ± 0.076), likely reflecting the gender-homogeneous composition of this population. Respiratory rate remained the most influential temporal feature across all datasets (training: 0.009 ± 0.013; validation: 0.009 ± 0.013; SICdb: 0.013 ± 0.018).Three-dimensional SHAP dependence analysis revealed complex nonlinear relationships between feature values, temporal evolution, and prediction contributions (Fig. 5, Figure S9–S10). In the SICdb cohort, heart rate exhibited pronounced temporal-magnitude interaction, with elevated values (> 100 bpm) during early hours (0–8 h) generating strongly positive SHAP values (0.02–0.04), while the same values produced diminished contributions during later time points (18–24 h). SpO2 demonstrated an inverse temporal pattern, with low saturation (< 94%) contributing maximally to mortality risk during the mid-observation period (10–16 h, SHAP: 0.02–0.04), but showing attenuated impact at boundary time points. Blood pressure parameters (SBP, DBP, MAP) displayed relatively uniform SHAP distributions across time, with extreme values consistently associated with elevated mortality risk regardless of timing. Respiratory rate showed the most complex three-dimensional structure, with both tachypnea (> 25 breaths/min) and bradypnea (< 10 breaths/min) contributing to positive SHAP values, amplified during hours 12–20. The MIMIC-IV cohorts demonstrated qualitatively similar SHAP patterns, with correlation coefficients of 0.82 and 0.76 when compared to SICdb, indicating reproducible feature-value-time interactions across diverse clinical settings.

Individual-level risk decomposition and clinical decision pathways

To illustrate the model’s decision-making process at the individual patient level, SHAP waterfall analysis was performed on representative cases from the SICdb cohort (Fig. 6). For a high-risk patient (ID: 334246, true outcome: death, predicted risk: 75.2%), static features contributed substantially: SOFA score (SHAP: +0.158), BMI (SHAP: +0.107), gender (SHAP: +0.105), and age (SHAP: +0.013). Critically, temporal features from specific time windows further augmented risk prediction, with each of the six vital signs contributing approximately + 0.105 SHAP units at their respective peak hours: DBP at hour 19 (55.7 mmHg), heart rate at hour 19 (68.8 bpm), MAP at hour 15 (67.1 mmHg), respiratory rate at hour 16 (12.8 breaths/min), SBP at hour 17 (103.4 mmHg), and SpO2 at hour 16 (93.6%). These time-specific physiological patterns elevated the prediction from baseline to 75.2%. Conversely, for a low-risk patient (ID: 669207, true outcome: survival, predicted risk: 9.2%), protective static features (SOFA: −0.140, age: −0.083, BMI: −0.044) and stable temporal vital signs (approximately − 0.001 each) resulted in a final low-risk prediction of 9.2%, demonstrating the model’s capability to integrate temporal physiological dynamics into accurate prognostic assessments.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

A grid search over 54 hyperparameter combinations (LSTM units: {32, 64, 128}; dropout rate: {0.1, 0.3}; learning rate: {0.0001, 0.001, 0.01}; batch size: {16, 32, 64}) was conducted to optimize model architecture (Table S7, Figure S11). Validation AUROC ranged from 0.741 to 0.785 across all configurations, with the optimal combination (LSTM units = 64, dropout = 0.3, learning rate = 0.001, batch size = 32) achieving the highest AUROC of 0.785. Pairwise heatmaps confirmed that this configuration resided in the highest-performance region across all hyperparameter projections, with LSTM units of 64 and learning rate of 0.001 consistently yielding superior performance. Sensitivity analysis of dense layer widths demonstrated stable performance across narrow (8-unit; AUROC: 0.778), current (16-unit; AUROC: 0.785), and wide (32-unit; AUROC: 0.781) configurations (Table S8), indicating that architectural choices for dense layers did not materially influence model performance. To evaluate the impact of excluding patients with incomplete vital signs, a sensitivity analysis incorporating LOCF-imputed patients was performed (Table S9). Model performance remained stable after imputation, with minimal AUROC changes across all cohorts (MIMIC-IV training: 0.845 vs. 0.837; validation: 0.785 vs. 0.777; SICdb: 0.767 vs. 0.762), suggesting that the exclusion of incomplete cases did not introduce substantial selection bias.

Discussion

This multicenter study developed and validated an interpretable LSTM neural network for predicting 28-day mortality in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients, achieving AUROC of 0.845 (training), 0.785 (internal validation), and 0.767 (external validation). The model significantly outperformed traditional ICU severity scores including SOFA (AUC: 0.683–0.733), SAPS II (AUC: 0.743), SAPS 3 (AUC: 0.678), and OASIS (AUC: 0.659), demonstrating superiority over both established and recently updated scoring systems. Compared with static-only baseline models, the full LSTM achieved an absolute AUC improvement of 0.10–0.17, with 100 repeated permutation trials confirming statistically significant temporal contributions across all cohorts (all P < 0.001). Temporal ablation identified the 12–24 h window as most predictive (ΔAUC = 0.062), with diastolic blood pressure and respiratory rate emerging as key dynamic predictors. SHAP analysis revealed that static features (SOFA: 0.125 ± 0.156; age: 0.056 ± 0.069) dominated prediction hierarchy, while temporal features provided complementary prognostic information. Five-fold cross-validation confirmed performance stability (mean AUROC: 0.785 ± 0.018), and the model maintained excellent negative predictive values exceeding 94% across all cohorts, enabling confident identification of low-risk patients suitable for de-escalated monitoring.

Our findings align with recent advances in machine learning for ovarian cancer prognosis. Li et al. developed a Support Vector Machine model achieving AUC 0.857 in internal validation and 0.750 in external validation, identifying red cell distribution width, bicarbonate, and chloride as critical predictors [7]. While their study utilized static laboratory parameters collected within 24 h, our LSTM approach captured temporal dynamics through hourly vital sign measurements, revealing delayed physiological deterioration patterns during the 12–24 h window. The complementary nature of these approaches suggests that integrating both static laboratory markers and dynamic vital signs may further enhance predictive accuracy. Chen et al. developed a random survival forest model for progression-free survival in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (C-index: 0.755), identifying tumor residual, HE4, FIGO stage, and CA125 as top contributors [1]. However, these oncologic factors become less relevant in ICU settings where acute physiological derangements predominate. Our study uniquely addresses this gap by developing ICU-specific prognostic tools tailored to ovarian cancer patients experiencing acute critical illness.

The identification of the 12–24 h window as most predictive contradicts conventional critical care paradigms emphasizing early stabilization. While general ICU populations prioritize early lactate levels and 6-hour resuscitation parameters, our ablation analysis revealed that early windows (0–6 h, 6–12 h) showed minimal impact (AUC drops: -0.003, 0.004). This delayed importance peak may reflect unique pathophysiology in ovarian cancer patients, including tumor-induced hypercoagulability, chemotherapy-related cardiotoxicity, and ascites-related respiratory compromise manifesting over hours [2, 15]. The SICdb cohort demonstrated inverse patterns with negative AUC drops for late windows. Systematic distributional comparison revealed substantial feature shifts between cohorts, particularly in respiratory rate (SMD = 0.994) and diastolic blood pressure (SMD = 0.612), suggesting that late-window temporal patterns learned from MIMIC-IV may not transfer directly to populations with markedly different physiological baselines, necessitating local model calibration. This heterogeneity underscores the importance of multi-center validation, as demonstrated by Xu et al.‘s systematic review showing that AI-derived biomarkers achieved pooled sensitivity 85% and specificity 91% for ovarian cancer diagnosis, with external validation significantly improving specificity (94% vs. 89%) [10].

Feature-specific temporal importance analysis revealed that diastolic blood pressure exhibited 40-fold importance increase from hour 0 to hour 21, while mean arterial pressure showed modest variation. These findings suggest that diastolic dynamics may capture progressive vascular dysfunction more sensitively than systolic or mean pressures, aligning with studies identifying cardiovascular complications as major mortality determinants in ovarian cancer patients [2]. Respiratory rate maintained consistently high importance, consistent with respiratory complications being leading ICU admission causes in ovarian cancer [16]. Oxygen saturation demonstrated 16-fold importance increase from hour 0 to hour 11, potentially reflecting progressive atelectasis, pleural effusions, or pulmonary embolism common in patients with ascites and hypercoagulability [15]. The predominance of respiratory metrics over hemodynamic parameters contrasts with general ICU populations, suggesting respiratory failure as a more critical mortality determinant in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients.

Global SHAP analysis revealed that static features dominated prediction hierarchy, with SOFA score exhibiting highest mean absolute SHAP value (0.125 ± 0.156), followed by age (0.056 ± 0.069) and BMI (0.049 ± 0.064). Among temporal features, respiratory rate demonstrated the greatest contribution (0.009 ± 0.013), though all temporal SHAP values were on the order of 10⁻³, substantially lower than static features. This finding warrants careful interpretation. Stratified vital sign trajectory analysis revealed broadly overlapping distributions between survivors and non-survivors with large standard deviations, particularly for blood pressure and oxygen saturation, explaining the limited discriminative signal available to the temporal pathway. Nevertheless, the collective temporal contribution was statistically confirmed through repeated ablation (ΔAUC = 0.034 ± 0.012 in training; all P < 0.001), indicating that while individual hourly features contribute modestly, their aggregate sequential patterns provide meaningful incremental discrimination that SHAP’s per-feature decomposition may underestimate. This hierarchical pattern—with organ dysfunction severity scores dominating—aligns with established literature [11, 16]. Liu et al.‘s multicenter study identified prognostic nutritional index and systemic immune-inflammation index as independent prognostic factors, with high PNI protecting against mortality [12]. Incorporating immunonutritional markers may further enhance our model’s performance. Three-dimensional SHAP dependence analysis revealed complex nonlinear relationships that traditional approaches would miss. Elevated heart rate during early hours generated strongly positive SHAP values, while identical values during late time points produced diminished contributions, demonstrating temporal-magnitude interaction effects. Low oxygen saturation contributed maximally during mid-observation (10–16 h) but showed attenuated impact at boundaries. Individual case analysis demonstrated transparent risk decomposition: high-risk patients accumulated substantial contributions from SOFA, BMI, and temporal features at critical hours, while low-risk patients benefited from protective effects of these features, addressing the “black box” criticism of deep learning [6, 17].

This study possesses notable strengths. First, the multicenter design incorporating geographically diverse cohorts enhanced external validity [5, 10]. Second, comprehensive temporal analysis with hourly resolution enabled detection of delayed patterns missed by static approaches [1, 7]. Third, SHAP integration provided interpretability revealing feature-time interactions facilitating clinical adoption [17]. Fourth, extensive robustness analyses—including five-fold cross-validation, comparison against five static machine learning baselines with DeLong testing, 100 repeated temporal ablation trials, hyperparameter sensitivity across 54 configurations, dense layer width sensitivity, and missing data imputation sensitivity—strengthened methodological rigor. Fifth, excellent negative predictive values enable identification of low-risk patients suitable for monitoring de-escalation. However, limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, retrospective design precludes causal inference. Second, small sample sizes limited subgroup analyses and prevented histologic subtype-specific models [9, 18]. Third, the model remains largely ICU-generic, relying primarily on vital signs and severity scores; the absence of cancer-specific variables such as disease stage, chemotherapy regimens, surgical debulking status, tumor markers (CA125, HE4), and molecular features (BRCA mutations) limits oncology-specific interpretability and clinical applicability [8, 19]. Fourth, although distributional shifts between cohorts were systematically characterized, discrepant temporal ablation patterns in SICdb indicate that SHAP-based interpretations may not be directly transferable across institutions with different patient populations and monitoring practices, and local recalibration is recommended before deployment. Fifth, focusing on 28-day mortality may inadequately capture long-term outcomes [16, 20]. Sixth, the exclusion of patients with incomplete vital signs, while necessary for temporal modeling integrity, may limit generalizability to real-world ICU environments where missing and irregular data are common; although sensitivity analysis with LOCF imputation showed stable performance (ΔAUROC < 0.01), more sophisticated imputation strategies or architectures tolerant of missing data warrant future investigation.

Clinical implications extend beyond mortality prediction to inform resource allocation, treatment decisions, and patient-family communication. The 12–24 h predictive window suggests deferring definitive prognostic discussions until temporal patterns emerge. High negative predictive value enables confident step-down unit management of low-risk patients. Transparent SHAP-based risk attribution facilitates shared decision-making. Future directions include prospective real-time validation, integrating additional predictors such as CA125, HE4, imaging features, treatment variables, and molecular markers to improve accuracy [1, 5, 21]. Investigating subtype-specific models may yield tailored prognostic tools [18]. Expanding to predict ICU length of stay and long-term survival would provide comprehensive prognostic information. Developing continuous risk monitoring systems could enable real-time stratification triggering interventions [10, 17].

Conclusion

This multicenter study successfully developed and externally validated an interpretable LSTM model accurately predicting 28-day mortality in ICU-admitted ovarian cancer patients using temporal vital signs and static features. The model significantly outperformed traditional and updated ICU scores (SOFA, SAPS II, SAPS 3, OASIS) and demonstrated robust cross-system generalizability. Temporal analysis identified the 12–24 h window as critical, with diastolic blood pressure and respiratory rate as key dynamic predictors. Comparison against static-only baselines and repeated ablation experiments confirmed statistically significant incremental contributions of temporal features. SHAP analysis revealed complex feature-time interactions enhancing clinical interpretability, though temporal SHAP values were modest relative to static features, reflecting broadly overlapping vital sign trajectories between outcome groups. Excellent negative predictive values enable confident low-risk patient identification for monitoring de-escalation. These findings establish a foundation for personalized risk stratification in critically ill ovarian cancer patients.
