Regulation of SNHG1, SNHG3, SNHG4 and SNHG5 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Dominika Podgórska, Marek Cieśla, Joanna Paśko, Bogdan Kolarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease in which long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) may contribute to immune dysregulation, but their regulation in circulating immune cells remains incompletely characterized. We measured the expression of four small nucleolar RNA host genes (SNHG1, SNHG3, SNHG4 and SNHG5) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 44 patients with RA and 20 healthy controls and examined their relationships with disease activity and autoantibody status. SNHG4, SNHG3, and SNHG1 were significantly lower in RA, whereas SNHG5 did not differ between groups. Reduced SNHG4 and SNHG1 were most pronounced in clinically active RA and in anti-citrullinated protein antibody–positive disease, and were observed in both rheumatoid factor–positive and rheumatoid factor–negative subgroups. Across patients, associations between SNHG levels and standard clinical or laboratory measures of disease activity were weak. These findings support compartment-specific regulation of SNHG lncRNAs in RA and suggest that SNHG1 and SNHG4 may contribute to future candidate molecules for molecular phenotyping. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovitis, progressive cartilage and bone damage, and extra-articular morbidity. Although disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and targeted therapies have improved outcomes, a substantial proportion of patients still develop persistent inflammation or structural progression, highlighting the need for biomarkers that better capture disease heterogeneity and for additional mechanistic targets1–3. Current models of RA pathogenesis emphasize coordinated interactions between innate and adaptive immunity and tissue-resident stromal cells, particularly fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), acting through pathways such as NF-κB and JAK/STAT4–6.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are RNA molecules longer than 200 nucleotides that do not encode proteins and have emerged as key regulators of gene expression at the chromatin, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional levels. In RA, lncRNAs are dysregulated in the synovium, FLSs, immune cells, and circulation. They modulate cytokine production, FLS proliferation/invasion, and innate– adaptive crosstalk, and they show promise as minimally invasive biomarkers and therapeutic entry targets7–9. LncRNAs act as scaffolds for ribonucleoprotein complexes, guides for chromatin modifiers, competing endogenous RNAs that sponge microRNAs, and modulators of RNA modifications (e.g., m6A)10–16.

Among these molecules, small nucleolar RNA host genes (SNHGs) are of particular interest because several family members converge on pathways central to RA immune-cell activation. SNHG1 promotes the proliferation and invasion of RA-derived FLSs through interaction with PTBP117. Reports on SNHG3 are heterogeneous, with reduced expression described in serum and synovium but increased levels reported in PBMCs, suggesting compartment-dependent regulation18–20. Direct RA data for SNHG4 and SNHG5 are more limited, but available studies link them to processes relevant to PBMC biology in RA, including JAK/STAT and NF-κB signaling, m6A-associated regulation, macrophage polarization, and miRNA-mediated control of inflammatory responses21–26.

By contrast, SNHG3 displays tissue- contextual regulation in RA. In a clinical cohort, SNHG3 was reduced in patient’ serum and synovium, discriminating RA from controls with 87,5% sensitivity and 84,4% specificity. Levels were also decreased in collagen-induced arthritis, and in lipopolysaccharide-injured FLSs, SNHG3 constrained inflammatory and oxidative phenotypes by negatively regulating miR-128-3p. Independently, an integrative “bioinformatics-to-bench” study found that SNHG3 is elevated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from RA patients and identified it as the strongest single lncRNA classifier (AUC ≈ 84.3%). SNHG3 levels were inversely correlated with ESR, consistent with compensatory upregulation during systemic inflammation. Notably, miR-128-3p promotes inflammatory signaling in RA immune cells by targeting TNFAIP3/A20 and intersects with redox and ferroptosis control in RA FLSs, providing plausible routes by which SNHG3-mediated downregulation of miR-128-3p could attenuate joint tissue injury. Together, these data support SNHG3 as both a mechanistic modulator in FLSs and a circulating biomarker candidate18–20.

SNHG4 illustrates how extra-articular mechanistic evidence can motivate RA-related hypotheses. In cerebral ischemia–reperfusion models, SNHG4 sponges miR-449c-5p to derepress STAT6, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and favoring an M2-like program. In lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory lung injury, SNHG4 inhibits METTL3, lowering m^6A on STAT2 mRNA, increasing STAT2 translation, and broadly dampening NF-κB-linked injury. Both axes: STAT6-dependent polarization and METTL3/m^6A-tuned interferon/NF-κB coupling are germane to the RA synovium, where macrophage states and FLS TLR/interferon responses shape disease trajectories, thereby nominating testable SNHG4 circuits in RA tissues21,22.

Finally, SNHG5 emerges as a molecule whose activity can be tuned by inflammatory cues. In endotoxin- induced acute lung injury, SNHG5 acts as a ceRNA for miR-205 to derepress COMMD1, a negative regulator that terminates NF-κB activity through ubiquitin- mediated turnover of Rel subunits and also constrains HIF signaling- mechanisms highly relevant to the hypoxic, inflamed synovium. Conversely, in status epilepticus models, SNHG5 upregulation in microglia is associated with enhanced NF-κB phosphorylation, underscoring its context specificity. Beyond immunity, osteoarthritis studies show chondroprotective SNHG5 activity (e.g., via the miR-181a-5p/TGFBR3 axis), which is mechanistically pertinent to cytokine- driven cartilage breakdown in RA. Collectively, these data position SNHG5 as a potential calibrator of NF-κB brakes and matrix homeostasis in joint tissues23–26.

These four SNHGs were investigated because SNHG1 and SNHG3 had already shown RA-associated dysregulation in tissue or blood compartments, whereas SNHG4 and SNHG5 are linked to immune-inflammatory pathways relevant to PBMC biology in RA17–19,22,25. We therefore expected measurable PBMC dysregulation in RA; decreased expression appeared more plausible for SNHG4 and SNHG5 given their predominantly anti-inflammatory profiles, whereas for SNHG1 and SNHG3 the expected direction was considered compartment- dependent rather than fixed. Accordingly, in this study, we measured the expression of the previously mentioned lncRNAs in PBMCs from patients with RA and healthy controls and examined their relationships with disease activity, RF status, and ACPA status5,6,10.

Patients and methods

The study included 64 participants. RA was classified according to the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria27. Serum rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) were previously measured by ELISA28. Disease activity was evaluated using the ESR-based DAS28 index29,30. Within the RA cohort, 22 patients had high disease activity (DAS28 > 5,1), 8 had moderate disease activity (DAS28 ≥ 3,2–5,1), 8 had low disease activity (DAS28 > 2,6 − 3,2), and 6 were in remission (DAS28 < 2,6). For analysis, patients were grouped as having active disease (DAS28 ≥ 3,2; moderate + high; n = 30) or mild disease (DAS28 < 3,2; remission + low; n = 14). The protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Rzeszow (No. 9/11/2020) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Exclusion criteria were infection or severe illness during hospitalization, other autoimmune disorders, connective tissue or bone tissue diseases, malignancy, and pregnancy.

Whole- blood samples were collected from the basilic vein. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation (Gradisol L, density 1,077 g/cm³; Aqua-Med, Lodz, Poland). Total RNA was extracted using the DNA/RNA Extracol Kit (EURx, Gdansk, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The detailed protocol has been described previously28.

Quantitative evaluation of lncRNA expression

A total of 250 ng of RNA was reverse- transcribed using the TranScriba Kit (A&A Biotechnology, Gdansk, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA samples used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) were diluted threefold. qPCR was performed using the SG qPCR Master Mix (EURx, Gdansk, Poland) in a QuantStudio 5 thermocycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an endogenous control. Thermocycling was carried out according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, with a total of 45 amplification cycles and an annealing/extension step at 56 °C for 30 s for SNHG1 and at 60 °C for 30 s for GAPDH, SNHG3, SNHG4, and SNHG5.

After each reaction plate, a melting- curve analysis was performed. Primers were designed using the online Primer-BLAST tool31, and their specificity was first assessed in silico analysis and then confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The detailed primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Relative expression was analyzed using the comparative Ct method (relative quantification, RQ). A calibrator sample was prepared from 10 randomly selected samples and used to normalize inter-plate variability. Data were analyzed using QuantStudio 5 Design & Analysis Software v1.5.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk W test. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD and were analyzed using parametric tests; otherwise, data are presented as median [lower- upper quartile] and were analyzed using non- parametric tests. Differences between two independent groups were analyzed using Student’s t- test or the Mann- Whitney U test. Differences among more than two groups were assessed using Kruskal- Wallis ANOVA followed by multiple- comparison post hoc analysis. Associations between continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Qualitative data are presented as numbers and percentages and were evaluated using the χ2 test with Yates’ correction. A Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, including the calculation of the area under the curve (AUC), was performed to assess the discriminatory potential of the examined markers. A logistic regression model was applied to perform the ROC analysis for the combination of the examined markers. P‑values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini–Hochberg method, and p-value ≤ 0,05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using STATISTICA version 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, California, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

The study enrolled 44 patients with RA and 20 healthy controls (HCs). The groups were comparable in age (RA: 57 years vs. HCs: 56; p = 0,37) and sex (women: 81,8% vs. 75,0%, p = 0,77). Among patients with RA, the median disease duration was 12 years. Autoantibody positivity was markedly higher in RA than in HCs (RF: 59,1% vs. 5,0%, p < 0,001; ACPA: 72,7% vs. 0%, p < 0,001). The inflammatory burden was greater in RA, with an ESR of 17 mm/h versus 8,5 mm/h in HCs (p = 0,021). At enrolment, overall RA activity was elevated (DAS28- ESR 5,06; CDAI 23; SDAI 23); joint counts and global assessments were SJC 2, TJC 6, VAS-PGA 50 [40–70], and VAS-PhGA 50 [29–70]. Detailed data are presented in Table 1.

In the disease- activity subgroups, 30 patients met the criteria for active RA and 14 for mild RA. Age, sex distribution, and disease duration did not differ significantly between the subgroups (all p ≥ 0,09). As expected, inflammatory and composite activity measures were higher in the active group: ESR 26 vs. 7,5, p < 0,001; DAS28- ESR 5,54 vs. 2,81, p < 0,001; CDAI 29 vs. 9, p < 0,001; SDAI 31,6 vs. 9,2, p < 0,001; SJC 5 vs. 0, p < 0,001; TJC 10,5 vs. 2, p < 0,001; VAS- PGA 69 vs. 35, p < 0,001; VAS- PhGA 60 vs. 20, p < 0,001. RF positivity was more frequent in the mild subgroup (85,7% vs. 46,7%, p = 0,034), whereas ACPA positivity showed a nonsignificant trend (92,9% in mild RA vs. 63,3%, in active RA, p = 0,09). Detailed data are presented in Table 2.

In the overall comparison between patients with RA (n = 44) and healthy controls (HCs; n = 20), three small nucleolar RNA host genes showed significantly lower expression in RA. Specifically, SNHG4 (median 1,63 in RA vs. 4,40 in HCs; p = 0,004, SNHG3 (1,41 vs. 1,63; p = 0,044), and SNHG1 (1,42 vs. 2,10; p = 0,001) were all reduced in RA, whereas SNHG5 did not differ between groups [Table 3]. Detailed calculations for Cliff’s delta, rank-biserial correlation and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals for comparisons between patients with RA and HCs were provided in the Supplementary Materials [Supplementary Table S2].

Discussion

In this study, we examined the expression of four small nucleolar RNA host genes: SNHG1, SNHG3, SNHG4, and SNHG5 in PBMCs from patients with RA and healthy individuals. We found that SNHG1 and SNHG4 were consistently downregulated in RA, with the most pronounced differences in clinically active and ACPA- positive disease and in both RF- positive and RF- negative subgroups. By contrast, SNHG3 showed only a modest reduction in RA and did not display robust subgroup- specific patterns, while SNHG5 expression was similar in patients and controls across all clinical strata. Correlations between SNHG transcript levels and conventional measures of disease activity or autoantibody titers were weak, suggesting that these lncRNAs are unlikely to serve as simple linear markers of inflammatory burden.

Our finding of reduced SNHG1 expression in PBMCs contrasts with previous reports of SNHG1 upregulation in RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes, where it promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion through interaction with the RNA-binding protein PTBP117.

These functional data position SNHG1 as a driver of pannus aggressiveness and a potential therapeutic target in the synovium17,32,33. The opposite direction of change in blood and synovial tissue supports a model of pronounced cell- type and compartment specificity in lncRNA biology: SNHG1 may be induced in stromal FLSs yet suppressed in circulating immune cells, reflecting distinct regulatory pressures in tissue versus blood34–37. In this context, decreased SNHG1 in PBMCs could represent a systemic signature of immune- cell reprogramming, whereas FLS- intrinsic upregulation acts locally to sustain synovial hyperplasia17,32,36. Our analyses, showing the greatest PBMC differences in active and ACPA-positive disease, are consistent with SNHG1 marking pathobiology enriched in more inflamed, seropositive RA rather than serving as a straightforward activity biomarker.

Previous studies on SNHG3 in RA are inconsistent. Some report reduced SNHG3 expression in serum and synovium18, together with protective effects against oxidative stress and NF-κB activation in injured FLSs via the SNHG3–miR-128-3p axis, whereas others describe increased SNHG3 in PBMCs with proposed diagnostic utility19,38. In our cohort, SNHG3 was only slightly lower in RA versus controls and did not show consistent associations with disease activity, RF status, or ACPA status. A plausible explanation is that SNHG3 is highly context- dependent and influenced by tissue compartment, cellular composition, and disease stage. If SNHG3 predominantly restrains pathogenic signaling in FLSs, modest alterations in bulk PBMCs may reflect secondary spillover from joint pathology or simply indicate that circulating leukocytes are not the main site of action. Larger studies incorporating cell-sorted PBMC subsets and paired synovial samples will be needed to clarify these possibilities.

SNHG4 emerged as the most consistently altered transcript in our analyses. We observed significantly lower SNHG4 expression in RA compared with controls, particularly in patients with active disease and in ACPA- positive subsets, and this effect was present in both RF- positive and RF- negative groups. Although direct RA data remain limited, studies from other inflammatory models link SNHG4 to pathways relevant to immune activation and resolution, including STAT6 signaling and METTL3/m6A-associated regulation21,22,39. Within this framework, reduced SNHG4 in RA PBMCs may weaken anti-inflammatory or pro-resolving circuits, thereby favoring cytokine production and sustaining synovitis40–42. The stronger signal in ACPA-positive disease is biologically plausible given the heightened innate- adaptive interplay and interferon- like signatures frequently reported in these patients19,43. Our data therefore highlight SNHG4 as a priority candidate for mechanistic studies in RA immune cells and as a potential component of composite blood- based biomarker panels.

SNHG5, in contrast, was stable across all comparisons. Given the context-dependent roles reported for SNHG5 in inflammatory and cartilage-related systems24,44–46, the absence of a PBMC signal does not exclude a role in RA; instead, it suggests that any disease-relevant regulation may be restricted to synovial, cartilage, or other local compartments, or masked in mixed PBMC populations25,47–51.

An important aspect of our results is the lack of strong linear correlations between SNHG transcript levels and clinical parameters such as ESR, joint counts, DAS28, SDAI, CDAI, and RF and ACPA titers. Rather than negating biomarker potential, this pattern suggests that these lncRNAs may act more as indicators of disease state or endotype than as direct readouts of momentary inflammatory intensity. In practice, SNHG1 and SNHG4 might contribute to multi- analyte panels, potentially in combination with selected microRNAs and proteins implicated in related pathways (e.g., PTBP1, COMMD1, and METTL3), to differentiate seropositive from seronegative disease or to enrich for patients with more active underlying biology. Prospective longitudinal studies will be required to establish their value for predicting flares, treatment response, or structural progression.

This study has several strengths, including a well-characterized RA cohort with detailed clinical and serological data, stratification by disease activity and autoantibody status, and standardized PBMC processing combined with rigorous qPCR methodology. However, several limitations should be considered. The relatively small, single-centre cohort limits statistical power and may reduce generalizability to broader RA populations with different demographic characteristics, disease durations, treatment exposures, and comorbidity profiles. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal changes in SNHG expression or its predictive value. Because the analysis was performed in bulk PBMCs rather than purified immune cell subsets, cell type-specific regulatory patterns may have been obscured, and interindividual differences in PBMC composition may have contributed to variability in transcript abundance and to the weak correlations with conventional disease activity indices. Although all samples were processed using a uniform protocol, detailed leukocyte subset data were unavailable; therefore, residual confounding by PBMC heterogeneity cannot be excluded. Future studies should address this issue by incorporating flow cytometry, single-cell transcriptomics, or CBC-based covariate adjustment. Another limitation is that only four SNHG lncRNAs were examined, without parallel assessment of downstream targets, protein-binding partners, or pathway activation. Although treatment distributions did not differ significantly across RA subgroups, the cohort showed substantial therapeutic heterogeneity related to the long mean disease duration (12 years), and the impact of specific therapies on SNHG expression warrants further investigation. Finally, mechanistic analyses could not be performed retrospectively because no biological material remained and the original study design did not include multidimensional pathway interrogation. Replication in independent cohorts and extension to synovial and cartilage compartments will be necessary to establish a comprehensive, compartment-resolved map of SNHG regulation in RA.

In summary, SNHG1 and SNHG4 were consistently downregulated in PBMCs from patients with RA, particularly in those with active disease and ACPA positivity, regardless of RF status. SNHG3 showed a modest reduction in RA compared with controls, but without reproducible subgroup-specific differences, whereas SNHG5 expression remained unchanged. These observations suggest that selected SNHGs may contribute to a more refined molecular characterization of RA and may have future value in disease subclassification and biomarker development. However, independent replication and larger, multicentre, longitudinal, and compartment-integrated studies, including analyses of synovial and cartilage tissues, are needed before SNHG-based measures can be considered for clinical application.
