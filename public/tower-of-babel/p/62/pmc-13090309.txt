Investigating the impact of human behavioural factors on the effectiveness of malaria interventions in malaria-endemic regions: A systematic review
Authors: Lilian Nkechi Agbo, Verner Ndudiri Orish, Danladi Chiroma Husaini, Polycarp Dauda Madaki, Orish Ebere Orisakwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a major public health challenge. Intervention effectiveness is heavily mediated by human behaviour. This review synthesizes evidence on how behavioural factors influence the uptake, use, and impact of malaria interventions in endemic regions. We conducted a systematic review of studies (2000–2025) from PubMed, Google Scholar, Dimensions, and HINARI. Using the PEO framework, we included studies on behavioural factors and intervention effectiveness from endemic regions. Quality was assessed with CASP checklists; narrative synthesis was guided by behavioural theory. A total of 59 studies were included. Evidence was concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (83%), with mostly cross-sectional designs (63%). Most studies (85%) exhibited a low risk of bias. Key themes studied were: (i) ITN use: ownership-use gap due to discomfort and misconceptions; (ii) Healthcare-seeking: delays and alternative care shaped by cost, access, trust; and (iii) Trust: distrust diverts care to informal providers. Protective determinants such as knowledge, perceived severity, and affordability were identified, whereas risk-enhancing determinants included discomfort, health system constraints, and perceived ineffectiveness. The findings indicate that malaria intervention effectiveness is fundamentally contingent on human behaviour. Technical strategies must address experiential barriers, build trust, and be locally tailored. Integrating behavioural insights is essential for sustainable control.Keywords: Malaria, Human behaviour, Intervention effectiveness, Health systems trust, Healthcare-seeking, Insecticide-treated nets

Introduction

Malaria is a life-threatening disease that is transmitted to humans by female Anopheles mosquitoes, which are infected with Plasmodium parasites (WHO, 2023a). The disease is of great interest worldwide because of its large burden in causing morbidity and mortality, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, which bears most of the global malaria burden (Chanda et al., 2015; WHO, 2011, 2023a, 2024). In Nigeria, 68 million cases and 194,000 deaths were attributed to malaria in 2021, which contributes to 31% of global malaria-related deaths in 2022 (Chilot et al., 2023; WHO African Region, 2023; WHO, 2023a), of which children under the age of five are mostly affected (WHO, 2024). Several interventions have been implemented over the years to control malaria transmission, such as the use of insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs), intermittent preventive treatment (IPT), seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC), indoor residual spraying (IRS), vaccination, and the use of larvicides to eliminate mosquito breeding sites (Chanda et al., 2015; Gimba and Bala, 2017).

Despite these numerous efforts, malaria continues to persist as a major public health challenge in several countries, including Nigeria (Mbacham et al., 2019; Nghochuzie et al., 2020). Although many factors have been shown to contribute to this challenge, one significant and sometimes disregarded factor is the practices adopted by individuals, families, and communities to prevent malaria (Monroe et al., 2021). This review is guided by established behavioural and health utilisation frameworks, such as the Health Belief Model and Andersen’s behavioural model, which posit that the uptake and effectiveness of interventions are shaped by a complex interplay of individual perceptions, enabling resources, and health system factors, including trust (Rosenstock et al., 1988; Andersen, 1995).

Human behavioural factors play an important role in the control of malaria transmission (Monroe et al., 2021; Casella et al., 2024). The World Malaria Report 2022 highlighted that the effectiveness of any intervention lies in the acceptance, correct use, maintenance, and retention of these interventions (WHO, 2022). For this review, we analytically distinguish between core behaviours (e.g. ITN use, treatment-seeking) and their determinants (e.g. knowledge, trust, access). Factors such as the usage level and proper maintenance of ITNs, despite high ownership, will determine the effectiveness of the ITN intervention (Casella et al., 2024). Similarly, the effectiveness of IRS depends on whether the sprayers are accepted into the house as well as not altering the wall after the spray (Opiyo and Paaijmans, 2020; Casella et al., 2024); likewise, successful case management relies on prompt care seeking by individuals, particularly high-risk groups such as under-five caretakers seeking care in an appropriate healthcare facility and completing the prescribed full drug dose. Also pregnant women going for scheduled antenatal clinics to receive IPTp and complete at least three doses as recommended by the WHO (Monroe et al., 2021; WHO, 2023b), Equally important is the role of healthcare providers in the effectiveness of these interventions, in terms of adhering strictly to national clinical guidelines for malaria diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, accurate and complete documentation of clinical registries and reports, correct management of malaria-related commodities in health facilities, and the consistent demonstration of both technical competence and strong communication skills (Monroe et al., 2021). The adoption and correct use of malaria interventions have been attributed to sociocultural and environmental factors which may vary between high- and low-risk regions (Ernst et al., 2016; Okpoko and Aniwada, 2017). Studies by Pulford et al. (2011) and Koenker et al. (2023) noted that behavioural factors affect the impact of malaria interventions.

However, for malaria interventions to be successful, the intervention must be available as much as also be consistently and properly used by communities (Orish et al., 2021). When the influence of behaviour is overlooked, policies risk misallocating resources and failing to achieve their intended impact, despite how technically sound the control strategies may appear (Bloland and Williams, 2002). This gap between intervention coverage and actual adherence indicates a significant research gap. A key component of this gap is understanding the role of trust, conceptualised here as a relational and systemic determinant shaping acceptance and sustained use alongside other social and cognitive factors.

This review seeks to address that gap by systematically bringing together evidence of how human behaviour affects the effectiveness of malaria interventions. The findings show how people's choices and actions can strengthen or weaken malaria programmes. Understanding these behaviours is important for creating strategies that encourage the better use of malaria interventions and make them more effective and sustainable. The review aims to assist policymakers, programme managers, and communities in designing malaria strategies that are effective and based on the needs of the community.

This paper presents a systematic review of studies published between 2000 and 2025 in malaria-endemic settings. It is guided by the following research questions:

RQ1: How do human behavioural factors, including specific behaviours (e.g. ITN adherence, healthcare-seeking) and their key determinants (e.g. knowledge, trust), influence the uptake, sustained use, and effectiveness of malaria control interventions?

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). A protocol for this review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD420251143145. The review aimed to collect, critically appraise, and synthesise published findings on how human behaviour influences the effectiveness of malaria interventions. This design was chosen because it enables a transparent and reproducible process of summarising existing knowledge (Pare and Kitsiou, 2017).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed based on the PEO (Population, Exposure, and Outcome) model (Munn et al., 2018), with components defined as follows: (i) Population (P): Communities and individuals residing in malaria-endemic regions, including both rural and urban settings; (ii) Exposure (E): Human behavioural factors that can influence the use of malaria interventions. Guided by the conceptual framework (see Section 1), these were categorised as (a) specific behaviours (e.g. ITN use, healthcare-seeking), and (b) key behavioural determinants (e.g. knowledge, trust, perceptions, social norms); and (iii) Outcomes (O): Indicators of malaria intervention effectiveness, including coverage, adherence, correct use, and reductions in malaria incidence or transmission.

The inclusion criteria applied were as follows:

•Study design: Primary research such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, qualitative research, and mixed-methods studies.

Study design: Primary research such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, qualitative research, and mixed-methods studies.

•Time frame: Studies published between 2000 and 2025, to indicate the period malaria intervention was scaled globally following the establishment of the Roll Back Malaria Initiative (Steketee and Campbell, 2010).

Time frame: Studies published between 2000 and 2025, to indicate the period malaria intervention was scaled globally following the establishment of the Roll Back Malaria Initiative (Steketee and Campbell, 2010).

•Language: Studies published in English.

Language: Studies published in English.

•Setting: Studies that are conducted in malaria-endemic regions.

Setting: Studies that are conducted in malaria-endemic regions.

Exclusion criteria included case reports, case series, editorials, and opinion pieces; secondary research (e.g. narrative or systematic reviews, although their reference lists were screened for primary studies); papers without full-text access; laboratory-based or entomological studies lacking behavioural components; and studies conducted in non-endemic malaria regions. Grey literature (e.g. theses, reports) was not systematically searched, as the review aimed to synthesise peer-reviewed evidence.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in four electronic databases: PubMed, Google Scholar, Dimensions, and HINARI. These databases were chosen for their broad coverage of global health literature and relevance to malaria research in low- and middle-income settings. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The search string used in PubMed was:

(“Malaria”[Mesh] OR “malaria”) AND (“insecticide-treated bednets”[Mesh] OR “ITN” OR “bed net” OR “bed nets” OR “long-lasting insecticidal nets” OR “LLIN” OR “case management” OR “malaria diagnosis” OR “malaria treatment” OR “intermittent preventive treatment” OR “IPTp” OR “malaria intervention∗” OR “malaria control” OR “malaria prevention”) AND (“patient compliance”[Mesh] OR “treatment adherence and compliance”[Mesh] OR “adherence” OR “compliance” OR “usage” OR “utilisation” OR “healthcare seeking” OR “treatment seeking” OR “care-seeking” OR “health-seeking behaviour” OR “health seeking behavior” OR “trust” OR “perception” OR “acceptability” OR “community participation” OR “community engagement” OR “health system trust” OR confidence).

Equivalent search strings were adapted for the other databases. The reference lists of all included studies were manually screened for additional relevant papers.

Study selection process

The study selection followed the PRISMA 2020 flow process (Page et al., 2021). All records were imported into Mendeley reference manager, and duplicates were removed. The screening was conducted by two independent reviewers (LNA and PDM). The process involved (i) title/abstract screening: article titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria, and (ii) full-text review: the full text of potentially eligible studies was retrieved and assessed in detail. Any discrepancies between reviewers at either stage were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (OEO). The results are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram for the systematic literature review.

A total of 3476 records were identified. After removing 1030 duplicates, 2446 records underwent title/abstract screening. A total of 209 full-text articles were assessed, and 59 studies met the inclusion criteria for the final review.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from included studies using a standardized, piloted form. Extracted information included: authors, year, country/region, study design, population, sample size, specific behavioural focus (categorised as ITN adherence, healthcare-seeking, trust, or a combination), key findings, and outcomes related to intervention effectiveness. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, referring to the original paper. This process ensured accuracy and consistency.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of included studies were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists, tailored for different study designs (cross-sectional, qualitative, cohort, RCT, mixed methods) (CASP, 2024).

Two reviewers independently appraised each study. Studies were evaluated on criteria such as clarity of aims, appropriateness of methodology, rigour of data collection/analysis, and ethical considerations. A scoring system (“Yes” = 1 and “No/Can’t tell” = 0) was used to calculate a percentage score. Studies were categorised as low (≥ 80%), moderate (60–79%), or high (< 60%) risk of bias. Disagreements in appraisal were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Data synthesis

Given the conceptual and methodological heterogeneity of included studies (e.g. diverse designs, behavioural variables, outcome measures), a meta-analysis was not feasible. A narrative synthesis approach was employed, structured by the review’s research questions and the behavioural categories defined in the conceptual framework. This process was adapted from (Pulford et al., 2011).

•Study characteristics: A descriptive summary of included studies (geography, design, population, behavioural focus) was tabulated. The geographical concentration of evidence was acknowledged and considered during synthesis.

•Thematic analysis for RQ1: Findings were organised thematically by behavioural focus (ITN adherence, healthcare-seeking, trust). Patterns, barriers, and facilitators were identified and summarised in a table.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 59 studies were included in this review. The evidence base is heavily concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (n = 49 studies, 83%), with multiple studies from Ethiopia (n = 13), Nigeria (n = 10), Uganda (n = 4), Gambia (n = 3), Ghana (n = 2), Kenya (n = 2), Rwanda (n = 2), and Malawi (n = 2). Ten studies (17%) were from other endemic regions, including India (n = 3), Myanmar (n = 2), Nepal (n = 2), Guyana (n = 3), Cambodia, Solomon Islands, and Vanuatu, providing points of comparison. The geographical distribution of included studies is shown in Fig. 2, illustrating the concentration of evidence in sub-Saharan Africa (83% of studies), with Ethiopia and Nigeria contributing the highest number of studies.

Study designs were predominantly cross-sectional (n = 37 studies, 63%), followed by mixed-method (n = 11) and qualitative designs (n = 9). There was one randomised community survey and one cohort study. The target populations and behavioural foci varied, enabling a review of behaviours across different demographic groups and intervention types (primarily ITNs and case management), as detailed in Table 1. The distribution of study designs presented in Fig. 3 highlights the predominance of cross-sectional studies (63%), followed by mixed-methods (19%) and qualitative designs (15%).

Insecticide-treated net (ITN) adherence and usage: Quantifying the ownership-use gap

A recurrent theme was the gap between ITN ownership and consistent use, which was quantified and contextualized by the evidence. While some studies reported very high usage, e.g. 91.67% in Malawi (Benito et al., 2024), 88% nightly in Uganda (Cohee et al., 2009), others revealed stark gaps despite high ownership, e.g. 99.5% ownership vs 27.1% use in Ethiopia (Hunde et al., 2023), 80.7% ownership vs 41.7% child use in Ghana (Konlan et al., 2019). The determinants of this gap were not merely binary but operated on a continuum of influence. Perceived effectiveness emerged as a powerful motivator, with those viewing nets as effective having nearly 5-fold higher odds of use (AOR = 4.956, Malawi; Benito et al., 2024). Common barriers discouraging use, reported in 19 studies, were physical discomfort (heat, itching) and perceived side effects. Practical challenges (hanging difficulties, poor net condition) were also significant (reported in 12 studies), with nearly one-third of nets in one Ethiopian study having holes (Berkessa et al., 2016).

Crucially, knowledge quality was a stronger predictor than general awareness; good knowledge of mosquito-borne transmission significantly increased odds of use (AOR = 3.44, Ethiopia, see Berkessa et al., 2016; AOR = 2.46, Ethiopia, see Hunde et al., 2023), while misconceptions (e.g. nets “expiring”, insecticide fears) directly suppressed it. Furthermore, intra-household dynamics were critical: use was consistently lower among school-aged children (30.7%, Uganda; Gonahasa et al., 2018) and in larger households with insufficient nets (net-to-family ratio ≤ 2 led to 20-fold higher odds of child use, Ethiopia; Zerdo et al., 2020).

Healthcare-seeking behaviour

The care pathway was typified by consecutive decision-making where behaviour went against advice. On the one hand, timely treatment-seeking (< 24 h) was mentioned in 12 studies and was up to 66.5% in Burkina Faso (Kaboré et al., 2022); on the other hand, the delays or lack of formal care were reported in 9 studies, and the delays of 3–4 days per episode were frequent (Asale et al., 2021). One of the important patterns (19 studies) was relying on alternative care (self-medication, traditional healers) and then seeking treatment at the facility, with 54% of gold miners in Guyana self-treating first (Olapeju et al., 2020). The form of formal provider utilized was different (17 studies) and depended on a complicated combination of trust, cost, and convenience.

The new discoveries measure the determinants of interest: living within 5 km of a facility (or less) augmented an immediate care likelihood by 2.7 (Thandar et al., 2015; Kaboré et al., 2022), and the intention of caregivers was the most potent individual predictor (aOR = 7.06, Norway; Oluchi et al., 2019). The tangible structural restriction was access distance (transport) in 14 studies, and cost-prohibitive, and 40% of women in Sierra Leone were first pushed to drug stores (Babawo et al., 2025). This early preference for informal care was usually a logical decision that was based on organizational constraints: stock-outs at facilities destroyed confidence and prompt seeking (Klootwijk et al., 2019).

Trust in the health system

Trust became an important cross-cutting determinant that has quantifiable effects on behaviour. The main role of trust was supported and defined by the results. Distrust served as a resolute obstacle at several points: fear of side-effects and rumours decreased adherence to mass drug administration in The Gambia (participation 67.9%; Dierickx et al., 2016), distrust in the quality of drugs used or the attitude of personnel directed care to the private sector. Six studies associated the choices in favor of the private pharmacy with the higher quality of drugs or services. Moreover, 6 studies mentioned that fear of side effects or rumour mistrust was a reason why people did not accept interventions or did not complete the treatment. On the other hand, strong trust in the implementing organisation was one of the facilitators of engagement in new interventions such as ivermectin MDA (Fehr et al., 2021). Social mediation of trust also occurred; the miners who trusted the way people treated them in Guyana were more likely to request formal testing (Yan et al., 2020). Qualitative and mixed-methods studies were almost the only ones to obtain these findings on trust, thus highlighting its contextual nature.

Methodological differentiation: How study design shapes evidence on behaviour

Cross-sectional surveys (37 studies) predominantly quantified the prevalence and strength of association of behavioural factors. For instance, they provided odds ratios for determinants like knowledge (AOR = 3.44 for ITN use; Berkessa et al., 2016) and proximity (AOR = 2.7 for prompt care; Kaboré et al., 2022). However, they could not establish temporal sequence or causation.

Qualitative studies (9 studies) and mixed-methods studies (11 studies) were essential for explaining the “why” behind the numbers. They uncovered the social meaning of behaviours, such as why heat is a barrier beyond discomfort, or how trust is broken by specific health worker attitudes. These designs were particularly critical for understanding complex constructs like trust, norms, and the decision-making process in care-seeking.

The single RCT and cohort study provided stronger evidence for causal relationships and behavioural dynamics over time. The cohort study in Uganda (Rek et al., 2020), for example, uniquely demonstrated how ITN non-adherence increases as transmission declines, a temporal pattern that cross-sectional studies could not capture.

This methodological triangulation strengthens the synthesis: quantitative data show what behaviours are prevalent and their statistical associations, while qualitative data explain the mechanisms and context behind these patterns.

Protective determinants

Accessibility and affordability were the most reported facilitators (22 studies for ITNs, 15 for care-seeking). Knowledge and awareness (19 studies), particularly quality knowledge, e.g. understanding mosquito-borne transmission (Berkessa et al., 2016), rather than general awareness, were consistently linked to better practices. Perceived severity (18 studies) and perceived effectiveness (17 studies) were strong motivators, with the latter increasing ITN use odds nearly 5-fold in Malawi (Benito et al., 2024). Social influence (10 studies) and demographic characteristics (e.g. pregnancy, ANC attendance) were also significant.

Risk-enhancing determinants

Perceived discomfort from ITNs (e.g. heat) was the most cited barrier to use (13 studies). Health system constraints (stock-outs, long waits, poor attitudes) directly discouraged timely care-seeking (9 studies). Socio-economic barriers presented a paradox: while poverty limited access, some wealthier households felt less need for ITNs (7 studies) (Ouattara et al., 2011). Perceived ineffectiveness (7 studies) and fear of side effects (6 studies) undermined trust and adoption.

Interaction of determinants and inferred causal pathways

Analysing the determinants collectively reveals recurring interactive pathways that influence behavioural outcomes. Two key patterns are evident.

•The knowledge action gap mediated by experience: High awareness and knowledge of malaria were frequently reported but poorly predictive of consistent intervention use on their own. The translation of knowledge into action was powerfully mediated by experiential factors. For instance, knowledge of ITN benefits was overridden by immediate perceptions of heat and discomfort (Taremwa et al., 2017; Benito et al., 2024), while knowledge of appropriate treatment was bypassed by past successful experiences with self-medication or negative experiences with health facility stock-outs (Das et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2020).

Comparative and contextual analysis of behavioural determinants

A comparative study showed that there is a pronounced geographical and contextual heterogeneity in behavioural drivers, which fundamentally qualifies a priori conclusions on human behaviour in malaria control.

•Sub-Saharan Africa intra-regional variation: Strong behavioural intentions based on perceived severity and self-efficacy were more often reported in the studies in East Africa (e.g. Rwanda; Asingizwe et al., 2019). Structural obstacles (cost, access) and experience-related elements (heat discomfort) were more significantly reported in West Africa (e.g. Nigeria, Ghana, Sierra Leone). In Ethiopia, there were invariably marked discrepancies between intent-to-own LLINs and low utilisation due to the region-specific practices (beliefs about net expiration, conical net preference, large household sizes).

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 59 studies to examine how human behavioural factors influence the effectiveness of malaria interventions (RQ1) and to identify key behavioural determinants of transmission risk (RQ2). The findings confirm that the success of technically sound interventions, ITNs, case management, IRS, is fundamentally mediated by human agency, social context, and health system interactions. Moving beyond a simple inventory of barriers and facilitators, our analysis, framed by health behaviour theories (e.g. Health Belief Model, Andersen’s model), reveals three critical, interlinked insights: (i) the central role of perceived value and experience over knowledge alone; (ii) trust as a systemic determinant shaping the entire care pathway; and (iii) the need for contextually differentiated strategies given geographical and socio-economic variation in behavioural drivers.

Interpreting key findings through a behavioural lens

The ITN adoption-use gap: Beyond ownership

One of the most striking results is that there is always a gap between the ownership and regular use of ITN (Pulford et al., 2011). The lack of awareness contributes less to this gap, whereas the experience and perception contribute more. Knowledge on malaria and its severity is common but not enough to sustain its use. Rather, behaviour is an indication of a perceived balance between perceived benefits and perceived barriers as postulated by the Health Belief Model (Rosenstock et al., 1988). Use is highly predicted by perceived effectiveness (OR = 4.956, Benito et al., 2024) but immediate barriers, especially, heat, discomfort, and inconvenience, are always stronger than abstract risk awareness.

Our synthesis also demonstrates that action-related cues are likely negative (high mosquito nuisance) and can deteriorate with reducing transmission, resulting in complacency (Atkinson et al., 2009; Rek et al., 2020). There is also qualitative evidence that focuses on misconceptions regarding insecticides, alternative use of nets and low self-efficacy in weakening consistent use. The self-confidence of the skills to use nets properly has proved to be one of the motivators of compliance in the environment (Babalola et al., 2022). Collectively, these results suggest that ITN programmes should no longer be distribution-based and risk messaging but instead should have an emphasis on comfort, usability, practical skills, and community participation to maintain use.

The healthcare-seeking pathway: A sequence shaped by trust and access

Malaria healthcare seeking is more of a series of steps than a single decision and it consists of symptom identification, physician selection and medication compliance. The delays usually start at the stage of understanding the symptoms, as cultural interpretation of the fever and the perceived lack of severity trigger the lack of urgency (Tynan et al., 2011; Mitiku and Assefa, 2017). Consequently, self-medication and conventional care can be the first-time response.

The provider choice is a result of the interaction of the structural and behavioural factors, which are in line with the behavioural model of Andersen (1995). Affordability and proximity are the most important enabling factors, but trust has a strong influence on decisions. Proximity is a major narrowing device (Das and Ravindran, 2010), yet mistrust of public facilities, such as stock-outs, perceived poor-quality drugs or negative personnel attitudes, will often lead to the use of the privately-owned pharmacies or informal providers, despite them not being the best choices in certain cases (Millar et al., 2014; Ogunwale et al., 2024). This distraction undermines proper management of cases.

Adherence and satisfactory treatment are not guaranteed even under the condition of access to relevant treatment. The awareness of the value of treatment completion enhances compliance (Yan et al., 2020), especially when presented as a social responsibility to safeguard other people (Jaiteh et al., 2021). On the whole, these results indicate that interventions focused on treatment adherence cannot work without addressing upstream behavioural and structural obstacles, in particular trust and access achieved by means of credible service delivery and respectful interactions between the providers and their patients.

The centrality and complexity of trust

Trust emerged not merely as an isolated factor but as a cross-cutting relational and systemic determinant that permeates both prevention and treatment behaviours. It influenced whether community sprayers were allowed into homes for IRS, whether ITNs were perceived as safe, and whether formal healthcare was sought. Our synthesis supports the conceptualization of trust as multi-dimensional, encompassing competence, integrity, and fiduciary responsibility (Gilson, 2003). Breaches in any dimension, such as stock-outs (systemic failure) or poor staff attitudes (relational failure), had cascading effects. This finding justifies the review’s specific focus on trust over related constructs like participation; while community engagement is crucial, it is often a mechanism to build trust, which is the foundational currency required for intervention acceptance and sustained use.

Moving from knowledge to ideation: A synthesis of psychosocial drivers

One of the most important lessons that we have learned during the synthesis is how little informational knowledge alone has the ability to influence behaviour and this claim has been confirmed by a number of studies in which high awareness has been accompanied by poor practice (e.g. Adebayo et al., 2014; Ovadje and Nriagu, 2016). Ideation, or the conglomeration of the psychosocial variables of perceived risk, social norms, self-efficacy, and attitudes, can offer a more explanatory concept (Olapeju et al., 2022). We consider the support of this model strong: in Rwanda, behavioural intentions were predicted by perceived severity, self-efficacy, and norms (Asingizwe et al., 2019); in Guyanese miners, a composite ideation score was positively correlated with care-seeking and testing (Olapeju et al., 2022). This is the reason why information campaigns do not work when they fail to address negative social norms, low self-efficacy, and attitudinal barriers at the same time (such as perceived ineffectiveness).

Geographical and contextual heterogeneity in behavioural drivers

The geographical concentration of evidence in sub-Saharan Africa, specifically Ethiopia and Nigeria, is, on the one hand, very informative, but on the other hand, requires careful generalizability of the results. In this area, the trends were different. As an example, positive determinants such as self-efficacy and perceived effectiveness were more often reported in East African studies (e.g. Rwanda) and structural barriers and discomfort were found in West African studies. Asian and Pacific studies focused on special contexts, including forest-going in Cambodia or migrant labour in Guyana, that posed special behavioural issues (e.g. mobility constrained the use of ITNs). Heterogeneity emphasizes the fact that on one hand the important behavioural principles (the role of trust, the benefit as barrier ratio) are universal, on the other hand they are local. Effective programming should be therefore guided by local behavioural diagnostics as opposed to general methods.

Limitations

The limitations to this review are a number of factors that qualify the interpretation of the findings. First, cross-sectional studies (63%) were prevalent in terms of evidence base, because they can determine association but not causality between behavioural factors and outcomes. Secondly, the over-representation of sub-Saharan African studies (83%) implies that the synthesis is best applied to African high-burden settings and may not be generalized to all endemic settings. Thirdly, most studies use the self-reported data, which may bring in the social desirability and recall bias. Fourthly, the conceptual and methodological heterogeneity of the studies included, though beneficial, prohibited the use of meta-analysis and necessitated a narrative synthesis, which is more prone to subjective interpretation. Lastly, despite an extensive search, the review was limited to English-language publications and may not have fully captured grey literature, potentially leading to the omission of relevant perspectives. These restrictions underscore the importance of additional longitudinal studies with mixed-methods research in different geographies to learn more about causality and context-specific dynamics.

Implications for policy and programme design

The findings can be translated into a number of practical policy and practice-based recommendations on malaria:

•Design for experience, not just efficacy: Product developers and programme managers must prioritize user-centred design for malaria tools. This includes developing more comfortable, durable, and aesthetically acceptable ITNs and ensuring treatment regimens are simple and affordable.

•Invest in trust-building as a core programme component: Malaria programmes should systematically monitor and actively build trust. This involves ensuring consistent commodity supply, training health workers in communication and empathy, and creating transparent feedback mechanisms for communities.

Conclusions

It has been unanimously established in this review that human behaviour is inseparably connected to the effectiveness of malaria interventions. Technical excellence in intervention delivery is required but not enough; to realise impact, interventions need to be delivered with a profound perspective of behavioural pathways that determine intervention acceptance, correct use and maintenance. The lack of coherence between ownership and use, the lack of coherence between the onset of symptoms and effective treatment, are essentially behavioural and systemic issues. Sealing these gaps requires behavioural knowledge to be firmly incorporated into designing, implementing and assessing malaria control programmes. Finally, eradicating malaria needs more than just the fight against the parasite, but empowerment and involvement of people and communities in the front lines of malaria transmission.
