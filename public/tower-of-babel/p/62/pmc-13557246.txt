Evaluation of Stress, IL-18, and IL-22 Levels and Their Interrelationships in Patients with Seborrheic Dermatitis
Authors: Lütfi Işık, Müzeyyen Gönül, Selda Pelin Kartal, Funda Güçel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis with multifactorial pathogenesis. Cytokines such as IL-18 and IL-22 are implicated in inflammatory and stress-related pathways, but their roles in SD remain unclear.ObjectivesTo investigate serum IL-18 and IL-22 levels in SD patients, evaluate their relationship with disease severity and perceived stress, and explore their potential relevance to SD pathophysiology.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 46 SD patients and 42 healthy controls were included. Disease severity was assessed using the Seborrheic Dermatitis Area Severity Index, and stress with the Perceived Stress Scale-10. Serum cytokine levels were measured by ELISA. Group comparisons and correlation analyses between cytokines, disease severity, and stress were performed.ResultsPatients had significantly higher stress scores than controls (mean 19.8 ± 4.6 vs 13.7 ± 4.2). Serum IL-22 levels were elevated in SD (median 369.3 vs 143.1 pg/mL; P<0.001). A cutoff of 202.8 pg/mL for IL-22 distinguished patients from controls, with 100% sensitivity and 97.6% specificity. IL-18 levels did not differ significantly between groups. IL-18 and IL-22 showed a moderate negative correlation (r=−0.409; P=0.005). Stress scores correlated positively with disease severity (r=0.336; P=0.022), and IL-22 correlated with stress across all participants (r=0.541; P<0.001).ConclusionsElevated serum IL-22 levels observed in patients with SD suggest a possible involvement of IL-22 in SD-related inflammatory processes. The observed associations between IL-22 and perceived stress support a potential interaction between immunological and psychosocial factors in SD. These findings should be considered exploratory and warrant confirmation in larger studies.Keywords: Seborrheic dermatitis, Stress, Cytokines, Interleukin-18, Interleukin-22

Introduction

Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a chronic, inflammatory skin disease characterized by erythema and yellow oily squames, which may be accompanied by pruritus, with episodes and remissions. Although the etiopathogenesis of the disease is still not fully elucidated, SD is considered a multifactorial disease involving interactions among sebaceous activity, microbial colonization, host immune responses, and environmental or psychological triggers. Among these factors, Malassezia species and psychological stress are widely recognized as major contributors to disease onset and exacerbation. Stress-related activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis may promote cutaneous inflammation through neuroimmune signaling and modulation of cytokine responses [1]. The pathogenic role of Malassezia in SD is supported by its preferential localization in lipid-rich skin regions, detection within lesional areas, and the clinical effectiveness of anti-fungal treatments [2].

Recent experimental studies have demonstrated that IL-18 levels increase significantly in ex vivo human skin models exposed to Malassezia in oily environment. Various Malassezia species have also been shown to activate the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and increase IL-22 production [3–5]. In addition, stress has also been associated with cytokine-mediated immune modulation, including pathways involving IL-18 and potentially IL-22 [6,7]. Collectively, these findings suggest that IL-18 and IL-22 may contribute to SD pathogenesis. While multiple cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-17 have previously been investigated in SD pathogenesis, the potential roles of IL-18 and IL-22 in SD have not been directly evaluated [8–10].

Interleukin-18 is a member of the proinflammatory IL-1 cytokine family. It is expressed in keratinocytes, macrophages, Langerhans cells, and dendritic cells and activated through NLRP3 inflammasome. Beyond its established involvement in infectious, autoimmune, and inflammatory diseases, IL-18 has also been implicated in several inflammatory dermatoses, including psoriasis, atopic dermatitis (AD), and allergic contact dermatitis (ACD) [11–13]. Importantly, experimental and clinical observations suggest that psychological stress may enhance IL-18 production via the HPA axis [6].

Interleukin-22 is a cytokine belonging to the IL-10 cytokine family. In contrast to IL-18, which is primarily produced by keratinocytes and acts on lymphocytes, IL-22 signals from lymphocytes to keratinocytes. IL-22 promotes epidermal hyperplasia, modulates keratinocyte differentiation, and enhances antimicrobial defense mechanisms [14]. Experimental studies suggest that IL-22 may exert both proinflammatory and regulatory effects through modulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation [1,15,16]. Increased IL-22 expression in skin and serum has been reported in several inflammatory skin diseases, including psoriasis, AD, and ACD, suggesting a broader role in epithelial immune regulation rather than disease-specific activity [17–19]. Furthermore, acanthosis, a histopathological feature to which IL-22 has been shown to contribute, is also observed in SD, similar to other papulosquamous dermatoses [20].

Emerging evidence indicates that IL-18 and IL-22 may functionally interact through inflammasome-related pathways. Experimental studies suggest bidirectional regulatory mechanisms involving NLRP3 activation, although conflicting findings have also been reported depending on tissue context and inflammatory environment [16,21,22]. Taken together, these observations raise the possibility of an immunological axis linking Malassezia-induced inflammasome activation and stress-related immune modulation through IL-18 and IL-22 signaling pathways.

Despite the recognized importance of both Malassezia colonization and psychological stress as key triggering factors in SD, and despite experimental evidence linking these factors to IL-18 and IL-22 regulation, the relationship between these cytokines and SD has not previously been investigated. Moreover, existing studies examining stress-related effects on these cytokines are largely derived from animal models, and their clinical relevance in humans remains unclear.

Objectives

The study aimed to investigate serum IL-18 and IL-22 levels in patients with SD, evaluate their associations with disease severity, perceived stress, and their interrelationship, and explore potential interactions between immunological and psychosocial mechanisms in SD.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional study between August 2023 and February 2024. It was approved by the Ankara Etlik City Hospital Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki Principles [08.02.2021, GO 104/01]. The study included two groups: an experimental group consisting of patients diagnosed with SD and a control group consisting of healthy individuals. The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.0.10 software, and based on a 95% confidence level, 90% power, and a recommended effect size of d=0.80, a minimum sample size of 78 participants (39 in each group) was determined to be sufficient. The experimental group included 46 SD patients, aged between 18 and 65 years, who presented to the Dermatology Department of Ankara Etlik City Hospital. The control group included 42 age- and sex-matched healthy individuals. All participants were of Caucasian ethnicity. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation in the study.

All patients were clinically diagnosed with seborrheic dermatitis by an experienced dermatologist based on established clinical criteria. Patients with clinical features suggestive of psoriasis, sebopsoriasis, or other papulosquamous dermatoses were excluded. Particular attention was paid to findings suggestive of psoriasis, such as sharply demarcated plaques, typical psoriatic scaling, extensor surface involvement, nail changes, and a personal or family history of psoriasis. Dermoscopic evaluation was performed when clinically indicated to further support clinical assessment.

Exclusion criteria for the experimental group included: age <18 or >65; pregnancy or breastfeeding; receipt of topical treatment for SD within the preceding two weeks or systemic treatment for SD within the preceding month; presence of any other inflammatory skin disease; diagnosis of any autoimmune or chronic inflammatory disease such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal failure, liver disease, thyroid disorders, or malignancy; use of any systemic or topical medication that could influence inflammatory or immune responses.

Measures

The participants’ demographic data, including age, sex, smoking and alcohol use, and medical history were recorded. Additionally, information was gathered regarding the onset and duration of SD, the presence of family history of SD, and any previous treatment for SD. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height and weight, and participants were classified according to BMI as normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), or obese (≥30.0 kg/m2).

Disease severity was assessed using the Seborrheic Dermatitis Area and Severity Index (SDASI), which is a scoring system developed by Cömert and colleagues. It assesses erythema, desquamation, and itching in nine anatomical regions (forehead, scalp, nasolabial area, eyebrows, postauricular area, auricular area, intermammary area, back, and cheeks or chin). Each region is scored from 0 to 3 (0; none, 1; mild, 2; moderate, and 3; severe), and these scores are multiplied by a constant representing the region’s surface area. The total SDASI score is obtained by summing these weighted scores, with a maximum score of 12.6. A score of 4 or higher, or involvement of three or more anatomical regions, indicates moderate-to-severe disease [23].

Stress levels were assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)-10, a validated tool designed to measure stress perception in individuals. The scale includes 10 items, with each item rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Four items are reverse-scored. The PSS-10 produces a total score ranging from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher perceived stress. According to established cutoff points, PSS-10 scores were categorized as low (0–13), moderate (14–26), or high stress (≥27) [24].

Sample Collection, Biochemical and Molecular Analysis

Venous blood samples were collected from all participants. After collection, the blood samples were centrifuged, and the serum was separated. The serum samples were stored at −80° C until the analysis day. The levels of IL-18 and IL-22 in the serum samples were measured using the Human Interleukin 18 and Human Interleukin 22 ELISA kits (Elabscience®, E-EL-H0253 and E-EL-H0106). The sensitivity of the kits was 6.5 pg/mL and 5.7 pg/mL, respectively. The measurements were performed using a Microplate Reader (BIO-TEK EL X 800) and an Auto Strip Washer (BIO-TEK EL X 50).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize categorical data, while continuous data were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). For normally distributed data, parametric tests were applied, including the Independent Sample t-test for comparing two independent groups. The normality of the continuous data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests, such as the Mann-Whitney U test, were used.

Pearson-χ2 test was used to examine the relationship between two qualitative variables. Correlation coefficients (Pearson or Spearman) were used to examine the relationships between quantitative variables, and the strength of correlations was categorized as very weak (0.0–0.19), weak (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), strong (0.60–0.79), and very strong (0.80–1.00). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to differentiate between patients with SD and the control group. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 88 participants were included in the study, comprising 46 patients with SD and 42 healthy controls. The patient group’s mean age was 30.3 ± 9.3 years, and 54.3% were male. There were no significant differences in age or sex between the SD patients and the control group, indicating that the groups were independent and homogeneous in terms of these variables. The median BMI in the patient group was 24.8 (range: 18.4–38.7), and 23.4 (range: 17.9–30.9) in the control group. The BMI was significantly higher in the patient group (P=0.033). Correlation Between IL-18, IL-22, SDASI, and PSS-10

There was no significant correlation between SDASI and IL-18 or IL-22. Similarly, PSS-10 scores showed no significant correlation with IL-18 or IL-22 when analyzed within the patient group. However, a significant positive correlation was observed between SDASI and PSS-10 scores (r=0.336; P=0.022). In addition, a moderate negative correlation was found between IL-18 and IL-22 levels (r=−0.409; P=0.005), suggesting that IL-22 levels decreased as IL-18 levels increased (Table 5). When all participants were considered, a moderate positive correlation was found between IL-22 and PSS-10 scores (r = 0.541; P<0.001) (Table 6).

Discussion

The recent literature on SD and Malassezia points to the IL-17–IL-23/Th17 axis in disease pathogenesis [4,8,10]. As is known, Th17 cells produce IL-22 along with IL-17. IL-22 can also be produced by γδ-T cells, natural killer T cells, Th22 cells, and IL-18-induced group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3) [14,25]. This is a pleiotropic cytokine that acts primarily through signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) activation and can exhibit both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects by inducing or suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome [3,15,16]. Previous studies have shown that IL-22 promotes epidermal hyperplasia and inhibits keratinocyte differentiation in various inflammatory dermatoses. In psoriasis, elevated IL-22 levels have been detected both in lesional skin and serum. Here, IL-22 stimulated by IL-23 contributes to acanthosis and dermal inflammation by activating STAT3 in keratinocytes, and an increased IL-22/IL-22BP ratio has been strongly associated with disease severity [20,26,27]. In AD, IL-22 is an important cytokine mainly derived from Th22 cells rather than the Th17/IL-23 axis, and clinical improvement with the IL-22 monoclonal antibody further supports its pathogenic role [28–30]. Moreover, significantly elevated IL-22 levels have also been reported in ACD, highlighting its broader importance in cutaneous inflammation [19]. Taken together, these findings suggest that IL-22 functions as a common epithelial immune mediator across multiple inflammatory dermatoses. Given the overlapping clinical and histopathological features of chronic inflammatory dermatoses like psoriasis, AD, and SD such as hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and dermal mononuclear infiltrate, it is plausible that IL-22 could also play a role in SD. Furthermore, a recent study demonstrated that Malassezia increases IL-22 levels and induces inflammatory responses in keratinocytes via the NLRP3 inflammasome [3]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that IL-22 may be involved in SD-related inflammatory processes and aimed to investigate its serum levels in SD patients for the first time.

Our study showed significantly higher IL-22 levels in the patient group compared to controls. ROC analysis yielded a cutoff value of 202.775 pg/mL with 100% sensitivity and 97.6% specificity. Although IL-22 correctly classified all SD patients and misclassified only one control subject, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the relatively small sample size and the lack of validation in independent cohorts. The elevated IL-22 levels observed in this study may reflect broader inflammatory signaling processes associated with SD rather than disease-specific mechanisms. The discriminative performance identified here should therefore be considered exploratory and warrants confirmation in larger, independent cohorts, including comparative analyses with other papulosquamous dermatoses, before clinical applicability can be established. Notably, IL-22 levels did not correlate with disease severity, suggesting that IL-22 may reflect inflammatory activity not directly captured by current clinical scoring systems. Alternatively, serum IL-22 levels may represent a parallel immunological process independent of clinical severity. Incorporating objective disease measures and tissue-level analyses in future investigations may help clarify this relationship.

Interleukin-18, proinflammatory cytokine produced by epithelial cells, is activated by caspase-1 via the NLRP3 inflammasome. In the presence of IL-12 and IL-15, IL-18 induces IFN-γ production from various hematopoietic cells, whereas in their absence, it contributes to Th2 responses. Some evidence also suggests a role for IL-18 in driving Th17 responses. IL-18 levels have been found to be increased in the serum and skin of patients with papulosquamous diseases such psoriasis, AD, and eczema as a potential disease biomarker [31]. Furthermore, Corzo-Leon et al. observed increased IL-18 levels in ex vivo human skin exposed to Malassezia in a lipid-rich environment [5]. In our study, we evaluated IL-18 levels in SD patients for the first time and found them to be elevated compared to controls, although this difference was not statistically significant. No significant association was found between IL-18 and disease severity or other patient-related parameters. While this suggests it is difficult to determine the role of IL-18 in SD pathogenesis, larger studies could yield more definitive results. Another possibility is that IL-18 may act locally in the skin without significantly increasing circulating levels, which highlights the need for further analysis of both lesional and non-lesional skin samples.

Previous studies indicate a bidirectional relationship between IL-18 and IL-22 across different tissues. In the intestinal epithelium, IL-18 synergizes with IL-23 to initiate IL-22 production from ILC3, Th17, and Th22 cells and enhance IL-22BP activity by downregulating it, while IL-22 maintains pro-IL-18 expression and increases IL-18 synthesis. A similar potentiating effect has been observed in tonsils via NF-κB-dependent pathways and in UVB-induced skin inflammation, where IL-22 increases IL-18 levels [21]. In contrast, Shimoura et al. reported that combined IL-23 and IL-18 stimulation in skin could suppress IL-22 expression, and Borghi et al. further showed that under Candida infection, IL-22 could exert anti-inflammatory effect through NLRC4 by inducing IL-1Ra and inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome [16,22]. In our study, however, we observed a moderate but significant negative correlation between serum IL-18 and IL-22 levels, diverging from the predominantly positive associations described in the literature. Specifically, IL-18 levels tended to decrease as IL-22 levels increased. One possible explanation is that IL-22, known for its dual role, may exert an inhibitory effect on IL-18 in seborrheic dermatitis by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby limiting IL-18 maturation and release. This discrepancy underscores the complexity of cytokine interactions and suggests that the IL-18/IL-22 axis may behave differently across disease contexts, tissue environments, or stages of inflammation. Further studies, ideally incorporating both serum and lesional skin analyses, are warranted to clarify whether IL-22 functions predominantly as a pro- or anti-inflammatory mediator in seborrheic dermatitis.

Stress is a common trigger for SD, with one study from Spain identifying it as the most frequent factor [32]. The bi-directional relationship between stress and SD has been suggested in various studies. A cross-sectional study on Korean soldiers found that elevated stress levels, measured by the PSS, were associated with increased SD incidence [33]. Saraç et al. recently reported positive correlations among SDASI, PSS, and depression scores. They emphasized the need for future studies to assess hormonal and inflammatory markers in the context of stress and SD [34]. In line with previous studies, we observed a significant positive correlation between SD and stress as well as a weak but significant correlation between SDASI and PSS scores, indicating a potential link between stress and disease severity. Nonetheless, the directionality of this relationship remains unclear, and it may form a vicious cycle contributing to chronicity and severity in some patients. These findings highlight the importance of considering psychosocial factors in the comprehensive management of SD.

Stress has been proposed to influence epidermal barrier integrity and cutaneous cytokine release via modulation of the HPA axis and neurotransmitter signaling [35]. Animal studies have shown that psychological stress may increase IL-18 mRNA expression in adrenal glands and other epithelial tissues, leading to inflammation [36,37]. Sekiyama et al. suggested that IL-18 suppression may help decrease stress-related effects [38]. However, most of these findings come from animal models, and no human study has investigated the stress–IL-18 relationship to date. In our study, we found no significant correlation between serum IL-18 levels and PSS scores in either SD patients or all participants combined, suggesting that stress-induced pathways in humans may differ from those in animal models.

Similarly, the relationship between IL-22 and stress has mostly been studied in animals, with conflicting findings. Some studies reported no change in IL-22 levels under acute or chronic stress, while others showed that psychological stress could impair gut protection by inducing apoptosis of IL-22–producing cells [39,40]. Conversely, several reports indicated that chronic stress increased IL-22 expression in the intestines or livers of rats [7,41]. In our study, no correlation was observed between IL-22 and stress scores within the SD group. However, when all participants were evaluated, a moderate, statistically significant positive correlation was identified. To our knowledge, this is the first human study to investigate this relationship.

Limitations of our study include a relatively small sample size, reliance on questionnaire-based stress assessment, and measurement of cytokines exclusively in serum without tissue-level confirmation. Although patients with clinical features suggestive of psoriasis were carefully excluded through detailed clinical evaluation, histopathological confirmation was not performed. The inclusion of a single ethnic group precluded race-based analyses and may limit the generalizability of the findings to more ethnically diverse populations. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships between stress, cytokine levels, and disease activity.

Conclusions

In summary, while our findings do not support a significant role of IL-18 in SD pathogenesis, they suggest that IL-22 may be involved in SD-related inflammatory processes. Within this cohort, IL-22 demonstrated high discriminative performance; however, these findings should be considered exploratory pending validation in independent populations. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate IL-18 and IL-22 in SD patients and to examine their relationships with stress in humans. The observed negative correlation between IL-18 and IL-22, along with the positive association between IL-22 and stress, underscores the complexity of cytokine interactions in SD and warrants further mechanistic investigation. Future studies incorporating larger and more diverse cohorts as well as tissue-level analyses are needed to clarify the role of IL-22 in SD and to determine its potential clinical relevance.
