Evaluation of antioxidant, anticholinesterase and antiproliferative potential of Artemisia herba-alba by artificial intelligence-assisted extraction optimization
Authors: Mustafa Sevindik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, in order to maximize the biological activity of Artemisia herba-alba Asso, extraction conditions were optimized by two different methods: Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Artificial Neural Network-Genetic Algorithm (ANN-GA). A total of 27 experimental conditions were created by evaluating the parameters of extraction temperature (45, 55 and 65 °C), time (5, 10, 15 h) and ethanol/water ratio (0, 50, 100%) and the obtained data were applied to the optimization algorithms. ANN-GA method provided higher biological activity parameters compared to RSM. The TAS value of the extract obtained with ANN-GA was determined as 9.449 mmol/L, DPPH value as 150.673 mg TE/g, FRAP value as 226.580 mg TE/g, TPC value as 303.120 mg GAE/g and TFC value as 363.583 mg QE/g. In anticholinesterase activity tests, AChE IC₅₀ value of ANN-GA extract was 41.923 µg/mL, BChE IC₅₀ value was 61.450 µg/mL, and it showed a stronger inhibitory effect than RSM extract. In antiproliferative assays, both extracts demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability on A549 (lung), MCF-7 (breast), and DU-145 (prostate) cancer cell lines. The strongest inhibitory effects were observed at 100 and 200 µg/mL concentrations. Although no statistically significant difference was found between the two methods, the RSM extract exhibited slightly greater antiproliferative efficacy across all three cell lines, particularly at higher con-centrations. These results indicate that both extraction approaches are effective in suppressing cancer cell proliferation, with the RSM method showing a marginal advantage in cytotoxic activity. In the phenolic compound analysis performed by LC–MS/MS, higher levels of pharmacologically important compounds such as naringenin (16,488.02 mg/kg), kaempferol (14,206.24 mg/kg), and caffeic acid (9291.10 mg/kg) were detected in ANN-GA extract. The results show that ANN-GA based optimization approach is an effective strategy to increase antioxidant, anticholinesterase and antiproliferative activities and support the pharmaceutical potential of A. herba-alba.Keywords: Extraction optimization, Artificial intelligence, Antioxidant activity, Anticholinesterase effect, Antiproliferative potential, Phenolic compoundsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Throughout history, medicinal plants have been an important resource for protecting human health and treating diseases. Thanks to their natural components, they have been used to relieve various ailments and have become one of the cornerstones of traditional medicine1. Different cultures have passed down herbal treatments from generation to generation, and over time, this information has been supported by scien-tific research. Today, interest in medicinal plants continues in complementary and alternative treatment methods along with modern medicine, and their effects on health continue to be investigated2.

Artemisia herba-alba Asso is a medicinal plant belonging to the Asteraceae family, adapted to desert ecosystems, and is widely used in traditional medicine for the treatment of various diseases. Especially its volatile oil components and phenolic content play an important role in the pharmacological effects of this plant. The volatile oil of A. herba-alba stands out with its antibacterial and antimicrobial properties and is evaluated for medicinal and aromatic purposes especially in regions such as Algeria and Saudi Arabia3. This volatile oil consists mainly of monoterpenes such as camphor, piperitone and thymol and has inhibitory effects against various pathogenic microorganisms. In addition, the phenolic components of the plant have significant antioxidant and antimicrobial properties4. The free radical scavenging effects of phenolic compounds indicate that they may provide a potential contribution to the prevention of diseases associated with oxidative stress. A. herba-alba, whose effects on metabolic diseases have also been investigated, has been reported to exhibit hypoglycemic activity and can be evaluated as a herbal agent in diabetes management5. In addition, its possible effects on SARS-CoV-2 infection and related comorbidities have also been investigated, and it has been suggested that the phytochemicals it contains have the potential to alleviate symptoms6.

In this study, extract optimization will be applied to maximize the biological activity of A. herba-alba. Optimizing the extraction conditions will increase the yield and bioactivity of the active ingredients obtained, allowing the development of a herbal product with higher standards in terms of pharmacological activity. Having a low toxicity profile makes A. herba-alba a safe candidate for pharmacological applications, and it maintains its therapeutic potential in traditional and modern medicine due to its antibacterial, antioxidant and hypoglycemic properties7,8. With the extract optimization studies to be conducted, the biological effects of the plant will be evaluated in more detail and more comprehensive information will be obtained in terms of clinical applications. In this context, the extract conditions of A. herba-alba will be optimized using RSM and ANN-GA methods. Extracts will be produced under the conditions obtained as a result of optimization and their antioxidant, anticholinesterase and antiproliferative activities will be determined. Then, the phenolic contents of the optimized extracts will be de-termined.

Material and method

A. herba-alba samples used in this study were collected from Gaziantep province of Türkiye. The identification of the plant species was carried out by Dr. Mustafa Sevindik, and the 5th volume of Flora of Turkey and the East Aegean Islands was used as a source in the identification process9. The collection of Artemisia herba-alba complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines. The species is not listed under the IUCN Red List or CITES. Samples were collected in Gaziantep (Türkiye), and a voucher specimen (MS-1514) is preserved in the personal herbarium of the author at Osmaniye Korkut Ata University. The aerial parts of the plant samples brought to the laboratory were dried for approximately 6–8 h at 50 °C using a Dalle SS-06A model dryer. After the drying process was completed, the dry material obtained was ground into fine powder with the help of a mechanical grinder. Then, extraction processes were applied on this powdered material to be used in the analysis processes.

Extraction procedure method

A full factorial experimental design approach was used in the creation of the extraction protocol, in which the effects of three basic parameters were systematically evaluated. In this context, three variables, namely extraction temperature, duration and ethanol/water ratio used, were tested at three different levels each. In this setup, a total of 27 independent experiments were carried out with the Soxhlet device. In the experimental studies, extraction temperatures were determined as 45 °C, 55 °C and 65 °C; durations were determined as 5 h, 10 h and 15 h; and solvent ratios were determined as 0%, 50% and 100% ethanol. The experimental data obtained were then analyzed in detail with Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and the optimization process was carried out. This analysis process was supported by an advanced artificial intelligence-based model created by integrating Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Genetic Algorithms (GA).

Response surface methodology (RSM)

In this study, the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) method was preferred for the optimization process. Extraction temperature, extraction time and ethanol/water ratio were considered as independent variables in the study; Total Antioxidant Status (TAS) value obtained from the extracts was determined as the response variable.

Artificial neural network-genetic algorithm (ANN-GA)

In order to develop the predictive model, Artificial Neural Network (ANN) approach was applied. Extraction temperature, time and ethanol/water ratio were used as input variables; TAS value was used as output variable. The dataset was divided into three groups as 80% training, 10% validation and 10% test. The training process was carried out with Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) algorithm.

In order to determine the most suitable network structure, the number of neurons in the hidden layers was tested with different configurations from 1 to 20. The learning coefficient and momentum value were kept constant (0.5), the maximum iteration number was set as 500, the validation control number as 50 and the error tolerance as 1 × 10−5. Each network model was evaluated with 1000 separate trainings.

Genetic Algorithm (GA) was chosen as the basic calculation approach in the optimization process. Different population sizes were tested and the behavior of the algorithm was observed, and the roulette wheel selection method was used to select the individuals to be transferred to the next generation. The single point crossover technique was applied in genetic crossover. The most appropriate number of iterations was determined by convergence graphs, and each optimization process was repeated at least 60 times in order to reach the results closest to the global optimum.

In this study, the 3-5-1 ANN architecture was selected after extensive testing of different configurations ranging from 1 to 20 hidden neurons. The 3-5-1 structure provided the best balance between predictive accuracy and computational efficiency, as reflected by the lowest MSE (0.0002) and MAPE (0.197%) values, and the highest correlation coefficient (R = 0.999). Increasing the number of hidden neurons beyond five did not yield significant improvements in accuracy, but considerably increased the risk of overfitting and computational cost. Therefore, the 3-5-1 architecture was considered the most robust and reproducible model structure. The training parameters (learning coefficient = 0.5, momentum = 0.5, maximum iteration = 500, error tolerance = 1 × 10⁻5) were selected based on preliminary trials to ensure stable convergence without overfitting, in line with previous ANN applications in extraction optimization studies.

Extraction for bioactivity

In this study, the optimal extraction conditions that ensure the highest level of biological activity were determined. As a result of RSM analyses, the most suitable extraction parameters were calculated as 54.048 °C temperature, 6.349 h of processing time and 41.266 ethanol/water ratio. In addition, optimization analyses performed with the ANN-GA method showed that the ideal conditions were 47.581 °C temperature, 11.596 h of extraction time and 84.878 ethanol/water ratio. These optimum parameters were applied with a computer-aided extraction process using the Gerhardt SOX-414 device. The extracts obtained were analyzed in accordance with these determined conditions and their biological activities were evaluated.

Anticholinesterase tests

The anticholinesterase activities of the optimized extracts were evaluated based on the method developed by Ellman et al.10. Galantamine was used as a reference inhibitor in the study. Stock solutions were prepared from the extracts at concentrations ranging from 3.125 to 200 μg/mL. Analyses were performed in microplate format, and firstly, 130 μL of phosphate buffer solution with a concentration of 0.1 M and pH 8 were added to each well. Then, 10 μL of extract solution and 20 μL of AChE or BChE enzyme solutions were added, respectively, and incubated for 10 min at 25 °C in the dark. After the incubation period was completed, 20 μL of DTNB (5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid)) solution and 20 μL of substrate (acetylcholine iodide or butyrylcholine iodide) were added, respectively, to initiate the reaction. The absorbance values of the products formed as a result of the enzymatic reaction were measured at a wavelength of 412 nm. Using the obtained data, enzyme inhibition percentages were calculated and IC₅₀ values (μg/mL) were determined and reported.

Antioxidant tests

Stock solutions of optimized extracts were prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). 1 mL of the prepared stock solution was taken and mixed with 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) solution with a volume of 160 µL and a concentration of 0.267 mM. In the preparation of the DPPH solution, 4 mL of 0.004% methanol solution was used. The resulting mixture was incubated at room temperature and in dark conditions for 30 min. After the incubation period was completed, the absorbance values of the samples were measured at a wavelength of 517 nm. The analysis results were calculated and reported as Trolox equivalent per extract (mg TE/g extract)11.

A stock solution of 100 µL was prepared from the optimized extracts and mixed with 2 mL of FRAP reagent. FRAP reagent was prepared by combining 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 40 mM HCl, 20 mM FeCl₃ 6H₂O and 10 mM 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-S-triazine solutions at a ratio of 10:1:1. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 4 min. After incubation was completed, the absorbance values of the samples were measured at a wavelength of 593 nm. The data obtained were calculated and reported as mg Trolox equivalent/g per extract11.

The total antioxidant capacity of the optimized extracts was determined using the Rel Assay TAS kit. The results were expressed in mmol Trolox equivalent/L. Total oxidant levels were analyzed using the Rel Assay TOS kit and the results were reported in µmol hydrogen peroxide equivalent/L12,13. Oxidative stress index (OSI) was calculated by dividing the total oxidant capacity by the total antioxidant capacity and expressed as a percentage14.

Total phenolic and flavonoid content analyses

A 1 mL stock solution (1 mg/mL) was prepared from the extracts obtained under optimum extraction conditions and 1 mL Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (1:9, v/v) was added and mixed well. The calibration curve was constructed using gallic acid as the standard (R2 = 0.998). Then, 0.75 mL of 1% Na₂CO₃ solution was added to the mixture and the samples were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. After the time was completed, absorbance values were measured at 760 nm wavelength. The results obtained were calculated in mg/g based on the gallic acid standard curve15.

Total flavonoid levels were analyzed using the aluminum chloride test. For this purpose, 0.1 mL of 10% Al(NO₃)₃, 0.1 mL of 1 M NH₄CH₃COO, 4.3 mL of methanol, 5 mL of quercetin solution (used as a reference standard for calibration) and 0.5 mL of plant extract were combined and mixed well. The calibration curve for flavonoids was established using quercetin (R2 = 0.997). The resulting mixture was incubated for 40 min and then absorbance measurements were performed at a wavelength of 415 nm. Total flavonoid content was expressed in mg/g15.

Antiproliferative tests

The antiproliferative effects of the optimized extracts were evaluated on A549 (lung cancer), MCF-7 (breast cancer), and DU-145 (prostate cancer) cell lines using the MTT assay. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 70–80% confluency, the cells were harvested using Trypsin–EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. After a 24-h incubation to allow for cell attachment, the cells were treated with the extract solutions at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL for another 24 h. Following treatment, the media were removed and replaced with 1 mg/mL MTT solution. Cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C until formazan crystals formed. The MTT solution was then discarded, and the crystals were dissolved in DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an Epoch microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate16.

Phenolic contents tests

The phenolic component profile of the optimized extracts was analyzed using the LC–MS/MS system. Within the scope of the study, 24 different standard phenolic compounds in the extracts were determined. The separation of the compounds was achieved using a C-18 Intersil ODS-4 analytical column with a size of 3.0 mm × 100 mm and a particle diameter of 2 μm, and the column temperature was kept constant at 40 °C. During the separation process, water containing 0.1% formic acid (Mobile Phase A) and methanol containing 0.1% formic acid (Mobile Phase B) were used as mobile phases. According to the analysis protocol, the flow rate was determined as 0.3 mL/min and 2 μL of sample was loaded into the system for each injection. The gradient elution program was set as follows: 0–4 min, 5% B; 4–7 min, 95% B; 7–7.01 min, return to 5% B; 7.01–12 min, 5% B for reequilibration. The maximum operating pressure was 660 bar. The chromatographic analysis was performed using a Shimadzu LCMSMS-8030 system with an autosampler (SIL-20ACXR) and column oven (CTO-10ASvp). This gradient profile was adapted and optimized based on previously published LC–MS/MS protocols17. The list of screened compounds included acetohydroxamic acid, kaempferol, fumaric acid, gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid, naringenin, quercetin, luteolin, catechinhydrate, vanillic acid, syringic acid, thymoquinone, resveratrol, myricetin, salicylic acid, phloridzin dihydrate, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, oleuropein, 2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone, silymarin, alizarin, and curcumin17.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses performed in this study were performed using SPSS 21.0 software running on Windows operating system. Independent samples t-test was used for pairwise group comparisons, and One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison of more than two groups. Duncan’s multiple range test was applied with significance level set as α = 0.05 to determine significant differences between groups after ANOVA. All experiments and analyses were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

The obtained data show that total antioxidant activity (TAS) reached the maximum level at 55 °C temperature and 10 h extraction time. Under these conditions, TAS values were measured as 8.029 ± 0.018, 8.038 ± 0.033 and 8.057 ± 0.040 mmol/L when the ethanol/water ratio was 0%, 50% and 100%, respectively, and this revealed that this combination represented the most efficient extraction parameters. Antioxidant activity

Plants are natural sources that exhibit strong antioxidant activity thanks to the phenolic compounds, flavonoids and other bioactive components they contain. These compounds neutralize free radicals, reduce oxidative stress and prevent cellular damage18. Plant extracts with high antioxidant capacity are beneficial for health and are widely used in the food, pharmaceutical and cosmetic sectors. Scientific studies show that plant antioxidants can prevent chronic diseases and have anti-aging effects19. In this study, antioxidant capacities of extracts obtained from A. herba-alba plant by different extraction methods were compared. ANN-GA method was superior to RSM method in all antioxidant parameters and higher values were obtained in terms of total antioxidant capacity (TAS), radical scavenging capacity (DPPH, FRAP) and total phenolic (TPC) and flavonoid (TFC) contents. In the literature, it was reported that total phenolic contents of different extracts of A. herba-alba were in the range of 133.43–320.43 mg/g and total flavonoid content was in the range of 17.80–76.55 mg/g20. It was observed that total phenolic contents of RSM and ANN-GA extracts obtained in our study were compatible with the values reported in the literature. On the other hand, total flavonoid contents were determined above the range reported in the literature. This situation shows that the extraction methods used in our study have the potential to increase the recovery of flavonoid components. Flavonoids are directly affected by the solvent type, temperature, time and optimization processes in terms of extraction efficiency21. The fact that the ANN-GA method provides higher extraction efficiency by increasing the solubility of flavonoids suggests that this method is an effective optimization tool that increases the functional use of plant compounds. There are studies in the literature evaluating the antioxidant activity of A. herba-alba with different methods7,20,22,23. Most studies reveal that the extraction method is a determining factor on the yield and antioxidant capacity of biologically active compounds24. The ANN-GA method used in our study also stands out as one of the modern techniques reported in the literature and has the potential to provide high antioxidant activity. This study confirmed that artificial intelligence-based extraction optimization methods are more efficient than traditional statistical modeling methods (RSM).

The TAS, TOS and OSI values of different plants have been reported in the literature. In this context, the TAS values of Campanula strigosa, Mentha longifolia, Origanum laevigatum and Hypericum spectabile were reported as 4.974, 6.094, 8.609 and 9.306 mmol/L, respectively. The TOS values were reported as 12.437, 14.050, 10.685 and 13.065 µmol/L. OSI values were reported as 0.251, 0.231, 0.124 and 0.14025–28. In our study, the TAS value of the RSM extract of A. herba-alba was determined to be higher than C. strigosa and M. longifolia, but lower than O. laevigatum and H. spectabile. In contrast, the TAS value of the ANN-GA extract was found to be higher than all compared plants. The TAS value represents the total amount of antioxidant compounds in a natural product29. These results show that the ANN-GA extract of A. herba-alba has higher antioxidant capacity compared to other medicinal plants reported in the literature. The TOS values of RSM and ANN-GA extracts of A. herba-alba used in our study were determined to be higher than C. strigosa, M. longifolia, O. laevigatum and H. spectabile. The TOS value is a parameter that shows the totality of oxidant compounds found in natural products29. This situation shows that A. herba-alba extracts also have a certain level in terms of oxidant compounds. The high TOS value suggests that some phytochemicals in the structure of the plant may exhibit pro-oxidant properties. Although phenolic compounds generally exhibit antioxidant properties, they can exhibit pro-oxidant properties by participating in oxidative processes at certain concentrations30. This situation may be due to the structural differences of the compounds obtained by the extraction method, environmental factors and plant growth conditions. In addition, although the extract obtained by the ANN-GA method has a higher antioxidant capacity, the increase in the oxidant level reveals that the effect of this method on antioxidant and oxidant compounds should be analyzed in more detail. In addition, the OSI values of the RSM and ANN-GA extracts of A. herba-alba used in our study were determined to be lower than C. strigosa and M. longifolia, but higher than O. laevigatum and H. spectabile. The OSI value indicates the extent to which oxidant compounds are suppressed by antioxidant compounds29. The variability of OSI values when compared with different plants suggests that the capacity of antioxidant compounds in herbal extracts to neutralize oxidant compounds is specific to plant species. This may be directly related to the amount of phenolic and flavonoid compounds, their structural properties, and their interactions with each other. While the ANN-GA method increases the antioxidant capacity, its effect on the neutralization process of oxidant compounds needs to be investigated in more detail. These findings show that artificial intelligence-supported optimization techniques allow more effective component recovery in herbal extraction processes. The ANN-GA method can better analyze the variables in the extraction process and determine the optimum conditions, thus ensuring that biologically active compounds are obtained efficiently. In particular, the high phenolic and flavonoid contents obtained suggest that the ANN-GA method extracts compounds that support antioxidant activity more effectively. However, it was observed that TOS and the OSI increased in the extracts obtained with the ANN-GA method. This may be related to the sensitivity of the extracted compounds to oxidative processes. It is known that polyphenols in particular can become reactive in an oxidative environment31. Therefore, further studies are needed at the cellular and in vivo levels to better understand the effects of this method in biological systems. The observed differences between RSM and ANN-GA optimized extracts highlight not only the impact of extraction methodology on yield but also on the quality of bioactive compounds recovered. This suggests that optimization approaches may influence not just concentration but also compound stability, which could explain the higher antioxidant indices observed for ANN-GA. Such methodological effects must be considered when evaluating reproducibility and industrial scalability.

Anticholinesterase activity

Plants are among the important biological resources that can exhibit natural anticholinesterase activity thanks to the various phytochemicals they contain. Especially secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds, alkaloids, flavonoids and terpenoids can prevent the destruction of acetylcholine by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) enzymes and thus show therapeutic potential in neurodegenerative diseases32. This biochemical mechanism plays an important role in processes associated with cognitive dysfunctions such as Alzheimer’s disease and is considered a critical parameter in the evaluation of the pharmacological efficacy of herbal extracts33. However, both extracts could not reach the efficiency of the reference inhibitor galantamine (6.430 ± 0.171 µg/mL for AChE, 15.287 ± 0.226 µg/mL for BChE). These results suggest that the ANN-GA method provides more efficient extraction of bioactive components. Especially, the higher inhibition rate on AChE compared to BChE indicates that this method can better isolate the targeted components in neurodegenerative diseases. Since AChE inhibition is of great importance in Alzheimer’s disease, it can be said that these extracts have pharmacological potential. However, their activities remain at a very low level compared to galantamine. Therefore, methods such as advanced extraction techniques, biodirected fractionation and purification should be applied to increase the cholinesterase inhibition potential of A. herba-alba. In addition, its use in combination with galantamine or other cholinesterase inhibitors can be investigated. A. herba-alba has been reported to have an inhibitory effect on AChE and BChE enzymes in the literature22,34. These studies show that the plant contains bioactive compounds that can inhibit cholinesterase enzymes. The current findings, although consistent with this literature, reveal that the inhibitory effect is well behind the reference inhibitor galantamine. In conclusion, although A. herba-alba extracts contain promising natural compounds for neurodegenerative diseases, further isolation, characterization and in vivo studies are needed for therapeutic use.

Antiproliferative activity

Plants are rich sources of natural compounds that can exhibit antiproliferative effects thanks to the bioactive phytochemicals they contain. Plants can exhibit anticancer potential through various mechanisms such as arresting the cell cycle by suppressing proliferation in cancer cells, inducing apoptosis, and preventing metastatic processes35. Scientific studies conducted in recent years have revealed that different herbal extracts can exhibit cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects on certain cancer cell lines36. In this context, within the scope of our study, the antiproliferative activity of the optimized extract obtained from A. herba-alba on the A549, MCF-7 and DU-145 cancer cell lines was evaluated and the results obtained are presented in Fig. 3.

Antiproliferative activity of Artemisia herba-alba extracts. (Control: The group not treated with chemicals, only kept in the medium; DMSO: The group in which the medium and DMSO were applied; the group in which the extract was applied at 25, 50, 100 and 200 µg/mL concentrations).

In this study, the antiproliferative effects of two optimized A. herba-alba extracts (RSM-extract and ANN-GA-extract) were evaluated on A549 (lung), MCF-7 (breast), and DU-145 (prostate) cancer cell lines. Both extracts demonstrated a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability in all three cell lines, with significant inhibition observed starting at 25 µg/mL and the strongest effects at 100 and 200 µg/mL. Control groups treated with solvents [serum-free (SF) culture medium and DMSO] maintained high viability, confirming the non-toxicity of the controls. Although the differences were not statistically significant, the RSM-extract consistently resulted in slightly lower cell viability values at higher concentrations across all three cancer cell lines. This indicates a marginally stronger antiproliferative effect compared to the ANN-GA-extract. The enhanced activity of the RSM-extract may be attributed to better preservation or enrichment of cytotoxic phytochemicals during the optimization process. These results emphasize the importance of extraction methodology in maximizing the therapeutic efficacy of plant-based compounds. The current findings align with existing literature on the cytotoxic and antiproliferative potential of A. herba-alba. Methanolic extracts have been shown to induce G2/M phase arrest and inhibit the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in colorectal cancer cells37, while also modulating apoptotic gene expression (e.g., p53, Bax, Bcl-2) in liver and lung cancer cells38. Additionally, selective toxicity of essential oils from specific botanical parts has been demonstrated39, and phenolic-rich ethanolic extracts have shown antioxidant and cytotoxic effects against NCI-N417 lung cancer cells40. In conclusion, optimized A. herba-alba extracts demonstrated potent, dose-dependent antiproliferative activity against lung, breast, and prostate cancer cells. Among the two optimization approaches, the RSM method yielded extracts with slightly higher cytotoxic efficacy, particularly at elevated concentrations. This highlights the critical role of extraction optimization in enhancing the biological potential of medicinal plant extracts. Further studies are needed to isolate the bioactive constituents, clarify their mechanisms of action, and evaluate their therapeutic potential in preclinical models. Although the present study demonstrated that the optimized extracts of A. herba-alba significantly reduced the viability of tumor cell lines, an important limitation is the lack of cytotoxicity evaluation on non-tumor (healthy) cells. Such analysis would allow assessment of the selectivity index and provide more reliable conclusions about therapeutic potential. Future studies should therefore include cytotoxicity assays on normal cell lines to better determine the safety profile and selectivity of the extracts. The slightly higher cytotoxicity observed for the RSM extract at elevated concentrations may be attributed to the selective preservation of compounds with higher antiproliferative potency. This interpretation aligns with previous studies reporting that subtle variations in extraction parameters can alter the bioactivity profile of phytochemical mixtures. Thus, our results underscore the importance of extraction methodology as a determinant of therapeutic potential, beyond simple yield maximization.

Phenolic contents

Phenolic compounds are among the secondary metabolites of plants and attract attention with their many biological activities. Recent studies have shown that phenolic compounds can provide protective effects on human health and have significant potential in functional food and pharmaceutical products41,42. In our study, the phenolic contents of the optimized extract obtained from A. herba-alba were scanned on the LC–MS/MS device. The findings are shown in Table 4. Among the 24 phenolic standards analyzed, only 9 compounds were detected in quantifiable amounts in the extracts. In this study, the phenolic contents of A. herba-alba extracts were determined by optimizing them with RSM and ANN-GA methods. The results obtained show that the ANN-GA method is generally superior in terms of extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds. Especially, kaempferol (14,206.24 mg/kg), caffeic acid (9291.10 mg/kg) and naringenin (16,488.02 mg/kg) contents increased significantly with the ANN-GA method. However, it was determined that the extract obtained with the RSM method was higher in terms of protocatechuic acid (1465.44 mg/kg) and caffeic acid (11,946.15 mg/kg). Although some phenolic compounds such as quercetin, vanillic acid and luteolin were found in similar concentrations in both methods, the ANN-GA method generally provided higher extraction efficiency. Considering the biological activities of phenolic compounds, it is thought that the extracts obtained by the ANN-GA method are more advantageous in terms of antioxidant and anticholinesterase properties. Kaempferol, naringenin and quercetin are compounds with strong antioxidant effects43–45, and the fact that the ANN-GA method extracts these compounds in higher amounts increases the potential of the extract in functional food and pharmaceutical applications. In addition, protocatechuic acid and caffeic acid obtained in higher amounts by the RSM method are important in terms of cardiovascular health and neuroprotective effects46,47. In addition, the significant increase in the amount of syringic acid with the ANN-GA method (1373.44 mg/kg) shows that this method can strengthen the antimicrobial and hepatoprotective effects48. The findings show that the ANN-GA method is generally more successful in increasing the extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds. However, it is thought that the RSM method can also be evaluated in terms of selective extraction strategies for obtaining more of certain phenolic compounds. In this context, the effects of extraction methods on the bioavailability, stability and pharmacokinetic properties of phenolic compounds should be examined in detail in future studies and the most appropriate method for application areas should be determined.

Conclusion

In this study, different optimization approaches such as RSM and ANN-GA were compared in order to increase the biological effects of A. herba-alba and it was deter-mined that ANN-GA method gave more effective results in terms of both antioxidant and anticholinesterase activity. Extracts obtained with ANN-GA method increased the extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds and provided significant increase in the amounts of pharmacologically important compounds such as kaempferol and naringenin. In addition, both extracts showed remarkable inhibition on A549 cells in terms of antiproliferative effects. These findings show that A. herba-alba is a promising natural source for functional food, supplementary products and pharmaceutical ap-plications. In future studies, the in vivo bioavailability, toxicological profile and mechanistic effects of the obtained optimized extracts should be investigated. In addi-tion, the bioactive components of the extract should be purified by fractionation and their targeted therapeutic potential should be examined in detail, especially on neuro-degenerative and cancer diseases. More widespread use of AI-based approaches such as ANN-GA in extraction science will open new horizons in herbal medicine and product development processes.
