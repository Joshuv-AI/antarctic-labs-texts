GABRINOX-2 protocol: a French, prospective, multicentre, randomised phase II trial evaluating gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel followed by FOLFIRINOX versus FOLFIRINOX alone as first-line treatment for metastatic pancreatic cancer
Authors: Fabienne Portales, Sophie Gourgou, Catherine Fiess, Fanny Salasc, Eric Assenat, Marc Ychou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPancreatic adenocarcinoma is a major public health concern due to its high metastatic potential and poor prognosis. However, treatment options remain limited. A promising therapeutic strategy involves the sequential administration of standard therapies. In a previous phase Ib-II trial, we evaluated a sequential regimen of gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (GEMBRAX) followed by FOLFIRINOX (FFX), which improved median overall survival (OS), progression-free survival and objective response rate (ORR), with acceptable toxicity. This phase II randomised trial will assess the efficacy of GEMBRAX followed by FFX compared with FFX alone as a first-line treatment for metastatic pancreatic cancer (mPC).Methods and analysisThe GABRINOX-2 (GemcitabineABRaxaneIRInotecanOXaliplatine-2) study is an ongoing prospective, multicentre, randomised phase II trial. A total of 162 patients with mPC will be randomised (1:1) to receive GEMBRAX treatment followed by FFX (arm A) or FFX alone (arm B). The primary objective is to compare the efficacy of GEMBRAX followed by FFX with FFX alone as a first-line treatment for mPC. Secondary objectives include treatment tolerability, ORR, disease control rate, OS and patient quality of life. Additionally, we aim to identify novel biomarkers associated with treatment response through the analysis of circulating tumour DNA and assess the messenger RNA signature’s predictive value for OS based on treatment approach.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the comité de protection des personnes Ile de France III (26 July 2021) and the French National Agency for the Safety of Health Products (17 September 2021). It will be carried out in accordance with European Reglementation 536/2014 on clinical trials, the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the French Public Health Code. The results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at national and international conferences.Trial registration numberThis trial has been registered on euclinicaltrials.eu (EU 2023-508594-84-00) and on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05065801).

Introduction

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma is a major public health issue and a leading cause of mortality in high-income countries due to its high metastatic potential. By 2030, pancreatic cancer is expected to become the second leading cause of mortality.1 2 Its prognosis remains poor, with an overall 5-year survival rate of 7% across all stages, primarily due to limited therapeutic options.1 Indeed, since 1997, gemcitabine has been the only first-line treatment for metastatic pancreatic cancer (mPC).3 However, in the early 2010s, two phase III trials highlighted the potential of two alternative chemotherapy regimens: a combination of oxaliplatin, irinotecan and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), that is, FOLFIRINOX (FFX) and gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (GEMBRAX).4 5 The ‘Actions concertées dans les Cancers COloRectaux et Digestifs 11’ (ACCORD11) trial demonstrated a significant improvement in median overall survival (OS) (11.1 vs 6.8 months, HR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.45 to 0.73, p<0.001), progression-free survival (PFS) (6.4 vs 3.3 months, HR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.37 to 0.59, p<0.001) and objective response rate (ORR) (31.6% vs 9.4%, p<0.001) in favour of FFX compared with gemcitabine.4 Similarly, the Metastatic Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma Clinical Trial (MPACT) demonstrated that GEMBRAX significantly improved median OS (8.5 vs 6.7 months, HR: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.62 to 0.83, p<0.001), PFS (5.5 vs 3.7 months, HR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.58 to 0.82, p<0.001) and ORR (23% vs 7%, p<0.001) compared with gemcitabine.5

However, the safety profiles of FFX and GEMBRAX are less favourable than that of gemcitabine. Conroy et al reported a higher incidence of grade≥3 adverse events (AEs) with FFX compared with gemcitabine, particularly neutropenia (45.7% vs 21%), febrile neutropenia (5.4% vs 1.2%), diarrhoea (12.7% vs 1.8%) and peripheral neuropathy (9% vs 0%).4 Similarly, Von Hoff et al reported an increased incidence of grade≥3 AEs with GEMBRAX compared with gemcitabine, including neutropenia (38% vs 27%), febrile neutropenia (3% vs 1%), diarrhoea (6% vs 1%) and peripheral neuropathy (17% vs 1%).5 Due to these high toxicity levels, only patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of ≤1 are currently eligible for these regimens, which require dose adjustments.

Nonetheless, median survival rates remain poor, with frequent relapse and the development of chemoresistance. One interesting alternative strategy to improve treatment outcomes could rely on the sequential administration of standard therapies, such as GEMBRAX followed by FFX, to enhance tolerability and maintain dose intensity. The phase II FIRGEMAX study demonstrated the feasibility of a sequential regimen with GEMBRAX followed by FOLFIRI-3 (5-FU, folinic acid and irinotecan); however, the incidence of grade 3/4 neutropenia remained high (46.9%).6 We also conducted a phase Ib-II multicentre trial to evaluate a sequential regimen of GEMBRAX followed by FFX (GABRINOX, (GemcitabineABRaxaneIRInotecanOXaliplatine)).7 In phase I, we established the recommended phase II dose of GEMBRAX (nab-paclitaxel 125 mg/m2 and gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2) and FFX (oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2, irinotecan 180 mg/m2, 5-FU bolus of 400 mg and continuous 5-FU infusion of 2400 mg). The phase II aimed to assess the ORR of sequential administration of GEMBRAX and FFX. We observed a high response rate, with a promising ORR of 64.9% (95% CI: 51.1% to 77.1%), a median OS of 15.1 months (95% CI: 10.6 to 20.1 months) and a median PFS of 10.5 months (95% CI: 6.0 to 12.5 months). Furthermore, these outcomes were associated with acceptable toxicity and remarkably low neurotoxicity, with no grade 3–4 neurotoxicity in phase Ib and only 5.2% of grade 3 neurotoxicity in phase II.7 Therefore, these results support the rationale for a randomised trial.

In this phase II randomised trial, we aim to evaluate the efficacy of GEMBRAX followed by FFX compared with FFX alone as a first-line treatment for mPC. Secondary objectives include assessing treatment tolerance, patient survival (ORR, disease control rate and OS) and patient quality of life (QoL).

Methods and analysis

Study design and settings

The GABRINOX-2 study (NCT05065801) is a prospective, open-label, multicentre, national, randomised phase II trial conducted across eight French healthcare centres. Recruitment began in January 2022 and is expected to finish by September 2025, with a 24-month follow-up period extending through October 2028. A total of 162 patients with mPC will be included in the study. Eligibility criteria are detailed in box 1. The study flow chart is presented in figure 1.

Patient and public involvement

The informed consent form (online supplemental material) and information document were reviewed by three patients from the patient committee for cancer clinical research of ‘La Ligue contre le Cancer’. Participants will be given access to the study results on request.

Study objectives

The primary objective of the study is to assess the efficacy of GEMBRAX followed by FFX (arm A) compared with FFX alone (arm B) in the first-line treatment of mPC.

Secondary objectives will include the tolerability of the intervention, the ORR, the disease control rate, the OS, patient QoL and the identification of novel prognostic and predictive factors.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint will be PFS, defined as the time from randomisation to the onset of first documented disease progression or death from any cause, according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST) V.1.1 criteria, assessed and validated by independent centralised review. Tolerability, assessed by the incidence of AEs according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) V.5.0 classification.

ORR, defined as the proportion of patients achieving a complete or partial response. Tumour response is evaluated according to the RECIST V.1.1 criteria.

Randomisation

Randomisation will be performed via an electronic case report form (eCRF) and the Ennov Clinical software. Once inclusion is completed, a notification with the inclusion number and the treatment arm will be automatically sent to the investigator, the sponsor (Montpellier Cancer Institute, Montpellier, France), the data centre and the biostatistician. Randomisation (1:1) will be conducted using a minimisation method and will be stratified by Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (0 vs 1), type of metastasis (synchronous vs metachronous) and patient age (18–64 years vs 65–75 years) at inclusion.

G-CSF will be systematically administered as prophylaxis from day 6 to day 12 following each FFX infusion (ie, from days 6 to 12 for arm B and from days 20 to 26, days 34 to 40 and days 48 to 54 for both arms). The investigator may adjust G-CSF doses based on the patient’s treatment and blood test results.

Before starting chemotherapy with GEMBRAX, a platelet count of ≥75 000/mm³ and a neutrophil count of ≥1000/mm³ are required. For FFX, a platelet count of ≥1 00 000/mm³ and a neutrophil count of ≥1500/mm³ are required. If these thresholds are not met, treatment will be delayed until recovery, with G-CSF support administered for subsequent cycles in case of neutropenia. A minimum of 7 days must elapse between two administrations of GEMBRAX and a minimum of 14 days between the administration of GEMBRAX and FFX.

Treatments will continue until disease progression in both arms. Due to the cumulative toxicity of oxaliplatin, a maximum of 12 months of chemotherapy will be administered to patients in arm A and a maximum of 6 months to patients in arm B during the study. Nonetheless, treatment may continue off-protocol at the investigator’s discretion.

Modification or discontinuation of allocated interventions

Dose adjustments will be implemented according to patient toxicities. Dose adjustments are detailed in online supplemental tables 1–5.

Early treatment discontinuation will occur under the following circumstances: disease progression according to RECIST V.1.1 by independent review, investigator’s decision, protocol violation, patient non-compliance with procedures, patient’s decision, pregnancy, unacceptable toxicity, treatment delay exceeding 14 days (or treatment interruption exceeding 28 days between the last chemotherapy dose and treatment resumption) or new cancer diagnosis.

Patients will be permanently withdrawn from the study in cases of major protocol deviation, patient decision or withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up or death. The reason for study discontinuation will be recorded in the eCRF.

Sample size calculation

To demonstrate a difference in median PFS between the two arms, from 6.4 to 10.5 months, corresponding to an HR of 0.61,4 5 with 80% power and a two-sided α risk of 0.05, 131 events are required. A total of 162 patients (81 per arm) will be included to observe these events, considering a 5% patient loss to follow-up.

An interim analysis is planned after the observation of 40% of events according to the O’Brien-Fleming boundaries: the null hypothesis will be rejected if p≤0.0008 (indicating superiority of GEMBRAX), and the alternative hypothesis will be rejected if p≥0.9086 (for futility).

Statistical methods

A detailed statistical analysis plan will be prepared prior to the database lock. Analyses of the primary endpoint (PFS) and OS will also be conducted on the intention-to-treat population. Efficacy endpoints (objective response and disease control) will be analysed in the per-protocol population, defined as eligible patients evaluable at baseline who received ≥1 treatment cycle and had ≥1 postinclusion evaluation, according to the treatment received. Safety analyses will be conducted on the safety-evaluable population.

Quantitative variables will be reported as the number of observations, median, minimum and maximum values and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis or Wilcoxon tests. Qualitative variables will be reported as the number of observations and percentages for each category. Missing categories will be enumerated, with percentages calculated relative to the total population excluding missing data. Proportions will be compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test.

Median follow-up will be estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Survival data will be analysed using the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate survival rates and median survival time. Comparisons of survival distributions will be performed using the log-rank test. HR will be estimated using a Cox non-proportional hazards model. In addition, a time-dependent Cox model will be performed to take into account the immortal time bias due to different durations of treatments in arms A and B.

Objective response and disease control rates will be summarised as percentages with associated 95% CIs (calculated using the exact binomial method).

QLQ-C30 and QLQ-PAN26 questionnaire scores will be computed according to EORTC guidelines, and longitudinal QoL analysis will be performed using a mixed linear model.8

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer has a particularly poor prognosis, primarily due to its intrinsic chemoresistant nature, which is driven by a hostile tumour microenvironment. This dense, fibrotic stroma restricts molecular exchanges, hampers the penetration of chemotherapeutic agents and contributes to the immunologically ‘cold’ nature of the tumour, making many immunotherapeutic approaches ineffective. Since the key results established by phase III ACCORD11 and MPACT trials on FFX and GEMBRAX,4 5 there has been limited progress in therapeutic options for mPC.

The development of a sequential therapeutic strategy has emerged as a promising approach to optimise treatment efficacy and overcome chemoresistance. A French phase IIb study demonstrated the feasibility and efficacy of the FIRGEMAX strategy, with manageable toxicities.6 Similarly, the GABRINOX phase Ib-II study demonstrated the feasibility of a sequential regimen of GEMBRAX followed by FFX. The study achieved its primary objective, with an ORR of 64.9% (95% CI: 51.1% to 77.1%), and reported promising secondary outcomes, including PFS and median OS.7 The treatment was well tolerated, with no treatment‐related deaths; the dose intensity was satisfactory, with patients receiving a median of 8.5 cycles and the incidence of neurotoxicity was low (5.2%). Given that neurotoxicity is a recognised limiting factor in FOLFOX and FFX regimens, the protocol was designed to administer GEMBRAX first, followed by FFX, with an intervening washout period for oxaliplatin and nab-paclitaxel. Our hypothesis was that this washout interval would mitigate cumulative oxaliplatin toxicity, thereby preserving the dose intensity and treatment duration necessary for optimal disease control.7 The GABRINOX‐2 trials now aim to validate the GEMBRAX-FFX sequential regimen through a randomised phase II study comparing it with the standard FFX treatment, with PFS as the primary outcome.

Identifying prognostic and predictive factors remains a critical challenge in pancreatic cancer research. Currently, carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) is the only biomarker routinely used in clinical practice; however, its utility is limited by a high false-negative rate and reduced specificity, particularly in the presence of cholestasis.911

Developing new prognostic factors is also essential for improving the management of pancreatic cancer, enabling early treatment selection and adaptation. Currently, therapeutic choice primarily relies on the patient’s global health status, age, liver function (ie, total bilirubin levels) and CA19-9 level. In addition, tumour response is generally assessed using CT scan imaging based on RECIST criteria after 2 months of therapy. However, this method has limitations in differentiating tumour tissue from fibrotic tissue, potentially leading to an overestimation of disease progression.12 In the GABRINOX-2 trial, we propose to improve patient management by evaluating ctDNA as a non-invasive prognostic biomarker. We will assess the correlation between ctDNA levels, therapeutic response, CA19-9 levels and other debated prognostic factors, such as the nomogram and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio.

Additionally, we designed an ancillary study to assess early predictive factors for FFX and gemcitabine responses using a non-invasive approach based on the RNA signature identified by the ACOBIOM test. Our objective is to determine the predictive value of the baseline RNA signature on OS in the management of mPC and assess the sensitivity and specificity of this test. ACOBIOM has developed two RNA signatures predictive of treatment response to FFX and gemcitabine in mPC.13 14 They identified nine blood-based gene markers and established a standardised real-time PCR protocol. Clinical validity was also confirmed through the GemciPANC study (NCT03599154) and two prospective cohorts: BACAP (Base Clinico-Biologique Nationale sur l'AdenoCarcinome du Pancréas, NCT02818829) and TSB BioBank (Translational Science Biocore BioBank, University of Wisconsin Carbone Cancer Centre, n=336). Among patients treated with gemcitabine or gemcitabine-Abraxane, those with a positive RNA signature (29%) had a significantly longer PFS (5.3 vs 2.8 months) and OS (10.4 vs 4.8 months).15 Similarly, FFX-treated patients with a positive RNA signature had a PFS of 14.3 versus 3.5 months and an OS of 23 versus 7.7 months.16 In the GABRINOX-2 study, we propose the prospective collection of blood samples to evaluate RNA signatures as a non-invasive biomarker for predicting treatment sensitivity to FFX and gemcitabine, thereby validating these preliminary findings. Given the challenges associated with tumour biopsies (heterogeneity and limited accessibility), this approach has been designed to require only a simple blood sample, collected during a routine follow-up visit as part of standard clinical care.

This study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. One limitation lies in the design of the study. Patients are theoretically scheduled to receive chemotherapy until disease progression, and the standard duration of FFX in the first-line metastatic setting is limited to a maximum of 6 months, that is, 12 courses, due to cumulative oxaliplatin-induced neurotoxicity. Accordingly, our protocol was designed to ensure that patients in both arms receive comparable maximum cumulative doses of oxaliplatin—specifically, up to 12 courses per arm. This corresponds to 6 months of treatment in Arm B (control arm: FFX alone) and 12 months in Arm A (GEMBRAX followed by FFX), given the sequential nature of treatment administration in the latter. The extended duration in arm A is also needed to accommodate the recommended administration schedules of both GEMBRAX and FFX, especially the intervals between the two regimens. This difference may introduce bias in the assessment of the primary endpoint. In addition, in clinical practice, FFX is frequently associated with significant neurotoxicity, and few patients complete the full 12 cycles. For instance, in the ACCORD11 trial,5 the median number of FFX cycles was 10 (range: 1–47). This parameter will be considered in the analysis of the results. The benefit-risk balance of this intensified treatment strategy will also be carefully evaluated, particularly regarding safety and QoL. The lack of reimbursement for nab-paclitaxel in France may limit access to GEMBRAX as a second-line option in arm B on disease progression. However, all second-line treatments can affect OS. It is also important to emphasise that no randomised trials have established GEMBRAX as the standard second-line therapy in this setting. Nevertheless, the impact of all second-line therapies on OS will be carefully accounted for in the analysis.
