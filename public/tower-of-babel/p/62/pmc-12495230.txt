Curated cases from the TB expert network: Unplugged! Series: Use of plasma microbial cell-free DNA metagenomic sequencing to diagnose Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Madeline Cohodes, Anmary Fernandez, David Ashkin, Caitlin Reed, Sarah Park, Niaz Banaei, Monica Fung, Karen Jacobson, Neela D Goswami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 52-year-old U.S.-born man with diabetes and a kidney transplant 3 months prior presented with fever of unknown origin and left-sided weakness. On admission, he was found to have right internal carotid artery thrombus and cerebral infarct. He developed respiratory failure and underwent treatment for Pseudomonas bacteremia. Fevers continued through hospital day 30 and computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrated an esophageal mass, hepatic lesions, and pulmonary nodules, which were new compared to pre-transplant imaging 2 years prior. While awaiting results from acid-fast bacilli (AFB) cultures from sputum and tissue, plasma microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) metagenomic sequencing was ordered on day 29 and was positive for Mycobacterium Tuberculosis (Mtb). Based on the results of mcfDNA sequencing, clinical presentation, and radiographic findings, a diagnosis of tuberculosis disease was made, and anti-tuberculosis treatment was initiated.While awaiting results from acid-fast bacilli (AFB) cultures and molecular studies from sputum and tissue, plasma microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) metagenomic sequencing was ordered on day 29 and was positive for Mycobacterium Tuberculosis (Mtb).The patient was discharged on hospital day 60; 27 days after discharge, 58 days after the Mtb PCR resulted positive from tissue biopsy, and 60 days after the positive mcfDNA, sputum cultures returned positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

The following case was presented April 4,2024 as part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention & Tuberculosis Centers of Excellence TB Expert Network: Unplugged! series.

Dr. Fernandez, adult infectious diseases specialist: A 52-year-old U.S.-born man with history of deceased- donor kidney transplant 3 months prior, status- post induction with alemtuzumab and maintained on tacrolimus and mycophenolate mofetil immunosuppression, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension presented to the emergency room with altered mental status after a fall at home. Vital signs were blood pressure 190/87, heart rate 131, respiratory rate 20, temperature 39.7° C, an oxygen saturation of 98 %. He was found to have new left-sided upper and lower extremity weakness and a left ear laceration along with complete hemianopia and mild sensory loss. He underwent rapid stroke evaluation; neuroimaging was significant for right internal carotid artery thrombus with right posterior watershed territory infarct, right apical petrositis, and right otomastoiditis with right maxillary sinus disease with possible internal mycetoma. On the day of admission, he rapidly progressed to acute hypoxic respiratory failure requiring intubation.

Blood cultures grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa and intravenous meropenem was initiated. Urine culture and repeat blood cultures were negative for growth. Despite treatment of bacteremia, the patient continued to have persistent fevers through hospital day 30. Mycophenolate mofetil was stopped and tacrolimus target level was adjusted to 6–8 ng/mL during hospitalization in the setting of persistent fevers.

Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen and pelvis demonstrated an esophageal mass as well as hepatic lesions and pulmonary nodules, which were new compared to an unremarkable pre-transplant CT chest from 2 years prior. Differential diagnosis included malignancy with metastases, septic emboli, histoplasmosis, disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), disseminated aspergillosis with secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, another disseminated fungal infection in the setting of immunocompromise, non-infectious etiology (e.g. drug fever), or an alternative unknown pathogen. The patient had no reported prior international travel.

QuantiFERON-TB Gold was also performed, which returned as indeterminate. The patient was noted to have had two pre-transplant QuantiFERON-TB Gold tests that had also resulted indeterminate.

Dr. David Ashkin, pulmonology and critical care specialist: We see a lot of serum QuantiFERON-TB Gold tests ordered in the hospital, often because clinicians are wanting to use a blood-based test to evaluate for tuberculosis disease (TB). It is important to remember that this test is actually not incredibly helpful for a diagnosis of TB. [[1], [2]] This test is in the category of interferon gamma release assays (IGRAs), which are primarily used as a screening tool for asymptomatic persons at risk of TB and as a diagnostic tool for latent TB infection. Once someone is sick with suspicion for TB disease; however, often their immune response is so altered that their IGRA result does not have a good correlation with their likelihood of active clinical disease, and is therefore not useful, such as in this case. Sometimes a positive IGRA can increase a clinician’s pretest probability of disease alongside a constellation of other findings, but we often see a bigger problem – clinicians cross TB off the list when the IGRA returns negative, and they shouldn’t do this.

Dr. Fernandez: On hospital day 29, he underwent further diagnostics with bone marrow biopsy, liver biopsy, and blood draw for plasma microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) metagenomic sequencing. We pursued mcfDNA sequence testing because the patient was continuing to do poorly, and we were not certain how quickly pathology would yield a diagnosis, if one at all. Tissues from the biopsies were sent for AFB culture and M tb PCR testing. The patient continued to clinically deteriorate despite broad-spectrum antibiotics and was transferred to the intensive care unit on hospital day 31. On day 31, mcfDNA sequencing detected Mycobacterium tuberculosis. On day 33 the Mtb PCR results returned positive as well.

Dr. Sarah Park, Medical Director, Medical Affairs at Karius, Pediatric Infectious Disease specialist:

The The Karius Spectrum™ is a type of mcfDNA sequencing performed using Illumina NextSeq to extract and sequence mcfDNA with an average of 20 million reads per sample. Multiple controls are employed to account for, for example, carry-over, sequencing bias, metagenomic sequencing quality, and sample mix-ups. Sequences are then mapped to > 20,000 clinical-grade references in the Karius-curated library to identify and report mcfDNA for up to 1,000 microbes. For each microbe detected, the absolute plasma concentration of mcfDNA is reported in molecules per microliter (MPM). [3].

Plasma mcfDNA sequencing is a pathogen-agnostic test best suited for patients with a broad differential diagnosis for infections, whether single or polymicrobial—e.g., immunocompromised patients with high clinical probability of infection. For severely ill patients, especially, the non-invasive nature and rapid turnaround time (median time from sample receipt to delivery of the reported result, 26 h) [4] may be beneficial in optimizing antimicrobial therapy. [5].

The clinical impact of mcfDNA sequencing, defined as change in patient management based on results, has been reported in four different studies at their respective institutions ranging from 30–59 %. [[6], [7], [8], [9]] The variation in impact likely reflects differences in adjudication of mcfDNA detections as well as the composition of the patients for whom the test is performed, the conditions for ordering mcfDNA sequencing, and the timing of sample collection (e.g., early or late in the course, whether pre- or post-antimicrobial therapy or procedures). Some studies have reported a much lower clinical impact. However, these studies highlight the need for standardization in definitions used for adjudicating. [10] As has been pointed out by Muller et al., not all would agree that clinical impact should equate only to a specific change in patient management. [11] For example, confirmation of conventional test results or clinical suspicion which does not result in change in antimicrobial therapy might still be considered a positive impact while a negative detection might support a non-infectious etiology. [[6], [7],9,11] The high sensitivity of mcfDNA sequencing (e.g., 93 % in one study [12] and 80 % in another8) might be countered by the reported low specificity (e.g., 59 % [Rossoff] and 58 % [Yu], respectively), which may be expected with the potential to detect translocating commensal microbes that cross a compromised mucosal barrier. However, it should be noted that at least 51 % of all commercial detections have reported only a single microbe, and 23 %, two microbes, suggesting that polymicrobial detections are not as common as some might think. [4] That said, diagnostic stewardship is critical to ensure the optimal yield from mcfDNA sequencing as is involvement of infectious disease experts to appropriately interpret the detections. [11].

Dr. Banaei, Clinical Pathology Specialist: mcfDNA sequencing is a broad test that can be used to detect a variety of pathogens. However, looking at the clinical impact of mcfDNA it has a low specificity as it also detects commensal organisms such as flora from the GI tract. For organisms such as Mtb and Legionella pneumophila, positive results from a mcfDNA test do seem to be accurate. Yet, the positive impact of metagenomic sequencing tests overall is relatively low; 7.3 % was reported in a multicenter study. [10] Therefore, you need to practice diagnostic stewardship because you may detect a lot of DNA from translocated bugs in the plasma rather than the pathogen that is driving the infectious process.

When tuberculosis is suspected, using a more targeted approach such as target amplification followed by detection via PCR from a sputum or biopsy specimen can have a high positive predictive value although a relatively low sensitivity. While there are no data using direct comparisons between mcfDNA, and a targeted approach, the sensitivity shown in these studies suggests that a targeted approach may have greater diagnostic sensitivity than mcfDNA. [[13], [14]].

Dr. Park: With regard to tuberculosis, a review of five separate publications that included patients in whom Mtb was detected found the positive result of sequencing was received days to weeks earlier than the usual test results, allowing the appropriate therapeutic regimen to be started earlier. Further, despite the small number of patients with positive detection of Mtb (n = 7), there were no false positive detections. [7,[15], [16], [17], [18]] While clinicians should consider TB-specific diagnostic tests when TB is high on their differential, for patients with a non-specific clinical presentation of an infectious process and a broad differential diagnosis, detection of M tb by pathogen-agnostic plasma mcfDNA sequencing may be impactful if the data leads to earlier appropriate TB treatment initiation with good outcomes. If TB is the leading diagnosis, then TB-specific testing is warranted. However, if the differential diagnosis is broad as it was in this patient, mcfDNA sequencing is a very reasonable option to consider.

Dr. Karen Jacobson, Infectious Disease Specialist: This testing is very exciting but is too expensive for most laboratories to perform regularly. However, it fits the niche for disseminated infection where TB is on the differential, and there are other bacterial infections on the differential as well. It may be difficult to rest the entire clinical diagnosis on a single mcfDNA test result. Maybe the ideal scenario for using a mcfDNA test is for immunocompromised patients where there are difficulties obtaining good responses on other testing.

Dr. Ashkin: We are seeing this test used to cast a wide net in fevers of unknown origin. In immunocompromised individuals where there may be clinical findings or suspicion for infection, a positive test that arises from a battery of bloodwork shines light on TB where it may not have otherwise been high on the differential diagnosis. A positive mcfDNA test, however, should always be followed by routine and more specific diagnostic tests for TB, since these tests lend additional critical clinical information. If a clinician is already suspecting TB, they may get more rapid and specific diagnostic information from TB PCR of sputum or non-respiratory samples or from acid-fast bacilli (AFB) stains of tissue, rather than using mcfDNA upfront. The diagnostic choice comes down to what makes the most sense from a timeliness, cost, and practicality standpoint, and there may be conflicting preferences of the clinician, patient, hospital system, and public health.

Dr. Fernandez: Based on the results of the mcfDNA sequencing, the clinical picture, and the radiographic findings, a diagnosis of TB disease was made and anti-tuberculosis treatment with high-dose Rifabutin 600 mg, Isoniazid 300 mg with Pyridoxine 50 mg, Pyrazinamide 2000 mg, and Ethambutol 1200 mg orally daily along with Levofloxacin 750 mg every other day was initiated. Prednisone 40 mg daily was added to address host inflammation in the setting of central nervous system disease. Rifabutin was used instead of rifampin to avoid drug-drug interactions, and levofloxacin was added in the setting of suspected high burden of disease.

Dr. Ashkin: Plasma microbial cell-free DNA sequencing has clinical utility in the diagnosis of MTB. Specifically, turnaround timeline may allow a more rapid diagnosis compared to TB-specific PCR/culture, and pathogen-agnostic technology may be useful in scenarios where TB is one of multiple infectious considerations or, in a fever of unknown origin, where the pre-test probability of infection is high and there are challenges or concerns with traditional TB diagnostics. However, currently, it can be cost-prohibitive, and interpretation of findings requires expert interpretation and validation with standard of care microbiology. In many if not most situations where TB is suspected, our traditional diagnostics may continue to make more sense as the upfront diagnostic tool. Karius and other similar tests require diagnostic stewardship and continued evaluations to optimize clinical utility. Regardless, its use is increasingly prevalent in inpatient settings, like in this situation.

Dr. Monica Fung, Infectious Disease Specialist: Current U.S. national guidelines on transplantation do not address mcfDNA sequencing in diagnostic evaluations for infectious pathogens including Mtb in organ transplant recipients. For this patient, it is unclear if his tuberculosis disease was a result of (1) donor-derived transmission or (2) reactivation of latent TB infection present pre-transplant. [19] American Society of Transplantation guidelines recommend screening for latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) in all transplant candidates either by tuberculin skin test (TST) or IGRA. If positive, therapy for LTBI is recommended. [20].

This patient, however, had an indeterminate IGRA pre-transplant. Recommended treatment for latent and active tuberculosis among solid organ transplant recipients is similar to the general population. Interactions between anti-tuberculous agents and transplant immunosuppressants are important to address. [21] For instance, rifamycin reduces levels of tacrolimus, cyclosporine, and sirolimus, necessitating close monitoring of levels and appropriate dose adjustment to reduce the risk of allograft rejection. [22] Calcineurin inhibitor levels should be increased 3- to 5-fold in order to combat this. [[23], [24]] Additionally, streptomycin and aminoglycosides need to be closely monitored as they can increase nephrotoxicity when combined with calcineurin inhibitors. Fluoroquinolones can be used as an alternative. [25].

Dr. Fernandez: The patient was unable to produce spontaneous or induced sputum. On day 38, bone marrow biopsy was reported as variably cellular marrow with trilineage hematopoiesis, mild dyserythopoeisis, and 1 % blasts. Few scattered hemophagocytic histiocytes were present. No granulomas were identified, and no growth was seen in AFB culture. Liver biopsy performed on day 40 showed granulomatous hepatitis, with AFB and Fite stains positive for acid-fast bacilli. Iron stain had 4 + marked mixed Kupffer cells and hepatocellular siderosis.

The patient was discharged on hospital day 60. Twenty-seven days after discharge, and 58 days after the positive mcfDNA, lung tissue cultures returned positive for Mtb. By day 99 while in rehab, the patient had completed the intensive phase of TB treatment and Pyrazinamide and Ethambutol were discontinued.

Dr. Ashkin: We are all familiar with the fact that Mtb cultures take a bit of time to confirm a TB diagnosis, often several weeks. Before even bringing mcfDNA sequencing into the picture, more rapid diagnosis can be made with molecular testing, and this testing can be done not only on respiratory samples, but on tissue samples as well. So, it is entirely possible that this patient may have had the same good clinical outcome in the absence of mcfDNA sequencing. The clinician may have chosen to empirically start this patient on TB medications given his immunosuppression as well as the clinical picture and liver pathology showing granulomas with positive AFB staining, for example. That said, there are challenges obtaining tissue biopsies in many hospitalized patients, so we may continue to see this test used in specific situations and need to stay abreast of its benefits as well as limitations.
