Intermediate Care Unit performance being properly assessed?
Authors: Silvia Accordino, Ciro Canetta, Fabiola B Sozzi, Francesco Blasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntermediate Care Units (ImCU) are worldwide considered as an intermediate setting of care between standard wards and intensive care units (ICU), specifically addressed to critically ill patients admitted from Emergency Departments, stepping down from ICUs or stepping up from general wards. ImCU formats vary considerably between institutions, missing univocal criteria of where they should be placed, how be formatted and staffed, what monitoring and treatments delivered, which patients admitted, and when discharged. Many published studies focused primarily on mortality as the main variable of interest, while the effects of ImCU implementations on healthcare costs, hospital governance and comprehensive inpatient outcomes remain controversial. A consensus on which measures should be most accurate and based on evidence is still lacking. Appropriate quality-of-care key indicators should concern structures, processes, outcomes and their relationships. The development of a multi-level framework to assess ImCU performance, tailored on resources and management of each specific reality, should consider their functional role on hospital macro-systems, warranting both by patient-level and system-level objectives to ensure effective benchmarking and to provide a substantial support to clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05676-1.

Intermediate Care Units (ImCUs) are worldwide considered as an intermediate setting of care between standard wards and Intensive Care Units (ICU), according to the Progressive Patient Care principle of pooling patients around the acuity of their conditions, medical and nursing needs.

ImCUs are in general characterized by two main recurring features, a nurse-to-patient ratio ≥ 1:4 and the provision of distinctive activities as continuous monitoring and non-invasive ventilation [1], unlike the advanced and invasive support to life-threatening multi-organ insufficiencies provided in ICUs.

However, there is conflicting evidence on the impact of ImCUs on hospital governance and quality of care. Starting from nomenclature (intermediate care, high-dependency, step-up, step-down, or transitional units), ImCU formats differ significantly among countries and institutions in terms of design, location (integrated or co-located in ICU or ward, or stand-alone unit), setting (number of beds), staffing (nurse-to-patient ratio, specialist physicians involved), and assisted patients [1, 2].

The reasons to endorse ImCUs have been primarily linked to benefits for ICUs (as earlier discharges and lower costs), yet ImCUs should also be considered for their role in reducing in-hospital clinical risk due to inappropriate bed use by critical patients in general wards (GW), evaluating costs, patient outcomes and local considerations, as demand, ICU and ward case-mix, and available resources [3].

The ImCU performance assessment remains a broad topic rarely defined in detail. From early experiences in 1970 s to now, studies focused on selected outcomes like ICU transfer rate, in-ImCU/in-hospital length of stay and mortality, but ImCU structural and management characteristics were, and keep on being, very heterogeneous and often underreported [1, 2, 4]. Consensus on which measures are most accurate and based on evidence is still lacking, moreover, the spectrum of ImCU patients is significantly wide, needing raw data adjustment based on case-mix and case-severity.

According to the Donabedian’s structure, process and outcome classification, frequently adopted to monitor and improve the effectiveness of healthcare services [5], an ImCU measurement framework has been proposed considering (1) structure, such as settings and in-hospital locations, (2) process, such as the interventions performed, type of admissions, patient flow and case-mix, (3) outcome, such as mortality, ICU transfers and length of stay, and the relationship between all these components [6].

To support intra-system evaluations and inter-system comparisons, a consensus according to the Utstein-model [7] suggested a template for uniform reporting of standard measures grouped, for emergency departments (ED), into six categories: (1) structure, (2) staffing and governance, (3) population, (4) process time, (5) hospital and health care system, and (6) outcome [8].

The careful evaluation of composite indicators could be helpful despite many of them could have a low rank-ability (the proportion of the variation between systems due to true differences), affected by selection biases and variations of local-specific characteristics or random effects [9].

To develop an extensive systematic assessment of ImCU performance, past literature has been considered to extract a set of key indicators, subsequently testing on them the ImCU studies published online in the current year. The detailed screening and selection process based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) methodology, search terms and inclusion/exclusion criteria to determine eligibility of articles are reported in supplementary material [Suppl.1].

Formulating foreground questions according to the SPICE format [10], “setting” (where) was identified with ImCUs, “perspective” (for whom) with ImCU population, “intervention” (what) with ImCU exposure, “comparison” (what the alternative to the intervention) with ImCU vs no-ImCU exposure or between different ImCUs, “evaluation” (which effects) with outcomes related to ImCU exposure.

Six observational studies were considered. Two Japanese multicentre retrospective studies based on nationwide databases: Ohbe et al. enrolling patients admitted to ImCUs in hospitals with and without ICU from 2016 to 2022 concluded that ImCUs should be placed in hospitals with ICUs otherwise risking worsening of patient outcomes [11], Ikumi et al. categorizing ICU patients into ImCU-group and general ward-group according to post-ICU destination concluded that ImCU-group had lower in-hospital mortality [12]. Two French studies: Beaussier et al. prospectively evaluated the appropriateness of ImCU stays reporting a lower 7-day mortality for patients with an ImCU stay than for those with similar disease severity not admitted to an ImCU [13], Migeon et al. retrospectively examined trends in ImCU utilization founding that nearly one-third of patients were ≥ 80 years old with high rehospitalization and mortality rate [14]. An Italian monocentre before-and-after ImCU implementation study reported a reduction in hospital stays, ICU transfers from general ward and admissions from ED, without affecting 30-day mortality [15]. Lastly, a Tanzanian prospective monocentre study described high-dependency patients and outcomes recognizing in sepsis and non-communicable diseases the most common diagnoses with a moderate accuracy of early warning scores in predicting mortality [16]. A summary of characteristics and data reported in the overmentioned six studies is depicted in supplementary material [Suppl.2].

The lack of standardized appropriateness criteria is a major topic leading clinical studies but, to date, research has primarily focused on results at the ImCUs themselves, without structured comparisons and with the recognized limitation of observational studies to represent an association and not a causation, even for big data samples and multicentre studies, while monocentric and time-limited experiences could raise questions about sustainability of solutions.

However, distinct well-defined objectives, designs and target population, ensure that each study contributes in a complementary manner to the other in outlining clinical insights to appropriately define the characteristics of ImCU workload, how their mission is intended for high-care patients admitted from ED, stepped-up from GWs or -down from ICUs and which ones could benefit from ImCU care.

To encourage critical appraisal and following the SPICE exploration questions [10], Donabedian- [5] and Utstein-style [8] frameworks were embedded with indicators adapted for ImCU to improve comparability within and between reports [Table 1].

The table shows how studies report an average of 42.2% [34.3%−50.0%] of the recognized items, mainly represented at the ImCU patient-level, as caseload, in-flow, and delivered activities warranting effective focused clinical results. Differently, the empty cells at comparisons and system-level crosswise reveal missing data mainly on healthcare context and enlarged hospital-management and -effects.

ImCUs can be envisioned as “flexible servers”, customized on each unique context and tailored on patient needs, staff competencies and resource availability [17, 18]; they are deeply involved in the in-hospital critical care chain, enhancing the need to consider key indicators at the ImCU itself, at the ICU and general wards, and at the hospital level and to complete patient-centred outcomes with hospital-based evaluations due to the complementary vision of the two approaches.

In conclusion, a comprehensive assessment of ImCU performance, addressed to deliver the appropriate level of care in the right setting, at the right time, to the right patients, should include adequate key indicators drafted on the ImCU functional role within hospital macro-systems [Figure 1]. The development of a multi-level measurement framework, adaptable to different experiences, models or studies, could reinforce evidence reached both by patient-level and system-level objectives to ensure effective benchmarking and to provide a substantial support to clinical practice.
