Glenohumeral cerclage with anterior cruciate ligament guide suture retrieval for instability in reverse shoulder replacement
Authors: Nathan Elder, Michael Vollant, Madison Kelly, Andrew Boltuch
Published: 2025

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) procedures have increased significantly in the last 25 years with an increase of nearly 700% in recent years.7 As the number of primary total shoulder replacements continues to rise, so too does the incidence of associated complications. Several studies have evaluated the complication profile associated with rTSA. The overall complication rate following rTSA has been shown to broadly range from 9.4% to 24% with a trend of decreasing complication rates noted in more recent studies likely attributable to improvements in surgical technique and implant design.3,12 Despite the improvements in complication rates, instability continues to be one of the leading causes of surgical complications, frequently requiring further surgical management.3, 4, 5,8 Parada et al found instability to be the second most common complication at an incidence of 1.4% with two-thirds of these cases requiring a revision operation.8

Revision surgeries often fall short of desired outcomes in shoulder arthroplasty, particularly in managing dislocations. Olson et al conducted a systematic review revealing an overall dislocation rate of 2.6% for rTSA, but notably a much higher incidence of 35% among revision cases. Furthermore, they found a persistent rate of instability for revisions in 66% of cases.6 Cronin et al retrospectively evaluated 50 patients with instability in the setting of rTSA at a minimum of a 2-year follow-up. Ten patients were successfully treated with a closed reduction, while 40 patients required a revision operation. After revision surgery, 17 (42.5%) patients reported continued instability, with 14 (35%) undergoing additional procedures.2

Managing instability in both primary and revision rTSA poses significant challenges. When approaching these operations, the source of instability should initially be considered, which Melbourne et al have broadly categorized into loss of compression, loss of containment, and impingement.5 Once the mechanism of instability is identified, a revision can be planned to stabilize the shoulder. The specific methods of which are beyond the scope of this article but in general, implant position should be optimized, all impinging structures should be removed/released, and increasing lateralization with the placement of larger implants are generally beneficial. Furthermore, advanced techniques including allograft-prosthetic composites and revision prosthetics have been described to facilitate stabilization in revision scenarios.5,10 Regardless of the approach to instability in rTSA, recurrent instability continues to be a major complication, commonly being solved with less-than-desirable interventions that includes hemiarthroplasty and resection arthroplasty.6 Tashjian et al was the first to report on a novel suture cerclage technique to help solve recurrent instability in a series of 3 rTSA patients.11 In these cases, a looped suture retriever was placed through a drill hole medial to the baseplate. The looped end was retrieved in the posterior shoulder and used to shuttle the high tensile sutures. Our technique for glenohumeral cerclage aims to build on this previously introduced concept with the novel approach of using an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) guide for suture passage and retrieval through the glenoid.

Technique description

Instruments required

2.4 mm Beath pin.

Looped high-tensile suture.

High tensile suture x2.

Oval tibial ACL guide.

Hewson Suture Passer.

2 mm drill bit.

Positioning and draping

The patient is placed in a standard beach chair position with special attention made to ensure adequate exposure of the posterior scapular region. At the time of final positioning, the posterior scapula and posterior glenohumeral joint is palpated to ensure the patient is sufficiently lateral on the beach chair table. The drapes are then placed with care to ensure they are appropriately medial in relation to the to the posterolateral scapula.

Step 2

Drill the Beath pin through the posterior glenoid on forward. Once past the posterior glenoid margin, check to ensure it is captured within the ACL guide by detaching the drill sleeve from the capture guide and pulling on the guide. Once drill capture is ensured, add the passing looped suture through the loop of the beath pin. Advance the pin on reciprocal mode of the drill through the skin posteriorly as seen in Figure 2. Once through the skin, the drill may be utilized from the back to pull the pin in a posterior direction. Maintaining reciprocal mode on the drill past the glenoid will help avoid damage to neurovascular structures and prevent the looped suture from entangling the Beath pin.Figure 2Advancement of Beath pin. Step 6

A 2 mm drill bit may then be used to place bone tunnels through the lateral aspect of the proximal humerus. The Hewson suture passer can then be used to shuttle the sutures from the posterior aspect of the humerus out anteriorly. Conversely, holes within the proximal humeral implant may be used to capture the suture, particularly when a revision of the humeral stem was not required.

Step 7

With the arm placed in 10°-30° of abduction, 20°-30° of forward elevation, and neutral rotation, the sutures may be sequentially tied completing the glenohumeral cerclage. When elevating the arm after the sutures are tied, it is notable that there is laxity within the cerclage as the 2 points of suture capture converge, allowing no significant limitation to forward elevation.

Case example

This is a 71-year-old female who presents with a previous surgical history of reverse shoulder replacement 4 years prior for inflammatory arthritis completed at an outside facility. The patient denied any significant pain prior to the incident, but awoke from sleep to shoulder pain and decreased function. She presented to the emergency department where she was diagnosed with a shoulder dislocation that was reduced. She was then sent to our institution for further evaluation and presented in a sling (Fig. 7). The patient was kept in a sling for 2 weeks after which range of motion was allowed. The patient initially did well until she presented with a recurrent dislocation three months later (Fig. 8) and the decision was made to proceed with revision shoulder arthroplasty. Preoperative infection workup included aspiration and blood work without significant findings.Figure 7Anteroposterior and scapular-Y radiographs demonstrating a well reduced reverse shoulder replacement with evidence of inferior scapular notching.Figure 8Anteroposterior radiograph taken at 3 months following the initial dislocation demonstrating a recurrent anteroinferior humeral dislocation in the setting of reverse shoulder arthroplasty.

At the time of revision, significant metallosis was noted throughout the joint and the baseplate was found to be loose without ingrowth. Five intraoperative frozen sections were taken without polymorphonuclear leukocytes noted per high power field and 5 soft tissue samples were taken for culture and ultimately resulted as negative. The baseplate was revised using the alternative spine line achieving excellent purchase. A lateralized 36 + 6 glenosphere was used with a +10 humeral tray and a +4 semiconstrained polyethylene. Due to the excellent purchase obtained during baseplate implantation, the glenohumeral cerclage technique was used for added stability, however its use in acute baseplate revisions should be carefully considered. At her 1-year postoperative visit (Fig. 9), the patient has a visual analogue scale pain of 1/10, active forward elevation to 125, external rotation to 40, and internal rotation to L3. Discussion

Instability following reverse shoulder replacement continues to be a challenging complication in the setting of reverse shoulder replacement. The techniques for obtaining stability generally involve increasing the soft tissue tension by lateralization or distalization of the construct, increasing constraint, and optimizing soft tissue releases/removal of impinging structures. The glenohumeral cerclage technique provides another method to improve stability in revision scenarios but should not be used in isolation without the optimization of the previously stated factors. The original technique involves the passage of suture in an anterior-to-posterior direction after drilling through the glenoid neck adjacent to the glenoid post. A looped suture retriever is used to shuttle sutures through the glenoid. Once through the glenoid the, high tensile sutures are passed either directly around the humeral component or through bone tunnels through the greater tuberosity. To improve the retrievability of sutures, a posterior-to-anterior approach has also been described.

Other described techniques for instability involve a case report of capsular reconstruction using an Achilles allograft to reinforce the shoulder capsule through bone tunnels, suture anchors, and suture buttons achieving postoperative stability at one year follow-up.1 An acromiohumeral cerclage technique has also been described, using drill holes through the acromion to secure the humeral component with high tensile sutures achieving stability in all 10 patients with average follow-up of 23 months.9 The glenohumeral cerclage technique, first introduced by Tashijan et al, successfully achieved stability in a case series of 3 patients undergoing revision reverse shoulder replacements for instability with short term follow-up.11 Similarly, our technique incorporates glenohumeral cerclage to manage recurrent instability with the novel approach of using an ACL guide for suture passage and retrieval through the glenoid. These approaches highlight evolving strategies to enhance outcomes in challenging revision scenarios of rTSA.

Conclusion

We present a modification of the technique for passing and retrieving sutures in the management of instability associated with rTSA through the use of glenohumeral cerclage. By using the ACL guide, high tensile suture may be shuttled through the glenoid and captured improving the ease of suture management associated with this technique. As the incidence of rTSA increases, novel techniques in revision surgery are necessary for addressing the associated complications.
