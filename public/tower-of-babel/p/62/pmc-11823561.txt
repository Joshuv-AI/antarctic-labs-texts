Immediate Hemodynamic Changes during Slow Uninostril and Alternate Nostril Breathing Patterns in Normotensive Adults
Authors: Vikram Pai, Nitin Borkar, Mohit Nirvan, Meghnath Verma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:This interventional randomized controlled study aimed to evaluate the immediate hemodynamic effects of slow uninostril and alternate nostril breathing (ANB) patterns in normotensive adults.Methods:A total of 132 normotensive adults aged 25–50 years were randomly allocated into one of four groups: Chandra anulom vilom (CAV), Surya anulom vilom (SAV), Anuloma viloma/ANB, or control. Hemodynamic parameters including systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and pulse rate were measured at baseline, once in between two prāṇāyāma rounds, postintervention, and 5 min after intervention completed.Results:The study showed a significant decrease in SBP and DBP postintervention in the CAV and ANB groups compared to the SAV group. Furthermore, MAP was significantly lower in the ANB group relative to the control group. These results suggest that different types of prāṇāyāma can induce immediate changes in hemodynamic parameters even in nonpractitioners of yoga.Conclusion:The study provides insights into the potential benefits of specific prāṇāyāma techniques on hemodynamic changes in normotensive individuals, indicating that such techniques could be beneficial in managing BP and overall hemodynamic status.

Introduction

Yoga is an ancient Indian science that maintains health and well-being. Prāṇāyāma is the breathing techniques used in yoga. Prāṇāyāma has been reported to have beneficial effects on health by reducing physiological and psychosocial stress, which is mediated through the improvement of autonomic functions.[123] Recently, it has been seen that psychosocial stress levels are on the rise in modern society due to lifestyle changes. Chronic psychosocial stress is associated with an increased risk of noncommunicable diseases including hypertension.[4] Many studies have shown that slow breathing and yogic breathing can cause a modest reduction in blood pressure (BP).[567] Earlier studies have been conducted on the immediate effects of uninostril and alternate nostril breathing (ANB) on BP. They suggested that differential nostril breathing produces distinct autonomic changes, but they involve regular practitioners of yoga.[8910]

Prāṇāyāma is considered important component of yogic practice. They involve controlling the rate, depth, and method of breathing to achieve certain physiological effects. Ancient sages have observed that both nostrils do not work simultaneously, and Yoga explained this phenomenon as the alternation of the flow of subtle pranic energy in the Ida nadi and Pingala nadi.[111] Research has revealed that the flow of air through the nose is greater in one nostril than the other due to temporary obstruction caused by the erectile tissue’s asymmetry in the nasal passages. This obstruction is periodic and alternates between nostrils, a phenomenon known as the nasal cycle, which is closely linked to autonomic arousal and is a sign of asymmetry in brain function.[12] Nasal cycles are regulated by lateralized neural rhythms that are integral to the hypothesis of the basic rest–activity cycle.[13] The mechanism of breathing and its corresponding cardiorespiratory mechanism are well-studied and fascinating to physiologists. For example, during deep inspiration, the heart rate tends to increase. This occurs because the stretch receptors in the lungs send impulses through the vagus nerve that inhibit the cardioinhibitory area in the medulla oblongata, which slows down the heart rate. However, during inspiration, this process is reversed, and the heart rate increases as the tonic vagal discharge decreases.[14]

Research has related the effects of unilateral forced nostril breathing to alterations in cerebral activity, cognition, and other autonomic-dependent phenomena.[13] Slow-breathing Prāṇāyāma has been shown to cause decreased oxygen consumption, decreased heart rate, decreased BP, increased theta wave amplitude in electroencephalogram recordings, and increased parasympathetic activity.[15] Many studies have evaluated the effects of slow uninostril and/or ANB on various physiological parameters such as cardiovascular changes[12381617181920] respiratory parameters,[1916] autonomic functions,[29161721] and brain functions.[222324] Many studies have also evaluated the effects of slow uninostril and/or ANB on hypertension[25] and anxiety.[26]

This study intends to study the immediate hemodynamic effects of slow uninostril and slow alternate nostril Yoga breathing practices on 132 participants. This study aimed to assess the physiological effects of certain modified yoga breathing practices on hemodynamic parameters such as systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and pulse rate (PR) in healthy adults who are nonpractitioners of yoga. In this study, slow yogic uninostril breathing practices such as Chandra anulom vilom (CAV) or left nostril breathing, Surya anulom vilom (SAV) or right nostril breathing, and anulom vilom or ANB were used as interventions.

Methods

This was an interventional, randomized controlled study. The faculty and staff of the AIIMS, Raipur, Chhattisgarh, India, were included in the study. To determine the sample size, the effect size (Cohen’s d) (0.249) was calculated by pooling the results of two previous studies[89] that were performed on the same intervention and outcomes in healthy subjects. The sample size (n = 129) was calculated for a paired t-test with α = 0.05, and power = 80% using G*power software (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). In total, 132 healthy male and female volunteers were selected using convenience sampling. Normotensive individuals aged 25–50 years were included in this study. Individuals suffering from any acute or chronic illnesses undergoing or requiring medical management, those with smoking and/or substance addiction, incidentally detected hypertensives, those diagnosed and undergoing treatment for psychiatric and endocrine disorders, diagnosed cases of deviated nasal septum, and regular yoga practitioners for more than 3 months were excluded from the study. After screening and obtaining written informed consent from all eligible subjects, they were randomly assigned using simple randomization technique to one of four groups, namely, the CAV, SAV, AV, or control groups. Randomization was done using computer software by a person not related to the study. Thirty-three volunteers were assigned to each group.

Each study participant was asked to sit comfortably on a chair with an erect spine to ensure correct posture and optimal lung capacity during the breathing exercises. Participants were then instructed to perform the specific type of breathing exercise (CAV/SAV/AV) according to the group assigned to them after the randomization process was completed. Each Prāṇāyāma consisted of inhalation lasting for a duration of 4–5 s, which was then followed by a postinhalation pause lasting 1–2 s. Subsequently, participants performed a slow exhalation which also lasted for 4–5 s, followed by a postexhalation pause of 1–2 s. Each inhalation and exhalation cycle thus lasted for a total of 10–12 s. Participants were made to continue this pattern for a total of 60 rounds. To avoid fatigue, a resting period of 1 min was provided after every 30 rounds of breathing exercises. In contrast, the control group was asked to sit comfortably on a chair with an erect spine as well, but they were instructed to breathe normally without any specific pattern or regulation. Standard parameters including SBP, DBP, MAP, PR, and oxygen saturation were assessed for each participant using a multipara monitor (Nihon Kohden).

To ensure accuracy, each participant was asked to sit on a chair in a comfortable position. The inflatable cuff of the multipara monitor was gently tied to their left arm, and the pulse oximeter clip was securely attached to their left index finger. In this relaxed position, participants remained for 5 min to get accustomed to the equipment and their surroundings, facilitating accurate readings without interference due to initial nervousness or adjustment issues. Five minutes after completing the intervention, the multipara monitor recorded the BP and PR simultaneously to observe any physiological changes resulting from the breathing exercises. This continuous monitoring throughout the intervention period allowed for the collection of comprehensive and accurate data for analysis.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (Version 6.00) software (Graphpad Software Inc, San Diego, California, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Repeated measures-analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA mixed-model) was used to assess the difference between means of inter- and intragroup within participants followed by Bonferroni posttests. Changes with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The findings of this randomized controlled trial (RCT) indicate that left nostril breathing (CAV), right nostril breathing (SAV), and ANB did not produce very significant changes in BP levels in healthy individuals with no prior experience in yoga practice. Overall, these breathing techniques were found to bring mild-to-moderate hemodynamic changes soon after the practice. Similarly, SBP decreased with very high statistical significance in ANB when compared to the SAV group, but because there was statistical significance at baseline, the comparison may not be appropriate to consider.

Several studies have reported a significant decrease in SBP following ANB and other slow uninostril breathing. One study noted a reduction in SBP after immediate and 8-week ANB practice in both sexes and in hypertensive individuals after ANB and breath awareness. The effects on DBP are less consistent, showing reductions only after 8 weeks of ANB in females or in hypertensive individuals.[1] ANB can decrease SBP and DBP in patients with essential hypertension, as informed by an RCT on 90 patients of both sexes. Interestingly, no clinically significant changes were found in any of the parameters studied in female participants.[27] Another study involving regular yoga practitioners conducted to see changes in heart rate variability after right and left uninostril breathing found no significant difference in heart rate variability immediately after the intervention.[10] However, it is important to note that previous studies have been conducted with regular practitioners of prāṇāyāma and this study included nonpractitioners of prāṇāyāma.

Normal breathing ratios in healthy adults are about 1:1:2, but prāṇāyāma techniques use a 1:2 ratio or more with prolonged exhalation, enhancing parasympathetic dominance and relaxation compared to equal ratio breathing.[28] Normal shallow breathing replenishes air only at the base of the lungs in comparison to deep breathing, which recirculates air all over the lungs.[29] The probable mechanism of action of the breathing techniques in this study likely involves the activation of pulmonary stretch receptors in the lungs and bronchi during sustained slow breathing. This increases the frequency and duration of inhibitory neural impulses, as in the Hering–Breuer reflex, which causes the onset of parasympathetic tone in the skeletal muscle blood vessels, leading to widespread vasodilatation, thus causing a decrease in peripheral resistance and decrease in BP.[228] Slow and deep breathing could also reduce stress-related hyperactivity of the central autonomic nervous system through vagal modulation.[28] ANB, in particular, may lead to reduced systolic and DBP due to enhanced parasympathetic activity, which promotes relaxation and vasodilation.[18213031] While many studies suggest immediate effects of yoga breathing on BP in healthy adults, results vary across techniques and populations. The effects depend on the specific technique, practice duration, and participant characteristics. Immediate effects may differ from long-term or clinical population effects. Regular yoga breathing practice over time may lead to more consistent and sustained BP changes.

It is important to interpret these findings within the limitations and context of the study. Several factors may have contributed to the lack of significant effects observed in this study. First, all the participants of this study were nonpractitioners of prāṇāyāma or any Yoga form. The unfamiliarity with these techniques may have influenced the way participants responded to the intervention compared to individuals who are regular practitioners. Second, the immediate effects of nostril breathing techniques on BP may be influenced by various individual factors such as baseline BP levels, overall health, and individual responsiveness to the techniques. For instance, those with high baseline BP levels might exhibit different responses compared to those with normal baseline levels. In addition, the duration and intensity of the intervention may also play a crucial role. This study focused on a single session of nostril breathing techniques, which may not be sufficient to induce significant changes in physiological parameters such as BP. In contrast, long-term or regular practice of nostril breathing techniques could potentially yield different and perhaps more pronounced outcomes. These points to the need for further research incorporating extended intervention periods to better understand the potential benefits of these techniques. Furthermore, it is important to consider the possibility of placebo effects. The participants in the intervention groups were aware that they were practicing prāṇāyāma, which could have influenced their perception of their own BP levels and overall well-being. Knowing they were part of an active intervention group might have led to a psychological effect, causing them to perceive improvements in their BP even if the physiological measurements did not show significant changes. Moreover, the sample size and demographic characteristics of the study participants could also have influenced the results.

Limitations

The absence of a sham intervention group limited our ability to differentiate between the specific effects of nostril breathing techniques and placebo. The inclusion of only healthy individuals with no prior yoga experience may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future studies with larger and more diverse populations, including individuals with preexisting medical conditions, could provide further insights into the effects of nostril breathing techniques on BP.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed mild-to-moderate immediate hemodynamic changes during both slow uninostril and ANB in normotensive adults. These changes included alterations in SBP, DBP, MAP, pulse, and SPO2. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the immediate hemodynamic changes that occur during prāṇāyāma practices in nonpractitioners of prāṇāyāma. These results suggest that slow uninostril breathing and ANB can modulate the autonomic nervous system and induce moderate immediate hemodynamic effects. These findings support the use of prāṇāyāma practices as a mind–body intervention to promote cardiovascular health and overall well-being in normotensive individuals. In addition, the study highlights the importance of studying long-term effects of prāṇāyāma, along with measuring other hemodynamic parameters, such as systolic volume, cardiac output, and peripheral vascular resistance response, to better understand the physiological adaptations and potential benefits of prāṇāyāma practices.
