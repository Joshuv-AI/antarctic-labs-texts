Self-charged polar nematic monopoles and hybrid topological states: intertwining and domain integration
Authors: Yu Zou, Jidan Yang, Zhongjie Ma, Zening Hong, Aile Sun, Xiang Huang, Minghui Deng, Mingjun Huang, Qi-Huo Wei, Satoshi Aya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolar topological solitons are quasi-particles essential in condensed matter physics, with a key challenge being their topological diversification, control, and arrangement. This study demonstrates that confinement asymmetry can tailor and diversify electrically polar topological solitons in fluid ferroelectrics, providing a key pathway to solving the long-standing problem of designing large-scale, complex domain structures. We discover nonclassical electric monopoles and hybrid topological states, including chiral meron and anti-meron variants and their composite hybridization with a nested skyrmion core. They exhibit intrinsic self-charge and interact via nematic elasticity and electric polar interactions. The unique mechanism leads to the formation of multibody topological configurations by intertwining their polar flux. We propose utilizing solitonic structures as fundamental quasiparticles to design large-scale charged arrays in insulating fluids that function as local conductors. The self-organization offers an unprecedented opportunity to realize large-scale engineering of in-plane domain structures that were previously unachievable in traditional ferroelectrics.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Liquid crystals

Introduction

Polar topological solitons—tiny, localised waves of vectorized fields composed of spin or dipoles—possess a dual nature of particle and wave and typically arise in confined non-centrosymmetric matter, such as quantum Hall1, cold atom2, ferromagnet3, and polar liquid crystal systems4–7. Isolated solitons exhibit orientational symmetry distinct from that of the far-field background. As particles, they are topologically protected, hosting intrinsic resistance to fluctuations and external perturbations, and are heralded as ideal candidates for lossless information and optical carriers8,9. Meanwhile, as waves, they are “flexible” in response to external stimuli like magnetic and electric fields10,11. In the last twenty years, a special focus has been placed on generating magnetic topological solitons in ferromagnetic systems12,13. A central discovery was that isolated elementary quasi-particles, such as magnetic skyrmions, merons, hopfions, and stripe textures, can be stabilized by balancing Heisenberg exchange and Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interactions14. As a unique consequence of the vectorized fields, some of the quasi-particles are spontaneously magnetized corona-like particles, corresponding to emergent magnets. The N- and S-poles localize at the center and outer surface of the particle, possibly forming magnetic monopoles like merons15,16. Although ground-state elementary solitons have been well explored, how to combine and stabilise multiple topological textures into organised solitonic assemblies, thereby generating new, distinct phases, remains unclear.

Electrically polar systems (e.g., crystalline ferroelectrics), as mirror systems of magnetics, have recently emerged and have shown numerous electric solitonic counterparts composed of polar flux fields. The exploration of these polar topologies has recently extended to moiré superlattices, where polar meron networks have been identified and characterised through quantitative subdomain markers17,18. Crucially, these topological states have been linked to nonlinear optical signatures like shift photocurrent vortices, providing a sophisticated means of optical detection19. Analogous to their magnetic counterparts, the electric solitons carry electric charges. What types of polar solitons may form, how they arrange in space (e.g., spatial coverage and size), and whether they are stable are far from predictable due to complex polar interactions, including electrostatic and flexoelectric interactions20,21. These factors are expected to determine whether emergent electromagnetic and optical properties unique to electrically topological phases will arise22–24. A few previous observations, though not yet designable and controllable, suggest that assemblies of polar solitons into integrated topological architectures may trigger non-classical and exotic phenomena.

The emerging polar liquid crystals (LCs) with nematic order are true three-dimensional (3D) polar fluids, such as the ferroelectric nematic (NF)25–27. In sharp contrast to crystalline ferroelectrics, polar fluids host a novel nexus between long-range soft orientational elasticity and adjustable polar interactions28–31. Recent studies have revealed diverse polar structures in NF systems, including interface-engineered domains and active droplet-like inclusions, where surface charges significantly affect interfacial structures and dynamics32–34. This multifaceted nexus gifts the system with topological tunability and transformability in response to either the static or dynamic geometrical factors and external fields35–39. In this work, we challenge to construct integrated topological architectures. The study consists of two primary objectives: the discovery of hybrid topological states and the engineering of topological particles into solitonic arrays. Technically, we have designed asymmetric confinement conditions to explore new topological solitonic particles composed of polar flux and their entanglements. Proper coupling between the long-range orientational elasticity, flexoelectricity, and electrostatics triggers the spontaneous formation of chiral meron and nontrivial hybrid meron-toron-skyrmion solitonic structures39. The hybrid topological state is accompanied by different topological dimensionalities, i.e., a mixing of 2D (meron and skyrmion) and 3D (toron) topology. Polar orientational interactions between these particles drive unique domain-structural assemblies, resulting in novel topological assemblies like bi- or triplet-merons and kinked loops. We show that the dynamic self-assembly process can be employed to engineer large-scale polar solitonic arrays with controlled polar and chiral orders of the particles. The polar solitonic network serves as a network of localised electric charges, which can be flexibly manipulated by an ultraweak electric field of the order of 1 mV μm−1.

Results

To explore polar topological solitonic states, we employ NF materials that can produce isolated ferroelectric bubbles emerging from the isotropic (I) liquid5. We mainly focus on a material, 3-ethoxy-4-((4-nitrophenoxy)carbonyl)phenyl 4-methoxy-2-nitrobenzoate (abbreviated RM-OC2). RM-OC2 is an RM734 variant with an ethoxy group and a nitro group attached to the second and third benzene rings, respectively4,5. While it shows a phase sequence of I −67.1 °C - NF −37 °C -Crystal on cooling in bulk, the crystallization is avoided under confinement, at least down to room temperature. When the material undergoes the I-NF transition, NF bubbles with spontaneous twisting emerge from the I background below 67.1 °C, regardless of the system’s lack of inherent chirality5. In this case, there is an equal probability of finding right-handed and left-handed NF bubbles5. To bias the twisting preference, we have doped a small amount of a chiral dopant, 0.3 wt% R811 or S811 for right-handed or left-handed, respectively (Methods). The resulting helielectric nematic (HN*) exhibits a helical pitch of ~10 μm. We have fabricated confinement spaces on glass substrates by photolithography (Methods). The other side of the confinement space is open to air. RM-OC2 is introduced into the confinement space via blade coating. Unlike cylindrical NF bubbles bounded by two flat glass substrates with an equal anchoring condition40,41 (Fig. 1a), the current confined NF experiences distinct anchoring conditions at the bottom flat glass substrate and the open air interface (Fig. 1b). In the former case of a cylindrical NF bubble, an in-plane concentric polar field at the flat bounded glass interfaces (i.e., xy plane) is favored by minimizing the flexoelectric and electrostatic energies. As a result, a polar meron-like topological structure appears as a stable electric monopole (Fig. 1a). In the latter case, the situation becomes nontrivial. Topological stability and structural details can be violated and significantly altered when the bubble anchoring condition or even the bubble shape changes, as in the case of NF bubbles with asymmetric anchoring. We will show that the distinct top and bottom surfaces provide an anchoring asymmetry of sufficient magnitude to serve as an essential and novel source of hybrid topological states (Fig. 1b).

Diversity of polar topological monopoles and hybrids with self-charges

Figure 1c, d show polarized light microscopy (PLM) images for four types of solitons: two being electric monopoles and the rest hybridized topological states. The monopoles correspond to polar chiral (or twisted) merons, dubbed LM and RM (Fig. 1c, i-iii and 1d, i-iii). The other two are hybrid merons hosting a nested skyrmion–toron core, named LH and RH (Figs. 1c, iv-vi and 1d, iv-vi), as confirmed experimentally and numerically below. They appear at different cooling rates: LM and RM under faster cooling (>ca. 0.1 K/min); LH and RH under slower cooling (<ca. 0.02 K/min). In the intermediate cooling rate, monopole and hybrid states coexist (Supplementary Fig. 1). The topological diversity stems from the cooling-rate-dependent nucleation pathway. The nucleation geometry of the NF bubbles changes from cylindrical to hemispherical as the cooling rate decreases. While the cylindrical confinement with flat boundaries favors the axisymmetric formation of monopoles, the hemispherical confinement introduces geometric asymmetry that facilitates the stabilization of hybrid topological solitons (see “Discussion”). Once they are formed, both monopole and hybrid states remain stable, persisting for at least several days at room temperature without structural switching and degradation. Under PLM, these solitons resemble a non-extinction Maltese cross, except the LH and RH clearly demonstrate a distinction between the inner and outer structure. Yet, the texture differs considerably in terms of optical rotation and appears distinct in the defect-core region. Decrossing the analyzer in the anticlockwise (clockwise) manner leads to extinction of LM and LH (RM and RH) bubbles, enabling the visualization of the opposite twisting sense, i.e., the helical handedness of the polar field along the depth z direction. It is essential to note that the twisting sense couples to the screw sense of the meron, which defines the direction of in-plane rotation of polar flux. In a polar meron, the polarization P gradually tilts from in-plane at the boundary to out-of-plane at the core, where the polar flux points either upward or downward along the z-direction. Specifically, for a left-handed system, a clockwise (or anticlockwise) vortex rotation dictates an upward (or downward) core flux (Supplementary Fig. 1). This screw sense becomes particularly significant when considering the intertwining of multiple bodies of polar flux, as discussed later. Fitting the optical transmission with a twisted model at each pixel through the Jones matrix quantifies the spatial distribution of the twisting angle (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3; “Methods”), thereby reconstructing a precise 3D polar structure except for the region near the defect core. Interestingly, the hybridized solitons RH and LH display a non-extinct and circular defect-core-proxy structure under PLM, differentiating them from the nearly extinct and cross-like defect in RM and LM. Remarkably, the circular defect-core-proxy structure appears as the initial structure during the nucleation of NF bubbles (also see Discussion for its origin). It shrinks to the central defect core during the later stage of the NF growth, replaced by a meron-like structure outside it (Supplementary Movie 1). The hybrid topological state can also be clearly visualized by simply decrossing polarizers (Fig. 1c, v and Fig. 1d, v).

To determine fine structures of the hybridized topologies, we employ fluorescence confocal polarizing microscopy42 (FCPM; Methods). The polar field of the RM topology corresponds to a chiral meron that twists along the z-direction in 3D space, as visualized by S-shaped cross-sectional FCPM images at each z-position under linear-polarization excitation (Fig. 1e, i). The polar flux primarily lies in the xy-plane and escapes into the third dimension (along the z-axis) at the center of the NF bubbles, evidenced by a continuous decrease of fluorescence toward the center under both the circular and linear polarized illumination (Fig. 1e, i-iii). To capture topological details, we project localized polar flux onto the order parameter space of the S2 manifold43 (Fig. 1e, iv). The RM topology occupies either the upper or lower half of the S2 manifold, characterized by the topological invariant, i.e., skyrmion number (or 2D topological charge)44,45 Q=±1/2. The sign of Q indicates the occupation of either the northern or southern sphere of the S2 manifold. They correspond to 3D meron variants, in which the traditional 2D meron with an out-of-plane structure is now modified to be accompanied by a twist along the depth direction.

The hybrid soliton RH exhibits similar FCPM images to RM at larger radii, but with a more complicated fluorescence variation near the defect core (Fig. 1f, i-iii). Notably, the circular defect-core-proxy structure demonstrates a circular-dichroism-like characteristic, making a sharp contrast between left-handed and right-handed circular polarization excitations (Fig. 1f, ii and iii). The fluorescence is much stronger under the excitation of the circular polarization with the same handedness as the chiral system than in the case of the opposite handedness. A dark circular area appears at a specific radius rd, indicating no projection of polarization P on the xy-plane therein, attributed to a nearly homeotropic state (inset of Fig. 1f, iii). Upon approaching the bubble’s center at radii r≤rd, the fluorescence increases again and finally decays in intensity. Combining PLM and FCPM images, along with optical simulation (Supplementary Fig. 3), enables the precise reconstruction of the 3D polar structure. The reproduced polar field demonstrates a hybridized topological state comprising an atypical inner skyrmion-toron hybrid and an outer chiral meron (Fig. 1f, iv). The corresponding preimage and projection of polar flux in the S2 manifold enable the quantification of topological invariants and the visualization of isohypsic surfaces of polar flux (Bottom of Fig. 1f, iv). The mapping of polar flux on the S2 manifold clearly shows that the inner and outer topologies exhibit different skyrmion numbers Q=−1 and +1/2 (or Q=+1 and −1/2), respectively. They correspond to a mixed state of a skyrmion-toron hybrid, enabled by the top-down surface anchoring asymmetry, and a twisted meron. The hybridization from the inner skyrmion-toron to the outer meron is achieved smoothly through a continuous rotation of the polar flux. Notably, the skyrmion-toron core is energetically stable. Transformation between the hybrid topology and the twisted meron must overcome energy barriers caused by polarization gradients and electrostatic interactions (see Supplementary Fig. 4). The well-known skyrmion (or toron) is enabled by weak (or strong) vertical anchoring of polarization P on interfaces, while the observed skyrmion-toron core is accompanied by a weak degenerate planar anchoring. On the bottom toron side, a singularity emerges due to the inconsistency between the strongly-anchored planar surface polarization P and twisted polar field in the bulk. The top skyrmion is non-singular thanks to weak planar anchoring. The tilt of polar flux on the top surface generates bound charge, which can be partially screened by ions naturally contained in the air46. These topological characteristics are well captured by preimages, where each isohypsic surface of polar flux corresponds to a point in the S2 manifold. In the outer meron, the preimage covering half of the S2 manifold shows a vortex-like arc connecting the upper and lower surfaces, visualizing the twist of the polar field along the z axis. In the inner skyrmion-toron, while arcs with distinct polar orientations intersect at the bottom toron, they remain uncontacted on the top skyrmion.

Intertwining between twisted merons towards multiple monopoles

Next, we show that the topological solitons serve as elementary quasi-particles for assembling into nontrivial topological configurations. As the simplest case, yet with unexpected complexity, we investigate the two-body interaction of twisted merons (RM) with the same twisting sense (i.e., chirality), which are generated upon relatively fast cooling (>c.a. 0.1 K/min). The screw sense of each meron, visualized by interferometric second-harmonic generation (SHG-I) microscopy47, acts as a rotational polar flux upon building meron complexes (Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Movies 2 and 3). When two merons share the identical screw sense (i.e., two merons with the same sign of Q as the twisting and screw sense are coupled as aforementioned), they exhibit repulsion. Two merons never coalesce, where a domain wall (DW) hinders the merging process (Fig. 2). On the other hand, two merons with the opposite screw sense experience attraction, ending up with a single rhombus-shaped defect (Fig. 3). Calculations of the energy landscape for meron pairs show that the elastic contribution is nearly independent of separation. In contrast, the electrostatic energy changes by ~10−16 J as the merons approach contact, decreasing for opposite-screw-sense pairs and increasing for same-screw-sense pairs (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). These results indicate that electrostatic coupling provides a robust long-range route to control polar soliton dynamics, distinguishing NF systems from conventional elastic-dominated nematics.

Figures 2b and 3b demonstrate PLM images of the two cases. In the case of two merons with the same screw sense, a DW structure with a π-flipping of polar flux is formed upon the contact of the merons (Fig. 2c). The polar field is distorted in the vicinity of the wall, evident from the distinct interference color of the area from the other area upon decrossing one of the polarizers under PLM (Fig. 2b, viii and ix). With the same characterisations of single meron, we reconstruct the 3D polar field (Fig. 2c, d). Mean-field simulations consistently reproduce all the structural and optical observations (Supplementary Methods 1 and 2; Supplementary Fig. 5). The DW separating the π-flipping of polar flux consists of surface defect lines positioned near the top and bottom surfaces, demonstrating out-of-plane twisting along the defect normal. The twisted DW configuration is favored because it reduces the electrostatic energy48,49 (Supplementary Discussion 1). The possibility of the DW being a sheet-like wall connecting the top and bottom surfaces is excluded since the interference color is uniform across the DW (Fig. 2b, vi–x; Supplementary Fig. 5). Topologically, the polar field around the DW is equivalent to a meron with the opposite sign of Q to that of the original two merons. The overall skyrmion charge in the confinement preserves Qtotal=+1/2=+1/2×2−1/2 (Fig. 2d), or also possible as Qtotal=−1/2=−1/2×2+1/2. It is well known that monopoles, such as merons, are not stable on their own. They can only be stabilized through forming a meron-antimeron pair within a uniform polar, typically magnetized, background50. Interestingly, in the current study, the confinement boundary condition acts as a new stabiliser, allowing merons and antimerons to exist as independent entities. This scenario enables these monopoles to be manipulated and processed individually.

The case of two merons with the opposite screw sense represents more complex features (Fig. 3). Two merons coalesce into a single entity (Fig. 3b, i–v; Supplementary Movie 3), during which a rhombus-shaped disclination ring forms. This disclination represents a line defect in the polar field, characterised by a ±1/2 winding of the polar flux around the defect core (Fig. 3c). Throughout the coalescence, the disclination ring undergoes continuous deformation, enclosing an area with local twisting, accompanied by optical rotation (Fig. 3b, viii and ix). Being metastable, the ring gradually vanishes by either self-annihilating or being expelled to the bubble boundary. Ultimately, the system relaxes into a single meron (Fig. 3b, v). Mean-field simulation clarifies topological dynamics of the transient defect states (Fig. 3c–e; Supplementary Fig. 6). The rhombus-shaped ring initially comprises a pair of Z-shaped disclinations (Fig. 3d). Their projection to the xy-plane overlaps to give a rhombus shape even as time goes by. This staggered arrangement of disclinations is experimentally confirmed by PLM analysis together with optical simulation (Supplementary Fig. 6). At the top surface (Fig. 3c, i), significant splay of polarization P in the 2nd and 4th quadrants as a source of space charge (σs=−∇⋅P), is eliminated by decreasing the order parameter and forming the disclination arcs. The smooth bend of polarization P in the 1st and 3rd quadrants maintains a disclination-free structure. Because of a nearly 90° twist of polarization P across the thickness, the complementary situation occurs at the bottom surface (Fig. 3c, iii). Topologically, this intertwining process depicts the collision of a Q=+1/2 meron and a Q=−1/2 meron with the same handedness. Upon the collision, the two merons connect their polar flux continuously in the middle plane, and the pairing inevitably generates two half-integer strength disclinations at the edge of their contact line. The two disclinations penetrate along the depth, shaping two vertical disclinations ending with the surface disclinations (Fig. 3d). As the intertwined structure further relaxes, either the Q=−1/2 or Q=+1/2 meron becomes progressively compressed and eventually vanishes, while the meron with the opposite sign expands continuously (Fig. 3e). This relaxation process is accompanied by the gradual shrinking and annihilation of surface disclination arcs that connects the vertical disclination lines. Once one of the original merons has been expelled to the boundary, both the surface and the vertical disclinations are eliminated, resulting in a disclination-free single-meron configuration. Consequently, the Skyrmion number is preserved at Q=±1/2.

Domain integration of monopoles with controlled charge arrays

To achieve our goal of engineering complex domain structures, the next stage is to explore how to make diverse, multibody-connected topology controllable. As seen, the probability of finding the complex two-body topologies in free space is equal, corresponding to their random nucleation and formation. Whether any field couples to the topological evolution is essential for obtaining desired domain structures. Here, we employ a flow field as the coupler to direct the nucleation and topological pairing of merons in a programmable manner. The ferroelectric fluid is confined in a series of connected, round-shaped confinement, which provides location-specified generation of monopoles (Fig. 4a). The flow field is precisely generated through a temperature gradient by using a laser tweezer technique (Methods). The method provides local control of the flow field with optical precision. Under the applied temperature gradient, the resulting Poiseuille flow imposes a shear across the middle microchannel, aligning polarization P along the flow-gradient direction51. In the absence of flow regulation, two types of two-body configurations appear with equal probability, corresponding to the variants of the polarization states described in Fig. 2—either straight or rhombus-shaped disclination lines forming along the microchannel (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). In the laser-tweezer controlled case, we begin by generating a seed meron with a specific screw sense. This seed then pulls a disclination wall along the channel, which induces the next meron to nucleate (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Movie 4). Due to the unidirectional flow field, the induced meron is distorted. Across the disclination wall, the polar flux orients in the opposite direction. The flow-induced orientational structure is metastable and sensitive to the electric field. Under a weak in-plane field (ca. 3 mV·μm⁻¹) perpendicular to the channel, this disclination readily drifts along the field direction, while the merons confined in the circular regions remain nearly unchanged (Fig. 4c, i). With increasing field strength (5 mV·μm⁻¹), the local polarization within the connecting channel aligns with one of the polarizers, resulting in the complete optical extinction (Fig. 4c, ii). This change in the polar field leads to the meron cores being displaced. Upon field removal, both meron cores relax back to the centers of their confinements. An additional horizontal disclination emerges across the channel, giving rise to a cross-shaped disclination (Fig. 4c, iii and iv). This intriguing field-responsive behavior originates from the unique polar topology and the associated charge distribution within the induced configuration. Unexpectedly, analyses using PLM, FCPM, and SHG microscopy reveal that the induced meron exhibits the same screw and twisting senses as the seed meron (Fig. 4d, e; Supplementary Fig. 9; Discussion). The induced two-body topological configuration can be regarded as a sequential assembly of monopoles with charges of Q=+1/2, −1/2, and +1/2 along the microchannel, conserving Qtotal=+1/2 (Fig. 4d), or also possible as Qtotal=−1/2=−1/2+1/2−1/2. This induced configuration can also be viewed as a locally charged soliton, where negative (positive) charge mainly accumulates near the vicinity of the surfaces and flips its polarity near the opposite surface (Fig. 4f). This charge separation arises because the polarizations at the surfaces are in-plane oriented to minimize bound charge (σb=P⋅v), while those in the bulk tend to escape out of plane to avoid singularities. As a result, the splay deformation required for the continuous reorientation near the surfaces gives rise to the observed space charge (σs=−∇⋅P). In the middle plane, the amounts of negative and positive charge are nearly equal and remain weak in magnitude. Applying a weak external electric field to the configuration, as in Fig. 4c, polarizations in the connecting channel are slightly reoriented toward the field direction, which leads to accumulation of bound charges on both sides of the channel (Fig. 4g). Owing to the narrow channel geometry, even a small amount of charge can generate a strong depolarization field that effectively screens the external electric field. As a result, the polarizations inside the channel remain primarily aligned along the channel direction, while the wall disclination is displaced toward one side. Upon removing the external field, the polar field relaxes into a pair of merons with the same screw sense. A cross-shaped disclination line replaces the original domain wall because of the locally discontinuous polar field (inset of Fig. 4g). The seeding mechanism suggests a novel pathway for producing large-scale domain structures composed of monopoles (Fig. 3h; Supplementary Movie 5). All the merons in the topological array are topologically identical to the seed, ensuring global consistency of topological information. Such programmable, complex domain structures offer one route toward large-scale control of polar solitonic networks in polar materials.

Discussion

The discovered topologies are self-charged, being electrically processible fundamental quasi-particles. Their ‘self-charged’ nature arises from the topology, which requires a nonzero polarization divergence (∇⋅P≠0), leading to intrinsic bound charges (Fig. 4). These solitons can be regarded as self-charged entities whose formation and integration are governed by the interplay between electrostatics and confinement. Below, we first examine the kinetic pathways that drive the emergence of these polar quasi-particles. The twisted meron and hybrid topological states are generated under different cooling rates. Under relatively fast cooling, an NF bubble emerges as a cylindrical nucleus primarily from the bulk, attaching to both ends at the surfaces (Fig. 5a). The meron topology accommodates well with both the confinement geometry and surface anchoring under this condition. Meanwhile, slow cooling near thermal equilibrium leads to the I-NF transition occurring first at the LC-air interface, where the temperature is lower than that at the bottom glass surface. In this case, NF bubbles appear as hemispherical confinement (Fig. 5a). A hemispherical environment facilitates the tilting of polarization P due to the curved surface. This geometrical effect allows for the stabilization of a skyrmion or toron core that cannot fit within the purely cylindrical confinement52. Figure 5b, d display the generation processes under cylindrical and hemispherical confinements (Supplementary Methods 1 and 2).

During the early stage of the I-NF transition, the system remains weakly polar, with the topology dominated by chirality (Fig. 5c, e). As a result, the polar field tends to twist along multiple directions, the so-called double twist, as in the blue phases53. In the fast-cooling case (Fig. 5b), the I-NF transition occurs rapidly, and the nucleated NF bubble immediately connects the top and bottom surfaces, thereby developing a cylindrical confinement. All the boundaries are flat and impose degenerate planar anchoring to prevent bound charges, except at the air-NF interface. This condition penalizes in-plane twist and permits only twist along the depth direction, leading to the formation of a meron5 (Fig. 5b). Since air can provide atmospheric ions to partially screen bound charges, the air-NF interface facilitates out-of-plane tilt, particularly in the core of the meron. Such screening is expected to reduce the effective bound charge to approximately 10⁻³ of its nominal value (see the estimation in Methods), thereby allowing electrostatic and other free energies to compete on comparable scales. Nevertheless, as the bulk polarizations tend to escape in the depth direction, some space charges can still be distributed near the vicinity of surfaces, where polarizations inevitably undergo splay deformation (Fig. 5b). As the twisted meron grows, the high cost of the electrostatic energy (Felec) is partially suppressed by sacrificing polarization gradient free energy (Fgrad). Along with this scenario, the cylindrical confinement also tends to homogenize the polar field, thereby restricting in-plane twisting. In turn, this leads to a modest increase in the twist free energy Ftwist (Fig. 5c).

Meanwhile, the formation of a curved I-NF interface during slow cooling, which forms during the development of hemispherical confinement, is essential for transforming a meron into a skyrmion or toron. Like the previous case, the curved I-NF interface also favors degenerate anchoring. In addition, the curved boundary geometrically provides a tilt of polarization P within the plane of the interface. As a result, the curvature facilitates twisting in both the in-plane and depth directions, serving as a key stabilizer for complex 3D helical structures, including skyrmions, torons, and even hopfion (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Figs. 10). As the hemispherical NF bubble expands, the polarization P twists continuously in both directions until it occupies the entire volume. This process is accompanied by a steady decrease in twist free energy, along with an increase in polarization gradient and electrostatic energies (Fig. 5e). Consequently, the resulting skyrmion or toron core remains stable, with its radius decreasing as determined by the system’s chirality (Fig. 5d). It is important to note that both monopole and hybrid states are (meta)stable states. The selection of the topological states, controlled by the cooling rate and the resulting confinement geometry, provides a mechanism of adjusting boundary or anchoring asymmetry to control topological characteristics. For instance, instead of using the air interface, we could employ a solid “capping” material, such as a coverslip or a glycerol layer, to achieve both meron and hybrid topologies. This finding highlights their resilience to environmental perturbations and their potential for manipulation under various electric field conditions.

Having clarified the intrinsic pathways of forming merons and their hybrid topological states, we next address the novel topological seeding mechanism, where a specific type of monopole can induce monopoles with identical topological nature under a flow field (Fig. 4). Figure 5f shows the numerical calculation of the topological development from a seed meron. As the NF develops from the I background and enters into the microchannel region, the flow field driven by the temperature gradient and the channel boundary force the polarization P to align along the microchannel (Fig. 5f). Meanwhile, to guarantee the continuity of the polar flux, the screw sense of the topology being generated in the microchannel should remain identical to that of the seed meron. Consequently, a domain wall forms at the channel center, separating antiparallel polarizations in the upper and lower halves. As the NF phase further extends into the neighboring reservoir, the pre-established polarization within the channel serves as a topological template, ensuring that the induced meron deterministically inherits the screw sense of its seed. This process enables reproducible generation of identical solitonic units, bridging isolated topological excitations with collective organization. Unlike conventional ferroelectric solids, where polar solitons are confined to nanoscale domains and their ordered assembly remains challenging, our work demonstrates controllable formation and integration of micrometer-scale polar solitons in a fluid medium. The synergy of fluidic reconfigurability and topological protection enables large-area, programmable soliton arrays to be realized via simple optothermal control, offering a versatile platform for exploring collective soliton dynamics and designing reconfigurable ferroelectric architectures and adaptive polar devices.

Methods

Sample preparation

The NF material RM-OC2 was synthesized in-house following the procedure described in Ref. 5, via a multistep organic synthesis starting from 2,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde. To bias the chirality of polar fields, RM-OC2 was doped with commercial chiral agents, (R)-2-Octyl4-[4-(Hexyloxy) benzoyloxy]benzoate (R811) or (S)-2-Octyl 4-[4-(Hexyloxy)benzoyloxy]benzoate (S811) at concentrations of 0.3–0.5 wt%. RM-OC2 and the chiral dopant were co-dissolved in chloroform, mixed thoroughly, and dried to remove the solvent, yielding a homogeneous solid mixture. The phase sequence was: I –65 °C – HN* – 37 °C – Crystal.

LC slab preparation

Confinement geometries were created on glass substrates using a DMD-based photolithography system equipped with a 380-nm ultraviolet light source. A photoresist was prepared by dissolving 1 wt% Irgacure 369 in pentaerythritol triacrylate (PETA) under ultrasonication and mild heating. To enhance adhesion, the glass substrates were plasma-cleaned for 10 minutes and treated with a silane coupling agent (KH570) for 30 min. Afterward, the substrates were rinsed with isopropanol and dried. The photoresist was then spin-coated at 2000 rpm, followed by UV projection of the designed patterns through the DMD system to induce PETA polymerization. After exposure, the substrates were rinsed with isopropanol, resulting in confinement geometries with a depth of approximately 5 μm. These substrates with predefined confinement geometries were placed on a temperature-controlled hot stage (HCS302 and mK2000B, Instec Inc.) and heated to 160 °C. A small amount of the RM-OC2 mixture, doped with the chiral dopant, was deposited onto the substrates. Once the chiral RM-OC2 mixture melted into an isotropic state, the fluid was carefully dragged to fill the patterned regions using a spatula. The sample was then slowly cooled to a temperature below 67 °C, during which NF bubbles nucleated and grew within the confined areas. In this configuration, the top surface of each NF bubble was exposed to air, while the bottom surface remained in contact with the glass substrate. The LC samples were used directly for PLM, FCPM, SHG, and SHG-I measurements.

Twist angle fitting by Jones Matrix

The twist angle of the director field was measured using a PLM (BH2, Olympus) equipped with crossed polarizers and a Nikon digital camera. A 550 nm monochromatic light source was employed. The sample was positioned between a fixed polarizer and a rotatable analyzer, which was rotated incrementally in 6° steps while recording the transmitted intensity under stable temperature and illumination conditions. Fluorescence confocal polarizing microscopy

FCPM measurements were conducted on an inverted microscope equipped with a laser-scanning confocal module and temperature-controlled stage. A 60× oil-immersion objective (NA = 1.4) and a 20× dry objective (NA = 0.95) were used for the imaging. The samples were doped with 0.05 wt% of a fluorescent dye, N,N′-Bis (2,5-di- tert -butylphenyl)-3,4,9,10-perylenedicarboximide (BTBP), whose transition dipole moment aligns preferentially along the local LC director. Excitation was performed using a 514 nm linearly polarized laser. The polarization is then rotated by a λ/2 waveplate and passed through a λ/4 waveplate. Five fluorescence images were collected for each texture: four under linearly polarized excitations (αk=kπ4,k=0, 1, 2, 3) and one under circularly polarized excitation. SHG and SHG interference (SHG-I) measurement

SHG and SHG-I measurements were performed using a Q-switched pulsed laser (1064 nm, 5 ns pulse width, 100 Hz repetition rate, MPL-III-1064-20µJ) as the excitation source. The linearly polarized fundamental beam was directed normally into the samples, and the transmitted SHG signals were recorded either by a photomultiplier tube (DH-PMT-D100V, Daheng Optics) or a scientific CMOS camera (Zyla-4.2P, Andor). For SHG-I, phase differences between the sample-generated SHG signal and a reference Y-cut quartz plate were introduced using two counter-rotating fused silica plates, minimizing optical-path variations during rotation. Optimal interference contrast was achieved by selecting appropriate plate rotation angles. Importantly, SHG-I provides a sign of the polarization. Combining SHG information with the orientation field reconstructed by FCPM allows us to reproduce the polar field in three dimensions.

Laser scanning for controlling flow and topology

Controlled growth of NF bubbles within patterned confinement was achieved using an optical tweezers system integrated with a homemade temperature-controlled stage. The experimental setup for the optical tweezers consisted of an inverted microscope (Eclipse Ti, Nikon) and a scanning optical tweezing system (Tweez 305, Aresis). The stage maintained the sample at the isothermal nanofluid (Iso-NF) phase transition temperature. A 1064 nm infrared laser was employed, with the focal-plane power adjusted between 5 and 50 mW. The laser was focused onto an indium tin oxide (ITO) glass surface with patterned microchannels, which absorbed light at 1064 nm and converted it into heat. After selecting the total laser power and action range, the beam was digitally split into multiple independently addressable foci. Each focus could be positioned arbitrarily within the microchannel and assigned a specific power fraction, while conserving the total distributed power. By optimizing the number, location, and power of these foci, we locally melted NF regions and controlled the position-dependent nucleation through the laser-induced temperature gradient in space (Supplementary Movie 5). Once the induced topology under the temperature gradient was achieved, the laser was completely switched off. The system was equilibrated under the NF phase for subsequent characterizations.

Numerical modelling of the polar field

Our theoretical framework employs a continuum Landau-type free energy functional to describe the energy minimization of mesoscale polar textures. In conventional nematics, the orientation order has head-to-tail symmetry, which can be derived from the gauge theory at the molecular scale (<10 nm). Such head-to-tail symmetry reflects the apolar nature of the director n at the mesoscale (~100 nm)54–57. At the mesoscale or coarse-grained level, these descriptions reduce to an effective mean-field formulation, where the free energy is expanded in terms of symmetry-allowed invariants (e.g., n, P). Since the structures we observed are significantly larger than molecular scales, and our focus is on (meta)stable configurations rather than critical fluctuations, a continuum description is advantageous. In the NF phase, the head-to-tail symmetry is broken, and the corresponding continuum theory should incorporate polar terms not present in conventional nematics. The free-energy density is:

felastic=12K11∇⋅n2+12K22n⋅∇×n+q2+12K33n×∇×n2 is the nematic elastic energy density in the framework of Oseen-Frank theory. K11, K22 and K33 are the elastic constants for splay, twist and bend deformations, respectively. n is LC director. q is the helical wavenumber determined by the chiral strength of the chiral dopant. In the simulation, the three elastic constants of the LC are set to 6 pN, 3 pN, and 8pN, respectively. The helical wavenumber q was set within the range [2π/10, 2π/5] μm−1.

fLandau=12μP2+14vP4+12h∇P2 is the Landau free energy density. P is the polarization vector. μ and v are the temperature-dependent expansion coefficients. The first two terms determine the equilibrium polarization strength of the system, i.e., P=−μ/v. In the simulation, v is set to 2×104 J·m⁵·C-⁴, and u is set within the range [40, 80] J·m·C-² to control P in the range of [0.04, 0.06] C m-². The last term is the polarization gradient energy with coefficients h, set as 1×10−12 J·m3·C-2 for simulations. This term penalizes spatial variations in polarization, favoring uniform ferroelectric order.

felectric=−12P⋅E is the electrostatic energy, where E comprises two components: the external electric field Eex and the depolarisation electric field Edep. Here we consider only the polar response of NF LCs to the electric field, which dominate over the dielectric response. Edep can be obtained by solving the Poisson equation ∇2Φ=−βσε and Edep=−∇Φ. σ is the space charge density, originating from both the splay deformation of the bulk polarization (−∇⋅P) and the polarization tilt normal to the boundary (P⋅v), with v being the unit normal to the boundary. ε is the dielectric constant. Φ is the electric potential, determined by numerically solving the Poisson equation using a finite-difference scheme combined with an iterative relaxation method. β is a phenomenological coefficient that accounts for the effective magnitude of the electrostatic effect. Following the order-of-magnitude estimate used in the Ref. 31, we note that in the unscreened NF state, the spontaneous polarization P~10−2C m−2 would generate depolarizing fields of order E~P/ε≈106−107 V m-1 with the dielectric constant ε~10−9−10−8 F m-1, corresponding to electrostatic energy densities (~12εE2) of 105−106J m−3. This value is several orders of magnitude larger than typical nematic elastic energies. The observation that electrostatic and elastic contributions compete in our simulations therefore implies strong ionic screening (Eeff=βE), corresponding to an effective screening factor β~10−3. This value is also comparable in magnitude to previous estimates inferred from independent experiments, such as electro-optic measurements34 and measurements of the effective splay elastic constant K1124, supporting the physical reasonableness of the present treatment.

fflexo=−γn∇⋅n⋅P is the flexoelectric energy, which describes the linear coupling between P and the splay deformation of n. This effect becomes particularly significant near the I-NF phase transition. γ is the flexoelectric coefficient, which is set to 1×10−5 V.

fsurf=12Wsn⋅v2 is the anchoring energy, which describes the effect of non-polar degenerate tangential anchoring of the LC at the confinement boundaries. v is the normal vector of the boundary. Ws is the apolar anchoring coefficient for NF LCs and is set to 1×10−5 J·m-2 in simulations.

The numerical simulations were carried out in MATLAB using custom finite-difference codes. Polar fields were obtained through an iterative relaxation process of the Euler–Lagrange equations for both the director and order-parameter fields. The electrostatic potential was determined by solving the Poisson equation in Fourier space, taking the prescribed confinement geometry into account. For the simulations involving optothermal induction, additional hydrodynamic coupling was included. More details about the numerical implementation, such as grid discretization, mask construction, initialization procedures, and iterative schemes, can be found in the Supplementary Information.
