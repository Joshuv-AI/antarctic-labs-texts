The relationship of forage intake, residual feed intake, and greenhouse gas emissions between lactation and gestation in beef cows
Authors: Samuel R Talley, Emma A Briggs, Bailey J Tomson, Mariana E Garcia-Ascolani, Paul A Beck, R Ryan Reuter, Andrew P Foote, Megan M Rolf, David L Lalman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this research was to evaluate dry matter intake (DMI), residual feed intake (RFI), and greenhouse gas emissions between lactation and gestation in mature beef cows fed an unprocessed grass hay diet. A two-year experiment was conducted using 67 mature Angus and Angus-cross cows (n = 41, Year 1; n = 26, Year 2) which included a minimum 85-d early-mid lactation trial (LACT) followed by a minimum 85-d early-mid gestation trial (GEST) in both years. Cows were housed in drylot pens at the Oklahoma State University Range Cow Research Center. Each pen was equipped with an individual intake monitoring system (SmartFeed, C-Lock Inc., Rapid City, SD) with a stocking density of 2.7 cows per feeder in Year 1, and 2.9 cows per feeder in Year 2. Cows were offered ad libitum access to long-stem, unprocessed bermudagrass hay (≥12.6% CP and ≥ 57% TDN, LACT; ≥12.4% CP and ≥ 54% TDN, GEST) and a mineral supplement. An open-circuit, portable gas quantification system (GreenFeed, C-Lock Inc., Rapid City, SD) was placed in pens to measure carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and oxygen (O2) flux. Cows were classified by K-means clustering into three categories of feed efficiency based on RFI of efficient (n = 23, LACT; n = 23, GEST), moderate (n = 27, LACT; n = 25, GEST), and inefficient (n = 17, LACT; n = 16, GEST) classifications. Efficient, moderate, and inefficient LACT DMI means were 13.9, 15.1, and 16.8 kg/d, and GEST DMI means for the 3 groups were 11.5, 12.6, and 15.2 kg/d respectively (P < 0.001). Efficient classified cows consumed 18.8% (LACT) and 27.8% (GEST) less hay than inefficient classified cows (P < 0.001). There were no differences for body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), milk yield, body condition score (BCS), or calf weaning weight (WW) between classification groups in either physiological stage (P ≥ 0.52). There were strong (P < 0.001) positive phenotypic correlations for DMI (r = 0.82) and RFI (r = 0.68) between LACT and GEST. There were positive phenotypic correlations (P < 0.001) between DMI and CO2, CH4, or heat production (HP) during LACT (r = 0.70 CO2; r = 0.61 CH4; r = 0.65 HP) and GEST (r = 0.58 CO2; r = 0.52 CH4; r = 0.58 HP). The results from this study suggest that identifying feed efficient mature cows based on RFI results in lower DMI with no difference in output metrics, and that RFI remains constant across physiological stages.Keywords: Beef cows, forage utilization efficiency, forage intake, gas flux, feeding behavior

Introduction

In U.S. cow-calf operations, the overriding factors driving profitability remain forage and supplemental feed costs, which represent 51% of the total operating costs for an average producer (Pendell and Herbel 2021). Additionally, 74% of the feed required to produce one pound of finished beef carcass and 77–81% of all enteric methane emissions per unit of carcass weight derives from the cow-calf sector (Rotz et al. 2019). With those statistics in mind, there is considerable potential to improve the economic and environmental sustainability of U.S. beef operations by identifying forage efficient animals in the cow-calf sector.

In recent years, there has been considerable research on feed efficiency in growing cattle fed energy-dense mixed diets, with the majority of genetic tools for feed intake and feed efficiency generated from this data (Cantalapiedra-Hijar 2018). The Beef Improvement Federation recommends a diet with a minimum of 2.4 Mcal of metabolizable energy (ME) per kg of dry matter (DM) for feed intake and feed efficiency testing of growing bulls and 2.9 Mcal ME/kg DM for finishing steers to maximize genetic potential for intake and growth (Beef Improvement Federation 2018). However, grazed forage, the basal diet for most cow-calf operations, ranges from approximately 1.6 to 2.6 Mcal ME/kg DM throughout the year (NASEM 2016). Compared to measuring mixed diets on an individual animal basis, measuring unprocessed forage intake is problematic because specialized equipment and increased labor is required (Holder et al. 2022). For these reasons, few studies are available investigating relationships of intake, performance, and feed efficiency in a strict forage environment.

Research on feed efficiency in mature beef cows remains scarce. Cows are in a pregnant, nonlactating state for approximately 160 days and a lactating state for about 205 days through each production cycle. Cumulative lactation energy requirements are approximately twice that of cumulative gestation energy requirements considering the longer time in lactation, increased maintenance energy required in lactating cows, and the energy required to produce milk (NASEM 2016). Nevertheless, little research has been conducted to determine the relationship of feed intake and efficiency across these physiological stages of production. This gap is problematic because meaningful progress in cow–calf efficiency depends on whether ranking for feed intake in gestation is reflective of ranking for feed intake in lactation.

There are reports that growing cattle with low residual feed intake (RFI) have lower maintenance energy requirements (Lawrence et al. 2012). There is also evidence that low RFI cattle have lower methane (CH4) emissions (Nkrumah et al. 2009; Jones et al. 2011), however results from other studies have been inconsistent (Briggs et al. 2022b; Freetly and Brown-Brandl 2013; Oliveira et al. 2016). Cows with lower maintenance energy requirements would be able to retain more energy for maternal tissue storage and milk yield at a lower feed intake. Additionally, if a positive relationship exists between maintenance energy requirements and greenhouse gas emissions, selection for cattle with lower maintenance energy requirements may also reduce the industry’s carbon footprint.

The objective of this study was to determine the relationships between DMI, RFI, maintenance energy requirements, and greenhouse gas emissions among gestating and lactating beef cows fed an unprocessed grass hay diet. The study also evaluated differences in animal performance, gas emissions, metabolizable energy for maintenance, dry matter digestibility, and feeding behavior between efficiency classifications during both stages of production.

Materials and methods

The procedures and protocols conducted in this study were approved by the Oklahoma State University Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-21-12). Sixty-seven fall calving 4- and 5-year-old Angus and Angus cross cows (n = 41, Year 1; n = 26, Year 2) were used in consecutive early-mid lactation (LACT) and early-mid gestation (GEST) intake trials over the course of two years. Year 1 LACT and GEST trials lasted 100 d and 89 d respectively, with a 21-d adaptation and 79-d data collection period during LACT, and a 14-d adaptation and 75-d data collection period during GEST. Some of the cows in yr 1 had not been exposed to the SmartFeed and/or GreenFeed machines before, thus the longer adaptation period in LACT. In yr 2, all cows had previously been exposed to the feed intake and gas flux systems and therefore, adaptation was conducted for 14 d followed by 75 d of data collection.

Animal housing and performance measurements

Cattle were housed at the Range Cow Research Center, North Range Unit, near Stillwater, OK. At the initiation of the LACT trial, cows and their calves were placed into drylot pens containing 15 SmartFeed individual animal intake units (C-Lock Inc., Rapid City, SD) with a stocking density of 2.7 and 2.9 cows per feeder in years 1 and 2 respectively. Cows had ad libitum access to unprocessed long-stem bermudagrass hay (Table 1) and a salt, mineral, and vitamin supplement (Ca, 9.5%; P, 9.5%; NaCl, 15.0%; Mg, 6.0%; K, 1.3%; Cu, 1,200 mg/kg; I, 60 mg/kg; Mn, 4,800 mg/kg; Se, 27 mg/kg; Zn, 3,600 mg/kg; Co, 12 mg/kg; Vit A, 68,039 IU/kg; Vit D-3, 6,804 IU/kg; Vit E, 68 IU/kg). Water was provided via automatic livestock watering tanks (MiraFount A3465, Miraco Automatic Livestock Waterers, Grinnell, Iowa) and pens contained shade cloths and windbreaks on the north and south perimeters. To validate feed intake measurements, round bales of hay were weighed on an electric scale prior to feeding. At 10-d intervals, or after a precipitation event, all orts and waste around feeders were collected, weighed, and sampled for DM. During LACT, calves were fed a calf diet (Table 1) in a designated creep feeding pen separate from cows at 1% of their BW once per day on a pen basis. The average calf age at initiation of the LACT experimental phase was 74 d in yr 1 and 54 d in yr 2. Feeding rate was adjusted after calf weight data collection at 14-d intervals.

During LACT, cows were bred via artificial insemination utilizing the 7 & 7 estrus synchronization program (Bonacker et al. 2020) on d 28 in yr 1 and d 8 in yr 2. If artificial insemination at estrus synchronization was not successful, cows were serviced via artificial insemination based on heat detection throughout the remainder of LACT. At the conclusion of LACT, calves were weaned, and cows were moved to pasture with a clean-up bull for a minimum of 14 d where they were managed as a contemporary group. Pregnancy was confirmed by a licensed veterinarian, and all cows that achieved a successful pregnancy were then brought back into the pens for the GEST trial.

Feeding events were calculated as the number of times a cow entered the SmartFeed system and consumed a minimum of 0.15 kg of hay. Nonfeeding events were calculated as the number of times a cow entered the SmartFeed and consumed less than 0.15 kg of hay. Daily feeding duration was calculated as the total time spent consuming feed associated with qualified feeding events, and eating rate was calculated as the total DMI per day divided by the feeding duration.

Initial and final body weights (BW) were collected at 0700 on consecutive days at the beginning and end of the data collection period for each trial, and at 14-d intervals. Weights were collected in a hydraulic squeeze chute equipped with electronic load cells (Tru-Test HD5T; Datamars, Mineral Wells, TX). Body condition scores (BCS; scale = 1–9; Wagner et al. 1988) and ultrasound body composition measurements of ribeye area (REA), rump fat (RUMP), and back fat (BF) were collected by trained technicians at the initiation and termination of each data collection period. Each individual cows’s body weight was regressed on days on trial (DOT), where the intercept of the regression was considered initial BW and the slope was considered average daily gain (ADG; Ferrell and Jenkins 1984). During LACT, milk yield was measured and milk samples were collected monthly based on procedures described by Wieseman et al. (2019). On the day prior to milking, calves were separated from their dams at 1630 h, during which time calves had no access to feed or water. At 2230 h, calves were returned to their dams and allowed to suckle until 2300 h, or until all calves were deemed to be satiated. At the end of the suckling period, calves were again removed from their dams and separated in a pen containing access to creep feed, hay, and water. At 0700 h the next morning cows were injected with 1 mL of oxytocin intramuscularly, milked with a portable milk machine (Portable Vacuum Systems, Springville, UT), and the weight of the milk collected was recorded. A 50-g fecal grab sample was collected from each individual cow at the initiation and termination of each trial data collection period, and every 14 d throughout the trial period, to determine dry matter digestibility (DMD). Fecal samples were dried at 55° C for 72 h and ground to pass through a 2-mm screen (Fritsch, Pittsboro, NC). Processed samples were composited over time points for each cow for further laboratory analysis. Hay grab samples were collected weekly, dried at 55° C for 72 h, ground to pass through a 2-mm screen (Fritsch, Pittsboro, NC) and composited across time points for further laboratory analysis.

Greenhouse gas emissions

A GEM system (GreenFeed, C-Lock Inc., Rapid City, SD), an open-circuit, portable, gas quantification system, was used to determine individual cow’s CO2 and CH4 emissions, and O2 consumption. In yr 1, the GEM was rotated through the pens every 3 weeks. In yr 2, only 1 pen was utilized, allowing cows access to the GEM at all times. Monthly CO2 recoveries were performed for GEM validation based on recommendations by C-Lock Inc. and automatic standard calibrations were performed weekly. The GEM was programmed to allow 4 visits per day for each cow in yr 1 and 2 visits per day for each cow in yr 2, with 7 drops of bait feed per visit in both years. Cows could visit the GEM no sooner than 4 h after the previous visit in yr 1 and 8 h after the previous visit in yr 2. A commercial pellet (Stillwater Milling Co., Stillwater, OK) was used as bait feed (18.5% CP and 73% TDN, Year 1; 18.8% CP and 72% TDN, Year 2) allowing 34.5 g/drop in yr 1 and 35.1 g/drop in yr 2. A cow’s gas flux data was only included in the final data set if they had a minimum of 30 GEM visits throughout the course of the trial, with each visit lasting a minimum of 3 min in length (Beck et al. 2024). Metabolizable energy for maintenance

Each cow’s digestible energy intake (DEI, Mcal/day) was determined using the individuals DMD and DMI. Once calculated, CH4 energy and estimated urinary energy was subtracted from DEI to determine metabolizable energy intake (MEI, Mcal/day).

Once RFI was determined, cows were classified into efficiency groups by K-means clustering (Ahlberg et al. 2019) of Efficient, Moderate, or Inefficient based on their RFI values using R 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025). To evaluate the difference in response variables across efficiency classifications, data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure of SAS 9.4 (SAS Inst., Cary, NC) with efficiency classification as a fixed effect and year as a random effect. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Tukey-Kramer method of least square means when the fixed effect of efficiency classification was significant (p < 0.05). Spearman rank and Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using R 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025) to determine relationships among response variables in lactation compared to gestation, and between DMI and gas flux data. Statistical significance was declared when p < 0.05, and a tendency was declared when p < 0.10.

Results and discussion

Physiological stage means and correlations

Means for variables within each of the two physiological stages are provided in Table 2. We acknowledge that data for the different stages of production are confounded with time, weather effects, and modest differences in hay nutritive value. Nevertheless, the timing of the two measurement periods, representing stage of production, reflect the timing employed in fall calving systems common throughout the Southern Great Plains region. There was no difference (p > 0.05) for GEM bait pellet intake between LACT (332 ± 125 g/d) and GEST (343 ± 40 g/d). Additionally, there was no difference (p > 0.05) for bait pellet intake between classification groups in either stage of production. Given the lack of difference for bait pellet DMI and the relatively small contribution to total daily intake, subsequent DMI mentioned in the results and tables will refer only to hay intake. Dry matter intake was 16.5% greater (p < 0.001; 2.3 kg/d) during lactation compared to gestation. Johnson et al. (2003) reported similar differences in DMI between gestation and lactation in Brangus cows consuming a grass hay diet. Cows from high milk expected progeny difference (EPD) sires consumed 2.65 kg more DMI during lactation, and cows from low milk EPD sires had 2.30 kg greater DMI during lactation (Johnson et al. 2003).

There were no differences in RFI, BW, or BCS between stages of production (p ≥ 0.16). Cow ADG was lower in LACT compared to GEST (p < 0.001). On average, the LACT hay diet supplied adequate energy to hold these cows at BW stasis while limiting daily milk yield to approximately 8 kg. Previous work from the same cow herd indicated that increased energy intake beyond BW stasis can be expected to increase milk yield and milk nutrient concentration (Williams et al. 2025).

Gas flux and estimated HP were greater (p ≤ 0.03) in LACT compared to GEST. These differences are likely driven by two primary factors: the increased energy required for maintenance during lactation (NASEM 2016) and increased feed intake. It has been well documented that DMI is a driving factor in CH4 (Galyean and Hales 2024) and CO2 (Arthur et al. 2018) production.

Estimated MEm requirements were 11% greater (p = 0.01) during lactation. Maintenance energy requirements have been reported to increase by 5% (Wiseman et al. 2019) to 38% (Neville 1974) during lactation with a default value of 20% in BCNRM (NASEM 2016). There was no difference (p = 0.27) in apparent DMD in either stage of production. During LACT, cows spent more time at the hay feeders (p < 0.001) with an increased eating rate (p < 0.001). Interestingly, during LACT, cows visited the hay feeders fewer times with fewer feeding and non-feeding events (p ≤ 0.02) compared to their behavior during GEST.

Correlations for variables between physiological states are presented in Table 3. There was a strong positive (p < 0.001) correlation for DMI and RFI between LACT and GEST (r = 0.82 and 0.68). Others have also documented moderate to high correlations for DMI and RFI between stages of production in beef females (Freetly et al. 2020; Archer et al. 2023). This data indicates that minimal re-ranking is expected for cow intake and feed efficiency between stages of production.

Body weight and BCS correlations between the two stages of production were strong and positive (p < 0.001) although this was expected because the two experimental periods were consecutive with only 16 to 49 d between the termination of LACT and beginning of GEST adaptation. There was no correlation (p > 0.15) for ADG between LACT and GEST although there was a weak, positive correlation (r = 0.25, p = 0.04, data not shown) for milk yield and conceptus-adjusted ADG in gestation. Estimates of MEm between the physiological stages were moderate and positively related (p < 0.001). There were strong, positive relationships (p < 0.001) between LACT and GEST for daily CH4, CO2, and HP (r = 0.80, 0.83, and 0.84 respectively), and this was likely due to strong correlations in physiological stage BW, BCS, and DMI. Similarly, there was a strong positive correlation (p < 0.001) for duration of time in the feeder between stages, as well as positive correlations (p < 0.01) for eating rate, feeding events, and nonfeeding events between physiological stages. Collectively, these results indicate that the relative ranking of cows for feeding behavior traits generally mirrors their ranking for feed intake.

There were positive correlations (p < 0.001) between DMI and CH4, CO2, and HP in both LACT and GEST (Table 4), though the correlations were slightly weaker in GEST compared to LACT. The lactation data is similar to results from Bird-Garner et al. (2017), Arthur et al. (2018), and Holder et al. (2022) who reported a correlation of 0.74 between roughage DMI and CH4, and 0.76–0.84 between roughage DMI and CO2. Holder et al. (2022) reported r = 0.67 for roughage DMI and HP in mature cows in gestation, which is similar to the results from this study. The biological mechanism for the weaker relationship between DMI and gas flux data in gestation is uncertain; however, the relationships reported may assist in formulating equations to predict DMI from gas emissions across production stages.

In both models, the average increase in DMI per kilogram of milk yield was 0.40 kg. This value is similar to those reported by Gross et al. (2024), Coleman et al. (2014), and Johnson et al. (2003) of 0.45, 0.55 and 0.35 respectively. The increase in intake due to milk production from these experiments is greater than the current default value suggested in the BCNRM (NASEM 2016) of 0.2 kg increase in DMI per kilogram of milk yield. This suggests that current models may underestimate the impact of milk production on forage intake. There was a difference in DMI across all 3 efficiency groups (p < 0.001), with efficient cows consuming 8.9% less hay than moderate cows, and 18.8% less hay than inefficient cows. Importantly, there was no difference (p ≥ 0.13) for mean BW, mean BCS, ADG, milk yield or energy composition, mean ultrasound body composition, or calf weaning weight (WW) between the three efficiency classifications.

In the subsequent gestation trial (Table 6), mid-test BW0.75, ADG, contemporary group, and previous lactation milk yield explained 48.0% of the variation in DMI in yr 1, and mid-test BW0.75, ADG, and milk yield explained 70.6% of the variation in DMI in yr 2. Interestingly, milk yield during the previous lactation remained a significant (p < 0.05) predictor of intake during gestation, suggesting a carryover influence of milk yield. In yr 1, the average increase in DMI per kilogram of previous lactation milk production was 0.40 kg, and in yr 2 it was 0.30 kg. Archer et al. (2023) reported a strong genetic correlation (r = 0.77) between DMI measured as a non-lactating cow and milk yield, supporting the theory that milk yield remains a driver of intake even during the dry period. The difference (p < 0.001) in DMI between efficient-classified cows and moderate or inefficient-classified cows was 9.5% and 27.8%, respectively. There was no difference (p ≥ 0.18) for mean BW, mean BCS, ADG, ADG of the gravid uterus, conceptus adjusted ADG, or mean body composition among the efficiency classifications.

The lack of difference between BW, ADG, and milk production between the three efficiency classification groups was expected, as RFI should be phenotypically independent of variables used in the model to predict DMI. Some studies have reported relationships between RFI and body fat composition (Lancaster et al. 2009, Hafla et al. 2013, Archer et al. 2023) with low RFI cattle having less body fat. The concern then being that selection for efficient females based on RFI may inadvertently increase the age at puberty and decrease the reproductive performance of the cowherd. However, in the current experiment, there was no difference for average BCS, back fat, or rump fat between the classification groups in either physiological stage. These results agree with those of Meyer et al. (2008) and Black et al. (2013) who reported no difference in BCS or ultrasonic measurements of body fat between mature cows of different RFI rankings. The lack of body fat difference between low and high RFI cows is encouraging; however, more research is required to evaluate lifetime productivity and reproductive performance of cattle with differing forage-based RFI.

Although direct comparisons among studies should be interpreted cautiously because RFI is calculated within a contemporary group and populations differed in animal type, intake level, nutrient requirements, and diet characteristics, the relative divergence in DMI between efficient and inefficient classifications across physiological stages has heuristic value. The 18.8% difference in DMI between efficient and inefficient cows in lactation was lower than that of Black et al. (2013) who reported a DMI difference of 27.7% between efficient and inefficient lactating cows fed bermudagrass silage. In that experiment, cows were fed bermudagrass silage plus dried distillers grains. Perhaps diet characteristics (moisture content, incorporation of concentrate feeds, roughage processing, and diet energy concentration) allowed for a greater difference in DMI among classification groups. However, the observed 27.8% difference in DMI between efficient and inefficient-classified cows during gestation in our experiment was greater than the values reported by Meyer et al. (2008) and Hafla et al. (2015) of 21% and 19% respectively for pregnant beef cows. It should be noted that in both experiments (Meyer et al. 2008; Hafla et al. 2015), efficiency classification was determined during heifer development. Interestingly, our results are similar to those reported by Meyer et al. (2008), who saw that efficient classified cows consumed 11% less forage than inefficient cows in lactation, but consumed 21% less in gestation. Work by Hughes and Pitchford (2004) investigating DMI and RFI in mice during lactation and gestation saw that the DMI of mice from low and high RFI lineage converged in early lactation but diverged in late lactation and through gestation with the low RFI lineage mice consuming less feed. A potential reasoning for this convergence of intakes between efficiency classifications in lactation compared to gestation may be due to increased energy demands brought on by lactation (Meyer et al. 2008). As energy demands are increased in lactation there is a subsequent rise in DMI to meet those increased demands. Inefficient cows with higher DMI are already consuming more energy in gestation than their efficient counterparts, therefore they may not require as drastic an increase in DMI to meet the energetic demands of lactation, whereas efficient cows require greater increases in energy intake to meet demands (Hughes and Pitchford 2004; Meyer et al. 2008). The relative difference in DMI between efficiency classifications associated with physiological stage is likely influenced by dietary energy concentration, as nutritional management during lactation often includes supplementation strategies or altered calving season intended to offset increased energy demands.

In LACT, efficient-classified cows spent 38 min less per day at the hay feeders compared to inefficient cows (p < 0.001, Table 5). Efficient classified lactating cows also had 4.3 fewer feeding events and 2.8 fewer nonfeeding events per day compared to inefficient cows (p < 0.001). Similarly, during GEST, efficient classified cows spent 38 min less per day at the hay feeders compared to inefficient cows (p < 0.001, Table 6). Efficient classified cows had 2.4 fewer feeding events per day and a slower eating rate compared to inefficient cows (p ≤ 0.01), but there was no difference for nonfeeding events (p = 0.28).

Like the current experiment, Basarab et al. (2007) reported fewer feeding events for low vs. high RFI heifers, and Fitzsimons et al. (2014) reported fewer feeding and nonfeeding events for low vs. high RFI females. Fitzsimons et al. (2014) and Hafla et al. (2013) also reported that efficient females spent less time consuming feed and had a slower eating rate compared to inefficient females. Most feed efficiency tests are conducted in confinement facilities due to the limitations of technology to measure individual animal intake on pasture. However, the majority of cow-calf operations rely on grazed forage throughout most of the production cycle. Additional research is needed to determine if high RFI cows, having more bunk visits and time spent consuming feed in a confinement setting, are also more active and spend more time grazing in pasture systems. Sprinkle et al. (2021) measured RFI in yearling heifers fed a forage-based diet in a dry lot setting, then monitored their behavior as 2-year-old lactating cows grazing spring and summer rangeland. The authors found a tendency (p < 0.10) for high RFI cattle to have a greater daily travel distance than low RFI cattle in one of the study periods, but there was no difference for bite rate or overall time spent grazing.

There was no difference in CH4 and CO2 emissions, O2 consumption, or HP (g/day or Mcal/day) between efficient, moderate, and inefficient classified cows in either physiological state (p ≥ 0.35). When emission data was evaluated as CH4, CO2, or HP intensity (g d−1/kg BW0.75 or Mcal d−1/kg BW0.75) there were observed differences among the efficiency classifications. In LACT, efficient classified cows had lower (p = 0.04) CH4 intensity compared with moderate classified cows (Table 7). In GEST (Table 8) there was a tendency (p = 0.07) for efficient classified cows to have lower CH4 intensity compared to moderate classified cows. Additionally, efficient classified cows in gestation had lower (p ≤ 0.04) CO2 intensity and HP intensity compared to the moderate classified cows. There was no difference for any of the emission intensity data between the efficient and inefficient classified cows in either physiological state.

As previously discussed, there are established relationships between DMI and CH4, CO2, and HP both in this data, and in data reported by other authors (Arthur et al. 2018; Bird-Garner et al. 2017; Holder et al. 2022). Given these relationships, it has been hypothesized that cattle with lower RFI will have reduced emissions given the difference in DMI between high and low RFI classifications. However, there is conflicting evidence in the literature, with some studies reporting no relationship between RFI and gas emissions. The lack of difference for total gas production (g/d) between efficiency classifications is similar to results found in several studies evaluating emissions and feed efficiency in various diets and physiological stages (Briggs et al. 2022b; Freetly and Brown-Brandl 2013; Jones et al. 2011; McDonnell et al. 2016; Oliveira et al. 2016). When evaluating CH4 intensity in a grazing experiment, Jones et al. (2011) reported that in lactation, cows from a low RFI lineage had 30% less CH4 intensity compared to cows from a high RFI lineage; however, there was no difference during the gestation period. The researchers hypothesized that this lack of difference in gestation was due to the forage decreasing quality during that physiological stage, which then did not meet the protein requirements of the cattle, impacting digestibility. Oliveira et al. (2016) performed feed efficiency tests on growing cattle in a feedlot setting, and then again on pasture, and measured CH4 emissions in both stages. There was a tendency for low RFI cattle in the feedlot to have a 7% decrease in CH4 intensity compared to high RFI contemporaries, which was similar to the difference seen between efficient and moderate cows in lactation (6.5%) and gestation (5.2%) in the current experiment. Oliveira et al. (2016) found no difference in CH4 intensity in the grazing portion of the trial. It should be noted that in the grazing portion of that experiment, there was also no difference in DMI between the RFI groups, which the researchers believed was due to errors in estimating DMI of grazing cattle. Nkrumah et al. (2009) reported lower CH4 intensity in low RFI steers compared to medium or high RFI steers. Arthur et al. (2018) reported a correlation of 0.24 between CO2 intensity and RFI in growing cattle. There was a correlation of 0.23 and 0.18 (data not shown, p ≤ 0.09) between RFI and CH4 and CO2 intensity respectively in lactation during this experiment; however, there was no correlation between any gas flux data and RFI in gestation (r ≤ 0.13, p ≥ 0.33). Further research evaluating lifetime gas flux data across cattle efficiency classifications is needed to evaluate RFI’s effect on CH4 and CO2; however, the data from this study would suggest that RFI explains very little or none of the variation in greenhouse gas emissions.

Differences in maintenance requirements may partially explain inter-animal variation in feed efficiency (Fitzsimons et al. 2017; Kenny et al. 2018). Lawrence et al. (2012) estimated 18% lower maintenance energy requirements in low RFI heifers on a forage-based diet compared to high RFI heifers. While it was not statistically significant, Castro-Bulle et al. (2007) reported a 10% difference in estimated MEm requirements between high and low RFI steers fed a concentrate diet. Briggs et al. (2022a) found a negative correlation of -0.41 between residual average daily gain and lactating cow metabolizable energy for maintenance, suggesting more efficient cows had lower MEm.

Within each experiment, we applied a constant partial efficiency (Garrett et al. 1980) to estimate the heat increment of energy intake (HiE). However, other work has reported higher partial efficiency of ME use for growth in low-RFI cattle (Nkrumah et al. 2006; Cantalapiedra-Hijar et al. 2018). Increased partial efficiency of ME use for maternal tissue accretion, milk production, or pregnancy increases the estimate of MEm, given the expression

Therefore, at least a portion of the difference among efficiency classifications assigned to MEm in Tables 9 and 10 are likely due to differences in HiE.

It has been estimated that 30% of basal energy expenditure results from visceral tissue metabolism (Ferrell et al. 1986). Additionally, as DMI increases, visceral organ size and energy requirements of the visceral tissue increase (Johnson et al. 1990). Therefore, the differences in MEm requirements across classification groups may be partially attributed to differences in visceral mass. However, the literature regarding viscera mass and RFI is conflicting, with some studies reporting lower viscera mass with low RFI cattle, and others reporting no relationship between viscera mass and RFI (Fitzsimons et al. 2017). Altogether, our results, along with the results of other studies, support the hypothesis that low RFI cows have lower maintenance requirements and (or) increased partial efficiency of ME use.

The mean MEm values reported in this experiment (197 kcal/kg BW0.75, LACT; 175 kcal/kg BW0.75, GEST; Table 2) are higher than the classically reported values of 130–145 kcal/of BW0.75 (Ferrell and Jenkins 1984). Freetly et al. (2023) reported ranges of MEm in lactating cows of 93–178 kcal/kg BW0.75 and ranges of 109–161 kcal/kg BW0.75 in gestating beef cows. One reason for the higher values in the current dataset may derive from ad libitum intake, compared to limit-fed diets in previous studies. Freetly and Nienbar (1998) reported decreased MEm requirements in mature cows when intake was restricted. Freetly et al. (2023) discussed that weight stasis estimates of MEm, where cattle are fed to a fixed level, may underestimate maintenance requirements compared to ad libitum fed cattle. The forage diet offered to the cows in this experiment may also play a role in the elevated estimated MEm requirements. Dong et al. (2015) reported increased MEm requirements of 10.6% in dairy cows fed an all-forage diet compared to cows fed a diet with less than 30% forage.

McDonnell et al. (2016) and Johnson et al. (2019) reported higher digestibility in low RFI cattle; however, results reported by Richardson and Herd (2004) and Lawrence et al. (2011) indicated no difference in digestibility from cattle of differing RFI classifications. The lack of observed difference in this study may be due to the unprocessed hay diet offered, as the effect of intake on digestion is lessened with long-stem forage compared to concentrate diets (Kenny et al. 2018). Additionally, the indirect marker methodology used in the current experiment may not be sensitive enough to detect minor differences in digestive potential (Fitzsimons et al. 2017).

Conclusion

Ranking within a contemporary group for beef cow DMI, RFI, gas flux, and feeding behavior is moderately to highly correlated across physiological states under the conditions of this pen-based forage feeding experiment. This suggests that testing for forage-use efficiency during either production phase should lead to reliable estimates of feed intake and efficiency throughout the production cycle. The moderate to strong relationship between DMI and CH4, CO2, and HP in both lactation and gestation, suggests these metrics may be useful in developing more robust equations to predict feed intake in beef cows. Low RFI beef cows have lower maintenance energy requirements, improved efficiency of ME use, or both. Finally, feeding behavior among efficiency classifications in these pen-based studies suggests that behavioral characteristics in grazing animals may be useful to identify forage-efficient cattle. Future work should evaluate the extent to which these relationships persist under grazing conditions where forage selection, locomotion, and environmental variability may further influence efficiency rankings.
