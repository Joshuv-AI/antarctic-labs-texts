Interplay of ECM organization, ROCK signaling, and cell polarity drives mesothelium formation and lung growth
Authors: Xinyuan Liu, Bingying Lin, Peiyi Li, Zhiqiang Cai, Weitao Cao, Weiping Yang, Jiahang Zeng, Le Li, Yumin Zhou, Danping Huang, Didier Y R Stainier, Pixin Ran, Wenguang Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mechanisms regulating organ size remain poorly understood. Here, we show that FREM2 is a critical modulator of lung size. Frem2 mutant mice exhibit defects in the formation of elastic fibers around mesothelial cells, which compromises phosphorylated myosin light chain (pMLC) signaling and mesothelial cell polarization, leading to lung growth inhibition. These processes are regulated in part through inhibition of p38-mediated upregulation of matrix metalloproteinase-2, as pharmacological decrease of p38 phosphorylation or MMP activity partially attenuates the Frem2 mutant lung phenotypes. Disruption of ROCK function also leads to defects in elastic fiber organization and mesothelial cell polarity, while inhibition of Vangl2-regulated mesothelial cell polarity causes defects in pMLC abundance and elastic fiber structure. Collectively, these processes constitute a positive feedback loop to regulate lung size. Notably, Frem2 mutant mice also display defects in the formation of the mesothelium and reduced growth of other internal organs. Importantly, patients with FREM2 mutations exhibit smaller lungs. These results reveal a shared role and mechanism for the mesothelium in organ size control.Subject terms: Organogenesis, Respiratory tract diseases, Development

Introduction

The lungs arise from the anterior foregut endoderm and start budding at approximately embryonic day 9.5 (E9.5) in mice1,2. Lung branching starts at E12.5 and continues until E16.5 via epithelial invasion into the mesenchyme to generate a tree-like structure. Previous studies have shown that branching is essential for lung growth3–5. The lungs are also surrounded by a layer of mesothelium derived from the mesoderm. The lung mesothelium is mechanically connected to the lung parenchyma and contributes to the mechanical properties of the lungs, as it helps to distribute the mechanical stress within the lungs to promote alveolar and bronchial patency6–8. Lineage tracing studies have shown that mesothelial cells can contribute to the populations of vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts and tracheal/bronchial chondrocytes in the lungs9–11. However, it remains unknown whether and how the mesothelium regulates lung size.

The FRAS/FREM proteins (FRAS1, FREM1, FREM2, and FREM3) are members of a newly recognized family of ECM proteins characterized by 12 consecutive chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (CSPG) motifs, a varied number of Calx-β domains and other motifs12,13. FRAS1, FREM1, and FREM2 form a ternary complex that plays a role in epidermal-dermal interactions, which are essential for the integrity of the skin and renal epithelium14–16. Mutations in the Fras1, Frem1, and Frem2 genes cause Fraser syndrome, which is characterized by cryptophthalmos, syndactyly and abnormalities of the genitalia and urinary tract in mice and humans12,13,17–19. Genetic studies have shown that deficiency of Fras1, Frem1 or Frem2 leads to fusion of the pulmonary lobes in mouse models15,20–22. However, whether FRAS/FREM proteins play a role in lung size control remains unknown.

Pulmonary hypoplasia (PH), a condition characterized by an abnormal reduction in lung weight and/or volume without the absence of any lung lobes, is one of the main lethal malformations in term neonates and it is caused by the incomplete development of the lungs23,24. In most cases, PH is secondary to congenital anomalies that limit fetal lung24–26. Currently, there is no effective postnatal treatment to improve the survival of preterm infants with PH. An in-depth understanding of the etiology and pathogenesis of PH is essential to facilitate the development of therapies.

Here, starting with a forward genetic screen in mice, we reveal a critical role of Frem2 in lung size control. We further show how extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, ROCK signaling, and cell polarity interact to drive mesothelium formation and lung size determination, defects in which leads to PH.

Results

A genetic screen reveals FREM2 as a mediator of pulmonary hypoplasia

To identify regulators of pulmonary hypoplasia (PH), we conducted a large-scale forward genetic screen in mice using N-ethyl-N-nitrosourea (ENU) mutagenesis27–30. One of the recessive mutant pups identified in this screen exhibits hemorrhage in regions around the eyes, along with blood-filled blisters and digit formation defects in the hindlimbs (Fig. 1a). These mutant pups also display smaller lungs with partial fusion among the right cranial (RCr) lobe, the right middle (RMd) lobe and the right caudal (RCd) lobe (Fig. 1b–f). The lung edge in mutant pups appear thickened and more rounded compared with that in the controls. Mutant animals are born at a ratio slightly lower than the Mendelian ratio, indicating that this mutation may cause embryonic lethality for some mice. However, most mutant pups die within 24 h of birth. To identify the phenotype-causing mutation, we performed whole-exome sequencing of G4 genomic DNA samples and identified Frem2, which encodes an ECM protein, as a candidate gene (Fig. 1g). Next, we carried out genetic linkage analysis by genotyping 156 G4, G5 and G6 mutant animals and found complete linkage between the lung phenotypes and the Frem2 G7229A/G7229A allele (Fig. 1h). The identified allele carries a missense mutation (c.7229 G > A) in a codon corresponding to a highly conserved amino acid that is predicted to cause an amino acid substitution in the transmembrane domain (p.C2410Y) (Fig. 1i). We then carried out a complementation test by crossing mice carrying the ENU-induced Frem2 allele (Frem2 G7229A/+) with mice carrying a Frem2 deletion allele (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), and found that complementation did not occur in Frem2-/G7229A double-heterozygotes (Fig. 1j–m), indicating that loss of Frem2 function is likely to be responsible for the lung phenotypes observed in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice. To further investigate the role for Frem2 in lung development, we analyzed lung formation in Frem2−/− mice. Frem2−/− mice exhibited reduced Frem2 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and smaller lungs (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c) with partial fusion of the right lung lobes (Supplementary Fig. 2 d), and most of these pups die within 24 h of birth. These phenotypes were similar to those of Frem2 G7229A/G7229A animals. These mutant pups also show hemorrhage around the eyes, blood-filled blisters in the hindlimbs and syndactyly (Supplementary Fig. 2e).

PH may be secondary to congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH)31. CDH is a developmental defect characterized by incomplete closure of the diaphragm and herniation of fetal abdominal organs such as the stomach, intestines and/or the liver into the thoracic cavity that causes pulmonary hypoplasia, postnatal pulmonary hypertension due to vascular remodeling and cardiac dysfunction32. Frem2ne/ne (Frem2C6479T/C6479T) mice on a mixed CAST/EiJ/B6 background have been reported to exhibit CDH33. We thus examined diaphragm formation and found that approximately 20% of Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice display CDH (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b) without the presence of abdominal contents in the thoracic cavity (Fig. 1f). These results suggest that primary defects in lung formation, rather than its lung compression, cause the reduction in the lung size in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice.

To examine the formation of the lungs in more detail, we performed a systematic analysis of lung development. Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice display no obvious differences in lung size, branching pattern or lobe separation compared with those of wild-type (WT) mice at E13 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Starting at E14.5, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs appeared smaller than those of their WT counterparts, and this phenotype became more pronounced at later stages (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Interestingly, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice exhibited shortened distal lung buds without significant changes in the number of lung branching tips at E15 (Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). Notably, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice also exhibited reduced cell proliferation rate in the epithelium compared with WT mice at E13.5 and E14.5 (Supplementary Fig. 4g–j). These results indicate that Frem2 may direct lung growth by promoting lung bud elongation. Collectively, these data suggest that loss of Frem2 function cause severe PH characterized by a dramatically reduced lung size.

Patients with FREM2 mutations display reduced lung volumes

Since Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice exhibit smaller lungs, we sought to determine whether FREM2 is required for lung growth in humans. We recruited five patients with unilateral or bilateral cryptophthalmos, a typical phenotype caused by FREM2 mutations, and their parents (Fig. 2a–e). We conducted whole-exome sequencing of genomic DNA samples to identify potential mutations in FREM2. Detected mutations were further validated by targeted Sanger sequencing (Fig. 2f–j). Four patients carry the same homozygous missense mutation (c.6499 C > T (p.Arg2167Trp)) in FREM2, and their parents are heterozygotes (Fig. 2f–i). The p.Arg2167Trp mutation is located in the fourth of five consecutive Calx-β domains; in this mutant protein, a highly conserved arginine is replaced with a tryptophan at position 2167 (Arg2167Trp) (Fig. 2k)34. The fifth patient carries compound heterozygous mutations in FREM2 (c.15delG; c.6499 C > T (p.Arg2167Trp)) (Fig. 2e, j). The deletion mutation (c.15delG) is predicted to cause a frameshift and results in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay of FREM234. Next, we examined lung size using high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and 3D reconstruction, a method for estimating lung dimensions35,36. Interestingly, the patients with FREM2 mutations display varying degrees of PH, characterized by smaller lungs, and no obvious changes in body mass index (BMI) compared with age- and sex-matched healthy controls (Fig. 2l–r and Supplementary Tables 1–3). Notably, the patient carrying the compound heterozygous mutations (c.15delG; c.6499 C > T (p.Arg2167Trp)) exhibits a more severe lung phenotype (Fig. 2e, j, p). These results in humans are consistent with the findings in mice, suggesting that FREM2 dysfunction contributes to symptoms of PH.

Mesothelial cell polarization is required for lung growth

We then examined the spatio-temporal expression patterns of Frem2/FREM2 in developing mouse lungs. FREM2 appeared to localize at the membrane of the pulmonary epithelial cells, mesenchymal cells in the lung parenchyma, and mesothelial cells during embryonic development (Fig. 3a, b). FREM2 can be detected in mesothelial cells labeled with PDPN as early as E12.5 and was also highly expressed from E16.5 to E18.5 (Fig. 3c). Frem2 mRNA is expressed in the lungs from E11.5 to P0 (Fig. 3d). Frem2 mRNA is highly expressed in mesothelial cells labeled with Wt1, with relatively lower expression in epithelial cells and other mesenchymal cells in E16.5 lungs (Fig. 3e, f). Meanwhile, FREM2 is identified as a marker for mesothelium in humans from A Census of the Lung: CellCards from LungMAP37. We found that FREM2 was also highly expressed in the mesothelium of human infant lungs (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 4).

Since Frem2 is expressed in the lung epithelium, mesenchyme and mesothelium, we examined changes in lung cell differentiation. Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice exhibit a WT-like distribution of PDPN+ alveolar type I cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a), SFTPC+ alveolar type II cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b), SCGB1A1+ club cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c), acetylated alpha-tubulin+ multiciliated cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c), PDGFRA+ or VIMENTIN+ fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e) and αSMA+ airway smooth muscle cells (Supplementary Fig. 5f). These results suggest that Frem2 is dispensable for epithelial cell or mesenchymal cell differentiation in the lungs.

Next, we examine changes in mesothelium in the lungs. The lung mesothelium of Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice appeared difficult to be separated from the underlying tissue, while that of WT mice can be easily isolated as one layer from the lungs (Fig. 4a). Interestingly, lung mesothelial cells of Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice exhibit abnormally rounded shapes, as determined by scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 4b) and hematoxylin and eosin staining (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mesothelial cells appear less flattened and more cuboidal than the corresponding WT cells (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6b–d). In WT PDPN+ mesothelial cells, PDPN was evenly distributed across the membrane, forming a continuous PDPN+ mesothelial layer in the lungs (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, PDPN tended to locate in discrete regions with a more compact distribution, forming a discontinuous PDPN+ mesothelial layer in the Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). However, we did not observe obvious changes in the differentiation of WT1+ mesothelial cells in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c).

Changes in cell shape are associated with the cell polarization state28,38–40. We therefore examined mesothelial cell polarity by analyzing the localization of the Golgi apparatus relative to the cell nucleus, using the cis-Golgi matrix marker GM130, a widely used method to determine cell polarity in different cell types28,41. In WT mesothelial cells, the GM130-labeled Golgi apparatus exhibits a ribbon-like morphology and localized preferentially by the long edges of the nucleus (Fig. 4e, f). However, in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mesothelial cells, the Golgi apparatus exhibits a more compact structure with changed distribution (Fig. 4e, f). Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs exhibit no obvious changes in the morphology or distribution of the Golgi apparatus in the airway epithelium (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). We further tested changes in the polarization of mesothelial cells using another polarity marker, ZO-1. The apical side of WT mesothelial cells exhibit clear ZO-1 localization. In contrast, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mesothelial cells exhibit decreased apical localization of ZO-1 (Fig. 4g, h and Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). This effect on mesothelial cell polarity appeared specific, as we did not observe significant differences between WT and mutant animals in cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d) or in cell apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f) in the lung parenchyma or in the mesothelium. These data suggest that Frem2 is required for the polarization of mesothelial cells toward a spindle shape during lung mesothelium formation.

Next, we examined whether disruption of mesothelial cell polarity can inhibit lung growth. Vangl2, a key component of the planar cell polarity pathway expressed in lung cells, including mesothelial cells, is essential for cell polarization during tissue formation42,43. To disrupt mesothelial cell polarization, we inhibited Vangl2 by using morpholino-mediated gene knockdown, a method that has been used in lung development studies44,45. After ex vivo treatment with two Vangl2 vivo-morpholinos (vivo-morpholino-1 and vivo-morpholino-2) for 48 h, E16.5 lungs exhibit significantly reduced organ size (Fig. 4i–k and Supplementary Fig. 9). We chose vivo-morpholino-1 for the following studies, and found that this treatment led to abnormally rounded mesothelial cells (Fig. 4l, m) and altered mesothelial cell polarity (Fig. 4n, o). These data indicate that mesothelial cell polarization is required for lung growth.

Mesothelial cell deletion of Frem2 compromises lung growth

To examine which cell type defects are responsible for the lung growth phenotype in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice, we generated a Frem2 flox allele (Supplementary Fig. 1c–e) and specifically deleted Frem2 using the Wt1CreERT2 line, which can induce efficient recombination in the lung mesothelium as early as E11.59. We conditionally deleted Frem2 in the lung mesothelium by intraperitoneal tamoxifen injection at E9.5 (Fig. 5a, b). Mice with mesothelial deletion of Frem2 exhibit smaller lungs (Fig. 5c, d) and alterations in mesothelial cell shape (Fig. 5e, f) and polarity (Fig. 5g–j), similar to Frem2 G7229A/G7229A animals. To determine the effect of FREM2 on WT1+ mesothelial cell-derived cells, we performed lineage tracing analysis. We did not observe obvious differences in the number of WT1+ mesothelial cell-derived cells in the lung parenchyma in Wt1CreERT2;Frem2 flox/flox;mT/mG mice compared with Wt1CreERT2;Frem2 flox/+;mT/mG mice (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating that FREM2 does not affect cell differentiation from mesothelial cells during lung development. We also deleted Frem2 using the Twist2Cre line, which induces efficient recombination in the lung mesenchyme and mesothelium as early as E10.5.46. Twist2Cre;Frem2 flox/flox mice exhibit hemorrhage in regions around the eyes (Supplementary Fig. 11a), fused and smaller lungs (Supplementary Fig. 11b–e) and altered mesothelial cell shapes (Supplementary Fig. 11f, g) compared with controls. However, epithelial cell-specific deletion of Frem2 (Nkx2.1Cre;Frem2 flox/flox) do not lead to obvious differences in lung size (Supplementary Fig. 11h–j). Notably, adult Twist2Cre;Frem2 flox/flox mice, but not adult Nkx2.1Cre;Frem2 flox/flox mice exhibit a lower forced vital capacity (Supplementary Fig. 11k, l), indicating reduced lung volumes in the smaller lungs. Collectively, these data suggest that the function of Frem2 is specifically required in the mesothelium for lung growth and function.

Since other internal organs, such as the heart and stomach, are also enveloped by a mesothelium (called the epicardium in the heart)47–49, we sought to determine whether Frem2 is also required for the growth of these organs. We found that Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice display smaller hearts than their WT siblings (Supplementary Fig. 12a-–). We also analyzed the distribution of FREM2 in the developing heart and observed that FREM2 locates around the epicardium at E16.5 (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Frem2 G7229A/G7229A hearts display a more rounded epicardial cell shape (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f) and altered epicardial cell polarity (Supplementary Fig. 12g, h). In addition, FREM2 is highly expressed around the stomach mesothelium at E18.5 (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Frem2 G7229A/G7229A stomachs also exhibit a reduced size (Supplementary Fig. 13b, c), a more rounded mesothelial cell shape (Supplementary Fig. 13d) and altered mesothelial cell polarity (Supplementary Fig. 13e). Collectively, these data suggest that FREM2-mediated mesothelial cell polarity plays the primary role in organ growth.

A positive feedback loop in the mesothelium drives lung growth

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying Frem2-mediated lung growth, we examined ECM protein formation and levels. Mesothelial cells are predominantly attached to the basement membrane, which is underlain by an ECM layer containing elastic fibers and dense bundles of fibrillar collagen that can be produced by mesothelial cells50–53. Since elastin provides tissues and organs with elasticity to promote organ formation50,54, we hypothesized that Frem2 deficiency might lead to structural defects in Elastin, which caused defects in pleural mesothelium formation and lung growth. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed Elastin structure and levels. The elastic fibers around WT mesothelial cells are relatively continuous at E13.5 and E16.5 (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). In contrast, fragmented elastic fibers are observed in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 14a, b) and Wt1CreERT2;Frem2 flox/flox lungs (Fig. 6c, d). However, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs exhibit no significant changes in the protein levels of Elastin, Fibronectin or Collagen IV (a basement membrane component) (Supplementary Fig. 14c, d), indicating that FREM2 is required for elastic fiber formation but not for Elastin protein expression. Next, we examined elastic fibers in the epicardium and the stomach mesothelium. Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice also exhibit disrupted elastic fibers around the epicardium (Supplementary Fig. 12i) and the stomach mesothelium (Supplementary Fig. 13f). Thus, we hypothesized that disruption of elastic fibers might phenocopy the Frem2 deficiency-induced PH and defects in pleural mesothelium formation. Interestingly, after treatment for 8 h or 48 h with 2 mU/ml elastase, the lung mesothelium became difficult to be separated from the underlying tissue compared with controls (Fig. 6e). After treatment for 48 h with 2 mU/ml elastase, E16.5 lungs exhibit a significant reduction in size (Fig. 6f-h) and alterations in mesothelial cell shape (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b) and polarity (Fig. 6i, j) compared with controls. These results suggest that FREM2-mediated elastin formation is essential for mesothelial polarization and lung growth.

Actomyosin forces can regulate cell shape and promote organ formation55,56. We hypothesized that compromised actomyosin contractility might also lead to defects in mesothelial cell shape and polarity, and a reduced lung size. After treatment for 48 h with Y27632, a ROCK inhibitor that can diminish actomyosin contractility57–59, E16.5 lungs displayed a significant reduction in lung size (Fig. 6k–m), reduced apical mesothelial enrichment of phosphorylated myosin light chain (pMLC) (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b), a marker of actomyosin tension, unaltered total MLC levels (Supplementary Fig. 16c, d), and alterations in mesothelial cell shape (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d) and polarity (Fig. 6n, o) compared with controls. Notably, Y27632 treatment also results in disruption of elastic fibers (Fig. 6p, q), while Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs and elastase-treated lungs also exhibit decreased apical enrichment of pMLC and unaltered total MLC levels in the mesothelium (Fig. 7a–d and Supplementary Fig. 16e–h). Interestingly, all of these phenotypes are also observed in Vangl2 morpholino-treated lungs (Fig. 7e–j). We further examined how Vangl2 affects elastin fiber formation, and we examined the expression of genes involved in elastin deposition and assembly. After ex vivo treatment with Vangl2 morpholinos for 48 h, E16.5 lungs exhibit significantly decreased levels of Fbn1, Fbln4, Fbln5, Lox and Loxl1 but not Fbn2 (Supplementary Fig. 17). These results suggest that Vangl2 is required for elastin deposition and assembly. In addition, intraperitoneal injection of Y27632 also leads to reduced lung size (Supplementary Fig.18). Since several pathways, including Wnt, FGF, hedgehog, and BMP signalings, are involved in the regulation of lung growth, we examined for changes in these pathways. We found that Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs exhibited no significant changes in Wnt signaling genes (Wnt2, Wnt3a, Wnt5a, Wnt7b, Axin2 or Ccnd1) (Supplementary Fig. 19a), FGF signaling genes (Fgf9, Fgf10, Fgfr1c, Fgfr2c, Spry1or Spry2) (Supplementary Fig. 19b), hedgehog signaling genes (Shh, Smo, Gli1, Gli2 or Gli3) (Supplementary Fig. 19c) or Bmp4 (Supplementary Fig. 19 d), indicating that Frem2 signals via a Wnt, FGF, hedgehog or BMP independent pathway to direct lung growth. Next, we examined hearts and stomachs and found that Frem2 G7229A/G7229A hearts and stomachs also display decreased apical enrichment of pMLC in the epicardium (Supplementary Fig. 20a) and mesothelium (Supplementary Fig. 20b), respectively. In addition, elastase, Y27632 or Vangl2 morpholino treatment also leads to decreased sizes of the heart (Supplementary Fig. 21a–f) and stomach (Supplementary Fig. 21g–l). Altogether, these data suggest that ROCK signal, elastic fibers and mesothelial cell polarity interact to drive lung and other internal organ growth (Fig. 7k).

Frem2 regulates lung growth via p38-mediated Mmp expression

We sought to further understand how FREM2 deficiency leads to impaired elastic fiber formation around the mesothelium. Our observation of short elastic fibers prompted us to measure the levels of MMP2 and MMP9, critical Elastin-cleaving enzymes29,30. Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs exhibit significantly increased levels of MMP2 in the mesothelium (Fig. 8a). Patients with FREM2 mutations also exhibit increased MMP2 levels in the serum compared with healthy controls (Fig. 8b). However, Mmp9 levels appear unaffected in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lung mesothelium compared with WT (Supplementary Fig. 22). Consistent with the above findings, knockdown of FREM2 in MeT-5A human pleural mesothelial cells led to increased levels of MMP2 but not MMP9 (Fig. 8c–f).

Based on the finding that MMP2 levels are elevated in the lung mesothelial cells surrounded by disrupted elastic fibers in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice, we hypothesized that MMP inhibition might rescue the Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lung phenotypes. GM6001, a broad-spectrum inhibitor of MMP, has been shown to reduce Elastin cleavage60–62. We thus tested whether GM6001 treatment could rescue the Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lung phenotypes. Interestingly, after in vivo treatment with GM6001, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs exhibit partially rescued lung growth (Fig. 8g–i) and mesothelial cell phenotypes (Fig. 8j, k), as well as partial restoration of elastic fiber formation (Fig. 8l, m), as assessed in a blind analysis. In addition, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs treated with MMP2 inhibitor I, an irreversible inhibitor of MMP230, also display partially rescued lung growth (Fig. 8n-p). These results suggest that increased MMP2 levels in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs partially accounts for the mesothelium formation and lung growth phenotypes.

We aimed to identify the mechanisms by which Frem2 regulates Mmp2 expression. Mmps have been identified to be downstream targets of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling63, and inhibition of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway can efficiently downregulate Mmp2 expression29,64. Thus, we hypothesized that Frem2 deficiency might lead to increased p38 MAPK activity. As expected, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mesothelium exhibits significantly increased levels of phosphorylated p38 compared with WT (Fig. 9a–d). Interestingly, after in vivo treatment with SB203580, a small molecule that decreases p38 phosphorylation65, Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs exhibit partial rescue of lung growth (Fig. 9e-g) and mesothelial cell shape (Fig. 9h, i), and restoration of elastic fiber formation (Fig. 9j, k) and MMP2 expression (Fig. 9l), as assessed in a blind analysis. These results indicate that the increase in p38 phosphorylation-mediated MMP expression in Frem2 G7229A/G7229A lungs partially accounts for the mesothelium phenotypes via its action on ECM homeostasis.

Discussion

Understanding the mechanisms underlying organ size control is a fundamental goal in developmental biology. Circulating factors including hormones and insulin-like growth factors have been shown to influence organ growth66. Nutrient restriction can cause reduced organ size66. Embryonic progenitor cells are important for organ growth in several contexts67,68. Several molecular mechanisms of organ autonomous size determination have been revealed in the past years. For example, the Hippo pathway functions as a organ autonomous size regulator to determinate organ size69. Interestingly, basement membrane (BM)-mediated integrin signaling coordinates epiblast cell polarity and apical constriction to promote lumenogenesis in the pluripotent epiblast70. Spatiotemporal remodeling of the BM can also coordinate embryo growth and morphogenesis71. Lumenal pressure and cortical tension of the trophectoderm form a positive feedback loop to regulate embryo size72. Our study has elucidated the pivotal roles of structural defects in elastic fibers, ROCK-mediated mechanical forces, and Vangl2-regulated cell polarity in the development of pulmonary mesothelium. These defects not only impair cell polarization and decrease mechanical forces, leading to a reduced lung size, but also establish a positive feedback loop critical for lung growth. Remarkably, our findings extend beyond the lungs, as similar mechanisms involving Frem2 mutations and disruptions in mechanical forces and cell polarization contribute to smaller sizes in hearts and stomachs. This broad applicability underscores the fundamental importance of mesothelium-related mechanical forces, ECM organization, and cell polarization in organ size determination. However, we have not directly measured mechanical forces in these organs here. It remains necessary to directly examine mechanical forces in future studies.

Y27632 can inhibit both ROCK1 and ROCK2. It has been reported that ROCK1 and ROCK2 can act redundantly to regulate MLC phosphorylation, actomyosin contractility and cell shape in mouse embryo fibroblasts (MEFs)73. Although it is possible that only inhibition ROCK1 or ROCK2 does not impair the mesothelium formation and lung growth, it will be necessary to examine the effect of mesothelial cell-specific deletion of Rock1, Rock2, or Rock1 and Rock2 on lung growth.

A previous study has reported that the myF11 mutaion in Frem2, which is predicted to result in a truncated protein, causes fused lung lobes, malformed or complete absence of the septa and valves of the heart in mice15. This study has not described the organ size phenotype. It is interesting to test whether the lungs and other internal organs are smaller in mice with the myF11 mutation. Since FREM2 is expressed in the mesothelium and lung parenchyma, our study determines that Frem2 functions in mesothelial cells to drive lung growth. Another study has reported that FREM2 is expressed in the basement membrane of the mesothelial layer and of the bronchial epithelium in images with low resolution74. However, our high-resolution images show that FREM2 is also located at the membrane of the pulmonary epithelial cells in the airways and alveolar walls, mesenchymal cells in lung parenchyma, and mesothelial cells, which makes it necessary to investigate the function of FREM2 in epithelial cell types and mesenchymal cell types during lung development or regeneration. In addition, our work shows that mesothelial/mesenchymal-specific deletion of Frem2 (Twist2Cre;Frem2 flox/flox mice) leads to a similar lung size reduction to mesothelial-cell-specific deletion (Wt1CreERT2;Frem2 flox/flox), suggesting that Frem2 mainly functions in mesothelial cells to drive lung growth. However, we do not exclude the possibility that Frem2 may also function in the mesenchyme to mediate lung growth. It remains necessary to examine the effect of specific deletion of Frem2 in the mesenchyme on lung size using Gli1Cre;Frem2 flox/flox mice. Because FRAS1, FREM1, and FREM2 form a ternary complex in tissues, it is also interesting to examine whether inhibition of FRAS1 or FREM1 can also lead to smaller lungs.

The recent studies in zebrafish have identified different subpopulations of epicardial cells, demonstrating functional diversity75,76. Our findings that pulmonary mesothelium exhibits cell polarity variability suggest cellular heterogeneity among these mesothelial cells. Future studies onthe identification of pulmonary mesothelial subpopulations and which subpopulation contributes to lung growth or pathology in diseases could possibly provide undiscovered strategies for the development of therapies for human lung diseases.

Point mutations and deletion mutations in FREM2 are associated with several human disorders34,77–80. In this study, we also observe PH characterized by a smaller lung size in four patients with FREM2 mutations. Given that Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice also display smaller hearts and stomachs than WT mice, it will be interesting to examine whether human patients with FREM2 mutations also exhibit changes in the heart and stomach sizes. It is also worth examining whether PH patients without mutations in FREM2 exhibit reduced FREM2 levels. Mutations in FREM1 and FRAS1, another two genes encoding ECM proteins which form a macromolecular ternary complex together with FREM2, have also been shown to be associated with congenital disorders in humans. It remains interesting to test whether these patients also display reduced lung size. We propose that synergistic interactions of ECM organization, mechanical forces and mesothelial cell polarization in promoting organogenesis is conserved in humans.

Methods

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Guangzhou Medical University and the local ethics committee (Regierungspräsidium Darmstadt, Hessen, Germany). All mouse husbandry was performed under standard conditions in accordance with institutional (Guangzhou Medical University) and national ethical and animal welfare guidelines. Thirty C57BL/6 J male mice treated with a 3 × 100 mg/kg dose of ENU were obtained from Dr. Monica Justice (Baylor College of Medicine, Houston, TX). After a period of 10 weeks for the recovery of fertility, the mutagenized G0 males were crossed with C57BL/6 J female mice. G1 males were outcrossed with C57BL/6 J females to generate G2 females. Four G2 females were backcrossed with their G1 father, and the resulting G3 P0 pups were subject to tracheal and lung dissection and analysis. The Frem2 deletion (Frem2KO) and floxed (Frem2 flox) alleles were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system and homology directed repair (Supplementary Fig. 1). The following mouse lines were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory: Wt1CreERT2 (JAX strain #: 010912), Nkx2.1Cre (JAX strain #: 008661), Twist2Cre (JAX strain #: 008712) and mT/mG (JAX strain #: 007676). PdgfraGFP reporter mice were provided by Dr. Christer Betsholtz. Male and female mice were randomly allocated to the investigation groups in this study.

Whole-exome sequencing analysis and genotyping

For whole-exome sequencing in mice, genomic DNA was isolated from two wild-type (WT) and two mutant mice using standard protocols by the SureSelect Mouse All Exon kit V1 (Agilent) kit, and sequenced using HiSeq 2000 (Illumina) with minimum average 50× target sequence coverage (BGI-Hong Kong). Sequence reads were aligned to the C57BL/6 J mouse reference genome (mm10) and analyzed using CLCBio Genomic Workbench (Qiagen, version 21.0.3) and GATK software (version 4.2.6.1). In order to minimize false negatives, variant calls were set at 5× minimum coverage and ≥20% alternate reads. Sequence variants were annotated to single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from dbSNP (version 142) and filtered against dbSNP128. For genotyping Frem2 G7229A/G7229A mice, primers Fwd (5′-TAGCCCTTCTGCATGTGGTC-3′) and Rev (5′-CACCTTCCTCTCTGCCGATG-3′) were used to generate a ~ 529-bp PCR amplicon. PCR products were then sequenced using the primer (5′-TTCTGAACAACACATTGCAT-3′). Mutations were analyzed using SnapGene Software (version 4.1.9). For whole-exome sequencing in humans, genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood using the standard protocols by the SureSelectXT Human All Exon V6 (Agilent) kit, and sequenced using DNBSEQ with minimum average 100× target sequence coverage (BGI-Wuhan). Sequence reads were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) (version 0.7.17) and analyzed by the GATK software. Sequence variants were annotated to SNPs from dbSNP (version 151). SNPs of FREM2 were then aligned to 45 disease-causing mutations which have been reported in HGMD Professional (https://portal.biobase-international.com/hgmd/pro/all.php). Identified SNPs in FREM2 were then confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Single-cell resolution in situ hybridization on tissues (SCRINSHOT)

To perform SCRINSHOT81, lung cryosections were treated with 0.1 M HCl for permeabilization, dehydrated with alcohol and then covered with hybridization chambers (Grace Bio-labs). After rehydration, sections were incubated with blocking solution containing 0.1 μM oligo-dT, Ampligase buffer, 0.05 M KCl, 20% formamide, 0.2 μg/μl BSA, 1 U/μl Ribolock (Thermo Fisher) and 0.2 μg/μl tRNA (Ambion) for 30 min. Padlock probes were incubated with sections for 15 min at 55 °C and then for 120 min at 45 °C, washed with 10% formamide in 2× SSC and ligated with SplintR (NEB) for 16–24 h at 25 °C. Rolling circle amplification was performed using Φ29 polymerase (Lucigen) and the primer TAAATAGACGCAGTCAGT∗A∗A. Amplified products were fixated with 4% PFA and then incubated with detection oligos at RT for 60 min. Coverslips were mounted with SlowFadeTM Gold Antifade mounting medium (Thermo Fisher) for imaging.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

For SEM analysis, embryonic lungs or hearts were fixed with solutions (2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer) overnight at 4 °C, fixed with solutions (1% osmium tetroxide and 0.8% potassium ferrocyanide in 0.1 M phosphate buffer) for 2 h and then processed using a standard protocol82. Images were taken using a HITACHI Regulus 8100.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA extraction from the lungs using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) and complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis using HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323, Vazyme) were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using CFX Connect Real-Time system (Bio-Rad) and ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme). The primers used for qPCR were shown in Supplementary Table 5. Mean Ct values were shown in Supplementary Table 6.

Wholemount immunostaining

The lungs were isolated from E16.5 embryos, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C and then washed three times in PBS for 1 h each. Samples were incubated in 5% FBS/PBS/0.5% Triton X-100/3% bovine serum albumin for 12 h and then in primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After six washes in 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS for 1 h each, samples were incubated in secondary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Samples were washed six times in 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS for 1 h each and mounted for imaging. To visualize elastic fibers, the lungs were stained for Tropoelastin.

Immunostaining of cryosections

The lungs were dissected in PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, incubated in 10% sucrose and 30% sucrose for 24 h each at 4 °C, mounted in OCT embedding compound, and sectioned at 10 µm thickness. Immunostaining was performed using standard protocols. Immunostaining for phospho-p38 and PDPN, Immunostaining for Tropoelastin and Mmp2 SCRINSHOT, in situ hybridization for Mmp2 and Mmp9, and immunohistochemistry for MMP2 were performed with WT and mutant lung sections on the same slide.

Western blotting

Isolated fetal lungs were lysed using RIPA buffer (9806, Cell Signaling Technology) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (5872, Cell Signaling Technology). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min, subjected to SDS/PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were probed with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) and were developed using the ECL detection system (Tanon).

In Vivo chemical treatments

GM6001 (2983, TOCRIS), MMP2 inhibitor I (sc-204092, Santa Cruz) or SB203580 (1202, TOCRIS) were dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), and fresh dilutions in saline were prepared immediately before administration to pregnant females. Intraperitoneal injection of GM6001 (100 mg/kg), MMP2 inhibitor I (20 mg/kg), or SB203580 (20 mg/kg) were performed once daily from E12.5 to E17.5. Tracheas and lungs tissues were collected from E17.5 embryos for analysis.

Ex Vivo chemical treatments

Tracheas and lungs were isolated from E16.5 embryos and cultured using an established protocol83. For elastase (LS002290, Worthington Biochemical Corporation) treatment, a 2 U/mL stock solution was diluted to 2 mU/mL. For vivo-morpholinos (Gene Tools LLC) treatment, a 0.5 mM stock solution in distilled water was diluted to 5 μM. Two morpholino oligonucleotides targeting Vangl2 pre-mRNA were designed to induce exon skipping to inhibit gene expression. The first morpholino targets the junction between exon 2 and intron 2. The second one targets the junction between exon 3 and intron 3. The sequences of these two morpholinos are as follows: Vivo-morpholino-1 antisense oligonucleotides to Vangl2: TGATCCGCTCGGAGCTAGACCCACC; Vivo-morpholino-2 antisense oligonucleotides to Vangl2: ACCACTTTAGCCCCAGTGCTCACCC. The standard control morpholino (CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA) was used as controls. The target regions of the pre-mRNA were illustrated with brackets in the following sequences (sense strand): Vangl2-1 target: TCCCGGAAGCACA[GTGGGTCTAGCTCCGAGCGGATCA]G; Vangl2-2 target: GGGGATGAGC[GGGTGAGCACTGGGGCTAAAGTGGT]TT. For Y27632 (S6390, Selleck) treatment, a 10 mM stock solution was diluted to 10 μM. Isolated tracheas and lungs were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium containing the above chemicals at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 48 hours. The medium was replaced every 24 hours before collection for analysis. After 48 hours ex vivo culture in the medium, the lungs were collected for analysis.

Cell culture and siRNA knockdowns

The MeT-5A cell line was purchased from ATCC and maintained in MeT-5A culture medium (CM-0666, Procell). The small interfering RNA (siRNA) of FREM2 was designed and synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). MeT-5A cells were transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequences of three siRNA for FREM2 are GCAGAGCAGTTTACTCAAA (FREM2 siRNA-1), GTCTTACTCTGATTACATA (FREM2 siRNA-2) and GGTTCATGATCACAAGCAA (FREM2 siRNA-3).

Antibodies

Computed tomography scans and lung volume measurement

High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT) scans were performed using Siemens CT scanner (Berlin and Munich) in the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. Children were scanned in the supine position during quiet breathing, with axial CT images reconstructed into 1-mm-thick slices at 10-mm intervals. All images were archived in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format for subsequent analysis. Lobar volume quantification and 3D reconstruction were performed using 3D Slicer software (version 4.10.2). Lung segmentation was conducted via the Parenchyma Analysis module and Interactive Lobe Segmentation module in Chest Imaging Platform. Semi-automated lobar segmentation (right upper/middle/lower lobes; left upper/lower lobes) combined region-growing algorithms to identify fissures and manual refinement for anatomical accuracy. Voxel dimensions derived from DICOM metadata were used to calculate lobar volumes.

Pulmonary function measurement

Pulmonary function was assessed using Forced Pulmonary Maneuver System (Buxco Research Systems; Wilmington, NC) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (200 mg/kg body weight) with supplemental doses administered as needed to maintain surgical anesthesia. Following tracheostomy, animals were intubated with a system-specific cannula and positioned in the sealed body chamber. The system was calibrated using a series of calibration syringes. Forced vital capacity (FVC) was determined by applying Boyle’s law to pressure-volume relationships measured during quasi-static inflation/deflation maneuvers.

Imaging

Imaging of wholemount lungs and lung sections was performed using a Zeiss AXIO Zoom.V16 or Zeiss 880 and 980 inverted confocal microscope. Quantification of immunofluorescence intensity, nuclear aspect ratio, Golgi-apparatus position relative to the nucleus, cell number, short elastic fibers, left lobe length and lobe surface area was performed using ImageJ (version 1.53). Immunofluorescence intensity: normalized to background signal using region-of-interest (ROI) thresholding with exclusion of saturated pixels; Nuclear aspect ratio: calculated as major axis/minor axis in DAPI-stained nuclei with exclusion of fragmented or overlapping nuclei; Golgi-apparatus positioning: measured as the Euclidean distance between Golgi centroid and nuclear centroid in XY coordinates; Cell counting: automated using ‘Analyze Particles’ function with size exclusion criteria (50–500 μm²) and manual verification; Short elastic fibers: defined as fibers <5 μm in length, quantified via ‘Skeletonize’ plugin after threshold-based binarization; Lobe morphometrics: left lobe length and surface area measured using polygon selection tool with pixel-to-μm conversion calibrated by stage micrometer.

Quantification of distal lung bud length

The distal lung bud length was measured using ZEN software (version 2.1). The length of the lung bud was defined as the distance from the most distal point of the lung bud to the basement of the bud.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad software (version 8.0.3). In cases where two groups were analyzed, samples were tested with unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. In cases where three groups were analyzed, the analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess two group differences in the ratio of fused lung lobe and the ratio of congenital diaphragmatic hernia. p < 0.05 indicates a finding is significant. p > 0.05 indicates a finding is not significant.

Statistics and reproducibility

All results were confirmed in at least three independent experiments with control and experimental samples processed in parallel. Sample sizes (n) refer to the number of animals analyzed per group and were chosen based on prior publications and standard practice to ensure adequate power. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Animals were allocated to groups according to genotype and/or treatment, and WT/mutant genotypes were blinded to investigators before analysis. For pharmacological and morpholino treatments, investigators were blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis; when blinding was not feasible due to obvious phenotypes, outcome measures were objective and quantifiable. Statistical analyses were performed on mean values across biological replicates, with details of sample size, statistical tests, and P values provided in figure legends.
