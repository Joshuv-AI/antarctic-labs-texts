The incidence and associated factors of pulmonary embolism after cardiac surgery: A retrospective nationwide inpatient sample database study
Authors: Lili Zheng, Hao Xie, Xianghua Cao, Zifeng Wang, Jianwen Li, Xiao Wang, Xueping Li, Bingquan Tu, Yongxiang Ren, Jian Wang, Wengui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac surgery is associated with a significant risk of postoperative pulmonary embolism (PE), a life-threatening complication. This study sought to evaluate the epidemiology, associated factors, and clinical outcomes of PE following cardiac surgery in the United States.MethodsUsing the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database, we conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent cardiac surgery between 2010 and 2022. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify patient- and hospital-level factors associated with PE. Clinical outcomes, including in-hospital mortality, length of stay, and total charges, were also assessed, stratified by pre-COVID (2010–2019) and COVID-era (2020−2022) periods.ResultsThe incidence of PE following cardiac surgery significantly increased from 0.6% in 2010 to 3.2% in 2022, and remained elevated during the 2020–2022 period. Independent associated factors included female sex, Black race, a higher comorbidity burden (≥3 comorbidities), Medicaid insurance coverage, admission to large or teaching hospitals, and prolonged hospitalization (> 9 days). High-risk comorbidities included metastatic cancer, drug abuse, and pulmonary circulation disorders. PE development was associated with increased in-hospital mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and increased total charges.ConclusionsThis large-scale analysis reveals a rise in the incidence of PE following cardiac surgery over the past decade. Identifying high-risk patient profiles and associated factors can guide targeted prevention strategies and optimize perioperative management to mitigate the substantial clinical and economic burden of this severe complication.Keywords: Cardiac surgery, Pulmonary embolism, Associated factors, Incidence, Nationwide inpatient sample

Introduction

Cardiac surgery represents a cornerstone of modern cardiovascular medicine, though it carries inherent risks. Among these, pulmonary embolism (PE) stands out as a significant postoperative complication. In the United States, the incidence of acute PE has shown a consistent upward trend over recent decades, attributed to both increased life expectancy and improved diagnostic techniques facilitated by advancements in imaging technology [1]. A similar pattern has been observed in European populations, with annual PE cases ranging from 300,000 to 600,000 across both regions [2]. Such a substantial prevalence highlights the urgent need to better understand and effectively manage the risk of PE in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

The etiology of PE encompasses both inherited thrombophilic conditions and acquired factors, with cardiac surgery representing a particularly high-risk setting due to the combination of prolonged immobilization, surgical trauma, and coagulation disturbances [3], [4].The pathophysiology of PE is characterized by obstruction of the pulmonary vasculature due to thromboembolism, most commonly arising from deep venous thrombosis (DVT) [2], [5]. Current evidence suggests that PE represents the third leading cause of cardiovascular-related mortality, following stroke and myocardial infarction [2], [3], [6]. The potential for sudden, fatal outcomes in this patient population emphasizes the crucial need for robust risk assessment and preventive strategies [7].

Although prior research [1], [8] has extensively documented associated factors for PE in oncology and other clinical settings, PE risk after cardiac surgery remains relatively less well characterized. Existing large-scale analyses have primarily focused on other populations, leaving the specific risk profile for cardiac surgery patients as an area with limited contemporary data. Furthermore, recent data encompassing the COVID-19 pandemic period, which may have influenced thromboembolic risk, are limited. Using the Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS), we sought to estimate the incidence of PE and identify potential associated factors in this cohort over a 13-year period (2010−2022), with a specific focus on comparing trends before and during the pandemic. Our findings may inform future evidence-based risk stratification tools and help refine perioperative management strategies, with the goal of improving outcomes in this high-risk group.

Material and methods

Ethical approval and data availability statement

The data utilized in this study were sourced from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database, widely acknowledged as the most extensive publicly available database in the United States [9]. As this research employed de-identified public data, it was deemed exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Data source

This retrospective analysis was conducted using data obtained from the NIS database, which encompasses patients who underwent cardiac surgery between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2022. The NIS database contains data uploaded by participating hospitals, covering a large proportion of the U.S. population across diverse geographic regions [9], [10].

Study population

Cardiac surgery patients were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th revisions (clinical revision codes ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM), with procedures categorized as follows: valvular/septal procedures, coronary vessel procedures, and other heart/pericardium procedures (Table S1). These codes have been validated in previously published studies [11]. Postoperative PE was identified using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes in secondary diagnosis fields (DX2-DX30/40), explicitly excluding the principal diagnosis (DX1). This follows the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Patient Safety Indicator #12 methodology to distinguish hospital-acquired complications from conditions present on admission [12], [13]. PE and comorbidities were similarly identified using validated ICD codes [14], detailed in Table S1. Only PE diagnosed for the first time during the index hospitalization was included; patients with PE documented at admission were excluded. After excluding individuals with missing data on hospital characteristics or demographic information (n = 242,302), a final cohort of 4,554,906 patients was obtained from the NIS dataset (Fig. 1).

Flow and inclusion/exclusion of all patients had cardiac surgery.

Covariates and outcomes

The multivariable logistic regression models for both time periods (2010–2019 and 2020–2022) included a set of baseline covariates: demographic factors (age, sex, and race), hospital characteristics (geographic region, bed size, teaching status, and urban/rural location), payer status (Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, etc.), and comorbidity burden (categorized as 0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3 conditions based on AHRQ v3.7 definitions via ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes). The type of cardiac surgery was also adjusted for in all analyses. To capture the evolving clinical landscape and healthcare system dynamics during the pandemic era, the model for the 2020–2022 cohort was further adjusted for two additional variables: transfer status (transfer from another hospital vs. non-transfer) and treatment modality (medical management vs. invasive strategies). These specific variables were excluded from the 2010–2019 model to ensure temporal consistency in variable definitions across the long-term analysis and to account for the lower prevalence of invasive interventions in earlier years. The primary outcome of the study was the diagnosis of PE, while secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, length of stay (LOS), and total charges (TOTCHG).

Statistical analysis

We first performed univariate analyses using chi-square tests to examine the incidence of PE across demographic and clinical variables. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors, adjusting for all covariates listed in Table 1. Given the large sample size and our focus on biological associations and clinical predictors within the cohort, we performed unweighted analyses using the NIS database; this approach avoids potential statistical inflation from weighting and provides stable estimates for hypothesis generation. Prespecified analyses stratified by period were conducted separately for the 2010–2019 cohort and 2020–2022 periods to account for temporal changes in coding practices (ICD-9 to ICD-10 transition) and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value of less than 0.001 was considered statistically significant—a strict threshold chosen to ensure clinical relevance and reduce the risk of Type I error given the large sample size. Owing to the exploratory nature of this study, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons; therefore, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Results

Incidence of PE after cardiac surgery

Analysis of the NIS database identified 4,554,906 patients who underwent cardiac surgery in the United States between 2010 and 2022, of whom 90,110 developed postoperative PE. Over this 13-year period, the incidence of PE rose substantially from 0.6% in 2010 to 3.2% in 2022, representing a more than fivefold increase. A particularly pronounced rise was observed during the 2020–2022 period, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 2).

Annual incidence of pulmonary embolism after cardiac surgery.

Patient demographics

Across both study periods, patients who developed postoperative PE were consistently younger, more often female, and had a higher proportion of Black individuals compared to those without PE. In the 2010–2019 cohort, the median age of PE patients was 60 years versus 65 years in non-PE patients (p < 0.001), with the highest proportion in the 45–64 age group; this pattern persisted in the 2020–2022 cohort (median age 63 vs. 67 years, p < 0.001) (Tables 2 and S2). Female sex was overrepresented in the PE group in both eras (2010–2019 cohort: 48.6% vs. 42.4%; 2020–2022: 46.1% vs. 41.3%; both p < 0.001). Black patients also constituted a higher proportion of the PE group (2010–2019 cohort: 16.9% vs. 12.3%; 2020–2022: 19.5% vs. 14.0%; both p < 0.001), while other racial groups generally showed lower proportions. Multivariable analysis consistently identified coronary artery surgery as an independent factor associated with higher PE risk across both periods (see Section 3.7).

Hospital characteristics associated with PE

Hospital characteristics associated with PE after cardiac surgery showed consistent patterns across both study periods. In the 2010–2019 cohort, patients who developed PE were more frequently treated in large hospitals (62.5% vs. 63.3% in non-PE, p < 0.001), urban locations (95.5% vs. 94.1%, p < 0.001), and teaching hospitals (74.7% vs. 64.9%, p < 0.001); geographically, PE occurred more often in the Midwest (23.3% vs. 22.5%) and South (40.2% vs. 41.1%) regions, and transfer from another hospital was significantly more common among PE patients (17.2% vs. 12.3%, p < 0.001) (Table 2). These associations persisted in the 2020–2022 cohort: PE remained more prevalent in large hospitals (57.8% vs. 56.5%, p < 0.001), urban hospitals (95.3% vs. 94.8%, p < 0.001), and teaching hospitals (81.5% vs. 79.5%, p < 0.001), with similar regional patterns (Midwest 23.7% vs. 21.8%; South 41.1% vs. 40.8%); notably, the proportion of transferred patients among those with PE increased to 22.1% (vs. 16.1% in non-PE, p < 0.001) (Table S2), underscoring the growing relevance of inter-hospital transfer as a related factor.

Adverse outcomes of PE after cardiac surgery

In the 2010–2019 cohort, patients with PE had significantly worse outcomes compared to those without PE, including prolonged median length of stay (15.1 vs. 7.6 days, p < 0.001), higher median total charges ($213,725 vs. $119,437, p < 0.001), and increased in-hospital mortality (16.2% vs. 5.5%, p < 0.001). Patients with PE also had a higher burden of comorbidities (≥3 comorbidities: 79.4% vs. 60.8%, p < 0.001) and were more likely to have Medicaid coverage (17.9% vs. 10.4%, p < 0.001) (Table 2). These adverse associations persisted in the 2020–2022 cohort, though the magnitude of differences narrowed for some outcomes: median length of stay was 10.0 vs. 6.0 days (p < 0.001), median total charges were $164,086 vs. $125,111 (p < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality remained substantially higher in the PE group (21.3% vs. 11.1%, p < 0.001). The comorbidity burden remained elevated (≥3 comorbidities: 81.0% vs. 78.5%, p < 0.001), and insurance patterns were consistent, with PE patients having lower Medicare coverage (47.3% vs. 58.0%, p < 0.001) and higher Medicaid coverage (18.4% vs. 12.8%, p < 0.001) (Table S2). Additionally, in the 2020–2022 period, patients with PE were significantly more likely to receive invasive treatment strategies (5.4% vs. 0.6%, p < 0.001), reflecting greater disease severity in this population (Table S2).

Preoperative comorbidities associated with PE

In the 2010–2019 cohort, comorbidities significantly associated with PE included pulmonary circulation disorders (OR 4.16, 95% CI 4.04–4.28), metastatic cancer (OR 2.20, 95% CI 2.11–2.29), drug abuse (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.90–2.04), and valvular disease (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.49–1.58) (all p < 0.001; Fig. 4). In the 2020–2022 cohort, most of these associations persisted, with strengthened effect sizes for cancer-related conditions and weight loss; pulmonary circulation disorders remained the strongest associated factor (OR 2.39, 95% CI 2.33–2.46), and newly identified associated comorbidities included liver disease, neurological disorders, and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) (Fig. S2). Notably, several conditions traditionally considered risk factors—such as congestive heart failure, diabetes, hypertension, and renal failure—exhibited protective associations in 2020–2022, with ORs ranging from 0.54 to 0.93 (all p < 0.001; Fig. S2). These counterintuitive findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect changes in coding practices, patient selection during the pandemic, or residual confounding, rather than true biological protective effects.

Relationship between pulmonary embolism and preoperative comorbidities (2010–2019 cohort).

Postoperative complications associated with PE

The association between PE and postoperative complications shifted markedly between periods (Fig. 5, Fig. S3). In the 2010–2019 cohort, PE was associated with increased risk of acute myocardial infarction (OR 1.827, 95% CI 1.776–1.879), stroke (OR 2.049, 95% CI 1.814–2.315), chest pain (OR 1.914, 95% CI 1.791–2.047), arrhythmias (OR 1.659, 95% CI 1.569–1.755), and peripheral nerve injuries (OR 1.408, 95% CI 1.256–1.578), but appeared protective against DVT (OR 0.055, 95% CI 0.054–0.057), respiratory failure (OR 0.836, 95% CI 0.811–0.863), aspiration pneumonia (OR 0.555, 95% CI 0.541–0.568), and acute renal failure (OR 0.787, 95% CI 0.747–0.830) (Fig. 5). In the 2020–2022 cohort, these associations reversed dramatically: PE became strongly predictive of DVT (OR 19.956, 95% CI 19.506–20.416), acute respiratory failure (OR 3.069, 95% CI 3.011–3.129), aspiration pneumonia (OR 1.536, 95% CI 1.484–1.590), and acute kidney injury (OR 1.534, 95% CI 1.505–1.564) (Fig. S3). PE remained a significant risk factor for stroke (OR 1.892, 95% CI 1.819–1.967), but paradoxically was associated with lower risk of acute myocardial infarction (OR 0.620, 95% CI 0.603–0.637), chest pain (OR 0.845, 95% CI 0.765–0.934), and arrhythmias (OR 0.759, 95% CI 0.742–0.775) (Fig. S3). The association with peripheral nerve injuries lost statistical significance (OR 1.084, 95% CI 0.833–1.410, p = 0.548) (Fig. S3).

Relationship between pulmonary embolism and postoperative complications (2010–2019 cohort).

Independent factors associated with PE

Multivariable analysis identified consistent factors across both periods (Fig. 3, Fig. S1). In the 2010–2019 cohort, significant factors included female sex (OR 1.127, 95% CI 1.105–1.149), Black race (OR 1.107, 95% CI 1.078–1.137), ≥3 comorbidities (OR 3.361, 95% CI 3.128–3.612), Medicaid insurance (OR 1.125, 95% CI 1.090–1.162), teaching hospital admission (OR 1.224, 95% CI 1.195–1.253), length of stay >9 days (OR 2.208, 95% CI 2.162–2.256), and coronary artery surgery compared to valve surgery (OR 1.195, 95% CI 1.146–1.247) (Fig. 3). In the 2020–2022 cohort, most factors remained significant but with altered effect sizes. Female sex (OR 1.207, 95% CI 1.184–1.231), Black race (OR 1.219, 95% CI 1.188–1.252), Medicaid insurance (OR 1.452, 95% CI 1.405–1.502), teaching hospital admission (OR 1.089, 95% CI 1.058–1.120), length of stay >9 days (OR 1.883, 95% CI 1.845–1.921), and transfer admission (OR 1.406, 95% CI 1.370–1.443) remained significant predictors (Fig. S1). The association with ≥3 comorbidities attenuated markedly (OR 1.148, 95% CI 1.069–1.233). Regarding surgical procedure type, valve surgery (OR 0.471, 95% CI 0.444–0.499) and other cardiac procedures (OR 0.490, 95% CI 0.476–0.505) were associated with significantly lower PE risk compared to coronary artery surgery (Fig. S1).

Factors associated with pulmonary embolism after cardiac surgery (2010–2019 cohort).

Discussion

From 2010 to 2022, the incidence of pulmonary embolism after cardiac surgery increased from 0.6% to 3.2%, a more than fourfold increase, consistent with the trend observed in population-based studies [15]. While improved imaging and systematic screening partly account for this rise [16], the magnitude also reflects demographic and procedural shifts: an aging surgical population, a higher burden of comorbidities such as cancer and obesity, increasingly complex cardiac operations [17]. The COVID-19 pandemic during 2020–2022 may have additionally contributed to increased thromboembolic risk through hypercoagulable states associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection and pandemic-related changes in healthcare delivery.

Female patients undergoing cardiac surgery demonstrated a significantly higher risk of PE compared to males, a finding consistently observed across both study periods and aligning with established literature [3], [18]. This sex-based disparity is influenced by a combination of factors, including age, autoimmune conditions, hormonal therapy, peripartum status, post-surgical immobilization, trauma, elevated body mass index, and cardiac dysfunction [3]. Understanding the underlying mechanistic pathways driving these sex-based differences could inform personalized risk stratification and prophylactic strategies.

Black patients exhibited a notably higher incidence of postoperative PE compared to White patients, with the disparity widening in the 2020–2022 period. This disparity in PE risk might be influenced, in part, by variations in the prevalence of thrombophilic factors. While factor V Leiden and prothrombin mutations are more frequently observed in individuals of Caucasian descent, Black patients often exhibit elevated levels of factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, plasmin-antiplasmin complex, and D-dimer [19]. However, the underlying mechanistic pathways driving these race-specific variations in thrombotic propensity require further elucidation. Beyond these potential biological contributors, socioeconomic determinants, inequities in healthcare access and quality, and implicit biases in clinical decision-making may also play a role in the observed racial differences in post-cardiac surgery PE rates [20].

Our findings also suggest that PE is more frequently diagnosed in teaching hospitals compared to non-teaching institutions, an association that remained significant across both periods. This trend may be attributed to the influx of more complex PE cases at larger, specialized centers, which possess the necessary advanced facilities and professional expertise to manage these conditions, including the ability to perform specialized interventions such as mechanical or surgical thrombectomy. In contrast, smaller community hospitals may face limitations in terms of specialized equipment and technical expertise, which could impact their capacity to manage severe cases of PE comprehensively [14].

In addition to hospital teaching status, transfer admission emerged as an independent factor strongly associated with PE following cardiac surgery in the 2020–2022 cohort. This finding likely reflects greater illness severity and more complex comorbidities among transferred patients [21], as well as potential risks associated with the transfer process itself, including prolonged immobilization during transport [22]. Additionally, transfers may disrupt the continuity of thromboprophylaxis, with gaps in medication administration during transport further predisposing to thromboembolic events, as anticoagulation interruption is a well-recognized thrombotic risk factor [23]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare system strain may have exacerbated these risks through delayed transfers and interrupted prophylaxis protocols, particularly as thromboembolic events were already elevated in this period [24]. The association between transfer status and PE underscores the need for uninterrupted thromboprophylaxis during and after patient transfer, and supports incorporating transfer status into perioperative risk stratification models to identify high-risk individuals who may benefit from enhanced preventive measures.

Patients who developed PE experienced significantly worse outcomes across both study periods, including higher in-hospital mortality, a greater burden of preoperative comorbidities, prolonged hospitalization, and increased total costs. These findings underscore the substantial clinical and economic impact of PE following cardiac surgery. A higher comorbidity burden often reflects poorer preoperative health status, and PE may further exacerbate outcomes through compromised organ perfusion, increased risk of complications, and delayed recovery—all of which contribute to elevated mortality [25]. Beyond the immediate clinical consequences, the extended length of stay and higher resource utilization associated with PE place significant demands on healthcare systems and incur substantial economic costs [26], reinforcing the need for effective prevention and risk-stratified management strategies.

Perioperative anemia can adversely affect the multiple organ systems, including the heart, kidneys, and central nervous system [27]. In the present study, chronic blood loss anemia was independently associated with PE in both periods, underscoring the importance of preoperative hemoglobin optimization. Cancer patients, particularly those with metastatic disease or those undergoing active treatment, face a substantially elevated risk of postoperative PE [28].This association strengthened over time, likely reflecting improved detection, more aggressive cancer therapies, or cancer-related cachexia leading to weight loss—an independent factor associated with VTE that also showed an increase in 2020–2022 [29]. Both extremes of body mass index—obesity and underweight—are recognized associated factors for VTE [30]. Obesity remained a stable predictor across periods, consistent with mechanisms including venous stasis, chronic inflammation, and impaired fibrinolysis [31]. Substance abuse, particularly drug abuse, maintained a strong association with PE, consistent with literature linking chronic nitrous oxide inhalation to hyperhomocysteinemia via methionine synthase inhibition [32]. Furthermore, fluid and electrolyte disorders emerged as an increasingly important associated factor, possibly reflecting their role in hemoconcentration and endothelial dysfunction. Finally, pulmonary circulation disorders remained the strongest predictor of PE across both periods, confirming that pre-existing pulmonary vascular compromise predisposes patients to thromboembolic events and adverse outcomes [33].

Analysis stratified by time period revealed marked shifts in the association between PE and postoperative complications (Fig. 5 and Fig. S3). In the 2010–2019 cohort, PE was associated with an increased risk of acute myocardial infarction, stroke, chest pain, arrhythmias, and peripheral nerve injuries, consistent with the expected pathophysiological consequences of hemodynamic instability, systemic inflammation, and compromised organ perfusion [33], [34]; paradoxically, PE appeared protective against DVT, respiratory failure, aspiration pneumonia, and acute renal failure during this period—findings that we previously attributed to fibrinolytic activation, anticoagulant therapy, and improved supportive care [5], [35]. However, in the 2020–2022 cohort, these associations reversed dramatically: PE became strongly predictive of DVT, acute respiratory failure, aspiration pneumonia, and acute kidney injury. The reversal in DVT association is likely driven by the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 coding, which enhanced specificity for concurrent DVT and PE diagnoses, correcting previous underestimation [36]; moreover, during the COVID-19 pandemic, many PE events were triggered by SARS-CoV-2-associated coagulopathy, which simultaneously predisposes to both DVT and in-situ pulmonary thrombosis, making the two conditions naturally co-occur [37]. Concurrently, PE demonstrated paradoxical protective effects against acute myocardial infarction, chest pain, and arrhythmias in 2020–2022, which may reflect real-world clinical phenomena: once PE is diagnosed, aggressive anticoagulation could stabilize coronary plaques and reduce acute coronary events, while heightened vigilance and early intervention in diagnosed PE patients might prevent secondary cardiovascular complications. Stroke risk, however, remained elevated, consistent with paradoxical embolism pathways [30], [38]. The association with lower limb peripheral nerve injuries lost statistical significance in 2020–2022, suggesting that improved perioperative positioning and early mobilization protocols may have mitigated this complication. These temporal variations underscore the importance of considering coding practices, pandemic effects, and evolving clinical management when interpreting real-world data, and the exact mechanisms underlying these shifting associations warrant further investigation through prospective registries with standardized definitions and granular clinical data.

In addition to patient- and hospital-level characteristics, the type of cardiac surgery itself can also significantly affect the risk of postoperative PE. Patients undergoing coronary artery surgery had a consistently higher incidence of PE across both study periods compared to those undergoing valve or septal surgery. This finding is consistent with previous studies [26], [39], and the increased risk may be due to prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic occlusion time, more extensive vascular manipulation, and inadequate postoperative antithrombotic measures [4]. Eisenga et al. achieved a 0% DVT rate in 211 low-risk coronary artery bypass graft patients solely through the implementation of an active, early initiated drug plus mechanical prophylaxis regimen [40]. In summary, existing evidence argues against a “one size fits all” anticoagulation strategy and advocates stratifying according to the surgical procedure: if there are no obvious contraindications for bleeding in patients undergoing coronary artery surgery, early or prolonged prevention of drug-induced thrombosis should be considered [41].

Across both study periods, approximately 5.5% of patients with postoperative PE received invasive treatment (systemic thrombolysis or catheter-directed therapy), a proportion consistent with real-world registry data [42]. This finding reflects clinical selection bias rather than a causal relationship: patients with more extensive thrombosis, hemodynamic compromise, or higher comorbidity burden are preferentially referred for intervention [43], as evidenced by the higher rates of transfer admission and comorbid conditions in this group. Despite advances in catheter-based techniques and guideline recommendations favoring catheter-directed therapy for intermediate-risk PE [42], the overall utilization of invasive strategies did not increase over time, possibly due to persistent uncertainty in patient selection, institutional resource variability, or pandemic-related disruptions [44]. Regional differences in treatment choice further highlight the influence of local practice patterns and the availability of specialized expertise. These observations underscore the need for multidisciplinary pulmonary embolism response teams to standardize decision-making and for future research to identify specific clinical and imaging factors that can better guide patient selection for different treatment modalities [42].

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the use of ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes to identify PE and comorbidities may introduce misclassification bias, as administrative data lack granular clinical details and diagnostic accuracy may vary across the coding transition period. Although we applied the AHRQ Patient Safety Indicator #12 methodology to distinguish hospital-acquired events from conditions present on admission, the absence of a present-on-admission flag for all variables may have led to residual confounding. Second, the NIS database captures only in-hospital events, potentially underestimating the true incidence of PE by missing cases diagnosed after discharge. Third, we lacked data on pharmacological or mechanical thromboprophylaxis, precluding assessment of whether post-operative PE represented prophylaxis failure or de novo events. Fourth, the absence of precise temporal data prevented us from differentiating early versus late PE or distinguishing catheter-related thrombosis from classical DVT with embolization. Fifth, long-term outcomes beyond hospital discharge were unavailable, limiting evaluation of post-discharge morbidity and mortality. Sixth, our unweighted analysis, while appropriate for examining biological associations within this large cohort, may not yield nationally representative estimates. Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2022) introduced unique confounders related to SARS-CoV-2-associated coagulopathy and healthcare delivery disruptions, which we could not fully disentangle from secular trends. These limitations underscore the need for prospective registries integrating real-time operative data, standardized radiologic confirmation, and structured longitudinal follow-up to better elucidate the chronology, mechanisms, and long-term consequences of post-cardiac surgery PE.

Conclusion

This nationwide analysis of patients undergoing cardiac surgery between 2010 and 2022 demonstrates an increase in the incidence of postoperative PE, from 0.6% in 2010 to 3.2% in 2022. Factors independently associated with higher PE risk included female sex, Black race, higher comorbidity burden (≥3 conditions), Medicaid insurance, admission to large or teaching hospitals, prolonged hospitalization (> 9 days), transfer from another hospital, and coronary artery surgery. Patients who developed PE experienced higher in-hospital mortality, longer hospital stays, and increased total charges. These findings may inform risk stratification and guide perioperative management to reduce the burden of this complication.

The data contained within these data sets are neither identifiable nor private and thus do not meet the federal definition of “human subject” as defined in 45 CFR 46.102.Therefore, these research projects do not need to be reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Statement of ethics

This observational study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board of DongGuan SongShan Lake Tungwah Hospital, which waived the need for consent because it used deidentified publicly available data. Review and/or approval by an ethics committee was not needed for this study because the data used in this study came from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample database, which was consistently recognized as the largest public database in the nation. This study used de-identified public data and was therefore considered exempt and did not require IRB approval or informed consent.
