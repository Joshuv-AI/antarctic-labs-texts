Serial Kidney Biopsies Reveal Progressive Pathology in Townes-Brocks Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Susumu Tsunoda, Shigekazu Kurihara, Toshiharu Ueno, Yuki Oba, Akinari Sekine, Hiroki Mizuno, Masayuki Yamanouchi, Eiko Hasegawa, Tatsuya Suwabe, Kei Kono, Kenichi Ohashi, Yutaka Yamaguchi, Naoya Morisada, Kandai Nozu, Yoshifumi Ubara, Takehiko Wada, Naoki Sawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTownes–Brocks syndrome (TBS) is an autosomal dominant hereditary disorder caused by SALL1 variants, characterized by auricular malformations, anal anomalies, and limb deformities, often accompanied by kidney dysfunction. We describe a case of TBS, which was suspected after birth, in a 27-year-old man presenting with progressive kidney dysfunction and obesity. At age 16, school urinalysis revealed proteinuria and impaired kidney function. A kidney biopsy at that time showed a perihilar variant focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) lesion, and the patient was treated with angiotensin II receptor blocker and sodium/glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor. Kidney function gradually declined, prompting a second biopsy at age 27, which revealed progressive FSGS lesions and interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic SALL1 variant, confirming the diagnosis. This case demonstrates the mechanistic link between SALL1 variants, congenital renal hypoplasia, impaired podocyte repair, and obesity-related hyperfiltration, culminating in rapid development of FSGS. Longitudinal histopathological analysis through repeated biopsy was essential for understanding this progression. The integration of sequential renal biopsies and genetic analysis provided unique pathophysiological insight and diagnostic clarity in TBS-associated kidney disease. In patients with TBS and congenital nephron reduction, weight control is particularly important to mitigate obesity-related hyperfiltration injury.Index Words: Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, longitudinal assessment, SALL1, obesity, podocyte injury, repeat kidney biopsy, Townes-Brocks syndrome

Introduction

Townes–Brocks syndrome (TBS) is an autosomal dominant hereditary disease caused by variants in SALL1, a gene critical for embryonic development.1 TBS is classically characterized by auricular malformations, anal anomalies, and limb deformities, with kidney dysfunction being increasingly recognized as part of its phenotype.2,3

Kidney abnormalities in TBS include fusion anomalies (most commonly horseshoe kidney), positional anomalies, malrotation, agenesis or hypoplasia, and polycystic kidney disease.4 Notably, kidney dysfunction may occur even in the absence of overt structural anomalies.2,3 Experimental studies in mice have demonstrated that deletion of SALL1 in interstitial progenitor cells results in widespread nephron dilation and premature death.5 Although nephron dilation is not prominent in neonatal Sall1 mutant mice, it progresses with age, suggesting a chronic disease process.5

Although previous reports have described kidney biopsies in TBS,6 to our knowledge, this is the first report documenting 2 sequential kidney biopsies in a single patient with progressive kidney dysfunction due to TBS. This longitudinal histopathologic documentation provides unique insights into the progression of kidney involvement in this rare genetic disorder.

Case Report

Clinical Course Leading to the First Biopsy

A 27-year-old man was suspected of having TBS at birth based on the presence of auricular malformations, a low-positioned imperforate anus, and deformities of the fingers and toes, thereby fulfilling all 3 major clinical criteria. No structural kidney abnormalities were detected at the time. During elementary and middle school, school-based urinalysis repeatedly showed transient proteinuria, although confirmatory testing always yielded normal results. He was therefore managed conservatively. At age 16, persistent proteinuria was detected on routine screening, prompting referral to our hospital for kidney evaluation. On presentation, the patient’s height was 163.0 cm, weight was 72.0 kg, and body mass index was 27.09 kg/m2. The right thumb had 3 phalanges (triphalangeal thumb), and the fourth and fifth metacarpal bones were fused (Fig 1A). The fifth toe overlapped with the fourth toe, and the fourth and fifth metatarsal bones were fused (Fig 1B). On computed tomography, the right kidney measured 98 mm and the left kidney 82 mm, suggesting bilateral renal hypoplasia relative to body size (Fig 1C). The patient also had sensorineural hearing loss and underwent cochlear implantation (Fig 1D). Laboratory findings at first biopsy are summarized in Table 1. Notably, his urinary protein-creatinine ratio was 0.41 g/g creatinine, serum creatinine level was 1.13 mg/dL (estimated glomerular filtration rate, 109.7 mL/min/1.73 m2), and 24-hour protein excretion was 0.39 g/d. Abdominal ultrasonography at that time did not reveal fatty liver. At that time, we discussed the option of genetic testing for a suspected syndromic condition with the patient and his parents. However, the family expressed considerable anxiety about what a definitive genetic diagnosis might mean for his future life course, including whether he would be able to reach adulthood and live independently, and they ultimately declined genetic testing after counseling. The first kidney biopsy was performed to determine whether a treatable glomerular disease was present rather than the congenital anomaly itself.

Pathological Findings of the First Biopsy

Four core samples were obtained from the right kidney, yielding a cortex-to-medulla ratio of 8:2. Of the 20 sampled glomeruli, 2 exhibited global sclerosis, and 1 showed a perihilar variant focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) lesion (Fig 2A-C). Mild proximal tubular epithelial degeneration was observed. There was no significant mesangial proliferation. Immunofluorescence revealed mild (1+) granular mesangial deposits of IgA, IgM, and C3, findings considered nonspecific. Electron microscopy showed segmental deposition of ill-defined electron-dense materials in the mesangium, without well-defined deposits. No podocyte vacuolization or hyaline droplet degeneration was seen. Endothelial swelling and focal subendothelial edema were noted (Fig 2D).

Clinical Course Leading to the Second Biopsy

The patient was initiated on losartan (25 mg) once daily, and despite treatment, proteinuria gradually increased to >3.0 g/g creatinine by age 24, along with a progressive decline in kidney function. Empagliflozin (10 mg) was initiated at 26 years of age, but proteinuria did not improve. A second kidney biopsy was performed at age 27, not only to reassess the underlying pathology and the extent of chronic lesions but also to exclude a superimposed glomerular disease that might alter clinical management.

At this time, physical findings included height 165.1 cm, weight 89.8 kg, and body mass index 32.94 kg/m2. The laboratory findings at second biopsy are summarized in Table1; notably, his urinary protein-creatinine ratio was 1.37 g/g creatinine, serum creatinine level was 1.78 mg/dL (estimated glomerular filtration rate, 40.1 mL/min/1.73 m2), and 24-hour protein excretion was 1.92 g/d. Abdominal ultrasonography at this time demonstrated hepatic steatosis.

Pathological Findings of the Second Biopsy

Of the 20 glomeruli examined, 4 exhibited global sclerosis, and 3 showed FSGS lesions (2 perihilar variant and 1 not otherwise specified) (Fig 2E-G). There was clear progression of glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy. Microaneurysm formation and hyalinosis were observed. Immunofluorescence findings were unchanged from the previous examination. Electron microscopy again revealed segmental deposition of smudgy, high-density material in the mesangium. No distinct electron-dense deposits, podocyte vacuolization, or hyaline droplets were observed. Subendothelial edema and mesangial matrix dissolution were evident (Fig 2H).

Clinical Course Following the Second Biopsy and Genetic Confirmation

By the time of the second biopsy at age 27, the patient was an adult and living independently, and a long-term, trusting relationship had been established with the treating physicians. After renewed counseling, he and his mother agreed to proceed with genetic analysis. Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous frameshift variant in the SALL1 gene (NM_002968.3:c.845del, p.Leu282Cysfs∗4), which confirmed a diagnosis of TBS. This single-base deletion in exon 2, a recognized hotspot for pathogenic truncating variants in TBS, has not been previously reported in ClinVar or other databases. Neither parent carried this variant, supporting the presence of a de novo mutation (Fig 1E). Figure 1F shows the family pedigree informed by genetic analysis. After genetic confirmation, we performed a targeted re-evaluation for subtle or atypical extrarenal manifestations. However, no additional abnormalities beyond those initially documented were identified. The patient is currently managed with an angiotensin II receptor blocker and sodium/glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor and remains under outpatient follow-up. Over the following year, his body weight decreased from 89.8 kg to 74.0 kg, his urinary protein-creatinine ratio was 1.86 g/gCr, serum creatinine level was 1.65 mg/dL, and estimated glomerular filtration rate was 43.1 mL/min/1.73 m2, indicating stable kidney function during this period.

Discussion

This case involved a patient who met the clinical triad of TBS at birth based on distinctive physical findings. Two sequential renal biopsies, performed over the clinical course to investigate progressive kidney dysfunction and persistent proteinuria, provided an exceptional opportunity for direct longitudinal histopathologic assessment. Genetic analysis of SALL1 ultimately confirmed the diagnosis. We propose that congenital renal hypoplasia and impaired podocyte repair due to the SALL1 variant established a predisposition to progressive kidney dysfunction, while additional obesity-related hyperfiltration stress accelerated the onset of FSGS lesions at age 27. This mechanistic link is supported by recent genotype–phenotype studies that demonstrate that truncating variants in SALL1 frequently result in severe kidney phenotypes including hypoplasia and early-onset chronic kidney disease with variable extrarenal features.3,6,7

Kidney manifestations of TBS include structural anomalies such as malrotation and positional abnormalities, which may contribute to progressive kidney dysfunction.2,4 The molecular pathogenesis of TBS-associated kidney abnormalities can be appreciated by understanding the role of SALL1 in kidney development. Balanced self-renewal and differentiation of nephron progenitor cells are essential for normal nephrogenesis.5 This process is tightly regulated by the transcription factor Six2, which antagonizes differentiation mediated by Wnt signaling.5,8,9 The nuclear factor SALL1 is expressed in both Six2-positive progenitor cells and differentiating nascent nephrons, playing a crucial role in kidney development.5,8,9 In mice, the loss of Sall1 in Six2-positive nephron progenitor cells causes severe progenitor cell depletion and apoptosis in differentiating nephrons.5 Loss of SALL1 leads to a reduction in the absolute number of nephrons, resulting in a decreased filtration surface area, which may contribute to kidney function decline.5 In our case, the right kidney measured 98 mm and the left kidney 82 mm in length at first biopsy, suggesting kidney hypoplasia relative to body size. Additionally, histopathologic examination revealed sparse glomerular density for age, consistent with reduced nephron number and diminished kidney reserve capacity. At the protein level, the c.845del change causes a frameshift at codon 282 and introduces a premature termination codon 4 amino acids downstream, predicting a markedly truncated SALL1 protein consistent with a loss-of-function effect. These findings align with the proposed pathophysiologic mechanism of SALL1 dysfunction leading to nephron deficiency.

The performance of 2 sequential renal biopsies in this case was instrumental in elucidating the mechanisms underlying progressive kidney dysfunction, permitting direct observation of histopathologic evolution over time. Repeat or longitudinal renal biopsies have demonstrated utility in assessing temporal changes, identifying transitions in pathological findings, and guiding patient management in various kidney diseases.10, 11, 12

The primary cause of podocyte injury in our case remains unclear. However, the patient’s elevated body mass index of 32.94 kg/m2 may have exacerbated hyperfiltration stress on the congenitally reduced number of glomeruli, thereby contributing to kidney injury.13,14 Under normal conditions, SALL1 is minimally expressed in mature glomeruli.15 However, it has been reported that SALL1 is re-expressed in glomeruli where podocytes are injured.15 In previous studies, the SALL1 gene response to adriamycin-induced podocyte injury was analyzed.15 Injured podocytes showed significant SALL1 gene upregulation, increased synaptopodin expression, and actin cytoskeleton reorganization, resulting in podocyte recovery.15 Therefore, the SALL1 gene may play a protective role in podocytes. Failure of SALL1 to participate in the repair process may have contributed to FSGS changes. We propose the following hypothesis for FSGS pathogenesis in this patient: kidney hypoplasia caused by the SALL1 gene variant resulted in a reduced filtration surface area, hyperfiltration related to obesity caused podocyte stress, and an impaired repair mechanism (due to the SALL1 variant) led to incomplete recovery and ultimately FSGS lesions.16,17 The observation that the FSGS lesions were of the perihilar variant and were accompanied by insudative changes lends supportive evidence to this hypothesis. This case demonstrates that 2 sequential renal biopsies, integrated with genetic analysis, are pivotal for understanding the mechanisms of progressive kidney dysfunction in TBS. Longitudinal evaluation revealed that congenital renal hypoplasia combined with impaired podocyte repair mechanisms due to SALL1 variants, especially in the context of obesity-related hyperfiltration, accelerates FSGS. Such repeated biopsies may improve disease monitoring and clinical management in rare genetic kidney disorders. Further accumulation of cases will be essential to clarify the full spectrum and renal prognosis of TBS.
