Unilateral blindness and complete ophthalmoplegia following middle cerebral artery aneurysm clipping: a case report
Authors: Farshad Afshar, Alireza Ramezani Majd, Mohsen Pourazizi, Ali Aghajani, Ahmadreza Rafiei, Paria Sanei, Mohammad Sadegh Khalilian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMiddle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms rarely cause ophthalmic complications due to their anatomical distance from ocular structures. We report an exceedingly rare case of acute unilateral blindness and complete ophthalmoplegia following MCA aneurysm clipping via a pterional/trans-sylvian approach, a complication not previously documented in the literature with concurrent optical coherence tomography (OCT) findings.Case presentationA 49-year-old man with a history of hypertension presented with a severe headache. Computed tomography (CT) and angiography confirmed a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) secondary to a ruptured complex aneurysm of the left MCA. Preoperative MRI also revealed significant ipsilateral sinusitis. He underwent an emergent left pterional craniotomy with a trans-sylvian approach; proximal control was achieved on the M1 segment, and the aneurysm was clipped. Notably, no dissection or manipulation of the internal carotid artery (ICA) or opticocarotid cistern was performed. Twenty-four hours postoperatively, he developed no light perception (NLP) vision, complete ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, chemosis, and reduced corneal sensation in the left eye. Fundus examination revealed diffuse retinal whitening without a cherry-red spot, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed inner retinal edema, suggestive of probable ophthalmic artery occlusion (OAO) or orbital infarction syndrome, inconsistent with direct MCA aneurysm effects. The right eye was normal. MRI/MRA and fluorescein angiography were not feasible due to his unstable condition. Three days later, rehemorrhage prompted reoperation, but he suffered cardiopulmonary arrest and died.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported case of OCT-documented inner retinal ischemia and complete ophthalmoplegia following a pterional/trans-sylvian clipping of an MCA aneurysm. It highlights the risk of rare, indirect ischemic injury to the orbital apex, potentially via retrograde microembolic or hemodynamic mechanisms, emphasizing the need for multidisciplinary awareness even in surgeries remote from the optic apparatus.

Introduction

Middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms, comprising 20–30% of intracranial aneurysms, are a major cause of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), with a rupture risk of 1–2% annually [1]. Surgical clipping remains a cornerstone treatment for complex or ruptured MCA aneurysms. While endovascular treatment (EVT) techniques, including coiling, flow diversion, and stent-assisted coiling, have emerged as alternatives for select cases, the wide neck and incorporation of branch vessels often make microsurgical clipping the preferred option for definitive repair [2, 3]. Ophthalmic complications, such as acute visual loss and ophthalmoplegia, are exceptionally rare after MCA aneurysm surgery due to the significant anatomical distance from the optic apparatus and orbital apex, in contrast to ophthalmic segment or paraclinoid internal carotid artery (ICA) aneurysms, which carry a 10.6–16.1% risk of visual deficits post-clipping [4]. This anatomical distinction underscores the singular nature of the complication presented in this case report.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old male with hypertension, managed on antihypertensive medications, presented with several weeks of persistent headache that acutely intensified. He reported no prior visual symptoms or neurological deficits. Clinical examination revealed neck stiffness without fever or severe proptosis. Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography demonstrated extensive subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) in the basal cisterns and left sylvian fissure. Cerebral angiography confirmed a complex left MCA aneurysm at the bifurcation and the CTA images also demonstrate the normal anatomical course of the right ophthalmic artery (originating from the ophthalmic segment of the ICA).

Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the aneurysm and additionally revealed evidence of significant ipsilateral sinusitis (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). He was classified as Hunt-Hess grade II, and required emergent microsurgical intervention.

The patient underwent a standard left pterional craniotomy. A trans-sylvian approach was utilized to access the MCA bifurcation. Proximal control was achieved on the M1 segment of the MCA. The complex aneurysm was meticulously dissected and clipped successfully without the need for anterior clinoidectomy, temporary clipping, or any dissection in the region of the proximal internal carotid artery (ICA) or opticocarotid cistern. Hemodynamics remained stable (systolic blood pressure 100–120 mmHg). A postoperative CT scan confirmed the position of the aneurysm clips (Fig. 4).

Twenty-four hours postoperatively, the patient reported complete left eye vision loss. Urgent ophthalmologic consultation was requested. Visual acuity was no light perception (NLP) in the left eye and 20/20 in the right. Pupillary examination revealed a 5-mm left pupil and a 3-mm right pupil in ambient light, with a 4 + relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) on the left. Ocular motility assessment demonstrated complete left ophthalmoplegia in all gazes (Fig. 5).

Severe ptosis was noted, with a margin reflex distance 1 (MRD1) of 1 mm on the left and 4 mm on the right. Corneal sensation was reduced in the left eye, consistent with possible V1 involvement, while intact in the right eye. Anterior segment examination revealed mild left upper eyelid edema, conjunctival chemosis, and hyperemia.

Fundus examination via direct ophthalmoscopy disclosed diffuse retinal whitening in the left eye, predominantly in the posterior pole, without a cherry-red spot, disc edema, pallor, or vascular attenuation. The right eye fundus was normal.

Macular optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated qualitative edema and increased thickness in the inner retinal layers (ganglion cell and inner plexiform layers), sparing outer layers, suggestive of acute ischemia (Fig. 6). Magnetic resonance imaging/angiography (MRI/MRA) and fluorescein angiography (FA) were not performed due to the patient’s unstable condition.

Three days later, the patient’s consciousness deteriorated. Repeat CT revealed re-hemorrhage with midline shift. Urgent reoperation for hematoma evacuation was performed, but he suffered intraoperative cardiopulmonary arrest and expired (Figs. 7 and 8).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of complete orbital apex syndrome (combined NLP vision, complete ophthalmoplegia, and cranial nerve V1 involvement) with objective OCT evidence of inner retinal ischemia following pterional/trans-sylvian clipping of an MCA aneurysm. This presentation is anatomically paradoxical, as the surgical field for an MCA aneurysm is remote from the orbital apex, optic nerve, and the origin of the ophthalmic artery [5].

The delayed onset of symptoms (24 h post-surgery) suggests an ischemic event in the territory of the ophthalmic artery and the orbital apex. The most plausible mechanism for this rare complication is an indirect, hemodynamic one. We hypothesize that manipulation of the M1 segment during clipping, in a susceptible individual, may have led to retrograde microembolization or a significant hemodynamic shift.

This could potentially redirect microemboli or low-flow states from the MCA circulation toward the ophthalmic artery via existing collateral networks, such as leptomeningeal anastomoses or flow through the posterior communicating artery complex [6, 7]. This mechanism has been suggested in cases of visual loss following endovascular treatment of more proximal ICA aneurysms, but its occurrence from a purely MCA approach is exceptionally rare [8].

The preoperative finding of significant ipsilateral sinusitis may have served as a contributing anatomical factor. Sinus inflammation can lead to a hyperemic state and increased venous pressure in the orbital region, potentially reducing the vascular reserve and making the orbital contents more vulnerable to ischemic insult from even minor changes in arterial inflow or microembolic events [9].

Alternative diagnoses such as direct surgical trauma, central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO), or cavernous sinus thrombosis are less consistent with the clinical picture. The absence of intraoperative manipulation near the optic nerve or ICA, the lack of a cherry-red spot (common in CRAO), and the absence of fever or severe pain make these diagnoses unlikely [10]. The presented case most closely aligns with an orbital infarction syndrome (OIS) secondary to ophthalmic artery compromise (Table 1).

The principal limitation of this report is the lack of confirmatory vascular imaging (MRA, CTA) in the immediate postoperative period to visualize the ophthalmic artery patency, which was precluded by the patient’s critical and unstable condition. Furthermore, epidemiological data on ophthalmic complications specific to MCA surgery are scarce, as literature focuses on paraclinoid aneurysms.

This case underscores a rare but severe potential complication of MCA aneurysm surgery. It highlights the necessity for heightened clinical vigilance among both neurosurgeons and ophthalmologists. Any new visual or ocular motor deficit after intracranial surgery, even when performed at a distance from the optic apparatus, should prompt immediate multidisciplinary evaluation to consider rare ischemic mechanisms.

Conclusion

This rare case of unilateral blindness and complete ophthalmoplegia following MCA aneurysm clipping via a pterional/trans-sylvian approach highlights the risk of indirect, ischemic injury to the orbital apex, potentially mediated by remote hemodynamic mechanisms. While limited by the absence of postoperative vascular imaging, the clinical and OCT findings provide a compelling description of this unique complication. This report emphasizes the need for awareness of this rare clinical scenario to ensure prompt recognition and management.
