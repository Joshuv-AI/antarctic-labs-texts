Uterine squamous cell carcinoma in a 10-year-old Vietnamese pot-bellied pig (Sus scrofa domesticus)
Authors: Le-lani Janse van Rensburg, Whatmore Munetsi Tagwireyi, Rungano Magadu, Miemie Grobler, Lida Avenant, Geoff Brown, Nicolene Hoepner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 10-year-old intact female Vietnamese Pot-bellied pet pig (Sus scrofa domesticus) presented to the Production Animal Clinic at the Onderstepoort Veterinary Academic Hospital in South Africa (OVAH) with recurrent vaginal bleeding as the primary complaint. Clinical examination, vaginoscopy with vaginal cytology and abdominal ultrasound localised the haemorrhage to the reproductive tract and initially revealed a pyometra. After the failure of medical treatment with a luteolytic agent, anti-inflammatory and antibiotics, an exploratory laparotomy was performed, during which two macroscopical uterine lesions were observed. Histopathology identified the lesions as a squamous cell carcinoma infiltrating the endo- and myometrium and a focal endometrial polyp. An ovariohysterectomy resulted in a full recovery. No evidence of pulmonary metastases was found.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05412-z.

Introduction

In South Africa, pigs form mainly part of the commercial farming industry as pork is the third most consumed meat in the country [1]. However, in recent years pigs kept as pets became more popular [2]. From a South African point of view, the most common types of pet pigs are ‘rescues’ or miniature pigs such as the litter runts, followed by Vietnamese pot-bellied pigs and KuneKune pigs [2]. The average life span of a healthy pot-bellied pig has been estimated to be 15 to 20 years of age [3]. Many pet pigs can reach such advanced ages due to owners considering them as part of their households and seeking veterinary care when needed [4, 5]. This makes them more prone to age-related diseases such as neoplasia when compared to commercial pigs that get slaughtered typically before 6 months old [6]. Neoplasia found in slaughtered pigs is mostly incidental and rarely produces any clinical signs [7]. This is especially true for neoplasia of the reproductive tract, as the pigs get slaughtered before they reach sexual maturity [7, 8].

In a retrospective study performed by Newman and Rohrbach [5] about the incidence and type of neoplasms found in pot-bellied pigs, specifically, neoplasia of the reproductive and gastrointestinal tract, and hepatobiliary system were the most common, with the latter having the greatest tendency to metastasise. However, in studies encompassing the entirety of the porcine species as their study population, neoplasia of lymphoid tissue and melanoma are regarded as the most common [9–11]. It has been demonstrated that old age is a great risk factor for developing uterine pathology in female miniature pet pigs [12]. Multiple studies have suggested that it is due to the reproductive hormonal stimulation that the intact female pig is repeatedly subjected to during her oestrus cycle every 21 days, especially when not being bred [4, 12–16]. This has been extrapolated from what is known in other species however, there is a lack of comprehensive research regarding the exact role sex hormones play in the development of reproductive tract neoplasia in pigs specifically [4].

Uterine or ovarian smooth muscle tumours such as leiomyomas and leiomyosarcomas are the most common reproductive tract neoplasia in pot-bellied pigs and are typically benign or locally infiltrating [4, 5, 11, 15–18]. However, one leiomyosarcoma was found to metastasize to the abdominal mesentery [5]. Other pig uterine neoplasia described in the literature include, fibromas, fibroleiomyomas [16, 19], adenomas [12], cyst-adenomas [19], adenomyomas [8], adenocarcinomas [4, 12–15, 20–23], squamous cell carcinomas [24, 25], and a choriocarcinoma-like tumour [26]. The adenocarcinoma and carcinoma-type tumours tend to be more malignant and can spread to distant sites [13, 14, 22–24, 26].

Pot-bellied pigs with reproductive neoplasia may exhibit individual or a combination of clinical symptoms, of which the most consistent is a haemorrhagic vaginal discharge or abdominal distention. Other signs are mostly nonspecific and can include behavioural changes, anorexia, weight loss, tenesmus, and even lameness [4, 13–16, 18, 20, 22, 26]. Pigs presenting without a vaginal discharge or abdominal enlargement may delay owners in recognising clinical signs and seeking veterinary care [20]. It also poses a challenge to veterinarians to localise the affected organ system to reach a timely and accurate diagnosis [20].

Only a few case reports regarding neoplasia, its clinical presentation, diagnostics, and treatment in pet pigs have been published, but it is slowly on the rise as the geriatric pet pig population grows. Therefore, in this case report, we aim to describe the occurrence of a squamous cell carcinoma in the uterus of a pot-bellied pig. According to the author’s knowledge, this would be the first case described in the literature. We also illustrate the diagnostic modalities and treatment options that led to the patient’s full recovery, which reinforces what has been suggested by previous studies to be successful.

Case presentation

History and presenting signs

A 10-year-old female intact Vietnamese Pot-bellied pig (Sus scrofa domesticus) presented to the Production Animal Clinic at the OVAH (University of Pretoria, South Africa) for recurrent vaginal bleeding of unknown origin and duration. No other clinical abnormalities were detected during the physical examination, and she was otherwise bright, alert, and responsive. The pig has no remarkable medical history prior to this presentation, was kept mostly indoors as a pet, and has not been bred before. A pyometra was suspected based on the diagnostics performed. Non-invasive treatment was preferred over surgical intervention however, failure of conservative treatment led to an exploratory laparotomy and subsequent ovariohysterectomy.

Diagnostic investigations

The haemorrhagic discharge from the vulva was confirmed to originate from the reproductive tract via a vaginal speculum examination upon arrival. Abdominal radiographs yielded unsatisfactory results due to superimposition of the gastrointestinal content and overlapping views because of large patient size. However, abdominal ultrasound revealed a distended uterus containing fluid with heterogenous echogenicity suspected to be haemorrhage or inflammatory exudate (Fig. 1) and a suspected persistent corpus luteum. The vaginal discharge was collected for cytological examination by the Clinical Pathology Laboratory of the Onderstepoort Veterinary Academic Hospital and bacteriological culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing by the Bacteriology Laboratory of the Department of Veterinary Tropical Diseases at Onderstepoort, University of Pretoria. Cytology revealed moderate macrophages (some exhibiting erythrophagocytosis and haemosiderin) and abundant non-degenerate neutrophils, consistent with inflammation. Vaginal epithelial cells were within normal limits, comprising a mixed population of parabasal, intermediate, and superficial cells. Few, atypical epithelial cells were observed, characterized by increased size, high nucleocytoplasmic ratio, and moderate basophilic cytoplasm. These could represent dysplasia in response to inflammation, or neoplasia and warranted further investigation. Bacteriological results were only received a week after the initial presentation. Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus was cultured and resistant to penicillin and polymyxin antibiotics. Prior to surgery, haematology and a basic serum chemistry profile were performed. A mild normocytic normochromic regenerative anaemia (haematocrit: 0.29 l/l, reference range: 0.32–0.5 l/l) with a mild to moderate leukopaenia (white cell count: 7.66 × 109/l, reference range: 11–22 × 109/l) was observed, which can be attributed to the ongoing vaginal haemorrhage and inflammatory leukocyte consumption, respectively. Differential diagnosis

The history, clinical signs and non-invasive diagnostic modalities were suggestive of a pyometra suspected to be secondary to a persistent corpus luteum. Endometritis and metritis were considered less likely because the pig had never been bred before. However, due to the haemorrhagic nature of the vaginal discharge and the presence of atypical epithelial cells on cytology, neoplasia was still regarded as a possibility. In all cases, surgical treatment would result in permanent resolution and future prevention and was thus advised to the owners.

Treatment

Conservative treatment was first attempted to resolve the persistent corpus luteum most likely responsible for the pyometra by the off-label and once off administration of 125 μg cloprostenol (Estrumate®; MSD Animal Health) intramuscularly. Response to conservative treatment would also assist in narrowing down a diagnosis as well as provide motivation for the owners to opt for surgical intervention. Additionally, 1 ml/20 kg amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (Synulox RTU®; Zoetis) OID and 0.4 mg/kg meloxicam (Metacam® 20 mg/ml; Boehringer Ingelheim) OID were administered intramuscularly to treat the infection and inflammation for 5 days and 3 days, respectively. After receiving the antimicrobial susceptibility results, the antibiotic was changed to 5 mg/kg subcutaneous enrofloxacin (Baytril 100®, Bayer) OID for 3 days. A limited response to conservative treatment was observed, and thus, the owners agreed to an exploratory laparotomy and subsequent ovariohysterectomy under general anaesthesia as it was the best available treatment to resolve the vaginal bleeding.

General anaesthesia

The premedication protocol included a combination of midazolam at 0.3 mg/kg (Dormicum®; Roche) and ketamine (Anaket-V®, Bayer) at 5 mg/kg via intramuscular injection. This was followed by endotracheal intubation for maintenance of anaesthesia on isoflurane (Isofor®; Safeline Pharmaceuticals) using a circle anaesthetic circuit. Intra-operatively, morphine (Morphine Sulphate; Pharma-Q) at 0.1 mg/kg intramuscularly and meloxicam (Metacam® 20 mg/ml; Boehringer Ingelheim) at 0.4 mg/kg subcutaneously were used to provide analgesia, while enrofloxacin (Baytril 100®, Bayer) at 5 mg/kg intramuscularly was continued for antibiosis.

Surgical approach

A routine ventral midline approach was followed for the exploratory laparotomy. No other abnormalities were detected within the abdominal cavity except for the reproductive tract, which had significant gross pathology upon exteriorisation. As a result, an ovariohysterectomy was performed. Samples of the ovaries, oviducts, uterine horns, uterine body, and gross abnormalities were collected for histopathology in 10% buffered formalin. The surgical approach of the ovariohysterectomy was identical to that of a bitch, followed by the routine three-layer closure of the abdomen. A stent bandage was placed over the incision site after application of a spray-on dressing (Opsite Spray®; Smith + Nephew). The stent bandage was further covered and kept in place by a hessian sack to protect it from dirt.

Outcome and follow-up

The pig made a full recovery after surgery. However, infrequent defecation was observed, for which 19.8 g of lactulose (Laxette®; Cipla) was initially administered per os OID as a loading dose, thereafter 13.2 g was administered as required to prevent post-operative constipation. She was still monitored post-operatively for 6 days in hospital whereafter she was discharged whilst awaiting the histopathology results. The same pre-operative treatment was continued as oral medication for ease of administration and cost-effectiveness: 0.08 mg/kg meloxicam (Petcam®; CiplaVet) OID for 11 days and 8 mg/kg ciprofloxacin (Ciprogen®; Mylan) BID for 7 days. The starvation prior to surgery and the continued use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs also prompted the administration of omeprazole (Losec MUPS®, AstraZeneca) at 40 mg/pig/day per os for the prevention of gastric ulceration. On her follow-up visit 31 days after discharge, the incision site was completely healed. A year after her surgery and at the time of writing this report, the owner still states that she is healthy with no other post-operative complications observed since.

Macroscopical pathology and histopathology

The samples collected in 10% buffered formalin from the ovaries, oviducts, uterine horns, body, and lesions were histologically evaluated by the Pathology Section of the Department of Paraclinical Sciences at Onderstepoort, University of Pretoria. Histopathology of the multinodular uterine sample (Fig. 2, white arrow) revealed an infiltrative, expansile squamous neoplasm forming islands, trabeculae, and cords throughout the endometrium and myometrium, accompanied by a marked desmoplastic response. Neoplastic cells exhibited pronounced pleomorphism (marked anisonucleosis, karyomegaly, occasional multinucleation, dyskeratosis) and a high mitotic index (29/10 HPF). Central necrosis, keratinization, and mixed inflammatory infiltrates were common. Adjacent lymphatics were markedly dilated; no definitive neoplastic emboli were identified. Findings are consistent with squamous cell carcinoma. The remaining uterine sample (Fig. 2, blue arrow) showed no neoplastic infiltration but demonstrated marked lymphatic dilation, severe endometrial oedema, and, in one case, focal polypoid endometrial hyperplasia This was histologically identified as a lymphatic cyst associated with a focal endometrial polyp that most likely caused obstruction and subsequent dilation of a lymphatic vessel ). Assessment of metastases

After the diagnosis of uterine squamous cell carcinoma, a right lateral thoracic radiograph was taken under sedation to examine the lungs for any evidence of metastases. Only one thoracic view could be taken that was of poor diagnostic quality due to challenges regarding the large size of the patient and the sedation wearing off which also resulted in severe motion blur artefact. Nonetheless, no overt pulmonary nodular lesions were observed on this view. However, spondylosis of the thoracic vertebrae was noticed, but was considered as an incidental finding.

Discussion

According to our knowledge, this is the first case report in the literature of squamous cell carcinoma in the uterus of a Vietnamese Pot-bellied pig. The histological findings of only two other cases closely resemble ours. The first is a case of uterine carcinoma with only small, focal areas of squamous epithelial cell differentiation [20]. The second involves a recent publication of primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma and suspected primary uterine carcinoma [24]. Both cases also exhibit other striking similarities to our patient in terms of signalment, recurrent vaginal bleeding as the only clinical sign, and pyometra. Previously, squamous cell carcinoma in pot-bellied pigs was only present in the oral and nasal cavities, and skin [5, 27–29]. The only reported cases of uterine squamous cell carcinoma in animals were in a uterine remnant of a spayed cat [30] and a rat undergoing experimental testing of toxins, namely dioxin [31].

Vulvovaginal squamous cell carcinoma is common in domestic animal species. However, cervical squamous cell carcinoma has only sporadically been described in cattle [8], sheep [32], goats [11], hamsters [33] and pot-bellied pigs [24]. No local extension of the cervical squamous cell carcinoma to the uterus was observed in the animal cases. Nevertheless, it has been seen in humans where it can either locally extend into the myometrium, like in our case, or rarely replace the endometrium alone [34, 35]. In our case, no macroscopical cervical, or vaginal lesions for that matter, were observed during the vaginal examination and exploratory laparotomy, but both were never investigated histologically. In a human case report by Olivier et al. [35], PSCCE was suspected after all diagnostic modalities revealed a normal cervix, but histological examination discovered a cervical squamous cell carcinoma that spread locally to the uterus. Additionally, no gross lesions or associated clinical signs were observed on the skin, nasal and oral cavity upon physical examination, where squamous cell carcinoma would normally be found in pot-bellied pigs that could have metastasized. Thus, we assumed a primary squamous cell carcinoma of the reproductive tract.

In the squamous cell carcinoma cases reported in pot-bellied pigs and PSCCE in humans, metastasis to the regional lymph nodes and lungs has been found [27, 36, 37]. However, malignant uterine neoplasms such as adenocarcinomas and carcinoma-type tumours have metastasized to regional lymph nodes, lungs, liver, kidneys, and adrenal glands [13, 14, 22–24, 26]. Typically, lymph node involvement and metastasis are evaluated in patients with confirmed malignant tumours to perform tumour staging [38]. Unfortunately, spread to the regional lymph nodes was not histologically investigated in this case, but no enlargement of the superficial lymph nodes was detected upon physical examination. Only one radiographic view (i.e., right lateral recumbency) was evaluated for pulmonary metastasis. Ideally, standard three-view thoracic radiographs must be conducted to assess for the presence of metastatic disease, as it could lead to a misdiagnosis in 12–15% of cases [39]. Accurate staging could therefore not be performed. Nonetheless, no evidence of pulmonary lesions on the single radiographic view was observed, and metastases were assumed to be absent.

The exact aetiology and pathogenesis of reproductive neoplasia in pot-bellied pigs are not completely understood. As mentioned previously, it has been suggested that repeated endometrial exposure to oestrogen results in endometrial hyperplasia, which can ultimately lead to tumour formation as described in other animal species (i.e., sheep and cattle) and people [40, 41]. Several studies have found cystic endometrial hyperplasia concurrently with uterine neoplasia in pot-bellied pigs, specifically adenocarcinoma and leiomyoma [4, 14, 15, 20, 22]. Interestingly, neither cystic endometrial hyperplasia or persistent corpus luteum was observed in this case, suggesting that pyometra was less likely, despite the treatment having possibly played a role. Furthermore, old age, nulliparity and being intact have also been described as related risk factors in pot-bellied pigs developing reproductive tract neoplasia [4, 12, 14], which is consistent with the signalment of our patient.

In most domestic animals, pyometra is a common sequelae of a persistent corpus luteum that results in anoestrus and cervical closure due to the influence of progesterone [42, 43], which was initially suspected here. Estrogen priming is critical in the pathogenesis of pyometra as it ensures cervical relation, entry of bacteria and progesterone receptor upregulation. In production sows, endometritis and metritis are typically precursors of pyometra [42–44]. Some reports have indicated concurrent pyometra with reproductive tract neoplasia in pot-bellied pigs [12, 17, 20, 24]. Therefore, it is unclear whether our patient suffered from a persistent pyometra that could have stimulated neoplastic differentiation like the case described by [45] or if the pyometra was secondary to the neoplasia because of compromised uterine immune defence mechanisms.

Reproductive tract neoplasia in pot-bellied pigs typically present with a vaginal discharge or abdominal distension, but can display non-specific clinical signs [15]. Of the 19 pot-bellied pigs with uterine neoplasia described in case reports, only two pigs did not present with a vaginal discharge or abdominal distension [14, 22]. The remaining pigs displayed either one or both clinical signs, like our patient, which prompted diagnostic testing such as vaginal cytology with or without bacteriological examination, abdominal radiographs and ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), or exploratory laparotomy [4, 13, 15, 16, 18, 20]. Two pigs were euthanised after clinical examination without further diagnostic testing [18, 24], and another had already passed away [26]. Vaginal cytology just revealed a bacterial infection [13, 20] however, in our patient, it also revealed the presence of neoplastic cells, which made us suspicious of a uterine tumour after abdominal ultrasound findings only indicated a distended, fluid-filled uterus. An abdominal or uterine mass was diagnosed on abdominal ultrasound in ten of thirteen pigs, of which eleven subsequently had a laparotomy for tumour excision, whereas two were euthanised [4, 15, 16, 18]. Of the remaining three pigs, a uterine mass was only diagnosed on a CT scan in preparation for surgery in one pig [20], and during an exploratory laparotomy in the other two pigs [15, 20] – as seen in our case. An ovariohysterectomy was curative in seven of the eleven pigs that underwent surgery which is also consistent with our findings [15, 16, 20]. The remaining four pigs were euthanised during surgery as the tumours were either too large or adhered to adjacent organs [4, 15, 16]. Furthermore, metastases to distant organs were observed histologically after necropsy in five pigs, as previously mentioned [13, 14, 22, 24, 26], but no other authors investigated the presence of metastases in live patients.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates the first uterine squamous cell carcinoma in a Vietnamese Pot-bellied pig. It further supports the findings of many cases already described in the literature by illustrating that uterine neoplasia must be considered in a geriatric, nulliparous, pot-bellied pig with recurrent haemorrhagic vaginal discharge. Vaginal cytology might be a useful, non-invasive tool to detect reproductive tract neoplasia in conjunction with abdominal ultrasound. It is still worth performing an exploratory laparotomy even if other diagnostic modalities fail to detect a uterine mass if the history, signalment and clinical presentation are suggestive of uterine neoplasia. Regardless of the specific type of neoplasia, an ovariohysterectomy is curative if diagnosed early in the absence of metastases. Therefore, it is recommended to perform an ovariohysterectomy at an early age in pot-bellied pigs, as already suggested by multiple authors [15, 16, 20]. The similarities of this case report to PSCCE in humans also warrant further research and may provide bidirectional insights into the condition in both people and pigs.

Limitations

This case study had a few limitations. Administration of cloprostenol may have affected ovarian and uterine morphology by inducing luteolysis of an existing corpus luteum and subsequent changes in progesterone-dependent tissues.
