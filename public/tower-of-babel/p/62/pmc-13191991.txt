Japanese encephalitis among children in India: proportion of cases, case fatality, and epidemiological patterns - a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: N. Siva, Krishna Kumari Samantaray, Pravati Tripathy, Baby S Nayak, Deena Sivakumar, Preethy D’Souza, Ramesh Chandrababu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJapanese Encephalitis (JE) remains a major cause of viral encephalitis in India, disproportionately affecting children in endemic regions. Although vaccination programs have reduced incidence in several districts, seasonal outbreaks and pediatric mortality continue to be reported. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled proportion of children among laboratory-confirmed JE cases and the pooled case fatality proportion among pediatric JE cases in India. Secondary objectives included narratively synthesizing morbidity patterns and vaccination coverage.MethodsThis review was conducted in accordance with MOOSE guidelines and registered with PROSPERO. PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, ProQuest, Web of Science, and J-Gate were searched for studies published between January 2011 and August 2025. Additional sources included Google Scholar, Indian journal platforms, and official national and state health department reports. Observational and surveillance studies reporting pediatric JE data from India were included. Two reviewers independently performed screening, data extraction, and quality appraisal using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies. Random-effects meta-analysis was used to estimate pooled proportions. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² and Tau² statistics. Publication bias was examined using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test.ResultsThirty-six studies comprising 69,905 participants were included. The pooled proportion of children among confirmed JE cases was (95% CI: 15%-34%; p < 0.001; I² = 98.8; Tau² = 2.1919) reflecting the share of pediatric cases within included hospital-based and surveillance cohorts rather than population-level prevalence. The pooled case fatality proportion among pediatric JE cases was 18% (95% CI: 0.14%-0.23%; p < 0.001; I² = 98.4; Tau² = 0.3702). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies. Clinical manifestations were dominated by fever, seizures, and altered sensorium. Vaccination coverage for the first dose frequently exceeded 95%, whereas second-dose coverage ranged between 81 and 88% in reported districts. Barriers included delayed second-dose uptake, parental hesitancy, and access-related challenges.ConclusionsChildren account for a substantial proportion of JE cases in endemic Indian settings, and nearly one in five diagnosed pediatric cases results in death. Despite vaccination efforts, regional disparities and surveillance variability persist. Strengthening standardized surveillance, improving complete immunization coverage, and enhancing early case management are essential to reduce pediatric JE mortality in India.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27237-z.

Introduction

Japanese Encephalitis (JE) is a mosquito-borne viral infection of the central nervous system caused by the Japanese Encephalitis Virus (JEV), a flavivirus transmitted primarily by Culex tritaeniorhynchus mosquitoes [1, 2]. JE is one of the leading causes of viral encephalitis in Asia and remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly among children in endemic regions [3, 4]. Globally, an estimated 68,000 clinical cases occur annually, with case-fatality rates ranging between 20 and 30%, and 30–50% of survivors experiencing long-term neurological or psychological sequelae [2, 5].

India contributes a considerable proportion of the global JE burden, accounting for approximately 15-20% of reported cases annually [2]. Transmission dynamics in India are shaped by ecological and environmental factors, including rice cultivation, pig rearing, rural habitation patterns, monsoon-dependent mosquito breeding, and climatic variability [6, 7]. Children under 15 years are disproportionately affected because adults in endemic settings often acquire partial immunity through asymptomatic infection [2, 7]. Recurrent outbreaks have been documented across multiple states, including Uttar Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Odisha, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, and Karnataka, with transmission typically peaking during monsoon and post-monsoon months [8–13].

Despite the availability of safe and effective vaccines and the implementation of the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Japanese Encephalitis/Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (NPPCJAES), JE continues to persist in several endemic districts [14]. Hospital-based reports suggest that 60-75% of JE cases occur in children, with case fatality proportions ranging from 10 to 30% depending on disease severity and healthcare access [7, 8, 14]. In addition to mortality, JE is associated with significant morbidity, including seizures, altered sensorium, cognitive deficits, motor impairments, and long-term neurological sequelae that contribute to disability and social burden [2, 15].

Accurate estimation of JE burden in India remains challenging. Surveillance systems often integrate JE within the broader Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (AES) framework, which may result in misclassification or underestimation of laboratory-confirmed cases [16, 17]. Variations in diagnostic methods, reporting practices, and healthcare access across states further complicate national-level burden assessment. Moreover, many epidemiological studies are hospital-based or conducted in endemic regions, limiting generalizability to the broader pediatric population [18, 19].

Although vaccination is the cornerstone of JE prevention, disparities in vaccine uptake, delayed second-dose coverage, logistical challenges, and regional differences in immunization implementation may influence persistent transmission patterns [8, 20]. Understanding epidemiological patterns alongside vaccination coverage and system-level factors is essential for informed public health planning and targeted interventions. Given these gaps and regional variations, a comprehensive synthesis of existing evidence is needed. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled proportion of children among laboratory-confirmed JE cases and the pooled case fatality proportion among pediatric JE cases in India. Additionally, morbidity patterns, vaccination coverage, and related health system challenges were narratively synthesized to contextualize the broader epidemiological burden and inform policy and surveillance strengthening efforts.

Methods

This review has been prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251143055. It adheres to the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) reporting guidelines [21] and follows established methodological standards for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (See supplementary File 1).

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted between August and September 2025 in consultation with subject experts. A structured search strategy using key terms such as “Japanese Encephalitis,” “prevalence,” “case fatality,” “mortality,” “morbidity,” “hospitalization,” and “healthcare utilization” was developed and adapted for each database using appropriate Boolean operators. The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL (via EBSCOhost), ProQuest, Web of Science, and J-Gate (detailed search strategy provided in Supplementary File 2). To ensure comprehensive coverage, region-specific sources including Indianjournals.com, Jaypee Publications, AV Publications, and institutional repositories were also explored. Official national and state health department websites were reviewed, along with databases from the National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme (NVBDCP), Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), and WHO, to obtain relevant surveillance and epidemiological data. Additionally, forward and backward citation tracking of eligible studies and related reviews was undertaken to identify further studies conducted in India that met the inclusion criteria.

Selection process

The Rayyan software was used to manage and organize the search results, facilitating the identification and removal of duplicate records obtained from various databases. After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to assess eligibility, categorizing each study as either “included” or “excluded” before proceeding to the next stage of full-text review. To enhance methodological rigor, a predefined screening checklist based on the eligibility criteria was applied. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and thoroughly reviewed by the same two independent reviewers to extract relevant details as per the eligibility criteria. The suitability of studies for final inclusion was further evaluated based on the relevance of outcome measures and the methodological quality in addressing the study objectives. Any disagreements that arose at any stage of the screening process were resolved through thorough discussion and consensus among all reviewers.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data [22]. This checklist evaluates nine domains: (1) appropriateness of the sample frame; (2) adequacy of participant recruitment; (3) sufficiency of sample size; (4) detailed description of study subjects and setting; (5) coverage of data analysis; (6) validity of methods used to identify the condition; (7) reliability and standardization of condition measurement; (8) appropriateness of statistical analysis; and (9) adequacy and management of response rate. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus between the reviewers.

Data extraction

After title and abstract screening, full-text articles of eligible studies were retrieved for detailed assessment. Two independent reviewers extracted relevant data using a standardized extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics (author, year, design, setting), sample size and age distribution, proportion of pediatric JE cases, case fatality proportion, clinical manifestations, and, where reported, vaccination coverage and related health system factors. To ensure methodological rigor, all studies were independently reviewed by two primary reviewers, and 10% of the included articles were randomly cross-checked by a third reviewer for quality assurance. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data analysis

To estimate the pooled proportion of pediatric JE cases and the pooled case-fatality proportion, a meta-analysis was conducted in R using RStudio [23]. The analyses were performed using the meta package [24] and the metafor package [25] to synthesize results across eligible studies. Given the expected variability in study design, settings, and populations, a random-effects model was applied when substantial heterogeneity was identified. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics, with I² >75% indicating substantial heterogeneity [26]. Where necessary, missing variance measures were calculated using standard statistical formulas, and all proportion estimates were converted to percentages for consistency. Study-level findings were summarized in tables, and forest plots were generated to present pooled estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test for asymmetry. In prevalence meta-analyses, publication bias mainly reflects small-study effects, where smaller studies with unusually high or low prevalence estimates may be disproportionately represented, potentially influencing pooled results [27, 28]. A narrative synthesis was undertaken to summarize findings related to clinical manifestations, vaccination coverage, health system support, and reported challenges or barriers associated with JE prevention and management.

Six studies [6, 11, 18, 20, 29, 30] reporting 100% proportion of JE cases within their study populations were excluded from the meta-analysis because they lacked variability in the outcome measure. As these studies were restricted to confirmed JE cases without a comparative denominator, their inclusion would have biased the pooled estimate and compromised interpretability.

Results

A total of 884 records were identified, including 770 from five international and one Indian database and 114 from other sources. After removing 189 duplicates using Rayyan, 695 records remained for screening. During title and abstract screening, 543 records from international databases were excluded, and of the 114 records from other sources, 88 were excluded upon manual review. Full-text screening was conducted for 64 articles, of which 28 were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. Ultimately, 36 studies were included in the review. Study and sample characteristics

A total of 36 studies were included in the review [3, 4, 6–13, 16–18, 20, 29–50], conducted across 15 Indian states: Uttar Pradesh (n = 7), West Bengal (n = 6), Assam (n = 5), Bihar (n = 4), Madhya Pradesh (n = 2), Odisha (n = 2), Karnataka (n = 1), Maharashtra (n = 1), Andhra Pradesh (n = 1), Tamil Nadu (n = 1), Telangana (n = 1), Arunachal Pradesh (n = 1), Manipur (n = 1), Jharkhand (n = 1), Lakshadweep (n = 1), and one study covering all of India. Regionally, 32 studies were from Northern and North-Eastern India and 4 studies from South India. The cumulative sample size across all studies was N = 69,905 participants, aged 0–19 years, with a gender distribution of 41,457 males and 28,448 females. Complete study and sample characteristics with key findings were provided in Supplementary File 3.

Risk of bias assessment

Each study was evaluated and rated as having either a “low,” “unclear,” or “high” risk of bias across different domains. Inter-rater reliability for the assessments was strong, with a Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.91 (See supplementary File 4).

Proportion of children among confirmed JE cases

A total of 30 studies were included in the pooled analysis. The combined estimate showed that children accounted for 23% of laboratory-confirmed JE cases (95% CI: 15%–34%; p < 0.001; I² = 98.8%; Tau² = 2.1919). This figure reflects the proportion of pediatric cases within the included hospital-based or AES surveillance cohorts and does not represent population-level prevalence among all children in India. The substantial heterogeneity likely arises from variations in study design, case definitions, denominators, and regional epidemiological differences. Overall, the findings suggest that children continue to constitute a considerable proportion of JE cases in endemic regions with ongoing transmission (Fig. 2).

Case fatality proportion among pediatric JE cases

A total of 19 studies reporting mortality among laboratory-confirmed pediatric JE cases were included in the pooled analysis [7–13, 16, 17, 29, 31, 32, 34, 36, 42, 44, 46, 47, 49]. The pooled case fatality rate among JE-affected children was 18% (95% CI: 14%–23%; p < 0.001; I² = 98.4%; Tau² = 0.3702) (Fig. 3). This estimate represents deaths among confirmed JE cases within the included study cohorts, which were largely hospital-based or surveillance-identified populations, and should not be interpreted as mortality in the general pediatric population. The substantial heterogeneity observed across studies likely reflects differences in healthcare access, timing of presentation, disease severity, and regional variations in clinical management and reporting practices.

Sensitivity analysis

Robustness of the findings was evaluated by excluding studies identified as having a high risk of bias based on the JBI quality assessment checklist. A total of nine studies exhibiting bias across all domains were excluded. The pooled prevalence estimates showed minimal variation after exclusion, confirming the reliability and stability of the results.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. For the pooled proportion estimates, the regression test showed no statistically significant association between study size and effect estimate (slope coefficient 0.53; 95% CI: −0.13 to 1.18; p = 0.11). However, the significant bias coefficient (− 18.15; 95% CI: −29.06 to − 7.24; p = 0.002) suggests the presence of small-study effects (Fig. 4). This indicates that smaller studies tended to report proportion estimates that differed from those reported in larger studies, either higher or lower, which may have influenced the pooled estimate. This pattern can arise from selective publication, methodological differences, or greater variability in smaller samples. Therefore, while the pooled estimate remains informative, it should be interpreted with caution given the possibility that smaller studies may have disproportionately affected the overall result. For the case fatality rate, although the slope coefficient was statistically significant (-1.25; 95% CI: -1.61 to -0.88; p < 0.001), the bias coefficient was not significant (-0.73; 95% CI: -5.90 to 4.45; p = 0.77), indicating no strong evidence of small-study effects for mortality (Fig. 5). This suggests that the pooled case fatality rate is less likely to be influenced by study size–related bias.

Clinical manifestations among pediatric JE cases

Nine studies reported data on morbidity, defined in this review as the clinical manifestations and complications among laboratory-confirmed pediatric JE cases. Across studies, fever was the most consistently reported symptom, occurring in 90–100% of cases. Altered sensorium (46–100%) and seizures or convulsions (19.6–89%) were also highly prevalent, forming the predominant neurological presentation [12, 32, 34, 42, 43, 45, 46]. Other frequently reported symptoms included neck rigidity (52–55%), unconsciousness (43%), headache (43–80%), and vomiting (27–64%) [12, 29, 41, 42]. Less common manifestations were gastrointestinal bleeding (39%), body ache (15%), rash (15%), cough (17%), abdominal pain (9%), and shortness of breath [39]. Overall, these findings indicate that JE morbidity among children in India is primarily characterized by high-grade fever and significant neurological involvement, particularly seizures and altered consciousness, with additional gastrointestinal and respiratory features reflecting multisystem involvement.

Complications and outcome patterns

Beyond the reported case fatality rate, JE is associated with significant short- and long-term complications among survivors. Neurological sequelae, including persistent seizures, altered mental status, and cognitive impairment, were frequently documented [9, 12, 38]. Additional complications such as renal dysfunction and neurocysticercosis co-infection were also reported in some cases [12, 38]. Poor healthcare access, delayed diagnosis, and limited infrastructure, particularly in rural and endemic areas, were identified as contributing factors to worse clinical outcomes and prolonged recovery [16, 18]. These findings highlight the substantial post-infection burden of JE beyond immediate mortality.

Vaccine coverage in India

Barriers to vaccination coverage

JE vaccination programs have been implemented through both routine immunization and mass campaigns targeting children aged 9–18 months and adults in the endemic districts. First-dose JE vaccination coverage often exceeds 95%, whereas second-dose coverage ranges between 81% and 88% in regions such as Arunachal Pradesh, a hilly state in Northeast India, and the Ormanjhi block of Ranchi district, Jharkhand, India [20, 38]. Major obstacles to achieving full coverage include parental unawareness, vaccine hesitancy, and logistic challenges [30]. The benefits of JE vaccination substantially outweigh the associated costs, demonstrating strong economic value in endemic settings. Although minor side effects such as fever and pain may occur, the vaccine is generally considered safe, and economic analyses suggest that its overall benefits exceed implementation costs [18]. Despite significant progress, outbreaks continue in regions with suboptimal coverage, highlighting the need for sustained surveillance and continuous public engagement [16, 35].

Other significant findings from the included studies

A few studies highlighted aspects of healthcare support, challenges, barriers, and the overall healthcare burden of Japanese Encephalitis. These findings were summarized and reported through a narrative synthesis.

Healthcare support

The Indian healthcare system has played a crucial role in improving JE vaccination coverage and reducing disease burden through timely diagnosis, effective case management, and strengthened surveillance mechanisms. In one study, 77.8% of JE patients were successfully discharged, with an 11.1% case fatality rate attributed to early diagnosis, prompt management, and the availability of pediatric intensive care facilities [9]. At the community level, frontline workers such as Auxiliary Nurse Midwives (ANMs) and Sahiyas (ASHAs in Jharkhand, known as “Sahiyas”) were instrumental in promoting vaccine acceptance 78.0% of parents reported taking the JE vaccine based on their advice, while 43.0% cited disease prevention awareness and 36.3% acknowledged health workers’ guidance as motivating factors [20]. Government efforts have further strengthened JE control through annual “Health Alerts,” standardized protocols for AES/JE management, establishment of sentinel laboratories in major hospitals, and financial assistance of ₹50,000 to families of children who died due to JE, demonstrating strong public health commitment [16].

Healthcare challenges and barriers

Despite these efforts, several challenges persist in JE prevention and control. Diagnostic laboratories remain under-equipped, with weak cross-verification mechanisms and poorly supervised surveillance systems leading to underreporting of cases. Participation from private healthcare institutions in AES reporting is also limited [16]. On the community side, vaccine hesitancy and logistical barriers remain major concerns. Child unavailability during immunization sessions due to illness or absence was the most frequent cause of missed vaccination, followed by fear of side effects, vaccine shortages, and disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic [20]. Furthermore, nearly one-third (31.4%) of families reported that their children were not vaccinated, primarily due to lack of awareness (36.8%) and hesitancy (35.3%), underscoring persistent behavioural and informational barriers [30].

Health system impact of Japanese encephalitis

Japanese Encephalitis places substantial pressure on healthcare systems in endemic regions of India, particularly during monsoon and post-monsoon outbreaks when hospital admissions increase sharply [31, 43]. High-burden states such as Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Assam, Odisha, West Bengal, and the North-Eastern regions frequently experience seasonal surges that strain pediatric wards and intensive care units [7, 29]. Severe neurological manifestations, including seizures, altered sensorium, and meningeal signs, often require prolonged hospitalization and advanced supportive care [9, 42]. Limited diagnostic capacity and delayed referrals from rural areas further exacerbate clinical severity and resource utilization [29, 42]. Outbreak response also demands strengthened surveillance, laboratory support, and vector control measures, increasing financial and workforce burden on already resource-constrained districts [7, 31].

Healthcare burden of Japanese encephalitis

Japanese Encephalitis continues to impose a substantial health burden in India, particularly in rural and endemic regions. The disease predominantly affects children and adult males, with the highest incidence during monsoon and post-monsoon periods [31, 42, 43]. Hotspot regions include Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Assam, Odisha, West Bengal, and the North-Eastern states [7, 29]. Clinically, JE presents with severe symptoms such as high fever, seizures, altered sensorium, meningeal irritation, and vomiting [9].

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the pooled proportion of pediatric involvement and the pooled case fatality among Japanese Encephalitis (JE) cases in India. The analysis showed a pooled pediatric proportion of 23% and a pooled case fatality proportion of 18%, highlighting the continued clinical burden of JE in endemic settings. The 23% estimate represents the proportion of children among laboratory-confirmed JE cases reported in hospital-based or AES surveillance cohorts, predominantly from high-transmission regions. It does not reflect population-level prevalence among all children in India and should not be interpreted as a national pediatric prevalence rate. The elevated pooled proportion likely reflects the concentration of studies in endemic districts and variations in study populations, case definitions, and denominators across included studies. The very high heterogeneity observed across studies (I² > 98%) indicates marked regional variation in the reported JE burden. This variability is likely influenced by differences in diagnostic methods, seasonal and climatic factors affecting vector density, surveillance intensity, and disparities in healthcare access and reporting practices across regions.

The pooled pediatric proportion observed in this review is consistent with reports from endemic areas of South and Southeast Asia, where JE remains a major cause of viral encephalitis among children [5, 51]. Studies from Nepal and Bangladesh have similarly documented that 15–30% of pediatric AES cases are attributable to JE, comparable to our findings [52, 53]. The pooled case fatality proportion of 18% in this review is comparable to earlier hospital-based case fatality rates reported among laboratory-confirmed or hospitalized JE patients, which have ranged between 20% and 30% in severe cases [12, 36, 47]. These estimates reflect mortality among diagnosed or admitted cases rather than mortality in the general pediatric population. Differences across studies likely arise from variations in case severity, referral patterns, healthcare access, and critical care availability. Variations in fatality estimates across studies may reflect differences in healthcare access, timing of referral, case severity, and improvements in clinical management over time. However, mortality alone does not capture the full disease burden, as many JE survivors experience long-term neurological sequelae, including cognitive impairment, recurrent seizures, and behavioral difficulties, resulting in sustained individual and societal impact.

The marked heterogeneity can be attributed to differences in study design, population characteristics, and temporal trends. Diagnostic variability was a key factor; studies using ELISA-based serology identified more subclinical infections compared to those relying on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or clinical diagnosis [4, 39, 51]. Geographic diversity within India also contributes to variation, as climatic and agricultural conditions strongly influence Culex mosquito breeding [17, 44, 54]. Seasonal monsoon rains and rice cultivation create ideal habitats for the JE vector, resulting in cyclic outbreaks in certain regions [10, 29]. Differences in health system capacity may also explain part of the variation [18, 37]. Regions with robust surveillance systems and laboratory support report higher case detection, while under-resourced districts may underreport cases [18, 38]. Such disparities highlight the need for uniform case definitions, strengthened laboratory infrastructure, and standard surveillance protocols.

India launched the National JE Vaccination Programme in 2006 using the live-attenuated SA 14-14-2 vaccine, which significantly reduced JE incidence in several endemic districts. Despite these efforts, incomplete vaccine coverage and inconsistent follow-up doses continue to challenge program success. Factors such as vaccine hesitancy, lack of community awareness, and logistical barriers in remote areas hinder full immunization coverage [7, 10, 37, 40, 55]. Moreover, environmental risk factors remain inadequately addressed [17, 38, 54]. The primary findings of this meta-analysis demonstrate a substantial proportion of pediatric JE cases and a pooled case fatality proportion of 18%, indicating a significant clinical burden among children in endemic regions. Several included studies reported seasonal clustering, rural predominance, and concentration of cases in high-burden districts, reflecting ongoing transmission [7, 31, 42]. Although the main objective was to quantify pediatric burden and mortality, the findings also suggest implications for prevention. Variations in vaccination coverage and continued outbreaks in certain districts indicate gaps in implementation and surveillance [8, 20, 35].

Implications

The persistence of JE among children highlights the need for timely diagnosis and effective management, as its symptoms overlap with other viral encephalitides. Strengthening the capacity of healthcare providers, particularly nurses and primary care physicians, is crucial for early case identification and improved outcomes [1, 2]. At the health system level, JE surveillance should be integrated into routine child health programs, and vaccination coverage must be ensured in rural, tribal, and underserved populations. Continuous monitoring of vaccine effectiveness, outbreak surveillance, and targeted immunization campaigns can maintain high immunity levels. Policy efforts should focus on improving vaccine access, cold-chain management, and booster dose schedules where necessary [19]. Strengthening surveillance through the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) and establishing sentinel laboratories in high-risk areas will improve early detection and response [56]. Community and behavioural interventions promoting environmental hygiene, mosquito control, safe pig-rearing practices, and early healthcare seeking can reduce transmission [57].

Routine, accurate, and standardized surveillance is critical for vaccine-preventable diseases such as JE. Variations in diagnostic methods and underreporting may underestimate disease burden, thereby affecting immunization policy, target population selection, and resource allocation. High-quality, laboratory-supported surveillance systems are essential to monitor vaccine impact, detect outbreaks, identify immunity gaps, and guide evidence-based immunization strategies. The WHO Global Strategy for Comprehensive Vaccine-Preventable Disease (VPD) Surveillance emphasizes that integrated surveillance systems are fundamental for strengthening vaccination programs and ensuring timely public health response [19]. Strengthening JE surveillance through standardized case definitions, improved laboratory confirmation, and integration with national disease monitoring platforms would enhance burden estimation and support more targeted vaccination campaigns [58]. Further research is needed to assess the duration of vaccine-induced immunity, monitor circulating JE genotypes, and conduct spatial mapping of high-risk areas to improve micro-planning of immunization strategies.

Strengths and methodological considerations

This meta-analysis represents one of the most comprehensive attempts to quantify the burden of JE among Indian children. It employed a robust search strategy, standardized data extraction, and a random-effect model to account for heterogeneity. The inclusion of studies from multiple states provides a broad national overview. Assessment of publication bias and heterogeneity added methodological transparency, strengthening the validity of findings.

However, variability in study quality and diagnostic criteria remains a concern. Some older studies relied solely on clinical diagnosis, which may overestimate prevalence due to misclassification of other viral encephalitides as JE. Conversely, underdiagnosis in resource-poor regions may underestimate true prevalence. The absence of standardized outcome reporting, particularly for mortality, limited subgroup analyses. Moreover, language restrictions and possible publication bias may have excluded smaller or non-English studies.

Limitations

This review has several important limitations. First, the very high heterogeneity across studies limits the precision and interpretability of pooled estimates. Second, we did not conduct pooled subgroup analyses by year of study, seasonality, geographic region, or study design due to inconsistent reporting and variability in denominators across studies. This restricts our ability to draw region-specific or time-trend conclusions. Third, most included studies were hospital-based or conducted in endemic districts, which limits national representativeness and may overestimate the proportion of children among JE cases and case fatality proportions. Fourth, differences in diagnostic criteria, laboratory confirmation methods, and surveillance systems across states reduced comparability. Fifth, mortality estimates largely reflected case fatality among diagnosed cases and did not capture long-term neurological sequelae or community-based deaths. Finally, the review relied primarily on published literature, and exclusion of unpublished or gray data may have introduced reporting bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that children account for a substantial proportion of reported JE cases in India, particularly in endemic regions, and that the pooled case fatality proportion among pediatric JE cases remains high at 18%. Clinical manifestations are predominantly neurological, including fever, seizures, and altered sensorium, reflecting significant morbidity among affected children. However, most available data originate from hospital-based or endemic-area surveillance studies, limiting generalizability to the national population. High heterogeneity across studies suggests variations in surveillance systems, case definitions, and healthcare access. Future research should prioritize well-designed, population-based surveillance studies with standardized denominators to accurately estimate incidence and age-specific burden. There is also a need for consistent reporting of laboratory-confirmed cases, outcomes, and long-term neurological sequelae. Strengthening integrated surveillance systems and improving data quality will be essential to better quantify disease burden and guide targeted JE prevention strategies in India.
