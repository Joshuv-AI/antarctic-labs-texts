A rubber-based sensor with over 100 million-level ultra-sensitivity (0–10% strain range) via 3D super-interface
Authors: Xinghuo Wang, Yaru Huang, Hui Wang, Shiheng Yin, Chuanhui Xu, Zuankai Wang, Yukun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstrained by materials, structures, and interface, achieving high sensitivity, wide strain range, and linearity simultaneously remains an impossible triangle for flexible sensors. Herein, we propose 3D super-interface strategy based on patterned rubber substrate-conductive crack layer, successfully developing a microcrack super-interface flexible sensor (MSFS) with ultra-sensitivity (0–10% strain, GF 1.1 × 10⁸ and linearity 0.98). The 3D super-interface relies on the synergistic micro/nano level physical anchoring, and hydrogen bonding interfacial interactions between the rubber matrix and conductive crack layer, achieving strong interlayer bonding. During the sensing process, the crack structure endows the sensor ultra-sensitivity within 0–10% strain range; while the 3D super-interface ensures continuous electrical conductivity under >50% strain conditions. MSFS holds potential application value in monitoring expansion in silicon anode batteries. When the battery expansion reaches 2%, its resistance change can be as high as 22-fold. This approach enables the customized design of flexible sensors for ultra-sensitivity applications.Subject terms: Polymer synthesis, Nanowires, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

Conventional flexible sensor, such as composites and typical multilayer structures, due to limitations in materials, structures, and their interfaces, exhibit complex dynamic behaviors under dynamic deformation, resulting in a nonlinear correlation between strain response sensitivity and response range1–3. Thus, sensitivity, strain range and linearity form an impossible triangle in the field of flexible sensors. In contrast, multilayer structures featuring crack-based conductive pathways integrated with a flexible substrate can effectively circumvent the inconsistent changes in the filler network during strain processes due to the independence of the cracks from the substrate’s extensibility4–7. This design confers a better linear correlation between strain and relative resistance change, along with enhanced response sensitivity. However, a notable drawback of the independent crack structure is that upon full expansion, the resistance of the flexible substrate becomes excessively high (e.g., the resistivity of rubber can reach 1 × 10¹¹ Ω/cm), leading to a complete loss of electrical signals. Even if signal recovery is possible, a dead zone persists, rendering this approach unacceptable in safety-critical or diagnostic scenarios such as continuous robotic motion or ongoing health monitoring8,9. Therefore, a critical challenge persists: the inherent trade-off between high sensitivity (gauge factor, GF > 10,000) and wide strain response range (>10%) in crack-based sensors. This dilemma arises from the conflicting requirements of crack instability (for sensitivity) and interfacial stability (for robustness)10–13.

Crack-based flexible sensors, relying on macro/nano-crack dis/re-connection mechanisms, achieve remarkable GF values (e.g., 85,000 at 30% strain14 or 2000 at 50% strain15). Yet, their performance is fundamentally constrained by two-dimensional (2D) interfacial interactions between the conductive layer and rubber substrate. The large modulus mismatch (e.g., ~GPa-level conductive layers vs. ~MPa-level rubber substrates) induces localized stress concentrations, leading to irreversible delamination under strains exceeding 10%16–18. Recent studies highlight that even advanced strategies—such as strain-mediated anisotropic conductive networks19 or hybrid conductive polymers20—fail to fully resolve interfacial instability, limiting practical applications in dynamic environments (e.g., wearable devices, and intelligent robotic equipment)21–23. Therefore, three-dimensional (3D) interfacial bonding strategies have gained traction. Unlike conventional 2D interfaces relying solely on van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonding, 3D interfaces integrate physical anchoring (via micro/nano-structured substrates) and covalent interactions (e.g., ester or amide linkages, etc.) to create hierarchical adhesion. For example, Zhao et al.24 fabricated a Cu@PPy NW-threaded AgNFs film, subsequently coating it with SBS/toluene solution (15 wt%) to establish a strongly 3D interface via solvent evaporation. Regrettably, the resulting flexible sensor exhibited multi-stage strain dispersion (strain x1-x2, GF1; x2-x3, GF2; x3-x4, GF3…), with a GF of 1000 at 0–50% strain; at the elongation at break of 130–185%, the GF reached a maximum of “1.28 × 10⁶”. Moreover, existing 3D interfacial designs remain limited by incomplete crack stabilization and non-uniform stress distribution. Yu et al.25 deposited step-gradient chromium and silver layers onto flexible polydimethylsiloxane substrates via chemical vapor deposition. The strong interfacial bonding endowed the cracked flexible strain sensors with exceptional stability, maintaining consistent performance through 15,000 cycles at 4% strain. However, the randomness of the crack structure resulted in low linearity between sensitivity and strain range, such as 0–10%, GF = 10, R² merely 0.93. Additionally, most studies focus on single-modulus systems, wherein the modulus of each individual layer within a multilayer structure remains unchanged, thereby neglecting the role of gradient interfacial layers in stress dissipation. Fortunately, the fracture toughness of polymeric materials exhibits distinct hierarchical control characteristics, enabling us to exploit this property to form regular crack structures (fracture toughness being a material’s resistance to crack propagation).

This study proposes a “3D super-interface ” structural design strategy, which develops a three-dimensional interface bonded super interface layer through the synergistic effect of non-ionic polyacrylamide/sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (PAM/CMC) conductive layer and carboxylated styrene-butadiene rubber (XSBR) substrate. Crucially, this 3D super-interface layer relies on the dual synergistic effect of two different mechanisms: (1) the physical anchoring effect of conductive polymers on micro/nanostructured rubber surfaces, (2) hydrogen bonding interface interactions. Our work introduces a microcracked super-interfacial flexible sensors (MSFS), combining a brittle PAM/CMC-AgNWs crack layer, a ductile PAM/CMC-SWCNTs transition layer, and a micro/nano-textured XSBR substrate. The MSFS leverages covalent ester bonds between PAM/CMC and XSBR, alongside single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT)-mediated crack confinement. Together, these elements form a 3D super-interface that is fundamentally different from traditional 2D planar adhesive configurations.

The MSFS developed based on this design demonstrates remarkable performance characteristics. Notably, it achieves a 100 million-level sensitivity (GF value 1.1 × 10⁸ under 0–10% strain (R2å 0.98)), while maintaining a wide strain response range exceeding 100%. Its sensing mechanism operates through a two-stage process. Initially, the micro-crack structure provides ultra-sensitivity by enabling rapid changes in crack morphology during the early strain stage (Stage Ⅰ, <10%). Subsequently, the 3D super-interface layer—possessing excellent tensile strength and conductive network stability during high-strain stages (Stage Ⅱ, >10%)—effectively suppresses disorderly crack expansion. This dual functionality ensures the sensor maintains ordered crack distributions and continuous electrical signal output even under extreme strains greater than 100%. The MSFS demonstrates potential applications in monitoring subtle deformations such as battery expansion, achieving a resistance change of 22 times at approximately 2% expansion. Furthermore, this study establishes a theoretical model for the GF value of MSFS and reveals the coupling mechanism between material properties, interface structure design, and sensing performance.

Results

Design strategy of microcracked super-interface flexible sensors (MSFS). To overcome the impossible triangle between sensitivity, wide strain response range, and linearity in flexible sensors (Supplementary Fig. 1), we introduce an interfacial bonding layer, 3D super-interface. This interface comprises a crack-generating conductive layer and a patterned rubber substrate: the former delivers ultra-sensitivity through crack orientation—for instance, higher modulus in the conductive film yields a net-like crack pattern (dark brown in the schematic diagram of Fig. 1a), while lower modulus enables parallel crack formation (light brown in the schematic diagram of Fig. 1a). The rubber substrate provides the requisite stretchability and flexibility (green regions in schematic Fig. 1a). Based on crack propagation within this sensing system, ultra-sensitive responses in micro/small strain ranges can be achieved, while sustaining certain electrical signal transmission under large strains (when the crack system is fully disconnected) (Fig. 1a). Herein, we employ XSBR as the matrix and PAM/CMC-AgNWs composite as the conductive crack layer. By constructing a 3D super-interface between the rubber and conductive crack layer and regulating crack structures, we fabricate a microcracked super-interface flexible sensor (MSFS) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The 3D super-interface is realized through precise control of micro/nanostructures on the rubber surface to increase specific surface area and roughness, thereby enhancing the density of interaction sites for strong interfacial bonding (Supplementary Fig. 3). The first step in manufacturing MSFS involves tailoring the micro/nanostructures on the nano XSBR surface (Supplementary Fig. 4) via multiphysics coupling during latex film formation to achieve interfacial structural control. Subsequently, the conductive slurry (PAM/CMC-AgNWs) is coated onto the XSBR surface and dried to form the conductive layer (Fig. 1b). Figures 1c, d show the AFM 3D structural and SEM images of the pristine XSBR film surface (p-XSBR) and the modified XSBR surface after conductive layer removal (m-XSBR), respectively. The roughness of m-XSBR reached 400 nm, representing 22-fold improvements over p-XSBR (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). Significantly, the super interface alleviates mitigates interfacial delamination and slippage between the conductive layer and substrate, thereby enhancing the strain sensing and mechanical stability of MSFS (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 7).

FTIR-ATR analysis (attenuated total reflection mode, probing ~1 μm below the surface) was performed on 5PAM/5CMC@XSBR bilayer films with varying 5PAM/5CMC thicknesses (1, 2, 3 and 5 μm), as shown in Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 8. For pure PAM, a sharp peak at 3466 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibration of –NH₂ groups, while XSBR exhibits a weak, broad carboxyl (–COOH) peak near 3400 cm⁻¹. In the 3 μm-thick film, a distinct peak emerges at 3424 cm⁻¹. For 2 μm and 1 μm films, peaks shift to 3414 cm⁻¹ and 3412 cm⁻¹, respectively. The temperature-dependent FTIR spectra of 5PAM/5CMC-2 μm further corroborate hydrogen bonding interactions (Supplementary Fig. 9). At lower temperatures, the -OH stretching vibration peak of 5PAM/5CMC-2 μm at 3200–3400 cm⁻¹ exhibits broader and multi-peaked characteristics. This indicates that both hydroxyl and amino groups are partially engaged in hydrogen bonding. As temperature increases, peak broadening progressively diminishes due to hydrogen bond dissociation, suggesting that most hydroxyl groups have transitioned to a free state. To further validate this interaction, the PAM/CMC composite layer was mechanically peeled from the XSBR substrate, and the exposed XSBR cross-section was analyzed. Supplementary Fig. 10 shows SEM-EDX of the peeled XSBR (m-XSBR) with PAM/CMC. These observations confirm that PAM/CMC is intricately entangled on the XSBR surface via mechanical interlocking and hydrogen bonding, preventing complete detachment. These results demonstrate that interfacial structural modulation significantly enhances the specific surface area and roughness of the rubber substrate, providing more binding sites for interfacial interactions.

Notably, PAM contains amide groups that can form strong hydrogen bonds with carboxyl groups in XSBR rubber. However, PAM exhibits poor film-forming properties, limiting its ability to generate microcracks or disperse conductive fillers. To address this, CMC was introduced, which not only improves the film-forming capability of the PAM/CMC composite but also strengthens the interfacial interactions between PAM/CMC and XSBR. A highly stretchable 3D super-interface spontaneously forms between the conductive layer and the XSBR substrate. The film was then pre-bent with a fixed curvature to induce controlled cracks in the conductive layer. Under small strains, similar to conventional crack-based sensors [15,22], crack propagation in the conductive layer causes a drastic resistance change. However, when the strain reaches the critical deformation threshold of the cracks (ε > 50%), the 3D super-interface maintains stable electrical signal transmission. The SEM image illustrates the structure of the conductive crack layer in the microcrack super-interface flexible sensor (MSFS) under 10% strain. This structure features fishnet-like and parallel-arranged cracks with a spacing of approximately 500–700 µm and a depth of about 25 µm (Fig. 1g). Such a structure stands in stark contrast to traditional crack-based sensors, where cracks typically propagate into the substrate at the nanoscale [6,15]. Remarkably, MSFS achieves an extraordinary GF of 110,000,000 within a 10% strain range, with linearity (R²) reaching 0.98 (Fig. 1h). To validate the critical role of the 3D super-interface, we fabricated a control sensor using XSBR as the substrate (no surface roughness or polar groups for interaction with PAM/CMC). Once the strain exceeds 6%, cracks in the XSBR-based sensor without 3D super-interface completely disconnect or delaminate (Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Fig. 12), resulting in resistance values exceeding the multimeter’s measurement limit (~1.2 GΩ). Under these conditions, the conductive pathways within the sensor’s active layer behave as an open circuit. In contrast, MSFS maintains stable strain responsiveness even at 100% strain (Fig. 1h). Thus, compared to state-of-the-art strain sensors14,23,25,26, MSFS represents a groundbreaking advancement, simultaneously achieving ultra-sensitivity (GF > 100 million), a broad strain response range (>100%), and ultrafast response speed (Fig. 1i).

Mechanisms of modulation of MSFS crack structure. The aforementioned design strategy of MSFS emphasizes that the balance of the conductive layer structure plays a decisive role in the stability and sensitivity of the sensor. As shown in Fig. 2a, by blending polyacrylamide (PAM) and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) in different ratios (9/1, 7/3, 5/5, 3/7, 1/9), we achieved controllable fracture toughness and controlled the crack structure of the conductive layer. The MSFS with a multilayer structure based on PAM/CMC-AgNWs/SWCNTs@XSBR exhibits a distinct conductive layer crack structure under tensile conditions, along with a super-interface that enables a brittle-ductile transition (Fig. 2b). FTIR-ATR spectroscopy (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 13) reveals changes in the interfacial interactions between PAM/CMC and XSBR at different ratios. Based on the PAM/CMC content, the samples were labeled as 9/1, 7/3, 5/5, 3/7, and 1/9. In pure PAM, the peak at 3466 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibration of the –NH₂ group. For the 7/3 composite, this peak shifts to 3441 cm⁻¹, while for 5/5, 3/7, and 1/9, the peaks appear at 3424 cm⁻¹, 3414 cm⁻¹, and 3412 cm⁻¹, respectively. Additionally, the characteristic peak of the C = O stretching vibration shifts from 1655 to 1650 cm⁻¹, which can be attributed to intermolecular and/or intramolecular interactions, such as van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonding between PAM and CMC26,27. Meanwhile, XPS analysis shows a decrease in the C = O binding energy from 532.3 to 531.9 eV, indicating that the hydrogen bonding between the amide groups of PAM and the carboxyl groups of XSBR weakens as the PAM content decreases (Fig. 2d, e). All the above results indicate that after the combination of PAM and CMC, strong interactions form between the two molecular chains, leading to changes in the modulus of the PAM/CMC composite film.

AFM testing (Fig. 2f) shows that the modulus of the PAM/CMC composite decreases as the PAM content decreases: from 70 (7/3) to 7.7 GPa (1/9). Under directional stress, variations in fracture toughness result in distinct crack morphologies. Notably, the 1/9 composite (90% CMC, modulus ~10 GPa) produces ordered cracks but experiences interfacial slippage and delamination at 30% strain due to poor bonding (Supplementary Fig. 14). Under this “brittle-ductile” regulation mechanism, PAM/CMC films with higher moduli (e.g., 70 for 7/3 and 50 GPa for 5/5) exhibit a fishnet-like crack pattern, while those with moderate moduli (20–30 GPa for 3/7) form parallel-aligned cracks (Fig. 2g, h).

Crucially, the tunable crack structures enable different strain ranges and sensitivities. For the 9/1 composite (high modulus), the sensor has a strain range of 4.5–10% and a sensitivity, GF of 2.0 × 10⁸. On the one hand, the resistive signal fails to form a complete linear relationship within the 0–10% strain range, and beyond this limit at 10% strain, an electrical open circuit occurs. In contrast, the 5/5 PAM/CMC (moderate modulus) achieves a wider strain range (0–10%) with a sensitivity (GF) of 1.1 × 10⁸ (linearity R² = 0.98), as shown in Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 15. These results emphasize that our design strategy not only optimizes the performance of individual sensors but also achieves an overall enhancement in performance, including stability, durability, and sensing capabilities, thereby resolving the long-standing trade-offs in flexible strain sensors.

The sensing mechanism of MSFS. Considering the hydrogen bonding interactions between PAM/CMC and XSBR, the composite film adjacent to the XSBR layer exhibits excellent flexibility (Fig. 3a). The interfacial interactions are constrained by spatial distance. Due to these interfacial interactions, the material near the interface exhibits properties of the other thin-film material, while the portion of the material farther from the interface retains its own material properties. The micro-morphology of slight wrinkling at the crack bottom is characteristic of the PAM/CMC film (approximately 1–2 µm thick). This indicates that under 120% strain, the sensitive layer of the MSFS is not completely ruptured (Fig. 1). On one hand, the super-interface layer ensures the structural stability of the MSF and is highly sensitive to micro/small strains (0–10%). Additionally, the upper interface layer provides a partially stable conductive network for the MSF, thereby preventing failure and signal loss under large strains (>50%).

We used a 3D optical profiler to analyse the depth of microcrack structures in the MSFS under strains ranging from 0 to 10% (Fig. 3b, Stage Ⅰ). Under normal conditions, the cracks have a depth of around 5 µm and a width of approximately 2 µm. At 1% strain, the width of the cracks in the MSFS increases to around 4 µm, while their depth reaches approximately 10 µm. At 10% strain, the crack width increases to around 30 µm and the crack depth grows to approximately 22 µm. SEM images reveal the micro-morphology of the MSFS crack structures, with adjacent cracks clearly arranged in parallel (Fig. 3c). Figure 3d–f display images of single-crack structures under 10%, 20% and 50% strains, respectively (Stage Ⅱ). Notably, the crack width increases to around 40 µm at 20% strain and to approximately 60 µm at 50% strain. At this point, the cracks are seen to propagate in the direction of the tensile strain (Fig. 3g). Magnified local details reveal that, when a small strain is applied to the MSFS, disentanglement initially occurs between the conductive layers (AgNWs-PAM/CMC), leading to an increase in resistance (Fig. 3h). Subsequently, as the applied strain increases, the conductive network gradually breaks. As the applied strain continues to rise, cracks in the super-interface layer (SWCNTs-PAM/CMC) separate and propagate perpendicular to the direction of the applied force (Fig. 3i, j). Fortunately, complete fracture does not occur, thereby maintaining signal transmission. In contrast, when the interfacial interaction is weak, such as in the case of 1PAM/9CMC, noticeable delamination and detachment occur at the interface during the stretching process (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Compared with traditional nanoscale crack-based strain sensors relying on metals or semiconductors, microscale crack structures can endow sensors with a greater resistance change, that is, a higher strain sensitivity (GF value). Combining the aforementioned expansion analysis of crack structures, Fig. 4a illustrates the resistance changes in different parts of the MSFS during the tensile deformation process. It can be confirmed that microcrack propagation is the primary cause of resistance signal changes. Under strains ranging from 0 to 10%, as the resistance of the AgNWs@PAM/CMC layer (R1) decreases, the initial bulk resistance of the MSFS (R0) also decreases, approximating R1 (R0 ≈ R1). When the resistance change slows down within the strain range of >10%–15% (Fig. 1f), the MSFS enters a buffering state, where crack propagation behavior has been completed, and electrical signal transmission mainly occurs in the SWCNT@PAM/CMC layer (R2). The AgNWs layer, as the main conductive material in the crack layer, determines the upper limit of the MSFS’s sensitivity, while the SWCNT layer, as the primary material of the flexible 3D super-interfacial layer, sets the lower limit of conductivity for the strain sensor. A higher content of the SWCNT layer results in more stable conductive pathways formed in the 3D super-interfacial layer, leading to smaller resistance changes (Rfinal ≈ R2). In the calculation of strain sensitivity, the strain response range should also be considered, which corresponds to the crack structures of different conductive layer resistance (R1) and 3D super-interface layer resistance (R2) (Fig. 4b). By comparing the maximum GF values of MSFS with different ratios to the simulated formula (GF=R2R1Δε), the close agreement (within 10%) validates our model (Supplementary Table 2-5). Signal tests conducted on the MSFS under both microstrain (less than 1%) and larger strain ranges (2–10%) demonstrate high signal intensity and stability. For instance, the relative resistance signal of the MSFS reaches 2.5 for a 0.5% strain signal (Fig. 4c); while it attains 7.0 × 10⁶ for a 10% strain signal (Fig. 4d). Such a wide strain response range and exceptionally high response sensitivity are highly remarkable. Moreover, when the MSFS is subjected to cyclic tensile loading and unloading at 4% strain for over 10,000 cycles, its relative resistance signal remains consistently stable at 1.1 × 10⁶, with a terminal error of less than 10% (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 17). Meanwhile, we evaluated the mechanical stability of the MSFS film under multiple cyclic tensile loading. After 1000 cycles of 50% strain cyclic tensile loading, the mechanical behavior and strain-resistivity relationship of the MSFS remains consistently stable (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 18). This excellent application stability is primarily attributed to the vulcanization of the XSBR rubber substrate (Fig. 4g, h). The excellent elastic matrix also confers good response sensitivity to the MSFS. Current research on sensor response sensitivity has only examined variations in sensor response time resulting from specific testing methods (such as prescribed strain rates and elongation lengths). However, differing strain rates and elongation lengths may induce distinct strain deformations, thereby yielding inconsistent outcomes. Therefore, analogous to the GF in sensitivity definitions, the authors introduce the concept of response sensitivity (GFt). Following testing, the response sensitivity of the MSFS was approximately 2.1 × 10⁷/s (Fig. 4i). That is, within the same time interval, the MSFS exhibits a more acute response characteristic to signal transients (Fig. 1i). Crucially, the MSFS exhibits excellent adaptability to complex environments. For example, its baseline resistance remains stable under prolonged exposure to varying humidity levels (30%, 75%, and 95% relative humidity, RH). Similarly, across a temperature range from ambient to 80 °C, the initial resistance shows negligible variation (Supplementary Fig. 19). Moreover, the MSFS demonstrates remarkable robustness against mechanical stress; even during rapid bending or complete folding, signal transmission remains stable (Fig. 4j).

MSFS shows potential applications in monitoring human health and exercise status under both small and large strains. Sensors have significant application value in monitoring human health or physical activity, particularly those with a wide strain sensing range and ultra-sensitivity. We mounted the MSFS sensors at the Adam’s apple and wrist pulse points to observe their actual monitoring performance of human activity. By recording changes in the sensor’s relative resistance during the swallowing process (Fig. 5a), we found that when swallowing occurs, the sensor produces significant electrical signal changes, with relative resistance changes reaching up to 400. Notably, when testing changes in pulse rate (heart rate) during human movement, the signal values not only increase significantly with rising heartbeat frequency but also exhibit a notable enhancement in signal strength (Fig. 5b). For instance, in a non-exercise state with a heart rate of 58, the signal strength is 128%; when exercising with a heart rate of 121, the signal strength reaches 260%. It is worth noting that, due to its excellent sensitivity, MSFS has great potential for application in early warning of abnormal conditions in the human body. As shown in Fig. 5c–e, we conducted simulated experiments applying MSFS to biological signal monitoring: the MSFS was simulated to be affixed to the human larynx, to monitor respiratory status during sleep or rest. When the body maintains normal breathing, the signal remains stable at around 150% (Fig. 5d). However, if abnormalities occur, such as sudden rapid breathing, the signal value can suddenly change to over 600% (Fig. 5e). Figure 5f–h show the relative resistance changes when MSFS is fixed to the finger, elbow, and knee joints, respectively. MSFS monitors the relative resistance changes caused by the movement of different human joints, such as the finger and knee, with a change amplitude of 30,000 to 90,000, and the signal is repeatable (Fig. 5f–h). The above experimental results demonstrate that the MSFS exhibits performance in monitoring human movement states and maintaining signal stability. Although MSFS currently simulates biological signals for testing purposes, we believe that future clinically relevant research represents a crucial step towards its biomedical applications. It can continuously monitor human health and movement states, and its strong signal effectively mitigates the impact of external factors on signal stability.

The application value of MSFS in micro-deformation applications such as battery expansion monitoring. As a flexible sensing film with ultra-high pressure-sensitive deformation detection capabilities, MSFS has significant application value in detecting minute deformations, such as battery deformation monitoring. To further demonstrate the high sensitivity of MSFS under non-tensile deformation, we cut the sample into strips measuring 5 cm by 1 cm and bent them at different curvatures (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 20). Surprisingly, due to the robust interfacial bonding between MSFS and the opening of crack structures under micro-deformation, the relative resistance change value of MSFS reached 2.3 at a curvature change of 0.2% and 120 at a curvature change of 1.0%. More importantly, as the bending degree increased further, the signal of MSFS became more significant, such as a relative resistance change value of 2000 at a curvature of 2% (Fig. 6b).

When the battery poses a potential danger, i.e., the expansion is too high, MSFS can effectively issue a warning.

To utilize MSFS more effectively for monitoring battery expansion during charging and discharging, we used a high-performance battery monitoring system to control and monitor the charging and discharging processes of silicon anode batteries (rated capacity: 4.25 V; rated current: 500–1500 mA). The charging and discharging currents were 1500 mA and the charging and discharging energies were 1 Ah (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 21). To secure the battery and reserve sufficient space for the MSFS, we designed a battery fixture with a 4 cm by 1 cm hole in the middle (Fig. 6d). After one round of charge-discharge testing, the expansion of the silicon anode battery (with a normal charging expansion of 2–4%) reached a maximum of 2.3%, corresponding to a relative resistance change value of 22 (see Fig. 6e). Furthermore, the expansion during the charge-discharge process is completely correlated with the resistance change value. The charging and discharging process of the silicon anode battery operates under normal working conditions, with the system temperature maintained at a constant level (Fig. 6f). Meanwhile, during the repeated bending and recovery process, which simulates high deformation changes, the MSFS signal remains highly stable and durable (Fig. 6g). These results demonstrate that MSFS can effectively monitor battery expansion during charging and discharging, distinguish between normal and excessive expansion, and provide early warnings of potential hazards (Fig. 6h). Unlike previous studies that monitored changes in battery temperature, this study was to use an external flexible sensor film to effectively monitor the expansion process of batteries. It was able to effectively monitor changes in the expansion even when the battery temperature did not rise (Fig. 6i). This study will effectively supplement the emerging battery safety detection methods currently in use.

Discussion

In this study, we have introduced a “ 3D super-interface” structure design strategy, leveraging patterned rubber substrates to successfully fabricate a MSFS with ultra-sensitivity reaching the 100-million level (GF of 1.1 × 10⁸, 0–10% strain range, R2 = 0.98). The core innovation lies in the 3D super-interfacial layer, which harnesses the synergistic effects of micro/nano-scale physical anchoring and hydrogen bonding interfacial interactions between the conductive layer polymers and the rubber substrate. This interaction mechanism establishes a three-dimensional interfacial interlayer bonding, a significant advancement over traditional two-dimensional planar bonding approaches.

During the sensing process, the carefully engineered crack structure endows the MSFS with ultra-sensitivity, particularly in the micro- and small-strain ranges, enabling precise detection of minute deformations. Simultaneously, the 3D super-interfacial layer plays a pivotal role in preventing the uncontrolled extension of the crack structure under large strains. This is attributed to its exceptional tensile properties and stable conductivity, which collectively maintain the sensor’s strain response integrity even under extreme deformation conditions. The practical applications of the MSFS have been extensively demonstrated, showcasing its potential in monitoring subtle deformations with high accuracy. Notably, in battery expansion monitoring scenarios, the MSFS achieved a remarkable resistance change of 24 times at an expansion of 2%. This highlights its capability to effectively distinguish between normal and abnormal battery expansion, providing early warnings of potential safety hazards. Furthermore, our research has revealed that the MSFS signal remains highly stable and durable during repeated bending and recovery processes, simulating high deformation changes, which ensures its reliability in real-world applications. Compared to previous studies that primarily focused on monitoring battery temperature changes, our approach represents a significant paradigm shift by utilizing an external flexible sensor membrane to directly monitor the battery’s expansion process. This method enables effective monitoring of expansion changes even when the battery temperature does not rise, offering a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of battery health and safety.

Preparation of MSFS. To prepare the SWCNT@PAM/CMC-XSBR composite film, a predetermined amount of sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) was dissolved in deionized water and magnetically stirred at room temperature for 2 h to obtain a 1% (w/w) CMC solution. Similarly, a 1% (w/w) solution of non-ionic polyacrylamide (PAM) was prepared by dissolving the required quantity of PAM particles in deionized water under magnetic stirring. These two solutions were then mixed in a specific ratio to form the flexible polymer matrix precursor. Subsequently, a suspension of single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) was uniformly dispersed into the PAM/CMC mixed solution to create the conductive precursor. The temperature of a blast oven was adjusted to an appropriate level, and a specific amount of carboxylated styrene-butadiene rubber latex (XSBR) was cast onto a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold. To ensure uniform film coating, the mold’s position was adjusted using a bubble level. Once the XSBR had vulcanized and formed a stable base, the conductive precursor was applied in layers onto the XSBR surface using a casting method. The blast oven temperature was set to 50 °C, and each layer was dried for 2 h. After all layers of the conductive precursor had been applied and dried, the entire assembly was further dried at 50 °C for an additional 12 h to ensure complete curing and solvent evaporation. This process yielded the SWCNT@P/C-XSBR composite film, ready for further characterization and testing. A certain amount of PAM/CMC mixed solution was taken, and a dispersion of SWCNTs was added to the mixed solution to prepare the flexible bonding layer. In another portion of the PAM/CMC mixed solution, AgNWs were incorporated to prepare the crack layer. Subsequently, the SWCNT layer and AgNWs layer were applied in batches by casting over a vulcanized XSBR layer. The fully dried AgNWs/SWCNT@P/C-XSBR composite film was cut into strips measuring 50 mm in length, 10 mm in width, and 2 mm in thickness. These strips were further dried at 70 °C for an additional 0.5 h. Subsequently, the strip-shaped film was bent along a metal cylinder with a curvature of 1 at this temperature, generating crack structures parallel to the direction of movement. Upon cooling to room temperature, the crack structures stabilized.

The initial resistance (R₀) was measured using a high-precision digital multimeter (Keithley DMM6500) on five separately fabricated sensors for each condition, after a 5-min relaxation period with no strain. All test specimens were fabricated as rectangular prisms with dimensions of 3 cm (length), 1 cm (width), and 0.3 cm (thickness). The reported R₀ and derived GF values are the average of these five measurements, with error bars representing the standard deviation. Prior to all electrical signal simulation tests for wearable applications, informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The response sensitivity (GFt) denotes the ratio of the change in ΔR/R₀ to the time required under step strain conditions (in this experiment, a tensile rate of 100 mm/min and 5% strain were specified)
