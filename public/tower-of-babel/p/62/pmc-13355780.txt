Progressive neurological decline in a measles survivor: A case report of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis in Zambia
Authors: Derby Tembo, Celia Greenlaw, Jombo Namushi, Evans Mpabalwani, Archana A. Patel, Nfwama Kawatu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis in a 14-year-old boy who presented to specialized care 17 months after the onset of symptoms, at advanced disease stage with cognitive decline, seizures, myoclonus and dystonia. A history of infantile measles, combined with characteristic electroencephalography, neuroimaging, and elevated cerebrospinal fluid measles IgG levels, confirmed the diagnosis. The prolonged delay to presentation, driven by barriers to healthcare access, limited community and provider awareness of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, and social stigma resulted in presentation at stage 3 disease and precluded any meaningful therapeutic interventions. Additionally, empirical antituberculosis therapy was initiated despite negative cerebrospinal and stool GeneXpert MTB/RIF results, illustrating the diagnostic anchoring that occurs in tuberculosis endemic settings. This case highlights the critical importance of early recognition of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, the need to strengthen healthcare access and neurological diagnostic capacity in resource limited settings and the risk of empiric treatment practices that may delay appropriate diagnosis and management. With the global resurgence of measles, increased awareness and surveillance for subacute sclerosing panencephalitis in high-burden settings are essential.

Introduction

Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE) is a rare, progressive, and fatal neurodegenerative disease caused by a mutated measles virus infection that evades immune clearance and persists within the central nervous system (CNS) [1], [2]. During a typical measles infection, a robust cell-mediated immune response eradicates the virus from the infected cells [3]. However, in SSPE, it is postulated that an inadequate cellular response leads to chronic intracellular infection. The virus can remain dormant in host neurons for years before becoming clinically evident [4]. This persistent infection ultimately leads to widespread CNS inflammation, demyelination, and neuronal loss [1].

SSPE manifests from one month to 27 years following the primary measles infection, with a typical latency period of 6–8 years [1], [5]. The condition predominantly affects children and adolescents, and a shorter latency period is frequently observed in familial cases or when the primary infection occurs before two years of age [2]. Diagnosis is established using the modified Dyken criteria (Table 1). EEG demonstrates characteristic periodic high amplitude slow waves (Radermecker complexes) in 65–85% of patients [6], [7], [8]. The risk of SSPE is estimated at 4–11 cases per 100,000 measles infections, with higher incidence when the initial infection occurs before five years of age [8], [9]. Regional differences mirror vaccination coverage: rates range from 0.01 per million population in the USA to 21 per million in India [1], [8], [10]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, limited data estimate the incidence at 0.39 per million, likely underestimated due to diagnostic challenges [11]. Zambia has experienced recurring measles outbreaks, the most recent reported in 2023 [12], [13]. Robust measles immunization programs protect against SSPE, as the vaccine itself does not cause SSPE [8], making expanded vaccine coverage essential, particularly to protect the youngest and most vulnerable children.

There is no definitive cure for SSPE, and management remains primarily supportive, with mortality rate of approximately 95% [1]. Antiviral and immunomodulatory agents including Isoprinosine, ribavirin, and intrathecal interferon alpha, have demonstrated only limited efficacy in prolonging survival [1], [14]. While ketogenic diet may help mitigate cognitive decline and myoclonic symptoms in patients unresponsive to antiseizure medications [15]. The management of SSPE in resource-limited settings is complicated by high cost and limited availability of advanced therapies. Even dietary interventions are often logistically and financially prohibitive to implement in low resource settings.

We present, to our knowledge, the first published case in indexed literature of SSPE in Zambia, highlighting the diagnostic and management challenges of SSPE in resource limited settings [11], with a particular focus on the factors contributing to delayed presentation, the consequences of late diagnosis on disease stage and outcome, the risks of empiric antituberculosis therapy in endemic settings.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 14-year-old male presented to the University Teaching Hospitals-Children's Hospital (UTHs-CH), the largest pediatric referral centre in Zambia. He presented with a 17-month history of progressive neurological decline. The illness began with episodic “startle-like” myoclonic limb movements that led to frequent falls and physical injuries. Initially occurring about five times daily, these episodes progressively increased in frequency, eventually impairing his ability to ambulate and perform daily activities. Over the subsequent 12 months, he developed additional abnormal movements, including sustained dystonic posturing of the limbs associated with myoclonus. During this period, caregivers noted declining school performance and social withdrawal. He subsequently developed progressive speech deterioration, dysphagia and significant weight loss. Two months prior to presentation, he began experiencing minute-long episodes of right gaze deviation, limb stiffening, and rapid breathing which were later diagnosed as seizures. At the time of hospital admission, the patient was nonverbal, non-ambulatory, and had profound dysphagia.

The 17-month delay to presentation at the tertiary centre was multifactorial. Multiple visits to local health facilities over the first 12 months yielded nonspecific diagnoses without referral for neurological evaluation. Transportation costs and out-of-pocket expenditure for investigations further delayed care. Social stigma surrounding the patient’s progressive decline led the family to conceal his condition, delaying the decision to seek specialized care until overt seizures and complete loss of ambulation occurred.

The past medical history was significant for uncomplicated measles infection at three months of age during an outbreak of measles in Zambia, from which he fully recovered. He was HIV-negative and had no family history of similar neurological disorders. On general physical examination, the patient appeared chronically ill, with severe muscle wasting, and pallor. Vital signs including temperature, respiratory rate, pulse rate and blood pressure were normal. Neurological examination revealed an awake but inattentive and nonverbal patient who was unable to follow commands. Frequent spontaneous myoclonic jerks and dystonic posturing were observed. Motor assessment demonstrated antigravity movement in the upper limbs, whereas lower limb movement was limited to the plane of the bed. Spasticity was present in all limbs. He had brisk reflexes throughout and bilateral extensor plantar reflexes, and wrist contractures.

Laboratory studies revealed mild hypochromic normocytic anemia and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate. White blood cell counts, platelet counts, renal and electrolyte panels (creatinine, urea, sodium, potassium) and transaminases were all within normal limits. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, including cell count, gram stain, microscopy, protein, glucose, chloride, and lactate, were unremarkable. Testing for tuberculosis with chest-Xray, CSF and stool GeneXpert MTB/RIF were negative. Malaria testing was also negative. Despite recurrent febrile episodes, blood, CSF, and urine cultures remained negative. Measles-specific IgG levels were significantly elevated in serum (1627 mg/dL; reference: 700–1000 mg/dL) and CSF (15.01 mg/dL; reference: 0–3.4 mg/dL), yielding a measles antibody index of 3.55 (positive >1.5).

The EEG showed diffuse electroclinical myoclonic jerks, periodic high amplitude delta complexes in the right centroparietal region, and background slowing consistent with a diffuse encephalopathy. MRI of the brain revealed symmetric cortical atrophy, and periventricular and subcortical hyperintense lesions (Fig. 1). Management focused on supportive and palliative care including nasogastric tube feeding and pharmacological treatment for myoclonus, dystonia, spasticity, seizures and anemia. Given his persistent fevers, chronic muscle wasting and neurological decline in a tuberculosis endemic region, empirical antituberculosis therapy (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol) was initiated based on clinical suspicion, despite negative CSF and stool GeneXpert MTB/RIF testing. This decision was driven by high local TB prevalence, overlapping features between disseminated TB and chronic neurological disease, paucibacillary nature of TB in the paediatric population and the perceived risk of withholding treatment in a critically ill patient. However, CSF analysis was unremarkable for TB meningitis and neuroimaging was more consistent with SSPE. Antituberculosis therapy was continued for three weeks and ultimately discontinued after the confirmation of SSPE diagnosis without clinical improvement on anti-tubercular drugs. No adverse drug reactions were observed during this period.

After six weeks of inpatient care, his clinical condition stabilized and he was discharged for palliative care. Three months later, he was readmitted with severe respiratory distress from aspiration pneumonia and died within 24 h of admission.

Discussion

SSPE is a rare but fatal complication of measles. While its incidence had markedly reduced following the implementation of robust immunization Programmes, recent years have seen a global resurgence of measles cases [16]. This case serves as a reminder of the devastating consequences of failing to eliminate this vaccine-preventable disease.

Our patient presented to care 17 months after the onset of symptoms, delayed by barriers to healthcare access and concerns regarding social stigma. This delay reflects patient level, health system and sociocultural factors common in resource limited settings. At the community level, limited awareness of SSPE and its early manifestations including myoclonus, behavior change and cognitive decline meant that the family did not recognize the initial symptoms as indicators of a serious neurological condition. At the health-system level, multiple visits to primary health facilities during the first 12 months of illness did not trigger referral for specialized neurological evaluation reflecting limited training of frontline healthcare workers and the absence of EEG and neuroimaging capabilities at peripheral facilities. Approximately 50% of the Zambian population is under 19 years old [17], yet the country has only three pediatric neurologists, meaning that children with progressive neurological conditions face significant barriers to specialist assessment. Financial constraints including transportation costs, out of pocket expenditure on specialized investigations and social stigma further compounded these barriers, as the family concealed the patient’s decline due to fear of discrimination.

The consequences of this delay were profound. The patient presented in stage 3 disease, while no curative therapy exists for SSPE, earlier diagnosis would have allowed for timely initiation of symptomatic management, nutritional support and physical therapy potentially preserving quality of life for a longer period. Early recognition would have enabled appropriate prognostic counselling and advance care planning for the family.

Our patient fulfilled the modified Dyken diagnostic criteria for SSPE, presenting with typical features including cognitive decline, myoclonus, and mutism. The diagnosis was supported by elevated measles specific IgG in serum and CSF with a positive antibody measles index of 3.55. Furthermore, the EEG pattern of periodic high-amplitude discharges on a slow background, coupled with MRI evidence of cortical atrophy and periventricular white matter lesions, confirmed advanced stage of the disease [1]. SSPE must be differentiated from autoimmune encephalitis, neoplasms, metabolic white matter disease, progressive myoclonic epilepsies and infectious etiologies such as tuberculosis in appropriate epidemiological settings. [6], [18] These conditions were all evaluated for in our patient and determined to be unlikely alternative diagnoses.

The decision to initiate empirical antituberculosis therapy in this patient, despite negative GeneXpert MTB/RIF warrants critical examination. In TB-endemic countries, empiric treatment is a common and sometimes guideline supported practice [19], particularly when clinical suspicion is high and diagnostic uncertainty persists. This case highlights the potential pitfalls of diagnostic anchoring in TB endemic settings; the cognitive bias of fixating on a prevalent diagnosis and interpreting subsequent findings through that lens. It can lead to premature closure on TB as the explanation for a patient’s presentation, delaying consideration of alternative diagnoses. The persistence of fever, muscle wasting, and chronic neurological decline which were likely attributable to advanced SSPE itself was reframed as evidence supporting TB, reinforcing the initial diagnostic anchor. Empiric antituberculosis therapy exposes the patient to potential drug side effects and may create a false sense of diagnostic security, reducing the urgency to pursue definitive diagnostic testing that would establish the correct diagnosis. When empiric therapy is initiated, it should be accompanied by a clear plan and timeline for diagnostic confirmation or de-escalation, and alternative diagnoses should be actively and concurrently pursued. In this case, the SSPE workup proceeded in parallel with empiric TB treatment and the correct diagnosis was ultimately established.

Early recognition of SSPE remains crucial for initiating supportive care and optimizing the patient’s quality of life. SSPE should be considered in the differential diagnosis for any child presenting with progressive cognitive decline, myoclonus, or new onset epilepsy in appropriate clinical and epidemiological context [14]. Investments in diagnostic infrastructure and training healthcare providers to recognize the clinical hallmarks of SSPE are essential, particularly in regions where measles remains endemic.

Conclusion

This case highlights the devastating consequences of delayed diagnosis in SSPE. The 17-months delay to presentation, driven by healthcare access barriers and social stigma, resulted in presentation at an advanced disease stage. The initiation of empiric antituberculosis therapy despite negative testing further illustrates how diagnostic anchoring in TB endemic settings can complicate the diagnostic process for rare neurodegenerative conditions. These findings underscore the urgent need to improve community and provider awareness of SSPE, strengthen referral pathways and diagnostic infrastructure, and adopt a systematic diagnostic approach when empiric therapy is used in high burden settings, ensuring concurrent pursuit of alternative diagnoses. Robust measles vaccination programs remain the most effective strategy to prevent SSPE.
