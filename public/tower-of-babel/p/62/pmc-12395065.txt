Multifaceted roles of mammalian heat shock factor 1 in the central nervous system
Authors: Nicholas B. Rozema, Rocio Gomez-Pastor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is a stress-protective transcription factor most associated with transcriptional regulation of genes involved thermal stress response and protein folding. The canonical activation cycle of HSF1, in which HSF1 recognizes a simple promoter binding site known as a heat shock element (HSE) to promote the transcription of molecular chaperones, has been well documented. However, it is now evident that mammalian HSF1 exhibits unexpected complexity and participates in the response to a vast array of cellular stress types. The versatility of HSF1 can be attributed to distinct local protein concentrations, posttranslational modifications (PTMs), and binding partners found in different anatomical regions of the mammalian system. Advances in our knowledge of HSF1 under different types of stress have illuminated its vast array of gene targets, ranging from protein folding to mitochondrial homeostasis to cytoskeletal stability and beyond. In this review, we explore current knowledge of mammalian HSF1 and its gene targets within the central nervous system. While HSF1 has been extensively studied in the context of neurodegeneration, our understanding of its diverse roles in this setting remains limited. We also highlight emerging evidence supporting a physiological role for HSF1 in the healthy brain, an area that has received relatively little attention. Advancing a more comprehensive understanding of HSF1 function in the mammalian brain may aid in the development of novel therapeutics aimed at alleviating symptoms across a range of neurological disorders.

Introduction

Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is a stress-responsive transcription factor commonly referred to as the master regulator of the cellular stress response whose primary function is the protection of cells from protein misfolding, aggregation, and apoptosis.1, 2, 3 It canonically governs the heat shock response (HSR), a highly conserved transcriptional program activated by the accumulation of misfolded or damaged proteins in the cytosol. This response triggers protective mechanisms, including the upregulation of heat shock proteins (HSPs) and other cellular stress-response genes. HSF1 has been shown to respond to a wide array of cellular stress types, including changes in temperature,4, 5, 6, 7 exposure to oxidants,4, 8, 9, 10 presence of metals,4 DNA damage,11, 12, 13 and metabolic dysfunction,14, 15 all of which induce protein misfolding and proteotoxic stress amongst other cellular insults. The original dogma for how HSF1 accomplishes its stress protective function is through the activation of protein chaperones and other protein quality control systems by recognizing a canonical heat shock element (HSE, TTCnnGAAnnTTC) in their target genes.16, 17, 18 The activation of these protective systems prevents protein misfolding, refolds misfolded proteins, and targets damaged proteins for degradation.19 However, it is now recognized that HSF1, and particularly mammalian HSF1, drives distinct transcriptional programs dependent on the context and stress conditions (Figure 1). These different transcriptional programs depend on local HSF1 protein concentration, HSF1 oligomerization state, posttranslational modifications (PTMs), and the presence of distinct binding and modifying partners within the cellular microenvironment determining HSF1's binding activity.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 This combination of determining factors has recently been referred to as the “Heat Shock Factor Code.”22 Moreover, recent evidence demonstrates that HSF1 regulates the expression of many different genes other than molecular chaperones, including genes essential to cell cycle regulation, glucose metabolism, inflammatory responses, cell wall and cytoskeletal stability, metabolism, and the development and maintenance of neuronal, reproductive, and sensory organs.25, 26, 27, 28, 29Fig. 1Unique stressors drive distinct HSF1 transcriptional programs. The continuous discovery of novel targets and roles for HSF1 beyond the HSR has illuminated the necessity to examine HSF1 across species and contexts to better understand its role in the HSR. Extensive and seminal work has been performed on the functions, interactions, and targets of HSF1 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Caenorhabditis elegans; however, mammalian HSF1 has exhibited increased complexity in its structure, DNA-binding selectivity, binding partners, and range of targets.3, 20, 21, 30, 31, 32 Moreover, this complexity is further exacerbated by the range of anatomical systems found in higher-order mammals containing distinct microenvironments that necessitate distinct transcriptional control under both physiological and diseased states. In this review, we will examine current knowledge regarding mammalian HSF1 with special attention to the central nervous system (CNS), an anatomical system in which HSF1 may play a role in driving cognitive deficits associated with aging and proteinopathies, as well as in basic neurodevelopment and structural maintenance of synaptic connections.

HSF1 in the CNS

HSF1 has been proposed to perform regulatory activity within the CNS for multiple processes essential for brain structure and synapse stability, neuronal differentiation and synaptic formation, as well as processes involved in aging, immune regulation, and stresses from insults such as cancer, metabolic stress, and neurodegeneration.30, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Neurons are post-mitotic cells with extremely high energy demand that necessitate a near-constant presence of protein quality control machinery to maintain proteostasis for proper cellular function. Moreover, the numerous proteinopathies of the CNS (eg, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease) demonstrate a propensity for the aggregation of proteins within neurons for which HSF1 and the HSR act as the first line of defense. It is currently hypothesized that HSF1 accomplishes this wide array of functions though differential regulatory programs that rely on distinct protein-protein interactions and PTMs that act to modulate HSF1 DNA-binding activity and stability.3, 22, 30, 31, 32, 38, 39, 40 Additionally, recent studies have reported that HSF1 protein levels and activity can be modulated by local release of neurotransmitters, such as serotonin.41, 42 This suggests that the combinatorial nature of HSF1 activity can be unique to particular microenvironments, indicating a plethora of novel roles and targets for HSF1 throughout the CNS. Many of these new emerging functions for HSF1 have been discovered by analyses in disease states using cell and/or animal models of various pathologies. However, a comprehensive understanding of the functions that HSF1 play in the mammalian brain, both under disease but also under physiological conditions, is still lacking. To that end, this review will examine the current state of HSF1 research in the CNS with particular emphasis on research relating to neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration, as well as emerging evidence for roles of HSF1 in non-stressed, physiological conditions. Such insights are needed to harness the actions of this pivotal transcription factor in both health and disease, paving the way for the development of innovative and effective strategies to manipulate HSF1′s actions.

HSF1 is essential for proper neurodevelopment

HSF1 is ubiquitously expressed in the developing brain,43 suggesting a need for HSF1 in neurodevelopment. Early evidence for the role of HSF1 in neurodevelopment came from studies of Hsf1-/- mice, where alterations in neuronal migration and differentiation were apparent.36 Mice lacking Hsf1 showed severe demyelination and loss of oligodendrocytes, the neuronal cell type responsible for myelin production. In addition, these mice presented with increased activated immune responses shown through astro- and microgliosis across multiple brain regions. Other studies of Hsf1-/- mice showed altered brain structure and impaired synapse formation. In the hippocampus, a brain region essential to learning and memory, lack of HSF1 reduced dendrite length and synaptic spine density37 driven by a lowered expression of polysialyated-neural cell adhesion molecule (PSA-NCAM), a protein fundamental to synapse formation. Finally, HSF1 also regulates the expression of brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), an important regulator of synaptogenesis.33, 44 Together, these findings have demonstrated that alterations in levels of HSF1 during embryogenesis result in significant impairment of neurodevelopment by both abnormal cellular differentiation as well as structural connectivity of synapses.

Beyond essential roles in normal neurodevelopment, HSF1 has also been shown to play a critical protective role in response to early prenatal stress. Specifically, exposure of Hsf1-/- mice to stress such as prenatal alcohol exposure or maternal epileptic seizure increased newborn susceptibility to neuropsychiatric disorders.43, 45 This phenomenon appears to be driven by differential PTMs on HSF1 under these conditions leading to reduced acetylation and sumoylation, possibly preventing its degradation. Additional effects on brain structure in these studies were related to interactions between HSF1 and another heat shock factor, HSF2, which may interact with and form multimers with HSF1 during its activation. HSF1-HSF2 heterotrimers act to downregulate doublecortin-like kinase 1 (Dclk1) expression, an important gene in neurogenesis and cell migration, that was impaired in mutant mice. Despite compelling evidence linking HSF1 to the expression of essential genes involved in brain development, a comprehensive understanding of the range of genes regulated by HSF1 under these conditions remains incomplete. Additionally, the precise mechanisms through which HSF1 governs this vital process throughout life are still not fully elucidated.

HSF1 as a therapeutic target in proteinopathies

Perhaps the best understood conditions of HSF1 in the mammalian CNS come from studies of neurodegeneration. Because of its central role in responding to the accumulation of misfolded and/or damaged proteins, HSF1 is an obvious choice for neurological disorders derived from aggregated, toxic proteins (ie, proteinopathies) and its role in various diseases has been widely documented.30, 33, 38, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53 Numerous reports have shown that HSF1 protein levels are reduced in neurodegenerative diseases;30, 54, 55, 56 however, due to variability in primary toxic protein species, regional and cellular susceptibility to cell death, and symptomatology between neurogenerative diseases, the cause of decreased HSF1 levels and its roles in combating disease pathology may vary from disease to disease. It is important to note that impairment in HSF1 is not causative for neurodegenerative disease, but instead loss of HSF1 appears to exacerbate the protein misfolding and aggregation already present. This could be caused in part by an overall depletion of the chaperone pool which results in decreased protein quality control essential to normal neuronal function. Due to the nature of proteinopathies, it would be expected to have an activated HSR increasing the levels of HSP and other protein chaperones in response to the accumulation and aggregation of misfolded proteins. However, across many different neurodegenerative diseases an overall decrease in HSP expression has been observed, indicative of a faulty activation of the HSR that could be due to alterations in HSF1 function or stability.54, 57 The specific contributions of HSF1 to neurodegenerative diseases have been extensively reviewed previously.20, 21 In this review, we will briefly discuss the contributions of mammalian HSF1 in Huntington’s disease (HD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and Parkinson’s disease (PD) (Figure 2). HSF1 in HD

HD is a devastating and fatal autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by a polyglutamine (polyQ) repeat expansion in exon 1 of the huntingtin gene (HTT).58 This expansion causes production of an expanded mutant HTT protein (mtHTT), leading to both a loss and gain of function and propensity for misfolding and aggregation into inclusion bodies.59 HD symptomatology is characterized by progressive functional impairments both in motor coordination, bradykinesia, and rigidity, as well as in cognitive performance.60 Although HD is classified as a rare condition, it stands out as one of the diseases with extensive research focused on the regulation of HSF1.

Early studies of HSF1 in the context of HD and other heritable polyQ expansion diseases showed impaired expression of HSF1 target genes, particularly protein chaperones and anti-apoptotic proteins.54, 57, 61, 62, 63 Additional target genes affected primarily related to cytoskeletal organization, focal adhesions, and the activity of GTPases, reflecting an early indication of non-canonical gene targets for HSF1 within the CNS. Knockout of Hsf1 in a mouse model of HD resulted in increased brain aggregation of HTT and a shortened lifespan,64 while expression of a constitutively active form of HSF1 alleviated aggregate burden and increased lifespan.65 Recent evidence has demonstrated that changes in HSF1 activity in HD are driven by an abnormal degradation of HSF1 in neurons expressing mtHTT.30 This phenomenon appears to be driven by increased expression of the protein kinase CK2α’ and the E3 ligase Fbxw7 leading to HSF1 phosphorylation and ubiquitinylation and promoting its degradation through the ubiquitin proteasome system. Importantly, efforts to prevent HSF1 degradation in HD by deleting CK2α’ in mouse models of HD have resulted in amelioration of HD-like symptoms, highlighting the therapeutic potential of restoring HSF1 levels in HD.66

Beyond its role in the activation of the HSR, HSF1 has other potential implications in HD disease pathogenesis. Excitatory synaptic dysfunction is an additional, early hallmark of HD in which excitatory glutamatergic input into the striatum, the brain region most profoundly affected by mtHTT aggregation, is lost, leading to expression of both cognitive and motor impairments within the disease. A recent study demonstrated a potential link between the direct transcriptional regulation of the postsynaptic scaffolding protein PSD95 by HSF1 and loss of synaptic density34 having implications not only in HD, but also in other neurodegenerative diseases and in normative aging in which general declines in synaptic density are observed.67, 68, 69, 70 This relationship between HSF1 and PSD95 will be further discussed below (Cytoskeletal organization and synaptic maintenance). HSF1 may also play a role in anti-apoptotic signaling in HD models through a reciprocal regulation with tumor suppressor p53,11, 12, 13 a major transcription factor in mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Studies in an HD mouse model have linked stabilization of p53 through impairment of the E3 ligase Mdm2 with increased degradation of HSF1. Conditional deletion of p53 in striatal neurons of a HD mouse model restored HSF1 levels and ameliorated HD-related phenotypes.71 Loss of p53 regulation through the degradation of HSF1 has additional important implications for neuronal health more broadly due to its important role in promoting intracellular Ca2+ signaling and neuronal energy demand. Broader implications of the p53-HSF1 relationship on neuronal health and function will also be further discussed below (Regulation of bioenergetics and neurotransmission through mitochondrial genes).

HSF1 in AD

AD is the leading cause of dementia in elderly individuals. The etiology of the disease is not well understood with >95% of cases occurring sporadically. Histopathologically, AD is characterized by the aggregation of the amyloid-β (Aβ) protein into extracellular plaques and the microtubule-associated protein tau into intracellular neurofibrillary tangles. The primary symptomatology of AD is characterized by cognitive impairment linked to degeneration of the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, brain regions closely associated with learning and memory. There have been several studies reporting decreased levels of HSF1 in AD models and patients. Specifically, decreased HSF1 levels have been observed in neural-derived exosomes from AD patients46 and lower HSF1 levels have also been observed in multiple brain regions from rodent disease models.47, 49, 52 In addition, reports have shown elevated levels of the protein kinase CK2 in both mouse models and patients which correlate with disease progression72 suggesting that HSF1 may be phosphorylated and targeted for degradation in a similar manner to what has been reported in HD. Some indication of this occurring has been reported by decreased HSF1 levels in neural-derived exosomes from AD patients.46

Numerous studies have examined the efficacy of HSF1 targeted therapies in AD models. Pharmacological activation of HSF1 using HSP90 inhibitors in mouse models of AD have been shown to ameliorate synapse loss and memory impairment33, 73 thought in part to be due to increasing the expression of HSP70 and HSP25, but also through direct increases in the concentration of various pre- and post-synaptic proteins. In the same vein, intranasal administration of HSP70 and HSP25 to mouse models was also sufficient to ameliorate AD-related hippocampal and cortical neuron loss, decrease the accumulation of Aβ, and improve spatial memory. Finally, overexpression of HSF1 resulted in increased Purkinje cell density of the cerebellum, lower Aβ accumulation, and improved cognition.47, 48, 50, 52, 74 Together, these studies suggest that HSF1-targeting therapies may act to slow disease progression through additive benefits of aggregate clearance and strengthening of synaptic connections.

HSF1 in PD

PD is a multisystem neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor impairment such as rigidity and bradykinesia, as well as cognitive impairments in attention, working memory, and executive function.75 The hallmark of PD is the accumulation of the protein α-synuclein into intracellular Lewy bodies. Several studies have reported decreased levels of HSF1 in PD. Overexpression of α-synuclein in human cells resulted in HSF1 depletion, a phenomenon that has also been observed in other cell models of PD.76 Further, HSF1 is depleted in the midbrain of mice expressing transgenic α-synuclein and in patients with diffuse Lewy body disease. Abnormal degradation of HSF1 is linked to the E3 ligase NEDD4, whose levels are increased in PD models, leading to ubiquitinylation of HSF1 and its degradation by the proteasome. Interestingly, the protein kinase CK2 is also reported to be increased in PD, and although there is evidence connecting elevation of CK2 with phosphorylation of α-synuclein, a direct link between elevated CK2 levels and HSF1 degradation has not been made in PD.77, 78

HSF1-targeting therapies have also been successful in ameliorating PD-related symptoms in animal models. Early studies in a fly model of PD showed overexpression of HSP70 prevented α-synuclein inclusions and cell toxicity79 and expression of a constitutively active form of HSF1 in human cells modeling PD decreased inclusion formation and toxicity, partially through elevated levels of HSP70.80 Lastly, pharmacological activation of HSF1 in a rat model of PD also showed increased levels of HSP70 that prevented inclusion formation and additional protective effects such as decreased microglial activation and improved motor behavior.81 This evidence suggests that restoring or enhancing the levels of HSF1 also provides beneficial effects in the amelioration of PD-like symptoms.

Commonalities of HSF1 dysfunction in neurodegeneration and aging

The fact that HSF1 levels are decreased in numerous age-related neurodegenerative diseases is not necessarily surprising given the shared histopathology of these diseases linked to protein misfolding and aggregation. In fact, depletion of HSF1 extends beyond research of HD, PD, and AD, and has been observed in other polyQ expansion diseases like spinocerebellar ataxia type-2 (SCA2), type-6 (SCA6), and spinal bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA)55 as well as in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).82, 83

HSF1-mediated pathology may also begin prior to the observed decreases in HSF1 protein level in these diseases mediated by inactivating PTMs that drive alterations to the DNA binding capacity of HSF1. For example, phosphorylation of HSF1 at Serine-303 and -307, known to repress transcriptional activity, has been seen to increase in several HD models prior to HSF1 depletion.30 Inactivating PTMs are also common in aging. Elevation of phospho-Ser303/307 has also been observed in the hippocampus of aged mice, along with increased acetylation of HSF1 that inhibits its DNA-binding activity.84 Intriguingly, these PTMs occur significantly less frequently in the long-lived Ames dwarf mouse, in which HSF1 DNA-binding activity and transcriptional regulation is maintained into later stages in life, suggesting a role for HSF1 in aging-related changes within the CNS.84 Lastly, disruption in HSF1 activity can also be driven by recruitment of cofactors that modulate transcriptional activity. This has been previously observed in an analysis of the HSF1 interactomes in control, heat-shocked, and HD cells, which revealed recruitment of the cofactor CTCF in a stress-inducible manner that represses the interaction of HSF1 with specific target genes.24 CTCF is one example of stress-specific cofactor recruitment; however, it is likely that other HSF1 protein-protein interactions occur in a disease- and tissue-specific manner.

Therapies targeted to ameliorate HSF1 degradation in various disease contexts have been successful in several preclinical models of neurodegeneration, as discussed above. Commonly, researchers report alleviation of protein aggregation, most likely through increased expression of protein chaperones such as HSP70 and HSP25, alongside improvement in disease symptoms ranging from motor performance to cognitive improvement. However, it remains unclear whether symptom improvement is driven exclusively by decrease in the aggregate load of relevant pathological proteins. With the large number of failures in clinical trials of therapies that successfully alleviated aggregate load with little or no improvement to underlying symptoms, the toxicity of protein aggregates and the benefit to patients in clearing them remains contentious.85, 86, 87, 88 This does not, however, negate the robustness of findings that HSF1-targeted therapies are promising candidates for the treatment of proteinopathies broadly but instead highlights the potential combinatorial effects of these therapeutic strategies beyond alleviation of protein aggregation. Importantly, numerous studies have reported the effects of HSF1-targeted therapies in the alleviation of synaptic deficits and bioenergetic impairments.30, 33, 34, 71, 73, 89 The role of HSF1 in the regulation of genes associated with these functions within the CNS, unfortunately, remains understudied, particularly with emphasis on HSF1 activity under physiological conditions that would be invaluable to better understand therapeutic effects observed in disease states.

It is important to consider that the single greatest risk factor for the development or emergence of symptoms across neurodegenerative diseases is aging. During aging, there is a decline in many homeostatic processes, including the HSR, increased oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation. Regulation of these processes, at least in part, can be attributed to HSF1. Indeed, studies have shown that manipulations of HSF1 have profound effects on lifespan, where its knockdown accelerates tissue decline and shortens lifespan, and its overexpression increases lifespan in C. elegans.90, 91, 92 Age-related decline of HSF1 has also been observed in human lymphocytes and skin fibroblasts.93, 94, 95, 96 In the CNS, HSF1 also declines throughout adulthood, which is thought to contribute to inflammation and neuronal dysfunction typical of normative aging.97, 98 Intriguingly, a recent study in long-lived Dwarf mice showed elevated levels of HSF1 in brain tissue throughout lifespan compared to their wild-type counterpart, resulting in a more robust response to age-related stressors. This was hypothesized to be driven by differential PTM of HSF1 in the Dwarf mice that acted to both prevent HSF1′s degradation and maintain its interactions with gene targets.84 Taken together, studies in longevity indicate HSF1 activity is a key driver of neuronal health and homeostasis, potentially beyond its role in disease-related stress; however, the role of HSF1 in physiological tissues generally has not been well characterized, much less its activity in healthy CNS. The current knowledge on this topic will be discussed in the following section.

HSF1 in healthy CNS

The activity of HSF1 within healthy tissue is a significantly understudied area of research. The canonical understanding of the HSF1 activation cycle comes from studies of cellular growth under stressed conditions. Under normal cell growth conditions, HSF1 exists as an inactive monomer repressed by protein-protein interactions with some of its own targets, such as HSP40, HSP70, HSP90, and TRiC.99, 100, 101 When cells are stressed, HSF1 disassociates from its repressors, oligomerizes, and translocates to the nucleus to initiate transcription of stress response genes.102 This paradigm of activation by stress and inactivation under physiological conditions has dominated the HSF1 field. However, this model has been challenged by studies in mammalian tissues where the localization and activity of HSF1 appear to deviate from that canonical understanding of HSF1′s regulation. In a recent study of localization patterns in human tissue performed for both HSF1 and HSF2 in benign tissues demonstrated that both display subcellular localization within the cytoplasm and nucleus across tissue types, including within the CNS.103 This confirms early findings reporting basal as well as stress-dependent accumulation of various chaperones within synaptosomes (isolated nerve terminals) from rodents.104, 105 These findings may be interpreted in several different ways. Possibly, the mammalian CNS may exist at a constant level of low-grade stress sufficient to require a constant presence of HSF1 transcriptional regulation in the nucleus. Neurons are post-mitotic cells with extremely high energy demands needed in order to perform the signaling cascades required for every aspect of cognitive experience. For this reason, a regular transcription of molecular chaperones acting to prevent protein misfolding and traffic proteins to distal sites such as synapses would be essential for proper neuronal function. In fact, it has been previously shown that chaperones exert protective functions at the synapse level. Sole expression of HSP70 in cortical neurons was sufficient to provide protection against glutamatergic excitotoxicity and improve synaptic transmission.106, 107 Chaperone expression in the CNS most likely contributes to a certain amount of HSF1 activities, even at basal levels. Still, numerous studies have additionally implicated HSF1 in transcriptional control of non-canonical neuronal genes essential for cytoskeletal stability, synaptic formation and maintenance, and synaptic transmission (Figure 3). Cytoskeletal organization and synaptic maintenance

Evidence for HSF1 role in the regulation of synaptic genes initially came from studies of neurodegeneration and neurodevelopment. Hsf1-/- mice displayed a significant decrease in the number of dendritic spines and dendrite length in the hippocampus, which was attributed to a decrease in PSA-NCAM driven by HSF1 regulation of polysialyltransferase genes.37 Similarly, administration of the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG in a mouse model of AD enhanced HSF1 activation and increased the expression of the presynaptic proteins synapsin-1 and synaptophysin and the postsynaptic scaffolding protein PSD95.33 Similar results were observed through the use of another HSP90 inhibitor, OS47720, in a separate AD model,73 but direct regulation of these synaptic genes by HSF1 was not reported. However, HSEs have been identified in some synaptic genes such as Dlg4 (PSD95) and Dlg1 (SAP97), indicating the potential for direct transcriptional regulation by HSF1.33, 108 Later, the precise HSE site within the Dlg4 gene was identified through chromatin immunoprecipitation from mouse striatal tissue. Importantly, the murine HSE site was identical to the HSE found in the human DLG4 promoter region.34 This demonstrated for the first time a direct link between HSF1 and the expression of a key cytoskeletal protein that is essential for anchoring neurotransmitter receptors to the postsynaptic membrane and that this interaction occurs across the lifespan of wild-type mice.

Additional evidence for the role of HSF1 in synaptic stability stems from findings that HSF1 regulates lipid raft formation, subdomains of the plasma membrane that are necessary for the insertion of postsynaptic receptors key to learning and memory consolidation.109, 110 It can be hypothesized, therefore, that HSF1 may regulate additional synaptic genes directly under physiological conditions. The extent to which this occurs; however, has not been determined and should be explored to gain insight into the physiological roles of HSF1 within the CNS.

Regulation of bioenergetics and neurotransmission through mitochondrial genes

HSF1 may also influence neuronal health and function through regulation of proteins important for energy production and ion homeostasis. Mitochondria are essential organelles to synaptic transmission, participating in ATP synthesis, Ca2+ homeostasis, synthesis of glutamate, synaptic vesicle recycling, and the functional maintenance of dendritic spines.111, 112 When mitochondria are dysfunctional, neurons experience disrupted Ca2+ signaling, energy failure, and eventual cell death in a phenomenon termed excitotoxicity.113, 114 This can be caused by excessive glutamatergic signaling, whose postsynaptic potential is mediated by insertion of postsynaptic receptors (AMPARs and NMDARs).115 As discussed above, HSF1 plays a role in key cytoskeletal proteins that mediate the localization and insertion of these receptors into the postsynaptic membrane. Additionally, HSF1 has been shown to play a significant role in directly regulating mitochondrial activity116 in multiple mammalian tissues. Within the CNS, HSF1 has been linked to modulate mitochondrial membrane polarization.30, 54, 117, 118 Specifically, studies have linked HSF1 and mitochondrial function through a complex interplay between HSF1 and the transcription factors p53 and PGC-1α, both involved in mitochondrial functions. HSF1 directly regulates the expression of αβ-crystallin, a protein essential to one of the degradation pathways of p53.11, 30 Reciprocally, p53 regulates the expression of elements of the HSF1 degradation pathway, CK2α’ and FBXW7, a protein kinase and an E3 ubiquitin ligase, respectively.30, 119, 120 p53 is a major transcription factor in mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, and its relationship with HSF1 accounts for some amount of the anti-apoptotic effects of HSF1 transcriptional activity. HSF1 also binds to a non-canonical HSE present in the promoter region of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), a major transcription factor that regulates the expression of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes and participates in the regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis, ROS detoxification, and oxidative phosphorylation.14, 30, 54, 117, 118, 121, 122, 123 In addition, HSF1 was recently shown to be directly involved in the transcription of genes essential to the mitochondrial protein response through a signaling cascade involving HSP70 and HSP40 (DNAJA1) that interconnects the cytosolic and mitochondrial proteostasis networks.124 As has been emphasized previously, neurons rely on a precise balance of energy metabolism and production to meet the needs of their signaling function. Therefore, it is likely that at least one role HSF1 plays in healthy CNS is maintaining this balance to promote proper signal transduction. However, this role has not been fully explored under physiological conditions and is not yet fully understood.

Conclusion

Since the first description of HSF1 in 1984,125 there have been hundreds of studies that are integral to our current understanding of this multifaceted protein. However, much remains to still be discovered regarding the role of HSF1 in a range of circumstances and contexts in which novel targets and consequences are regularly observed. Only within the last 20 years has this research pushed into the complex interplay of pathologies that characterize neurological disorders. HSF1-targeted therapies remain promising candidates for the improvement of symptoms plaguing patients, and the field has made tremendous advancements in our understanding of HSF1 in diseased states. Nevertheless, many questions remain to be answered that may shed light on new pathways and avenues to improve treatments. Chief among these is a better understanding of the role HSF1 plays under physiological conditions within the adult brain. It is evident that HSF1 is essential to neuronal function from earliest neurodevelopment to death; however, we are hampered by insufficient characterization of these roles. To design effective therapies, a unified understanding of the multitudinous targets and downstream effects of HSF1 interactions with the genome is essential. Additionally, this must be accomplished across brain regions and ages, as it is evident from the data so far uncovered that the context in which HSF1 is found is paramount to the transcriptional paradigm it elicits. Robust understanding of HSF1 within the CNS will lead to effective therapeutics to treating disorders of aging and improve the lives of countless individuals.
