Prevalence and associated factors of methicillin-resistant and vancomycin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus among clinical isolates at Hawassa university comprehensive specialized hospital, Ethiopia, 2019–2023
Authors: Israel Tsige, Dawit Yihdego Reda, Marshet Kefeni, Yared Merid, Abel Abera Negash, Gaurav Kwatra, Ermias Sissay Balcha, Musa Mohammed Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) has become a major public health concern due to its increasing resistance to antibiotics. Methicillin and vancomycin-resistant S. aureus strains are considered as high-priority pathogens contributing to increased morbidity and mortality worldwide. There is limited data on the prevalence and trends of methicillin and vancomycin resistant S. aureus among clinical isolates in Hawassa, Ethiopia.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the prevalence, temporal trends, antibiotic resistance patterns, and associated factors of Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and Vancomycin-Resistant S. aureus (VRSA) among patients admitted to Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH) Hawassa, Ethiopia.MethodsA hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted from April 20 to June 27, 2024, involving patients diagnosed with S. aureus infections at HUCSH between 2019 and 2023. Stored S. aureus isolates were re-cultured and identified using standard biochemical tests. Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the disc diffusion method for cefoxitin and other antibiotics, and the Epsilometer test (E-test) for vancomycin. Socio-demographic and clinical data were extracted from the bacteriology culture registration book and medical records using a structured data collection format. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regressions were performed to identify predictors of MRSA infection, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsOf the 151 S. aureus isolates recovered, 17.9% (27/151), (95% CI: 11.9%-23.8%) were MRSA. The MRSA was most frequently isolated from abscesses (40.7%) followed by blood (29.6%) and other specimens (14.8%). All isolates were susceptible to vancomycin (MIC ≤ 2 µg/mL), with no VRSA detected. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was observed in 15.9% (24/151) of isolates. Resistance to clindamycin, erythromycin, and penicillin was 19.9%, 26.5%, and 89.4%, respectively. The highest MRSA prevalence was recorded in 2023 (n = 6; 26.1%), and the lowest in 2021 (n = 3; 8.8%). Patients admitted to the surgical ward had significantly higher odds of MRSA infection compared to the other wards (AOR: 6.55; 95% CI: 1.52%-28.13%; p = 0.01). There is a significantly higher prevalence of MRSA infections (AOR = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.103–2.974) among female patients as compared to male patients.ConclusionThe MRSA prevalence observed aligns with other studies conducted in Ethiopia. Temporal variations in MRSA prevalence underscore the need for continuous surveillance to identify contributing factors and inform targeted infection control strategies aimed at mitigating the spread of resistant S. aureus strains. There is a significant association between MRSA occurrence and admission to the surgical ward, highlighting the need for targeted infection prevention and control strategies in high-risk hospital wards. Unlike other studies, a higher prevalence of MRSA was observed among female patients, warranting further investigation to identify potential underlying factors.

Background

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a Gram-positive opportunistic pathogen responsible for a wide spectrum of illnesses ranging from mild skin lesions to life threatening conditions [1]. S. aureus is a leading cause of hospital-acquired bacteremia which can lead to severe complications including sepsis, endocarditis, and metastatic infections [2]. Additionally, it causes toxin-induced syndromes such as toxic shock syndrome and food poisoning [3, 4].

Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) represents a significant challenge due to its antibiotic resistance. MRSA strains have developed resistance to a broad range of antibiotics, including β-lactam antibiotics such as methicillin, dicloxacillin, as well as carbapenems and most cephalosporins [5–8]. Methicillin was initially effective in treating infections caused by S. aureus strains that had developed resistance to β-lactam antibiotics such as penicillin; however, MRSA was first identified in hospitalized patients in the 1960 s, marking a turning point in treatment efficacy [9, 10].

Following MRSA’s emergence, vancomycin became the primary antibiotic for treating severe S. aureus infections in the 1980’s [11, 12]. However, the widespread use of vancomycin particularly among critically ill patients, has led to the emergence of vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA) [13]. Vancomycin resistance is primarily mediated by the horizontal transfer of a plasmid-borne vanA gene transposon from vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus to S. aureus [5], crossing the genus barrier [5, 14].

The first clinical case of VRSA was reported in the United States by the Centers for disease control and prevention (CDC) in June 2002 [14]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the pathogenicity and resistance patterns of S. aureus pose a significant global health threat. VRSA and MRSA are important public health pathogens due to their potential to cause high mortality if appropriate treatments are not identified [15]. In 2019, S. aureus was responsible for over 250,000 deaths globally, ranking as the second leading pathogen associated with antimicrobial resistance related deaths [16]. MRSA infections are particularly concerning due to their antibiotic resistance profile, which contributes to prolonged illness, increased mortality, and higher healthcare cost [17]. A systematic analysis further highlighted the severity of the issue, estimating that MRSA accounted for more than 100,000 antimicrobial resistance-associated deaths globally in 2019 [16].

According to WHO reports, MRSA prevalence exceeds 20% across all WHO regions, with some countries reporting rates as high as 80% [18]. In Ethiopia, MRSA prevalence ranges from 13.2% [19] to 76.7% [20]. The global prevalence of VRSA is estimated at 1.5% with Ethiopian studies reporting prevalence between 5.1% and 14.1% [21–23].

Although several studies have assessed MRSA and VRSA prevalence in Ethiopia, including research conducted in Hawassa, most have focused on specific infection types or limited timeframes [22]. A previous study in Hawassa examined only wound infections [23]. There is limited data on MRSA and VRSA prevalence across diverse clinical specimens in this region. This study aims to address the gap by assessing the prevalence, temporal trends, antimicrobial resistance profile, and associated factors of VRSA and MRSA at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH), Hawassa, Sidama, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design and area

A hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at HUCSH Hawassa, Ethiopia from April 20 to June 27 2024. The study population included patients with a culture-confirmed S. aureus infection at HUCSH between 2019 and 2023. All patients with documented S. aureus-positive results in the bacteriology culture registration book, complete clinical and laboratory records, and available stored S. aureus isolates were included in the study. Patients with incomplete data were excluded.

Data collection methods

Data were extracted from bacteriology culture registration book and archived medical records using a structured data collection format. Socio-demographic and clinical information were retrieved from the hard copies of patients’ medical records. Corresponding S. aureus isolates were obtained from the HUCSH microbiology laboratory’s deep freezer, where they were stored at -60 °C in 20% glycerol-tryptone soya broth.

Thawed isolates were thoroughly mixed and inoculated onto blood agar plates (BAP), followed by aerobic incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Colony morphology was assessed, with typical S. aureus colonies appearing yellow to cream or white, measuring 1–2 mm in diameter, and often showing beta-hemolysis [24]. Preliminary identification was performed using Gram staining followed by confirmatory biochemical tests such as catalase and coagulase tests [24, 25].

Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using disc diffusion method according to CLSI 2023 guidelines [26]. In brief, AST was conducted for selected antibiotics, including clindamycin, erythromycin, penicillin, and tigecycline. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in 5 mL of normal saline and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard under appropriate lighting conditions. Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) medium plates were inoculated using sterile swabs in three directions for uniform distribution.

Cefoxitin discs (30 µg) were used to assess methicillin resistance. Discs were placed 15 mm from the plate edge and 25 mm apart [26]. A vancomycin Epsilometer test (E-test) was placed centrally on the inoculated MHA plates. Plates were incubated aerobically at 35 °C for 16–18 h. For the vancomycin E-test, incubation was extended to 24 h to detect heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (hVISA) or vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA) phenotypes, although VRSA was the primary target [26].

Zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters, and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value was determined from the E-test ellipse intersection. Interpretations followed CLSI 2023 standards [26]. Quality assurance measures included sterility and performance checks for media, gram staining, catalase and coagulase tests, and antibiotic susceptibility testing. S. aureus ATCC-25,923 and ATCC-29,213 served as quality control strains for disc diffusion and E-test, respectively.

Data processing and analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 23 software. Frequencies of independent variables were tabulated. Descriptive statistics included the overall proportion of MRSA and its proportion across the independent variables. Prevalence was calculated by year and by specimen type. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regressions were used to identify predictors of MRSA infection. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Operational definition

Multidrug Resistance (MDR): Defined as resistance to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes [27].

Results

Between 2019 and 2023, the Microbiology Laboratory of HUCSH processed a total of 12,065 clinical specimens. From these, 381 S. aureus isolates were identified from various clinical specimens. Of the total isolates, 189 (49.6%) were stored. After sub-culturing on BAP, 173 (91.5%) of the stored isolates were confirmed as S. aureus, while 16 were identified as coagulase-negative staphylococci. Among the 173 confirmed isolates, 22 (12.7%) were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete data such as missing ward information, admission date or clinical diagnosis. Accordingly, 151 (87.3%) isolates were eligible for final analysis.

Distribution of S. aureus over the five years

Between 2019 and 2023, the number of S. aureus isolates recovered at HUCSH ranged from 23 to 36 annually. Over this period, a declining trend in the proportion of S. aureus isolates was observed. The highest prevalence was recorded in 2019, followed by 2021, 2020 and 2022, with the lowest in 2023 (Fig. 1).

Prevalence of MRSA and VRSA and their trends over five years

The overall prevalence of MRSA among the 151 S. aureus isolates was 27 (17.9%) (95% CI: 11.9–23.8%). All isolates were susceptible to vancomycin (MIC ≤ 2 µg/mL), and no VRSA was detected. The prevalence of MRSA varied annually, peaking in 2023 (6/23; 26.1%), and reaching lowest in 2021 (3/34; 8.8%) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Trend of MRSA at HUCSH from 2019 to 2023

Distribution of MRSA across socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

The prevalence of MRSA was higher among female patients (n = 13; 28.9%) compared to male patients (n = 14; 13.2%). Age-wise distribution revealed the highest MRSA proportion in patients under 8 years of age (n = 12; 23.1%), followed by those aged 9 to 25 years (n = 9; 16.7%). MRSA prevalence was 5 (21.7%) among outpatients. Systemic infections showed highest prevalence of MRSA (n = 5; 27.8%), followed by skin and soft tissue infections (n = 15; 16.1%). Antibiotic resistance profile of S. aureus

Among 151 S. aureus isolates recovered, 27 (17.9%; 95% CI: 11.9–23.8) were resistant to cefoxitin, indicating MRSA. All isolates remained susceptible to vancomycin and no VRSA isolates were identified. Resistance to clindamycin and erythromycin was observed in 30 (19.9%) and 40 (26.5%) isolates, respectively. A high proportion of isolates (n = 135; 89.4%) showed resistance to penicillin. In contrast, all S. aureus isolates were fully susceptible to tigecycline (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Antimicrobial Resistance profile of S. aureus isolated at HUCSH from 2019 to 2023 (n = 151)

Lower MIC values were observed in a small number of isolates: 0.5 µg/mL in 2 isolates (1.3%) and 0.75 µg/mL in 4 isolates (2.6%). Factors associated with MRSA

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of MRSA was 17.9% (95% CI: 11.9–23.8%), closely aligning with the 17.5% reported at Yekatit 12 Hospital in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [28]. This similarity suggests a consistent burden of MRSA across certain healthcare facilities in Ethiopia. Moreover, our findings are comparable to the pooled MRSA prevalence among the elderly (14.69%) [29] and healthcare workers (13.6%) [30]. This consistency may reflect shared risk factors such as host susceptibility, microbial virulence, and infection control practices across diverse populations and settings. The alignment across studies indicates the significance of MRSA as a persistent public health concern in both community and healthcare environments.

The MRSA prevalence observed in our study is lower than that reported from other regions of Ethiopia, where MRSA prevalence has ranged from 27% to 57.1% [22, 31–33]. It is also lower than figures from countries such as Iran (53.7%) [34], Afghanistan (56.2%) [35], Kenya (27.8%) [36], and India, with pooled prevalence of 37% [37]. These differences may be attributed to factors such as geographic and demographic variations, differences in laboratory methodologies, and whether S. aureus isolates were clinical or colonizing strains. Additionally, factors such as study design, inclusion criteria, study duration, and duration of healthcare exposure can influence reported prevalence. MRSA prevalence often tends to be higher in hospital settings, particularly among patients with prolonged stays or those undergoing invasive procedures [38].

No VRSA was detected in our study, consistent with a previous study from Hawassa, Ethiopia, which also reported no VRSA using the E-test method [23]. However, our findings contrast with studies by Daka [39] and Deyno et al. [40], who reported VRSA prevalence of 40.4% and 74.2%, respectively, in the same region. A meta-analysis further highlights this difference, reporting a VRSA prevalence of 1.5% [21]. It is important to note that studies from Hawassa primarily used the disk diffusion method, which may overestimate VRSA prevalence.

Additional discrepancies are observed when compared to international data. For instance, a study from Lahore, Pakistan reported a 14% VRSA prevalence using broth microdilution [41], while research from Osogbo, Nigeria found a prevalence of 1.4% using the E-test [42]. A recent study in Ethiopia [22] using automated systems like VITEK-2 Compact and BD Phoenix M50 reported a VRSA prevalence of 5.1%. These variations in findings may be due to differences in laboratory methods (e.g., VITEK vs. E-test), study populations (e.g., ICU patients vs. general population), or other contextual factors that warrant further investigation. In this study we have used E-test due to unavailability of broth microdilution in our setup. The use of the E-test, rather than the gold standard broth microdilution method, for VRSA detection may have affected our findings. Importantly, S. aureus can lose antibiotic resistance genes including those carried on plasmids over time, especially during long-term storage in a deep freezer. This may explain the absence of VRSA among the isolates stored at HUCSH in our study.

The proportion of MRSA among outpatients was higher, 21.7% than that of inpatients, 17.2%. This finding contrasts with a study from other part of Ethiopia [43], where MRSA prevalence was higher among inpatients (72.8%) than outpatients. Similarly, a study in Turkey [44] reported majority of MRSA (85.1%) were recovered from inpatients. On the other hand, similar to our study, a study in Hawaii noted an increase in MRSA infections among outpatients [45]. A possible explanation for the higher prevalence of MRSA among admitted patients is their increased susceptibility to hospital-acquired infections due to prolonged exposure to the healthcare environment.

The prevalence of MRSA in the surgical ward was 32.3%, which is higher than the prevalence reported in other studies from Ethiopia (21.1%) [33] and Malaysia (8.53%) [46] but lower than 60% reported from Cameroon [47]. The higher prevalence in the surgical ward may be attributed to factors such as frequent invasive procedures, postoperative antibiotic use, and increased risk of surgical site infections. Surgical patients often experience extended hospital stays, comorbidities, and open wounds which facilitate MRSA colonization and transmission. Environmental contamination and breaches in aseptic technique may also contribute to its spread.

All MRSA isolates in this study were susceptible to vancomycin, consistent with findings from Afghanistan [35], Pakistan [48], Malaysia [49], Turkey [44], and Ethiopia [23, 43]. However, this is higher than the 94.7% susceptibility reported from Kenya and 81.2% reported from other studies in Ethiopia [22, 42]. Our findings suggest that vancomycin remains an effective treatment option for MRSA in our setting.

All MRSA isolates were also susceptible to tigecycline, consistent with findings from Iran [50] and Kenya [37]. Tigecycline susceptibility findings are clinically relevant for managing infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria including MRSA. When in vitro susceptibility is confirmed, tigecycline is a valuable treatment option, particularly for complicated intra-abdominal, skin, and soft tissue infections. In vitro susceptibility testing helps guide therapeutic decisions when few or no alternative antibiotics are effective [51].

However, clinical interpretation of tigecycline in vitro susceptibility testing must consider its pharmacokinetic limitations. Tigecycline achieves low serum concentrations, making it less suitable for bloodstream infections and certain types of pneumonia. Despite in vitro susceptibility, clinical outcomes may be suboptimal in infections where adequate drug levels cannot be achieved. Additionally, resistance may develop during therapy, so its use should be cautious and guided by severity, infection site, and other available alternatives [51].

In our study, high resistance was observed for clindamycin, erythromycin, and penicillin, with penicillin resistance reaching 89.4%. This finding has public health implications, underscoring the recommendation to avoid penicillin for empirical therapy.

Multivariable analysis revealed that patients admitted to the surgical ward were significantly more likely to develop MRSA infections (AOR = 6.55; 95% CI: 1.52–28.13). This finding is consistent with a study from Hawassa, Ethiopia [52], which reported that surgical ward admission increased the risk of MRSA colonization by 12-fold (AOR = 12.2; 95% CI: 0.98–152.38). The higher odds of MRSA among admitted patients may be due to increased risk of hospital-acquired infections associated with wounds, implants, invasive procedures, and prolonged hospital stays, any of which can lead to MRSA infection. High prevalence of MRSA among patients in the surgical ward highlights the need for strengthened infection prevention and control measures in the ward.

In our study, a significantly higher prevalence of MRSA infections was observed among female patients (AOR = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.103–2.974). While many studies have reported higher MRSA prevalence among males [53], our findings align with reports suggesting that females may, in certain contexts, exhibit increased infection rates [54]. This gender disparity may be attributed to several factors. Females may often have more frequent contact with healthcare settings such as through prenatal care, gynecological services, or caregiving roles which increase their risk of healthcare-associated infections, including MRSA. Social and behavioral factors, such as differences in hygiene practices and a greater likelihood of seeking medical care, may contribute to this trend. Biological factors, including hormonal influences on immune response and differences in skin microbiota, could further affect susceptibility to MRSA. For instance, a study in Yemen found higher MRSA carriage among females with gingival infections, attributing this to oral hygiene behaviors and healthcare utilization [54]. In contrary, studies from other regions [53, 55] report higher prevalence among males, likely reflecting variations in exposure, behavior, or healthcare access. These findings highlight the need of additional studies to identify the biological and social factors responsible for these differences.

This study indicated a fluctuating pattern in MRSA prevalence over the five-year study period, with a decline from 25% in 2019 to 8.8% in 2021, followed by a notable increase to 26.1% in 2023. This trend suggests that MRSA prevalence is dynamic and may be influenced by many factors. The initial decline could be attributed to improved infection prevention and control measures, increased awareness among healthcare workers, or more effective antibiotic stewardship initiatives. It may also align with public health interventions, such as enhanced hygiene practices implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have indirectly reduced MRSA transmission. However, the rise of MRSA in 2023 could be due to a relaxation of these measures or emerging challenges in antimicrobial resistance control and management. For example, resource constraints, inconsistent adherence to infection prevention and control measures protocols, or shifts in antibiotic prescribing behavior could contribute to the revival of MRSA. Additionally, the increase may reflect changes in the circulating MRSA strains or possible outbreaks.

This fluctuating pattern contrasts with a retrospective study from Hawaii, which reported a steady increase in MRSA prevalence from 24% in 2000 to 30% in 2002 [45]. These differing trends highlight that MRSA epidemiology is often context-specific, shaped by local healthcare infrastructure, community transmission patterns, antimicrobial use, and the effectiveness of hospital-based infection control programs.

These observations indicate the importance of continuous, localized surveillance and appropriate response strategies to effectively monitor and manage MRSA. Continuous training of healthcare workers, strict adherence to infection prevention protocols, identification of outbreaks, and regular review of antibiotic prescribing practices are vital components for MRSA control.

Limitation of the study

This study had several limitations. First, not all S. aureus isolates identified between 2019 and 2023 were included due to the loss of some isolates, and inadequate documentation restricted the inclusion of the whole intended study population. These constraints, along with small sample size may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Second, as a retrospective study relying on secondary data, we were unable to collect comprehensive independent variables necessary to assess all potential factors associated with MRSA prevalence. The reliance on outdated records may also affect data accuracy, potentially limiting its relevance to current prevalence of MRSA and VRSA in the study area. Third, the use of the E-test method instead of the broth microdilution technique due to its unavailability in our setup may have influenced the accuracy of our susceptibility results. Fourth, the use of stored isolates instead of fresh ones may have affected the detection of VRSA and MRSA. Fifth, the limited number of drugs tested within each antibiotic class may have impacted the accuracy of our multidrug resistance (MDR) finding.

Conclusion

The prevalence of MRSA found in this study is comparable to other studies conducted in Ethiopia. Although no VRSA was detected, the potential for emergence remains a concern, underscoring the importance of continued surveillance and prospective molecular level studies in Hawassa. This study demonstrated a significant association between MRSA occurrence and admission to the surgical ward, highlighting the need for targeted infection prevention and control strategies in high-risk hospital ward. In contrast to reports from other countries, a significantly higher prevalence of MRSA was observed among female patients in this study, warranting further investigation to identify potential underlying factors.
