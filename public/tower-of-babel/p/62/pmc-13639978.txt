Generation of induced pluripotent stem cell (JHUi009-A) and isogenic control (JHUi009-A-1) lines from a patient with vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS) harboring a pathogenic c.2456G > A in COL3A1
Authors: Stanley Chun Ming Wu, Deborah DiSilvestre, Gordon F. Tomaselli, Kenneth R. Boheler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We generated and validated a vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS) human iPSC line, JHUi009-A, carrying a pathogenic COL3A1 c.2456G > A variant, and a CRISPR/Cas9-edited isogenic control line, JHUi009-A-1. Reprogramming was performed on expanded erythroid progenitor cells using an integration-free Sendai virus system. Both lines showed typical pluripotent colony morphology, expressed stemness-associated transcription factors, retained a normal 46, XY karyotype, and differentiated into derivatives of all three germ layers. These hiPSC lines differentiated into vSMCs, with mutant vSMCs showing higher contractile marker expression than the corrected control, supporting their use as an in vitro model of a COL3A1-associated vascular phenotype.

Resource utility

The COL3A1 mutant hiPSC line and gene-edited isogenic control offer a cell-matched platform for investigating disease mechanisms in vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. These cells are useful for vascular lineage differentiation and for assessing mutation-related alterations in matrix biology, cytoskeletal organization, contractile phenotypes, and responses to candidate interventions.

Resource details

COL3A1 encodes the pro-α1 chain of type III collagen, essential to the structural support of vascular and visceral tissues. Pathogenic COL3A1 variants are the major cause of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS), a connective tissue disorder associated with marked fragility of arteries and other internal organs. The clinical course of vEDS is complicated by life-threatening events like arterial dissection, bowel perforation, and uterine rupture (Adham et al., 2021). Spontaneous vascular events are particularly concerning because they may arise in the absence of overt aneurysm formation (Cherecheanu et al., 2023). Despite improvements in diagnosis and surveillance, the biology underlying arterial fragility in vEDS remains incompletely understood. Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have become a powerful platform for modeling inherited disorders, through preservation of patient-specific genetic information and differentiation into multiple lineages relevant to disease. Here, we established human iPSC lines from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) obtained from a patient with vEDS carrying a heterozygous COL3A1 c.2456G >A variant (Gly819Asp) and an isogenic control.

Both the patient-derived line JHUi009-A/JHU004 clone 3 (C3) and gene-corrected line JHUi009-A-1/JHU004 clone F6 (F6) exhibited conventional hiPSC morphology, with compact colonies and prominent nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios (Fig. 1B). The stemness state of both lines was supported by positive immunostaining for NANOG and OCT4 (Fig. 1B). The heterozygous COL3A1 c.2456G > A variant in C3 and the wild-type sequence at the targeted COL3A1 site in F6 after gene editing were verified by Sanger sequencing; however, the allelic status (homozygous) is undetermined for the isogenic control line (Fig. 1A, Table 2). Genomic integrity, with a normal 46, XY karyotype, was confirmed by KaryoStat analysis (Fig. 1D, Supplementary File). The cell lines were Sendai virus free (Fig. 1C). Directed lineage induction showed that RNA encoding stemness-related proteins decreased with differentiation, while transcripts associated with endoderm (SOX17, GATA4, FOXA2), mesoderm (HAND1, TBXT, NKX2–5), and ectoderm (OTX2, PAX6, SOX1) increased (Fig. 1F). These results confirm the potency of both lines. Mycoplasma testing by PCR was negative (Fig. 1E), and short tandem repeat (STR) profiling ensured the identity and stability of the cultures. Stemness validation results are summarized in Table 1 and are available either through the journal or at hPSCreg.

As a proof-of-concept, both iPSC lines successfully differentiated into vascular smooth muscle cells (vSMCs), using established protocols (Cheung et al., 2012; He et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2025; Wu and Boheler, 2026). Immunostaining of vSMCs derived from both lines confirmed the presence of calponin 1 (CNN1) and transgelin (TAGLN), consistent with smooth muscle differentiation. However, vSMCs derived from the mutant line displayed increased signal intensity for these markers when compared with the corrected F6 line, similar to our published findings with line JHUi007-A-1 (Wu and Boheler, 2026). In parallel, phalloidin staining of vEDS vSMCs indicated an increase in stress fiber formation, thickness and organization relative to gene-corrected controls (Fig. 1G). Together, these observations confirm the suitability of using JHUi009-A and JHUi009-A-1 hiPSC lines to investigate how COL3A1 mutations may influence the phenotypes of vascular cells in vitro.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reprogramming

Isolated PBMCs were expanded as erythroid progenitor cells in complete serum-free medium as described previously (Chua et al., 2025). Cells were reprogrammed with CytoTune™ Sendai viral vectors encoding the Yamanaka factors (Hall et al., 2024). Newly established, independent colonies with hiPSC-like morphologies were isolated and transferred onto Geltrex-coated plates. Cells were maintained in Essential 8 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), passaged using Accutase and Y-27632 ROCK inhibitor every 4 days and cultured under standard humidified conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). (Huang et al., 2025).

CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing

COL3A1 gene editing was performed by Synthego, Inc. on p37 hiPSCs using electroporation of ribonucleoprotein complexes containing recombinant SpCas9, chemically modified synthetic guide RNA, and repair template. Genomic DNA was collected 48 h post-electroporation, and target loci were analyzed by PCR, Sanger sequencing, and ICE (Inference of CRISPR Edits; Synthego). Edited clones were isolated by limiting dilution into 96-well plates (<1 cell per well) and expanded. Cells were screened by the PCR–Sanger sequencing–ICE genotyping workflow. Off-target assessment in this study was limited to vendor-provided in silico guide evaluation, and no additional off-target validation was performed.

Sequencing

DNA was extracted from clones using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and sequenced (Johns Hopkins Genomics Core).

Characterization of hiPSC clones

Chromosomal integrity was confirmed by KaryoStat + analysis (ThermoFisher Scientific) using p37 JHUi009-A and p43 JHUi009-A-1 hiPSCs. Stemness markers were assessed in JHUi009-A (p39) and JHUi009-A-1 (p44) by immunocytochemistry. Cells were fixed and incubated with primary antibodies diluted in Antibody Diluent (ThermoFisher Scientific), followed by species-appropriate secondary antibodies (Table 2). Nuclei were visualized with DAPI, and images were collected using a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope. Trilineage differentiation was induced with the STEMdiff™ Trilineage Differentiation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) on JHUi009-A (p42) and JHUi009-A-1 (p45). RNA isolation was carried out using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN), and reverse transcription was performed with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression analysis was then conducted by quantitative PCR using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with primers listed in Table 2.

STR analysis

DNA (Method 3.2) was collected from JHUi009-A (p38) and from JHUi009-A-1 (p44) hiPSCs. STR testing was completed by the Johns Hopkins Genetics Research Core Facility. STR profiles were compared across loci to support authentication and relatedness of the parental and edited clones.

Sendai virus and mycoplasma detection

The absence of Sendai virus was evaluated in JHUi009-A (p37) and JHUi009-A-1 (p43) hiPSCs by immunocytochemical staining with the antibodies listed in Table 2. Mycoplasma testing was performed on samples at the same passages using the Venor®GeM Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Sigma-Aldrich).

vSMCs differentiation

vSMC differentiation was performed as previously described (Cheung et al., 2012; He et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2025; Wu and Boheler, 2026). Differentiated cells were replated on gelatin-coated coverslips (Millipore Sigma), maintained for 5 days, and fixed for immunostaining (see Table 2).
