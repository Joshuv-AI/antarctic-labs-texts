The effect of severe renal impairment on the pharmacokinetics, safety and tolerability of mitiperstat
Authors: Chandrali S. Bhattacharya, Patricia Ely Pizzato, Maria Heijer, Mikael Sunnåker, Julie Holden, Monika Trebski, Karin Nelander, Hodan Ali, Diyan K. Genov, Malin Aurell, Anna Collén, Hans Ericsson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimsMitiperstat is a novel, highly potent myeloperoxidase inhibitor being evaluated in patients with cardio‐metabolic disease (phase 2). These patients often have impaired renal function, which may affect mitiperstat pharmacokinetics. This study assessed mitiperstat pharmacokinetics, safety and tolerability in participants with severe renal impairment and normal renal function, to inform inclusion of participants with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 in phase 3.MethodsParticipants with severe renal impairment (eGFR ≥15 and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2) who were not on dialysis (n = 10) and group‐matched controls (eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2; n = 10) received a single mitiperstat 2.5 mg oral tablet. Blood samples were collected at intervals for 2 weeks and urine samples for 24 h post‐dose.ResultsTotal apparent mitiperstat clearance was 10.83 L/h in the severe renal impairment cohort and 25.62 L/h in the control cohort. The area under the plasma concentration–time curve was 2.37‐fold higher (90% confidence interval [CI]: 1.79, 3.12) in the severe renal impairment cohort than in the control cohort, with longer elimination half‐life and similar maximum concentration. Non‐renal clearance was similar between the cohorts.ConclusionsMitiperstat apparent clearance was approximately twofold lower in individuals with severe renal impairment than in those with normal renal function. Lower clearance was driven by reduced renal clearance; non‐renal clearance was similar. Mitiperstat was generally well tolerated by participants with severe renal impairment and normal renal function. These findings, together with efficacy and safety/tolerability data from phase 2b, will guide the dosing regimen for phase 3.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease remains a major cause of death in industrialized countries, despite significant recent advances in cardiology. 1 Beyond optimal control of risk factors such as blood pressure and lipid and glucose levels, drugs targeting novel molecular pathways may improve outcomes in high‐risk patients by exerting direct beneficial effects on macrovascular and microvascular structure and function. 2

Myeloperoxidase is a highly abundant protein mainly present in the azurophilic granules of neutrophils, constituting 5% of the cellular dry weight. 3 Multiple lines of evidence suggest that myeloperoxidase is involved in cardiovascular diseases, including heart failure and atherosclerosis. 4 , 5 Plasma myeloperoxidase levels independently predict the risk of myocardial infarction and other major adverse cardiac events. 6 , 7 In chronic heart failure, elevated plasma myeloperoxidase levels are associated with more advanced disease and can predict poor clinical outcomes. 8

Mitiperstat is a highly potent myeloperoxidase inhibitor in clinical development for the treatment of heart failure, non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Myeloperoxidase inhibition with mitiperstat is hypothesized to improve microvascular and macrovascular function in patients with heart failure. 9 , 10 , 11

The pharmacokinetics, safety, tolerability and effect on myeloperoxidase activity of single (5–405 mg) and multiple (5–45 mg) ascending doses of mitiperstat have been investigated in healthy volunteers in previous phase 1 studies. 14 , 15 , 16 With the exception of generalized maculopapular rash, mitiperstat was generally well tolerated in healthy participants.

Previous results show that mitiperstat is rapidly absorbed after administration of an oral suspension, with approximately dose‐proportional exposure (at dose range mentioned above), 14 , 15 and that elimination occurs both via multiple metabolic pathways and via renal excretion. 16 , 17 In a human mass balance study, following oral administration of 10 mg mitiperstat solution, 32–44% was excreted unchanged in the urine. 16 Because renal excretion contributes to overall elimination, exposure to mitiperstat may be increased in patients with renal impairment. The population of patients with cardiovascular disease who may benefit from mitiperstat includes elderly patients with multiple comorbidities, including poor renal function. 18 The present study evaluated the effect of severe renal impairment on the pharmacokinetics, safety and tolerability after single oral administration of mitiperstat 2.5 mg.

METHODS

This was a single‐dose, open‐label, non‐randomized, parallel‐group study of the pharmacokinetics of mitiperstat (AZD4831) in participants with severe renal impairment and group‐matched participants with normal renal function (Figure 1). The design followed regulatory guidance on the investigation of pharmacokinetics in patients with impaired renal function. 20 , 21

Study design. BMI, body mass index, eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate (measured using the CKD‐EPI formula). 19

Conduct

The trial was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki, with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice, with all applicable regulatory requirements and with the AstraZeneca policy on bioethics. The Ethics Committee for Clinical Trials, Bulgaria, approved the trial. The study was registered (EudraCT: 2019–003498‐25; ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04949438). All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment.

Participants

Eligible participants were male or female, of non‐childbearing potential and aged 18–80 years, weighed at least 50 kg and had a body mass index (BMI) of 18–35 kg/m2. In the severe renal impairment cohort, participants had to have an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of at least 15 to less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 at screening (measured using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration [CKD‐EPI] formula), 19 with stable renal function (i.e. no clinically significant change in eGFR within 3 months or longer prior to study screening, as determined by the investigator) and on stable doses if taking medications with cardio‐renal activity (i.e. if participants are on statin, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers, beta‐blocker, diuretic or on any other cardiorenal relevant treatment, no dose adjustments were allowed within 2 weeks prior to screening). Patients who were on dialysis or had undergone kidney transplantation were excluded. In the group‐matched control cohort, eligible participants had an eGFR of at least 90 mL/min/1.73 m2. Participants in the control cohort could not be more than 10 years younger than the youngest participant or more than 10 years older than the oldest participant in the severe renal impairment cohort, and could not have a BMI more than 20% below the lowest BMI or more than 20% above the highest BMI of participants in severe renal impairment cohort. The proportion of male and female participants was to be balanced across cohorts.

Planned enrolment was approximately 10 participants per cohort. No formal sample size estimation was performed. The planned sample size was considered to be adequate to meet the study objectives.

Procedures

Screening took place during a 21‐day period before the start of the study. Enrolled participants were admitted to the study centre on the evening of Day −1, fasted overnight, received a single mitiperstat 2.5 mg oral tablet on Day 1, and were discharged at least 24 h after dosing. Participants then visited the study centre on Days 3, 5, 8, 11 and 15. Blood samples were collected before dosing and 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 96, 168, 240 and 336 h after dosing. Pooled urine samples were collected from 0 to 24 h after dosing. Safety was evaluated at all post‐dose study visits.

Mitiperstat quantitation

Plasma and urine samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis within specified time periods. Mitiperstat concentrations were determined using liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry following liquid–liquid extraction for plasma and sample dilution for urine according to validated and published methods. 14 , 15 , 16 The intra‐batch and inter‐batch precision for both the plasma and urine method validations, reported as coefficient of variation (CV), were well below the accepted ≤15% (≤20% at LLOQ) at all levels. Intra‐batch and inter‐batch bias were well within the accepted 15% (±20% at LLOQ) of the nominal concentration at all levels. The lower limit of quantitation of mitiperstat was 0.2 nmol/L in plasma and 20 nmol/L in urine. Results from in‐study quality control samples (CV ≤ 9.3% and inter‐batch bias −8.5% to 8.3%), calibration standards and incurred reanalysis samples indicated acceptable assay performance. All study samples were analysed within the established stability periods, 433 days and 439 days in plasma and urine, respectively.

Pharmacokinetics

Mitiperstat plasma and urine pharmacokinetic parameters were estimated by non‐compartmental methods using Phoenix WinNonlin v8.2 (Certara Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA). Parameters included maximum concentration (C max), time to C max (t max), elimination half‐life associated with terminal slope of a semi‐logarithmic concentration–time curve (t ½λz), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) from time zero to infinity (AUC∞) and to last measurable concentration (AUClast), and apparent volume of distribution (Vz/F). Renal clearance was the ratio of the quantity excreted in urine to AUC from time zero to 24 h. Non‐renal clearance (CLNR) was the difference between total apparent clearance (CL/F) and renal clearance (CLR).

Pharmacokinetic parameters were analysed using a linear model with the natural logarithm of AUC∞, AUClast and C max as response variables, renal function cohort as a fixed effect, and assumption of equal variance in the two cohorts. Ratios of geometric means of the above pharmacokinetic parameters and associated two‐sided 90% confidence interval (CI) were estimated for the severe renal impairment cohort vs. the control cohort. Regression analysis used individual participants' baseline eGFR values as the independent variable and their CL/F values as the dependent variable.

Safety

Safety was evaluated in all participants who received mitiperstat. Evaluations included the incidence, intensity and severity of adverse events and serious adverse events, monitoring of vital signs, electrocardiography and laboratory tests. Maculopapular rash was an adverse event of special interest.

RESULTS

Participants

Twenty participants were enrolled in the study, 10 in the severe renal impairment cohort and 10 in the group‐matched control cohort (Figure 1). Participants' mean age and BMI were 57.4 years and 28.8 kg/m2 in the severe renal impairment cohort and 56.8 years and 30.0 kg/m2 in the control cohort (Table 1). All participants were White and there were five male and five female participants per cohort. Mean eGFR was 21.9 mL/min/1.73 m2 (range, 15–28) in participants with severe renal impairment and 97.2 mL/min/1.73 m2 (91–104) in control participants (Table 1). All participants received mitiperstat and were included in all analyses.

Pharmacokinetics

Mitiperstat was rapidly absorbed following administration of a 2.5 mg oral tablet, followed by apparent biphasic decline in plasma drug concentrations (Figure 2). C max and t max were similar in participants with severe renal impairment and control participants with normal renal function (Table 2). Total apparent clearance (CL/F) after oral dosing was approximately halved in participants with severe renal impairment vs. control participants. Exposure to mitiperstat, as assessed by AUC values, was consequently approximately twofold higher in participants with severe renal impairment than in control participants. Given the lower CL/F and the similar Vz/F, the t ½λz was prolonged in participants with severe renal impairment vs. control participants, with geometric mean values of approximately 131 h and 50 h, respectively (Table 2).

Safety

Adverse events were reported in 3/20 participants (15%) (Table 3). Headache was reported in one participant in the severe renal impairment cohort and one participant in the matched control cohort. Nausea was reported in one control participant. All adverse events were mild in intensity and resolved without further intervention within 1 day. None were considered related to mitiperstat by the investigator. There were no serious adverse events or deaths. There were no adverse events of maculopapular rash. No clinically significant trends or shifts from baseline were reported for laboratory results, vital signs or electrocardiographic findings.

DISCUSSION

Mitiperstat is a highly potent myeloperoxidase inhibitor in development for treatment of heart failure, NASH and COPD. Comorbid renal impairment is common in patients with these conditions, and can influence the safety and efficacy profiles of drug therapies. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 The pharmacokinetics, safety and tolerability of mitiperstat have been well characterized over a wide dose range in single (5–405 mg) and multiple (5–45 mg) ascending dose studies in healthy participants. With the exception of generalized maculopapular rash, mitiperstat has been well tolerated. 14 , 15 , 16 In this phase 1 study, total apparent mitiperstat clearance following an oral dose of 2.5 mg was approximately twofold lower in participants with severe renal impairment (eGFR of ≥15 to <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, not on dialysis) than in matched control participants with normal renal function (eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2). Consequently, mitiperstat exposure (AUC) was approximately twofold higher and half‐life was prolonged. The slower total apparent clearance was mostly attributable to an approximately sixfold reduction in renal clearance. Safety and tolerability data were consistent with previous studies and there were no new findings of concern. 10 , 14 , 15 , 16 No events of maculopapular rash, an adverse drug reaction for mitiperstat, were reported. Mitiperstat had a generally acceptable safety and tolerability profile, as expected from previous studies. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Overall, these findings support enrolment of patients with renal impairment in phase 2b–3 clinical studies of mitiperstat.

Renal mitiperstat clearance in control participants was similar to values observed in previous studies, at approximately 14 L/h. 14 , 15 , 16 This renal clearance is greater than the product of the previously published unbound plasma mitiperstat fraction and healthy glomerular filtration rate (0.34 × 7.5 L/h = 2.6 L/h), 16 , 29 suggesting that urinary mitiperstat excretion involves not only glomerular filtration but also tubular secretion. A trend towards decreased renal clearance was observed in healthy volunteers receiving single oral doses of mitiperstat of 45 mg or higher, 15 which could indicate saturation of renal drug transporters. In a separate study, no change in renal clearance at steady state was observed in healthy volunteers receiving oral mitiperstat doses up to 15 mg per day. 14 In vitro renal transporter profiling indicated that mitiperstat is not a substrate of organic anion transporters 1 or 3, organic cation transporter 2, or multidrug and toxin extrusion 1 or 2 k (OAT1, OAT3, OCT2, MATE1, MATE2k; AstraZeneca data on file).

In addition to reducing renal excretion, kidney disease may also affect non‐renal disposition of drugs that are metabolized in the liver and intestine, for example by downregulation of cytochrome P450 activity. 27 , 30 The disposition of mitiperstat involves several metabolic pathways and possibly enterohepatic recycling in addition to the major renal route of elimination. 16 , 17 The non‐renal clearance was only slightly lower in patients with severe renal impairment than the observed non‐renal clearance in the control cohort (severe renal impairment, 7.9 L/h; control, 7.2 L/h). Assuming the non‐renal mitiperstat elimination represents the metabolic route, the present findings therefore indicate that this route is preserved in participants with renal impairment. Also, these findings agree with results from the human mass balance study, in which renal clearance contributed to the overall elimination with 32–44% of the mitiperstat dose recovered unchanged in urine. 16 , 27 , 30

A limitation of this study is that it did not include participants with mild or moderate renal impairment, but only those with severe renal impairment, plus matched control participants. This was in accordance with regulatory guidance on the use of a “reduced pharmacokinetic study design” in participants with renal impairment. 20 , 21 It represents the “worst‐case” clinical scenario and allows assessment of the greatest potential impact of impaired renal function on pharmacokinetics but limits the numbers of patients with renal impairment evaluated and to further explore the relationship between the CL/F vs. baseline eGFR over a broad eGFR range. In previous studies, the pharmacokinetics of mitiperstat have been approximately dose‐proportional over a wide range. 10 , 14 , 15 , 31 In the present study, regression analysis indicated a linear relationship between renal function and total apparent clearance, suggesting applicability of the findings across both a range of doses and eGFR range evaluated. However, the relationship was derived without inclusion of any patients with mild or moderate renal impairment and small numbers of patients with severe renal impairment. Nevertheless, the regression analysis suggested an approximately twofold AUC increase in mitiperstat exposure in a patient with an eGFR of 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 compared with a patient with normal renal function (eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2). An exclusion criterion of <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 was also applied in the phase 2a SATELLITE study. 10 Thus, no dose adjustment based on renal function is applied in the ongoing ENDEAVOR study (exclusion criterion of eGFR ≤30 mL/min/1.73 m2). 11 Population pharmacokinetic analyses as part of phase 2b–3 trials including patients with renal impairment will be used to further characterize and confirm the impact of renal impairment on mitiperstat exposure. To further elaborate, this twofold increase in AUC in participants with severe renal impairment (compared with matched controls) will have to be put in the context of efficacy and safety readouts from the phase 2b–3 ENDEAVOR study (mitiperstat studied at two dose levels, 2.5 mg and 5 mg) to further guide subsequent dosing recommendation in this and other relevant patient populations.

Mitiperstat exhibits moderate plasma protein binding of approximately 65%. 9 Plasma protein binding is often altered in patients with impaired renal function, 32 , 33 but this was not assessed in the present study. For drugs with low plasma protein binding (e.g. <80%), pharmacokinetic changes resulting from alterations in protein binding in patients with impaired renal function are generally expected to be small relative to those in patients with normal renal function. 20 The similar volume of distribution between the severe renal impairment and control cohorts indicates that the distribution of mitiperstat was not altered, in agreement with the above expectation.

In conclusion, mitiperstat had a generally acceptable safety and tolerability profile according to the results of this study and as reflected in previous studies. 10 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The mitiperstat exposure (AUC) was approximately twofold higher in participants with severely impaired renal function than in group‐matched control participants. The findings from this study, together with efficacy and safety/tolerability data (from ENDEAVOR or other phase 2b–3 data in a relevant target population) will guide the dosing regimen for phase 3 and future development of mitiperstat in patients with various degrees of renal impairment.
