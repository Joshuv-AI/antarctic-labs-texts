General practice trainee, supervisor and educator perspectives on the transitions in postgraduate training: a scoping review
Authors: Michael Tran, Joel Rhee, Wendy Hu, Parker Magin, Boaz Shulruf
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransitions are a period and a process, through which there is a longitudinal adaptation in response to changing circumstances in clinical practice and responsibilities. While the experience of the transition in medical student learning and in hospital-based specialty training programmes are well described and researched, the experience of the transition in community-based postgraduate general practitioner (GP) training has not been described comprehensively.ObjectiveWe aimed to identify, and categorise, the formative experiences of transitions in GP training and their impacts on personal and professional development.DesignWe adopted Levac et al’s scoping review methodology. Of 1543 retrieved records, 76 were selected for data extraction. Based on a combined model of the socioecological and multiple and multi-dimensional theories of transitions, data relating to the experiences of transitions were organised into contextual themes: being physical, psychosocial, organisational culture and chronological.Eligibility criteriaEmpirical studies focused on general practice trainees or training, that discussed the transitions experienced in general practice training and that were published in English were included.Information sourcesPubMed, MEDLINE and Web of Science databases were searched in January 2024 with no date limits for empirical studies on the transition experiences of GP into, and through, training.ResultsOur findings describe context-dependent formative experiences which advance, or impede, learning and development. Time is a significant modulator of the factors contributing to more negative experiences, with some initially adverse experiences becoming more positive. Identification of the inflection point that represents a shift from initially adverse to more positive experiences of transitions may help moderate expectations for learning and performance at different stages of training.ConclusionChallenges in training can either advance development and contribute positively to professional identity formation and clinical competency, or detract from learning and potentially contribute to burnout and attrition from training programmes. These findings will assist future research in identifying predictive factors of positive and adverse experiences of transitions and may strengthen existing and nascent GP training programmes. The findings are transferable to other community-based specialty training programmes.

Introduction

General practitioner (GP) trainees are exposed to repeated changes in contexts and responsibilities as they traverse the medical education continuum1 2 from training as junior doctors, to training to be independent, specialist GPs. Transition describes an ongoing process of psychological, social and educational adaptation over time due to changes in context, interpersonal relationships and identity.3 The transitions experienced by GP trainees occur in the context of shifting geographies, workplaces, career foci, roles and responsibilities, colleagues, and peer groups.246

Definition of transitions in GP training

Definitions of transitions have not always been clearly elucidated in the extant literature. A nuanced definition recognises ‘transitions’ as both a noun and a verb. A composite definition of the transition in GP training is thus one of both a period in time, as well as a process of change and transformation. It begins at the start of postgraduate GP-specific specialist training and ends with commencement of independent specialist GP practice (with completion of licensure examinations and fellowship of a general practice college). Over this period, there is a longitudinal adaptation in response to the changing circumstances of practice and responsibilities, with the adoption of the professional identity, interpersonal relationships and competencies, both clinical and non-clinical, associated with working as, and being, a GP. Competency frameworks in general practice will suggest that this endpoint represents attainment of an appropriate level of clinical competence and adoption of professional identity, to enable independent work in general practice. In reality, the transition, being a continuous process, would extend beyond the end of the GP training period. This is outside the scope of this review.

Historically, transition periods have been associated with detrimental levels of stress and negative emotions.1 More recent research accepts transitions as inevitable and necessary periods in medical training, suggesting that such experiences are imbued with opportunity for further development.1 The transition into undergraduate medical programmes, from medical student to junior doctor, and onwards to specialist practice, particularly in institutional settings such as hospital-based practice, has been explored in previous research. The transition experienced by community-based GP trainees remains under-researched and represents a gap in knowledge. General practice training is characterised by unique clinical and professional environments with exposure to a wide breadth of problems, chronic disease management, independence in decision-making, time pressure, uncertainty, limited availability of resources and supervision, and differing practice systems. Furthermore, clinics and practices are often geographically dispersed with most training occurring in small, usually private, practice in a strongly apprenticeship-like model. GPs are the polymaths of physicians, with an ever-increasing scope of practice7 and increased exposure to complex medical and surgical care,8 especially in areas with a maldistributed GP workforce.9 There is potentially greater workload, autonomy and responsibility,10 burnout7 and appreciable risks of occupational violence.11

It is unclear precisely what factors affect the personal and professional development of general practice trainees as they transition through training and into independent practice. Also not understood is the best way to manage the tension between creating opportunities for progressive clinical independence as a learning mechanism, and managing the negative effects including burnout and the dysfunctional effects of other psychological sequelae.12

Understanding the impacts of various factors in transition will help to distinguish between positive formative struggles within the transition process that advance learning, and adverse processes that impede learning and development. This understanding will help identify factors that could be the targets for further support and intervention, to optimise the balance of influences on the training experience. The existing body of literature is diverse in breadth, methodology and outcomes measured. What is lacking is a synthesis of the experiences in GP training, where the interplay of these experiences and modifying factors can be appreciated in the context of the whole transition period. This scoping review aimed to clarify concepts, identify knowledge gaps13 and form a baseline synthesis of the understanding of transitions in GP training to inform an agenda for further research.

Identifying the research question

This scoping review focused on the following research question:

What are the reported experiences, and impact on personal and professional development, of postgraduate trainees in general practice training during the transition towards independent specialist general practice?

Answering this question will help identify a range of formative experiences in transitions in GP training, including those which are challenging but positive, and those which are detrimental to personal and professional development. Furthermore, the enablers and barriers of positive, formative experiences of transitions are likely to be elucidated. Situating these experiences and influencing factors within broader psychological, educational, social and developmental contexts will also provide a framework to better understand, and potentially improve, the transition process in GP training.

Methods

It is not the aim of this scoping review to critically appraise individual studies and assess bias.14 Nevertheless, a structured and logical approach to data collection and analysis ensures the rigour of the reported outcomes.15 In considering a broad range of study types, with heterogeneous approaches to exploring and reporting the experience of transition a similarly broad initial scoping strategy was adopted.16 This scoping review is conducted following the five stages as described by Levac et al: (a) identifying the research questions17; (b) identifying the relevant studies; (c) study selection; (d) charting the data and (e) collating, summarising and reporting results.18

Framework for evaluating transitions in GP training

The experiences of general practice training were described within physical, psychosocial, organisation cultural and chronological contexts.2 19 Within each context, we extracted data about the transition in terms of positive, formative experiences that advance development and adverse, detrimental experiences that impede progress. Using this framework allowed a structured evaluation of the factors influencing the experience of transitions in GP training to inform theory-driven research and the transferability of findings. The framework used is a combination of the multiple and multi-dimensional theory (MMT) of transitions2 and socioecological model.19 The former suggests that the processes of task mastery, role clarification and acculturation20 occur across physical (including workplace and home), organisation cultural (including organisational structure and pedagogical processes), psychological (including cognitive experiences and adjustments) and social (including work role and interpersonal relationships) domains.2

Adaptation of the socioecological model identifies the immediate physical and psychosocial environments of trainees as constituting the microsystem, and the interactions among these environments as the mesosystem. Broader social, political and economic influences comprise the exosystem and these themselves are influenced by the macrosystem which includes general beliefs, attitudes and culture within general practice, the wider medical community and the communities they serve.21 The chronosystem encompasses the dimension of time as it relates to personal and professional development.21 There is a cross-over of several domains of the socioecological model and the MMT of transition. Intercalating the two models results in a combined framework of the socioecological model and MMT of transition suggests that in the transition, the processes of task mastery, role clarification and acculturation each are experienced across multiple physical, psychosocial, organisational culture and chronological domains. Identifying relevant studies

Three databases, MEDLINE, PubMed and Web of Science, were searched from their respective dates of inception until 1 May 2024. The search strategy, determined through team discussion, was constructed using search strings composed of Medical Subject Heading terms and text terms related to transitions in general practice training, combined using Boolean operators.

A sample search strategy for PubMed is outlined in box 1. We used no date limits and conducted the search on 1 May 2024. Final search results were limited to 1990 onwards to ensure contemporaneousness of the review.

Collating, summarising and reporting results

Title abstracts were screened by the primary investigator (redacted at editor’s request) against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full-texts of included title abstracts were retrieved and (redacted at editor’s request) assessed the full-texts for review inclusion. The first author (redacted at editor’s request) thematically analysed and extracted data to answer the research question and meet the objectives of this scoping review. Study population and outcomes were narratively summarised. Content analysis of the study outcomes enabled development of explanatory schema from thematic analysis. EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) was used to download the bibliographic details of studies yielded from the database searches and duplicates were deleted. The research team (redacted at editor’s request) reviewed and discussed the findings. Thematic analysis was iterative and refined through team discussions. The team consists of a combination of clinicians, researchers and medical educators. The findings represent our interpretations of the extant literature, with experiences and perspectives not always explicitly described by researchers in the context of transitions.

Patient and public involvement

For this scoping review, patients and the public were not directly consulted, however, the decline of the general practice workforce contextualises the research question as being socially significant. Research involving general practice trainees and medical educators has also informed development of the research question. The authors (redacted at editor’s request) have lived experience of these roles.

Positive and adverse formative experiences of transitions in general practitioner (GP) training

Positive and adverse factors in transitions in GP training

The initial thematic analysis distinguishes between experiences of transition that, though difficult, are positive and formative, and those that are adverse and detrimental to trainee progression.

Positive experiences of transition

Geographical variation, developing new communities of practice with like-minded individuals with common goals, growing support networks and developing confidence in working with different patient populations were positively reported experiences.4951 An improved sense of social standing within communities that were being served contributed to an ongoing desire to work within these communities.41 52 53 This contrasts with the experiences of GP trainees in hospital environments where, once they had left that environment, there was a stronger sense of purpose and being valued by the local community.5153

Transitioning into the GP style of work, with flexibility, work-life balance, continuity of care for patients, diversity of patients and medical issues and greater autonomy was reported as being a challenging, yet positive experience.4951 5456 Managing diagnostic uncertainty highlighted to many trainees the importance of a good therapeutic relationship between doctors and patients and the need to adapt to a GP-specific style of clinical reasoning and patient management.51 53 57

Adverse experiences of transition

Geographical isolation was often associated with social and professional isolation. Burnout was common and factors associated with this included low self-efficacy beliefs, intolerance of uncertainty, high workload, difficult patients, burned-out and difficult colleagues, feeling undervalued, being subject to harassment, not achieving a good work-life balance, having difficulties with emotional regulation, being a parent and being older.2425 28 40 5861 Personal relationships often suffered during GP training, with isolation from friends and family while rotating to different practices.46

GP trainees reported pervasive perceptions of having low social and professional prestige.54 59 62 63 This perception was present prior to, during and at the completion of training. Only in the context of a supportive community of practice and with good social standing among communities being served, did this negative perception explicitly appear to change. In addition, trainees felt unsupported and devalued in hospital rotations where they reported intimidation, harassment and discrimination because of their career choice.266163 Trainees express that they were treated like ‘second class citizens’37 when undertaking low-utility hospital-based rotations which they were given when identified as not having a career trajectory that matched hospital-based expectations. This resulted in negative professional relationships with hospital-based specialists.266164 Similarly unproductive relationships were also encountered with fellow GPs who themselves were experiencing burnout and overwork and who were not able to provide supervision and constructive advice to trainees.50 59

The flexibility of working arrangements and the diversity of patient presentations were negatively perceived when they resulted in increased workloads, being denied autonomy, lack of supervision and physical and emotional exhaustion.46 50 59 65 Many trainees were unprepared for non-clinical tasks such as scheduling, organising payroll and insurances and running a business.26 33 35 49 63 66 67 Managing diagnostic uncertainty and adopting the ‘wait and see’ approach and style of clinical reasoning was often perceived negatively by trainees.4957 6870 Diagnostic uncertainty is an inevitable,71 salient72 and defining feature of general practice. Intolerance of uncertainty is higher in GPs73 and in settings where access to advice is reduced.74 Hospital-based training provides the opposite environment, where intolerance of uncertainty is reduced.75

Enablers and barriers of formative experiences of transitions

The use of a combination of the multiple and multidimensional transitions model and the socioecological model enables a better understanding of the relationships and contexts that affect the experience of transitions. What is also observed, are those factors which can potentially predict, or enable, a more positive transition, and those which are barriers to this experience.

Enablers of positive, formative experiences of transition

Having structured arrangements for supervised practice in training and ongoing education was important and increased trainee confidence.55 This was also observed with remote supervision, independent of geographical proximity, provided there was a good relationship between supervisor and trainee.76 Greater time spent in supervised practice was associated with increased confidence and coverage of deficits and gaps in clinical skills.6667 69 7779 The provision of organised formal teaching, separate to clinical work, was also conducive to learning while training.26 31

The confidence and adaptability of trainees are proposed as indicators of overall preparedness for independent specialist practice.80 Adapting to their role as a GP while training helped to improve self-efficacy and confidence.30 49 62 63 65 68 Confidence and adaptability improved with time spent in training and coincided with a strengthening professional identity and sense of role development within a community of practice.67 69 77 78 81 82 Establishing this professional identity also helped develop productive relationships with non-GP specialists.47 49 52 62 Where families could move with trainees and when there were adequate employment and schooling/childcare opportunities, there was increased social support and professional satisfaction for the GP trainee.51 53

Sharing knowledge and having positive role models helped trainees navigate the clinical, ethical and professional questions that inevitably arose during training.29 62 66 76 While many GP trainees felt unprepared for non-clinical tasks, being involved in, and learning about management and leadership, where possible, was empowering.47 67 77 80 83

GP trainees felt better prepared with greater time spent in training as there was greater opportunity to benefit from mentorship, address clinical skills deficits and focus on non-clinical tasks related to general practice.66 67 69 77 78 84 Adaptation and confidence improved with time spent in training,3063 65 6769 77 78 80 with an attenuation of burnout.42 A complementary finding was that the very start of GP training was reportedly the most challenging point of the transition process,23 47 68 associated with tension, anxiety, frustration and uncertainty.85

Studies conducted towards the end of, or after the completion of, training presented a more holistic view of the training process that balanced positive, formative, experiences and adverse ones. The initial struggles with low self-efficacy,65 managing diverse clinical conditions and patients in different practice locations67 83 and navigating clinical uncertainty57 58 86 were tempered over time spent in training with the realisation of a stronger professional identity35 49 51 52 67 69 77 87 and clinical competence.30 33 63 66 80 There was no consensus on how long a training programme should be to fully prepare trainees for independent practice.

Barriers to formative experiences transition

Being unable to adapt to GP work with respect to the consultation, patient characteristics, workload and responsibility for ongoing patient care was associated with an intolerance of uncertainty, burnout and loss of motivation.58 88 Working with unsupportive peers and supervisors exacerbated feelings of professional isolation, from both peers and supervisors.59

Further barriers to formative experiences in training occurred when there was understaffing, poorly organised rotations,32 39 89 90 an imbalance in patient presentation types, and where teaching and supervision could not be adequately provided.27 39 46 52 This was observed more within nascent general practice programmes and faculties.

Interplay of positive and adverse experiences of transitions in general practice training

Many of the same factors explored in this review were perceived both positive and negatively. The context in which factors were encountered and the enablers or barriers present was important as this influenced whether it was perceived positively or negatively. For example, diversity of patient presentations and the workload of general practice was well-received by those in supportive communities of practice with like-minded colleagues, but resulted in burnout and exhaustion in those with unhelpful or burned-out colleagues. Trainees towards the end of, or having completed, their training had balanced views about the impact of different factors on their transition experience. They were more likely to appreciate the positive formative qualities of their training experiences and the impact on confidence and adaptability. It is acknowledged that this population represents trainees who have not dropped out of training and so their perspective is likely to be positively biased. There is limited available data regarding the attrition rate of GP trainees and understanding the perspective of this population would be invaluable.

The use of a combination of the sociocultural model and multiple and multidimensional model of transitions allows for an understanding of the context, that is, the dynamic relationships between personal and environmental factors21 that occur in transitions. Some factors were both positive, and adverse, depending on the context and timepoint at which they were experienced. Not all factors began positive, but many of these improved with time which identifies it as a significant modulator of the training experience. Identifying the inflection point where experiences may change from adverse to positive, may help moderate expectations for learning and performance at different stages of training. The combined model of transitions allows charting of various perceptions that change with time, including those that improve, and those that remain adverse. Perceptions that improve with time include a reduced reliance on physically proximate supervision (task mastery, physical domain), becoming more adept at managing one-on-one consultations (task mastery, physical domain) and an improvement in tolerance of uncertainty (task mastery, psychosocial domain). There is an improvement in the adaptation to GP roles and work (role clarification, psychosocial domain), as well as a reduction in social and professional isolation (acculturation, physical domain) and increased development of professional networks (acculturation, psychosocial domain). Perceptions which remained adverse with time included low social and professional opinion of the profession (acculturation, psychosocial domain) and inadequate business and administration knowledge and skills (task mastery, organisational culture).

It would be overly simplistic to define an ‘ideal’ transition as simply the absence of negative experiences, and being prepared for all aspects of the training programme and graduating confident for work as an independent specialist. A more realistic developmental perspective views the transitions undertaken in training as challenging, but part of the formative process can be harnessed to provide motivation for adaptation with growth in confidence in both clinical and non-clinical tasks and eventual realisation of professional identity as a GP. While the training process is universally recognised as a challenging one, a common goal for all stakeholders (including trainees, supervisors, educators, policy-makers and researchers), will be to acknowledge and account for these known challenges as part of a formative process with the eventual realisation of professional identity and clinical competency in working as a GP. Achieving this goal will mean mitigating or eliminating challenges that have no redemptive or formative qualities.

Limitations

This initiative for this research came from MT whose background as an inner-city GP graduate of an apprenticeship-style of training informs and potentially may bias the research. Authors JR, WH and PM are academic GPs with experience in clinical practice and medical and health professions education research. BS has a background in psycho-educational assessment in higher education. The experiences and perspectives of the authors as clinicians and researchers from within and outside general practice provide conceptual and interpretive depth to the research findings through consideration of alternative viewpoints and evaluation of the literature.

A scoping review is necessarily broad, at the expense of depth, and does not include assessment of bias of included studies. The wide focus of the scoping review also means it will be inevitable that some relevant literature will not have been identified. This scoping review represents an interpretation of relevant findings from the existing and available literature, but the included study list is not exhaustive.

Further research

The task for further research and policy development will be to find the balance between positive and adverse factors in the transition experienced with training, to develop the optimal environment for positive formative transitions. Much has been explored regarding the negatives of the transition process and few previous studies have adopted the appreciative inquiry approach or examined positive factors related to transitions. Reframing the negative perceptions of inevitable experiences and challenges in the transition process as formative opportunities will be challenging,1 especially if they cannot be readily or entirely eliminated or mitigated with supervision, support and other existing strategies. Identifying the inflection point which is a negative factor in the transition process acquires formative qualities that may be beneficial in helping trainees to navigate this period of their career. It will also allow for greater structural and organisational support to be provided at critical points in general practice training.

Few previous studies were quantitative in nature, with the majority being qualitative. Fewer yet measured the outcome from any intervention in the transition process, apart from extending training programmes. There exists a dearth of longitudinal studies assessing the experiences of trainees undergoing the transition process. Further assessment of the issues and themes explored in this scoping review would include identifying which are modifiable and potential targets for an intervention, and which are inherent, unchangeable components of the transition experience.

An understanding of the transitions experienced in community-based general practice91 may be transferable to other community-based specialties including allied health physicians, primary care staff and hospital-based non-GP specialists who may be working in private rooms or in geographical isolation with training and learning consequences in comparable social milieus to general practice.92 Countries with existing general practice training systems may benefit from further refinement of their training programmes to address potential sources of trainee loss and overall retention issues.93

Conclusion

The context in which many training factors are experienced contributes to their being perceived positively or negatively. Meaningful and supportive engagement with supervisors, positive and like-minded communities of practice and being able to spend quality time in training potentially contribute to positive perceptions of the challenges of GP training. Strengthening these contextual factors would optimise the environment in which formative transitions may occur. Identifying the inflection points at which initially negative factors change from having negative impacts on the transition process to being more beneficial to readiness-to-practice independently, will help assist trainees in navigating this challenging period of their career.
