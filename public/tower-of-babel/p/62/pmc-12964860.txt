Have changes in practice in response to COVID-19 been sustained? A qualitative study of experiences and perceptions of health system stakeholders in Colombia
Authors: Simon Turner, Dennys Paola Fernandez Gonzalez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA wealth of qualitative research has been published on initial organizational responses to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Research shows that a variety of changes in practice were introduced by health services internationally to address the challenges presented by the pandemic. These include the accelerated implementation of so-called service innovations, interventions to protect health system workers, admitting a role for trial-and-error approaches to decisionmaking, participation in international networks of information and expertise, combining so-called soft and hard coordination mechanisms and drawing on intersectoral collaborations to support the health system. However, less is known about the sustainability of responses.MethodsFocus groups and one-to-one interviews were conducted with 18 stakeholders within the Colombian health system from November 2021 to March 2022. Participants were derived from a broader set of 118 interviews to assess initial responses to COVID-19 in Colombia during 2020. Qualitative data collection was informed by a topic guide that examined the validity, acceptability and sustainability of six recommendations on the basis of initial changes in practice observed in response to the pandemic in 2020. Focus group data were analysed thematically by two researchers applying a coding framework.ResultsThe findings show that initial changes in practice, and identified gaps in the response, have varied regarding their sustainability. Gaps remain concerning the evaluation of accelerated innovations and formalizing learning from processes of trial-and-error experimentation. While coordination mechanisms within the health system, across sectors and over international networks have persisted, these often rely on informal coordination (e.g. self-nominated individuals) or precarious hard agreements among provider organizations that require institutional support to be sustained. Interventions to safeguard the healthcare workforce tend to rely on proactive behaviour by individual organizations; coordinated action at the local system and national levels is required to provide consistent support to the workforce.ConclusionsMany of the challenges identified with Colombia’s initial responses to COVID-19 in 2020 have persisted. Health research system investment is needed to support intimate working relationships between researchers and health provider and commissioning organizations to support the translation of informal, trial-and-error learning, often undertaken at the coalface of policy decisionmaking into validated, sustainable practices to which researchers can contribute ongoing evaluative methodological expertise.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-026-01455-8.

Background

A wealth of research internationally has been published on health system responses to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Much of this evidence captures initial organizational responses to COVID-19 during the first severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) wave of the pandemic. A systematic review of qualitative studies internationally identified several organizational factors associated with navigating change during the pandemic: its role as a catalyst for accelerating service change (in part, due to stimulating shared purpose); the burden placed upon front-line staff in navigating change and the consequent need for workforce support (including providers’ use of more engaging or open leadership); and various coordinated activities across local health systems, from workforce training to updating regulations [1]. It is not known whether these trends and other initial responses have been sustained. A qualitative study of elective surgery in London found that staff were given increased discretion and resources to adapt services yet raised questions about the sustainability of the temporary solutions or workarounds introduced [2]. A review of how work environments were initially affected by COVID-19 made a call for longitudinal studies that can evaluate how the work pressures experienced during the early months of the pandemic have affected the workforce and organizations in the longer term [3].

We take sustainability to mean “the ability to maintain programming and its benefits over time” [4]. There is debate concerning the appropriate organizational mechanisms for maintaining the efficacy of interventions over time. One distinction can be found between scholars who emphasize the need to garner organizational resources to maintain an intervention’s fidelity [4], such that an intervention’s performance remains faithful to its founding logic and design, and those that advocate programme learning through adaptation [5, 6], whereby an intervention is theorized to be sustained because attention is given to its ongoing adaptation to fit potential change in the context of its implementation.

Organizational mechanisms for maintaining fidelity to sustain innovations focus on internal managerial support and resources, including operational integration, support systems, advocating leadership, sufficient staffing and long-term financial planning [4, 7]. Organizational mechanisms for supporting adaptation to sustain innovations focus on the ongoing fit between the innovation and the context, including human resources, information systems, structure and culture as well as staff training [5]. In both perspectives, there is a suggested role for feedback or learning loops concerning the ongoing implementation of an intervention, whether that be to maintain programme-driven fidelity or to support context-led adaptation. Moreover, both perspectives emphasize the critical role of evidence in the design of intervention programmes: allocating resources to the sustaining of an intervention – by attending to its fidelity or adaptation – presupposes that the design or logic of an intervention carries evidential support.

With regard to health system responses to COVID-19, some caveats around the conceptualization of sustainability can be posited. Firstly, changes in practice introduced to address the demands of the pandemic may not have been underpinned by strong evidence, owing to the emergent and fluid nature of the evidence associated with COVID-19, which could have implications for the ways in which innovations are evaluated and sustained (i.e. evidence may have been lacking initially or subject to revision which could influence sustainment decisions). Second, the emergent and provisional status of evidence associated with initial responses to COVID-19 would suggest an important role for learning by adaptation to support the sustainability of changes in practice, to aid their fit with a shifting context.

The objective of this study was to understand how organizational factors, including evaluation of innovations, measures to support the workforce and ongoing interunit coordination, influenced the sustainability of initial pandemic responses. It meets this objective by addressing the following research question: which local and national organizational factors influenced the sustainability of initial responses to the COVID-19 pandemic in Colombia up to 2 years after its arrival?

Methods

Using focus-group data collected between November 2021 and March 2022 – a year after an initial round of interviews were completed between June and November 2020 – this paper explores the sustainability of changes up to 2 years after the arrival of COVID-19 in Colombia. This 2-year period aligns with the postimplementation timeframe identified in a systematic review of sustainability studies in health care settings (64% assessed outcomes two or more years post implementation; the remainder studied a shorter timeframe) [6]. This 2-year follow-up period enables analysis of whether emergent actions in response to COVID−19 we identified in our first round of interviews were institutionalized in practice. Were emergent innovations introduced at an early stage of the pandemic evaluated? Did trial-and-error learning remain relevant in approaches to decisionmaking? What became of formal and informal networks of collaboration that were used to share emergent information and experiences? Which interventions remained for supporting the mental and physical health of the workforce? Were early interorganizational collaborations (e.g. around disease surveillance) maintained? Did cross-sectoral resources and relationships among the health system, academia and the private sector continue to be influential?

Study context

This paper focusses on the sustainment of initial changes in practice, and perceived gaps in responses, by which the COVID-19 pandemic was addressed in Colombia. Colombia was severely affected by COVID-19. As of December 2022, Colombia reported the fourth highest number of deaths (141,881) in Latin America and the Caribbean, with a mortality rate of 278.84 per 100,000 people (the sixth highest in the region) [8]. Colombia is classified as an upper middle-income country[9]; it has a social insurance model of health care, in which services are funded through contributory and subsidized tax regimes, governed through centralized pricing and regulation and delivered through local networks of public and privately owned providers [10–13]. The health care workforce has been particularly affected by COVID-19, with deficits reported in use of personal protective equipment (PPE) [14], concerns about morale and wellbeing [15] and occupational stress and anxiety among physicians [16]. Colombia represents a valuable case study because it suffered from resource pressures, budgetary constraints and system fragmentation at the pandemic’s onset, all of which influenced its ability to respond to this environmental shock.

To provide temporal context to the study reported in this paper, COVID-19 arrived in Colombia in March 2020. In response, local and national governments introduced a series of lockdown measures and other mechanisms to contain the spread of the virus over a 17-month period, from March 2020 to August 2021. Our first round of interviews (118), which examined initial health system responses to the pandemic, was conducted from June 2020 to November 2020, coinciding with the first wave of COVID-19 in Colombia (initial lockdown measures were eased from September 2020). Further waves of cases affected Colombia from December 2020, triggering local lockdowns and other public health measures that varied by city and region over the period January 2021 to August 2021. Our second round of data collection, which informs this paper’s analysis of the sustainability of changes in practice, was undertaken from November 2021 to March 2022, approximately 3 months after the strictest public health measures had ended and up to 2 years after COVID-19 arrived in Colombia.

Recommendations on guiding major system change in response to COVID-19

This study involved evaluating the validity and sustainability of practices denoted by six recommendations that emerged from our investigation of early responses to COVID-19. The recommendations were informed by an international systematic review [1]; qualitative research findings on early responses to the pandemic in Colombia based on interviews conducted in 2020 [15, 17–20]; and conceptual development by considering the relevance of major system change theory (MSC) [21] to pandemic contexts.

Drawing on this empirical and conceptual work, six recommendations for guiding ongoing health system responses to COVID-19 and future pandemics were developed [22]. Recommendations for guiding change in response to pandemics

The six recommendations were made available through interactive guidance aimed at policymakers and practitioners [22] and derived from study findings that were published in international, peer reviewed publications.

Conceptually, the recommendations engaged with the topic of implementing coordinated MSC, understood as a social process of aligning multiple stakeholders’ preferences, activities and resource use [21]. While MSC literature developed prior to the pandemic considered change as a longer-term process, to allow for the building of an evidence-based case for change and stakeholder involvement [23], the emphasis of our recommendations reflects the need to navigate change in the turbulent environmental context of crises characterized by time pressure, uncertainty and emergent information [24].

The recommendations are aligned with wider calls from within the health systems research literature to maintain evidence-based systems for informing and evaluating service change [25–27], despite the need for swift action felt during pandemics and other health emergencies. For example, while emergent actions in response to crises are highlighted, e.g. acknowledge the need for trial-and-error learning (R2) and construct networks of information and expertise (R3), other recommendations emphasize building a durable infrastructure that embodies or institutionalizes [28] learning from crisis management (i.e. evaluate accelerated innovations (R1), invest in collective resources before crises hit (R6) and institutionalize coordination by combining hard and soft mechanisms (R5)). Finally, existing literature on MSC has largely neglected its impact on health system staff, leading to the recommendation based on observations of the pandemic to safeguard the workforce (R4).

Process of developing recommendations

The six recommendations, which covered the perceived strengths of Colombia’s response to the pandemic and areas for improvement, were developed through a series of steps: (1) reviewing stakeholders’ priorities for improvement and feedback preferences stated in response to topic guide questions 11 and 12 from the 2020 study (Additional file 1); (2) revisiting our empirical analysis of various organizational themes written up in published or draft papers; (3) developing a long list of recommendations on the basis of data from steps 1 and 2; (4) rationalizing the long list through debate at internal research meetings (e.g. amalgamating recommendations with similar content, while being attentive to any empirical insights not sufficiently covered in the recommendations); (5) presenting provisional findings and recommendations for feedback from the study funder and potential research users; (6) refining the recommendations for clarity of definition, inclusion of sub-themes and illustrative examples; and (7) documentation of the recommendations in interactive guidance, an online PDF available on a university website [22]. The guide is organized around the six recommendations, including a brief description of each one, the evidence upon which each is based, examples to illustrate the recommendation (e.g. key quotations from study participants), implications for practice, additional resources (e.g. links to newspaper articles, full academic papers published through the research study) and a checklist for users to assess their performance in relation to each.

Data collection for focus group study

To explore the sustainability of the changes denoted by the six recommendations, focus groups were arranged with health system stakeholders from Colombia. Focus groups, which involve focussing on interaction among participants [29], were chosen to capture variations in opinion held by a variety of stakeholders re-invited from the 2020 study. The sample strategy involved inviting again stakeholders who had participated previously in an interview; the original dataset included participants from national level entities (e.g. ministries and medical associations) and senior representatives from planning and provider organizations in the major cities of Bogotá, Cali and Cartagena, including health insurance companies, secretaries of health, hospitals and universities. The original sample reflected the multilevel geographical focus of our study: macro responses at the national level and case studies of service planning and provision in three major cities (local responses at the meso- or organizational level), to see how responses to COVID-19 were influenced by managerial actions at each level. The sampling strategy for the focus groups involved re-inviting these purposively selected stakeholders to obtain their perceptions and experiences of organizational factors that influenced whether early responses to the pandemic had been sustained in the period since the initial interviews.

Participants in the 2020 round of interviews (118) attended six focus groups, and two individual interviews, which were conducted with 18 participants who accepted our second invitation between November 2021 and March 2022 (Additional file 2). Senior representatives within each organization, rather than front-line staff, were sampled to obtain a comprehensive overview of how each organization responded initially to the pandemic and in the 2-year period since its onset.

The response rate (15%) was low; however, a similar variety of stakeholders were represented in both phases of data collection, namely local and national government, insurers, hospital directors, medical associations and academics. Capturing this variety of stakeholder perspectives in our focus group sampling was key to meeting our objective of receiving feedback on the recommendations from the predominant types of actors involved in the response to COVID-19. The focus groups were informed by a topic guide (Additional file 3), audio-recorded and transcribed professionally. Participants were approached by email, received an information sheet and provided informed consent. The study was carried out in accordance with consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies [30] (Additional file 4). Each focus group involved two to three participants; this was sufficient for enabling group interaction and debate in response to the stimulus presented, understood as a key role of focus groups [29].

One researcher (D.F.) conducted the focus groups (S.T. attended some); each was organized around the six recommendations, whereby the participants received a written summary and examples relating to each. Recommendations were introduced verbally at the focus group and the participants were invited to discuss each ones’s validity, acceptability and sustainability drawing on their own experiences of responding to the pandemic and through hearing about and discussing others’ experiences. The focus groups averaged 85 min and were conducted virtually. The aim was to cover all six recommendations in the focus groups; where time constraints precluded complete discussion of some recommendations, those were prioritized in subsequent focus groups, to ensure all six received attention.

Data analysis for focus group study

The focus group data were analysed thematically, combining inductive and deductive approaches [31]. The analysis was deductive in that participants were asked about the validity and sustainability of predefined recommendations; however, in the process of evaluating each one, the participants drew on personal perceptions and experiences which introduced an inductive element. The approach to data analysis combined both approaches because our coding was ordered by deductive recommendation yet involved documenting inductively formed perceptions and experiences stimulated by the discussion.

Two researchers coded the transcripts manually by assessing each recommendation for: (1) internal validity, where the recommendation resonates with the participants’ own experiences in Bogotá, Cali and Cartagena (the focus of our study); (2) external validity, where the information was perceived to be relevant to experiences in rural zones and other cities in Colombia outside those included in our study; (3) feasibility of implementation; (4) acceptability to stakeholders; (5) sustainability of the changes denoted by the recommendations; and (6) potential gaps in the recommendations from participants’ perspectives (notably more detailed workforce themes beyond the scope of this paper). Emergent themes, and saturation of stakeholder views on each theme, were discussed regularly by the researchers. Gaps in our analysis (e.g. details of network continuation) were reviewed with a subset (2) of participants through additional, follow-up interviews during the data analysis process.

The researchers used a codebook for defining key terms – including validity, sustainability, feasibility of implementation, acceptability and gaps in recommendations – during the data collection and analysis process (Additional file 5). To assess internal and external validity, we mapped stakeholders’ opinions against each recommendation that suggested agreement, disagreement or gaps regarding the sustainability of practices that it denoted. This assessment was made qualitatively by the researchers in response to stakeholders’ situated perceptions and experiences rather than categorized using a numerical scale. Information from the focus groups and interviews was combined with results from our first wave of interviews to analyse service and network changes that represented (1) initial responses to the pandemic and (2) if these were sustained during 2021 and Q1 2022 (Tables 2, 3, 4). Changes introduced to safeguard the workforce

We acknowledge that a larger sample size of focus group participants relative to the first round of interviews would improve trustworthiness of the findings; however, as noted above, a similar variety of senior level stakeholders were included and a robust approach to the conduct of the focus groups, and analysis of the resulting data, was undertaken using established qualitative research methods [29–31].

Results

Make time to evaluate accelerated changes to services

The participants reported a series of changes in practice in response to COVID-19: splitting and equipping intensive care units (ICUs) to create dedicated spaces for patients with or without COVID-19; telemedicine, including online chat services, for triaging and conducting medical appointments; setting up communication channels with isolated patients’ families to give progress reports; adapting treatment approaches, and biosecurity measures, as new information emerged from the World Health Organization (WHO) and other sources; and drawing on resources in adjacent sectors, including university laboratories, to increase testing and health workforce capacity.

Study participants put forward several reasons as to why some changes in practice remained unevaluated. First, evaluation was thwarted by the need to respond to ongoing health system challenges associated with successive waves of COVID-19. The intensity of each challenge, and their sustained nature, limited both the time and the workforce capacity available to document and evaluate changes in practice:“I believe that we have a pending task there. […] my team have listened to me many times about the systematisation that we should have of our experiences, and I believe that you are doing it a little bit from the universities, perhaps because you also have the time. Because, whether we like it or not, we are still governing and there are problems every day. But I believe that although there is memory, although there are constructions of the experiences we had, I still miss a methodological rigour for this. […] I believe that we need more [evaluation], we worked so fast, we had such a high level of stress in which we had to perform, that the circumstance of documenting and recording has been a little bit lame.”(Government staff, Bogotá, Bog1Go1110).

Second, many healthcare organizations were said to lack historically an internal culture, training and resources to carry out evaluative activities. Recognizing these barriers to evaluation in the context of the pandemic, a call for independent evaluation, by academic researchers with the necessary technical skills to bring additional resources and expertise, was made by one of the participants:“In the health sector the evaluation should be independent or there is a need of additional budget to be able to evaluate as they are overloaded”(Professional Association, Bogotá, BogAc1102).

However, some examples of strategic planning, documentation and evaluation were given by participants at different levels of the health system (Table 2). For example, a physician in a Bogotá hospital, identified as being at risk of developing complications from COVID-19, was reallocated to an administrative role that involved documenting responses to COVID-19 in the hospital to improve patient experience:“From the beginning we created a log leader, she began documenting along with the communications office and another nursing group. We document everything, absolutely everything in relation to time and case tracking, etc., to be able to then systematise and deliver the experience”(Hospital director, Bogotá, BogHo1103).

That is, what happened to the response plan in these low-complexity services? How were they organised? How was the response?”(Government staff, Bogotá, BogGo1102).

Finally, some coordinated responses to the pandemic abated over time without being formally evaluated (for example, the frequency of meetings among Bogota’s crisis committee fell from daily, to weekly, to monthly, before terminating in January 2022).

Acknowledge the need for trial-and-error learning

Examples where trial-and-error learning was applied included: the grafting of imagined principles of academic debate onto improvised policymaking; use of the best available evidence and experiences to achieve impact at pace; and use of crisis committees to respond to emerging information on COVID-19.

While trial-and-error learning appeared to support initial responses to the pandemic, some participants suggested limitations of sustaining learning through trial and error. First, it was claimed that reliance on trial and error was not planned or intended but became the de facto approach in a context of uncertainty:“No, that is not planned to do so by trial and error, I think that is given by circumstances; because there is no other option”(Local government, Cali, CalGo1103).

“No, that is not planned to do so by trial and error, I think that is given by circumstances; because there is no other option”

(Local government, Cali, CalGo1103).

Second, emergent policies could be revised in response to stakeholder interests, which appeared to reflect political power rather than feedback on the efficacy of policies. One participant gave the example of a national decree concerning lockdown measures being revised in the course of a day, owing to concerns about its impact on industry:“It even happened once a decree comes out in the morning and in the afternoon, they change it due to external pressures. Because, as I said, here in Cartagena the “white chimney” industry is very strong and that is what the city lives on, and how were we going to close the city and what was the city going to live on?(Hospital director, Cartagena, CarHo1112)

Third, it was suggested that approaches involving trial-and-error learning should be linked to evaluation of accelerated innovations, as discussed previously. That is, where approaches are trialled, there should be evaluation of their efficacy (e.g. through environmental feedback) and then the approach is validated or alternatives sought. The insights derived from trial-and-error learning should be subjected to evaluation, as the following participant argued:“it is a very important learning to involve [that approach] within the planning of a unit, but discipline and scientific behavior is required so as not to make certain anything that arises from trial and error.”(Professional Association, Bogotá, BogAc1102).

Build networks of expertise to share emergent information and experiences

Networks, varying in scale, actor type and informational role, supported the initial response to COVID-19 in Colombia. The focus groups suggested that most networks identified were being sustained (Table 3):“I believe that even this is something that occurs almost by default at this time… they prevailed, and I believe that this is not going to go backwards”.(Hospital director, Cali, CalHo1116).

“I believe that even this is something that occurs almost by default at this time… they prevailed, and I believe that this is not going to go backwards”.

(Hospital director, Cali, CalHo1116).

Virtual communication among physicians has continued to be valued as a way of spreading knowledge without requiring co-location. While patient care-oriented networks, based on virtual interaction, had been utilized before the pandemic, its onset gave such virtual networks added impetus, given policy restrictions on physical proximity associated with COVID-19. This informational mechanism associated with network interaction (e.g. via WhatsApp and other messaging services) was perceived to be fulfilling an ongoing need among physicians in rural areas of Colombia in particular:“Doctors from rural zones looked, and still look for, advice and information from the main cities. The access to social media and communication platforms helped to spread the knowledge between different actors despite the city or town. Rural Colombia is eager for accompaniment”(Hospital director, Cartagena, Car2Ho1116).

“Doctors from rural zones looked, and still look for, advice and information from the main cities. The access to social media and communication platforms helped to spread the knowledge between different actors despite the city or town. Rural Colombia is eager for accompaniment”

(Hospital director, Cartagena, Car2Ho1116).

There was debate among focus group participants about the relevance of formalization to sustain informational networks. Some argued that the value of networks lay in their spontaneity and independence, which could be undermined by external intervention:“What makes them endure for me is that they are of individual initiative, institutional if you like, but they are not forced from the strategic thinking of an organisation or forced from the strategic thinking of the government but are responses to a need that has proven its value.”(Professional Association, Bogotá, BogAc1102).

“What makes them endure for me is that they are of individual initiative, institutional if you like, but they are not forced from the strategic thinking of an organisation or forced from the strategic thinking of the government but are responses to a need that has proven its value.”

(Professional Association, Bogotá, BogAc1102).

Others suggested that the value of established and prospective networks could be enhanced through strategic planning. For instance, a hospital director in Cartagena argued that topics of common interest for communication networks should be identified, that draw upon expertise from within both academia and public health entities, that would also have the benefit of defining what each sector could learn from the other (Car3Ho1125).

Acknowledging both perspectives, one way forward is given by the physician-led network described earlier, where the direction of the network was open and participation remained voluntary, but it was supported by leaders of public hospitals and public universities who encouraged, but did not mandate, participation from within their organizations.

Safeguard the workforce

The focus groups suggested that the impact on the mental and physical health of the workforce continued beyond the initial response to the pandemic. The impact on staff appeared to be compounded by the need to continue working in a context of crisis over a sustained period (i.e. subsequent waves of the pandemic). There was a lack of recognition by employers of this impact, and use of mitigating actions, to protect their staff:“In many cases it has been seen that [innovation] impacts, for example, the mental health of the health sector workers. Because in these innovations, workloads and tasks were increased for many members of the health teams, with the very valid justification, obviously, of having a response with greater timeliness and coverage. Shifts were doubled, extra-mural tasks were increased—or in places outside the health workers´ place of residence—and this generated effects on mental health.”(Government staff, Bogotá, BogGo1102).

“In many cases it has been seen that [innovation] impacts, for example, the mental health of the health sector workers. Because in these innovations, workloads and tasks were increased for many members of the health teams, with the very valid justification, obviously, of having a response with greater timeliness and coverage. Shifts were doubled, extra-mural tasks were increased—or in places outside the health workers´ place of residence—and this generated effects on mental health.”

(Government staff, Bogotá, BogGo1102).

The ongoing neglect of the workforce contributed to mental health problems among staff – “burnout made a killing” (Hospital, Cartagena, Car2Ho1116) – with some clinical staff, including nurses, reportedly choosing to leave the profession owing to ongoing dissatisfaction with poor wages and working conditions (Medical association, Bogotá, BogAc1129).

Mitigating actions were introduced to support health care workers at the organizational and regulatory levels (Table 4). These were patchy in response to the initial wave of the pandemic (e.g. resting on human resource practices by individual employers) and with regard to whether workforce interventions were sustained. One participant who managed a private hospital in Bogotá stated that additional benefits provided to their physicians and nurses to increase motivation (e.g. additional paid vacation leave during 2020, restrooms and direct contracts) were sustained until January 2022. At national level, there were mixed views on the addressing of working conditions. Improved compensation had not been sustained (i.e. this was limited to a one-off bonus in 2020), but there was acknowledgement by the government of the need to improve working conditions in public entities, including job security through contractual changes and recognition of COVID-19 as an occupational disease.

Combine soft and hard coordination mechanisms

There were instances reported of soft collaboration being sustained. For example, while various health system stakeholders were already connected, it was suggested that people now “answer the phones”. Improved collaboration for other diseases (e.g. dengue fever) was reported because trust and informal relationships among local actors had improved following their experiences of working together during the pandemic:“Right now, here in Cartagena with dengue, they [the chamber of commerce] are also supporting. In other words, I already believe that a culture of support is being generated at all levels to deal with these endemic diseases, or as in this one, which is a pandemic”.(Hospital director, Cartagena, Car2Ho1116).

“Right now, here in Cartagena with dengue, they [the chamber of commerce] are also supporting. In other words, I already believe that a culture of support is being generated at all levels to deal with these endemic diseases, or as in this one, which is a pandemic”.

(Hospital director, Cartagena, Car2Ho1116).

Some concerns were expressed about sustaining softer forms of coordination. While intrinsic motivation had encouraged actors to offer support in response to the pandemic, there was concern that a lack of institutional recognition of, and gratitude for, such contributions would undermine future support. Improved relationships were connected to specific individuals, especially leads of organizations or functions, generating concerns that interorganizational relations would be threatened where personnel leave organizations or move into different roles. This was seen as a particular risk for public officials whose roles are linked to the political cycle. To mitigate the risk, one participant argued that any coordinating agreements struck between different institutions during the pandemic needed to be formalized:“The agreements depend a lot on the people who are now at the head of the different institutions, we would have to look forward to mature the agreements in such a way that they are institutionalised from the organisational point of view and that they do not depend so much on the actors.”(Government staff, Bogotá, Bog1Go1110).

“The agreements depend a lot on the people who are now at the head of the different institutions, we would have to look forward to mature the agreements in such a way that they are institutionalised from the organisational point of view and that they do not depend so much on the actors.”

(Government staff, Bogotá, Bog1Go1110).

Some hard agreements were formed in response to the pandemic that enabled interagency collaboration. For example, tripartite agreements were formed between insurers, health service providers and local service commissioners that enabled coordinated approaches to domiciliary testing for COVID-19. Such collaborative relationships have since been drawn upon to support other activities (e.g. vaccine programme coordination and responding to an outbreak of dengue fever) but not with the same level of intensity.

There was concern that the hard agreements struck would diminish without active management. Difficulties of sustaining coordination were reported between higher level or national entities (e.g. regulation and control, health ministry) and those involved in the planning and delivery of health services on the local front line. In relation to telemedicine, there was a need for national entities to recognize new standards, but this had been challenging. Review of the legal and regulatory framework to support the adaptability of services was needed:“We must review the role of the central level, because it can be a facilitator or it can be a barrier, in some ways it was a barrier and almost clandestinely we had to organise metropolitan areas without the ministry noticing”(Local government, Cali, CalGo1103).

“We must review the role of the central level, because it can be a facilitator or it can be a barrier, in some ways it was a barrier and almost clandestinely we had to organise metropolitan areas without the ministry noticing”

(Local government, Cali, CalGo1103).

Improving local collaboration was dependent on, and stimulated by, support from national level actors within a centralized regulatory system, such as the health ministry and national regulator, that oversee service quality, pricing and access.

Invest in intersectoral resources

Nongovernmental and private organizations offered support at an early stage of the pandemic, including universities, private companies and local chambers of commerce. Evidence of the fruits of improved intersectoral collaboration was highlighted in research trials, e.g. reviewing a study protocol in 5 days, rather than the typical 5 months. Improved collaboration was stimulated in part by the concept that ensuring public health in a context of crisis was not limited to the health care sector; instead, a variety of actors from different sectors of the economy could make a potential contribution to improving public health.

The participants highlighted barriers to sustaining improved intersectoral coordination. New regulatory standards needed to be defined, and the tone of legislation adjusted, to support emerging forms of intersectoral collaboration. For example, public entities in particular required formal permission to take on new roles, as this sector faced legislative constraints not encountered in the private sector. Moreover, the so-called rules of the game, especially the financing of new intersectoral roles and relationships needed to be defined. For example, one participant (quoted below) reported collaboration between their own organization and local universities in responding to an outbreak of dengue fever. However, such relationships remained informal and were not recognized in the formal system of public health, limiting the agility with which coordinated responses to future public health crises could be made:“I talk to you for example about dengue, we have permanent, academic information, with people from [University A], with people from [University B], with infectologists from here, with people from [University C], and those groups exist, and those groups are doing research and those groups have the predictive models and such, but those groups are not attached to the control entities that manage public health in the region, so of course when there are outbreaks there, yes they call people, hold meetings, and there go the academics and say the things, too late.”(Hospital director, Cartagena, Car3Ho1125).

With regard to resources, a distinction was made between, on the one hand, repurposing existing resources in the context of crisis and, on the other, use of strategic planning to improve coordinated responses to future crises. An example of the former, in relation to the COVID-19 pandemic, was repurposing university-owned laboratories to expand the national testing capacity for this disease. However, forward planning to improve the intersectoral coordination of resources was still lacking, as this interviewee explained:“It's more of a strategic component of being able to adapt public and private resources, workforce, infrastructure and economic, to a crisis situation. This is more how I understand this concept, and not as much as... and now what do we have?”(Local government, Cali, CalGo1103).

Discussion

Many of the practices observed in the 2020 round of interviews have persisted in the Colombian health system. For example, the ongoing lack of evaluation of new practices introduced in response to the pandemic appears to be linked to the series of environmental shocks associated with COVID-19. The pandemic was not experienced as an isolated, short-lived event but as an unfolding process in which highly challenging situations were continuously revealed that confounded learning through evaluation. Some progress in the area of evaluation appears to be associated with the exploitation of existing evaluation capabilities – whether through partnering with external entities that possess evaluation skills (e.g. facilitated by Non-Governmental Organization [NGO] funding) or via local practices of evaluation within specific institutions where an existing culture of learning exists (e.g. documentation and further evaluation of new practices by a private hospital in Bogotá).

Early responses to the pandemic related to networks of expertise, coordination mechanisms and intersectoral actions had continued, yet some stakeholders expressed concern about the precariousness of these practices. As these practices are often currently underpinned by informal trust and goodwill among local actors, they lack the institutional support necessary to be sustained, especially through recognition from, and incorporation into, future responses by state entities at the national level.

Areas of regression relate to trial-and-error learning and safeguarding the workforce. The recognition of learning through experimentation that was seen as valuable in our analysis of initial responses during 2020 [17] needs to be tempered with acknowledgement that such trials, critical for supporting early responses to COVID-19, were a pragmatic response to crisis, rather than a sustainable approach to decisionmaking, as some focus group participants indicated. Decisions about sustaining innovations introduced through trial-and-error experimentation should be informed by formal processes of learning and evaluation, suggesting a role for health research systems to bring evaluative expertise to bear upon informal learning processes navigated during the pandemic.

With regard to the workforce, the focus group participants highlighted continuing pressures related to capacity, resources and wellbeing which had been exacerbated by the extended period that staff had been called upon to respond to subsequent waves of the pandemic (often with little recognition), including the withdrawal of additional financial compensation that was paid to staff in 2020.

Research implications

The study findings have implications for the literature on sustaining service changes introduced during crises. Sustaining change tends to imply its institutionalization through embedding in norms of practice and/or regulatory activities [28]. However, the evaluation of sustainability in critical times such as a pandemic needs to take into account the urgent context associated with change. This affects how the emergent practices stimulated by crises should be treated.

First, motivation to participate in informal collaboration, e.g. professionally led networks, emerged bottom-up on the basis of a collective clinical need [32], together with professional uncertainty and fear, associated with treating a novel disease. As the context of urgency abates, what sustains those early impulses to participate in professional networks? Network formalization or institutionalization does not, of itself, compensate for the diminishing context of urgency from which collaboration was stimulated. In the Colombian case, networks were sustained bottom-up by professional recognition of the value of knowledge sharing in relation to new and adjacent service problems. Motivation to participate could be undermined by the institutional formalization of these networks (as one participant remarked, they are effective because “they are not forced from the strategic thinking of an organisation”). Sustaining knowledge sharing from an organizational perspective could focus on understanding how knowledge sharing is rooted in professional culture, then working on the existing organizational culture to reflect the motivations associated with professional values. This is a radical twist on the adaptive approach to sustaining change, whereby the existing context is repopulated by new norms associated with emergent innovations, like rewarding network development, rather than by seeking to fit the intervention with the prevailing norms of the extant context. [5].

Second, emergent practices were often underpinned by workers placed under intensive pressure to perform new roles or with additional pace. While the outcome might be desirable (e.g. review of a study protocol in 5 days, not 5 months, as one participant described), it is important that the processes underpinning accelerated task completion are reviewed from a workforce burden perspective. Soft mechanisms of innovation like shared purpose, which were triggered by a common threat during the pandemic, are unlikely to be sustainable unless matched with hard institutional slack and financial support, as we explore further below.

Third, without evaluation, promising practices can be susceptible to being discarded as new health system priorities emerge (e.g. initiation of macro health reform following a change of government, as seen in Colombia after Gustavo Petro’s administration took office in August 2022). Evaluators, with appropriate stakeholder involvement, should analyse which aspects of practices were only intended to be temporary (as short-term fixes), and which should be sustained because they have longer-term value despite emerging impulses for organizational and macro-level change. While noninstitutionalized practices may represent waste of human and financial investment [28], it is also problematic to persist with practices through institutionalization beyond the intended context of their use owing to nonevaluation. Why do particular practices achieve normalization? In evaluating which policies and practices to sustain, the political power associated with prominent stakeholder interests (as one participant described with regard to industry interests informing the design of lockdown measures), should be acknowledged in literature on sustaining change. For example, the adaptive approach to sustaining change emphasizes the ongoing alignment of innovation and context [6]; however, this approach presupposes that a unified set of contextual interests can be identified or negotiated to drive an innovation’s ongoing design and use. Carefully designed evaluations, based on the input of multiple stakeholders and diverse forms of evidence that reflect their perspectives [33, 34], are an important component of decisionmaking on sustaining innovations that emerged in the pandemic to control for powerful stakeholder influence.

Fourth, this study suggests that both adaptation- and fidelity-based approaches have important roles to play in decisions about sustaining innovations that emerged in crisis contexts. At early stages of crises, responses to the COVID-19 pandemic indicate the importance of learning through adaptation [5] to collect feedback from the turbulent context associated with health emergencies. Aspects of our suggested recommendations – namely, permit trial-and-error experimentation, build networks of expertise and utilize soft coordination mechanisms – have critical roles in supporting organizational adaptation by enabling collaborative responses to crisis and allowing provisional evaluation of how those responses perform in addressing the sources of challenges. For example, inviting varied stakeholder opinions in the course of trial-and-error learning, drawing vicariously on experiences in other clinical contexts via distanciated professional networks or brokering trusting relationships between health organizations to make available otherwise proprietary infrastructural and human resources. An adaptive approach is necessary but insufficient for sustaining the journeys of emergent innovations. The fidelity approach – with its emphasis on identifying and stabilizing the key components of innovations and facilitating their use and spread across organizations – is needed to ensure that only evidence-based practices are sustained and appropriate organizational arrangements (including formal evaluation of innovations, hard contracts to maintain collaborations and enabling access to decent working conditions) are put in place to support innovative practice needs when the initial motivation to adapt spurred by urgency dissipates.

On the basis of the qualitative findings of this study, incorporating aspects of trial-and-error experimentation, participation in networks of expertise and intersectoral collaboration are three suggested ways of improving the efficacy of the feedback loops to respond to new information concerning the best available evidence and contextual fit [5] to support the introduction of innovations. Once the evidence around a particular intervention has solidified, then there can be a stronger role for ensuring fidelity to the intervention and garnering organizational resources to support the sustainability of interventions. Our study identifies further organizational capabilities [4, 7], namely, an established internal culture of evaluation and/or partnerships with external agencies that can guide evaluation, which are important for facilitating formal evaluation design to inform decisions about sustaining innovations by health system organizations.

Finally, we suggest an area where literature on sustaining service change requires further development. Learning from the context of crisis associated with the pandemic, our study shows that supporting human resources to sustain innovations goes beyond ensuring sufficient staff capacity [7] or adapting staff training [5]. Promoting the sustainability of innovations depends on sustaining human resources that represent core components for delivering existing and adapted services (as one interviewee noted, “burnout made a killing”, over the 2-year period examined as unremitting heightened expectations took their toll upon staff). Ensuring the quality of working conditions for health providers, underpinned by appropriate and stable financial and nonfinancial incentives (that include regard for their long-term wellbeing), is critical for sustaining health innovations in addition to staff numbers and training.

Policy and practice implications

The analysis of the sustaining of new practices that developed in response to the pandemic in Colombia, including areas of fragility where practices have continued or regressed, suggests a need to strengthen policy and practice in several ways. First, there is a need to evaluate collaboration forms and learning that has been garnered in response to COVID-19, recognizing the strong risk that positive actions associated with responding to the pandemic may be lost without evidential support or other forms of public recognition. State leadership and investment, at national and local levels, is needed to sustain emergent practices of adaptation that appear to rest precariously on informal, often voluntary, forms of collaboration. Policy evaluation and planning concerning health systems should acknowledge, and seek to mitigate, risk that lessons are lost across political cycles. Investment in cross-sectoral evaluation capacity is needed to inform decisions about sustaining, adapting or decommissioning new practices that emerged in response to the pandemic. This includes the appropriate place of trial-and-error experiments in relation to formal planning processes used to respond to future pandemics. Such research infrastructure investments should be sustained to enable timely responses to future health crises [35].

Second, the workforce pressures acknowledged in studies of early responses to the pandemic have persisted in the Colombian context. While there was some early recognition by the state of the need to support health care workers (e.g. staff bonus payments), the critical role that the workforce plays in sustaining the delivery of the quality of care needs to be acknowledged through governmental and regulatory interventions that target workforce financing, capacity building and staff motivation and wellbeing. Our research in Colombia shows that a variety of local, seemingly ad hoc measures were introduced by some provider organizations with the aim of improving staff wellbeing. National and local health system entities should coordinate progress by making validated practices available more widely to support staff wellbeing across Colombia’s health system. Given the reported pressures experienced by academics [36] and health system leaders [19] alike during the pandemic, acknowledgement of mental health and wellbeing at work should extend across the health research ecosystem, including universities.

Limitations and future research

This study examined stakeholders’ perceptions and experiences of initial responses to COVID-19 and how and why these were sustained, or gaps persisted, within one health care system, situated in an upper middle-income country, that may not be generalizable to other contexts. The context of Colombia adds to the health research systems analysed in the journal’s special collection on responses to the COVID-19 pandemic [37]. Our analysis of Colombia similarly highlights the need for strong components of health research systems to be in place before pandemics emerge [37], notably through investment in collaborative resources at the intersectoral level, our sixth recommendation, that maps onto the function of “creating and sustaining resources” to support capacity building that emerged in other countries [37, 38]. Future studies should evaluate our country-specific recommendations which emphasize the sustaining of resources that emerged in response to the pandemic, taking note of how sustainability of initial responses are shaped by variation in governance approaches, funding arrangements, evaluation systems and workforce policies across national contexts.

While the time interval between initial responses to COVID-19 and our follow-up focus groups to assess sustainability of practice was in line with other studies [6], we acknowledge that some domains of practice could be institutionalized, or regress, outside a 2-year timeframe (e.g. the results of formal evaluations, notably those involving external peer review, can require a more extended period of time to emerge and influence decisionmaking on further service change), suggesting a need for ongoing surveys, administered at 1- or 2-year intervals, to categorize and monitor sustainability by innovation type.

Conclusions

This paper addresses a gap concerning the sustainability of health system changes made in response to COVID-19. The qualitative data from stakeholder focus groups conducted in Colombia suggest that practices identified in 2020 have persisted (some gaps in evaluation of innovations), are precarious (networks of expertise, coordination mechanisms and intersectoral actions) or have regressed (formalizing trial-and-error learning and safeguarding the workforce). In organizational sustainability terms, these postpandemic outcomes reflect reliance on short-term adaptations to crisis motivated by an urgent context yet apparent deficits in institutional capacities to identify, formalize and amplify emergent practices worthy of sustaining within the health system, as emphasized in the fidelity approach. Sustaining health system change is complicated by the rapidity with which new practices are introduced in crisis contexts, as robust evaluation tends to lag behind, and institutional commitments ebb, as the public crisis that motivated urgent actions passes. Learning through trial-and-error adaptation and then enhancing fidelity to practices validated through rigorous evaluation are both critical stages for enabling and sustaining promising innovations that emerged rapidly in response to COVID-19. Researchers, with a variety of evaluative methodological expertise, need to be involved early at the trial-and-error stage to support the translation of promising practices into sustainable innovations.
