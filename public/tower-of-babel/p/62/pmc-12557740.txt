Surgical stabilization of flail sternum and bilateral chest wall injury in an octogenarian after horse trampling injury
Authors: Jesse WS Tai, Bona Ko, Madeline E Adams, Chloe K Nobuhara, Ariel W Knight, Joseph D Forrester
Published: 2025

Surgical dilemma

An older man in his 80s presented to a referring hospital after sustaining severe blunt chest wall trauma after being trampled by a horse. He was hypotensive on presentation but responded appropriately to two units of packed red blood cells. Chest X-ray revealed a right-sided hemopneumothorax, which was treated with a thoracostomy tube.

CT revealed extensive chest wall trauma, including bilateral hemopneumothoraces, a displaced transverse mid-body sternal fracture, bilateral costosternal joint dislocations, and multiple bilateral rib fractures with multiple radiographic flail segments (figure 1). On the left, there were displaced fractures of the anterior 4th through 7th ribs and posterior lateral fractures of the 4th through 10th ribs, creating several flail segments. On the right, anterior fractures were noted at the 4th through 6th ribs. The patient also had a clinical flail chest involving his bilateral chest wall and lower sternum. Numerous healed bilateral rib fractures were also noted from prior equine-related trauma.

(A) Anterior view of CT-3D reconstruction of the thoracic cage demonstrating a mid-body sternal fracture with distraction, bilateral costosternal joint disruptions, and bilateral rib fractures. (B) Corresponding caudal view of CT-3D reconstruction. 3D, three-dimensional.

A left-sided thoracostomy tube was placed after CT imaging. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for non-invasive respiratory support and multimodal pain control, which included ketamine, dexmedetomidine, and fentanyl. He developed severe ICU delirium. He was transferred to our American College of Surgeons’-verified Level 1 trauma center on postinjury day 5 for a higher level of care and was pre-emptively intubated prior to transport.

What we did and why

Given the patient’s extensive chest wall instability, we thought surgical intervention was warranted to restore his breathing mechanics and prevent future pulmonary complications.1 Despite his age and comorbidities (hypertension, atrial flutter, and intermittent complete heart block with pacemaker implantation), he was functionally independent and still running a farm. After discussing the risks and benefits with the patient and his wife, we proceeded with definitive fixation.

A Chevron incision was made along the sternum and extended beneath both pectoral folds. The left pectoralis major was dissected off the chest wall; the right side had been traumatically avulsed. The sternum was dissociated from the chest wall with bilateral costosternal cartilage disruptions and a 5 cm displaced fracture of the mid-sternal body. The stomach had also herniated into the thoracic cavity but remained viable (figure 2A).

Blue arrow points to herniated stomach through the diaphragm. (B) Intraoperative photo after repair showing vertical titanium plates securing the mid-body sternal fracture and horizontal PEEK plates stabilizing bilateral rib fractures and costosternal cartilage disruptions. PEEK, polyetheretherketone.

We then elevated and reduced the distal sternum and stabilized it with two contoured titanium plates secured with screws. Right ribs 5–7 and left ribs 5–6 were similarly reduced and plated with polyetheretherketone (PEEK) plates. To restore the bilateral costosternal cartilages, bilateral bridging PEEK plates spanning the 5th and 6th ribs, costosternal cartilage, and sternum were applied and secured for additional anterior chest wall stabilization (figure 2B). These plates were bridged across the previously placed sternal titanium plates where their paths overlapped. The diaphragmatic hernia was repaired using an Enform mesh and Vicryl sutures.

Postoperatively, the patient remained in the ICU for 6 weeks, requiring a tracheostomy for ventilator dependence. He also experienced an ischemic stroke (without residual deficits), upper gastrointestinal bleeding necessitating multiple endoscopic interventions, and acute cholecystitis managed with percutaneous cholecystostomy tube. Ultimately, he was decannulated, transferred to the floor, and discharged to acute rehabilitation 7 weeks postoperatively (figure 3). 4 months after his operation, he returned home and is currently living independently with his wife.

Reports of operative management in older patients with severe blunt chest wall injury remain sparse,2 3 and rare reports of equine-related trauma show similarly devastating chest wall injury.4 5 Surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) has been shown to reduce ICU length-of-stay, ventilator dependence, pneumonia, and mortality in select patients.6 7 Although SSRF reduces mortality in patients aged 80 years and older (relative risk, 0.41; p<0.01), it may also prolong hospitalization (11 days vs 6 days; p<0.01).8 When examining a cohort limited to frail (modified Frailty Index≥0.25) geriatric patients, a similar reduction in mortality with lengthened hospital stay is observed.9 Our patient experienced multiple complications and prolonged hospital stay after the operation, likely due to his advanced age and comorbidities. Still, we think that this operation aided his ability to survive his extensive chest wall injuries and conferred an overall mortality benefit long-term.10 11 Moreover, SSRF has also been shown to decrease re-admission rates for any reason as well as specifically for pulmonary complications, which may particularly benefit older patients.12 13 Our case showcases the feasibility of surgical stabilization of flail sternum and severe chest wall injury in an octogenarian who underwent high-impact trauma.
