The Crucial Role of Physical Activity Index, in Predicting the Incidence of Pacemaker Syndrome
Authors: Alireza Malekrah, Shadi Amiri, Abbas Alipour, Nader Asgary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile AV dyssynchrony is present in all cases with a single‐chamber pacemaker, pacemaker syndrome (PMS) occurs in only a minority of patients. This study aims to investigate the role of physical activity in the incidence of this syndrome.MethodPhysical activity data of 200 patients receiving a dual‐chamber pacemaker were obtained from the International Physical Activity Questionnaires. They were divided into two groups: Group A with a physical activity level of less than 500 MET‐min/week, and Group B with a higher level. After switching to VVIR mode at discharge, patients were followed for 6 months to monitor the development of pacemaker syndrome.ResultsForty‐seven cases of PMS were recorded, representing an incidence rate of 23%. Group B had significantly more PMS cases than Group A (33 vs. 14, p = 0.002). There were no significant differences in the prevalence of coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and smoking between the two groups. However, hypertension and hypercholesterolemia were more prevalent in Group A. The receiver operating characteristic curve analysis revealed a cut‐off value of 339 MET‐min/week, with a sensitivity of 97.8% and a negative predictive value of 98% for predicting PMS occurrence. The area under the curve was 0.713 (p < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.65–0.77).ConclusionA physical activity level below 399 MET‐min/week can predict the non‐occurrence of PMS with a 98% predictive value. This can help physicians identify patients less likely to develop symptoms.

Introduction

Nowadays, pacemaker implantation is considered the cornerstone of treatment for patients with symptomatic bradyarrhythmia. However, it is not without risks and challenges. One of the most important and well‐known complications is pacemaker syndrome (PMS), which results from atrioventricular dyssynchrony.

This iatrogenic syndrome encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical symptoms resembling heart failure. However, rather than being triggered by contractile dysfunction, it arises from a dyssynchrony in cardiac activity. Although inappropriate programming in a dual‐chamber pacemaker may lead to this syndrome, the most common form of atrioventricular (AV) dyssynchrony results from single‐chamber pacemakers. Given that the diagnosis of this syndrome is primarily based on the patient's complaints and subjective symptoms at a specific time interval after pacemaker implantation, it is challenging to accurately estimate its incidence. In different studies, the reported incidence ranges from 5% to 80% [1, 2].

Although AV dyssynchrony occurs in all patients with a single‐chamber pacemaker, PMS only occurs in some patients. Therefore, beyond the identified underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, it is plausible that another interacting factor plays a significant role in symptom manifestation.

This study investigates the relationship between the physical activity index and PMS, examining whether daily activity intensity and energy expenditure influence symptom occurrence.

Method

This prospective multicenter study was conducted on patients with symptomatic bradyarrhythmia receiving a dual‐chamber PPM. Excluded from the study were patients aged 65 years or older, those with severe CAD, LVEF < 50%, atrial fibrillation, and those with less than 50% ventricular pacing.

Physical activity data obtained from the International Physical Activity Questionnaires (IPAQ) to determine metabolic equivalent (MET) – minutes per week [3]. To calculate MET‐min/week, the MET score for each activity (Table 1) is multiplied by the minutes for each activity over a seven‐day period.

Accordingly, patients were divided into two groups based on their physical activity index: Group A with a low physical activity index (≤ 500 MET‐min/week) and Group B with a high physical activity index (> 500 MET‐min/week).

A total of 733 patients with symptomatic bradyarrhythmia were implanted with a double‐chamber pacemaker in two centers during 5 years from the beginning of 2018 to November 2022. One hundred ninety‐one patients had a high level of physical activity, 100 of whom consented to participate in the study. Each patient in this group was matched with one patient with a low physical activity index in terms of age, with an age difference of no more than 3 years (Figure 1). After implanting a dual‐chamber pacemaker in all patients, the device mode was changed to VVIR at the time of discharge, and the patients were examined monthly for 6 months in terms of both objective and subjective findings.

Study population.

Symptoms of heart failure including shortness of breath, fatigue, dizziness, palpitations, pulsation in the neck, orthopnea, chest pain, cough, and pre‐syncope were considered as subjective evidence of pacemaker syndrome.

If the symptoms were severe enough to require changing the mode to DDD, it was considered a possible pacemaker syndrome, and a definitive diagnosis was made in those cases where symptoms disappeared after changing the mode. Patients were blinded to the device's mode.

The objective evidence of PMS was a drop in blood pressure of more than 20 mmHg when the pacemaker works with VVI mode compared to DDD mode. Blood pressure measurements were performed in patients who reported symptoms of syncope or pre‐syncope.

Ventriculoatrial (VA) conduction was detected using a VVI pacing mode set at a rate of 100 beats per minute. Intact VA conduction was defined as having five or more consecutive beats with a QRS followed by a P wave at a fixed interval. Since VA conduction may be normal at the time of implantation and then become disturbed, it was monitored during each patient visit.

Results

Among 200 patients enrolled in two study groups, 47 cases of PMS were recorded, which accounted for 23% of the patients. The number of PMS cases in the high‐activity group was significantly higher than in the low‐activity group (33 vs. 14, respectively, p = 0.002). The gender distribution was not significantly different between the two groups. There were 45 female patients in Group B and 54 in the other group, with a p value of 0.02. The difference in mean age was not statistically significant (p = 0.7) between the two studied groups, with a mean age of 60.45 years in Group A and 60 years in Group B. Notably, despite the absence of significant differences in the prevalence of underlying conditions such as coronary artery disease (CAD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and smoking between the two groups, we observed that hypertension (29 vs. 16, p = 0.02) and hypercholesterolemia (24 vs. 16, p = 0.04) were significantly more prevalent in Group A. Additionally, no significant difference in retrograde ventriculoatrial conduction was observed between the two groups (Table 2).

Discussion

The main finding of this study was the higher incidence of pacemaker syndrome in patients with a high physical activity index compared to those with a low physical activity index.

The relationship between single‐chamber pacing and the occurrence of pacemaker syndrome due to atrioventricular dyssynchrony is well‐known. Since the diagnosis of PMS is defined based on various symptoms and mostly subjective criteria, different statistics regarding its prevalence have been reported. The findings of this study, showing a 23% prevalence, fall within the previously reported range of 5%–80% and are close to the 20% incidence reported by Link et al. [4].

Although pacemaker syndrome occurs due to the pathophysiology of AV dyssynchrony, based on the available evidence, this underlying disturbance is thought to cause the PMS in only about one‐fifth of cases. Several studies have examined the predictors of this disorder. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the role of the physical activity index as a primary predictor of PMS.

The most probable mechanism for the higher incidence of PMS in patients with a high physical activity index.

VA Conduction

In about 90% of hearts with a normal conduction system, retrograde conduction occurs. In contrast, the incidence of retrograde conduction is ~15% in patients with atrioventricular block, according to previous studies [5, 6]. Retrograde conduction causes non‐physiological depolarization and contraction of the atrium, out of sync with ventricular timing.

Nishimura et al. conducted a study on patients who underwent pacemaker implantation with VVI mode. They showed that following ventricular pacing in patients with intact retrograde conduction, the blood pressure drop was greater than in patients with impaired VA conduction. This finding suggests that retrograde conduction is an important predictor of PMS incidence [7].

In the current study, the number of patients in Group B and Group A was 17 and 20, respectively, and this difference was not statistically significant. This implies that VA conduction alone cannot explain the difference in PMS incidence between the two groups, regardless of the physical activity index.

Aging and Cardiovascular Diseases

Since the symptoms of PMS, such as orthopnea, shortness of breath, fatigue, and so on, are like those of heart failure, the confounding factors of aging as well as underlying cardiovascular diseases can mimic PMS by producing similar symptoms, making it challenging to draw conclusions.

In this study, we found that there were no significant differences in average age and prevalence rates of smoking, CKD, and COPD between the two investigated groups. While hypercholesterolemia and HTN were more common in Group A, the higher incidence of PMS in Group B does not necessarily imply a causal relationship between these conditions.

Isolated Ventricular Dyssynchrony

Apart from AV dyssynchrony, which is considered the underlying pathophysiology of PMS, Soto et al. reported a distinct cause. In their examined case, despite the implantation of a dual‐chamber pacemaker with appropriate settings and maintenance of atrioventricular synchrony, the symptoms of the syndrome occurred after pacing. Echocardiography with speckle‐tracking imaging revealed that pacing, compared to intrinsic conduction, causes dyskinesia in the lateral and apical walls and a significant decrease in global longitudinal strain (GLS). Therefore, isolated LV dyssynchrony was proposed as the cause of PMS [8].

Based on this, an important question is raised: Is isolated LV dyssynchrony the primary predictor and predisposing factor through which exercise exerts its effect as a precipitating factor? Previous studies have shown that LV dyssynchrony is a dynamic phenomenon that can change in response to various factors, including physical activity. While exercise exacerbates LV dyssynchrony in 34% of cases, it can improve it in 29% of cases [9]. Therefore, this variation does not fully account for the significant differences in PMS incidence between groups (~200%).

Increased Percentage of Pacing

The MOST trial showed that the only pre‐ and post‐implantation predictors of PMS incidence would be a higher programmed rate and a higher percentage of paced beats, respectively. Although the MOST trial was conducted on patients with sinus node dysfunction (SND), this predictor is completely in line with the findings of our study and can have a pathophysiological justification in AV block patients as well. In patients with SND, as well as those with paroxysmal AV block, an increase in pacing leads to a reduction in native beats and intrinsic conduction. Consequently, there would be an increase in the percentage of AV dyssynchronous beats.

Since there is no intrinsic conduction and AV synchrony in persistent AV block, an increase in pacing rate does not affect the percentage of dyssynchronous beats but rather worsens the degree of dyssynchronicity. For example, if the most severe dyssynchrony is assumed to be the simultaneous contraction of the atria and ventricles, it is expected that the greatest number of such AV coincident beats will occur during exercise and with rate‐adaptive mode. In this scenario, diastolic time is reduced, causing the mitral valve to remain closed for most of the exercise‐induced tachycardia episode.

The impact of physical activity on PMS may counteract the positive effects of aerobic exercise on cardiovascular adaptations. This underscores the need for further research into the effects of aerobic training on individuals with pacemaker implants.

Based on these findings, it seems that the physical activity level in these patients is the most important predictor of an increased pacing rate and, consequently, PMS incidence.

This study and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis reveal that a physical activity level below 339 MET‐min/week, which is the optimal cut‐off value, can predict the PMS occurrence with a negative predictive value of 98%. The importance of this feature lies in its ability to assist physicians in making informed decisions about the pacing mode for elderly, frail patients, as well as cases with intermittent bradycardia or limited venous access, without the concern of PMS occurrence [10]. This is especially important because overall complications and pneumothorax are less likely to occur with a single‐cavity pacemaker implantation [11].

Limitation

A significant limitation of the current study was the lack of double‐blinding. The patients were unaware of the fact that the mode had changed, while the doctor recording the symptoms was aware of these changes. This bias is particularly crucial when the gathered information pertains to subjective symptoms that cannot be precisely measured and relies solely on observers' judgment.

Conclusion

Although pacemaker syndrome occurs in the setting of atrioventricular (AV) dyssynchrony, it only affects about one‐fifth of these patients.

The present study focuses on patients' daily activity patterns and their intensity. Our results suggest that the most significant predictor of this syndrome is the physical activity level, which increases the rate and severity of atrioventricular (AV) dyssynchrony.

According to the findings of this study, a physical activity level of less than 339 MET‐min/week can predict the non‐occurrence of PMS. This indicates that such a level can help physicians identify patients who are unlikely to develop symptoms, with a predictive value of 98%.
