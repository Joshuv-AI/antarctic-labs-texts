Custom distal humerus reconstruction for severe bone loss in revision and complex primary total elbow arthroplasty
Authors: Alexander A Hysong, Alex R Oles, Joseph E Tanenbaum, Jaques L Williams, Micah J Nieboer, Joaquin Sanchez-Sotelo, Mark E Morrey
Published: 2026

“A basic overriding consideration in planning any (total elbow) reimplantation procedure is the use of an implant that is significantly different from the one that failed.”11 Although published over 40 years ago, this statement by Bernard Morrey remains highly relevant, as the need for innovative solutions in complex primary and revision total elbow arthroplasty (TEA) continues to grow.

TEA remains an effective treatment for patients with debilitating elbow pathology.4 Although performed less frequently than other large joint arthroplasties, TEA is associated with substantially higher complication rates. One systematic review estimated the long-term complication rate of TEA to be approximately 16%, with a revision rate of 14%, most commonly due to aseptic loosening.7 Furthermore, despite a steady rise in primary TEA utilization, projections suggest that by 2060 the number of revision TEA procedures may exceed primary procedures.3

While a few revision procedures are limited to débridement and polyethylene exchange, many are substantially more complex. Numerous reconstructive strategies have been described to address bone loss at the time of TEA, including impaction grafting, strut allograft augmentation, allograft prosthetic composites (APCs) reconstruction, and metallic “tumor” prosthesis.13

In elbows with severe segmental bone loss, APC with triceps reconstruction has been successful in our practice for the ulnar side of the joint.1 On the contrary, the failure rate of APCs on the humeral side has been disappointing. In 2023, Cheema et al4 reported the outcomes following humeral APC reconstruction with compression plate fixation. Despite acceptable patient-reported outcomes, reoperation and nonunion rates remained high at 34% and 36%, respectively.

Given these challenges, alternative reconstructive strategies are needed for patients with severe segmental distal humeral bone loss. Recently, we have increasingly utilized custom 3D-printed distal humerus components in an effort to provide a more durable and reliable solution. The purpose of this article is to describe our current approach to the design of these custom implants and their surgical implementation (Video 1).

Indications and pre-operative planning

Patient selection, indications, and contraindications

Indications for custom distal humerus reconstruction parallel those for distal humerus APC reconstruction. When any residual humeral bone stock is insufficient to support a standard humeral component, even if strut augmentation or impaction grafting were attempted, a custom implant can be considered. Typically, these elbows present with absence or compromise of both epicondyles and bone loss extending into the distal third to 2/3 of the humerus (Fig. 1). This can be the result of failed replacement or revision replacement surgery, periprosthetic fractures, oncologic resections, or rarely the sequelae of severe trauma.4

Two examples of severe bone loss about the elbow necessitating custom megaprosthesis reconstruction. (A) A patient who has had significant distal humerus and proximal ulna resection for recurrent infection; (B and C) A patient who had aseptic loosening of his humeral component and subsequent periprosthetic fracture with a well -fixed ulnar component.

Custom prostheses are relatively contraindicated when the residual proximal humeral bone is limited to the humeral head and proximal humeral metaphysis. In these circumstances, we have been successful with APC reconstructions provided the fixation is achieved using an extended deltopectoral approach, which is counterintuitive in the setting of revision elbow arthroplasty.5 Another possible contraindication for the use of these implants is persistent infection, and oftentimes these implants are used at the second stage of a 2-stage reimplantation in these circumstances.

Vendor selection and manufacturing considerations

Vendor selection is an important component of pre-operative planning. Several domestic and international companies offer custom three-dimensional-printed implants, each with unique design capabilities and production workflows. At our institution, we have worked with multiple vendors; our greatest experience has been with Restor3D (Durham, NC). While some features described in this article reflect our experience with this company, the principles outlined are broadly applicable regardless of manufacturer, and none of the authors have a potential conflict of interest with Restor3D.

Lead time prior to implantation is a critical consideration. Manufacturing timelines, including design iteration, printing, quality assurance, and shipping, frequently range from 6 weeks to several months. Early recognition of the need for a custom implant allows coordination with staged procedures, such as in the setting of periprosthetic infection requiring prolonged antibiotics.

The planning process requires use of computed tomography with relatively thin slices to allow for accurate segmentation of the remaining humerus and any retained implants and cement. Bilateral radiographs with magnification markers are extremely useful too, and the patient range of motion needs to be considered when deciding on implant length, since too short of an implant will lead to hyperextension, whereas restoration of native length in a patient with a severe pre-operative flexion contracture may be problematic.

Implant design principles

We reason that humeral component and APC failure after revision TEA is mostly the consequence of torque. When individuals use their upper extremities in space for activities of daily living, the weight of the upper extremity and any objects being held will impart an internal rotation moment that can surpass the primary stability of implants and plate fixation.9,11

When we help design custom elbow humeral implants, we try to incorporate several features intended to resist axial, bending, and torsional stresses while addressing deficiencies in bone stock. The principal design elements include (Figs. 2 and 3): (1) a humeral stem long enough and with flutes to provide additional rotational stability secondary to cement macro interlock, (2) a posterior plate to accommodate locking screws across the humeral cortices and through the stem, (3) an anterior flange, and (4) a porous ingrowth surface.

If there is a retained ulnar implant that will not be exchanged, the manufacturer will need the specifications of the corresponding humeral implant articulation; for example, if there is a retained Latitude EV ulnar component, the manufacturing company must have access to the specifications of the corresponding small, medium, or large humeral spool so that the implants can be properly articulated. If the ulnar implant will be exchanged or reimplanted, the surgeon can choose the preferred ulnar component and match the humeral distal articulating portion accordingly.

Resection strategy and humeral body design

Selection of the resection level is an important first step of the component design. Diseased and nonstructural bone is best removed while preserving essential soft tissue attachments, particularly the deltoid insertion for more proximal defects. Ideally, a circumferential cortical segment is maintained to permit compression at the bone–implant junction and facilitate cementation of the stem (Fig. 4).

Example of a proposed resection level for the patient with aseptic loosening of his humeral component. As shown, the proposed bony resection is just proximal to the level of nonstructural bone.

Whenever possible, the resection level is chosen at a site with distinctive osseous anatomy to assist with intraoperative implant positioning (Fig. 5). This can represent a retained metallic wire, a change in the shape of the cortex, or any other easily identifiable landmark. The known length of failed components is useful to plan the resection level as well. As mentioned before, although restoration of native humeral length is ideal, slight shortening is sometimes advantageous. Many patients present with contracted soft tissues and triceps deficiency due to prolonged bone loss; modest shortening helps facilitate wound closure and extensor mechanism reconstruction.

In this patient with a very large humeral defect, we see how the bony resection plan accounts for the unique surface anatomy. The planned resection allows for appropriate implant positioning and rotation. Including bony landmarks like these can greatly assist with implant placement.

Implant width at the bone–implant interface is matched to native cortical dimensions to maximize contact and potential ingrowth. Distally, the implant is tapered slightly before widening to recreate the natural distal humeral flare (Fig. 6).

Note that the width of the humeral body matches the native bone at the bone-implant interface before tapering slightly and then expanding into a native epicondylar flare.

Stem design

Stem dimensions are determined in collaboration with device engineers. In general, stem length approximates two-thirds of the humeral body length, though this varies depending on residual bone stock (Fig. 7). We design the stem to accommodate a minimum of 3 3.5 mm locking screws engaging both the posterior plate and the stem.

Stem length and width: (A) Note that the stem length is roughly two-thirds the length of the implant's humeral body. (B). Lateral view of the stem demonstrating an intentional 1-2 mm gap between the stem and the endosteal bone. This facilitates seating and permits an adequate cement mantle.

Stem diameter is intentionally undersized to allow approximately 2 mm (Fig. 7) between the endosteal bone and the stem core. This facilitates seating and permits an adequate cement mantle, optimizing fixation.9,10 Care must be taken with any areas of thick retained cement that may not be easily accessible, since a smaller diameter stem may be required in those circumstances. Six peripheral flutes, approximately 75 mm in width, are incorporated around the stem (Fig. 8). The flutes increase the surface area of the stem in contact with the cement and theoretically enhance rotational stability of the implant, as mentioned previously.

Stem flutes—(A) 3D rendered image depicting an end-on view of the implants' fluted stem. Outlined in red, we see the 6 × .75 mm flutes that are designed into the stem to both increase surface area and enhance rotational stability. (B) Intraoperative image of the stem. Also seen are the holes made in the stem that allow for interlocking screw fixation. 3D, three-dimensional.

Posterior plate and interlocking screw fixation

A posterior plate extension of the humeral body rests on the dorsal cortex of the residual humerus. Although not specifically studied in this clinical scenario, biomechanical data from humeral trauma and oncologic reconstruction support improved axial, bending, and torsional stability with interlocking screw fixation.2,3 Typically, 3 to 4 3.5 mm locking screws are incorporated with pre-planned trajectories based on CT imaging. Screws are designed to engage both cortices and pass through the stem (Fig. 9). Because proximal exposure is frequently limited by neurovascular structures and the soft tissues (Fig. 10), the most proximal screw is often angled approximately 30° to facilitate instrumentation and potentially improve stress distribution along the length of the implant (Fig. 9).

Example of our posterior plate-screw-stem interlocking construct. Note the angle of the proximal screw. Orienting the screw proximally significantly aids with instrumentation and decreases the need for more proximal exposure.

Example of the typical intraoperative humeral exposure when there is significant bone loss. While possible to extend far proximal from a posterior approach, the altered anatomy and neurovascular strictures often make this difficult. (A) Radial nerve; (B) Ulnar nerve.

Anterior flange

Inclusion of an anterior flange is believed to be a critical design component. It is generally accepted that a properly supported anterior flange improves load transfer and resists posteriorly directed and rotational forces in TEA.6,11 More recent data suggest that a flange measuring at least one-quarter of the stem length may reduce the risk of re-revision in revision TEA.14 As such, flange length is typically one-quarter of the stem length or slightly longer.

We design the flange to sit approximately 2–3 mm proud of the anterior cortex. This is important because if the distance between the posterior plate and the anterior flange is too narrow, it may impede stem seating. However, the anterior flange must be supported (Fig. 11). Autograft cortical bone is preferred when available; alternatively, allograft strut or cement may be placed between the anterior cortex of the humerus and the posterior aspect of the flange.

Example of the anterior flange design. Note distance between the flange and the anterior cortical bone. This permits space for either structural bone grafting or cement placement.

Porous ingrowth surface

Although fixation primarily relies on cement and screw fixation, we incorporate Restor3D's proprietary “tidal” ingrowth technology in select regions for theoretical biologic fixation.10 This porous surface is typically applied to the anterior aspect of the posterior plate and the proximal humeral body at the bone–implant junction—areas most likely to contact native bone without intervening cement. Future strategies may incorporate additional hybrid stem fixation; however, we have not yet adopted ingrowth stem integration.

Surgical technique

Exposure

Surgical approach depends on the extent of bone loss and triceps integrity. When the triceps mechanism is intact, we prefer a lateral paraolecranon approach with medial paratricipital extension as needed (Fig. 12). The radial and ulnar nerves are identified early and protected with vessel loops (Fig. 13). Given the frequency of prior surgery and dense scarring, meticulous dissection is required. When possible, dissection proceeds proximally into more native tissue planes to safely mobilize neurovascular structures.

Example of our typical incision and approach for cases with isolated humeral bone loss. We often attempt to achieve a majority of our exposure through a lateral paraolecranon approach.

Example of early identification of critical neurovascular structures. (A) Radial nerve; (B) Ulnar nerve.

Bony preparation

Following neurovascular protection, enough proximal exposure is obtained to accommodate the length of the posterior plate. Pre-operative plans and 3D-printed guides and trials are referenced repeatedly in the operating room to ensure accurate resection (Fig. 14). Meticulous soft tissue clearance is essential to expose osseous landmarks. After resection, the humeral canal is sequentially reamed according to the pre-operative plan if needed (Fig. 15).

Trialing and soft tissue assessment

A 3D-printed plastic trial component is inserted to assess seating, rotation, and alignment. Adjustments to the resection may be performed if necessary to facilitate appropriate flush implant positioning. It is important to note that the trial stem is slightly shorter than the real stem. Thus, if a proximal humeral implant is present, the surgeon must critically assess whether or not the stem length is acceptable to ensure the proper length and depth of insertion. Other means of ensuring the overall depth of the stem length should be employed. The diameter of the stem trial is also slightly smaller, and this should also be carefully assessed to ensure no additional reaming is necessary to seat the final implant. Soft tissue tension is then carefully evaluated. With the humeral trial articulated to the trial ulnar component (or retained implant), the arm is allowed to extend freely. The goal is for the anterior soft tissues to provide sufficient tension to prevent hyperextension of the elbow, even when the forearm is unsupported (Fig. 16). In select cases, slightly proud implant positioning may optimize soft tissue tension. Alternatively, if elbow extension is very limited with the trials, consideration can be given to resecting some humeral bone to place the component deeper, but this may be limited by the amount of remaining bone available in the proximal humerus. When the ulnar side of the elbow also needs bone reconstruction, additional tension adjustment can be achieved through ulnar component positioning.

Trialing. (A) Example of our 3D-printed trial used to assess soft tissue tension. Note that the trial stem is often slightly shorter and narrower than the real implant by several mm. (B) Note full flexion. Goal is for a normal hand-to-mouth flexion arc without undue varus or valgus stress applied to the elbow. (C) Anterior soft tissues providing sufficient tension to resist hyperextension at the elbow. 3D, three-dimensional.

Final fixation

Cementation is performed according to “Morrey criteria,” aiming for less than 1 mm of radiolucency at the cement–bone interface and cement extending beyond the stem tip.8,12

A cement restrictor is placed approximately 1–2 mm proximal to the stem tip. The canal is irrigated and dried. Two batches of poly methyl methacrylate cement with 1 g of vancomycin and a few drops of methylene blue per batch are used. Cement is pressurized into the canal prior to stem insertion. The implant is inserted in the planned position and rotation and the cement is allowed to cure.

The posterior plate is designed to accommodate a locking screw drill guide from a preferred vendor, ensuring proper drill trajectory and ultimately screw insertion (Fig. 17). Although pre-operative measurements are obtained during planning, intraoperative confirmation of screw length with a depth gauge is recommended, as measured lengths may deviate slightly from the pre-operative plan.

Posterior plate designed to accommodate a locking tower of the surgeons preferred vendor. This helps insure appropriate screw trajectory and insertion.

Finally, an autograft, allograft, or cement is placed behind the anterior flange to provide additional structural support as mentioned prior.

Ulnar reconstruction

Ulnar instrumentation is performed as indicated by bone stock. As mentioned before, in elbows with severe proximal ulnar bone loss and triceps deficiency, we have been successful with the use of APC incorporating triceps allograft reconstruction.1 A detailed description of this technique is beyond the scope of this article.

Closure and post-operative management

The triceps is repaired with nonabsorbable #2 braided suture in a side-to-side fashion. Deep tissues are closed with absorbable sutures, and skin is closed with staples. An incisional wound vacuum may be used at surgeon discretion. Patients are splinted in extension for 2–3 weeks to allow posterior incision healing. In the absence of triceps reconstruction, gentle range of motion is initiated after the first post-operative visit. When triceps reconstruction is performed, extension splinting is maintained for 6 weeks, followed by gradual flexion using a hinged elbow brace (Fig. 18).

Conclusions

This technique describes a custom distal humerus reconstruction strategy for severe humeral bone loss in revision TEA and selected complex primary TEA. Our early experience has been encouraging, with robust humeral-sided fixation that we believe may offer improved durability compared with our prior experience using humeral APC reconstructions.

One additional benefit of this strategy is a reduction in the overall surgical time. However, all these benefits must be balanced against the prolonged time to plan and manufacture, the substantial average price of these implants, the limited availability of manufacturer specifications for cross-compatibility, and the lack of long-term results.

Although longer-term follow-up is needed, we do anticipate that this approach may provide a more reliable solution for complex revision scenarios and stimulate continued innovation in the management of severe distal humeral bone loss.
