Long-term treatment of Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome in an adult patient using multimodal laser therapy
Authors: Hannah Welp, Hailey Grubbs, Anthony Benedetto
Published: 2026

Introduction

Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome (KTS) is a complex vascular disorder characterized by a triad of port-wine stains (PWS), varicosities, and hypertrophy involving soft tissue and bones.1 Diagnosis requires at least 2 of these hallmark features.2

Pathogenesis is attributed to somatic gain-of-function mutations in phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha, leading to disrupted cell growth and malformed vessels. Mutations involving angiogenic factor with G-patch and FHA domains 1 have also been associated with KTS.1,3

KTS commonly presents with unilateral involvement of the upper and lower extremities. Rare cases may include the trunk, head, and neck.4 Abnormalities may arise in capillaries, lymphatics, and veins, causing patchy capillary malformations, varicose veins, and venous atresia. Limb length discrepancies and hemihypertrophy result from bone and soft tissue overgrowth. Complications include pain, stasis dermatitis, lymphedema, hemorrhage, ulceration, thrombosis, and thrombophlebitis. Multisystem involvement can manifest with intestinal bleeding, pulmonary embolisms, and pericardial effusions.2,5 Management requires multidisciplinary care to address complications, provide symptomatic relief, and improve quality of life. We discuss long-term use of multimodal laser therapy in our patient with KTS.

Case report

Our patient is a 64-year-old woman with KTS who presented in 1998 for treatment of facial redness. Examination revealed PWS with spontaneous bleeding and hemihypertrophy of her left face, arms, abdomen, and left leg. She reported gradual darkening and thickening of her PWS and progressive left face and neck hemihypertrophy over the years (Fig 1). Initial management targeted angiomatous facial plaques and nodules using a potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) laser with 2 mm spot size, 8 J/cm2 fluence, 9 ms pulse duration, and 4 pulses per second and a 595-nm pulse dye laser (PDL) with 7 mm spot size, 7 to 7.5 J/cm2 fluence, and 6 to 10 ms pulse duration. Despite significant post-treatment purpura and edema (Fig 2), managed with cool compresses, she noted improvement in her PWS thickness and redness. She had 3 KTP and 7 PDL treatments over 2 years, with device usage and treatment timeline dependent on device and patient availability. The patient was lost to follow-up while raising her children. Fourteen years later, she returned in 2013 for additional treatments after noting progressive facial enlargement. Alternating treatments using the 595-nm PDL and a 1064-nm neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser targeted deeper vascular malformations and nodular components. Nd:YAG settings included a 1.5 to 3 mm spot size, 60 to 200 ms pulse width, and 200 J/cm2 fluence. While the Nd:YAG was effective in treating deeper vascular blebs, tissue necrosis resulted in several areas that required additional treatment with a fractionated CO2 laser to improve residual scarring and textural abnormalities. Over the last 12 years, she completed 31 PDL, 8 Nd:YAG, and 4 fractional CO2 treatments. After numerous treatments, her facial appearance has improved (Fig 3). Textural improvements demonstrated transformation of cobble-stoned plaques into smooth patches. The dark purple color became a light pink-violet hue. After a few treatments, spontaneous bleeding resolved. Both the treating physician and patient noted slowing of soft tissue growth, and her face now appears smaller. Despite persistent facial hemihypertrophy, she is highly satisfied with treatment outcomes.

Discussion

KTS is a rare multisystem condition requiring multidisciplinary care. Dermatologists play an important role addressing the cutaneous vascular malformations. Our case represents an uncommon KTS presentation involving the head and neck. Various laser treatments spanned decades and improved her psychosocial distress.

Initial usage of the KTP laser and 595-nm PDL targeted angiomatous plaques and nodules through selective photothermolysis and energy absorption by hemoglobin to promote fading of superficial vascular lesions. The tissue hypertrophy of KTS from arterial overgrowth and hypertrophic lesions is not effectively treated with the KTP or PDL, which prompted use of the 1064-nm Nd:YAG laser. Decreased absorption by hemoglobin allows the 1064-nm wavelength to penetrate deeper but requires higher fluences and should be used cautiously because of the risk for bulk heating and tissue necrosis, as seen in our patient. The 755-nm Alexandrite laser could offer an alternative option, allowing deeper penetration than PDL with reduced risk of necrosis.6 As angiomatous nodules improved, a fractional CO2 laser addressed textural changes and pigmentation. CO2 lasers target water via selective photolysis, causing superficial ablation of the epidermis and parts of dermis,7 which stimulates re-epithelialization and dermal remodeling to improve pigmentation and texture.8 Advancements in laser technology, including larger spot sizes and integrated contact cooling, have improved treatment of larger vascular areas while minimizing collateral damage and show promising treatment options.

Management of PWS in KTS differs from isolated PWS in infants. The PWS in KTS can involve deeper venous and lymphatic components, underlying tissue hypertrophy, and progressive evolution. A proposed mesodermal anomaly affecting angiogenesis could contribute to various types of tissue overgrowth seen in KTS.5 Current guidelines recommend compression therapy, limb elevation, and lifestyle modifications. Interventions such as laser therapy, sclerotherapy, radiofrequency, or laser ablation are often also warranted.9 Early intervention benefits isolated PWS and may prove similarly in KTS to minimize the excessive soft tissue growth after complete PWS clearance.6,10 Our patient’s 13 years without treatment led to notable soft tissue growth but slowed when treatments resumed. Study limitations include lack of control to evaluate rates of post-treatment soft tissue hypertrophy. Further studies would help determine the effects of early intervention in KTS. Exploring treatment options that target genetic or angiogenic pathways may offer enhanced control of tissue overgrowth. Other lasers, like krypton, alexandrite, and copper lasers, could target darker, deeper refractory lesions.9 We propose an ideal treatment interval of 4 to 6 weeks, allowing for resolution ecchymosis, edema, and erythema while minimizing disease progression. Given the need for multiple treatments, strict adherence may be difficult, and further research could evaluate optimal protocols.

This case highlights the value of multimodal laser therapy in KTS; with advancements in laser technology and increased awareness, early intervention could significantly reduce morbidity in this rare and complex syndrome.
