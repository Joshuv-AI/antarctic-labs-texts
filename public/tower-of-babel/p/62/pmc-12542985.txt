Delayed gastric conduit emptying after esophagectomy: attempt at a clinically relevant classification
Authors: Marcel A Schneider, Lorenzo Viggiani D’Avalos, Stephan Gerdes, Diana Vetter, Christian A Gutschow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDelayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE) is a common functional syndrome after esophagectomy. The clinical phenotype is characterized by regurgitation, reflux, and the inability to meet caloric requirements by oral intake. Diagnosis and cause-directed therapy are often challenging because of variable underlying pathomechanisms. Against this background and based on our clinical experience, we propose a classification that categorizes DGCE according to the predominant case-specific pathophysiology: Class I: functional—DGCE due to impaired antro-pyloric motilityClass II: conduit-related—DGCE caused by specific patho-anatomical features of the conduitClass III: hiatal—DGCE resulting from mechanical obstruction at the diaphragmatic hiatusClass IV: other—Other causes of DGCE at or distal to the pylorus (e.g. luminal occlusion, intestinal adhesions or peritoneal carcinomatosis)We believe that this classification has the potential to promote a better understanding of the symptom spectrum of DGCE and to facilitate cause-oriented, focused treatment. Moreover, we are convinced that a structured definition of DGCE according to pathophysiology will improve the comparability of different patient cohorts and thus promote future collaborative research.

INTRODUCTION

Esophagectomy with gastric conduit reconstruction is the procedure of choice for curative treatment of esophageal cancer. It is a life-changing surgery with significant modifications to the anatomy and physiology of the digestive tract. The rate of impaired alimentary function is high: more than 80% of patients experience at least some sort of digestive discomfort at follow-up.3,4 The spectrum of functional syndromes following esophagectomy is broad and includes dysphagia, gastroesophageal reflux, dumping syndrome, and delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE).5,6 Most patients have only minor and temporary symptoms that can be attributed to the inevitable postoperative changes in foregut anatomy and physiology—management is therefore usually conservative. Selected cases, however, may benefit from therapeutic interventions.

Among all post-esophagectomy functional syndromes, DGCE is one of the most common and troublesome, affecting up to 50% of patients.1,3,7,8 DGCE refractory to conservative treatment occurs in approximately 1–5% of patients after esophagectomy with gastric conduit interposition1,9–11 and is associated with impaired long-term outcomes such as malnutrition and reduced quality of life.12–16 The pathophysiology of DGCE includes various potentially overlapping functional and anatomical abnormalities. Clinical assessment and diagnosis are therefore complex, making therapy highly challenging. In this context, the diagnostic criteria for DGCE that have been recently published as part of an international expert consensus represent an important achievement.17,18

However, owing to the difficult pathophysiology and the relative rarity of the disease, there is clearly a lack of consensus on clinical management. Against this background, we herewith propose a new classification of DGCE. We believe that this classification not only facilitates targeted therapy of DGCE but also has the potential to improve comparability of outcomes between different centers. We are therefore confident that our proposal will be met with interest, as there is no classification for this important postoperative syndrome available in the current literature.

DIAGNOSTIC WORK-UP IN SUSPECTED DGCE

Clinical presentation and assessment of symptoms

Symptoms of DGCE are unspecific and range from early satiety, bloating, postprandial fullness, and chest pain to reflux symptoms, regurgitation, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and dysphagia.19,20 In advanced situations, patients may struggle to meet caloric requirements by oral intake. In case of DGCE caused by paraconduit herniation, the clinical picture may be even more variable, as herniated organs or dilated conduits may lead to displacement and compression of thoracic structures, resulting in pulmonary or cardiac problems. In contrast, patients with DGCE caused by intestinal obstruction usually present with a clinical picture typical of ileus including abdominal distension, cramps, and vomiting.

Due to the variable clinical phenotype, structured symptom assessment is of central importance and should be based on the established DGCE symptom rating criteria by Konradsson et al.18 Depending on individual symptoms, a broader assessment using other validated questionnaires such as the EORTC QLQ C30, the GERD-HRQL, or the Eckardt score21 can be performed.

Upper-GI endoscopy, contrast radiography, and CT scan

Upper-GI endoscopy and conventional contrast examinations are standard first-line clinical examinations for diagnosing DGCE. Conventional upper GI contrast series can be performed with either water-soluble or barium oral contrast and it should be noted that the technical steps of radiological imaging for DGCE are not defined, so that interpretation remains subjective and examiner dependent.

Second line investigations

In clinically unclear situations, an extended diagnostic attempt can be made with scintigraphy22 or a ‘smart pill’ (Given Imaging, Yoqneam, Israel), which uses wireless transmission of pH data to indicate passage across the pylorus into the alkaline duodenal environment. Similarly, the 13C-octanoate breath test monitors the transpyloric passage of a 13C-isotope labeled meal. Although its clinical value has not yet been conclusively clarified, the functional luminal imaging probe (FLIP) is another promising and increasingly used adjunct to assess the physiological properties of the pylorus in DGCE.5,23 A new method for noninvasive assessment of post-esophagectomy conduit function is the evaluation of gastric electrophysiology at high resolution via Gastric Alimetry® (Alimetry Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand).24,25 However, further studies are needed to clarify whether these promising approaches are effective in patients following esophagectomy.

Pathophysiological mechanisms of DGCE and therapeutic options

Impaired antro-pyloric motility

Vagotomy, sympathectomy, and disruption of the intramural myenteric plexus are unavoidable collaterals of esophagectomy for cancer, and it is generally recognized that such total denervation can result in severe functional impairment of the conduit. Although antro-pyloric motility and even acid secretion have been shown to recover over time,26–28 gastric motor dysfunction and lacking relaxation of the pylorus remain the most common causes of DGCE at short- and long-term follow-up16. In addition, the pressure gradient between the thoracic and abdominal body cavities contributes to prolonged conduit voiding after gastric pull-up.29

Due to its high availability and efficiency and because of its low invasiveness and complication rate, endoscopic balloon dilatation of the pylorus is the procedure of choice for primary treatment (Fig. 1A). It is performed in up to 20% of patients in the first 6 months after esophagectomy, with around 30% of these patients requiring multiple procedures.16,30–32 More recently, FLIP-guided balloon dilatation has emerged as an elegant procedure combining diagnostics and therapy in a controlled manner.33 In contrast, injection of botulinum toxin into the pylorus has been largely abandoned due to its temporary only mechanism of action and possibly elevated rates of complications,34 but can be a valuable diagnostic tool to substantiate the diagnosis of DGCE owed to pyloric spasm. If balloon dilatation has only temporary clinical effect, Gastric PerOral Endoscopic Myotomy of the pyloric region (G-POEM) may be another endoscopic treatment option.35–37 However, G-POEM requires advanced endoscopic skills and should only be performed by highly specialized endoscopy units. Likewise, the implantation of neurostimulators may be beneficial; however, the published evidence is too limited for a general recommendation.38,39

In case of failure of interventional treatment, surgical pyloroplasty, or diversion of the conduit with a Roux-en-Y jejunal loop can be an option40,41 (Fig. 1B). Due to the high efficacy of interventional endoscopy, surgical pyloroplasty is rarely done in the postoperative setting. However, some surgeons perform preventive pyloroplasty during index surgery. Roux-en-Y conduit diversion remains a useful tool, although its main advantage probably lies not in reducing DGCE but in preventing bilio-pancreatic reflux. It requires transection of the prepyloric antrum while strictly avoiding injury to the vascular arcade. A jejunal limb is then brought up and anastomosed to the proximal gastric stump, and the Roux anastomosis is created 50–80 cm distally. An RCT found decreased amylase concentrations in the gastric tube, albeit at the cost of increased intraoperative blood loss and longer operation times.42

Conduit-specific DGCE

Anatomical abnormalities of the esophageal substitute may severely affect its emptying. In this category, the most common conditions include conduit dilatation (CD) and conduit redundancy (CR). CD is characterized by an enlarged esophageal replacement organ, while CR is defined by excess length of the conduit. Of note, CD and CR often overlap, and clinical differentiation is difficult due to similar clinical presentation. Both conditions can result from technical errors during index surgery, but most commonly develop during follow-up because of progressive ‘wear and tear’ due to the unphysiological conditions after gastric pull-up. Excessive conduit length may lead to kinking and delayed luminal passage,43 whereas DGCE in CD depends on Laplace's law, where a larger diameter results in lower luminal pressure, thereby impeding voiding. Due to the degenerative etiology, most patients present symptoms during later follow-up. More rarely, however, technical errors such as inaccurate stapling or twisting of the gastric tube may lead to conduit stenosis above the diaphragmatic level, typically resulting in an hourglass-shaped conduit with pre-stenotic dilatation (Fig. 1C). In our experience, these patients become symptomatic early during postoperative diet build-up.

In patients with conduit-specific issues, we believe that a primary treatment attempt with endoscopic balloon dilatation is always warranted, because even conduit tortuosity or stenosis may benefit from interventional measures. Some patients tolerate a soft oral diet after stent implantation, and a previously dilated and decompensated conduit may re-tonicize following the resolution of mechanical obstruction. However, endoscopic interventions often provide only temporary relief, and stents have a high risk of migration due to the relatively large conduit diameter. Therefore, reoperations, individualized and minimally invasive whenever possible, must be considered for a significant subset of patients suffering from CD/CR. In the literature, success rates for revisional surgery in terms of complete symptom relief or minimal persistent symptoms range from 65% to 90%.10,43 In one of the largest series, Kent et al. reported 65% of patients with minimal or absent symptoms after surgery, 20% with improved but still persistent symptoms, and 15% without improvement.10

Mechanical obstruction at the diaphragmatic hiatus

A relatively narrow diaphragmatic hiatus may cause mechanical obstruction and DGCE. This condition may derive from an individual anatomical predisposition or a technical error during index surgery, such as a pre-emptive crurorrhaphy to mitigate the risk of PCH.44 Similarly, herniation of intra-abdominal content through the hiatus can lead to hiatal narrowing and subsequent DGCE. Of note, PCH is a frequent phenomenon occurring in up to 10% of patients during long-term follow-up,19,45–47 with the transverse colon, the omentum, and the small intestine being the most commonly herniated organs.48,49 In our experience, particularly obese patients without hiatus hernia are at risk due to a mismatch between the fatty, bulky conduit, and the narrow hiatal opening. In these situations, transection of the base of the right diaphragmatic crus may be considered to achieve a straighter course of the conduit. When done preemptively during index surgery, this maneuver also facilitates transhiatal gastric pull-up. In our own patient cohort, we have performed transection of the right diaphragmatic crus only in selected cases.

Another subset of patients experienced gastric conduit emptying problems due to PCH (Fig. 1D). Surgical repair is the treatment of choice for PCH and indicated even in asymptomatic patients due to the risk of incarceration. Minimally invasive approaches are preferred50 and performed on average 2–3.8 years after the index operation.43,51 Repositioning of herniated structures to the abdominal cavity often requires meticulous division of adhesions to the conduit, the diaphragm and to mediastinal structures, avoiding damage to the vascular arcade of the conduit. Hiatus repair usually entails crurorrhaphy with optional mesh augmentation.52,53 Recurrence rates after PCH repair are high (up to 29%) and do not differ between patients with or without mesh reinforcement.10 Of note, repairs with mesh may result in higher rates of postoperative pleural complications.54

Secondary DGCE due to other types of obstruction

Lastly, chronic or acute ileus caused by adhesions or peritoneal carcinomatosis and luminal bolus obstruction can ultimately result in secondary DGCE. In this situation, symptoms vary greatly depending on the degree of obstruction and the portion of bowel affected, ranging from intermittent to highly acute presentations. Treatment is highly individualized and generally follows the principles of ileus therapy. Specifically, management of peritoneal carcinomatosis ranges from palliative measures, such as decompression of the conduit with a nasogastric tube, to surgical procedures, such as intestinal bypass and stoma creation.

Proposal for a new classification

Considering the outlined different causes responsible for impaired conduit emptying, DGCE should be seen as a multifactorial syndrome requiring specific therapies tailored to individual needs. We therefore propose four categories of DGCE based on pathophysiology and pathoanatomy:

Own clinical experience

We retrospectively examined all patients who underwent Ivor Lewis esophagectomy in our department between 2019 and 2024 (Table 2). DGCE Type I: functional—motility-related

In our own patient cohort, DGCE Class I typically manifested early in the postoperative course, either during the index hospitalization or during initial follow-up after rehabilitation. All patients with DGCE Class I (n = 40) underwent endoscopic balloon dilation of the pylorus (median number of interventions: 1, interquartile range [IQR] 1–2.5) and, in one case, botulinum toxin injections. Two patients underwent additional stent placement and one patient G-POEM. One patient with persistent DGCE, however, required Roux-en-Y conduit diversion due to persistent symptoms after several endoscopic dilatations.

DGCE Type II: conduit-related

Anatomical conditions of the conduit were the cause of DGCE in four patients and they presented at a median of 8.5 months after esophagectomy. In DGCE Class II patients, the underlying conditions were CR and CD (n = 2), and conduit torsion (n = 2). While endoscopic interventions were initially attempted in all patients (median number of interventions: 3, IQR 1.75–4.25), surgical revision was ultimately required for symptom relief in three patients. Traditionally, surgical revision of the conduit entails right thoracotomy combined with an abdominal approach to mobilize, straighten, and secure the conduit; however, this approach carries a high risk of morbidity.55 Our experience in alternative revisional surgery for Type 2 DGCE entails the following two procedures:

Inverting gastroplication

In patients with CD and CR, it is often observed that the lesser curvature retains its original length because it is stabilized by the firm staple line of the stomach, while it is the greater curvature that is actually stretched and elongated. This results in a typical ‘spinnaker shape’ resembling a billowing sail attached to a fixed mast. In this situation, re-stapling of the lesser curvature is generally recommended to achieve an adequate 4–5 cm conduit diameter.49 However, we found this technically difficult because it results in an additional length of the conduit that requires either segmental resection and re-anastomosis or straightening through the hiatus via a separate abdominal approach.

To solve this problem, we believe that Inverting Gastroplication can be an interesting alternative. After full mobilization, both the anterior and posterior walls of the gastric conduit are separately inverted with a series of single mattress plication stitches using braided, non-resorbable 2–0 suture material (Ethibond, Ethicon Surgical) (Fig. 2A). The technique avoids new staple lines and leads to shortening, straightening and reshaping of the passage, potentially eliminating the need for additional abdominal access (Fig. 2B,C). We have performed gastroplication with favorable functional outcomes via thoracoscopy (n = 1) and thoracotomy (n = 1). In one additional patient, the sutures were placed through interventional endoscopy with an aim to avoid surgery, as recently described.56,57 Unfortunately, this patient experienced post-interventional gastric perforation requiring emergency thoracotomy, which is why we have abandoned the endoscopic approach since.

Conduit twist and the ‘Camera Shutter Phenomenon’

Torsion of the conduit may lead to a distinctive endoscopic pattern similar to the shutter mechanism in a camera. We have treated one patient presenting with dysphagia for solids and regurgitation. After transthoracic full conduit mobilization, it became apparent that the twist was due to a fibrous adhesion of the gastric staple line to the right main bronchus. After de-rotation and pexy of the conduit to the prevertebral fascia under endoscopic control using interrupted, non-resorbable single stitches (Fig. 2D-F), the patient made an uneventful recovery and was able to resume a solid diet.

DGCE Class III: mechanical obstruction at the hiatus

In our own cohort, n = 14 were diagnosed with obstruction at the hiatus at a median of 1 year after the operation. Dilatation only was performed in 2 patients, while 11 patients underwent surgery. This encompassed division of the right diaphragmatic crus to straighten the course of the conduit in one patient, and elective or emergent surgery with hiatoplasty for symptomatic PCH in 10 patients.

DGCE Class IV: other causes of DGCE at or distal to the pylorus

There were no surgical interventions for Class IV DGCE, as patients presenting with this condition were considered palliative and treated accordingly.

DISCUSSION

As the chances of survival after esophagectomy improve with the introduction of multimodal approaches and immunotherapy,58,59 appropriate follow-up and aftercare as well as a thorough understanding of postoperative pathophysiology are becoming increasingly important. DGCE is probably the most common and complex functional syndrome after esophagectomy, and diagnosis and treatment require comprehensive and individualized approaches. In this context, it should be borne in mind that there is only a fine line between the functional background noise that inevitably occurs after esophagectomy on the one hand, and clearly pathological DGCE on the other. Furthermore, there are no ‘ideal’ anatomical characteristics of a conduit, as—depending on individual surgical concepts—esophago-gastric reconstruction can result in various conduit shapes, diameters, reconstruction routes, and anastomotic levels.60 Moreover, many patients with grotesque deformity of the foregut anatomy have only mild or no symptoms and do not require treatment. Therefore, we believe that the term DGCE should only be used in symptomatic patients with obvious pathology and requiring specific treatment.61

The intricate pathophysiology of DGCE complicates both clinical assessment and treatment, while the lack of standardized definitions limits the validity and comparability of the current literature on preventive and therapeutic options.1,2 In an earlier attempt to classify DGCE, Kent et al.10 have outlined four potential causes: excess conduit left above the diaphragm during esophagectomy, mechanical outlet obstruction, a twisted conduit, and dilatation without a clear mechanical cause, such as gastric dysmotility due to truncular vagotomy. However, this list does not consider the complex pathophysiological conditions, which ultimately require different therapeutic approaches.

Against this background, we hereby propose a pathophysiologically oriented, comprehensive clinical classification system for DGCE comprising four different functional types. Our initiative aims to create a ‘common language’ to improve clinical management and to provide a structured framework for diagnosis and treatment (Fig. 3). We are aware that different DGCE classes may overlap in individual patients. Therefore, we currently use primarily the clinically dominant DGCE class to classify each case. If necessary, this can be supplemented with an additional designation (e.g. Class I/III).

In summary, we believe that our classification represents a significant advance in the understanding and treatment of this complex syndrome, as it has the potential to improve patient outcomes and facilitate future research collaborations. As a next step, we intend to validate our new classification as part of the RESTORANT project, an international collaborative study that aims to describe the incidence, techniques, and outcomes of revisional surgery for post-esophagectomy DGCE based on a large multicenter database. Furthermore, an expert Delphi consensus should guide the further development of the current classification.
