Parent‐child agreement on children's psychopathology and the impact of parental depression
Authors: Johanna Löchner, Stephanie Hämmerle, Sarah Ghezih, Kornelija Starman‐Wöhrle, Gerd Schulte‐Körne, Belinda Platt
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParental depression increases children's risk of mental illness and may simultaneously impair the detection of children's symptoms. Here we investigate the nature of parent‐child agreement of children's psychopathology in children of parents with current (cMD) versus remitted (rMD) major depression.MethodsBaseline data from 100 parent‐child dyads including healthy children aged 8–17 (M = 11.89, SD = 2.83) and their parents with a history of depression were analysed. The presence of sub‐clinical psychopathology (yes/no) in children was assessed using semi‐structured diagnostic interviews with child and parent (K‐DIPS). Self‐ (YSR) and parent‐report (CBCL) questionnaires were used to measure the severity of symptoms. Parent‐child agreement was calculated using Chi‐square tests and Cohen's kappa respectively. We compared whether agreement differed between children of parents with cMD (n = 52) versus rMD (n = 48).ResultsIn the interviews parents more frequently reported sub‐clinical child psychopathology than the children themselves (χ 2 1,100 = 4.63, p < 0.001, d = 0.59). This pattern characterised parents with cMD (χ 2 1,52 = 7.99, p = 0.005; κ = 0.582) but not rMD (χ 2 1,48 = 000, p = 0.686; κ = −0.010), a difference which was statistically significant (z = 3.14, p < 0.001, d = 0.66).ConclusionSince agreement between parents and children about the severity of children's symptoms was particularly poor in families where parents were currently depressed, parental mental illness should be taken into account when assessing youth psychopathology.Keywords: diagnostic, high risk group, parental depression, youth depression

INTRODUCTION

The offspring of depressed parents face an elevated risk not only for depression (Weissman, Pilowsky, et al., 2006, Weissman, Wickramaratne, et al., 2006), but also internalising and externalising disorders generally (Ashford et al., 2008). They are also more likely to experience early‐onset depression (Collishaw et al., 2016; Hosman et al., 2009; Weissman et al., 1997), which is associated with higher risk of somatic diseases (Scott et al., 2011), other psychiatric disorders and suicide (Rao & Chen, 2009). Detecting symptoms as soon and as accurately as possible is important for the prevention and treatment of mental disorders. Parents as primary caregivers have a crucial role in the detection of mental disorders but may be impaired by a mental disorder themselves. Here, we aim to investigate how the current status of a parental mental disorder may influence the parent‐child agreement on the child's mental health assessment.

The gold standard for diagnosing mental disorders in childhood and adolescence are standardised semi‐structured interviews such as the Diagnostic Interview for Mental Disorders in Childhood and Adolescence (K‐DIPS; Schneider et al., 2009). Given that symptoms of mental disorder may express themselves in different contexts, a multi‐informant assessment approach, in which interviews are conducted with the child, parent and/or teacher, is recommended for increased reliability (De Los Reyes et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2016; Luby et al., 2007). Since diagnostic interviews are time‐consuming and require clinically‐experienced interviewers (Adornetto et al., 2008; Bruchmüller et al., 2011), child‐ and parent‐report questionnaires assessing symptom frequency can be a useful tool for early and rapid identification of mental disorder. They are also used in clinical practice to validate diagnostic interviews and evaluate the therapy process.

A substantial body of research demonstrates discrepancies between self‐ and parent‐reports of psychopathology. In a meta‐analysis of 341 studies the overall cross‐informant correlation between multiple informants was found to be statistically significant but low‐to‐moderate in size (r = 0.28) (De Los Reyes et al., 2015). The correspondence was slightly higher for externalising (r = 0.30) versus internalising (r = 0.25) symptoms for each dyad of informants (e.g., parent‐child, parent‐teacher, …). Previous studies have found that adolescent depression is reported more frequently (and with greater symptom severity) by adolescents themselves compared with their parents (Grills & Ollendick, 2002; Orchard et al., 2019; Angold et al., 1987). In a more recent study including fathers as well as mothers with depression, in 38% of the cases children and parents agreed on a DSM‐IV major depression diagnosis, while 53% of the adolescents only endorsed the criteria for a full‐blown disorder. Parent‐child disagreement was also shown to be associated with poorer overall treatment response (Goolsby et al., 2018). Understanding the factors which moderate this relationship and confidence in the outcome measures is crucial for a reliable diagnosis of youth mental disorder in this high‐risk group and the following clinical indication.

One factor which may moderate the correspondence between parent and child reports of psychopathology is parental depression. Depression has been shown to have a significant negative impact on parenting behaviour (Giallo et al., 2014; Lovejoy et al., 2000; Wilson & Durbin, 2010). This may be due to a number of symptoms of depression including sustained negative affect, negative cognitive style, sleep disturbances and reduced social contact. Importantly, effects on parenting are strongest for parents in a current episode of major depression (cMD), but also characterize parents in remission (i.e., those not in a current episode of MD rMD) (Lovejoy et al., 2000). Given its substantial impact of parenting, it seems plausible that parental depression may also impact the accuracy with which parents detect and report psychopathology in their offspring. On the one hand, greater severity of parental depression may lead to less awareness and empathy of for example, anxiety, in their children (Affrunti & Woodruff‐Borden, 2015). Alternatively, the cognitive biases for negative information known to characterize depression (Everaert et al., 2017) may lead parents to ‘over‐report’ psychopathology (Angold et al., 1987). Given that children of parents with depression have an increased risk of developing mental disorder themselves, research into the impact of parental depression on parent‐child agreement could have important clinical implications.

Although numerous studies have shown that children of parents with depression (vs. parents with no mental disorder) are themselves more likely to experience internalising and externalising symptoms (Hosman et al., 2009; Weissman, Pilowsky, et al., 2006, Weissman, Wickramaratne, et al., 2006), relatively few have investigated the impact of parental depression on the agreement between parents and children about their children's psychopathology. In one study of diagnostic interviews with 214 parents and 181 children aged 7–25 years, parents with depression (vs. those without a history of MD) reported more symptoms of depression in their children, despite the fact there were no group differences in children's reports of their own symptoms (Angold et al., 1987). These findings suggest that parents with depression may over‐report symptoms of depression in their children. Literature on parent child discrepancies and clinically depressed parents is contradictory: some studies emphasized a maternal reporting bias when reports of clinically depressed mothers were compared with their children's reports, indicating a more negative maternal assessment (Boyle & Pickles, 1997; Chilcoat & Breslau, 1997; Yuh et al., 2013). More specifically, Boyle and Pickles, (1997) found maternal depressive symptoms to be correlated with mother rating errors in among adolescent boys rather than girls and suggested the presence of a ‘maternal bias’. Müller et al. (2011) confirmed maternal psychopathology as most important predictor of cross‐informant disagreement in their structural equation model based on questionnaire data of mothers and children. Youngstrom and colleagues found an impact of severity of maternal depression on the maternal reports of their offspring (Youngstrom et al., 1999, 2000). Although Kiss et al. (2007) also reported higher maternal ratings, they interpreted it as a better agreement between mothers with depression and their offspring due to an improved maternal recall of mood congruent symptoms. In contrast, other studies found a worsening parent‐child agreement due to maternal depression (Renouf & Kovacs, 1994; Youngstrom et al., 2000).

Regarding gender, Boyle & Pickles (1997) as well as Kiss et al. (2007) found higher maternal reports of symptoms in boys, not girls. Furthermore, the agreement between mothers and children was increased as children got older (Kiss et al., 2007), while other studies did not support this finding (Baumgartner et al., 2020). The child's gender‐identity and mental health were further identified as significant predictor for child–parent agreement child's quality of life as outcome in a sample of mentally ill parents (Radicke et al., 2021).

Since the status of parental depression, that is, whether it is current (cMD) versus in remission (rMD), affects parenting behaviour (Lovejoy et al., 2000), an important question is whether the status of depression also affects the accuracy of parental reports of children's psychopathology. As far as we are aware, only one study has addressed this issue previously, but included a sample of parents with bipolar disorder I and II (Maoz et al., 2014). The authors found no group‐differences in rates of psychopathology yielded form conducted clinical interviews (K‐SADS), except for more depressive disorders in offspring of parents with current mania/hypomania compared to parents in remission. In contrast, in the questionnaire‐based assessment parents with current depression reported more psychopathology symptoms in their offspring. We are not aware of a recent study focusing on the current status of parental depression and child‐parent agreement. In addition, former studies mainly focused on maternal depression and did not include fathers (Boyle & Pickles, 1997; Kiss et al., 2007; Yuh et al., 2013).

The overarching goal of this study was to explore parent‐child agreement regarding children's psychopathology (internalising and externalising symptoms) in families affected by parental depression. We analysed both reports from diagnostic interviews as well as questionnaires. Firstly, we investigated how much parents with depression and their children agree in their reports of children's psychopathology. Based on a meta‐analysis of parent and self‐reports of child psychopathology (De Los Reyes et al., 2015), we expected to find a significant but low‐to‐moderate correlation between parents' and children's reports of child psychopathology (H1). Secondly, we investigated whether the status of parental depression (current vs. remitted) influenced the association between child‐ and parent reports of child psychopathology. We hypothesized that discrepancy between parents and their children would be higher in parents with cMD versus rMD (H2). This study more broadly aims to inform the validity of gold standard clinical assessment instruments in a group at high risk for mental illness.

METHOD

Participants

The 100 parent‐child dyads took part in a randomised controlled trial for a primary prevention of depression programme for children and adolescents with a parent suffering from depression (see sample and trial description and power calculation in more detail here: Löchner et al., 2021). Data from the first baseline assessment was drawn for the current analysis. The sample size calculation was based on the necessary sample size of 92 needed to detect an effect size equivalent to d = 0.60, a power of 80% and a 5% alpha level. The necessary sample size was exceeded (n = 100). The sample in this study was identical, hence no subsample was drawn. Recruitment took place in diverse sites in Munich (see Löchner et al., 2021).

Since the intervention was designed to prevent a first episode of depression in the offspring of parents with depression, the following inclusion criteria were defined.

Parents

Parents were included if they fulfiled the DSM‐IV diagnostic criteria for a depressive disorder during their child's lifetime, assessed via diagnostic interviews (see Section 2.3). They were excluded if suffering from alcohol or substance abuse, bipolar disorder, reported psychotic symptoms, personality disorder, suicidal crisis. Fifty‐two of the participating parents reported a current depressive disorder (cMD), which was the first episode in 14.8% of the cases. Forty‐eight parents were currently in remission (rMD). According to questionnaire (BDI‐II) data 64.5% parents had mild symptoms of depression (<6 symptoms; DSM‐IV), 12.5% parents reported more severe symptoms (>6 symptoms; DSM‐IV). Thirty‐nine parents had a comorbid disorder (31 anxiety disorder, 6 eating disorder, 2 obsessive compulsion disorder).

Children

Children were included if they were aged 8 and 17 years, did not fulfil the DSM‐IV criteria for any mental illness and had an IQ > 85. Although children did not fulfil the criteria for a diagnosis of mental illness, children of parents with depression showed significantly more psychopathology symptoms (internalising and externalising in parent‐ and self‐report) than children of parents without a history of depression (Wilks' λ = 0.565, F 1,79 = 3.78, p < 0.001; η 2 p = 0.435) (Loechner et al., 2020). Where multiple children from one family were eligible for the study, data from the oldest child were included. Procedure

The study took place in the research department of the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, Psychosomatics and Psychotherapy, University Hospital, Ludwig‐Maximilian‐University Munich. Families were informed about the study procedure, potential risks and gave written consent for study participation. Thereafter, parents and children were invited for the diagnostic interviews that were conducted in parallel (JL, KS‐W, PW, DW). As main participant of the trial, the parent suffering from depression was interviewed about their children's health. All interviews were audiotaped and recorded. Questionnaires for parents and children were filled out at home and asked to be sent back within 2 weeks. Families were rewarded with €25 for taking part in the first session of the trial. The study was approved by Medical Division Ethics Committee Munich (Study ID: 3–14) (Platt et al., 2014) and conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Diagnostic status of parents and children

To assess study inclusion criteria that is, whether parents and children met DSM‐IV criteria for a mental illness, the Diagnostic Interview for Mental Disorders (DIPS; Schneider & Margraf, 2011) and its version for children and adolescents (K‐DIPS, DIPS; Unnewehr et al., 2009) was used. Parents completed one interview about their own psychopathology and one interview about their child's psychopathology. If both parents suffered from a mental disorder, both were included in the study, but only one interviewed about the child's symptoms. Children completed the interview about their own psychopathology. In other studies including an adult population the inter‐rater reliability of the instrument (K‐DIPS) between the rating clinicians was high (κ = 0.72–0.92) (Suppiger et al., 2008). 20% of the audio‐recorded parent and child interviews were randomly selected and re‐rated by two independent researchers (LT, KM). Accordance regarding the presence or absence of a mental disorder among all interviews was excellent (κ = 1.00). In addition to coding for the presence of mental illness, we also coded for ‘sub‐clinical’ symptoms of mental disorder in children (e.g. when a symptom was present but did not fulfil the time criteria or just appeared occasionally, not leading to subjective burden). Hereby all symptoms of a disorder are recognized, although they may not be sufficient to fulfil the diagnostic criteria for a diagnosis. This dichotomous variable (‘sub‐clinical psychopathology’ or ‘no symptoms’) was used for the main data analysis. If the informant reported any problems or symptoms, subclinical symptoms were assessed. ‘No symptoms’ meant no symptoms at all or a very mild expression of a potential symptom (e.g. discomfort at altitude but no avoidance behaviour).

Children's symptom severity

To assess the severity of internalising and externalising symptoms in children the Youth‐Self Report (YSR) (Döpfner et al., 1994) and its parent‐version the Child Behaviour Checklist (CBCL) (Döpfner et al., 1994) were used (both versions are validated translations of the original Achenbach questionnaires). The YSR and CBCL consist of 123‐items on a 3‐ to 4‐ point Likert scale (0 = ‘not applicable’; 1 = ‘somewhat or sometimes applicable’, 2 = ‘accurate or often applicable’). In addition to subscales for ‘internalising’ (withdrawn, somatic complaints, anxiety and depression) and ‘externalising’ (social problems, rule‐breaking and aggressive behaviour) symptoms, the measure can also be used to generate a total score which also incorporates attention problems, aggressive behaviour and delinquent behaviours. High internal consistencies are reported for the internal and external symptoms scale of the YSR (r = 0.86), sufficient internal consistencies were found for other subscales (e.g. ‘aggressive behaviour’, r > 0.70). Internal consistency of the internalising and externalising subscales of the CBCL is also high (r > 0.85) (Döpfner et al., 1994). For the current study we used the internalising and externalising sub‐scales as our main outcomes but also explored the other sub‐scales (YSR, CBCL). In addition to using the raw values, we also dichotomized YSR and CBCL scores using a cut‐off of one standard deviation above the normative mean to distinguish ‘sub‐clinical’ versus ‘symptom‐free’ children (t‐value >60). Data were available from 76/100 parents (CBCL) and 74/100 children (YSR).

Parent's symptoms of depression

The German version of the 21‐item Beck's Depression Inventory (BDI‐II) (Hautzinger et al., 1994) was used to assess parental depression symptoms. It has a 4‐point Likert scale (0 = ‘I do not feel sad’ to 3 = ‘I am so sad or unhappy that I can't stand it.’). In a period of 5 months a re‐test reliability of r = 0.78 was identified (Hautzinger et al., 1994). There are high correlations between the BDI‐II and other questionnaires concerning symptoms of depression such as the FDD‐DSM‐IV (Fragebogen zur Depressionsdiagnostik nach DSM IV) (Kühner, 1997) (r = 0.72–0.89).

Analysis strategy

Aim 1 (association between parent‐ and child‐reports) was investigated by running a Chi‐square test testing whether sub‐clinical psychopathology in children (yes, no) differed between interviews with parents versus children. The same test was conducted for internalising and externalising disorders separately. In order to compare our findings with previous meta‐analyses (De Los Reyes et al., 2015), we also calculated bi‐variate Pearson correlations for the parent‐ (CBCL) versus child‐based (YSR) questionnaire measures of internalising and externalising symptoms.

To investigate aim 2 (association between parental depression and parent‐child agreement on child psychopathology) we first wanted to exclude the possibility that children of parents with cMD versus rMD experienced more symptoms of psychopathology. We therefore ran a MANOVA with parental depression (cMD, rMD) as the independent variable and internalising and externalising symptoms according to parents (CBCL) and children (YSR) as the dependent variables. There was no evidence that the frequency of symptoms differed between children of parents with cMD versus rMD. To test the association between the status of parental depression (current, remitted) and the presence of sub‐clinical psychopathology in children (yes, no) according to interviews with children versus parents, two Chi‐square tests were conducted (for children of parents with cMD and rMD respectively). We tested for significant differences between groups but transforming the kappa values into z‐scores (using Fisher's r‐to‐z transformation) and comparing these using the Cohen and Cohen formula (Cohen & Cohen, 1983). The same method was used to test whether the agreement between questionnaire‐based parent‐ (CBCL) and child‐reports (YSR) of psychopathology differed between children of parents with cMD versus rMD. To evaluate the impact of a current episode of parental depression on the parent‐report of a child's psychopathology, we also conducted an ANCOVA with group (rMD, cMD) as the independent variable and parent‐reported children's psychopathology (CBCL) as the dependent variable, with children's self‐reported psychopathology (YSR) as a covariate.

DISCUSSION

Summary of findings

This study sought to investigate parent‐child agreement on child psychopathology in families in which a parent has depression. The key aim was to determine whether the status of parental depression (current, remitted) was associated with parent‐child agreement. Firstly, we found a significance difference in the frequency of sub‐clinical psychopathology in interviews with parents versus children: parents more frequently reported sub‐clinical psychopathology in their children than the children themselves (d = 0.59). Data on symptom severity from parent‐ and child‐report questionnaires showed similar findings, albeit weaker: the correlation between child‐ and parents‐reports was significant but of a small‐to‐moderate effect size. A few of the subscales in the CBCL and YSR correlated significantly (to a low to moderate degree) with each other (see Table 3). The second set of findings relate to the role of the status of parental depression (current vs. remitted). The tendency of parents to ‘over‐report’ psychopathology in their children characterised parents in a current episode of depression, but not those in remission according to the clinical interviews (d = 0.66). In contrast to the interviews, the parent‐child agreement in questionnaire‐based reports of children's psychopathology did not differ between dyads with a parent who was currently depressed versus in remission (η 2 = 0.067).

These results confirm that we cannot fully rely upon the validity of measures administered to currently depressed parents, and that adaptations to clinical outcome measurement processes are going to be needed in research and practice, to enhance reliability but also overall treatment response (Goolsby et al., 2018).

Interpretation of findings

Our finding that parents and children show low‐to‐moderate agreement in their ratings of child psychopathology is in line with a meta‐analysis of previous studies (De Los Reyes et al., 2015) and confirms the validity of a multi‐informant approach to assessment. In our study, dis‐concordance between parents and children was particularly pronounced in the diagnostic interviews compared with the questionnaire‐based measures. This might reflect the fact that when being actively listened to by a mental health professional, parents were more able to express their concerns about their children. Alternatively, being in front of a mental health professional may have led children with a depressed parent to under‐report their real feelings. This is in line with a previous study which reported low concordance between interviews and questionnaires in the assessment of adolescent depression (Orchard et al., 2019). This might suggest that for the purposes of screening for youth psychopathology, the parental psychopathology should be taken into account when informants are interviewed. Ideally, multiple reports, for example, from teacher, other parents, questionnaire and interview‐based can be collected. In contrast with previous studies which have found children to report more frequently (and with greater severity) psychopathology than their healthy parents (Orchard et al., 2019), we found the opposite pattern of findings to characterize parents with a current depression. These findings are in line with Angold et al. (1987), who found that parents with depression tended to report more symptoms in their children, compared with parents without depression. Such findings were supported by following studies on parent child discrepancies and clinically depressed parents (Boyle & Pickles, 1997; Yuh et al., 2013). Furthermore, a worsening child‐parent agreement was found for the currently depressed parents, going along with previous similar studies (Kiss et al., 2007; Renouf & Kovacs, 1994; Youngstrom et al., 2000).

This finding could be related to the fact that parents in the present study were very worried to transmit their depression to their children, ruminating a lot about a potential lack of well‐being in their children and therefore enroled in the prevention trial in the first place. Someone listening actively and asking questions about their children might elicit a more frequent report of such worries than a ‘neutral’ questionnaire. Hence, the present parent sample might report more symptoms and might be characterized by a motivation to seek support to prevent depression in their offspring, compared to parents with depression who have not sought prevention of offspring depression.

The finding that the concordance between parents and children depends on the status of parental depression is highly novel. We found that parents in remission (rMD) reported similar levels of psychopathology in their children to the children themselves whereas parents with cMD reported more psychopathology in their children than the children themselves. This may be due to the cognitive biases for negative information known to characterize depression which appear to be stronger in currently versus remitted patients (Everaert et al., 2017). Alternatively, this may reflect the selective well‐educated sample in the current study: parents were well informed about the risks of transmission of depression, an alarming fact that motivated them to take part in the preventive intervention in the first place. This particular group of parents might be more vigilant in observing and more worried about their children to develop the same mental disorder as they have experienced. Misinterpretation or a projection of parent's symptoms onto their children are conceivable (Grills & Ollendick, 2002). Another reason for the discrepancy may be that children of parents with depression might underreport their own symptoms. Offspring of parents with depression often adapt their behaviour and expression of needs to avoid additional burden for the parent (Lenz, 2005). A lack of family communication, that is often observed in families affected by mental illness, together with the interpretation of the interview questions might also cause the discrepancies (Kramer et al., 2004; Treutler & Epkins, 2003).

The finding regarding the status of parental current or remitted depression requires replicating in an independent sample before strong interpretations can be drawn. If replicated, this finding would suggest that relying on parent accounts of child psychopathology without knowing the status of parental psychopathology could result in unreliable diagnosis. Where possible, parents should be asked not only about their child's psychopathology, but also about their own.

Strengths

A major strength of this study lies in the use of standardized diagnostic interviews (with high interrater‐reliability; κ = 1) which enabled more valid conclusions about the nature of parental depression and the presence of psychopathology in children to be drawn. Many previous studies have relied on questionnaire measures alone. A second strength is the inclusion of parents with a current versus past episode of depression. In addition, only few studies focused on parental, not only maternal depression in parent‐child agreement studies. As far as we are aware this is the first study to investigate the effect that the status of parental depression has on parent‐child agreement in mothers and fathers.

Limitations

One limitation of the study is the representativeness of the sample. The data were collected from an opportunistic sample of families who signed up to participate in a prevention trial and may therefore have been unrepresentative in terms of their sensitivity to children's psychopathology symptoms. A related limitation is the low variance in symptom severity between children included in the sample. Since families were recruited to a primary prevention trial, children with clinical levels of mental illness were not included. Although the children in the sample had significantly elevated symptoms compared to children of parents without a mental disorder (Loechner et al., 2020), we were unable to test whether the status of parental depression predicts reports of clinical (vs. sub‐clinical) depression. Despite the effect of age on parent‐child agreement that was fond in some studies (Jensen et al., 1999; Kiss et al., 2007; Renouf & Kovacs, 1994), but not in a more recent one (Baumgartner et al., 2020), we did not include the child's age. Although this is probably an important factor for the general agreement, it does not have an implication for our main hypothesis on group differences, since age should be equal in both groups.

Conclusion

This study highlights the impact of a current depressive episode on parent‐child agreements regarding the child's health in a semi‐structured clinical interview, which is considered the gold standard. Since the offspring of parents with depression are at increased risk for developing mental illness, accurate and timely diagnosis of disorder is critical. Whereas previous studies suggest that parents tend to under‐report mental illness in their children, some studies have suggested that this may depend on whether the parent themselves has a mental illness or not. In this study we found that parents with depression tended to report psychopathology in their children more frequently and with greater severity than the children themselves. This effect was largely driven by parents who were currently depressed rather than those in remission. The effect was only apparent in interviews rather than questionnaire measures of psychopathology. The findings suggest that health professionals should take parental mental illness and potential informant bias into account when assessing children and adolescents. Furthermore, these results call into question the safety of the gold standard and encourage the search for alternative diagnostic tools, especially for samples whose judgement is impaired. Future research for the enhancement of diagnostic reliability in this particular risk group may benefit from time‐series assessment and other, potentially additional measures as digital (e.g. digital phenotyping) or biochemical markers.
