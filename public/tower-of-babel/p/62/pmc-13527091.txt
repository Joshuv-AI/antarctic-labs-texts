Effect of the nature of cation of ionic liquid on the CO2 conversion to cyclic carbonates—a theoretical study
Authors: Annum Ahsan, Hadiqa Sahar, Khurshid Ayub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConversion of CO2 into useful products is one of the favorable methods for alleviation of CO2 and its further utilization. In this regard, ionic liquids (ILs) have been reported as efficient CO2 conversion materials. One of the unique characteristics of ILs is their tunable physicochemical properties. The slight changes in the structure of ions (cation or anion) results in a number of cation/anion combinations which change the characteristics of ILs and overall, their functioning is also affected in this way. In the current study, we have considered a series of 1-alkyl-3-methyl-imidazolium chlorides ([CnMIM][Cl]) (n = 1 to 6) for conversion of CO2 to cyclic carbonates. These ILs are different from each other based on substituents attached to the imidazolium. Epoxide i.e., propylene oxide, is used as another reactant with CO2 in the presence of ILs. The detailed mechanistic study shows that the lowest energy barriers are observed for CO2 conversion process with [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl]. The change in nature of cation affects the energy barriers by decreasing the barrier from [C1MIM][Cl] to [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl]. However, further increase in barriers is observed with [C4MIM][Cl], [C5MIM][Cl], and [C6MIM][Cl] due to combined electronic and steric effects. The trend in the energy barrier is well understood through non-covalent interactions (NCI) analysis. Our study reveals that imidazolium with medium-sized alkyl chain is predicted to exhibit higher catalytic efficiency based on the computed activation energy barriers and free energy changes. Among the ILs studied, [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl] show the most exergonic overall free energy change (-2.43 kcal/mol for step 1 with [C2MIM][Cl] and − 12.82 kcal/mol for step 2 with [C3MIM][Cl]), suggesting a modest thermodynamic preference for medium-chain ILs, though the small ΔG differences (~ 1–2 kcal/mol for step 1) are within the typical uncertainty of DFT. Our computational findings may serve as a preliminary guide for experimental design of imidazolium-based ILs for CO2 cycloaddition reactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57455-9.Keywords: Ionic liquids, 1-alkyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([CnMIM][Cl]), NCI analysisSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology

Introduction

The climate or weather is an imperative environmental aspect of any region or country. For any area, the transitions in environmental factors over a long period of time give rise to climatic change. The changes in climate influence almost every aspect of life including animals, plants, agricultural section, patterns of wind, water cycle, quality of soil, and patterns of rainfall etc. As almost every aspect of life is affected by changes in climate so, for quite a long time, significant research has been carried out on the analysis of variability in climate during the second half of the 20th century. As a conclusion to all this research, the 5th Assessment Report (AR5) presented by Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) points out that the rise in average global temperature observed from 1951 to 2010 is most probably a consequence of elevated concentration of greenhouse gases through anthropogenic sources1. Moreover, the report presented by IPCC in 2017 shows that temperature has been elevated globally by almost 1 °C i.e., higher as compared to the temperature recorded during pre-industrial era. And, it is expected to elevate at a rate of 0.2 °C every coming decade2. Hence, it is very important for the safety of earth to slow down this rise in temperature. For this purpose, it is imperative to reduce the concentration of GHGs in atmosphere as these gases play a major role in rising temperature.

The common greenhouse gases (GHGs) are carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide, methane, and halogenated compounds. All these GHGs contribute towards global warming. Depending upon their concentration and life span in the atmosphere, they contribute differently. According to the literature, CO2 concentration in the atmosphere has dramatically boosted from 340 ppm in 1980 to 422 ppm in 2022 leading to an adverse impact on the environment. The impact or contribution of CO2 towards global warming, its concentration and most importantly, its life span (hundreds to thousands of years) in the atmosphere has compelled the researchers to work on ways to lower the concentration of CO2 and to introduce methods through which further addition of CO2 into the environment can be reduced.

The different approaches currently used for capturing CO2 fall into three broad categories i.e., oxy-fuel combustion, pre-combustion technique, and post-combustion technique. In oxy-fuel combustion method, fuel is burnt in the presence of pure oxygen rather than air. This type of combustion results in CO2 rich flue gases which simplifies the process of CO2 separation. Otherwise, in the presence of N2 (as its concentration is quite high in the air i.e., 79%), the flue gases contain about 3 to 15% of CO2. Such a lower amount of CO2 is not easy to separate. Pre-combustion technique involves removal of carbon from the fuel i.e., gas or coal, before combustion process. In this process, the fossil fuels are transformed into synthesis gas, i.e., a mixture of CO + H2 (syn-gas)3. Further, the carbon-monoxide portion of syn-gas is converted to carbon-dioxide. At this stage, mixture of CO2 and H2 is obtained. CO2 is further separated from this mixture resulting in production of H2. The post combustion technique involves capturing CO2 from exhaust gases produced as a result of combustion of fuel in thermal power plants. Moreover, the exhaust gases treated in post-combustion technique contain NOx, SO2, and particulate along with CO2. Before CO2 removal from exhaust gases, removal of SO2 (desulphurization), NOx (denitrification), and dust particles is carried out.

While using different techniques for capturing CO2, the technology employed for separation of CO2 is very important. Out of all the methods used for CO2 capture, absorption by aqueous alkanolamines is considered as benchmark for the industrial processes4. But, the limitations associated with this method are the corrosivity5, and degradation of alkanolamines6–8 when used under high temperature and high solvent losses. For overcoming the challenges allied with using alkanolamines, ionic liquids (ILs), also known as green solvents, have gained significant attention and are considered as the most suitable candidates for CO2 capture. ILs are the solvents that are used for CO2 capturing in post-combustion processes mostly. They show quite unique characteristics like high thermal stability, tunability, low volatility, high polarity, and low toxicity. All these properties make the ILs superior over commonly used organic solvents of volatile nature. They are the green solvents because of their environmentally friendly nature. The analysis of structural features of ILs shows that generally they consist of cation of organic nature (pyrollidinium, ammonium, imidazolium) while their anion can be of organic or inorganic nature (dicyanamide, triflate, chloride). This composition demonstrates wide range of possible structures of ionic liquids. The selection of cation and anion influences properties of ILs and a large number of cation/anion combinations are possible.

In addition to capturing of CO2, its storage after being captured has been suggested as a technically viable alternative to combat global warming9. In addition to the extensive research on CO2 capturing, the research on the development of methods for transformation of CO2 into useful chemicals after capture has been under keen consideration10,11. Due to the scope of this study, those technologies are becoming more pertinent which are working on carbon capture and utilization (CCU). These methods involve CO2 fixation by using CO2 as a solvent or as a reactant in various reactions, for example, in synthesis of organic chemicals12,13. Among the studies involving CO2 transformation/conversion, the generation of cyclic carbonates from epoxides has garnered a lot of interest14,15. The epoxides that are used most commonly in cycloaddition of CO2 in order to produce ethylene and propylene carbonate are ethylene and propylene oxide, respectively. The reasons for the use of CO2 in these reactions include relatively favored thermodynamics and environmental improvement as compared to the industrial practices using phosgene16. For the reactions involving CO2 transformation, a variety of catalysts have been used for example, metal-organic frameworks17, quaternary ammonium salts18, organocatalysts19 and ionic liquids etc. The studies reveal ionic liquids (ILs) as a promising option for this purpose. The reason is again their high thermal stability, solvent properties, non-flammability, negligible vapor pressure, and most importantly their amendable affinity for capturing of CO220. A number of ionic liquids have been utilized in the conversion of CO2 to the cyclic carbonates, for example, halide anions based ionic liquids18,19 where halide ion works in the ring opening of epoxide during the reaction20–22. Some other ILs are also used in the CO2 conversion reactions like carboxylic-based ionic liquids, benzyl, hydroxyl, and triazine-substituted ionic liquids, metal-containing ionic liquids and also protic ILs. A recent experimental study reported by Goodrich et al.21 shows CO2 conversion reaction through aprotic heterocyclic anions (like benzimidazolate and triazolate) based ILs where good yields are obtained along with good selectivity.

Owing to the unique characteristics of ILs and their well-known usage in the CO2 conversion process, we have conducted a detailed study on CO2 conversion to cyclic carbonate through a series of ILs i.e., alkylmethylimidazolium chlorides [CnMIM][Cl] where n = 1 to 6. The ILs used specifically are, 1,3-dimethylimidazolium chloride ([C1MIM][Cl]), 1-ethyl-3-methyl-imidazolium chloride ([C2MIM][Cl]), 1-proyl-3-methyl-imidazolium chloride ([C3MIM][Cl]), 1-butyl-3-methyl-imidazolium chloride ([C4MIM][Cl]), 1-pentyl-3-methyl-imidazolium chloride ([C5MIM][Cl]), and 1-hexyl-3-methyl-imidazolium chloride ([C6MIM][Cl]). These ionic liquids differ on the basis of substituents attached to the cation. The main purpose of our study is to analyze the effect of nature of cation on the overall progress of the reaction of conversion of CO2.

Research methodology

The ionic liquids i.e., [C1MIM][Cl], [C2MIM][Cl], [C3MIM][Cl], [C4MIM][Cl], [C5MIM][Cl] and [C6MIM][Cl] are used for conversion of CO2 at B3LYP/6-311G(d, p). All the calculations have been performed at Gaussian 0922 and for visualization of input and output files GaussView is utilized. The molecule of CO2 is converted to cyclic carbonates in the current study. For this purpose, the mechanistic study is carried out in detail. It includes the reaction between one of the epoxides i.e., propylene oxide and CO2 in the presence of these ILs separately as catalysts. For studying mechanism of conversion process, the transition states are optimized and the barriers are calculated through energy differences between the transition states and reactant molecules (Eq. 1). In this regard, for the confirmation of true minima in optimization processes and location of transition states, vibrational frequency analysis is considered at the same level of theory. Through frequency analysis, the number of imaginary frequencies show whether a transition state or a minimum has been located. When the result of frequency analysis shows all the positive frequencies, its confirmation of a true minimum while arousal of only one imaginary frequency validates the location of a transition state. The calculations have also been performed in solvent, i.e., water, using the polarizable continuum model (PCM). The purpose of calculations in solvent is to study effect of presence of solvent on mechanism of CO2 conversion.

In order to study the orbital interactions between reactants and ionic liquids, FMO analysis is considered. It divulges the changes in orbital interactions when the reaction takes place. Moreover, in the process of conversion of CO2, non-bonding interactions between ILs and the reactants play main role while the reaction proceeds forward. Therefore, it is imperative to study the varying types of interactions when the reaction progresses from reactants to transition state species and further to the product. In this respect, the non-covalent interactions (NCI) analysis is considered which distinguishes and visualizes different nonbonding interaction forces i.e., repulsive forces, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic interactions. Moreover, when the process of conversion of CO2 is performed, the calculated energy barriers can be well understood or justified through the study of noncovalent interactions between the reactants and ILs. Through NCI analysis, we get 2-D reduced density gradient (RDG) plots along with 3-D isosurfaces of complexes which are generated with the help of Multiwfn 3.8 software23. The 2-D RDG graphs depend on electron density (ρ) and reduced density gradient24. Their mathematical relationship is given as:

The RDG is derived from electron density and its gradient. It enables the identification of regions of non-covalent interactions which are characterized by low density and low-density variation.

In RDG analysis, we work with the electron density ρ(r) and its second derivatives, collected in a matrix called the Hessian of electron density. The Hessian consists of three eigenvalues i.e., λ1, λ2, and λ3. Out of these three eigenvalues, λ2 tells us whether electron density is locally accumulating or depleting perpendicular to an interaction region. It is the most sensitive indicator of the nature of interaction. The sign of λ₂ helps in differentiation between repulsive (when λ₂ > 0), attractive (when λ₂ < 0) and dispersive (when λ₂ ≈ 0) interactions. Accordingly, the RDG isosurfaces are presented with colors such that red represents repulsive forces, greenish-brown region depicts London dispersion forces, and blue regions correspond to electrostatic interactions. The spatial extension and thickness of these different colors qualitatively reflect the prominence of the underlying type of interactions, while the color coding distinguishes their physical nature.

Results and discussion

Mechanism of CO2 conversion

The mechanistic study of conversion of CO2 is carried out in the current study. The conversion process involves the use of alkylmethylimidazolium chlorides ([CnMIM][Cl]) i.e., [C1MIM][Cl], [C2MIM][Cl], [C3MIM][Cl], [C4MIM][Cl], [C5MIM][Cl] and [C6MIM][Cl] (Fig. 1) separately as catalysts. With ILs as catalysts, the process takes place by the use of one of the epoxides i.e., propylene oxide (PO), as another reactant with CO2. The general mechanism (Fig. 2) that is followed in the current study is the one that is considered as more competitive and has been testified as the one with lower energy barriers according to previous studies25,26. It involves the nucleophilic attack of halide ion of the ionic liquid i.e., Cl−, on the propylene oxide for ring opening. Ring opening leads to the formation of oxy-anion which further reacts with CO2 to form cyclic carbonate.

Structure of ILs with varying alkyl chain length (figures generated using GaussView v5.0).

General mechanism for cycloaddition of propylene oxide and CO2 in the presence of B+A− (IL) where B+ represents a cation and A− represents an anion of ionic liquid (drawing software used is ChemDraw v15.0).

With [CnMIM][Cl], anion (Cl−) acts as a nucleophile while the cation (CnMIM) functions to stabilize the transition state specifically through hydrogen bonding interactions via its acidic proton spot. The reactants, transition states, intermediates and product are abbreviated as R(IL), TS-1(IL)/TS-2(IL), Int(IL) and P(IL) where IL indicates the particular IL in the presence of which reaction takes place. For example, the symbols R([C2MIM][Cl]), TS-1([C2MIM][Cl])/TS-2([C2MIM][Cl]), Int([C2MIM][Cl]) and P([C2MIM][Cl]) are used when reaction takes place in the presence of [C2MIM][Cl]. Moreover, the figures of the structures of optimized transition states are provided in Fig. S1.

Following the general mechanism, with [C1MIM][Cl], first step is performed where reaction between [C1MIM][Cl] and propylene oxide is carried out. In this step, Cl− of [C1MIM][Cl] attacks PO leading to the ring opening of propylene oxide. The ring opening resulting in oxy-anion formation proceeds through a transition state. The transition state (TS-1([C1MIM][Cl])) is located/optimized for the calculation of energy barrier involved in oxy-anion formation. The barrier is equal to 29.59 kcal/mol for an oxy-anion formation through [C1MIM][Cl]. The next step involves the reaction between oxyanion formed as intermediate in the reaction (Int([C1MIM][Cl])) and CO2. The negatively charged oxygen of oxyanion attacks the C-atom of CO2 and results in formation of cyclic carbonate. This step also proceeds through a transition state (TS-2([C1MIM][Cl])) and the energy barrier calculated for TS-2([C1MIM][Cl]) in this step is equal to 17.55 kcal/mol.

The conversion with [C2MIM][Cl] is also conducted through a similar mechanism. In this case, the energy barrier for transition state (TS-1([C2MIM][Cl])) involved in oxy-anion formation, is equal to 28.45 kcal/mol. While, for the next step i.e., the reaction between the oxyanion and CO2, the energy barrier for transition state (TS-2([C2MIM][Cl])) is equal to 16.89 kcal/mol. Comparison of energy barriers for both of these ILs shows that the barriers are reduced with [C2MIM][Cl] as compared to [C1MIM][Cl]. The reason can be the structure of cation in case of [C2MIM][Cl]. [C2MIM][Cl] contains a methyl and an ethyl group on the cationic ring while two methyl groups are present in case of [C1MIM][Cl] cation. The presence of ethyl in place of methyl (with more electron donating effect) in [C2MIM][Cl] results in reduction of positive charge of the cation ([C2MIM]) which decreases the strength of a cation and further affects cation-anion interaction. It has been reported in literature that increase in alkyl chain length of the substituents on the cationic specie reduces the effect of charge of cation27. This further causes decrease in cation’s interaction with the anion. In CO2 conversion reaction mechanism, the anion attacks the PO as first step of the reaction. When anion’s interaction is weaker with the cation, this allows the anion for stronger nucleophilic attack on PO as anion can be detached easily from cation. This is not possible in the cases where cation-anion interact strongly with each other. Moreover, in this way, the IL provides more room to the reactant species and overall involvement of the IL in the reaction increases.

The same conversion when performed with [C3MIM][Cl] showed the energy barrier for transition state (TS-1([C3MIM][Cl])) equal to 28.35 kcal/mol. While for TS2([C3MIM][Cl]), the energy barrier is equal to 17.49 kcal/mol. The barriers with [C3MIM][Cl] remain almost equal to the activation barriers calculated with [C2MIM][Cl] especially for step-I which is rate limiting step. The reason can be, the difference in electron donating effect of ethyl and propyl groups is negligible. Such lower difference causes the ring’s positive charge and acidity of the hydrogen of the cation to be almost similar hence the stabilization of transition state is also identical.

Moreover, CO2 conversion with other ILs i.e., [C4MIM][Cl], [C5MIM][Cl] and [C6MIM][Cl] shows further increase in energy barrier up to 29.79, 29.44 and 31.02 kcal/mol for TS1 while the barrier for TS2 remains around 17 kcal/mol. In these ILs, the length of alkyl chain exceeds to 4, 5 and 6 carbons, respectively. As the alkyl chain increases in length it tends to curl slightly and occupy the space near the acidic proton. This overall restricts the vibrational freedom of the PO (TS-1) and hence the barrier can be seen to be increased with these ILs (highest for [C6MIM][Cl]. Figure 3 shows the potential energy diagram illustrating variation in activation barriers with alkyl chain length of the ionic liquids.

The interaction distances between the atoms of transition state species i.e., between the atoms of ILs and the atoms of reacting molecules (Fig. S1) show that in [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl], the distances are shorter as compared to [C1MIM][Cl]. The shorter distances not only point towards the stronger interactions but the providence of more room to the reacting bodies by the [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl]. Hence, overall in the presence of [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl], more room is provided to the reacting bodies due to the presence of weaker interactions between the cation and anion of the IL and ultimately the energy barrier is lower in the presence of these ILs. However, the surprising results have been seen for the next three ILs i.e., [C4MIM][Cl], [C5MIM][Cl] and [C6MIM][Cl]. The interaction distances in the presence of these ILs are still shorter and in fact equal to the interaction distances calculated with [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl]. The reason can be, as the alkyl chain is increasing in size, it is intervening in such a way that although the PO manages to reach closer to the acidic proton of the cation but such closeness and nearby presence of long alkyl chain reduce vibrational freedom of the transition state. This results in elevation of energy barrier for longer alkyl substituents containing ILs (i.e., the highest for [C6MIM][Cl])). The structures with bond distances are presented in Fig. S1.

Additionally, we observe that the energy barriers for the first transition states (in case of all the selected ILs), where opening of PO takes place, are higher as compared to the second step where CO2 is utilized in the reaction and is converted to the product (Fig. 3). The reason for lower energy barrier is strong nucleophilic nature of oxyanion which attacks the carbon atom of CO2 easily thus involving the molecule of CO2 in the formation of propylene carbonate. Although for TS-1, the opening of ring of PO occurs which is the process in which ring strain is released. Yet the barrier for TS-2 is lower. This can be due to the fact that in case of TS-2 there are three oxygen atoms in the structure which develop strong hydrogen bonding interactions with the atoms of the catalyst (ILs). The larger number of electronegative sites which are prone to developing stronger interactions with the atoms of ILs (especially hydrogen atoms of cation) overall bring the R-2 and TS-2 to lower energy as evident from their energies (as compared to R-1 and TS-1). So, we can say that the TS-2 is stabilized more as compared to TS-1 (in case of all the ILs) due to stronger interactions between components of TS-2 specie and ILs i.e., three electronegative oxygen atoms (with one oxygen completely negatively charged) and one positively charged carbon atom. Additionally, the results of our study are consistent with the previously reported CO2 conversion studies with ionic liquids which say that the energy barrier for the ring opening step is usually higher as compared to the CO2 insertion step28.

Furthermore, the reaction is also conducted in solvent (water). The results of the energy barriers calculated in solvent clearly show the reduction in values of the activation energies. For the first step (epoxide ring opening), the activation barriers decrease to the range of 24.51 to 25.48 kcal/mol for [CnMIM][Cl]. While for the second step (cyclization), the barriers decrease to approximately 9.76–10.04 kcal/mol. The polar solvent stabilizes the transition states due to the more polar nature of transition states. This ultimately reduces the energy barriers. Moreover, solvent-phase results (using PCM/water as a polar continuum model) suggest the process becomes more favorable in a polar environment.

Additionally, the change in Gibb’s free energy of the reaction (ΔG) is also calculated (through Eq. 2) in order to study the thermodynamic feasibility of the reaction (Table 1). The values of ΔG calculated for first step of the reaction range from − 0.15 to -2.43 kcal/mol while for the second step of reaction ΔG is in the range of -7.24 to -12.82 kcal/mol. Overall, with all the selected 1-alkyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride, the reaction is exergonic (including both the steps of the reaction). However, the first step i.e., the formation of the oxyanion intermediate is less exergonic because, although ring strain of the epoxide is relieved in this step, but a localized negative charge is generated which leads to a relatively high-energy ionic intermediate. On the other hand, in the second step, cyclic carbonate is formed which is significantly more exergonic due to charge delocalization within the carbonate specie, formation of additional strong C–O bonds, and resonance stabilization. The enhanced thermodynamic stability of the formation of cyclic carbonate relative to the oxyanion intermediate accounts for the larger free energy release in the second step. Moreover, comparing ΔG values of all these ILs, we see that the larger values are obtained in case of [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl] (− 2.43 kcal/mol for step 1 with [C2MIM][Cl] and − 12.82 kcal/mol for step 2 with [C3MIM][Cl]) suggesting a modest thermodynamic preference for medium-chain ILs, though the small ΔG differences (~ 1–2 kcal/mol for step 1) are within the typical uncertainty of DFT.

In this way, the imidazolium-based ILs show fascinating results for their use as CO2 conversion agents. Also, imidazolium with medium sized alkyl chain length is more efficient in their CO2 conversion ability. Wang et al. has proved through the similar theoretical study at B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level of DFT that without a catalyst, the reaction barriers for CO2 cycloaddition to propylene oxide are as high as 52.7 and 59.0 kcal/mol26. Moreover, we have also performed a CO2 conversion reaction with tetramethylammonium based IL in another study. The results of that study show that the barriers for the reaction range up to 35.5 kcal/mol25. Hence, with imidazolium based ILs, the barriers are reduced overall. Comparing the tetramethylammonium chloride (TMACl) with imidazolium chloride, the difference lies in the structure of cation. The charge is concentrated in case of TMACl while it is delocalized in case [CnMIM][Cl]. The delocalization of charge decreases the strength of cation’s positive charge ultimately decreasing cation-anion interaction which is stronger in case of TMACl. This further affects CO2 conversion efficiency (as discussed above). Our study reveals that nature of cation of IL clearly affects the CO2 conversion efficiency of ILs. The change in nature of cation affects the energy barriers by decreasing the barrier from [C1MIM][Cl] to [C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl]. However, further increase in barrier is observed for [C4MIM][Cl], [C5MIM][Cl], and [C6MIM][Cl] due to combined electronic and steric effects.

Previously, different ionic liquids with different cation-anion combinations have been utilized for conversion of CO2 up till now. For example, quaternary ammonium (as cation) based IL, imidazolium-based IL, and pyridinium based IL have already been tested for their CO2 conversion capabilities29. Moreover, an experimental study reveals that ILs containing imidazolium cation with medium sized alkyl chain show higher catalytic efficiency as compared to the imidazolium with longer chains30. The results of this study are consistent with our computed activation barriers but the reason behind this barrier landscape is still unclear. We have explored the origin of this trend by observing electronic and steric effects involved.

Electronic characteristics

Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis

The densities or position of HOMO/LUMO (given in Fig. 4/Figure S2) and HOMO-LUMO gaps (H-L gaps) (Table 1) of ILs show variation when reactants/transition state species interact with ILs. In case of bare [CnMIM[[Cl] series, the densities of HOMO lie mainly over the anions of ionic liquids while LUMO is distributed over the cationic ring. This distribution shows the nucleophilic character of anion and electrophilic character of the cation of selected ILs. However, upon interaction with reactants/transition states, the distribution of the densities of HOMO and LUMO change slightly showing the orbital reorganization and electronic coupling between ILs and reacting species.

HOMO and LUMO densities of reactants and transition state species with [CnMIM][Cl] (where n = 1) (figures generated using GaussView v5.0 from Gaussian 09 output files).

Moreover, the values of H-L gaps show that the energy gaps also change when reaction starts or when reacting bodies interact with ionic liquids. This confirms the overall reorganization of the orbitals of ILs in the presence of reactants. Additionally, it has been observed that the H-L gaps of the [CnMIM][Cl] change after their interaction with the reacting bodies but the more pronounced reduction in H-L gap is observed for the TS-complexes as compared to the reactants. Such behavior can be ascribed to greater electronic polarization, increased charge delocalization and enhanced orbital mixing in case of the transition states as the processes of bond forming and bond breaking occur simultaneously. Overall, the results of FMO analysis show the role of the ionic liquids in facilitating the reaction pathway.

Non-covalent interactions (NCI) analysis

In order to analyze the nature of interactions between the reactants/transition states and ILs, NCI analysis is performed. These interactions have influence on the energy barriers of the reaction. NCI analysis gives visual illustration of interactions through 3D structures and 2D graphs of the complexes under study. The analysis works through a color scheme where each color in the structure and graph depicts a specific type of interaction. In graphs as well as 3D structures, greenish-brown color represents London dispersion forces, while the hydrogen bonding is illustrated by blue color. Moreover, red color is the depiction of repulsive forces. The colored patches or isosurfaces in 3D structures generated through NCI analysis spot the atoms/group of atoms between which the various types of non-covalent interactions are present. While the thickness of these patches shows the strength of interaction forces. The thicker the patches or isosurfaces, the stronger the strength of interactions. Additionally, the graph is generated between λ2(ρ) and reduced density gradient. The graph contains the spikes of the above-mentioned colors where color of spikes shows the type of interactions while the density and position of spike over λ2(ρ) points towards the strength of interactions. The spikes lying over more negative area of λ2(ρ) represent the stronger interactions.

The main purpose of NCI analysis in the current study is to understand the reason behind the differences in energy barriers calculated for reactions with ILs with nature of cation slightly different from one another.

The 3D figures generated for [CnMIM][Cl] (n = 1 to 6) (Fig. 5/ Fig. S3) show that [C2MIM][Cl]-TS1 and [C3MIM][Cl]-TS1 complexes contain clear and intense blue patches between acidic hydrogen and PO showing strong and stable hydrogen bonding. On the other hand, minor green patches can be seen between the PO and the alkyl chain of the cation in the same complexes. The graphs confirm the interactions present in the 3D figures. These features reveal that these two ILs give most appropriate or efficient configuration where cation provides minimum physical interference but maximum electronic stabilization. However, as discussed earlier that the energy barrier rises for [C4MIM][Cl], and [C5MIM][Cl], the NCI analysis results for these ILs show that notable green patches begin to appear between the PO and the alkyl chain of the cation. Although technically the arousal of green patches points towards the stabilizing of the complex but at the same time it signals that the alkyl chain is physically occupying the same space as the PO. This overall restricts the vibrational freedom of the PO (TS-1). Hence the result is the elevation of barriers for these ILs. Moreover, for [C6MIM][Cl], the barrier jumps to 31 kcal/mol because the longer hexyl chain is even curled and its interference with the PO is even more. We can see the greenish patches where the alkyl chain faces PO. So, although the distance between the PO and cation is shorter in this case as the alkyl chain is squeezing the PO into the acidic proton but this compression is energetically expensive. The system needs to overcome a high energy penalty to overcome the steric repulsive interactions between the hexyl chain and the PO.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the mechanistic study conducted in the current study utilizes chloride anion (Cl⁻) only. The effect of nucleophilic action of chloride is explored in detail while slightly changing the structure of cation. Moreover, only a single epoxide is considered in the reaction. Hence, this work can also be proceeded forward offering more detailed analysis of the reaction by considering other anions and other epoxides as well. Moreover, in the current study, the primary energy barriers reported are gas-phase values; since these reactions occur in condensed phase, the absolute barrier heights may differ under experimental conditions. Furthermore, a single ion-pair model has been utilized as the catalyst; this does not capture bulk ionic liquid properties such as long-range Coulombic interactions, ion clustering, or solvation shells present in real ionic liquid media. Additionally, thermodynamic quantities are computed at 298.15 K, whereas CO₂ cycloaddition reactions are typically conducted at 80–120 °C; the relative trends may shift at elevated temperatures.

Conclusions

A systematic mechanistic analysis of conversion of CO₂ to cyclic carbonates catalyzed by [CnMIM][Cl] (n = 1–6) ILs shows a non-monotonic dependence of computed activation energy barriers on the alkyl chain length of the substituent attached to the cation. The results show that medium-sized alkyl chains ([C2MIM][Cl] and [C3MIM][Cl]) exhibit the lowest activation energy barriers and most favorable thermodynamics. While the longer chains show higher energy barriers due to combined electronic and steric effects. Moreover, NCI analysis also supports the role of optimized ion–reactant interactions in stabilizing transition states. Overall, the results demonstrate that catalytic efficiency is the result of a balance between steric and electronic factors, providing useful computational guidance for the selection of imidazolium chloride-based ionic liquids for CO₂ cycloaddition reactions.
