Graft joint inflow strategy for visceral debranching in hybrid thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair
Authors: Kazuhiro Ueno, Takashi Shuto, Takeshi Wada, Takayuki Kawashima, Shinji Miyamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn hybrid thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair, inflow selection is a critical determinant of visceral perfusion. Conventional limb-based inflow may result in unfavorable hemodynamics, whereas direct trunk inflow can compromise the distal landing zone for endovascular repair. We describe a graft joint inflow strategy for visceral debranching that uses the junction between the trunk and limbs of a bifurcated graft. This configuration relocates the inflow closer to the abdominal aortic axis while preserving landing zone flexibility. The technique is presented as an alternative inflow strategy in selected hybrid procedures.Keywords: Graft joint inflow, TEVAR, Hybrid repair, Thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, Surgical technique, Visceral debranching

Hybrid repair combining visceral debranching and thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair (TEVAR) is an established, less invasive alternative to open repair for thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms in elderly or high-risk patients.1, 2, 3 Durable visceral revascularization is essential, as inadequate graft patency or visceral perfusion can adversely affect outcomes.

In visceral debranching with prosthetic grafts, the inflow site is often selected based on anatomical constraints or institutional preference. Conventionally, inflow is obtained from a limb of a bifurcated graft; however, computational and experimental studies have shown that iliac-based inflow is associated with reduced renal and visceral perfusion and disturbed flow patterns.4,5 In contrast, an inflow positioned closer to the abdominal aortic trunk provides a more favorable flow distribution.4 Despite this advantage, direct trunk inflow is not always feasible when preservation of an adequate distal landing zone for subsequent TEVAR is required.

Accordingly, we describe a graft joint inflow strategy for visceral debranching that balances proximal hemodynamic benefit with technical feasibility by using the junction between the trunk and limbs of a bifurcated graft as the inflow source.

Technique

Patient selection

This graft joint inflow strategy was applied in patients undergoing visceral debranching as part of hybrid repair for thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms. At our institution, visceral debranching is routinely performed in conjunction with infrarenal abdominal aortic replacement using a bifurcated prosthetic graft or by using a previously implanted bifurcated abdominal graft in patients with a history of prior abdominal aortic aneurysm repair. In the early phase of our experience, the inflow anastomosis for visceral debranching was created on a limb of the bifurcated graft (Fig 1, A). However, owing to concerns regarding the adequacy of limb-based inflow for visceral perfusion, the inflow configuration was modified from approximately 2017 onward to use the graft joint between the trunk and limbs of the bifurcated graft (Fig 1, B).

Surgical setup

Visceral debranching was performed through a midline transperitoneal approach under general anesthesia. In patients undergoing concomitant abdominal aortic replacement, the infrarenal abdominal aorta was replaced with a bifurcated prosthetic graft. In patients with a history of prior open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, the existing bifurcated abdominal graft was used as the inflow source without replacement. Visceral reconstruction was performed using a quadrifurcated graft (InterGard Quattro; Getinge Group), which was anastomosed to the bifurcated abdominal graft. Branch diameters were selected according to the target vessels, with 6-mm grafts used for renal arteries and 7-mm grafts used for the superior mesenteric artery and celiac trunk, consistent with our standard institutional practice.

Step-by-step description of the graft joint inflow technique

Step 1: Identification of the graft joint

The graft joint, defined as the junction between the trunk and limbs of the bifurcated abdominal graft, is identified and prepared as the inflow site. Care is taken to maintain the native orientation of the bifurcated graft and to avoid distortion of the graft limbs.

Step 2: Orientation of the visceral graft

The quadrifurcated graft is oriented along the central flow axis of the bifurcated graft to achieve a coaxial configuration. The main trunk of the visceral graft is positioned with a smooth and gently curved trajectory toward the visceral arteries. The individual branches are arranged with distributed branching toward the target visceral arteries, minimizing crossing, sharp angulation, and excessive lateral deviation. This orientation facilitates physiological flow distribution and reduces unfavorable graft geometry at the branch origins.

Step 3: Inflow anastomosis at the graft joint

An anastomosis is created between the trunk-limb junction of the bifurcated graft and the main trunk of the quadrifurcated graft using a continuous polypropylene suture. Because the anastomosis spans the graft junction, inflow is derived from the abdominal aortic trunk rather than from a single limb. This configuration relocates the inflow closer to the abdominal aortic axis while preserving an adequate distal landing zone for subsequent TEVAR.

Step 4: Visceral branch reconstruction

After completion of the inflow anastomosis, the proximal renal arteries are occluded and transected using Hem-o-lok (Teleflex). Both renal arteries are reconstructed with end-to-end anastomoses using 6-mm grafts. The proximal portions of the celiac trunk and the superior mesenteric artery are also occluded using Hem-o-lok and reconstructed with end-to-side anastomoses using 7-mm grafts. All vascular reconstructions are performed through retroperitoneal exposure after medial visceral rotation via the initial transperitoneal approach. After each anastomosis, graft orientation is reassessed to confirm the absence of tension, excessive angulation, or branch kinking.

Step 5: Preparation of the access route for TEVAR

If the iliac arteries are unsuitable, an alternative access route for subsequent TEVAR is created. A separate 10-mm graft can be anastomosed to the trunk portion of the quadrifurcated graft, and its distal end is anastomosed to the femoral artery for use as the access for TEVAR (Fig 1, C).

Step 6: Completion and flow confirmation

Adequate pulsatile flow is confirmed visually and by Doppler ultrasound assessment in each reconstructed branch. The graft configuration is inspected to ensure stable geometry without twisting or compression before abdominal closure.

Clinical experience

This graft joint inflow strategy was applied in patients undergoing visceral debranching as part of staged hybrid repair for thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms. All procedures were performed at a single institution by experienced vascular surgeons using a standardized operative approach. Technical success is defined as the successful completion of all planned visceral anastomoses using the graft joint inflow configuration, followed by subsequent TEVAR. Postoperative anticoagulation was not routinely administered for the reconstructed visceral branch grafts. During follow-up, graft patency was assessed using contrast-enhanced computed tomography according to the institutional surveillance protocol (Fig 2, Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). To date, this graft joint inflow strategy has been applied in 11 patients undergoing hybrid thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair (TEVAR), involving a total of 41 visceral anastomoses. During a mean follow-up period of 1.5 ± 1.7 years, no graft occlusion was observed. No patient required continuous renal replacement therapy, chronic hemodialysis, or renal transplantation during follow-up, and no reintervention attributable to the inflow configuration has been observed. Technical success, defined as completion of all planned visceral anastomoses followed by subsequent TEVAR, was achieved in all cases. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Oita University Hospital (approval number: 2955), and the requirement for individual patient consent was waived.

Discussion

The selection of an appropriate inflow source remains a key technical consideration in visceral debranching during hybrid repair of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms. Conventional limb-based inflow is widely used; however, prior hemodynamic and clinical studies have suggested that inflow originating farther from the abdominal aortic trunk may be associated with less favorable visceral perfusion.4, 5, 6 The graft joint inflow strategy described in this report represents a pragmatic technical modification intended to position inflow closer to the abdominal aortic trunk while preserving procedural flexibility.

In our earlier institutional experience with limb-based inflow, branch graft occlusion was observed, raising concerns about the durability of distal inflow configurations. In contrast, no branch graft occlusion has been observed in the current graft joint series during follow-up. These observations are descriptive and not intended as a formal statistical comparison.

By using the junction between the trunk and limbs of a bifurcated graft, this approach positions the inflow closer to the central flow axis without compromising the distal landing zone required for staged TEVAR. The graft joint provides a reproducible and technically accessible anastomotic site, allowing stable orientation of the visceral graft. In selected cases, this configuration also permits extension of the graft trunk to the femoral artery, facilitating the creation of an alternative access route for subsequent TEVAR when standard iliac access is unsuitable. Although total endovascular repair using multibranched devices has expanded in recent years, hybrid repair remains relevant in selected patients with unsuitable anatomy, prior abdominal aortic reconstruction, limited device availability, or urgent presentations.

Conclusions

A graft joint inflow strategy offers a simple and reproducible approach for inflow selection in visceral debranching during hybrid TEVAR. Further investigation is warranted to better characterize its hemodynamic implications.
