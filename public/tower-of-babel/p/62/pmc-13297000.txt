An examination of the availability and characteristics of social needs data in the electronic health records: a path to social data harmonization and standardization at Johns Hopkins medicine
Authors: Elham Hatef, Christopher Kitchen, Thomas Richards, Benjamin Martin, Nailu L G Lopes, Harold P Lehmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo analyze social needs documentation across structured electronic sources in Johns Hopkins Health System, describing practices, patient characteristics, and utility for care, research, and population health.Materials and MethodsRetrospective study of electronic health records (EHR) data, 2016-2023. Social needs domains were extracted from flowsheets, ICD-10/SNOMED codes, the Social Needs Registry, and the Wellness Registry. A 6-step process refined and aggregated flowsheet entries; regression models assessed associations between documentation and demographic/clinical factors.ResultsAmong 1 042 184 patients, overall, 63 595 unique patients (6.1%) had social needs documented in at least one data source. Demographically, patients with documented social needs were more likely to be Black or African American (40.2%) or Hispanic/Latino (10%) and had higher comorbidity (Charlson Comorbidity Index scores of 2.31 vs 0.84) and healthcare utilization (69.5 vs 6.1% hospitalized). Among patients with 3 or more domains of documented social needs, residential instability was the most prevalent, affecting 85.2% of this subgroup. Regression models showed African American race, Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, legal separation, and clinical severity were associated with greater documentation.DiscussionDocumentation of social needs in structured fields was sparse and inconsistent across sources, reflecting variable workflows and limited integration of structured data. These gaps hinder measurement, intervention, and equity goals.ConclusionGiven the inconsistencies we observed in social needs documentation across EHR components and need for properly harmonizing extracted values, we identified practices and workflows that enhance usability for clinical care, research, and population health, and to support equity. Standardized screening, staff training, and reporting are always needed to improve data interoperability and quality.

Introduction

Social needs data and associated clinical risks are critical for understanding patient care beyond typical clinical documentation. Social needs influence access and outcomes through multiple pathways, including barriers to primary care (eg, transportation, work instability),1,2 increased acute care utilization (eg, emergency department visits driven by unmet needs), and exacerbation of chronic disease management (eg, medication adherence impacted by financial strain).3,4 Systemic factors, including structural racism and socioeconomic disadvantage, contribute to differential exposure to social needs across race/ethnicity and comorbidity profiles, motivating our stratified analyses.5

Thus, systematically collecting, standardizing, and integrating this information into electronic health records (EHRs) can improve quality of care delivery, enhance research capabilities, and support population health initiatives.6 The expanded capabilities of health information technology and interoperable platforms now provide unprecedented opportunities to capture, harmonize, and analyze social needs data effectively in a common data model (CDM).

Currently, data on social needs and risks within EHRs originate from various sources, including clinical notes, diagnostic codes, surveys, and questionnaires. Some EHR vendors, such as EPIC, offer tools to facilitate structured data collection; however, a universally accepted standard for documenting, organizing, and analyzing social needs data has yet to be developed. This complicates efforts to share and interpret data both internally and across healthcare systems.7–12 Interoperability initiatives, such as the Chesapeake Regional Information System for our Patients (CRISP), Maryland’s Health Information Exchange,13 and collaborations with community-based organizations hold significant promise but consistently rely on harmonized data formats for effective integration and collaboration.14,15 These CDMs can be diverse and incomplete for capturing social needs data, even with specific tools such as the EPIC social determinants of health (SDOH) Wheel.16,17

EPIC flowsheets, for example, serve as valuable repositories for clinically relevant, high-volume data, including routine clinical assessments, laboratory findings, organizational workflow data, and symptom screening. However, the organization and governance of recording flowsheet data are often complex.18 Each record corresponds to specific forms, rows, or templates, and without consistent standards, these records frequently store a mix of duplicative, deprecated, or diverse clinical observations and administrative procedures. For instance, a recent study revealed that approximately 63% of clinicians documenting vital signs for one health system included free text comments in flowsheets, a practice many deemed necessary to capture complex clinical issues.19 This reliance on free text can complicate the effective use or validation of observations, particularly when numeric values are stored as free text alongside more descriptive information, leading to challenges in managing the diverse structures of the database. Additionally, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) Z codes for social needs are frequently underused in practice, shifting reliance to other, less interoperable sources for consistent data capture.20

Despite these challenges, health systems have successfully leveraged flowsheets in their EHRs to systematically capture and document social needs such as housing insecurity, food insecurity, and transportation challenges.21 To streamline data collection and facilitate the downstream use of this information in clinical decision support and care management workflows, these organizations have implemented standardized flowsheet templates derived from tools like the Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patients’ Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE).21 However, to ensure the usability of the information contained within flowsheets, Robust data abstraction and curation—standardizing values, resolving duplicates, and harmonizing fields—are necessary to enable reliable analyses and interoperability.

The secondary use of social needs data for patient care, research, and population health is dependent on data quality, standardization, and harmonization. It is critical to understand how social needs data are collected, represented, and organized in localized EHR implementations.22 Using CDMs—for example, the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP)—and extract-transform-load (ETL) processes enables the portability of these datasets across different systems.23,24 Harmonizing social needs data also facilitates the development of interoperable clinical decision support tools that can improve care delivery at scale.

As a large, integrated healthcare system that offers a range of inpatient and outpatient care settings, the Johns Hopkins Health System (JHHS) provides a unique and diverse environment for exploring how social needs data are collected, represented, and utilized. This project aims to lay the groundwork for the interoperability and secondary use of social needs data, ultimately supporting regional data-sharing initiatives like those coordinated by CRISP13 and enhancing patient care, research, and population health practices.

The objectives of this project were to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the availability of documented social needs and risk data in the JHHS EHR and to assess the characteristics of patients with documentation of selected social needs domains. The analysis focused on data captured in structured EHR elements, such as flowsheets and questionnaires, to better understand how social needs information is documented and organized within the health system. Throughout, we refer to documented social needs in structured EHR data. Absence of documentation should not be interpreted as absence of need.

Methods

We examined the following social needs domains: financial instability, residential instability (such as homelessness and housing insecurity), utility needs, food insecurity, transportation needs, and interpersonal safety. Domains were specified a priori based on stakeholder input (clinicians, social workers, informaticians) and review of available EHR flowsheet and coding content.

We conducted a retrospective study to evaluate prospective year outcomes using JHHS EPIC-based EHR data from 2016 to 2023. The inclusion criteria required that patients have one or more valid clinical encounters (including both inpatient and outpatient settings) in both concurrent and prospective years. Concurrent year refers to the same calendar year in which a social need was documented. Prospective year refers to the calendar year immediately following documentation. Additionally, patients must have non-missing age and sex information, a non-missing address within the state of Maryland, and be aged 18 years or older. The Johns Hopkins institutional review board reviewed and approved this study as exempt and approved the EHR data extraction for the secondary analysis of deidentified data.

Structured data extraction

We developed the study dataset based on the available data and technical infrastructure. We extracted structured EHR data on social needs domains using existing templates and guides. We used structured query language (SQL) queries to retrieve data elements (eg, residential instability, food insecurity) and generated counts of patients, encounters, locations, and providers.

We also obtained ICD-10 and Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED) codes related to social needs using the “Compendium of Medical Terminology Codes for Social Risk Factors” developed by the Social Interventions Research and Evaluation Network,25 and extracted information on social needs from the JHHS EHR Social Needs Registry and Wellness Registry. These registries are Epic data mart tables that aggregate patient health information across multiple domains, including social history and risk scores. Their completeness depends on the availability and quality of structured data in Epic across clinics, service lines, and documentation workflows, resulting in variable data completeness and inconsistent adoption across sites.

After reviewing the classification of the codes by Social Interventions Research and Evaluation Network, we selected the codes related to financial instability, residential instability (such as homelessness and housing insecurity), utility needs, food insecurity, transportation needs, and interpersonal safety. We also reviewed available information on social needs in the EPIC Social Needs and Wellness Registry and selected variables corresponding to 1 of the 6 domains of social needs. We collapsed the responses available for each variable to generate a binary variable (“yes” or “no” indicator), suggesting the presence or absence of a social need. Null values were treated as missing; “No” responses were retained as documentation of absence for that encounter and considered in domain-level reconciliation. We identified documentation about social needs for each patient across the entire study period.

Flowsheets and questionnaires data extraction

We extracted flowsheet and questionnaire entries related to predefined social needs domains from JHHS-EHR. We applied keyword-based identification, normalization of response values, and domain mapping.

We de-duplicated records using unique EHR patient identifiers across hospitalizations, ambulatory encounters, and data sources (flowsheets, ICD-10/SNOMED codes, registries). For domain-level reconciliation, a patient was classified as having a documented social need if any encounter or source recorded a positive indication during the study period. Conflicting entries over time were retained for encounter-level analyses; for patient-level indicators, we prioritized explicit “Yes/Present” over “Unknown/Null” and treated “No” as documented absence at the encounter level. Detailed steps and rules, including data reshaping procedures and full keyword lists, are provided in the Supplemental Methods and Figure S1.

Thus, throughout this paper, we referred to “documented social needs” as positive documentation indicating one or more unmet social needs (eg, an affirmative response in a flowsheet, an ICD-10 Z-code for a social risk factor, or a registry entry indicating an unmet need). We distinguished this from “no documentation,” which encompassed either negative documentation (an explicit entry of “no social needs”) or the absence of any social needs documentation in the EHR.

Data analysis

Analyses characterize documentation practices of social needs in structured EHR data rather than the absolute presence of social needs. We conducted descriptive analyses to examine the study population, including their demographic characteristics and patterns of social needs documentation across structured and flowsheet EHR data. Additionally, we evaluated the associations between demographic (eg, age, gender, race, ethnicity, and marital status) and clinical characteristics (eg, Charlson Comorbidity Index,26 hospitalizations, and emergency room visits) with the documentation of social needs. To quantify these relationships, we fitted a multivariate linear regression model to assess the number of documented social needs domains, adjusted for both demographic and clinical factors. Analyses of concurrent and prospective outcomes were conducted at the patient level by assigning documentation year and examining utilization in the same and subsequent year.

Results

General findings

We identified a total of 1 042 184 patients across the study period. Overall, 63 595 unique patients (6.1% of sample) had social needs documented in at least one data source, including ICD-10 and SNOMED codes, Social Needs Registry, Wellness Registry, and Flowsheets, during the study. Most patients had a social need documented in only one source; Of these, total of 33 168 patients (52.2%) had information on selected social needs domains documented through ICD-10 and SNOMED codes and a total of 43 449 patients (68.3%) had data on selected social needs domains available in the flowsheet source. A total of 3166 patients (0.1%) had data in the Social Needs Registry, and 5185 patients (8.2%) had data available in the Wellness Registry. Overlap across sources was limited; among patients with documented social needs, only 16 568 (26.1%) had documentation in more than one source (Figure S2).

Of the 156 038 patients having at least one emergency room encounter, an observed 23 299 (14.9%) were found to have structured data indicating residential instability, 20 019 (12.8%) had a interpersonal safety concern, and 16 612 (10.5%) transportation needs. For patients with at least one hospitalization, 19 925 (19.2%) had housing concerns, 18 678 (18.0%) safety and 16 935 (16.3%) transportation, and for patients with neither emergency room or hospital care these percentages were just 0.7%, 0.2%, and 0.2% for each (see Table S1).

Table S2 presents the breakdown of the flowsheets for each documented social need domain by multiple levels (ie, row name, question, and value). These findings reflect documentation practices and not the absolute presence of social needs.

Figure 1 presents the counts of patients with selected documented social need domains across the study period. Residential instability consistently was the most commonly documented domain across all years, peaking at nearly 12 000 patients in 2021; However, residential instability remained higher than all other documented social needs domains throughout the observed period. Domains related to financial instability and food insecurity exhibited much smaller patient counts compared to residential instability, transportation needs, and inter-personal safety. The utility needs domain consistently had the lowest number of patients across all domains, with negligible changes in reporting or growth during the observed period.

Patients with documented social needs were, on average, slightly older (mean age: 54.8 years, standard deviation [SD] = 22.9) than those without documented social needs (mean age: 51.3 years, SD = 23.1). The proportion of females was lower in the documented social needs group (56%) compared to the no documented social needs group (58.8%). Patients with documented social needs included a markedly higher proportion of Black or African American individuals (40.2% vs 25.2%), while those without documented social needs had greater representation of White (57.5% vs 44.1%) and Asian individuals (5.8% vs 2.1%). Hispanic or Latino patients were more prevalent in the documented social needs group (10% vs 5.8%). Marital status also differed notably, with a higher proportion of patients with documented social needs being single (51% vs 38.1%), divorced (10.9% vs 6.1%), or legally separated (2% vs 0.7%). In contrast, patients without documented social needs were far more likely to be married (47.1% vs 23.8%).

Patients with documented social needs exhibited significantly greater overall comorbidity compared to those without documented social needs, as reflected in higher Charlson Comorbidity Index (mean score: 2.31, SD = 2.14 vs 0.84, SD = 1.22). The prevalence of chronic and severe medical conditions was also substantially higher in the documented social needs group. For instance, conditions such as Congestive Heart Failure were present in 22.9% of patients with social needs compared to 5.7% in those without. Similarly, Chronic Pulmonary Disease affected 38.6% of patients with documented social needs vs 18.5% in the no-documented-social-needs group, while AIDS occurred in 4.1% of the documented social needs group compared to only 0.8% in those without. Diabetes was nearly twice as common among patients with social needs (21.9% vs 11.7%), and metastatic cancer was also more prevalent (8.2% vs 4.8%). Less common but severe conditions, such as malignant liver disease, were strikingly more frequent in the documented social needs group, affecting 2.9% of individuals compared to just 0.5% of those without documented social needs.

Healthcare utilization was also notably higher among patients with documented social needs across both concurrent (current) and prospective (future) settings. Concurrent hospitalizations were observed in 69.5% of patients with documented social needs compared to just 6.1% in the no-documented-social-needs group, and concurrent emergency room visits were alarmingly higher at 76.1% vs 11%. Prospective hospitalizations were similarly elevated in the documented social needs group (36% vs 2.5%), as were prospective emergency room visits (47.1% vs 4.8%).

The majority of patients (93.9%) had no documented social needs, with 4.3% having one documented social needs domain, 1.2% having 2 domains, and only 0.6% having 3 or more domains (Table 2). Residential Instability was the most common documented social need, with its prevalence rising sharply as the number of documented social needs domains increased. It affected 41.7% of patients with one domain, 62.6% of patients with 2 domains, and 85.2% of patients with 3 or more domains. Utility Needs and Food Insecurity were less common compared to other social needs. Utility needs were documented in just 0.1% of patients with one domain but increased to 0.7% in 2 domains and 4.2% in 3 or more domains. Across all documented social needs domains, the prevalence of specific documented social needs increased markedly as patients experienced more domains of social needs. For example, financial instability was present in 5.7% of patients with one domain, 25% of those with 2 domains, and 63.7% of those with 3 or more domains.

Age had a very small but statistically significant positive effect (relative risk [RR] of 1.020, 95% confidence internal (CI) of 1.016-1.024) on the documentation of social needs. Compared to White patients, Black or African American patients had significantly more documented social needs (RR: 1.044, 95% CI: 1.042-1.046), and Hispanic or Latino patients had substantially more documented social needs compared to those who were Not Hispanic or Latino (RR: 1.080, 95% CI: 1.076-1.084). Discussion

Key findings

In this study, social needs documentation was sparse and inconsistent across sources, with limited overlap between methods and greater documentation among patients with higher utilization and comorbidity. Flowsheets and ICD-10/SNOMED codes were the primary recording mechanisms, while specialized registries (eg, Social Needs and Wellness Registries) captured only a small fraction of patients. These patterns highlight opportunities to improve documentation practices and integration across data sources.

This pattern is consistent with prior studies showing that ICD-10 codes capture only a small subset of patients with documented social needs,27–30 whereas structured screening tools have identified substantially higher prevalence (approximately 16%-19%).31–33 In our cohort, the prevalence identified via flowsheets in the JHHS EHR was lower than these estimates, suggesting under-recording in routine structured fields. Accordingly, absence of documentation should not be interpreted as absence of social needs. Also, the limited concordance across sources suggests these data capture distinct aspects of documentation workflows and patient interactions, underscoring the need for harmonized capture strategies.34

The patterns of documented social needs across the study period (Figure 1) likely reflect operational workflows (eg, screening occurring in specific service lines) and encounter-driven opportunities to document social needs. Improved capture at consistent touchpoints could reduce bias toward higher-utilizing patients and enable more comprehensive identification across populations.

Demographic and clinical insights

Our study revealed differences between patients with and without documented social needs. Patients with documented social needs were disproportionately Black or African American or Hispanic or Latino and exhibited higher rates of marital instability and chronic conditions, such as diabetes and congestive heart failure.35 They had significantly higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores, reflecting greater clinical risk, and experienced markedly increased healthcare utilization.3,36,37 These findings highlight the systemic and clinical burden associated with unmet social needs.

The prevalence of specific documented social needs domains strongly correlated with patients experiencing multiple overlapping social needs. Residential instability emerged as the most frequently documented need, particularly among patients with multiple domains of documented social needs. The adjusted regression model further highlighted the association between social needs documentation and demographic and clinical factors, with groups facing greater social and clinical vulnerabilities being more likely to have documented needs. These findings emphasize the additive nature of social risks and their pivotal role in shaping outcomes for highly vulnerable populations with overlapping demographic and clinical vulnerabilities. The co-occurrence of documented social needs we observed is consistent with prior studies documenting clustering of housing instability, food insecurity, and transportation barriers; this underscores the need for integrated interventions addressing multiple domains.38-40 Furthermore, the results suggest a potential documentation bias, as patients with greater social or clinical vulnerabilities tend to have more frequent healthcare encounters, thereby increasing the likelihood of their documented social needs being identified and recorded.

Comparison with prior work and methodological considerations

Our findings align with prior work regarding the challenges and opportunities of capturing social needs data.10,20,22,41 Structured fields, including flowsheets and questionnaires, are recognized as important sources of information, yet the lack of standardization across EHR vendors continues to pose challenges for interoperability. Prior studies have emphasized variability in flowsheet design and the need for harmonization efforts, particularly around mapping to standard terminologies such as ICD-10, LOINC, or SNOMED.10,20,41 As noted by other work, the integration of documented social needs data across systems often relies on emerging frameworks such as OMOP for semantic consistency.10,22 In our study, within a single health system, we successfully integrated data across flowsheets, ICD-10/SNOMED, and registries for this analysis. Broad interoperability across systems—while outside our present study scope—remains a critical challenge for scaling social needs documentation, particularly for standardized representation and exchange.

Methodologically, leveraging structured data sources—especially flowsheets—requires meticulous extraction, cleaning, and aggregation to ensure validity and usability.10,42,43 Inconsistent naming conventions, redundant variables, and variability in design complicate these efforts.44,45 Despite these challenges, flowsheets remain a promising resource for capturing social needs data, particularly when complemented with standardized CDMs and automated ETL processes. Modern frameworks such as OMOP show potential for improving data interoperability,22 but institutional variability and non-standardized field usage present barriers to scaling these efforts.46,47

Furthermore, variability in the completeness of documentation across data sources underscores broader challenges in standardizing social needs data collection workflows.22 This structural complexity has limited the use of flowsheets in secondary analyses, such as research and population health initiatives. Nonetheless, flowsheets present an opportunity to complement other data sources, such as ICD-10 codes and registries, to provide a more comprehensive perspective on social needs. Investigating the potential of flowsheet-like structures in non-Epic systems and understanding their compatibility with frameworks like OMOP could further advance efforts to standardize and expand the use of social needs data in healthcare.

To improve the documentation of social needs in flowsheets, we recommend: standardizing flowsheet screening questions and response options; embedding social needs screening in intake workflows (eg, primary care, inpatient admission, ED triage); enabling auto-population of standard codes from flowsheet responses; implementing periodic data quality audits; and training staff on documentation practices tied to referrals and follow-up.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several important limitations. Our analysis focused exclusively on structured EHR data; however, unstructured sources such as clinical notes represent a rich repository for social needs information and are being explored in ongoing projects using natural language processing,21,48,49 with future findings expected to complement the results presented here. Variability in social needs documentation across years, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, may reflect transient shifts rather than durable practices.

Because flowsheet design and use vary across settings, interpretive validation is needed to ensure extracted information aligns with intended questions and responses. Nonetheless, when embedded in routine intake, flowsheets provide more consistent capture than coded data (ICD-10/SNOMED), which may under-record social needs when coding practices are inconsistent. A practical gold standard would employ validated screening instruments (eg, PRAPARE) at standardized touchpoints, with results mapped consistently to structured fields and codes. Future work should quantify the sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value of structured indicators against these gold-standard assessments (or manual review) to support their use in descriptive epidemiology.

Our approach to using flowsheets for social needs documentation may not be fully translatable to non-Epic CDMs.17 Reliance on Epic flowsheets highlights the need to assess whether similar structures exist in competing platforms or alternative CDMs (eg, OMOP) and what adaptations would be required to achieve comparable success.46,50,51

While the large sample increased precision (narrower CIs) and the likelihood of detecting statistical significance, effect magnitudes varied. We therefore emphasized effect sizes with CIs and clinical/operational relevance over P-value; for example, the RR of 2.09 for hospitalization indicated a clinically meaningful association. It is important to note that, “no social needs” should be interpreted as “no documented social need,” as the absence of documentation should not be taken as the absence of need. Screening and structured EHR fields capture documented presence but not confirmed absence, and documentation may be incomplete or inaccurate due to deception, omission, miscoding, and other errors. In our analysis, we could not distinguish between explicit negative documentation (eg, an entry of “no social needs”) and a complete lack of social needs documentation, so we could not reliably separate “no needs” from “uncertain needs.” Universal screening was not performed and is not feasible in these data.

Inclusion criteria requiring age, race, and address may disproportionately exclude populations with social needs—including unhoused individuals, those with fragmented care and limited data in the JHHS EHR, individuals without access to care, and patients reluctant to provide demographic information due to concerns about marginalization—potentially biasing prevalence estimates downward and skewing demographics toward patients with more complete records. Additionally, greater encounter frequency increases documentation opportunities, introducing ascertainment bias.-record social needs.

Finally, this study is limited to JHHS, and findings may not generalize to other systems with different patient populations and EHR implementations. Nonetheless, these insights provide a framework for future cross-institutional collaborations to improve the scalability and consistency of social needs data collection.

Conclusion

The findings of this study provide a comprehensive analysis of social needs documentation practices, revealing significant gaps in consistency, scope, and integration by leveraging a multi-source approach across flowsheets, ICD-10/SNOMED codes, and registries. To improve utility and scale social needs documentation, health systems should identify service lines with low documentation for targeted implementation; integrate social needs screening with referral pathways to community resources; use dashboards to monitor screening rates and follow-up; and align documentation with billing/coding practices to support sustainability. In parallel, standardized workflows, harmonized data practices (eg, OMOP), and better integration of structured and unstructured data can align documentation with care delivery and facilitate collaboration across systems. Collectively, these efforts can enhance equity and improve outcomes for diverse patient sub-populations.
