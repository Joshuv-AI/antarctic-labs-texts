Concentration–QTc Analysis of Valemetostat in Patients With Hematologic Malignancies
Authors: Masaya Tachibana, Bill Poland, Yasuyuki Kakurai, Yang Chen, Claire Li, Yvonne Lau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study assessed the potential risk of QT prolongation associated with the dual enhancer of zeste homolog 1/2 inhibitor valemetostat. An evaluation of the relationship between plasma valemetostat concentration and heart‐rate‐corrected QT (QTc) interval was performed. Time‐matched plasma concentration and 12‐lead electrocardiogram data were collected from the phase I studies DS3201‐A‐J101, in patients with relapsed/refractory B‐/T‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NCT02732275), and DS3201‐A‐U102, in patients with relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (NCT03110354). A prespecified linear mixed‐effects model was used to assess the effect of valemetostat on change in QTc corrected by the Fridericia method (ΔQTcF). A population‐specific method (ΔQTcP) was also used to remove the heart rate interval (RR) dependence. The final dataset contained 769 electrocardiogram measurements from 100 patients. Linear mixed‐effects modeling found no significant demographic or clinical covariate effects. The slope versus concentration was significant (95% confidence interval [CI] of the coefficient excluded 0) in the final models for ΔQTcF, but not ΔQTcP, while the relative standard error of the slope was > 50% for both models. Baseline QTc had a negative effect on ΔQTc in all models. At the steady‐state geometric mean maximum concentrations in the dose range of 100–700 mg tested in the DS3201‐A‐J101 and DS3201‐A‐U102 studies, the 90% CI upper bounds for model‐predicted ΔQTcF and ΔQTcP were 1.52–8.38 ms, all of which were below the clinically significant threshold of 10 ms. The analysis supports a lack of a clinically meaningful effect on the QTc interval for valemetostat.

Introduction

Valemetostat tosylate (valemetostat) is a novel, potent, orally administered selective dual inhibitor of enhancer of zeste homolog (EZH)2 and EZH1, which are overexpressed or mutated in a range of hematologic and solid cancers [1]. In clinical studies, valemetostat has demonstrated robust efficacy in patients with advanced hematologic malignancies, including relapsed or refractory (R/R) non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs). DS3201‐A‐J101 (“J101”; NCT02732275) was the first‐in‐human, phase I, open‐label, dose‐escalation and ‐expansion study that established the preliminary safety, efficacy, and recommended phase II dose (200 mg/day) of valemetostat in patients with R/R B‐ or T‐cell NHLs [2]. Based on the favorable clinical activity demonstrated in this study, valemetostat was further assessed in patients with R/R peripheral T‐cell lymphomas (PTCLs) (DS3201‐A‐U202; NCT04703192) [3] and adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) (DS3201‐A‐J201; NCT04102150) [4], leading to regulatory approval in Japan for both indications [5, 6].

Valemetostat has also been investigated for the treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). The phase I, open‐label, ascending‐dose, DS3201‐A‐U102 (“U102”; NCT03110354) study assessed the safety and efficacy of valemetostat in patients with R/R AML or ALL. In U102, valemetostat demonstrated a manageable safety profile but limited clinical efficacy, with response rates of 10% (2/20) and 17% (1/6) in patients with R/R AML and ALL, respectively [7].

The pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of valemetostat was characterized based on multiple studies. In healthy participants, the valemetostat mean maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) values increased in a dose‐proportional manner over the 50–200 mg dose range. Peak exposure was observed at 3.5–4.5 h post dose with a terminal elimination half‐life of approximately 20 h [8]. The effect of mild‐to‐moderate hepatic impairment on valemetostat exposure was not clinically meaningful; the unbound valemetostat AUC was 19% lower and 15% higher in participants with mild and moderate hepatic impairment, respectively [9]. Food reduced valemetostat Cmax by ~50%–60%, and AUC by ~30%–50% [8]. Valemetostat is a substrate of CYP3A and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp); concomitant administration of itraconazole (a strong inhibitor of CYP3A and P‐gp) or fluconazole (a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A) increased valemetostat Cmax by approximately 190% and 60%, respectively, and AUC by approximately 320% and 60% [10]. In patients with NHL, following once daily (QD) oral administration of valemetostat, the median time to Cmax (Tmax) was 2–4 h across the tested dose range of 150–300 mg. Valemetostat showed high inter‐participant variability in exposure, with a percentage coefficient of variation for total valemetostat Cmax and AUC of > 70%. Both Cmax and AUC from time 0 to tau (24 h) (AUCtau) overlapped across a dose range of 150–250 mg/day, and a dose‐dependent increase in exposure was not observed. Following QD dosing at 200 mg, the mean accumulation ratio for AUCtau was 1.19 for total valemetostat and 1.27 for unbound valemetostat [4].

Guideline E14 of the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) recommends assessing the impact of pharmaceuticals on cardiac repolarization, particularly the potential to prolong the QT interval [11]. This is highly relevant to anticancer medications that have a higher potential for adverse electrocardiographic effects, particularly QT interval prolongation, which can be associated with potentially serious arrhythmias, including torsades de pointes [12, 13].

A comprehensive evaluation of the potential effects of valemetostat on cardiac repolarization was prospectively included in the clinical development plan. Here, we present the results of a concentration–QT corrected (QTc) analysis using data from phase I studies in patients with R/R NHLs and AML/ALL that assessed escalating valemetostat doses of 100–700 mg per day.

Methods

Study Design

J101 was a phase I study of valemetostat in patients with R/R T‐ or B‐cell NHLs in Japan and the USA, consisting of a dose‐escalation and dose‐expansion part. Overall, 77 patients received 150, 200, 250, or 300 mg of valemetostat QD in continuous 28‐day treatment cycles [2]. Patients with evidence of prolongation of the QT/QTc interval before enrollment, including repeated episodes of Fridericia‐corrected QTc interval (QTcF) > 450 ms, were not eligible for participation.

U102 was a phase I study of valemetostat at doses of 100, 150, 250, 500, or 700 mg/day in 28 patients with R/R AML or ALL in the USA [7]. Exclusion criteria for U102 included QTcF prolongation at rest (mean QTcF interval > 450 ms on triplicate electrocardiogram [ECG]) or additional risk factors for torsades de pointes.

Both studies were approved by institutional review boards or central ethics committees at each participating institution and were conducted according to the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonisation consolidated Guideline E6 for Good Clinical Practice, and applicable regulatory requirements. All patients provided written informed consent prior to participating in any study procedure.

Both studies prospectively included time‐matched plasma PK and ECG assessments. In J101, 12‐lead ECGs were to be taken in triplicate (~1 min [min] apart) on Cycle (C) 1, Day (D) 1, and C1D15; predose; at 1, 2, and 8 (or 6) hours (h) postdose; and predose on C1D2, C1D8, C1D16, and C1D22, while PK samples were collected at each ECG timepoint. Similarly, U102 mandated serial triplicate 12‐lead ECGs (taken ~5 min apart) and companion PK samples collected on C1D1 and C1D8; predose; at 1, 2, 4, and 8 (or 6) h postdose; predose on C1D2, C1D15, and C1D22, and on D1 of all subsequent cycles; and at the end of treatment (EOT). Patients were kept in the resting supine position for at least 10 min prior to ECG collection. ECG outcomes were reviewed by a central laboratory (ERT). All PK blood sample collections were to be performed after the ECG collection (within 15 min following the ECG collection in U102). QTcF was calculated as QT/RR1/3, with QT in ms and RR in seconds.

Valemetostat plasma concentrations were determined using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), with a lower limit of quantification at 1 ng/mL for the J101 study and 20 ng/mL for the U102 study. The detailed analytical methods were described previously [8, 14].

Concentration–QTc Model Data Collection and Assembly

The effect of valemetostat exposure on the QTc interval was evaluated according to the ICH E14 guidance recommendation regarding the collection of ECGs at multiple timepoints. The concentration–QT model included data from both studies at the described prospective C1 timepoints, along with U102 data from C2D1 and EOT.

Key variables in the analysis dataset included triplicate‐averaged ECG data for QT, QTcF, RR, PR, and QRS intervals matching PK information (plasma concentration with collection time minus last ECG time), and candidate covariates, including tumor type (i.e., NHL or AML/ALL), age, sex, race, and baseline electrolytes (calcium, potassium, and magnesium). Derived variables included baseline QTcF and population‐specific corrected QT interval (QTcP); change from baseline in QTcF, QTcP, and RR (ΔQTcF, ΔQTcP, ΔRR) intervals; and the elapsed time from the last ECG to the PK sample.

Patients in the concentration–QTc analysis set must have had a baseline triplicate ECG predose on Day 1 (D1) and at least one post‐baseline triplicate ECG with a time‐matched PK concentration.

ECG‐evaluable participants were defined as participants in the safety analysis population who had a baseline triplicate ECG (taken at D1 predose, or the last D1 predose ECG if more than one) and at least one post‐baseline triplicate ECG with a time‐matched concentration. For these participants, triplicate sets of ECG measurements were averaged and treated as a single measurement at the time of the last measurement of the set. Unscheduled ECG assessments were excluded.

Observed valemetostat plasma concentrations were used to construct the concentration–QTc relationships. Concentrations below the lower limit of quantitation, as well as any missing concentrations at baseline (predose on C1D1), were set to zero for this analysis. The analysis did not use any pre‐baseline ECG data. For each ECG measurement, only concentrations measured for samples taken within 0–15 min after the last ECG measurement were used for the time‐matched concentration. A sensitivity analysis was carried out for the final model, where any concentration sample assigned to the same timepoint as the ECG for a participant/visit was allowed, regardless of timing. If no concentration fell within the time window for an ECG record, then that record was excluded from the sensitivity analysis.

Data Analysis

The Fridericia correction was primarily used to remove the dependence between the QT interval and HR (or its inverse, RR). If the plotted linear regression of baseline QTcF versus RR did not remove this dependence based on a visual inspection of the plot, a QTcP (QTcP = QT/RRp), in which the power (p) is estimated rather than predetermined (at 1/3), was to be used in a sensitivity analysis of the final model. The p was to be found by minimizing the absolute value of the Pearson (bivariate) correlation coefficient between baseline QTc and RR as a function of p.

These analyses employed a static exposure–response model, developed based on a prespecified linear mixed–effects model [15] using the equation: ΔQTcFi,t=θint+ηint,i+θslp+ηslp,iConci,t+θb,lQTcFi,0–QTcF0+εi,t where ΔQTcF i,t is the observed change from baseline QTcF in patient i at time t; θ int , θ slp , and θ b,l are fixed effects for the intercept, slope, and baseline QTcF, respectively; η int,i and η slp,i are random effects for the intercept and slope, where (η int,i , η slp,i ) follows a bivariate normal distribution with mean 0; Conc i,t is observed concentration; QTcF i,0 is the baseline QTcF in patient i; QTcF 0 is the mean of QTcF i,0 over all patients; and ε i,t is a normally distributed, independent residual error with mean 0.

A baseline QTcF effect by patient was included in the model, as it negatively correlates with ΔQTcF due to natural regulation or regression to the mean. Additionally, a between‐patient variability with covariance was included for the intercept and slope terms. In case of model convergence failure, additional steps were included. Covariates were tested individually as additions to the prespecified model in a sensitivity analysis using a standard stepwise forward selection and backward elimination search strategy (p < 0.01).

Model Evaluation and Applications

Model parameter estimates were tabulated with standard errors of the estimate and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (calculated with R [16] [version 4.1.2] using the intervals command). The adequacy of the final model was tested using two goodness‐of‐fit methods [15]: (1) standard diagnostic plots, including observed ΔQTc versus model‐predicted ΔQTc, standardized residuals versus model‐predicted QTc, concentration, time from first dose, and baseline QTc, as well as a standardized residual quantile–quantile plot to check for approximate normality; (2) mean observed ΔQTc with 90% CIs (i.e., the 5th to 95th percentiles) versus observed concentration in exposure bins with overlaid model‐predicted ΔQTc curves. Predictions assumed mean values of any covariates included in the model.

The final model was used to predict mean ΔQTcF at the steady‐state geometric mean Cmax over the range of administered doses in both studies (150–300 mg/day in J101 and 100–700 mg/day in U102). The 95th percentile (upper bound of the 90% CI) of mean ΔQTc was compared with a 10 ms threshold (as well as a secondary 20 ms threshold if the 10 ms threshold was exceeded) to assess the potential QTc prolongation liability [17].

Software and Approximations

Dataset processing, data exploration, and modeling were performed using R (version 4.1.2). Estimation of linear concentration–QTc modeling, model fits, the CI for the mean ΔQTc effect, and the Satterthwaite approximation of degrees of freedom in the t distribution were done using lme4, nlme, pbkrtest, and lmerTest packages of R, respectively [18, 19, 20, 21]. Covariate search employed the maximum likelihood estimation, as it is consistent with the likelihood ratio test.

Results

Study Population and Sample Disposition

The initial dataset consisted of 105 patients with a total of 1256 records, while the primary analysis dataset included 769 ECG records from 100 patients in the J101 (568 records from 72 patients) and the U102 (201 records from 28 patients) studies. Reasons for excluding records included non‐triplicate ECGs (n = 21), non‐triplicate corresponding baseline records (n = 29), missing post‐baseline concentrations (n = 17), missing PK concentrations within the allowed window of time (n = 419), and missing post‐baseline records after the previous exclusions (n = 1). Considering the large number of ECGs that were excluded due to missing PK data, a sensitivity analysis was performed that kept records when any concentration at the same timepoint could be matched but continued to exclude cases for other reasons. This sensitivity analysis excluded a total of 23 records with no PK data at the ECG timepoint, thereby leaving 1166 ECG records for analysis.

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 100 patients retained in the primary analysis dataset. The overall population was comprised of 50% males, 49% Asian patients, and 41% White patients, with a mean age of 63.8 years. In total, 56% of patients in the two studies combined received valemetostat 200 mg/day.

QT Correction and Exploratory Analysis

QT interval correction using Fridericia's method did not fully eliminate the relationship between the baseline QT interval and HR, as indicated by a persistent negative correlation between the QTcF and RR interval (Figure S1). To address this, QTcP was derived by optimizing the exponent applied to the RR interval, resulting in an estimate of 0.443, that is, QTcP = QT/RR0.443, which led to the complete removal of the slope of the regression line with an R 2 value of 0 (Figure S1).

Valemetostat concentrations, QTc, and ΔQTc by visit/timepoint are shown in Figure 1A and Table S1. They suggest a lack of hysteresis between concentration and QTc measures, which is further supported by plots of QTcF versus concentration by ordered timepoint (Figure S2). Valemetostat plasma concentration and ΔQTcF or ΔQTcP indicate a trend for a slight increase in both parameters as valemetostat plasma concentration increases (Figure 1B). These changes remained below the clinically significant ΔQTcF threshold of 10 ms, suggesting that valemetostat does not increase the QT interval at the therapeutic target dose levels. A negative correlation was observed between baseline QTcF and ΔQTc. These findings informed the subsequent modeling approach and supported the utility of baseline QTcF as a covariate in the prespecified model (Figure S3).

Modeling Results

A base model was successfully estimated with a baseline QTcF effect and between‐patient variability on the intercept and slope terms (and with their correlation). Subsequently, individual covariates, including study population (NHL vs. leukemia), race, age, sex, and baseline electrolyte levels, were tested sequentially in each model. Different residual standard deviations (SDs) for the two studies were found to significantly improve the fit and were incorporated. No other covariate was statistically significant in the stepwise forward selection and backward elimination process for QTc changes.

The final models for ΔQTcF and ΔQTcP included parameters for intercept, valemetostat concentration–ΔQTcF or ΔQTcP slope, baseline QTcF or QTcP effect on the intercept (fixed and random effect), and residual error SD with a study effect (Table 2). The final model equation for a patient's typical value was (ΔQTcF or ΔQTcP) = Intercept + Slope × Conc + Cov + ε, where Cov = covariate coefficient × (covariate—mean). The slope coefficient for valemetostat concentration was statistically significant for QTcP (95% CI boundaries were both above zero) but not for QTcF. Given that QTcP was previously identified as the more robust correction method, this finding suggests a small but measurable exposure–QTc relationship when using this correction method.

Baseline QTcF and QTcP had a significant negative effect on the intercept; with every ms increase in the baseline QTcF or QTcP, the overall mean ΔQTcF and ΔQTcP were reduced by 0.266 or 0.287 ms, respectively. Overall, the base model, final model, and timepoint‐match sensitivity analysis model (Table S2) demonstrated similar outcomes with slightly positive slopes (non‐significant for the QTcF final model), a significant and negative baseline QTc effect, and a higher residual SD (indicating larger variability) in the U102 study.

Model Evaluation and Predictions

Model adequacy was assessed using standard goodness‐of‐fit diagnostic plots (Figure S4). Plots of observed versus model‐predicted ΔQTc values showed a reasonably balanced distribution of the observations around the identity line. In contrast, plots of standardized residuals versus population predictions and valemetostat concentrations showed a more extreme distribution of the lowest and highest residual quantiles compared with the corresponding theoretical quantiles. This suggests heavier tails than those assumed in a normal distribution. However, the deviations appeared to be symmetrically distributed, without systematic bias, and were acceptable given the focus of the modeling on mean ΔQTc rather than on variability. The standardized residual appeared to increase over the last study days; however, this should be interpreted with caution due to the small dataset. Furthermore, diagnostic plots for the base model and timepoint‐match sensitivity model were similar to the final model. Taken together, model evaluations suggested that the final model adequately fit the observed data.

The model was then used to predict the mean and 90% CIs of mean ΔQTcF at mean steady‐state Cmax for all the tested valemetostat doses in both studies involving patients with NHL (J101) or ALL (U102) (Table 3). At the therapeutic steady‐state Cmax (1830 ng/mL) with a recommended dose of valemetostat (200 mg QD) for R/R ATLL and PTCL, the predicted mean ΔQTcF was 2.41 ms (90% CI, 0.92–3.91). All predictions at the given Cmax values up to supratherapeutic dose (700 mg QD) were less than the clinically acceptable value of 10 ms.

Discussion

Our current analysis assessed the impact of valemetostat on the cardiac QT interval in patients with R/R NHL, AML, or ALL using pooled data from two phase I studies. The pooled dataset included 769 time‐matched PK and ECG measurements from 100 patients who received valemetostat doses ranging from 100 to 700 mg/day. Overall, this population concentration–QTc analysis successfully characterized the relationship of QTcF and QTcP to valemetostat concentrations. The slope coefficient for valemetostat concentration was not statistically significant for QTcF, but it was for QTcP. There was a modest increase in the predicted mean ΔQTcF and ΔQTcP at the specified valemetostat steady‐state Cmax values, reaching a maximum of the upper bound of the 90% CI of 8.19 and 8.38 ms, respectively, at the highest dose of valemetostat (700 mg QD). Currently, valemetostat is approved in Japan for the treatment of R/R PTCL, including ATLL, with 200 mg QD dosing. Valemetostat is in clinical development for the treatment of other types of lymphoma and solid tumors in a similar dose range. The upper bound of the 90% CI for predicted ΔQTcF and ΔQTcP remained below the 10 ms threshold, suggesting that valemetostat does not increase the QT interval at therapeutic target dose levels. Although the ΔQTcP measure was able to correct for heart rate more completely than ΔQTcF, predictions for the two measures were similar. The estimated slope in concentration was slightly higher for ΔQTcP, but the estimated intercept was closer to zero, resulting in similar predictions as ΔQTcF. These data demonstrate that the valemetostat concentration–QTc analysis with a serial time‐matched ECG and exposure data can replace a dedicated QT study (e.g., a thorough QT study), and periodic on‐therapy ECG monitoring is sufficient in future valemetostat clinical studies, based on the ICH E14 guidance with revised Q&A (R3) [22].

The predicted upper bound of the 90% CI of ΔQTcF was 3.91 ms at steady‐state Cmax (1830 ng/mL) observed at the recommended dose of 200 mg/day for R/R PTCL, including ATLL (Table 3). As valemetostat is a substrate of CYP3A and P‐gp, drug–drug interaction with a strong CYP3A and P‐gp inhibitor, itraconazole, increased valemetostat Cmax by 190% [10]. Therefore, the current concentration–QT model was used to predict the valemetostat concentration at which the upper bound of the 90% CI for QTcF exceeds the 10 ms threshold for QTcF (6058 ng/mL) and for QTcP (5783 ng/mL); both values are higher than the expected mean Cmax when co‐administered with a strong CYP3A and P‐gp inhibitor (data on file). Furthermore, either a 75% dose reduction or avoidance of concomitant use of a strong CYP3A and P‐gp inhibitor is recommended for valemetostat in the package insert in Japan [6]. Taken together, we consider that the current concentration–QT analysis covers the highest exposure expected in clinical settings.

Preclinical safety studies were conducted in 2015, a year before the initiation of the J101 first‐in‐human study [2]. Valemetostat inhibited human ether‐à‐go‐go‐related gene (hERG) currents in human embryonic kidney cells stably expressing hERG channels by 22.7% at the highest concentration of 100 μM; therefore, the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was > 100 μM (approximately 21 times the Cmax of valemetostat on C1D15 at 200 mg/day). In a telemetered dog study, QT interval and QTc interval were significantly increased up to 7.2% and 8.3%, respectively, in female dogs after 7 days of valemetostat administration at 60 mg/kg/day, but not at 15 mg/kg/day (a human equivalent dose of approximately 500 mg). Therefore, valemetostat appeared to induce a mild effect on QTc prolongation with a no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level (NOAEL) of 15 mg/kg/day in cardiovascular safety pharmacology studies. However, no effect of valemetostat on cardiac pathology was observed in rats or dogs in 4‐week repeated dose‐toxicity studies (data on file).

Overall, the strengths of this analysis were the use of pooled data with a range of valemetostat doses and timepoints, common methods of ECG measurement with central laboratory evaluation, and a robust concentration–QTc modeling approach that included baseline QTc as a covariate. Some limitations of this analysis included the lack of a control arm and insufficient data to account for diurnal variation in the QT interval. In study J101, the dose range was relatively narrow (150–300 mg/day) with missing PK/ECG sampling at 4 h postdose, which did not support an adequate evaluation of the drug effect around the Tmax at the steady state between 2 and 4 h. However, the use of data from the U102 study, which included a wider dose range (100–700 mg/day) and PK/ECG data around the Tmax, helped to address the limitation of the study‐specific concentration–QTc analysis conducted in the J101 study. It is recommended in the ICH E14 guidelines that ECG data from placebo control are included in the concentration–QTc model to minimize the effect of diurnal variation and to exclude small effects in the QTc interval. However, placebo treatment is considered unethical in oncology patients, and therefore, was not implemented in the J101 and U102 studies. In addition, standard 12‐lead ECGs were measured at prespecified time‐points to ensure the QTc effect throughout the dosing interval, particularly around the Cmax. Valemetostat is intended for once‐daily dosing [6]. Following administration, peak plasma concentrations of unchanged valemetostat and its major metabolite, CALZ‐1809a, were observed within 6 h postdose, and both declined in a bi‐exponential manner thereafter. In addition, no hysteresis between PK and QTc is observed in the current analysis. These data suggest that the lack of ECG data between 8 and 24 h post valemetostat dose is of minimal concern.

In conclusion, this concentration–QTc analysis using data from phase I studies in patients with R/R NHL, AML, or ALL demonstrates the absence of a clinically relevant impact of valemetostat on QT prolongation.
