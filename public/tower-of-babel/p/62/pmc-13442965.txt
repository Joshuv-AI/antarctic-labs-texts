Precision Prevention in CIED Infection: Interpreting the Signal Beyond the Pooled Estimate
Authors: Hee Tae Yu
Published: 2026

The clinical landscape of cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) therapy has expanded significantly over the past decade, driven by an aging population and broadening indications for complex devices such as cardiac resynchronization therapy and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators. However, this remarkable progress is continuously shadowed by the persistent threat of CIED infections, which carry substantial morbidity, mortality, and staggering financial burdens on healthcare systems worldwide.1) In this issue of the Korean Circulation Journal, Mahajan et al.2) present a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy of antibiotic-eluting envelopes (AEEs). By pooling data from over 80,000 patients, this study offers a critical vantage point for electrophysiologists to refine their preventive strategies in an increasingly complex patient demographic. While peri-procedural systemic antibiotics remain the fundamental cornerstone of prevention according to current international guidelines, the emergence of the AEE has introduced a more proactive and localized approach to infection control.3)

The narrative of AEEs in modern electrophysiology was firmly established by the landmark Worldwide Randomized Antibiotic Envelope Infection Prevention (WRAP-IT) trial, which demonstrated a significant 40% reduction in major CIED infections.4) However, the translation of controlled trial efficacy into daily real-world effectiveness often encounters subtle nuances that merit closer inspection. Mahajan et al.2) contribute significantly to this discourse by including a wide array of study designs beyond idealized trial conditions. A pivotal finding in their meta-analysis is the nuanced distinction between data derived from prospective and retrospective sources. While prospective studies consistently showed a protective effect, the overall pooled analysis—when integrated with retrospective data—did not always reach statistical significance for all-cause infection. This apparent paradox does not necessarily refute the efficacy of AEE; rather, it reflects a profound selection bias or confounding by indication prevalent in clinical practice. In many retrospective cohorts, AEEs are strictly reserved for the highest-risk patients, potentially masking the envelope’s true preventive benefit when compared against lower-risk controls. This highlights a crucial clinical lesson: the benefit of AEE is most pronounced when applied through a systematic, risk-stratified framework rather than as a reactive measure for the most vulnerable.

The meta-analysis further underscores that the efficacy of AEE is particularly robust and clinically meaningful in high-risk populations, showing a notable reduction in infections at 12 months. For the practicing clinician, the primary challenge remains the objective and consistent assessment of patient risk. Validated tools such as the Prevention of Arrhythmia Device Infection Trial (PADIT) score provide a necessary framework to identify patients who would derive the most significant absolute benefit from AEE.5) In an era of value-based medicine and increasing scrutiny over healthcare expenditures, the universal application of AEE may be viewed as cost-prohibitive. However, dedicated economic analyses have suggested that for patients undergoing generator replacements or those with multiple comorbidities—such as end-stage renal disease or diabetes—the use of AEE is not only clinically effective but also cost-effective by preventing expensive extraction procedures and prolonged hospitalizations.6) The current data strongly support the transition of AEE from an optional adjunct to a standard of care for these specific high-risk subsets.

Beyond efficacy and economics, the sustained release of rifampicin and minocycline within the pocket raises important questions regarding antimicrobial stewardship. The global burden of antimicrobial resistance is now recognized as one of the most pressing threats to public health. Device-based antibiotic exposure has been associated with potential selection of resistant organisms, although definitive long-term clinical data remain limited.7) In regions with documented rifampicin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, concerns regarding localized antibiotic exposure may warrant careful surveillance.8) While no clear signal of increased resistant infections emerged from the WRAP-IT trial,4) clinicians must carefully balance the immediate benefit of infection prevention for the individual patient against the broader public health responsibility. Precision may ultimately define the optimal role of AEEs. This meta-analysis reaffirms that while AEEs are a powerful technological adjunct, they must be utilized alongside meticulous surgical technique and strict adherence to established systemic antibiotic protocols.3)

Rather than viewing the neutral overall result in some analyses as a refutation, it should be interpreted as a call for precision. Importantly, this call for precision should extend to research methodology. Future meta-analyses should consider stratifying outcomes according to validated risk scores, such as PADIT categories,5) rather than relying primarily on study design. Because baseline infection risk determines absolute benefit, risk-based analyses may provide a more clinically meaningful synthesis of evidence. This perspective also applies to clinical implementation. A one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to be optimal. Instead, a selective strategy informed by validated risk assessment and procedural complexity is consistent with both the clinical and economic evidence presented.2)5)6) In conclusion, the work by Mahajan et al.2) serves as a vital reminder of the evolving and complex nature of CIED infection management. It encourages a clinical shift from a historical, binary debate over universal adoption toward a more sophisticated, risk-stratified strategy. By integrating clinical efficacy with economic considerations and antimicrobial stewardship, this nuanced approach optimizes both patient outcomes and healthcare resource allocation. These findings provide a solid, evidence-based foundation for clinicians to enhance patient safety while navigating the inherent technical and biological complexities of modern cardiac electrophysiology.
