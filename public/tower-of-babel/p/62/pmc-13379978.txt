Body mass index, sedentary lifestyle, and HbA1c predict cardiac autonomic neuropathy in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Hassan Thabit Haji, Ramla Muhidin Ali, Ahmed Gharib Khamis, Ashabilan Ebrahim, Mwajuma Ramadhani Muya, Kaushik Ramaiya, Fredirick L Mashili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN) is a serious but often underdiagnosed complication among type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients that increases the risk of morbidity and mortality. Evidence on the burden of CAN and its associated factors in Zanzibar is very limited. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and predictors of CAN in patients with T2DM attending a tertiary diabetes clinic in Zanzibar.MethodsThis was a hospital-based cross-sectional study among patients with T2DM attending Mnazi Mmoja Hospital, Zanzibar. All participants were selected by systematic random sampling. CAN was measured using the CAN 504 analyzer with Ewing’s cardiovascular reflex tests. Sociodemographic, clinical, anthropometric, and biochemical data were collected using standardized procedures. A modified Poisson regression model with robust variance was used to identify factors associated with CAN.ResultsA total of 364 patients were analyzed, with a mean (SD) age of 56 (10.9) years, and the majority were female (n = 243, 66.8%). The prevalence of CAN was found in 291 (79.9%) patients. Of those with CAN, 220 (60.4%) had early CAN, 65 (17.9%) had definite CAN, and only 6 (1.6%) had severe CAN. Patients with normal body mass index (BMI) had lower risk of CAN (aPR 0.86, 95% CI 0.76–0.99, p = 0.036), while patients with sedentary lifestyle (aPR 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00, p = 0.002) and elevated HbA1c levels (aPR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, p = 0.013) had higher risk.ConclusionThis study revealed a high prevalence of CAN among adults with T2DM attending a tertiary diabetes clinic in Zanzibar. Normal BMI was associated with lower CAN prevalence, whereas greater sedentary behavior and higher HbA1c were associated with higher prevalence. These findings support earlier screening and targeted risk factor modification in high-risk patients.Keywords: Cardiac Autonomic Neuropathy, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, BMI, Predictors, Zanzibar

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and sustained hyperglycemia, leading to progressive multi-organ dysfunction [1, 2]. The global burden of T2DM continues to rise due to population ageing, urbanization, and lifestyle changes, increasing the number of individuals at risk of complications [3, 4]. In Africa, demographic and lifestyle changes are driving a rapid rise in T2DM, placing additional pressure on already strained health systems [5].

According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), 9.8% of adults (20–79 years) in Tanzania are living with diabetes, representing nearly 3 million individuals [6]. This places Tanzania among the countries with a high diabetes burden in the African region [6]. In Zanzibar specifically, a population-based study reported a diabetes prevalence of 4.4%, and only 11.9% of affected individuals had achieved glycemic control, highlighting major gaps in detection and long-term management [7]. Despite this growing burden, evidence on diabetes-related complications, particularly CAN, remains scarce in Zanzibar. The increasing burden of diabetes has important clinical and public health implications, particularly due to its progressive involvement of multiple organ systems and the development of both microvascular and macrovascular complications [5].

CAN is a serious but often underdiagnosed complication characterized by impaired cardiovascular autonomic regulation [8]. Among individuals with T2DM, the prevalence of CAN ranges from 25% to 75%, depending on diagnostic criteria and study population [8, 9]. It results from damage to the autonomic neurons, including parasympathetic and sympathetic fibers, particularly the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves, which are responsible for regulating heart rate and vascular tone [10, 11]. Clinically, CAN may present with syncope, arrhythmias, orthostatic hypotension, resting tachycardia, and exercise intolerance [12]. Although commonly linked to chronic hyperglycemia, CAN may develop early, even before a formal diabetes diagnosis [8].

Furthermore, CAN was reported to be a risk factor for increased mortality, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.55 to 2.14 after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors [13]. Additionally, CAN independently predicts the development of diabetic nephropathy and chronic kidney disease in diabetes [14, 15]. Despite the growing burden of diabetes in Zanzibar, data on CAN remain limited. Therefore, this study will provide contex-specific evidence, with implication for clinical screening and guiding future research. Understanding CAN prevalence in T2DM patients helps primary care physicians develop care plans that reduce cardiovascular risk and lessen the medical and economic burden of diabetes [16].

Previous studies assessing cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN) in type 2 diabetes mellitus have utilized methods such as heart rate variability, deep breathing, and Valsalva maneuvers, although these approaches may be limited by variability in testing conditions and operator dependence [16, 17]. In this study, we employed standardized Ewing’s cardiovascular reflex tests using an automated CAN analyzer (CAN 504) to ensure objective, reliable, and non-invasive assessment of CAN and its severity [18, 19].

This study aimed to utilize an automated device, Cardiac Autonomic Neuropathy System Analyzer (CAN 504 Kody Medical Electronics Private Limited), to determine the prevalence of cardiac autonomic neuropathy and identify predictors among adults with T2DM in Zanzibar. Early identification and management of CAN can lead to tailored therapeutic strategies, potentially mitigating the progression of cardiovascular complications and improving the overall quality of life for diabetic patients [20].

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted within the Endocrinology Department of Mnazi Mmoja Hospital (MMH) in Zanzibar, where the diabetic clinic is located and serves as the primary unit for specialized diabetes care. MMH is the main tertiary referral hospital receiving patients from all districts across Unguja and Pemba islands, while also functioning as the principal healthcare facility for Zanzibar City. The hospital manages a high patient volume, with approximately 81 inpatients and 223 outpatients per day. Data collection was conducted between December 20th, 2023, and March 20th, 2024.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were patients aged 18 years or older, permanent residents of Zanzibar, and had a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM and were on at least one prescribed anti-diabetic medication. Participants categorized as not using oral hypoglycemic agents were those receiving insulin-only therapy.

Individuals with serious comorbid conditions such as chronic kidney disease or severe liver disease were excluded because these conditions can independently affect autonomic nervous system function, which could confound the assessment of diabetes-related autonomic neuropathy. Pregnancy less than 12 weeks postpartum and currently pregnant (to avoid potential effects of pregnancy and gestational diabetes) were also excluded.

Sample size and sampling strategy

A systematic random sampling strategy was used to enroll 364 patients. The sample size was calculated using a standard formula for prevalence studies, based on the expected prevalence of CAN in the study population, a 5% margin of error, and a 5% significance level (α = 0.05). To account for potential non-response, the sample size was increased by 5%. No formal priori power calculation was performed, as the primary objective was to estimate prevalence with acceptable precision. A systematic random sampling strategy was then used to enroll the required number of participants.

Socio-demographic information

Socio-demographic and disease-related data were gathered using a standardized, interviewer-administered structured questionnaire. The instrument consisted of a standard set of predetermined questions that were given to each participant consistently under the guidance of an interviewer. Important factors like alcohol use, smoking habits, and diabetes management were recorded in the questionnaire. Self-reported treatment histories were cross-checked with clinic records to increase data accuracy and reduce recollection bias.

Physical measurements

Anthropometric and hemodynamic measurements were performed by trained research personnel. Each participant’s height, waist, and hip circumferences were taken using a non-stretchable measuring tape. Weight (kg) was measured using a weighing scale (SECA, GmbH &Co.KG, Hamburg, Germany). Body mass index (BMI) was then calculated as weight in kilograms (kg) divided by height in meters squared (m²). BMI was classified according to WHO cut-off points: underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m²) [21]. Abdominal obesity was determined using sex-specific thresholds: ≥88 cm for women and ≥102 cm for men [22]. Also, blood pressure was assessed using an automated sphygmomanometer (CH-456, CITIZEN SYSTEMS JAPAN CO., LTD) after the participant had rested in a seated position. Hypertension was defined as systolic/diastolic pressure ≥140/90 mmHg or ongoing use of antihypertensive medication [21, 23].

Physical activity assessment

Physical activity data were collected using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) and analyzed using the Kobo tool to estimate metabolic equivalent task (MET)-minutes per week. Participants were classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline, whereby those achieving <600 MET-minutes per week were categorized as physically inactive (sedentary) [24], while those achieving ≥600 MET-minutes per week were considered physically active [25].

Biochemical measurements

Blood samples were collected under aseptic conditions, appropriately labeled, and transported to Lancet Laboratories following standard handling procedures. Samples were processed and analyzed using standardized analytical workflows. High-performance liquid chromatography (Cobas Integra 400, Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) was used to measure HbA1c in accordance with the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) aligned quality standards.

Random blood glucose was used to exclude hypoglycemia prior to assessment, with levels < 3.9 mmol/L considered hypoglycemic. Serum Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) (mmol/L), Serum High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) (mmol/L), and triglyceride levels were also measured. All analyses were conducted in accordance with established laboratory standard operating procedures, including routine calibration and internal quality control measures to ensure accuracy and reproducibility [26].

Biochemical parameters were classified using established clinical reference standards. HbA1c was categorized as normal <6.5% or abnormal ≥ 6.5%, recommended by the American Diabetes Association [27]. Lipid abnormalities were defined as LDL cholesterol ≥3.0 mmol/L, HDL cholesterol <1.0 mmol/L in men and <1.3 mmol/L in women, and triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, in accordance with established international guidelines [28].

Data quality assurance

Data quality was ensured through multiple procedures. The questionnaire was adapted from previously validated instruments and pretested in a pilot sample to assess clarity, contextual relevance, and internal consistency. All data collectors received standardized training to ensure uniform understanding and administration of the tool.

Participants were provided with detailed information about the study prior to enrolment, and written informed consent was obtained. Routine checks were performed during data collection to ensure completeness and consistency. In addition, data collection was closely supervised by the principal investigator (HTH) and co-investigators to ensure adherence to study protocols and maintain data integrity.

Measurement of CAN

Six (6) standard Ewing non-invasive tests that involve multi-parameter electrocardiography (ECG) were used to assess cardiac autonomic functioning. Before the procedure, patients were instructed to fast for 12 h and refrain from using antidepressants, neuroleptics, caffeine, nicotine, antihistamines, alcohol, or smoking. The tests were carried out in a quiet room with a constant temperature of 22–24 °C. Each test was followed by a two-minute rest period to allow heart rate stabilization.

The measurement of CAN was conducted through standard cardiovascular reflex tests outlined by Ewing Clarke [29]. A resting 3-lead ECG was taken for all participants using the Cardiac Autonomic Neuropathy System Analyzer (CAN 504, Kody Medical Electronics Private Limited), which was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before data collection.

These procedures minimized inter- and intra-observer variability and ensured reliable assessment of both the sympathetic and parasympathetic components of the autonomic nervous system. Both the sympathetic and parasympathetic autonomic nervous systems were assessed through a battery of six tests.

Measurements were taken over 5 min while patients were in a supine position following 10 min of rest and stabilization of heart rate. CAN as outcome of interest was defined by the following: Resting heart rate, Deep breathing, Standing, Valsalva maneuver, Postural hypotension, and sustaining handgrip. It was then categorized based on severity by summing up the points from each test.

CAN was considered absent if the total points were 0, early CAN for a score of 0.5–1.5 points, definite CAN if the score was 2–3 points, and severe CAN if ≥ 3.5 points [19, 30]. The CAN was evaluated according to standardized protocols described previously [19].

Resting heart rate

Resting heart rate

Participants were asked to lie supine quietly. After about 5 min of continuous monitoring, the resting heart rate and electrocardiogram (ECG) parameters were recorded. This step provided a baseline measurement for further comparisons.

2.Deep Breathing

Deep Breathing

Participants were asked to lie supine and perform deep breathing exercises. They were instructed to breathe deeply and regularly, following a pattern of six breaths per minute (bpm). The heart rate response to deep breathing was measured for one minute. This test assesses parasympathetic function by calculating the difference between the maximum and minimum heart rate during the breathing cycle.

3.Lying to Standing

Lying to Standing

Participants were asked to lie supine quietly. After about 5 min of continuous monitoring, the participant was instructed to stand. The postural index (PI) was calculated by measuring the heart rate response to the change of position from recumbent to standing over 180 s after getting up. This was done using the ‘30–15 ratio,’ defined as the longest RR interval of beats 20–40 divided by the shortest RR interval of beats 5–25, starting from the first beat during the process of standing up. A postural index of < 1.00 was considered abnormal.

4.Valsalva Maneuver

Valsalva Maneuver

Participants were asked to perform the Valsalva maneuver by exhaling forcefully into a mouthpiece connected to a manometer, maintaining a pressure of 40 mmHg for 15 s. The heart rate response was recorded before, during, and after the maneuver. The Valsalva ratio was calculated by dividing the longest RR interval after the maneuver by the shortest RR interval during the maneuver. This test evaluates both sympathetic and parasympathetic functions.

5.Postural Hypotension

Postural Hypotension

Participants were asked to lie supine quietly for 5 min, and then to stand up quickly. Blood pressure was measured at baseline (supine) and intervals after standing (1, 3, and 5 min). The drop in systolic and diastolic blood pressure was recorded to assess orthostatic hypotension, defined as a decrease in systolic blood pressure of 20 mmHg or more, or in diastolic blood pressure of 10 mmHg or more, upon standing.

6.Sustained Handgrip

Sustained Handgrip

Participants were asked to maintain a sustained handgrip at 30% of their maximum voluntary contraction for 3 min. Blood pressure was measured before and during the handgrip exercise. The increase in diastolic blood pressure during the handgrip test was recorded. This test evaluates sympathetic function by measuring the autonomic response to sustained muscle contraction (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA version 18. To summarize participant characteristics, descriptive statistics were used and displayed in tables. Additionally, multiple-response analysis was conducted, with visual representations created using Excel. Robust estimators were applied in both univariable and multivariable Modified Poisson Regression models [32] to examine factors associated with CAN. In the multivariable models, all variables included in the univariable analyses were retained based on a priori considerations, guided by existing literature and biological relevance. Retaining all variables allowed proper adjustment for potential confounding and ensured that the associations between the main exposures and outcomes were appropriately estimated.

Human ethics and consent to participate

The Declaration of Helsinki, the Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice (GCP), the International Council for Harmonization (ICH), and, where applicable, the principles of Good Clinical and Laboratory Practices (GCLP) were among the pertinent national and international ethical guidelines and regulations that were closely followed throughout the course of the study. Ethical approval was given by the Zanzibar Health Research Ethics Committee (ZAHREC), reference number ZAHREC/05/ST/DEC/2023/184, and the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Institution Review Board (reference number MUHAS-REC-02-2023-1528). Each subject was given a consent form to fill out before enrollment. The research team guaranteed participants’ confidentiality by deleting all personally identifying information from the dataset and assigning unique identification codes at the time of data collection.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN) among adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in Zanzibar. In general, a high prevalence of CAN was observed in patients attending the MMH diabetic clinic, with 79.9% of participants diagnosed with some degree of autonomic dysfunction. Our analysis identified body mass index (BMI), glycated hemoglobin (B-HbA1c), and sedentary lifestyle as significant factors associated with the presence and severity of CAN.

Compared with other African countries, the observed prevalence is higher than that reported in some African settings, such as South-East Nigeria, 44.3% [33] and Uganda 52.2% [31], but lower than the 89% reported in Sudan [34]. Similar variability has been reported globally, likely reflecting differences in study populations, diagnostic criteria, and healthcare settings. The high prevalence in our study may be partly explained by the tertiary care setting and the use of sensitive diagnostic methods, which may detect early or subclinical cases.

Recent studies using alternative screening tools, such as SUDOSCAN, have also demonstrated a high burden of autonomic dysfunction and its association with other complications, such as diabetic nephropathy, supporting the clinical relevance of early CAN detection [35]. Emerging screening approaches, including the ProCVT smart-sheet, further highlight the growing emphasis on accessible and early identification of autonomic dysfunction [36].

While previous studies have reported an association between age and the prevalence of CAN [31], our study did not find a significant relationship between age and CAN. Nonetheless, a high prevalence of CAN was evident among participants aged 40–60 years, suggesting an earlier onset of autonomic dysfunction [31]. This may reflect delayed diagnosis, limited access to care, and suboptimal glycemic control, which are common in low- and middle-income settings [37, 38].

Participants with normal BMI had a lower prevalence of CAN compared with overweight and obese individuals. Increased adiposity promotes inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which contribute to autonomic nerve damage [39].

This is supported by studies linking visceral and ectopic fat to impaired autonomic function. For example, Jang et al. [40] reported that increased visceral fat was associated with impaired cardiovascular reflexes in T2DM, while Ouyang et al. [41] demonstrated that epicardial fat partially mediated reductions in heart rate variability. However, Risi et al. [42] reported that ectopic fat was associated with CAN only in autoimmune diabetes, suggesting potential differences in fat distribution and metabolic mechanisms.

Interventional evidence further suggests that improving metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance and body weight, may enhance autonomic regulation [43]. These findings indicate that although normal BMI is associated with lower risk, ectopic fat may still contribute to autonomic dysfunction independently of BMI. In this study, waist and hip circumferences were measured but not included in the final analysis; therefore, the role of central adiposity could not be fully evaluated.

Greater sedentary time was associated with a higher prevalence of CAN in this study. Physical inactivity is known to impair autonomic balance by increasing sympathetic activity and reducing parasympathetic tone [44]. Evidence from systematic reviews shows that aerobic exercise improves heart rate variability and autonomic function in patients with T2DM [45]. These results support recommendations promoting regular physical activity, minimizing sedentary time, and interrupting sitting with regular movement [46].

Elevated HbA1c levels were associated with a higher prevalence of CAN. This aligns with earlier landmark trials such as the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) and the EURODIAB Prospective Complications Study, which identified chronic hyperglycemia as a major driver of diabetic neuropathies [47]. In addition to mean HbA1c, several studies have demonstrated that greater HbA1c variability independently predicts increased CAN severity, underscoring the detrimental impact of fluctuating glucose levels over the course of diabetes [48]. Furthermore, a study from Nishi Chaturvedi explained that beyond mean HbA1c, glycemic variability and “metabolic memory” can perpetuate autonomic damage even after HbA1c normalization [49].

Persistent hyperglycemia, rather than intermittent hypoglycemia, emerges as the principal contributor to CAN in diabetes patients [50]. Collectively, these findings suggest that even moderate mean HbA1c combined with high variability may accelerate CAN development. These observations reinforce that stable and sustained glycemic control is essential for preventing autonomic dysfunction, rather than focusing on short-term HbA1c targets. Other modifiable factors, such as adiposity and physical inactivity, may further increase the risk of diabetic autonomic neuropathy.

Importantly, evidence from interventional studies suggests that autonomic dysfunction may be partially modifiable. The HypoCOMPaSS sub-study demonstrated improved hypoglycemia awareness regardless of CAN status [51]. Recent evidence suggests that cardiac autonomic dysfunction may begin before overt diabetes. A cross-sectional study showed that individuals with elevated 1-hour post-load glucose had impaired autonomic function [52].

Beyond glucose exposure alone, autonomic nerve damage may continue to occur progressively in patients with T2DM, even in the presence of apparently good glycemic management [53]. This process frequently begins with parasympathetic impairment before sympathetic dysfunction becomes clinically manifest. Early parasympathetic damage often shows as a higher resting heart rate and lower exercise tolerance, reflecting altered vagal control of cardiac function [54].

These findings have important clinical implications for the management of patients with T2DM. The high prevalence of CAN and the identified risk factors emphasize the need for routine CAN screening, especially for patients with an overweight, sedentary lifestyle and abnormal HbA1c. Primary care physicians should implement comprehensive management plans that include regular monitoring of autonomic function, rigorous glycemic control, lifestyle modifications, and targeted interventions to address autonomic dysfunction.

The use of sensitive diagnostic methods, such as Ewing’s cardiovascular reflex tests, may facilitate the detection of early or subclinical cases of CAN [19]. Although these tests are considered a standard approach for assessment, they are subject to inter-test and operator-dependent variability. In this study, variability was minimized through standardized protocols, controlled testing conditions, and trained clinical personnel; however, some degree of variability cannot be entirely excluded.

The high prevalence of cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN) observed should be interpreted within the context of the tertiary care setting, which likely included patients with longer diabetes duration and a higher burden of risk factors. Despite the exclusion of major comorbid conditions such as chronic kidney and liver disease to reduce confounding, the study population may still represent individuals with poorly controlled diabetes.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Physical activity was self-reported and may be subject to recall bias and reduced reliability. Although participants with known cardiovascular comorbidities were excluded, unmeasured factors such as medication use and hypertension severity may have influenced the observed associations. The absence of a non-diabetic control group further limits causal attribution and comparative interpretation.

Despite these limitations, the use of a semi-automated device strengthens measurement accuracy. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and validate these findings in broader populations, while the substantial burden of CAN observed underscores the need for routine, non-invasive autonomic function screening at secondary and tertiary levels of care.

Conclusion

The study underscores the high prevalence of CAN among T2DM patients in a tertiary health care setting in Zanzibar and identifies key factors associated with its presence and severity. These findings highlight the importance of routine screening and lifestyle interventions for early identification of high-risk individuals, although further studies are needed to explore causal relationships.
