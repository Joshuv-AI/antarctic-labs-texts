Emerging role of herbal nanogel formulations in skin cancer therapy: mechanistic insights and translational advances in nanomedicine
Authors: Suraj Kumar, Rishabha Malviya, Phool Chandra, Sathvik Belagodu Sridhar, Javedh Shareef, Tarun Wadhwa, Daniel Arockiam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSkin cancer remains a major global health concern, necessitating the development of innovative therapeutic strategies that enhance treatment efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. This review aims to explore the emerging role of herbal nanogels as a promising nanotechnology-based drug delivery system for skin cancer therapy.MethodsA comprehensive review of recent literature was conducted focusing on nanogel-based drug delivery systems, herbal bioactive compounds, and stimulus-responsive nanotechnology approaches for cancer treatment, particularly skin cancer.ResultsNanogels, defined as crosslinked nanoscale polymeric networks, have gained significant attention due to their high drug-loading capacity, tunable physicochemical properties, and ability to respond to various internal and external stimuli. They can be synthesized using techniques such as emulsion methods, micro-molding, and photolithography, allowing precise control over size and morphology. Advanced stimulus-responsive nanogels, including tumor microenvironment-sensitive and ligand-targeted systems (e.g., LHRH-targeted nanogels), enable controlled and site-specific drug release. Recent developments in herbal nanogels integrate plant-derived bioactive compounds within nanogel matrices, enhancing solubility, stability, and therapeutic efficiency against skin cancer.DiscussionHerbal nanogels represent a promising interdisciplinary approach combining nanotechnology and phytomedicine, offering targeted, less invasive, and potentially more effective treatment options for skin cancer. Despite significant progress, challenges such as clinical translation, large-scale production, and long-term safety evaluation remain. Continued research in smart and multifunctional nanogel systems may significantly advance future skin cancer therapies.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Healthcare professionals and researchers are exploring possibilities for innovative treatment techniques that might improve effectiveness while reducing undesirable side effects as a result of the worldwide growth in the prevalence of skin cancer, which offers a rising burden to the healthcare sector. In the past few decades, the convergence of nanotechnology and medicine has generated considerable interest because of its potential to provide novel solutions to longstanding therapeutic challenges [1]. Among these advances, herbal nanogel formulations have emerged as promising delivery systems for skin cancer therapy by integrating naturally derived bioactive compounds with nanotechnology-based carriers. Herbal constituents possessing antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiproliferative, and pro-apoptotic properties have attracted increasing attention for their potential to inhibit tumor progression while minimizing systemic toxicity [1]. Skin cancer is a group of malignancies arising from different cell types of the skin and includes melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers [2]. Excision of the tumour remains the primary treatment approach; however, because of its invasive nature and the risk of deformity, alternative therapeutic strategies are needed [3]. Systemic chemotherapy remains effective but often leads to debilitating side effects owing to non-specific toxicity toward healthy tissues [3]. Radiation therapy is another widely used option, although its effectiveness is constrained by the risk of injury to surrounding healthy tissues. Consequently, research on nanotechnology-based approaches has accelerated, offering new opportunities for improving the management of skin cancer [4]. Particular attention has been directed toward herbal therapeutics such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, epigallocatechin gallate, berberine, and aloe vera-derived phytoconstituents because of their demonstrated anticancer potential. Despite their therapeutic promise, many herbal compounds exhibit poor aqueous solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid degradation, which limit their clinical utility [5]. Encapsulation of these phytochemicals into nanogel systems represents an attractive strategy to overcome these limitations and enhance localized delivery to skin tumors [4]. Nanogels, which are nanosized cross-linked three-dimensional polymeric networks, are capable of encapsulating a wide variety of therapeutic agents and delivering them to the target site with high precision [4]. In herbal nanogel formulations, these networks serve as carriers for plant-derived bioactive molecules, providing sustained release, enhanced penetration through the skin barrier, improved stability, and reduced off-target toxicity. Such characteristics make herbal nanogels particularly suitable for topical and localized treatment of skin cancers [6]. Nanogels are responsive to various stimuli, including pH, temperature, and enzyme activity, thereby enabling controlled release of encapsulated agents. They are further classified into physically cross-linked gels, expandable liposomes containing nanogels, micelle-like nanogels, hybrid polymeric nanogels, and chemically cross-linked nanogels [6]. Various fabrication approaches, including photolithography, micro-molding, polymer preparation, emulsion-based techniques, and free-radical polymerization, are employed to tailor nanogel properties and optimize drug delivery performance [7]. Understanding these processes is essential for designing effective nanogel systems [8]. Recent developments have focused on the fabrication of herbal stimulus-responsive nanogels capable of releasing phytoconstituents selectively within the acidic and enzyme-rich tumor microenvironment. Such systems not only enhance therapeutic efficacy but also reduce undesirable systemic effects associated with conventional chemotherapy [9]. Furthermore, the incorporation of herbal molecules into pH-sensitive and enzyme-responsive nanogels has opened new avenues for achieving controlled and targeted treatment of skin malignancies [10]. Another important aspect of skin cancer treatment is the combination of herbal medicine with nanogel technology. The integration of traditional botanical knowledge with advances in nanotechnology has led to the development of multifunctional herbal nanogels capable of improving the therapeutic performance of phytochemicals. These systems have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing drug stability, prolonging retention at the tumor site, and increasing the effectiveness of natural anticancer agents [11]. Examples include curcumin-loaded nanogels, resveratrol-based nanogels, and quercetin-containing nanogel formulations designed for topical management of melanoma and other skin cancers [10]. Nanogels have emerged as promising carriers for localized drug delivery in cancer therapy owing to their ability to provide sustained release, enhance drug retention at the target site, and potentially reduce systemic exposure compared with conventional chemotherapy [12]. These properties have demonstrated encouraging therapeutic outcomes in several preclinical studies. Conventional topical dressings and conventional wound-based treatment approaches used in skin cancer management exhibit several limitations that restrict their effectiveness [13]. Traditional dressings primarily function as passive barriers and lack the ability to actively deliver therapeutic agents or respond to the tumor microenvironment. They often fail to provide controlled or sustained drug release, leading to suboptimal drug concentration at the target site [14]. In addition, poor penetration of active compounds through the stratum corneum further reduces therapeutic efficiency. Frequent dressing changes may also cause patient discomfort, increase the risk of infection, and delay wound healing. Moreover, conventional materials do not possess stimulus-responsive or targeted delivery capabilities, which limits their effectiveness in heterogeneous tumor tissues [15]. In many cases, they are unable to prevent tumor recurrence due to insufficient local drug retention and inadequate eradication of residual cancer cells. However, the purported advantages of localized nanogel delivery should be interpreted with caution, as their clinical benefits remain largely unvalidated. Concerns have been raised regarding formulation instability, batch-to-batch variability, limited penetration into heterogeneous tumor tissues, and the possibility that incomplete eradication of primary tumors may contribute to disease recurrence or facilitate metastatic progression under certain circumstances [16]. Furthermore, issues related to immunogenicity, long-term toxicity, and reproducibility continue to pose significant challenges to their successful translation into clinical practice. In contrast, systemic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for advanced and metastatic cancers because of its ability to target disseminated tumor cells throughout the body [17]. Nevertheless, its therapeutic effectiveness is often constrained by dose-limiting toxicities and non-specific drug distribution. Consequently, recent research has increasingly focused on developing integrated therapeutic strategies that combine the localized and controlled delivery capabilities of nanogel-based systems with the broader anticancer activity of systemic therapies. Such combination approaches may offer improved therapeutic outcomes and reduced off-target effects; however, their long-term safety, pharmacokinetic behavior, and clinical efficacy require further investigation through rigorous preclinical and clinical studies [18]. These strategies include stimulus-responsive nanogels and the integration of localized therapy with immunotherapeutic approaches to elicit broader antitumor responses [13]. Given that extensive studies are still required to establish their safety and efficacy, these approaches remain highly attractive because of their potential to minimize systemic toxicity while maintaining therapeutic effectiveness [19]. The convergence of nanotechnology and herbal medicine has ushered in a new paradigm in skin cancer therapy. Herbal nanogels provide a versatile platform for the efficient delivery of phytochemicals, enabling enhanced bioavailability, targeted release, and improved therapeutic outcomes. Continued advancements in formulation strategies and a deeper understanding of the interactions between natural compounds and nanogel matrices are expected to accelerate the clinical translation of herbal nanogel systems [20]. Therefore, focuses specifically on recent advances in herbal nanogel formulations and highlights their emerging role in developing safe, effective, and minimally invasive therapeutic approaches for skin cancer management. Herbal Nanogel-Mediated Skin Cancer Therapy: Mechanistic Basis and Clinical Translation Prospects

Type of responsiveness

Nanogels can be categorized according to their ability to respond to external or internal stimuli, which is an important feature for achieving controlled and site-specific drug delivery. In herbal nanogel formulations, stimulus responsiveness plays a crucial role in regulating the release of phytoconstituents at the tumor site, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic toxicity.

Non-responsive nanogels

Non-responsive nanogels undergo passive swelling in the presence of water and other suitable solvents. Their behaviour is primarily governed by diffusion and absorption mechanisms and they do not respond to external stimuli [24]. These systems provide predictable and sustained drug release profiles, making them suitable for applications requiring prolonged therapeutic action [25]. However, the lack of responsiveness limits their utility in targeted therapies, particularly in complex pathological conditions such as cancer. Non-responsive nanogels have been investigated as carriers for plant-derived bioactive compounds to achieve sustained topical delivery. Such systems may improve the stability and retention of phytochemicals on the skin surface; however, their inability to respond to tumor-specific conditions restricts their effectiveness compared with smart nanogel systems.

Stimuli-responsive nanogels

Stimuli-responsive nanogels undergo reversible physicochemical changes, such as swelling or shrinkage, in response to environmental triggers including pH, temperature, ionic strength, magnetic fields, electromagnetic radiation, and enzymatic activity [26]. These smart nanogels possess enormous potential for cancer therapy because they can release therapeutic agents selectively at the tumor site in response to cancer-associated conditions such as acidic pH and elevated temperature [27]. Multifunctional polymeric nanogels capable of responding to multiple stimuli further enhance targeting accuracy and therapeutic efficacy [28]. Their applications include controlled drug delivery, imaging, and theragnostic systems. In herbal nanogel formulations, stimulus-responsive systems are particularly advantageous for improving the delivery of phytochemicals such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and other natural anticancer agents. The acidic and enzyme-rich tumor microenvironment of skin cancer can trigger the controlled release of encapsulated herbal constituents, thereby increase their local concentration and therapeutic effectiveness while reduce off-target toxicity [29]. Consequently, pH-responsive and enzyme-sensitive herbal nanogels have emerged as promising platforms for melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer therapy.

Type of linkage

Nanogels are also classified according to the nature of the cross-linking interactions that form the three-dimensional polymeric network. These interactions influence the mechanical properties, stability, swelling behaviour, and drug-release characteristics of nanogels. Herbal nanogel formulations, the type of linkage significantly affects the encapsulation efficiency, stability, and controlled release of phytoconstituents intended for skin cancer therapy.

Physically cross-linked nanogels

Physically cross-linked nanogels, commonly referred to as pseudogels, are formed through weak non-covalent interactions such as hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic attractions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces [30]. Their preparation is relatively simple and does not require chemical cross-linking agents [31], making them environmentally friendly and less toxic. Their properties can be modulated by factors including polymer concentration, pH, temperature, and ionic strength [32]. These nanogels are commonly formed by self-assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers or by electrostatic complexation between oppositely charged polymers [33]. However, compared with chemically cross-linked systems, they generally exhibit lower mechanical strength and stability [34]. Physically cross-linked herbal nanogels have attracted considerable interest owing to their biocompatibility and mild fabrication conditions, which preserve the biological activity of sensitive phytochemicals. Natural polymers such as chitosan, alginate, gelatin, and hyaluronic acid have been extensively explored for encapsulating herbal compounds with anticancer potential [35]. These systems provide sustained release and improved skin permeation, making them attractive for topical skin cancer therapy.

Liposome-modified nanogels

Liposome-modified nanogels have demonstrated considerable promise as drug carriers for the treatment of various diseases. The release of liposomes embedded within the nanogel matrix is regulated by several factors, including pH, temperature, and ionic charge. For example, succinylated poly(glycerol)-containing liposomes have been shown to enhance cytoplasmic delivery of calcein at pH 5.5 [36]. Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)-based liposome-modified nanogels also exhibit thermoresponsive drug-release behaviour following subcutaneous administration [37]. The incorporation of herbal compounds into liposome-modified nanogels offers additional advantages by combining the high loading capacity of nanogels with the membrane-mimicking characteristics of liposomes [38]. Such hybrid systems can improve the solubility, stability, and cellular uptake of hydrophobic phytoconstituents, thereby enhancing their therapeutic potential against skin cancer [38].

Micellar nanogels

Micellar nanogels are formed through the self-assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers or graft copolymers in aqueous media. These nanogels exhibit a characteristic core-shell structure in which the hydrophobic core is surrounded by a hydrophilic polymer shell [39]. The hydrophilic component facilitates hydrogen bonding and contributes to the stability of the micellar structure. Core-shell micellar nanogels possess excellent stability and responsiveness to external stimuli [40]. Their large surface area enables efficient loading of therapeutic agents through physical entrapment, while the hydrophobic core protects encapsulated compounds from enzymatic degradation and hydrolysis [41]. Researchers are investigating Y-shaped nanoparticles composed of biodegradable and biocompatible polymers such as PLGA, chitosan, and oleic acid-isopropylacrylamide copolymers for efficient drug delivery (Fig. 3) [42]. Micellar herbal nanogels are particularly suitable for the encapsulation of poorly water-soluble phytochemicals commonly used in skin cancer therapy. Bioactive compounds such as curcumin, resveratrol, quercetin, and berberine can be effectively incorporated within the hydrophobic core, resulting in enhanced stability, improved bioavailability, and prolonged release profiles [43]. These characteristics contribute to increased accumulation of herbal agents at tumor sites and represent a promising strategy for the treatment of melanoma and other skin malignancies.

Nanogel hybridization

Hybrid nanogels are fabricated by incorporating nanoparticles into organic or inorganic polymeric matrices, thereby integrating the complementary properties of multiple materials within a single multifunctional platform. These hybrid systems exhibit high sensitivity to environmental cues and can respond to a variety of physiological stimuli. Hybrid nanogels formed through the self-assembly of polymers in aqueous media have demonstrated remarkable efficiency in the rapid and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents, as illustrated in Fig. 4 [44]. Various polymers, including polysaccharides, pullulan-PNIPAM, and related materials, have been employed in the fabrication of hybrid nanogels [45]. Among these systems, cholesterol-pullulan hybrid nanogels have been extensively investigated and have shown significant potential as drug delivery vehicles for genetic materials, RNA, proteins, peptides, oligosaccharides, and other bioactive compounds [46]. Furthermore, hybrid nanogels have demonstrated the capability for site-specific and stimuli-responsive delivery of insulin and anticancer agents. Numerous studies have reported that cholesterol-pullulan hybrid nanogels can effectively encapsulate and transport genetic materials, RNA, peptides, fatty acids, and other therapeutic molecules. These nanogel systems have also been explored for the localized and controlled delivery of anticancer drugs, highlighting their potential in precision medicine and targeted therapy [47] Hybrid nanogels represent highly versatile carriers for the encapsulation and delivery of phytoconstituents used in skin cancer therapy. The combination of polymeric matrices with inorganic nanoparticles or lipid-based components enhances the stability, loading capacity, and controlled release characteristics of natural bioactive compounds [48]. Herbal molecules such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, epigallocatechin gallate, and berberine can be efficiently incorporated into hybrid nanogel systems to overcome limitations associated with poor solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid degradation [49]. In addition, hybrid herbal nanogels facilitate enhanced skin penetration and prolonged retention of phytochemicals at the tumor site, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity [50]. The integration of natural polymers, such as chitosan and polysaccharides, with synthetic polymers or inorganic nanomaterials further provides multifunctional properties, including stimulus-responsive release and improved biocompatibility [51]. Consequently, hybrid herbal nanogels have emerged as promising platforms for the localized treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers and represent an important area of ongoing research in nanomedicine [51].

Chemically coupled nanogels

Chemically cross-linked nanogels are synthesized through covalent bonding between polymer chains. The morphology and properties of these gels depend on the nature of the functional groups present in the polymeric matrix and the strength of the chemical interactions involved (Fig. 5) [52]. This approach has proven effective for the fabrication of a wide range of nanogel systems. The incorporation of multifunctional cross-linking agents facilitates the formation of hydrophilic and amphiphilic copolymeric networks when suitable vinyl monomers are employed [53]. The physicochemical characteristics and stability of nanogel formulations are largely dependent on the type of cross-linkers used [54]. Furthermore, the dimensions, architecture, and morphological properties of nanogels are significantly influenced by the cross-linking mediators. Sustainable PLGA-PEG diblock nanogels containing expandable pendant thiol groups have been successfully prepared using disulfide cross-linking strategies [55]. These nanogels were designed with particle sizes ranging from 20 to 200 nm. In addition, environmentally friendly polymerization approaches based on green chemistry principles have been employed during their synthesis [56]. Chemically cross-linked nanogels offer superior structural integrity, controlled drug release, and enhanced stability compared with physically cross-linked systems. These characteristics are particularly advantageous for the encapsulation of plant-derived bioactive compounds used in skin cancer therapy. Phytochemicals such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and other naturally occurring anticancer agents often suffer from poor aqueous solubility and rapid degradation, which limit their therapeutic efficacy [57]. Chemically coupled nanogels provide a stable three-dimensional network capable of protecting these phytoconstituents and ensuring their sustained and site-specific release [58]. Moreover, the use of biodegradable polymers such as PLGA, PEG, chitosan, and hyaluronic acid enable the development of biocompatible herbal nanogel systems with improved skin penetration and prolonged residence time at the tumor site [59]. Stimuli-responsive chemically cross-linked nanogels can further exploit the acidic and enzyme-rich microenvironment of skin tumors to achieve selective release of encapsulated phytochemicals. Such properties contribute to enhanced anticancer activity, reduced systemic toxicity, and improved patient compliance [60]. Consequently, chemically coupled herbal nanogels have emerged as promising platforms for the localized treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers and represent an important area of investigation in contemporary nanomedicine [39].

The manufacture of nanogels

The fabrication process plays a critical role in determining the physicochemical characteristics, drug-loading capacity, stability, and release behaviour of nanogel formulations. In herbal nanogel-based skin cancer therapy, the selection of an appropriate manufacturing technique is particularly important for preserving the biological activity of phytoconstituents and ensuring efficient delivery to tumor tissues. Various fabrication approaches have been employed to produce herbal nanogels with improved therapeutic performance and controlled drug-release properties.

Photolithography method

Photolithography is one of the techniques employed for the fabrication of three-dimensional nanogel structures. Photolithographically prepared polymeric molds possess a large surface area, enabling the incorporation and retention of substantial amounts of therapeutic agents. The photolithographic process consists of five major steps.

A UV-cross-linkable polymer, acting as a substrate with a high surface area, is immersed in water for a prolonged period and coated with a previously dried photosensitive material [40]. Photolithography offers several advantages over conventional printing techniques because it enables the fabrication of particles with well-defined size and geometry without requiring template wetting [44]. The Particle Replication in Non-Wetting Templates (PRINT) technology makes it possible to fabricate nanogel systems with controlled dimensions, morphology, and stability. This technique has demonstrated considerable potential for the precise delivery of therapeutic molecules, including drugs, fatty acids, genetic material, proteins, and RNA [45]. Photolithographic approaches provide an efficient platform for producing nanogels with uniform particle size, high loading efficiency, and reproducible release profiles. These characteristics are particularly beneficial for encapsulating plant-derived bioactive compounds used in skin cancer therapy [61]. Phytochemicals such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, and epigallocatechin gallate are susceptible to degradation and exhibit poor aqueous solubility. Therefore, the ability of photolithographic techniques to generate structurally uniform nanogels contributes to enhanced stability, improved bioavailability, and controlled release of these herbal constituents [61]. Furthermore, the precise control over particle architecture offered by PRINT technology facilitates targeted and localized delivery of phytochemicals to melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer tissues. Such herbal nanogel systems can enhance skin penetration, prolong residence time at the tumor site, and minimize systemic toxicity [61]. Consequently, photolithography-based fabrication methods represent promising strategies for the development of advanced herbal nanogel formulations aimed at improving the therapeutic outcomes of skin cancer treatment [45].

Micro-molding process

Although photolithography and micro-molding share certain similarities, micro-molding offers advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness and ease of fabrication [46]. A variety of particle geometries, including hexagonal prisms, discs, and string-like structures, can be generated using micro-molding techniques. During the fabrication process, suspended cells are dispersed within aqueous precursor solutions containing photo-initiators. These precursor systems commonly include PEGDA (polyethylene glycol diacrylate), methaacrylated hyaluronic acid (MeHA), and related compounds [47]. Subsequently, ultraviolet light-induced photocrosslinking is employed to form hydrogel structures within plasma-treated hydrophilic PDMS molds. The resulting microgel particles containing encapsulated cellular components are then isolated, washed, and collected [48]. Micro-molding represents a versatile approach for producing particles with precisely controlled shapes, sizes, and drug-loading characteristics. Such control is particularly important for preserving the stability and biological activity of plant-derived bioactive compounds used in skin cancer therapy. The technique allows the fabrication of uniform nanogel structures capable of efficiently encapsulating phytochemicals, thereby improving their therapeutic performance [49]. Furthermore, micro-molding-based herbal nanogels can enhance the solubility, stability, and sustained release of natural anticancer agents such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, and epigallocatechin gallate [50]. The ability to tailor particle morphology and size distribution facilitates improved skin penetration and prolonged retention of phytoconstituents at tumor sites. These features are particularly advantageous for the localized treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers, where sustained exposure to herbal therapeutics can enhance antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity [51]. In addition, the biocompatibility of polymers such as PEGDA and methaacrylated hyaluronic acid makes micro-molding an attractive strategy for developing multifunctional herbal nanogels with potential applications in targeted drug delivery and skin cancer therapy [48]. Consequently, micro-molding techniques provide an effective platform for the fabrication of advanced herbal nanogel systems with enhanced therapeutic potential [48].

Method for synthesizing bi-polymers

Natural biopolymers derived from carbohydrates play an important role in the fabrication of nanogel systems. Commonly employed biopolymers include chitosan, albumin, hyaluronic acid, and dextran. Owing to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low toxicity, these materials have been extensively utilized for the preparation of nanogels, microgels, and hydrogels [61]. Various fabrication approaches, including self-assembly and swelling techniques, have been employed to successfully produce these systems from natural biopolymers [4]. The use of natural biopolymers is particularly advantageous in the development of herbal nanogel formulations for skin cancer therapy. These materials provide a suitable matrix for the encapsulation of plant-derived bioactive compounds and contribute to improved drug stability, enhanced skin permeability, and sustained release characteristics [62]. Moreover, the inherent biocompatibility of natural polymers minimizes adverse effects and supports their application in topical and localized cancer treatment. Several approaches have been developed for the preparation of nanogels based on natural polymers, which may be broadly classified as follows (1) Spray-drying technique [23] (2) Homogeneous aqueous gelation [49] (3) Chemical cross-linking of dextran [24]. Among these approaches, polysaccharide-based systems such as chitosan-, dextran, and hyaluronic acid-derived nanogels have attracted considerable attention for delivering phytochemicals with anticancer activity [63]. Natural compounds including curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and aloe vera constituents can be effectively incorporated into these biopolymeric matrices to overcome limitations associated with poor aqueous solubility, rapid degradation, and limited bioavailability [64]. Furthermore, natural biopolymer-based herbal nanogels exhibit excellent mucoadhesive and skin-adhesive properties, facilitating prolonged retention of therapeutic agents at the tumor site. Their ability to provide controlled release and enhanced penetration through the skin barrier makes them promising carriers for the treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers [65]. Consequently, natural biopolymer-derived nanogels represent an important class of delivery systems for improving the therapeutic efficacy of herbal medicines in skin cancer management [66].

Heterogeneous emulsification technique involving water and oil

The water-in-oil (W/O) heterogeneous emulsification method is one of the most widely employed approaches for the fabrication of nanogel systems. In this technique, aqueous solutions containing hydrophilic biopolymers are dispersed within a continuous lipophilic phase using suitable lipid-soluble surfactants [67]. Subsequently, cross-linking of the biopolymeric network is achieved through the use of hydrophilic cross-linking agents, leading to the formation of stable nanogel particles [68]. The W/O heterogeneous emulsification technique has gained considerable importance in the preparation of herbal nanogel formulations because it enables efficient encapsulation of plant-derived bioactive compounds while preserving their physicochemical stability [69]. The method offers excellent control over particle size and morphology, thereby facilitating the production of nanogels with enhanced drug-loading capacity and sustained-release characteristics [69]. Natural polymers such as chitosan, dextran, albumin, and hyaluronic acid are frequently employed in this method to fabricate biocompatible herbal nanogels [70]. These systems can effectively incorporate phytochemicals with anticancer potential, including curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, and epigallocatechin gallate, thereby overcoming challenges associated with poor aqueous solubility, instability, and limited bioavailability [71]. Furthermore, herbal nanogels prepared through W/O emulsification exhibit improved skin penetration and prolonged retention of phytoconstituents at the tumor site, which are desirable characteristics for the localized treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers [72]. The controlled release properties of these formulations contribute to enhanced therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Inverse mini emulsion technique

Inverse miniemulsion techniques involve the dispersion of aqueous biopolymeric solutions within a continuous hydrophobic phase using high-speed homogenizers, agitators, or continuous stirring processes. Water-insoluble surfactants, such as Span-80 and Aerosol OT, are employed to stabilize the resulting microemulsion system, thereby facilitating the formation of nanogel matrices [51]. These microemulsions enable the incorporation of various biologically active molecules, including RNA, drugs, and genetic materials, into hydrophilic particles [52]. Subsequently, chemical cross-linking agents such as glutaraldehyde are utilized to stabilize the hydrophilic particles. The resulting microgel particles are subjected to precipitation, centrifugation, and repeated washing with appropriate organic solvents, including isopropanol, before undergoing lyophilization [53]. Miniemulsion technology has also been widely employed in the preparation of gelatin-based nanogel systems [54]. The process primarily involves fusion and fission mechanisms. Persistent nanogel structures can be obtained by fusing miniemulsions of polymeric solutions, such as gelatin and poly(butylene-co-ethylene)-block-poly (ethylene oxide), followed by ultrasonic treatment and cross-linking with glutaraldehyde [55]. These gel particles generally exhibit particle sizes ranging from 100 to 400 nm. As demonstrated by Kim and co-workers, a relatively high concentration of surfactants is required in miniemulsion systems to obtain stable gel particles [56]. This technique allows the preparation of particles within both the nanometer and micrometre ranges. Inverse miniemulsion techniques provide an efficient platform for encapsulating phytoconstituents with poor aqueous solubility and limited bioavailability. The method offers excellent control over particle size distribution and enables the production of stable nanogel systems capable of protecting sensitive herbal molecules from degradation [57]. Natural bioactive compounds such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and aloe vera-derived constituents can be successfully incorporated into miniemulsion-based nanogels. Encapsulation within these polymeric matrices improves the stability, skin permeation, and sustained-release behaviour of phytochemicals, thereby enhancing their therapeutic potential against melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers [58]. Furthermore, gelatin-based and polysaccharide-derived herbal nanogels prepared by inverse miniemulsion techniques exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, making them attractive carriers for localized drug delivery [59. The nanoscale dimensions achieved through this approach facilitate enhanced penetration into skin tissues and prolonged retention at tumor sites, thereby increasing anticancer efficacy while minimizing systemic adverse effects. Consequently, inverse miniemulsion technology represents a promising strategy for the development of advanced herbal nanogel formulations for skin cancer therapy [60].

Micellar reverse method

The reverse micellar technique is based on the water-in-oil (W/O) dispersion phenomenon and is similar to the inverse miniemulsion approach. In this method, a large amount of lipophilic surfactant is employed to produce a thermodynamically stable micellar system consisting of water-containing droplets dispersed throughout a continuous oil phase [57]. The resulting gel particles are highly compact and generally exhibit particle sizes ranging from 10 to 150 nm (Fig. 6). The formation of uniformly sized particles requires suitable cross-linking agents, while an appropriate concentration of oil-soluble surfactants is essential for obtaining thermodynamically stable nanosized systems [58]. The reverse micellar technique has attracted considerable attention in the fabrication of herbal nanogels because it enables the preparation of particles with narrow size distribution and high encapsulation efficiency [61]. Such characteristics are particularly advantageous for incorporating phytochemicals intended for skin cancer therapy, as many natural compounds suffer from poor aqueous solubility and limited bioavailability. Herbal bioactive compounds, including curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and aloe vera-derived constituents, can be effectively entrapped within reverse micelle-based nanogel systems [62]. Encapsulation within these nanosized carriers enhances the physicochemical stability of phytochemicals, protects them against premature degradation, and facilitates sustained and controlled release. Furthermore, the small particle size obtained through the reverse micellar approach promotes enhanced penetration through the skin barrier and improves the accumulation of phytoconstituents at tumor sites [63]. These properties are particularly beneficial for the treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers, where localized delivery and prolonged retention of therapeutic agents are desirable. Natural polymers such as chitosan, dextran, hyaluronic acid, and gelatin are frequently utilized in reverse micellar systems owing to their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [64]. The combination of these polymers with herbal bioactive compounds results in multifunctional nanogel systems capable of improving therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity. Consequently, reverse micellar techniques represent a promising strategy for the development of advanced herbal nanogel formulations for targeted skin cancer therapy [64].

Membrane emulsification technique

The membrane emulsification technique is an efficient approach for producing nanogel particles with narrow size distributions and highly dispersed spherical morphologies. This method utilizes a membrane filtration process involving Shirasu porous glass (SPG) membranes containing uniformly distributed pores ranging from 0.1 to 20 μm [59]. Under controlled pressure conditions, droplets of similar sizes are transported through the membrane to form either water-in-oil (W/O) or oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions with uniform particle size distributions (Fig. 7). Furthermore, this technique can be employed for the preparation of various emulsion systems, including solid/O/W, O/W/O, and W/O/W dispersions [60]. The resulting particles can exhibit diverse morphologies, including microspheres, hollow particles, crystalline structures, and core-shell microparticles [61]. Subsequently, thermally stable nanogels are produced by combining membrane emulsification with sequential cross-linking strategies [62]. Initially, ions undergo gelation to generate fine nanoparticles, which are dispersed within ionic hardening solutions and then subjected to chemical cross-linking. Finally, the particles are purified and collected [63]. Membrane emulsification offers several advantages, including precise control over particle size, enhanced encapsulation efficiency, and improved reproducibility. These characteristics are particularly important for preserving the biological activity of plant-derived phytochemicals intended for skin cancer therapy [64]. The technique is highly suitable for incorporating natural anticancer compounds such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and aloe vera-derived constituents into nanogel matrices. Encapsulation within membrane-emulsified nanogels enhances the stability and solubility of these phytochemicals while protecting them from premature degradation [65]. Moreover, the formation of homogeneous particles facilitates controlled and sustained drug release, leading to prolonged retention of herbal compounds at the tumor site. Such properties are advantageous for topical and localized treatment of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers, where enhanced skin penetration and site-specific delivery are essential for maximizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic adverse effects [66]. Natural polymers such as chitosan, hyaluronic acid, dextran, and gelatin are frequently employed in membrane-emulsification-based herbal nanogels owing to their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [67].

Stimuli-sensitive nanogels

LHRH-targeted nanogels

Luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH), also referred to as gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), is a decapeptide with the amino acid sequence pGlu-His-Trp-Ser-Tyr-Gly-Leu-Arg-Pro-Gly-NH₂ that plays a central role in regulating reproductive functions [68]. Beyond its physiological role in the pituitary gland, LHRH receptors (LHRH-R) are aberrantly expressed in a wide range of malignant tissues. Overexpression of the LHRH-R gene has been reported in breast, endometrial, ovarian, and prostate cancers, as well as in hormone-independent malignancies including glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, lung carcinoma, and melanoma [69–70]. The high expression of LHRH-R in tumors has therefore attracted considerable interest for receptor-mediated targeted drug delivery. Previous studies have demonstrated that LHRH-R engagement may induce receptor-mediated internalization and cellular uptake, thereby enhancing the selective accumulation of therapeutic agents within tumor tissues [71]. Notably, several nanocarrier systems functionalized with LHRH ligands have been developed using conventional chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin. Although these systems do not constitute herbal nanogels, they provide important proof-of-concept evidence for the utility of LHRH-mediated targeting in cancer therapy [72]. The mechanistic insights gained from such studies are highly relevant to the rational design of herbal nanogel platforms, where LHRH ligands may similarly be employed to enhance the tumor-specific delivery of phytochemicals such as curcumin, resveratrol, berberine, and other bioactive compounds [73]. LHRH-targeted nanocarriers is intended to provide a translational framework for future receptor-targeted herbal nanogel systems rather than to present existing herbal Skin cancer therapy, LHRH-mediated targeting represents a promising strategy for enhancing the selective delivery of herbal nanogel formulations to tumor tissues. Recent research has increasingly focused on integrating LHRH-targeting ligands with nanometre-sized drug delivery systems (DDS), thereby improving tumor-specific accumulation and therapeutic efficacy [74]. Such targeted delivery approaches are particularly advantageous for phytochemical-loaded nanogels, as they enhance the bioavailability, stability, and cellular uptake of poorly soluble herbal bioactives while minimizing off-target toxicity [75]. Herbal nanogels incorporating bioactive phytoconstituents such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and aloe-emodin have demonstrated considerable potential in skin cancer management due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative, and pro-apoptotic properties. Functionalization of these nanogels with LHRH analogues may further facilitate receptor-mediated uptake and targeted intracellular delivery to cancer cells overexpressing LHRH-R [76]. A modified PEG-derived polymethacrylic acid (PMAA) block copolymer, designated PEG-b-PMAA, was developed by Alam et al. using a polyethylene glycol linker compound [77]. Cisplatin-carrying copolymers were conjugated with [D-Lys6] GnRH. Nanogels suspended in PBS or plasma remained stable without aggregation for at least fifteen days while maintaining equilibrium [78]. The GnRH-R-expressing ovarian tumor carcinoma cell line A2780 exhibited 47% greater uptake of targeted nanogels after thirty minutes compared with non-targeted nanogels [79]. Furthermore, targeted nanogels generated higher intracellular concentrations of cisplatin and exhibited superior anticancer activity relative to non-targeted nanogels, whereas free cisplatin accumulated non-selectively [80]. Although the aforementioned system employs a synthetic chemotherapeutic agent, it provides valuable insights into receptor-mediated targeting strategies that can be translated to herbal nanogel formulations for skin cancer therapy. Similar targeting approaches may be employed to deliver phytochemicals selectively to melanoma and other cutaneous malignancies, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity [81]. The GnRH-targeted nanogels produced tumor reduction of up to 75% in nude mice bearing human ovarian tumor xenografts, with sustained inhibition observed over twenty days [82]. In contrast, free cisplatin and non-targeted nanogels achieved a maximum tumor inhibition of approximately 50%. Notably, mice treated with targeted nanogels exhibited no significant changes in body weight [83]. The potential of ligand-mediated targeting strategies, such as LHRH functionalization, for the development of advanced herbal nanogel systems in skin cancer therapy. Future studies should focus on integrating receptor-targeted delivery with herbal bioactives to achieve enhanced therapeutic outcomes and improved translational applicability in melanoma and other skin cancers (Fig. 7) [84]. Receptor-targeted delivery systems offer exciting opportunities for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of phytochemicals against skin cancer. Natural bioactive compounds such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and genistein possess significant antiproliferative, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-apoptotic activities; however, their clinical application is hindered by poor solubility and limited bioavailability [85]. Incorporation of these phytoconstituents into ligand-functionalized nanogels may facilitate selective accumulation within tumor tissues and enhance intracellular uptake. Particularly in melanoma, where LHRH receptors have been identified [86], LHRH-mediated targeting strategies may provide an effective approach for the localized delivery of herbal therapeutics. Functionalization of herbal nanogels with receptor-specific ligands can improve the retention and controlled release of phytochemicals at the tumor site while minimizing systemic toxicity. Such targeted systems combine the advantages of nanotechnology with the pharmacological potential of plant-derived compounds and may represent an emerging strategy for personalized skin cancer therapy [87]. Furthermore, the combination of receptor-mediated targeting with phytochemical-loaded nanogels has the potential to enhance therapeutic outcomes by increasing tumor specificity and reducing the adverse effects associated with conventional chemotherapeutic agents. Therefore, ligand-targeted herbal nanogels represent a promising next-generation platform for the treatment of melanoma and other skin malignancies, warranting further investigation for clinical translation [88].

Nanogel sensitive to bacteria-accumulated tumor artificial environment

Although tumor-targeting bacteria have emerged as promising vectors for cancer-specific therapeutic delivery due to their inherent ability to selectively colonize tumor tissues and transport therapeutic cargos [98], their application in the context of herbal nanomedicine remains largely exploratory. In skin cancer therapy, particularly melanoma, the primary emphasis has increasingly shifted toward biocompatible nanogel platforms capable of delivering plant-derived bioactives with enhanced specificity and reduced systemic toxicity [99]. Bacteria-responsive nanogel systems reported to date have primarily been developed for the delivery of synthetic chemotherapeutic agents rather than herbal bioactives. Therefore, these systems are discussed in the present review as proof-of-concept examples of stimuli-responsive nanomedicine whose design principles may be adapted for future herbal nanogel formulations [100]. Their extensive gene-loading capability enables the simultaneous delivery of multiple therapeutic genes and the construction of specialized cellular communication pathways, thereby facilitating complex anticancer activities [101]. Therapeutic strategies are increasingly focused on the encapsulation of phytochemicals such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, epigallocatechin gallate, and berberine, which possess intrinsic antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiproliferative properties against skin cancer cells. Nanogel-mediated delivery of these herbal compounds enhances their stability, solubility, skin permeation, and sustained release, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [102]. Despite their significant therapeutic potential, successful treatment of malignancies will likely require combinatorial approaches because of the molecular and histopathological heterogeneity of cancer. Consequently, engineered tumor-targeting microorganisms are anticipated to become valuable tools in cancer therapy, and their clinical applications are currently being explored [103]. Nevertheless, future translational research in skin cancer management is expected to increasingly integrate receptor-targeted and stimuli-responsive herbal nanogel systems, either alone or in combination with advanced biological approaches, to achieve enhanced therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse effects [104]. Herbal nanogel formulations, bacteria-assisted targeting strategies provide a novel approach for improving the site-specific delivery of phytochemicals used in skin cancer therapy. The selective accumulation of tumor-targeting bacteria within hypoxic and necrotic regions of tumors can be exploited to enhance the localized release of phytochemical-loaded nanogels [105]. Such systems may increase the concentration of herbal bioactive compounds at the tumor site while minimizing exposure to healthy tissues. Natural anticancer agents, including curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, and genistein, exhibit promising antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic activities against melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers [106]. However, their poor solubility, instability, and low bioavailability limit their clinical application. Incorporation of these phytochemicals into bacteria-responsive nanogels offers a strategy to overcome these limitations and achieve controlled and targeted drug release [107]. Furthermore, bacterial enzymes and metabolites present within the tumor microenvironment can act as biological stimuli to trigger the degradation of polymeric matrices and promote the release of encapsulated herbal compounds. Such bacteria-responsive herbal nanogels can provide enhanced penetration into tumor tissues, prolonged retention, and improved therapeutic efficacy [108]. The integration of engineered bacteria with phytochemical-loaded nanogels represents an emerging multifunctional platform that combines biological targeting with nanotechnology-based drug delivery [109]. Although still in its early stages of development, this approach has the potential to improve the effectiveness of natural anticancer agents and may contribute to the development of safer and more personalized treatments for melanoma and other skin malignancies [110].

Triple-layered polymeric nanogel with lipase sensitivity

One of the major challenges in nanomedicine is the site-specific delivery of therapeutic agents to diseased tissues [160]. Nanoparticles are capable of adapting to the microenvironment of the target site while encapsulating therapeutic compounds, thereby minimizing premature drug leakage and ensuring controlled drug release [161]. A variety of internal and external stimuli, including pH, redox potential, temperature, enzyme activity, competitive chemical interactions, photoirradiation, and magnetic fields, have been exploited to activate responsive drug delivery systems [162]. Nanoparticles can be fabricated from numerous materials, including magnetic nanoparticles, multilayered cross-linked micelles, and mesoporous silica particles [163]. Most of these systems are responsive to the extracellular and intracellular tumor microenvironment, allowing them to function as passive or active targeting systems for cancer therapy [164]. Although many stimuli-responsive nanocarriers have been developed for the delivery of conventional chemotherapeutic agents, their design principles are increasingly being explored for the encapsulation and targeted delivery of plant-derived bioactives [165]. Accordingly, the following discussion on lipase-sensitive nanogels is included to provide mechanistic insights relevant to the future development of herbal nanogel systems for skin cancer therapy. Considerable efforts have also been directed toward the development of advanced antimicrobial delivery systems [165]. Liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, nanosuspensions, and conjugates have demonstrated the potential to improve pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy [166]. In 2012, Kaur et al. developed a novel bacteria-responsive delivery system based on a triple-layer nanogel (TLN) containing a bacterial lipase-sensitive poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) interlayer [118]. Lipase-sensitive TLN platform was originally designed for the delivery of synthetic therapeutic agents rather than herbal compounds and therefore does not represent a true herbal nanogel system. Nevertheless, its enzyme-responsive architecture provides a valuable proof-of-concept for engineering phytochemical-loaded nanogels with site-specific and stimuli-responsive drug release capabilities. In this system, the PCL intermediate layer is positioned between a poly (ethylene glycol) shell and a cross-linked polyphosphoester core (Fig. 12). The compact PCL barrier prevents premature drug leakage and protects the encapsulated therapeutic agents before reaching the site of infection [167], thereby minimizing adverse effects associated with uncontrolled drug release [167]. Bacterial lipases, which are abundantly secreted by numerous pathogenic microorganisms, subsequently hydrolyse the PCL barrier and trigger drug release [168]. Because lipase production has been implicated as a virulence factor in several bacterial species, it has been investigated as a biological trigger for controlled drug delivery [169]. Accordingly, degradation of the hydrophobic PCL layer facilitates on-demand release of therapeutic agents and suppresses microbial proliferation. Herbal nanomedicine, similar lipase-responsive nanogel systems could potentially be adapted to encapsulate phytochemicals such as curcumin, resveratrol, quercetin, berberine, and epigallocatechin gallate, thereby improving their stability, bioavailability, and tumor-specific delivery in skin cancer therapy [170]. A polymeric triple-layer nanogel system with spontaneous release characteristics was fabricated using a simple “arm-first synthesis” strategy [171]. The incorporation of a lipase-sensitive PCL interlayer enabled enzyme-triggered release of encapsulated drugs [172]. In the absence of lipase-producing bacteria, negligible drug leakage was observed, whereas rapid drug release occurred when lipase or lipase-secreting microorganisms were present. These findings demonstrated that nanogel-mediated delivery effectively inhibited extracellular bacterial growth [173]. Stimuli-responsive systems provide an attractive platform for the targeted delivery of phytochemicals in melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers. The tumor microenvironment exhibits unique biochemical characteristics, including acidic pH, elevated enzyme concentrations, oxidative stress, and altered redox states, which can serve as endogenous triggers for the selective release of encapsulated herbal compounds [174. Natural anticancer agents such as curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, berberine, epigallocatechin gallate, genistein, and aloe vera-derived constituents possess remarkable antiproliferative, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-apoptotic activities [175]. However, their poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability limit their clinical application. Encapsulation within stimuli-responsive nanogels can overcome these limitations by enhancing stability, protecting phytochemicals from degradation, and enabling controlled drug release at the tumor site. Enzyme-responsive herbal nanogels represent a particularly promising strategy because enzymes overexpressed within the tumor microenvironment can selectively degrade polymeric matrices and trigger localized release of phytoconstituents [176]. Similarly, pH-responsive and redox-responsive herbal nanogels exploit tumor-associated biochemical changes to achieve site-specific delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity. Natural polymers such as chitosan, dextran, hyaluronic acid, gelatin, and alginate have been extensively explored for the fabrication of multifunctional herbal nanogels owing to their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [177]. These systems facilitate enhanced skin penetration, prolonged retention, and sustained release of phytochemicals, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy against skin cancers. Consequently, stimuli-responsive herbal nanogels represent an emerging class of intelligent drug delivery systems that combine the advantages of nanotechnology and phytotherapy. Such multifunctional formulations hold considerable promise for improving the safety, specificity, and therapeutic outcomes of skin cancer treatment and may contribute significantly to the future development of personalized nanomedicine [178].

Bifunctional polymeric nanogels

Their superior stability and drug loading characteristics, in addition to the fact that they release drugs at different rates depending on environmental factors like temperature and pH, redox mechanisms, enzymes, and other biological stimuli, have motivated much interest in the development of stimuli-stimulated nanogels that can serve as functional drug delivery vehicles [152]. Some of the representative methods that are employed in the preparation of smart colloidal assemblies are inverse microemulsion polymerization [153], cross-linking of preformed polymeric micelles [98, 99] or vesicles (also known as polymersomes) [154], and one-step ring-opening polymerization. Such assemblies are generally made of cross-linked cores or shells of hydrophilic polymeric networks or core-shell architectures. The zwitterionic nanogels that were developed by Ganie et al. are biodegradable and multipurpose; they were able to include fluorescently labelled dextran into the gel for use as a medicinal product design, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles into the gel for use as an MR-based imaging probe [155]. Utilising the contrary microemulsion-based polymerisation of carboxylesterase methacrylate with l-cystine bis(acrylamide) as a disulphide (redox-sensitive) cross-linked component was the method that was used to accomplish [156]. It was stated that Kameshwar et al. successfully managed to synthesise two polymeric graft copolymers that had acrylic acid (AAc) and 2-methacryloylethyl acrylate (MEA) as components as the backbone. Additionally, grafting materials (B) PNIPAAm and monomethoxy (ethylene glycol) (mPEG) were described as having been synthesised. Emerging clinical trials involving nanogels

Nanogels have attracted considerable interest as multifunctional drug delivery platforms owing to their tunable physicochemical properties, high drug-loading capacity, and ability to enable controlled and targeted drug release. These characteristics have stimulated increasing efforts toward their clinical translation across a broad range of malignancies [158]. However, despite encouraging preclinical findings, the clinical development of nanogel-based therapeutics remains relatively limited, and currently available evidence should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, most ongoing clinical investigations involve nanotechnology-enabled formulations rather than herbal nanogels specifically designed for skin cancer. Several clinical studies have explored nanocarrier-mediated delivery of conventional chemotherapeutic agents for solid tumors with the aim of enhancing intratumoral drug accumulation and reducing systemic toxicity [159]. Clinical trials such as NCT03532538 and NCT03167051 have evaluated the delivery of doxorubicin and paclitaxel in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer and non-small cell lung cancer, respectively [160]. Although these studies highlight the potential of nanotechnology-assisted chemotherapy, their findings cannot be directly extrapolated to herbal nanogel formulations or to cutaneous malignancies because of differences in formulation composition, tumor biology, and therapeutic objectives. In haematological malignancies, nanotechnology-based approaches have also been investigated for the delivery of agents such as daunorubicin and vincristine. Clinical studies including NCT03116326 and NCT02751598 have focused on acute myeloid leukaemia and relapsed or refractory acute lymphoblastic leukaemia [161]. While these investigations demonstrate the broader applicability of nanocarrier systems in oncology, the long-term safety, pharmacokinetic characteristics, and comparative therapeutic advantages of nanogel-based formulations remain to be fully established. Targeted nanocarrier systems functionalized with tumor-specific ligands or antibodies have further expanded the scope of precision medicine. Clinical studies such as NCT03653572 and NCT03466727 have investigated antibody-mediated drug delivery strategies in ovarian cancer and HER2/neu-positive breast cancer, respectively [162]. Nevertheless, challenges associated with manufacturing complexity, batch-to-batch reproducibility, immunogenicity, and regulatory approval continue to impede their widespread clinical implementation. In addition, nanotechnology platforms are increasingly being explored in cancer immunotherapy for the delivery of immune-modulating agents and vaccines. Clinical investigations including NCT03685466 and NCT03989096 have evaluated their potential in non-small cell lung cancer and glioblastoma [163]. Although these approaches represent promising therapeutic avenues, evidence regarding their long-term efficacy and safety remains limited, and further studies are required to clarify their translational relevance. Overall, current clinical investigations provide valuable insights into the potential of nanotechnology-enabled drug delivery systems in oncology. However, there is a notable paucity of clinically validated studies specifically addressing herbal nanogel formulations for skin cancer. Therefore, the translational promise of these systems remains largely supported by preclinical evidence, underscoring the need for well-designed clinical studies, standardized manufacturing processes, comprehensive safety assessments, and regulatory frameworks to facilitate their progression from laboratory research to clinical application. Emerging controversies and knowledge gaps in herbal nanogel-based skin cancer therapy

Although herbal nanogel formulations have attracted increasing attention as a strategy for localized and targeted skin cancer therapy, several controversies continue to hinder their widespread acceptance. A major concern is the discrepancy between the impressive anticancer activity observed in preclinical studies and the scarcity of successful clinical translation. Most of the study show the promising antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory effects; however, these findings are predominantly based on in vitro cell lines and murine tumor models. Such models often fail to accurately reproduce the heterogeneity and immunological complexity of human skin cancers. Another challenge lies in the intrinsic variability of herbal bioactive compounds. Factors including geographical origin, harvesting conditions, extraction methods, and phytochemical composition can significantly influence biological activity, resulting in inconsistent therapeutic outcomes [179]. Moreover, uncertainty remains regarding the exact mechanisms responsible for the synergistic interactions between phytoconstituents and nanogel matrices. While numerous studies describe enhanced efficacy, mechanistic evidence explaining cellular uptake, immune modulation, and tumor microenvironment interactions remains limited. Furthermore, concerns have been raised regarding nanoparticle accumulation, long-term toxicity, and environmental impact following large-scale production. Consequently, future investigations should emphasize mechanistic studies, clinically relevant disease models, and standardized evaluation protocols to bridge existing knowledge gaps. 6.1 Translational Barriers and Clinical Challenges [180]: Despite encouraging preclinical findings, the translation of herbal nanogel formulations into clinically approved therapies remains challenging. One of the principal barriers is the limited availability of pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, and long-term toxicological data. Recent reports indicate that fewer than 10% of nanomedicine candidates successfully progress from preclinical development to clinical trials, highlighting the complexity of translating laboratory findings into therapeutic products [181]. Tumor heterogeneity and patient-to-patient variability further complicate treatment efficacy. Differences in skin thickness, vascularization, pH, oxidative stress levels, and immune responses may substantially influence drug penetration and release behavior. Additionally, the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which is frequently cited as the basis for nanoparticle accumulation, has shown considerable variability in clinical settings and remains a subject of ongoing debate [182]. Another important challenge is the limited number of clinical studies evaluating herbal nanomedicines. Most currently available evidence originates from experimental models, with insufficient data concerning dose optimization, adverse effects, and long-term safety in humans. Addressing these limitations will require multidisciplinary collaboration among pharmaceutical scientists, oncologists, dermatologists, and clinical researchers. 6.2 Regulatory Considerations for Herbal Nanomedicines [183]: The regulatory evaluation of herbal nanogel formulations represents a complex task because these systems combine the challenges associated with botanical products and nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems. Regulatory agencies such as the FDA, EMA, and ICH have established general guidelines for nanomedicines; however, there remains no universally harmonized framework specifically addressing herbal nanotechnology products [184]. Recent regulatory discussions have emphasized the importance of implementing Quality-by-Design (QbD) approaches, comprehensive physicochemical characterization, impurity profiling, and robust safety assessments [78]. Critical quality attributes including particle size distribution, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, and release kinetics must be carefully controlled to ensure product consistency. Moreover, the complexity of phytochemical mixtures poses additional challenges for standardization and quality assurance [185]. Regulatory authorities increasingly advocate the use of advanced analytical techniques, including HPLC, LC-MS/MS, and metabolomics-based fingerprinting, to ensure reproducibility and batch consistency [186]. The development of dedicated international regulatory frameworks for herbal nanomedicines is expected to become a major priority in the coming years. 6.3 Manufacturing Scalability and Quality Assurance [187]: Large-scale production remains one of the most significant barriers preventing the commercialization of herbal nanogel formulations. Although laboratory-scale synthesis methods have demonstrated excellent therapeutic performance, their translation into industrial manufacturing often encounters challenges related to process reproducibility, stability, and cost-effectiveness ]188]. Variability in herbal raw materials and extraction procedures can result in batch-to-batch inconsistencies, affecting particle characteristics and therapeutic performance. Furthermore, conventional preparation techniques may be difficult to reproduce on a commercial scale. Recent advances in microfluidics, continuous manufacturing, and Quality-by-Design approaches have emerged as promising strategies for overcoming these limitations. Artificial intelligence-assisted process optimization and machine learning-based predictive models are also gaining attention for improving formulation robustness and manufacturing efficiency [189]. Such technologies may facilitate the transition from small-scale production to commercially viable manufacturing processes while ensuring regulatory compliance and product quality. 6.4 Reproducibility and Standardization Issues [190]: Poor reproducibility has emerged as a major concern in nanomedicine research. Variations in plant species, extraction solvents, geographical sources, storage conditions, and nanoparticle preparation techniques can lead to substantial differences in biological activity [191]. Consequently, results obtained by different research groups often exhibit limited comparability. Another important issue is the lack of standardized protocols for characterization and biological evaluation. Differences in methods used for determining particle size, drug loading, release kinetics, cytotoxicity, and stability frequently result in conflicting findings [192]. Recent recommendations from international nanomedicine communities have highlighted the need for standardized reporting guidelines and minimum characterization requirements. Advanced analytical tools such as dynamic light scattering, electron microscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, differential scanning calorimetry, and omics-based approaches are increasingly being employed to enhance reproducibility and improve quality assessment. Establishing harmonized protocols and reference standards will be essential for accelerating clinical translation [193]. 6.5 Commercialization Challenges and Market Perspectives: Despite the remarkable growth of the global nanomedicine sector, the successful commercialization of herbal nanogel formulations remains limited. Recent market analysts estimate that the global nanomedicine market was valued at approximately USD 190.6 billion in 2023 and is projected to exceed USD 410 billion by 2030, driven primarily by advances in oncology, targeted therapeutics, and precision medicine [194]. Nevertheless, herbal nanomedicines represent only a small segment of this rapidly expanding market, reflecting the substantial gap between academic innovation and clinical implementation. One of the major impediments to commercialization is the complexity associated with translating laboratory-scale formulations into industrially viable products. Although numerous herbal nanogel systems have demonstrated promising anticancer activity in preclinical studies, only a limited number of nanomedicines have progressed to regulatory approval. Recent studies have identified uncontrolled scale-up processes, high manufacturing costs, formulation complexity, and insufficient understanding of nano–bio interactions as major barriers to clinical translation [195]. Manufacturing-related challenges are particularly critical for herbal nanogel formulations. Variability in phytochemical composition arising from differences in plant sources, cultivation conditions, extraction procedures, and storage conditions can lead to significant batch-to-batch inconsistencies [195]. Furthermore, maintaining particle size distribution, encapsulation efficiency, sterility, and long-term stability during large-scale production remains difficult. Consequently, regulatory authorities increasingly emphasize the implementation of Quality-by-Design (QbD) principles, critical quality attributes (CQAs), and process analytical technologies to ensure reproducibility and product quality [196]. Recent reports suggest that QbD-based approaches, coupled with machine learning and artificial intelligence-assisted optimization, may improve process robustness and facilitate commercial manufacturing [196]. Regulatory uncertainty represents another important challenge. Herbal nanogels occupy a unique position at the interface of botanical medicines and nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, resulting in ambiguity regarding their regulatory classification. Although the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have established general frameworks for nanomedicines, dedicated guidelines specifically addressing herbal nanogel formulations are currently lacking. Consequently, developers are required to provide extensive physicochemical characterization, stability studies, toxicological assessments, pharmacokinetic data, and evidence of clinical efficacy, which substantially increases development costs and timelines [197]. Intellectual property protection further complicates commercialization. Unlike synthetic drugs, naturally derived phytoconstituents are often difficult to patent, thereby limiting market exclusivity and reducing the attractiveness of these products to industrial investors. Moreover, reimbursement uncertainties, stringent regulatory requirements, and the relatively limited availability of robust clinical data discourage private-sector investment. Recent commercialization analyses have therefore advocated a transition from a material-centred approach toward a “commercialization-by-design” strategy, in which market demand, regulatory expectations, health economics, and clinical utility are considered during the early stages of product development [198]. Another important issue is the high attrition rate observed during nanomedicine development. Despite decades of research, only a small proportion of Nano formulations successfully reach clinical use, and several promising candidates have failed during late-stage clinical evaluation owing to inadequate therapeutic efficacy or unfavourable pharmacokinetic behavior. These experiences highlight the necessity for more predictive preclinical models and comprehensive translational assessment. Nevertheless, recent advances in continuous manufacturing technologies, microfluidics, artificial intelligence-assisted formulation optimization, and Quality-by-Design methodologies are expected to improve scalability and reduce manufacturing variability. Strategic collaborations among academia, pharmaceutical industries, biotechnology companies, regulatory agencies, and public funding organizations are likely to play a pivotal role in accelerating commercialization [199]. In addition, the development of harmonized international guidelines, standardized toxicological evaluation protocols, and GMP-compliant manufacturing platforms will be essential for bridging the gap between preclinical promise and commercial success [200]. Therefore, future progress in herbal nanogel-based skin cancer therapeutics will depend not only on demonstrating biological efficacy but also on overcoming the interconnected challenges of manufacturing, regulatory approval, intellectual property protection, and market accessibility. Conclusion

Herbal nanogels represent an emerging and promising drug delivery platform that integrates the therapeutic potential of natural bioactive compounds with advances in nanotechnology-based delivery systems for skin cancer management. Preclinical studies suggest that these formulations may enhance dermal penetration, improve solubility and stability of phytoconstituents, and enable localized and sustained drug release at tumor sites. Such properties may help overcome several limitations associated with conventional herbal preparations, including poor bioavailability, rapid degradation, and lack of site specificity. Experimental evidence further indicates that herbal nanogel systems can modulate key biological processes involved in skin cancer progression, including tumor cell proliferation, oxidative stress pathways, and apoptosis. In addition, their potential for combination with established treatment modalities such as photodynamic therapy and immunotherapy has been explored in preclinical settings, suggesting possible synergistic effects. Moreover, the incorporation of stimuli-responsive strategies—including pH-responsive, enzyme-responsive, receptor-targeted, and microenvironment-sensitive nanogels—offers additional opportunities for achieving precision delivery of phytochemicals within the unique skin cancer microenvironment characterized by weak acidity, elevated enzyme activity, and complex tumor heterogeneity. Such approaches may enhance therapeutic specificity while minimizing off-target toxicity. However, despite these encouraging findings, the clinical translation of herbal nanogels remains limited. Most available evidence is derived from in vitro and animal studies, and robust clinical trials evaluating their safety, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic efficacy in humans are still lacking. Furthermore, the presence of the stratum corneum barrier, interpatient variability in tumor microenvironmental characteristics, and limited penetration into deeper skin lesions pose additional challenges to the effective clinical implementation of topical and transdermal herbal nanogel systems. Important challenges such as formulation standardization, long-term safety, large-scale manufacturing, regulatory approval, and batch-to-batch reproducibility continue to restrict their clinical adoption. The inherent variability of herbal extracts, including differences in phytochemical composition arising from plant source, cultivation conditions, and extraction methods, further complicates quality control and regulatory standardization of herbal nanomedicines. Therefore, while herbal nanogels hold significant potential as next-generation nanomedicine platforms for skin cancer therapy, their clinical relevance remains to be fully established. Future research should also focus on patient-specific nanomedicine approaches, scalable manufacturing technologies, and standardized evaluation protocols to facilitate successful bench-to-bedside translation. Integration of artificial intelligence, advanced biomaterials, and personalized medicine strategies may further accelerate the clinical development of herbal nanogel-based therapeutics for skin cancer. Future research should prioritize rigorous clinical validation, mechanistic studies, and translational frameworks to bridge the gap between experimental promise and real-world therapeutic application. A cautious and evidence-driven approach will be essential to determine their true role in future oncological practice. Collectively, although substantial scientific progress has been achieved, herbal nanogels remain largely at the preclinical stage, underscoring the need for multidisciplinary collaborations among pharmaceutical scientists, oncologists, material engineers, and regulatory agencies to establish their safety, efficacy, and long-term clinical utility in skin cancer management.
