Beta 2 adrenergic receptor agonists as a treatment for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)
Authors: Brennan S. Winkler, Kristina M. Stayer, Abhinav K. Rao, Ehtesham Arif, Tsultrim T. Mendenhall, Kristy L. Thomas, Kylie R. Driggers, Xiaofeng Zuo, Wayne Fitzgibbon, Peifeng Deng, Yanhui Su, Yujing Dang, Marie Gerges, Daniel Kagan, Vishwajeeth Pasham, Bethany Wolf, Don C. Rockey, Jessica H. Hartman, Joshua H. Lipschutz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MASH is a leading cause of liver transplantation. Here, we investigated formoterol, a long-acting β2 adrenergic receptor agonist (LABA), in MASH. Mice treated with a high-fat diet (HFD) for sixteen weeks developed liver steatosis and were treated with formoterol or vehicle for four weeks. Steatosis largely resolved following formoterol treatment. To investigate mechanism, we evaluated mitochondrial biogenesis and found in HFD mice treated with formoterol versus vehicle that: PGC1α levels and electron transport chain components were significantly higher; mitochondrial number was increased; and lipids were decreased. Human HepaRG liver cells were then exposed to free fatty acids and/or formoterol. Formoterol attenuated lipid accumulation and increased ATP-linked basal and maximal respiration. Finally, a retrospective analysis of 59,644 patients with MASH showed that patients taking LABAs had fewer complications of advanced liver disease and lower mortality. Together, these data raise the possibility that LABAs, especially formoterol, could be a novel MASH treatment.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) are closely associated with type 2 diabetes (T2D). There are an estimated 436 million adults with T2D globally, and the most recent global pooled prevalence of MASLD among patients with T2D was 65%, and among patients with liver biopsy data, the global pooled prevalence of MASH was 66%1. Insulin resistance leads to liver steatosis which drives the metabolic dysfunction associated with MASH2,3. Over time, patients that progress to MASH, especially those with T2D, develop fibrosis which increases the risk for hepatocellular carcinoma, cirrhosis, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hepatorenal syndrome, hepatic decompensation, the need for a liver transplant, and death2.

There are currently only two approved treatment for MASH. The first is resmetirom, an oral thyroid hormone receptor-β (THR-β) agonist, that is moderately effective only in a subset of patients and has side effects4. Resmetirom acts by mitigating inflammatory responses in the liver by reducing macrophage infiltration and downregulating genes related to inflammation and fibrosis5. The second is the very recently approved semaglutide6.

We recently showed that the long-acting beta 2 adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) agonist formoterol in a high fat diet (HFD) mouse model of T2D reversed diabetic kidney disease at the ultrastructural, histologic, and functional levels. The mechanism appeared to be improved mitochondrial and metabolic function following formoterol treatment. Importantly, β2-AR agonists were strongly associated with protection from progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) in a large retrospective cohort of patients that we studied, and, in turn, long-acting β2-AR agonists were associated with greater protection from progression of CKD than short-acting β2-AR agonists7. Diabetes is the leading cause of ESKD8 and is very highly associated with MASH. Importantly, Tao et al. showed that β2-AR knockout in the liver exacerbated steatosis in a HFD mouse model9. Therefore, we hypothesized that formoterol treatment may be able to overcome the metabolic deficiencies that occur in MASH.

Here we investigate whether β2-AR agonist use can improve liver steatosis in a mouse model of T2D and in human hepatic cell culture. We then explore the clinical translation of this model, asking whether there is a concomitant association with improved outcomes in humans with MASH who receive β2-AR agonists.

Results

Formoterol treatment decreases liver steatosis in mice

Given our previous data showing that formoterol reversed kidney disease at the ultrastructural, histologic, and functional levels in a HFD mouse model of T2D7, we wanted to test if formoterol would also reverse liver steatosis in this model. Eight-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were fed a HFD (60% of total calories from fat) for 20 weeks which leads to liver steatosis. After 16 weeks of HFD, mice were given daily IP injections of formoterol (1 mg/kg) on weekdays for 4 weeks (5 days/week). Mice were sacrificed after treatment. As in our previous study, mice fed a HFD gained significant weight (Supplementary Fig. 1A). HFD led to a significant increase in body weight as compared to the low-fat diet (LFD), and HFD mice treated with formoterol had a trend toward lower body weights than HFD mice treated with vehicle, though the difference was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 1). Liver weights, adjusted to bodyweight as a covariate were not significantly different between mice treated with formoterol or DMSO (Fig. 1A). Steatosis largely resolved in mice treated with formoterol compared to untreated mice (Fig. 1B). To complement our steatosis histological scoring (Fig. 1C), a NAFLD activity Score (NAS) was calculated by our liver Pathologist for each of the mice based on liver histology (Fig.1D). The NAS score combines scores for steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocellular ballooning to a maximum combined score of 810. Formoterol treated mice had significantly lower NAS scores than controls (Fig.1C). Formoterol treatment decreases specific classes of lipids in mice

To further define the effect of formoterol on liver lipids, we performed thin layer chromatography (TLC) to analyze changes to broad classes of lipids in the mouse livers. Lipid species were normalized to wet liver weight. Triacylglycerides, phospholipids, monoglycerides, 1,2-diacylglycerides, 1,3-diacylglycerides, and cholesteryl esters were quantified. We found that livers of HFD mice treated with formoterol had significantly decreased triacylglycerides and monoglycerides. Formoterol treatment leads to downregulation of pro-inflammatory signaling, cell cycle, lipid synthesis and metabolism, and extracellular matrix pathways in mice

We postulated that the reduced steatosis reflected increased mitochondrial beta oxidation of fatty acids and oxidative phosphorylation. To determine the transcriptional programs altered by formoterol treatment, we performed bulk RNA-sequencing analysis from livers of DMSO- and formoterol-treated mice. We found significantly up- and down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A) that corresponded to upregulated pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, amino acid metabolism, and translation, with downregulated pathways including pro-inflammatory signaling, cell cycle, lipid synthesis and metabolism, and extracellular matrix (Fig. 5B). The oxidative phosphorylation pathway interestingly showed strong upregulation of some components and repression of others, suggesting remodeling of the pathway (Fig. 5C). We saw broad repression of extracellular matrix (Fig. 5D) and lipid metabolism (Fig. 5E) genes. Genes regulating hepatic lipid uptake (Cd36, Ldlr, Mfsd2a), triglyceride synthesis (Gpam), and lipid droplet formation (Plin2, Cidec) were reduced, while select apolipoproteins were altered, consistent with coordinated suppression of hepatic lipid accumulation (Fig. 5F). Formoterol treatment decreases lipid accumulation in HepaRG cells

We next utilized a human cell line to allow further functional testing of the mechanism of formoterol-induced reduction in steatosis. HepaRG bipotent hepatocyte/cholangiocyte cells were differentiated in culture and subsequently exposed to free fatty acids with and without formoterol. Palmitic and oleic acids were selected for these experiments as they are the most abundant free fatty acids present in the Western diet. To determine lipid burden in culture, Bodipy was utilized to stain intracellular lipid droplets. Formoterol treatment increases respiration in HepaRG cells

Next, we examined cellular respiration by measuring oxygen consumption by HepaRG cells over time using a Resipher device (Lucid Scientific). Cells were treated with formoterol or vehicle (DMSO) with or without free fatty acids. After establishing a stable basal oxygen consumption rate, we utilized oligomycin, FCCP, antimycin A, and Rotenone to measure changes in ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, and proton leak (Fig. 7A). We found that HepaRG cells treated with free fatty acids, formoterol, or free fatty acids plus formoterol had significantly greater basal respiration than control cells. The cells treated with both formoterol and free fatty acids had the greatest basal respiration, indicating a combined effect of formoterol treatment and free fatty acid exposure (Fig. 7B). We observed this same effect when measuring ATP-linked respiration. HepaRG cells treated with free fatty acids plus formoterol had the largest and most significant increase in ATP-linked respiration (Fig. 7C). Interestingly, we found that the spare respiratory capacity of cells treated with free fatty acids plus formoterol was minimal, suggesting that the mitochondria are functioning near their maximum capacity under these conditions. Cells treated with fatty acids or formoterol alone had a spare respiratory capacity similar to, though slightly greater than, the non-treated control cells (Fig. 7D). There was also a small but significant increase in proton leak in the cells treated with fatty acids plus formoterol, but not with either treatment alone (Fig. 7E). Formoterol treatment in HepaRG cells leads to changes in pathway regulation by RNA-sequencing analysis

To further define the mechanistic effects of formoterol in the HepaRG cells, we performed RNA-sequencing analysis. Pathway analysis revealed interesting pathways changed by free fatty acids alone, formoterol alone, or the combination (Fig. 8A). Interestingly, biological oxidations, PPAR signaling, bile acid synthesis, and cholesterol biosynthesis were similarly stimulated by formoterol and free fatty acids alone but repressed in the combination. On the other hand, free fatty acid treatment alone repressed oxidative phosphorylation and translation pathways while formoterol stimulated these pathways regardless of whether free fatty acids were present. We wanted to dive deeper to understand the consequences of formoterol treatment, so we examined intersecting differentially expressed genes between control v. formoterol and between free fatty acids v. free fatty acids/formoterol (Fig. 8B). We found that formoterol strongly increases CPS1 (Fig. 8C), a mitochondrial urea cycle enzyme consistent with enhanced mitochondrial function and oxidative capacity. In parallel, expression of BAAT, which encodes bile acid-Coa:amino acid N-acyltransferase, was increased by formoterol alone but repressed when formoterol was administered in the context of fatty acid exposure, indicating context-dependent repression of bile acid conjugation (Fig. 8D). Expression of ACKR3 (CXCR7), a chemokine receptor that scavenges inflammatory chemokines, was increased by formoterol (Fig. 8E), supporting an anti-inflammatory effect. Similarly, formoterol repressed MMP3, a matrix metalloprotease linked to fibroinflammatory remodeling (Fig. 8F). Formoterol also repressed ABCD2, a peroxisomal fatty acid transporter induced by lipid overload, suggesting a reduction in lipid stress (Fig. 8G). KDR which encodes the VEGF receptor VEGFR2 was downregulated by formoterol (Fig. 8H). Finally, when comparing FFA treatment to the combined treatment with FFA and FOM, lipid uptake and metabolic enzymes (LPL, FABP4, ACACB, ACSM5, ABCD2) were decreased while genes involved in fatty acid activation and lipid transport (ACSL4, ACSF2, ABCA7, ABCA12) were increased, indicating remodeling of fatty acid handling pathways (Fig. 8I). Long-acting β2-AR agonist use in patients with MASH is associated with significant decreases in complications

Such an analysis can be performed since conditions treated with β2-AR agonists, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are highly prevalent in this population. For example, COPD affects almost 15% of the United States Veterans Health Administration (VHA) users11 and long-acting β2-AR agonists including formoterol and its derivatives have historically been used as a first-line therapy in COPD12,13. Discussion

We report several principal findings, all of which are important for the understanding and treatment of MASH. First, our animal and cell data identify a strong therapeutic effect for β2-AR agonists, specifically formoterol, on liver steatosis and mitochondrial function. Treatment with formoterol over four weeks significantly reduced steatosis in our T2D mouse model. In cell culture, three days of formoterol treatment attenuated the accumulation of lipid droplets in human HepaRG cells. Formoterol has been shown to modulate cell metabolism in general and induce mitochondrial biogenesis through the GB-gamma-AKT-eNOS-sGC pathway14. Through direct cell signaling, it is known that activation of the β2-AR activates adenylyl cyclase and increases cAMP which activates protein kinase A (PKA)15. Activation of PKA through β2-AR signaling, stimulated by isoproterenol, leads to lipolysis in Hep3B cells via phosphorylation of cytosolic lipases ATGL and HSL16. It has also been shown that the β2-AR is crucial for maintaining insulin receptor sensitivity and function in murine hepatic tissue17. In addition, formoterol has been shown to be systemically anti-inflammatory and to mitigate a pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype in a HFD-induced obesity mouse model18. Thus, formoterol treatment could lead to attenuation of steatosis by increasing metabolic capacity, direct lipolysis, and anti-inflammatory signaling in the liver with the potential for sensitizing the insulin receptor.

In complementary in vitro and in vivo models, we show a robust formoterol-induced mitochondrial response coupled to improved hepatic lipid burden. In mice, we observed increased mitochondrial proteins via western blot and increased mitochondrial number per cell area via TEM, suggesting significant mitochondrial biogenesis. We also observed decreases in multiple lipid types in formoterol-treated liver tissue compared to controls, suggesting a net improvement in hepatic lipid accumulation. These phenotypes were supported by transcriptomic analyses showing significant induction of oxidative phosphorylation and amino acid metabolism pathways, with repression of extracellular matrix programs and lipid synthesis and metabolism. In cell culture, we measured mitochondrial function using Resipher. There was increased oxygen consumption in cells exposed to free fatty acids, formoterol, or both. This observation is consistent with evidence from patients with MASH where hepatic mitochondria exhibit increased basal respiration and decreased ATP-linked respiration compared to control patients19, reflecting a compensatory but dysfunctional bioenergetic state. We show that HepaRG cells treated with free fatty acids and formoterol have greater ATP linked basal respiration compared with control cells, suggesting that elevated oxygen consumption may be due to β2-AR-induced lipolysis and beta oxidation16,20,21. However, our transcriptomic analyses in mouse liver and HepaRG cells did not show induction of canonical lipid metabolism or beta-oxidation pathways, despite clear enhancement of mitochondrial protein content, abundance, and oxygen consumption. Instead, the coordinated induction of oxidative phosphorylation and amino acid metabolism pathways suggests that formoterol may promote metabolic competence through non-lipid sources, including amino acids. This interpretation is strengthened by the marked induction of CPS1, a flux-generating urea cycle enzyme and robust marker of hepatocyte metabolic functional capacity22,23. Multiple studies in MASLD/MASH show downregulation of urea cycle enzymes, including reduced CPS1 and impaired ureagenesis24–26, consistent with loss of metabolic functional capacity in MASLD. Thus, the strong upregulation of CPS1 by formoterol along with an increased oxidative phosphorylation pathway supports a potentially beneficial hepatic metabolic shift in formoterol-treated mice. Intriguingly, oral administration of a cocktail of amino acids and amino acid precursor N-acetylcysteine reduced liver fat content compared to placebo in a 16-week randomized clinical study27, supporting the potential of this metabolic pathway to improve liver steatosis. Our data do not align with the findings of Shi et al. who showed increased steatosis in C57Bl/6 mouse livers after acute exposure to formoterol21. Shi et al., however, used a dose of formoterol that was twenty times higher than the dose used in this study and their treatment was just 24 h in a non-HFD model, which likely explains the different outcomes.

Second, we did not see a significant change in fibrosis in our HFD mice. This agrees with previous literature demonstrating that 24 weeks on a high-fat diet induced significant steatosis and body weight gain without fibrosis in C57BL/6 J mice28. However, our mouse transcriptomic data showed clear repression of fibrosis-relevant genes in the extracellular matrix pathway, lending support to its potential as an anti-fibrotic agent. These changes were absent in the HepaRG model which consists of hepatocyte-like and biliary-like cells and lacks matrix-producing hepatic stellate cells. Further studies are needed to determine the impact of formoterol on fibrosis, and multiple fibrosis models that show more rapid fibrosis could be utilized in the future to investigate changes in fibrosis more fully29,30.

Third, our data support the hypothesis that the therapeutic benefit of formoterol is tied to mitochondrial biogenesis. β2-AR agonists are known modulators of oxidative metabolism and energy expenditure31,32. Indeed, we previously showed that the mechanism of action of formoterol in the recovery from acute kidney injury (AKI) and diabetic nephropathy was via mitochondrial biogenesis7,33. We show here that mitochondrial biogenesis and ETC proteins were increased by treatment with formoterol. The ETC deficits in livers from mice fed with a HFD or western diet were previously published by Malik et al.29. Here, we showed a significant increase in ETC proteins with formoterol treatment in our HFD model. It has also been shown that PGC1α expression is decreased in human MASH livers19. The rescue of the loss of ETC protein complexes and PGC1α via formoterol treatment shows an attenuation of mitochondrial dysfunction.

Fourth, our large retrospective cohort of 59,644 patients with MASH from the TriNetX database showed a significant decrease in rates of cirrhosis, ascites, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hepatorenal syndrome, and all-cause mortality in patients who received LABAs compared to those who did not. The finding that there was a significant reduction in the incidence of new cases of cirrhosis is potentially important (Table 1) and, along with our transcriptomic data, suggests the possibility of an effect of β2-AR agonists on fibrosis. A recent retrospective cohort study from another group also showed that long-term use of β2-AR agonists was associated with a protective effect against diabetic vascular complications34.

β2-AR agonists such as formoterol have been successfully used by clinicians for decades and are safe and inexpensive35. Toxicity studies on formoterol fumarate dihydrate show low toxicity even at high concentrations36. The formoterol dosing for mice in this study of 1 mg/kg, which was well below the lowest toxic dose for rodents of 36 mg/kg36, has been safely used in multiple previous studies7,37,38. For the HepaRG co-culture, 30 nM of formoterol was determined to be the dose that maximally increases basal oxygen consumption without signs of toxicity and this same concentration has been used with other cell lines32. Our transcriptomic data supports that formoterol exposure was not toxic at the doses used and did not initiate frank toxicity pathways in cell culture or in mouse liver. By contrast, we saw significant upregulation of the apoptosis pathway by free fatty acid exposure in HepaRG cells that was not observed in response to formoterol. We observed upregulation of protective pathways by formoterol such as “response to chemical stress” and “NRF2 pathway”, which could suggest that formoterol bolsters protection against oxidative and chemical stressors.

There are possible cardiovascular risks of systemic administration of formoterol in humans including increased heart rate and decreased blood pressure, especially at the doses we gave to the mice. If it turns out that higher doses in humans are needed or if adverse effects are seen using the standard dose, a targeted delivery system to the liver could be used. A recent study from the Schnellmann Lab used biodegradable and biocompatible polymeric nanoparticles containing formoterol that targeted the kidney, thereby decreasing the effective dose, and showed renal protection following ischemia and reperfusion injury in mice39. A similar approach in the liver could lessen cardiovascular effects while reducing steatosis and restoring liver function. Our findings may suggest that variability in the reduction in liver weight is partially explained by body weight; however, the experiment did not have sufficient power to test this hypothesis. The lack of power to evaluate the effect of treatment adjusting for body weight is a limitation of our study. Finally, it is possible that formoterol has off-target effects, though using β2-AR knockout mice we showed that formoterol was acting via the β2-AR receptor to rescue the kidney following acute kidney injury37.

In summary, given that T2D, MASLD, and MASH are present in epidemic proportions there is a critical need for new treatments. The next step will be to rigorously test the hypothesis that β2-AR agonists are protective by performing a randomized prospective study. In fact, we are currently enrolling patients with T2D, stage 2 or 3 CKD, and proteinuria in a 36-week prospective randomized study of formoterol to investigate the effects of formoterol on both CKD/proteinuria and MASLD/MASH (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07022418). Fibrosis 4 (FIB4) and liver elastography are being measured pre- and post-treatment.

Methods

Animals

Male 7-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Strain#000664) and allowed to acclimate for one week in the MUSC vivarium. All the experiments were approved by the MUSC Institutional Animal care and Use Committee (IACUC) and were performed according to the accepted protocol. After acclimation, 8-week-old animals were placed on a high-fat diet (60% calories from fat, Inotiv TD.06414) for 20 weeks. Animals were maintained in a temperature and humidity-controlled environment in a pathogen-free facility on a 12h- light dark cycle. Starting at week 13, mice were injected subcutaneously with corn oil (CO) (10 mL/kg) for four weeks as vehicle control for a larger study. For the final four weeks, mice were treated with formoterol at 1 mg/kg or vehicle (1% dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO) daily by intraperitoneal injection (10 mL/kg). Mice were sacrificed 24 h after receiving the last dose of formoterol or DMSO by CO2 euthanasia followed by thoracotomy for confirmation. The livers were harvested and split into thirds with 1/3 being fixed with 10% formalin for tissue block preparation and 2/3 being flash frozen using liquid nitrogen. A small piece of tissue was excised and placed in ice-cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde for at least 24 h for EM analysis. After 24 h in 10% formalin, liver tissue was washed with deionized water and transferred to 70% ethanol. After 24 h in 70% ethanol, tissue samples were sent to the MUSC Histology Core where they were embedded in paraffin, sliced at 5 µm thickness, and mounted on charged slides.

Histological Staining and Analysis

Paraffin embedded 10% formalin fixed livers were stained with either hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or picrosirius red (PSR). For PSR staining, slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated using 100% xylene and an ethanol gradient (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%, 50% and DI water). Slides were incubated in saturated picric acid (Sigma 925-40) containing 0.1% Sirius red (Sigma D0303) and 0.1% fast green FCF (Sigma F7258) and covered with aluminum foil for 40 minutes. Slides were then destained and dehydrated using an ethanol gradient (DI water 50% 75% 85% 95% and 100%) ending with xylene. Slides were mounted with Permount mounting media (Fisher Chemical SP15-100) and allowed to cure overnight at room temperature.

Liver samples fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde were processed by MUSC’s electron microscopy (EM) core. Tissues were rinsed in phosphate buffer followed by treatment with 2% OsO4 for 1 h then exposed to an ethanol gradient (50%, 70%, 95%, 100%, 100%, 100%). Tissues were then placed in propylene oxide followed by embedding in EmBed 812 (EM Sciences) via gradients of EmBed 812:propylene oxide (1:4, 1:2, 2:1, then EmBed 812 only) and solidification of the EM block in an oven overnight. Blocks were then submitted to the Vanderbilt Electron Microscopy Core for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Mitochondria in the EM images were manually annotated and measured in Image J.

For histological scoring of steatosis, H&E images were uploaded into Blinder software40. Images were scored on a scale of 0–3 indicating the amount of steatosis per image (0 = no steatosis; 1 = < 33% steatosis; 2 = 33–66% steatosis; 3 = > 66% steatosis). Multiple images were repeated during the analysis to determine consistency of scoring. Scores were documented through the software and reported.

NAFLD activity score (NAS)

All liver sections were digitally scanned into a. svs format and viewed virtually in Aperio ImageScope (Leica Biosystems). An overall estimate of steatosis was given based on visualization of all liver sections at multiple magnifications (40×, 80×, and 100× magnifications) and a steatosis score was provided based on NAS scoring systems (0 = no/minimal steatosis, 0–5%, 1 = mild steatosis, 5-33%, 2 = moderate steatosis, 33%-66%, and 3=severe steatosis, greater than 66% of the sample). All lobular inflammatory foci were marked and counted at 200x magnification and a score for lobular inflammation was provided based on NAS scoring systems (0 = none, 1 = mild, 1–2 foci per 200× field, 2 = moderate, 2–4 foci per 200× field, 3 = severe, greater than 4 foci per 200× field). Ballooning degeneration was evaluated at 100× to 200× magnification across the liver samples, evaluating for markedly swollen or rounded hepatocytes and a score was provided based on NAS scoring systems (0 = no conspicuous ballooning, 1 = few/focal ballooning, 2 = numerous/conspicuous ballooning)10. Statistics were performed using a Mann Whitney U test.

Western blotting, antibodies, and reagents

Liver tissues were lysed in radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, and protein estimation was performed using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method (ThermoFisher 23227). Protein lysates samples of 10 μg were used for western blot analysis. Images were collected and densitometric analysis was performed using a LI-COR imaging station (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE). Primary antibodies against PGC-1α (Proteintech 66369-1-Ig), oxidative phosphorylation proteins (OXPHOS) (cocktail of complex I–V, Invitrogen 45-8099), and GAPDH (Sigma G8795) were used for immunoblotting.

Lipid Analyses

Lipids were extracted from mouse livers using a modified Bligh and Dyer method41 and were standardized to inorganic phosphorous concentration by the colorimetric molybdenum blue assay42 for loading on TLC plates. The use of inorganic phosphorous as a normalization strategy for lipid measurements has been demonstrated to be robust and appropriate for minimizing biological variability compared to other techniques43. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) was then used for separation of lipids. Preparative TLC plates (Sigma Catalog # Z513032-1PAK) were scored with 2 cm lanes and heated at 100 °C for 1 h before use. The plates were allowed to cool to room temperature before spotting the samples. The lipid standard, Supelco Lipid Standard #1787-1amp (Bellefonte, PA) was spotted in the first lane of the plate. The samples were run with the solvent system of HPLC grade chloroform:methanol:water (65:25:4, Methanol Sigma Catalog #34860 and Chloroform Sigma Catalog #366927) until the solvent front was 2 cm from the top of the plate. The plate was air dried for 5 min in a fume hood and was exposed to iodine vapors (Iodine flakes, Sigma 376558) overnight for visualization of lipid bands. All TLC plates were imaged on BioRad Universal Hood II with the same exposure time. TLC plates images were measured in ImageJ for densitometry of bands which correlates to lipid concentration and were normalized to wet liver tissue weights.

RNA-sequencing transcriptomic analysis

RNA was isolated from mouse livers by homogenizing bulk liver tissue in RLT Buffer (Qiagen RNeasy kit) containing β-mercaptoethanol with 0.5 mm Zirconia/Silica beads in a Bullet Blender at power setting 8 for five consecutive 30-s cycles with 1 min cooldown on ice between cycles. Cells were lysed in RLT buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol in Qiashredder columns. For both cases, homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min to pellet debris and mixed with equal volumes of 70% ethanol before loading on RNeasy columns. RNA purification was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions including the optional on-column DNaseI digestion to remove genomic DNA contamination. RNA quality check (yield, purity, integrity), cDNA library construction, and Illumina sequencing were performed by Novogene. Raw data in fastq format were processed by Novogene to remove adapter reads and low-quality reads, then cleaned paired-end reads were mapped to the reference genome (mm39 for mouse, hg38 for human) using Hisat2 v2.2.1. Mapped reads were assembled by StringTie (v1.3.3b) and featureCounts v2.0.6 was used to count reads mapped to each gene. Finally, fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) were calculated based on gene length and read counts. Differential expression was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.42.0). The resulting p-value was adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s method to control the error discovery rate. Genes were considered as differentially expressed if they had an absolute log2fold change of > 0.5 and q (adjusted p value) < 0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using the easy Visualization and Inference Toolbox for Transcriptome Analysis (eVITTA)44. Pathways were considered to be enriched if the q-value < 0.05. Raw RNA-seq data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information’s GEO and are accessible through GEO series accession number GSE315974 (HepaRG cell data) and GSE315991 (mouse liver data).

Cell Culture

HepaRG cells were purchased from Biopredic Inc (Saint-Grégoire, France). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. HepaRG growth media consisted of 1 L of Milli-Q water, 1 packet (10.9 g/L) of Williams Medium E with L-glutamine without sodium bicarbonate (HiMedia AT125-10x1L), 26.19 mL 1 M sodium bicarbonate (Thermo Fisher J62495.AP), and 2.17 uL 46 mM triamcinolone acetonide (Sigma T6501-250MG) in DMSO. Media was filter sterilized using a sterile disposable filter unit with 0.22 µm polyethersulfone filter. Following filtration, 100 mL of FBS (VWR 89510-186), 10 mL of 100x PenStrep (Gibco) and 1333 μL of insulin (4 mg/mL, Gibco) was added before storing at 4 degrees. Media was refreshed every other day. Cells were passaged between 5- and 7-days after seeding when they reached 90-100% confluency. For passaging, cells were washed twice with sterile PBS (Corning 21-040-CV) and dissociated from the flask using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA 1x (Gibco). 15 mL (T-75) cell culture flasks were seeded with 4–5 × 106 cells, 96-well plates were seeded with 25–50,000 cells/well, and 6-well plates were seeded with 300-500,000 cells/well. For HepaRG differentiation, cells were allowed to reach confluence over two weeks in normal growth media, and then during the following two weeks differentiation was promoted by adding 1% DMSO to the HepaRG growth media for one media change, followed by 2% DMSO for two weeks. Cells were maintained in media containing 2% DMSO after differentiation until use and throughout the experiment.

Live Cell Imaging

HepaRG cells were plated and differentiated in 35 cm glass bottom dishes (MatTek P35G-1.5-14-C). Non-control cells were treated with 30 nM formoterol or/and 0.25 mM palmitic + 0.5 mM oleic acids. Sodium palmitate (Sigma P9767-5G) and sodium oleate (Sigma 07501-1 G) powder were added to prewarmed 50 °C PBS buffer and emulsified via sonication 2X for 15 s at an amplitude of 50, 30 s apart, using a Qsonica sonicator, then added to the media. Fatty acids were made fresh daily. During cell treatment, culture media was changed daily. Cells were treated for 3 days, after which media were removed, and cells were stained and imaged. For fat staining, Bodipy (Thermofisher D3922) and Cell Tracker Blue 7-amino-4-chloromethylcoumarin (CMAC) (CTBC) (Thermofisher C2110) were added to Live Cell Imaging Media (Gibco A59688DJ) at a final concentration of 10 μM and 20 μM, respectively. Cells were stained with CTBC for 15 min, followed by staining with both CTBC and Bodipy for another 15 min, both at 37 °C. After staining, cells were washed once with PBS buffer then placed in prewarmed Live Cell Imaging solution for imaging. Cells were then imaged at 10× using an Olympus 10i microscope. The DAPI (405 nm 42.3%, Sensitivity 49.3%) and Azami Green (473 nm 5.6%, Sensitivity 39.6%) channels were used for CTBC and Bodipy respectively. Images were analyzed using FIJI software. HepaRG cells are a co-culture between hepatocytes and cholangiocytes wherein the cholangiocytes form a monolayer that the hepatocytes grow upon. Hepatocytes form 3D stacks known as “islands” on top of the cholangiocyte monolayer. CTBC-stained hepatocytes were easily distinguished against the monolayer background which appeared dark. Regions of interest (ROIs) were set using a threshold to only include CTBC-stained hepatocyte islands. Mean fluorescence intensity was measured for each channel per ROI.

Resipher

HepaRG cells were plated and differentiated in 96-well Nunc dishes. Non-control HepaRG cells were treated with 30 nM formoterol and/or 0.25 mM palmitic + 0.5 mM oleic acids in HepaRG culture media. Oxygen consumption was measured via Resipher device (Lucid Scientific) and recorded in Lucid Lab over the course of 72 hours. Media and treatments were changed every 24 h. 1 μM Oligomycin (Sigma 203342), 1 μM FCCP (Enzo BML-CM120-0050), 1 μM antimycin A (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-202467A)/1 μM rotenone (Enzo Life Sciences ALX-350-360-G001) were added at 48 hours to plate rows A&B, C&D, and E&F respectively. Changes in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were measured as fold change and exact difference from day 2 to day 3 per well within a specific 4-h window encompassing the FCCP peak. Reported values were obtained by subtracting the difference or fold change of OCR from day 2 to day 3 of oligomycin, FCCP, and antimycin A/rotenone treated cells within each treatment group (control, FFA, FOM, FOM + FFA) from the difference or fold change of OCR from day 2 to day 3 of the control cells within each treatment group for ATP linked respiration (oligomycin) and spare respiratory capacity (FCCP). Proton leak was performed similarly, however instead of subtracting from controls, the difference or fold change of OCR from day 2 to day 3 of antimycin/rotenone-treated cells was subtracted from the difference or fold change of OCR from day 2 to day 3 of oligomycin treated cells within each treatment group (control, FFA, FOM, FOM + FFA).

Human retrospective cohort study

Data collection

We performed a retrospective study using clinical data from the TriNetX U.S Collaborative Network, a federated health research network that provides access to de-identified electronic medical records for 120,129,236 patients from 69 Healthcare organizations (as of 2/27/25) and primarily includes academic institutions which collected data from their main hospital, satellite hospitals, and outpatient clinics. Given that the collected data were de-identified, the study was exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Classification of disease

We used the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes for collecting patients’ demographics, and diagnoses, Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for procedures, RxNorm and Veterans Affairs Drug Classification System and codes for medications, and Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC) for laboratory tests.

Study population and design

We used clinical data from the large multisite TriNetX U.S. Collaborative Network to compare inhaled long-acting β2-AR (LABA) treatment (LABA group includes formoterol, salmeterol, arformoterol, and vilanterol) versus no inhaled LABA treatment (control group) in patients with MASH. Inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥ 18 years with MASH from January 1, 2016, to January 1, 2025. Patients with liver transplantation were excluded. Patients with MASH were identified using ICD-10 coding as described45. The index event in the LABA group was defined as the date of the first instance when the patient with MASH was prescribed inhaled LABA treatment, while in the control group it was defined as the first instance of MASH, but without inhaled LABA treatment. To achieve an incident user design, patients with prior use of inhaled LABAs before the index event were excluded. To reduce confounding bias, we conducted propensity score matching (PSM) at a 1:1 ratio using 45 variables including demographics, comorbidities (including COPD and asthma), medications, and components of the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score (INR, total bilirubin, and creatinine) to control for liver disease severity. Patients were followed for a maximum period of two years. Primary outcomes included all-cause mortality, and secondary outcomes included progression to cirrhosis and portal hypertensive-related complications such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), variceal bleeding, hepatorenal syndrome (HRS), and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP). HE was captured using lactulose or rifaximin prescriptions as described46. ICD-10 and other varying codes used for identifying cirrhosis etiology and clinical outcomes are described in the supplementary information (Supplementary Tables 2,3,4).

Covariates

To reduce confounding bias in our analysis, 45 variables were used for propensity score matching (PSM) between both cohorts (Supplementary Table 4). Covariates may have been present at any time up until one day before the diagnosis of MASH in the control group or before both MASH diagnosis and LABA initiation in the LABA group.

Statistical analyses

For outcomes with a single measure per animal (liver fibrosis, PGC1α, CII-SDHB, CIII-UQCRC2, CV-APT5A, all mitochondria and fatty acid measures), associations between each outcome with treatment (FOM or DMSO) and biologic sex were evaluated using a 2-way ANOVA approach. ANOVA models included main effects for treatment and sex. For outcomes for which the interaction was significant, pairwise comparisons were considered and p values were adjusted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD). Liver weights were compared using an ANCOVA adjusting for body weight as a covariate (Supplementary Table 5a). An ANOVA model of the effect of treatment on the ratio of liver weight to body weight was also performed (Supplementary Table 5b). Repeated measures of steatosis on a 4-point scale were also examined. The association of steatosis with treatment was evaluated using the generalized estimating equations (GEE) method for cumulative logits model approach47,48. The GEE model fixed effects for treatment and included a random mouse effect to account for correlation between measures captured in the same animal. The interaction between treatment was also considered in all models but was only retained if p < 0.05.

“Seahorse” data were evaluated using a linear mixed model approach. The model included fixed effects for day, treatment (control, FAA, FOM, or FFA + FOM), seahorse treatment (control, oligomycin, FCCP, or Antimycin A/Rotenone), and interactions between treatments and time as well as a random well effect to account for correlation between measures on the same well over time. Differences within groups between days and differences between groups were examined using a series of linear contrasts. We also examined fold change in respiration at day 3 relative to day 2 using a generalized linear mixed approach using a log link. The model included the same fixed and random effects. Results from the model were reported as fold change and the variance for fold change and difference between groups in fold change was estimated using the delta method. P-values for all comparisons were adjusted for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni correction.

Statistical analysis of the human retrospective data: Patients’ baseline characteristics in both groups were presented as means [SDs] or counts [percentages]. Categorical variables were compared by χ2 test and continuous variables by independent two-sample t-test. To balance baseline characteristics, both cohorts were propensity matched at a 1:1 ratio using the greedy nearest neighbor algorithm with a caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs. Standardized difference (SD) was used to validate the balance of the baseline characteristics in the propensity score-matched populations, and SD ≤ 0.1 indicated a minimal difference between groups. Cox proportional hazard models were conducted using R’s Survival package v3.2-3 to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and their associated 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). The assumption of proportional hazards was evaluated using the generalized Schoenfeld approach integrated into the TriNetX network. Patients with any outcome of interest prior to the index event were excluded from risk calculations. Kaplan–Meier was employed to estimate survival probabilities, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p-value less than 0.05. We used HRs to assess time-dependent risk of the outcome of interest. Statistical analyses were conducted using the built-in function analytic tool on the TriNetX platform (R version 4.2.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing), Python version 3.7 (Python Software Foundation), and Java version 11.0.16 (Oracle)).
