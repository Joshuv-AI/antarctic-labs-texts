Perianal Bronchogenic Cysts: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Dongqin Wang, Zhiqiang Yuan, Chunxin Liu, Yingnan Yang, Ya Lv, Hao Huang, Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo the best of our knowledge, we report a rare case of a multilocular perianal bronchogenic cyst, confirmed by ciliated epithelium and cartilage. Clinicians should consider this diagnosis for atypical perianal masses and ensure complete excision with long‐term follow‐up.

Introduction

Bronchogenic cysts (BCs) are congenital foregut‐derived anomalies that typically develop in the mediastinum or along the tracheobronchial tree [1, 2]. They originate during Weeks 3–7 of gestation due to abnormal budding or branching of the primitive foregut's tracheobronchial tree [3, 4], with most cases located in the posterior mediastinum [5, 6]. Rare ectopic occurrences in the retroperitoneum or pelvis [7, 8] may result from premature separation and migration of tracheobronchial remnants prior to pleural fusion [3, 8]. Most cysts ultimately present with symptoms of mass effect, drainage, infection, or evolve into complex cysts [9]. Previous reports indicate that cutaneous manifestations of BCs are rare across all age groups, particularly in adults. When skin manifestations do occur, they often appear above the abdomen [10]. However, occurrences in the perianal region are even rarer; as of 2025, only three cases, including ours, have been reported [11]. In this study, we report a rare case of a multilocular perianal bronchogenic cyst in a 22‐year‐old male. Perianal bronchogenic cysts are a rare developmental anomaly requiring diagnosis through clinical examination combined with imaging and pathological assessments, usually managed via surgical intervention. This report provides significant epidemiological evidence for the rare occurrence of anal bronchogenic cysts. The case report discusses a rare instance of an anal bronchogenic cyst, exploring its clinical manifestations, diagnostic challenges, and management, supplemented by a literature review.

Case History

The patient, a 22‐year‐old unmarried male, had a 1.5‐year history of an asymptomatic anal mass. He recently presented with a 2‐day history of a protruding anal mass accompanied by hematochezia and was admitted on June 20, 2024. Preoperative pelvic CT imaging was performed to evaluate the lesion. Axial and sagittal views revealed a well‐defined cystic lesion within the subcutaneous tissue of the perianal region, confined to the local area without extension into the pelvic cavity or involvement of adjacent organs (Figure 1A,B). This finding supported the clinical impression of a localized benign cystic mass and guided the surgical approach. He was afebrile and exhibited no signs of systemic infection. This clinical study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Baoshan Luodian Hospital (LDYY‐KY‐2025‐28), and the patient provided written informed consent.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The differential diagnosis for perianal cystic lesions includes perianal bronchogenic cysts, epidermoid cysts, dermoid cysts, caudal cysts, rectal duplication cysts, anal glandular cysts, and perianal abscesses.

On June 21, 2024, the patient underwent hemorrhoidectomy combined with thrombotic hemorrhoid excision and anal papilla resection under intravenous anesthesia. With the patient in the lithotomy position, a soft, oval‐shaped mass was identified at the 12 o'clock perianal region, with a smooth surface, no tenderness, and measuring approximately 3 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm (Figure 2A). A subsequent 1‐cm linear incision at the 12 o'clock position drained approximately 10–15 mL of this thick, odorless fluid (Figure 2B) [11]. This discolored fluid suggests two underlying pathological processes: Chronic microhemorrhages from cyst wall vasculature can lead to hemosiderin deposition, producing characteristic chocolate‐colored fluid. Bronchogenic cysts often contain mucinous secretions that oxidize over time, similar to the chocolate‐colored fluid described in thoracic bronchogenic cysts. The cyst was carefully dissected from surrounding adipose tissue using electrocautery and removed en bloc without rupture. The intact specimen was sent for histopathological evaluation. Postoperatively, the wound healed well, with good granulation tissue growth, a firm texture, minimal oozing of blood, and a red coloration (Figure 2C).

Conclusion and Results

Gross examination of the excised specimen revealed a multilocular cystic structure containing three distinct cavities filled with chocolate‐colored fluid exudate. Histopathological results (H&E staining) showed that the cyst cavities were lined with pseudo‐stratified ciliated columnar epithelium (Figure 3A,C). The wall contained glandular structures and smooth muscle (Figure 3B), leading to a diagnosis of bronchogenic cyst. Additionally, the cyst in this report exhibited areas of squamous metaplasia, which is a standard reactive change of bronchial epithelium. The pathological diagnosis (Pathology No. 2422853) indicated the presence of external hemorrhoids accompanied by the formation of a perianal bronchogenic cyst. We acknowledge that the absence of immunohistochemical staining (e.g., CK7, TTF‐1, CDX2) constitutes a limitation in our study, as the scant tissue from the small core biopsy was entirely utilized for diagnostic H&E staining.

Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged on July 11, 2024 (20 days post‐surgery). The patient was followed up 1 month after surgery, and the skin manifestation of the cyst had resolved completely, with no tenderness, bleeding, swelling, or other signs of inflammation. To ensure rigorous long‐term surveillance, a subsequent telephone follow‐up was conducted on April 21, 2026 (22 months postoperatively), which confirmed that the patient remained completely asymptomatic with no signs of local recurrence. Discussion

From an embryological perspective, bronchogenic cysts are believed to originate from abnormal budding of the primitive foregut, essentially arising from lung embryonic tissue during early embryogenesis [2]. Two main hypotheses have been proposed to explain their ectopic occurrence in atypical sites like the perianal region. The first hypothesis suggests that a portion of the developing tracheobronchial tree becomes sequestered and aberrantly migrates caudally along the midline prior to pleural fusion [1, 3]. Alternatively, the in situ development hypothesis posits that local cutaneous perianal tissues undergo abnormal heterotopic differentiation or metaplasia into respiratory epithelium without a migrating bronchial remnant [1, 12]. Despite the paucity of definitive direct evidence [13], these theories offer a plausible theoretical framework for understanding how a bronchogenic cyst could appear in a perianal location, accounting for both the aberrant migration and anomalous differentiation potentially responsible for our case. Microscopically, bronchogenic cysts typically consist of a unilocular cystic structure lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium [14]. The cyst wall often contains components characteristic of bronchial tissue, such as bronchial glands, smooth muscle, hyaline cartilage, and seromucous glands [15, 16]. Bronchogenic cysts are most commonly found in the mediastinum of the thoracic cavity [6]. Generally, they are small and asymptomatic, though occasionally they can cause complications such as infection, acute hemorrhage, or compression of adjacent organs [9]. Their reported frequency by location is highest in the intrathoracic region and lowest cutaneously [1, 17, 18]. Cutaneous or subcutaneous bronchogenic cysts are exceedingly rare, mostly noted at birth or in early childhood [12, 19]. Approximately 30% occur at the suprasternal notch, followed by the anterior neck, scapular area, and presternal chest. To date, literature on cutaneous bronchogenic cysts in the perianal region is exceedingly sparse, with very few documented cases [11], underscoring the extreme rarity of our patient's presentation.

Further investigation into embryological development is needed to clarify why bronchogenic cysts virtually never occur in perianal skin. In our case, the cyst's histopathology was consistent with a bronchogenic origin. However, departing from the typically unilocular presentation, our case uniquely presented as a multilocular cystic structure containing three distinct cavities. The definitive diagnosis was established by the classic morphology: the distinct cavities were lined by ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium, and the wall contained smooth muscle and glands, in line with previously reported cases. Notably, focal areas of squamous metaplasia were observed in the cyst lining—a common reactive change to chronic irritation or inflammation, which does not detract from the diagnosis. By reviewing other reported bronchogenic cysts in unusual locations and comparing them with this perianal case, we found that our case shares the characteristic histological features despite its atypical location. In conjunction with the related literature, our report underscores the unique multilocular nature of this perianal bronchogenic cyst and contributes to the characterization of this rare entity.

Clinical insights from this case center on two main aspects: diagnostic challenges and patient management. In terms of diagnostic challenges, patients with bronchogenic cysts often remain asymptomatic, making diagnosis in rare locations difficult, which can lead to misdiagnosis as common perianal lesions (such as hemorrhoids or anal fistulas). Existing case reports indicate that perianal bronchogenic cysts are usually located in the subcutaneous tissue around the anus. Previous literature has reported these as solitary [11], while our case uniquely presents as a multilocular cyst. To our knowledge, this is the first report highlighting a multilocular presentation in a perianal bronchogenic cyst, which may reflect complex abnormal budding during its embryological migration. The palpable soft tissue masses can compress surrounding anal tissues, resulting in pain during defecation and hematochezia. Additionally, there is a risk of infection within the cysts, potentially leading to symptoms such as the discharge of chocolate‐colored fluid. Diagnosis is typically made through imaging studies such as ultrasound, CT scans, and MRI, combined with physical examination and medical history. Confirmation of the cyst's origin and type is achieved through biopsy or pathological examination after surgical removal. It is emphasized that when rare tumors are found in the perianal area, bronchogenic cysts should be considered in the differential diagnosis, with imaging and pathological analysis playing crucial roles in confirmation. Clinically, our patient presented with a well‐circumscribed, painless, perianal mass without any signs of inflammation, sinus tract, or external openings—findings which are atypical for pilonidal disease. There was also no evidence of hair, debris, or recurrent infection, which are commonly associated with dermoid or pilonidal cysts. Importantly, no skin adnexal structures such as hair follicles or sebaceous glands (as seen in dermoid cysts) were identified. These combined clinical and histological features support the diagnosis of a perianal bronchogenic cyst and effectively exclude dermoid, epidermoid, and pilonidal cysts. In the context of a broad differential diagnosis, immunohistochemical analysis for molecular diagnosis can provide valuable reference information. Reports of unique molecular features of bronchogenic cysts from other locations indicate that the majority express CK7 while not expressing CK20 [20, 21] or CDX‐2 [21]. TTF‐1 (a transcription factor present in the lung and thyroid) is significant in diagnosing bronchogenic cysts, as its positive expression can aid in confirming the pulmonary characteristics of tumors or cysts through a molecular pathology perspective [22], assisting in distinguishing bronchogenic cysts from other types of cysts or tumors, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy. The combination of CK7 and CK20 helps narrow the differential diagnosis and supports the pulmonary origin of these tumors. Thus, the combination of CK7+, CK20− and TTF‐1+ is of considerable reference value in diagnosing PBCs [21].

As summarized in Table 2, our case appears to be the first documented report highlighting a multilocular (three‐cavity) presentation of a perianal bronchogenic cyst. While the multilocular nature of this cyst remains an unusual finding, it is plausible that it shares a similar underlying embryological mechanism to solitary lesions, though this remains entirely speculative. Complete excision remains the definitive treatment.

In the treatment and management of perianal bronchogenic cysts, preventing the occurrence of complications remains a critical consideration during the treatment process. Potential complications from untreated or improperly treated cases, such as infections and malignancies, are concerning; existing cases show that cysts have exhibited chocolate‐colored purulent fluid, indicating a potential risk for infection or malignancy [23]. Furthermore, cysts may present symptoms related to mass effect or evolve into complex cysts, necessitating the complete removal of the cyst wall during initial surgical procedures. The treatment strategy for perianal bronchogenic cysts is similar to that of bronchogenic cysts in other rare locations, with surgery being the optimal treatment method. According to existing case reports [11], perianal bronchogenic cysts are typically benign lesions, and surgical excision is the most effective approach for achieving a definitive diagnosis, alleviating patient discomfort, and reducing the likelihood of malignant transformation. Although the recurrence rate of cysts is low, regular follow‐up is essential to monitor for recurrence. In our case, initial follow‐up at 1 month post‐surgery revealed that the patient remained asymptomatic. To address the relatively short initial observation period, an extended follow‐up at 22 months post‐surgery was conducted, confirming the durable absence of clinical recurrence.
