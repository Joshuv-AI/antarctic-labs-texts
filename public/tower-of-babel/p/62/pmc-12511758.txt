Clinical Burden of Pediatric Influenza A and B in Malaysia: Outcomes and Resource Utilization in A Tropical Setting
Authors: Vishnu Arvindran Chandra Mohan, Chuin-Hen Liew, Kah Kee Tan, Naveen Nair Gangadaran, Pon Ling Lau, Syaniza Shaharudin, Yasothai Chandran, Asuwani Maran, Farah Nuruliayana A. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfluenza is a leading cause of pediatric respiratory illness globally, yet comparative data on influenza A and B in tropical regions remain scarce. This study aimed to compare clinical features, healthcare utilization, and outcomes in children hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza A and B.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of children aged ≤12 years hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza at a tertiary referral centre from May 2022 to December 2023. Influenza diagnosis was established using antigen-based detection via direct fluorescent antibody testing. Demographics, clinical features, laboratory results, interventions received, and patient outcomes were analyzed.ResultsAmong 177 hospitalized children, 116 (65.5%) had influenza A and 61 (34.5%) had influenza B. Seizures were significantly more common in influenza A (27.6% vs. 3.3%, P<0.001). Influenza B was associated with higher rates of diarrhea (31.1% vs. 14.7%, P=0.010) and signs of respiratory distress (tachypnea: 42.6% vs. 26.7%, P=0.031; chest recessions: 41.0% vs. 25.0%, P=0.028; adventitious breath sounds: 45.9% vs. 29.3%, P=0.028). Children with influenza B more frequently required non-invasive ventilation (13.1% vs. 3.4%, P=0.015), and intravenous fluids (70.5% vs. 55.2%, P=0.048). Median hospital stay was longer in influenza B (3 vs. 2 days, P=0.008).ConclusionInfluenza A was more frequently associated with neurologic manifestations, whereas influenza B showed a higher prevalence of lower respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms and required greater supportive care. These findings highlight the distinct clinical profiles of influenza A and B and their implications for healthcare resource utilization.

INTRODUCTION

Influenza is a leading cause of acute respiratory illness in children worldwide. Its clinical presentation ranges from mild self-limiting symptoms to severe lower respiratory tract infections that may potentially require hospitalization and pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission [1]. Young children under five years are particularly vulnerable to complications and represent a significant proportion of influenza-related healthcare burden [23].

In tropical countries like Malaysia, the epidemiology of influenza differs from temperate regions. Malaysia’s equatorial climate is characterized by high humidity, uniform temperatures and bimodal rainfall patterns [4]. While influenza activity is typically seasonal in temperate regions, it circulates continuously throughout the year in Malaysia, with intermittent seasonal surges [5]. This continuous circulation pattern poses unique challenges for surveillance, outbreak preparedness and vaccination strategies in tropical settings [6].

The epidemiology of influenza has undergone notable shifts in recent years due to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Implementation of public health measures such as masking, physical distancing and school closures led to an unprecedented decline in global influenza circulation. However, as these measures are lifted, influenza activity rebounded. In Malaysia, national surveillance data submitted to FluNet revealed alternating periods of influenza A and B dominance post COVID-19 pandemic (Supplementary Fig. 1).

While there are many published studies on pediatric influenza from temperate countries, data from tropical regions, in particular from Southeast Asia, remain limited. Representative data from this region is important given the unique differences in influenza seasonality, healthcare infrastructure, access to care and vaccine uptake. Therefore, we aimed to compare the clinical features, healthcare resource utilization and outcomes in children hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza A and B. The findings from this study will provide valuable insights into the differential burden of these infections, improve healthcare resource planning and inform local influenza vaccination strategies for children.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Setting and design

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Hospital Tuanku Ja’afar Seremban, Negeri Sembilan, the sole tertiary referral centre in the state equipped with PICU facilities. The hospital provides care for a population of approximately 1.2 million, including an estimated 290,000 children below 12 years. We included all children aged ≤12 years who were hospitalized with laboratory confirmed influenza between 1 May 2022 to 31 December 2023. Patients with concurrent bacterial or co-detection of other respiratory viruses were excluded to reduce confounding effects on clinical presentation and outcomes.

Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized data collection form. Variables collected included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, influenza vaccination status, presenting symptoms, laboratory findings and hospital outcomes such as PICU admission, duration of hospitalization, and mortality. Utilization of hospital resources such as intravenous fluids, bronchodilators, corticosteroids, empirical antibiotics and respiratory support were also documented. The use of these interventions was based on the clinician’s discretion according to the individual patient needs.

2. Ethics statement

The study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee, Ministry of Health Malaysia [NMRR-22-02497-W9I(3)] and received informed consent exemption. No personal or identifiable data were collected during the conduct of the study.

3. Study definitions

Clinical signs and symptoms were recorded at the time of hospital admission. Fever was defined as a body temperature of ≥37.5°C [7]. Illness duration was calculated from the onset of symptoms to admission. The likely source of infection was classified as an adult or sick contact, based on previous definitions [89]. An adult sick contact was defined as a sick contact with an index case above 12 years old, whereas a child sick contact was defined as sick contact with an index case below 12 years old. Sick contact was defined as a symptomatic household or social contact (spending face-to-face within 1 meter for >15 minutes) in the preceding 2 weeks prior to the onset of the patient’s symptoms. In households with multiple symptomatic individuals, the earliest symptomatic person was identified as the probable sick contact. Adventitious breath sounds refer to the presence of crackles, wheeze or rhonchi on chest auscultation. Patients were classified into clinical phenotypes based on the presenting features, including upper respiratory tract infection, lower respiratory tract infection, asthma exacerbation, croup and acute gastroenteritis, as described previously [1011], and detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Neurological manifestations was used to describe children who presented with seizures, behavioral changes, altered consciousness, or other signs suggesting central nervous system involvement.

4. Laboratory methods

Nasopharyngeal aspirates (or endotracheal aspirates from mechanically ventilated patients) were obtained within 24 hours of admission from all patients presenting with fever and/or cough, regardless of clinical phenotype. Viral antigen detection was performed using the D3 Ultra™ direct fluorescent antibody Respiratory Virus Screening and Identification Kit (Diagnostic Hybrids, Inc., Athens, OH, USA), which detects influenza A, influenza B, adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, human metapneumovirus and parainfluenza virus types 1-3.

5. Data management

The dataset used in this study comprised 177 patient records with 91 variables retained after data cleaning. Two non-informative variables and two variables with more than 60% missing data (serum creatine kinase and direct bilirubin levels) were excluded. Missing data from the remaining variables were imputed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Outliers were retained as natural outliers after confirming they were not due to data entry errors. All continuous variables were non-normally distributed based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Detailed information on variable characteristics, missingness and imputation procedures are provided in Supplementary Tables 2, 3, 4, 5 and Supplementary Figure 2.

6. Statistical analysis

To achieve the study objectives of comparing clinical features, healthcare resource utilization and outcomes between children hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza A and B, we employed the following statistical approach: categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables, which were non-normally distributed, were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

MICE Imputation was performed using the scikit-learn Python library (version 1.3.0). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed through the statsmodels Python library (version 0.14.0). Figures were generated using matplotlib Python library (version 3.7.2) and seaborn Python library (version 0.12.2). SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical tests, including Little’s Missing Completely at Random test, Chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, and Mann–Whitney U test. A detailed list of software packages, developers and versions is provided in Supplementary Table 6.

RESULTS

A total of 177 children were hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza, comprising 116 (65.5%) with influenza A and 61 (34.5%) with influenza B. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the overall cohort was 43 months (IQR, 17–71), with no significant difference between the two groups (P=0.069). The overall male proportion was 58.2%, and 17.5% had at least one comorbidity. Only one child (0.6%) had received the influenza vaccine in the previous year. Most baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups. However, a higher proportion of patients with influenza A reported sick contact compared to influenza B (79.3% vs. 63.9%, P=0.027).

Clinical features and laboratory findings are presented in Table 2. Fever and cough were common in both groups. However, seizures were significantly more frequent in children with influenza A compared to influenza B (27.6% vs. 3.3%, P<0.001), with two children in the influenza A group developing encephalopathy (Table 3). In contrast, children with influenza B exhibited a higher prevalence of gastrointestinal symptoms and respiratory signs, including diarrhea (31.1% vs. 14.7%, P=0.010), tachypnea (42.6% vs. 26.7%, P=0.031), chest recessions (41.0% vs. 25.0%, P=0.028), and adventitious breath sounds (45.9% vs. 29.3%, P=0.028). Myalgia was infrequently reported in both groups, with no statistically significant difference observed (2.6% vs. 4.9%, P=0.416). Children with influenza A had higher median total white cell counts (8.5 vs. 6.5×109/L, P=0.002) and platelet counts (281 vs. 211×109/L, P<0.001).

Children with influenza B required greater supportive care than those with influenza A. Notably, a higher proportion of children with influenza B received respiratory support (45.9% vs. 30.2%, P=0.038), particularly with non-invasive ventilation (13.1% vs. 3.4%, P=0.015). Intravenous fluid administration was also more common in influenza B patients (70.5% vs. 55.2%, P=0.048). The median hospital stay was slightly longer among influenza B patients (3 days vs. 2 days, P=0.008). No significant differences were found between the groups in the use of intravenous antibiotics, inotropic support, or mechanical ventilation (Table 3). Although PICU admissions were more frequent in the influenza B group (16.4% vs. 7.8%), this did not reach statistical significance (P=0.078). Complications and mortality rates were low in both groups and did not differ significantly. The two deaths in our cohort occurred in the influenza A group. The first was a 2-year-4 month old boy who developed cardiomyopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI) and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIVC). The second was a girl of similar age who presented with status epilepticus and severe transaminitis, later complicated with AKI and DIVC. Cranial computed tomography did not show features of acute necrotizing encephalopathy and a cerebrospinal fluid multiplex meningoencephalitis panel for 15 bacterial, viral and fungal pathogens was negative. Notably, both patients had no known pre-existing comorbidities.

DISCUSSION

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the global epidemiology of influenza, including in tropical regions where transmission patterns are typically less defined than in temperate climates. Prior to the pandemic, tropical countries generally experienced continuous, year-round influenza activity with irregular seasonal peaks [12]. During the pandemic, influenza activity declined sharply on a global scale. In the post-pandemic period, tropical Southeast Asian countries reported notable epidemiological changes, including unpredictable timing of outbreaks, abrupt surges and irregular subtype predominance [13]. In Malaysia, this was reflected by a reemergence of influenza activity characterized by alternating periods of influenza A and B dominance (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In this context, we compared the clinical features, healthcare resource utilization and outcomes of children hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza A and B. Although both types of influenza shared many common symptoms such as fever and cough, some distinct clinical differences were observed. Seizures were significantly more frequent in children with influenza A, whereas influenza B was associated with a higher prevalence of diarrhea and signs of lower respiratory tract involvement. Additionally, children with influenza B required greater supportive care, including more frequent use of non-invasive ventilation, intravenous fluids and had a longer median duration of hospitalization.

Historically, most studies have focused more on influenza A due to its potential to cause pandemics and its ability to infect both humans and animals. In contrast, influenza B lacks an animal reservoir and is confined to human transmission [14]. This absence of pandemic potential has led to a perception that influenza B poses a lower public health threat, resulting in it being relatively understudied. Our study was conducted during the period of post-pandemic resurgence of influenza B, which provided an ideal opportunity to compare differences in the clinical manifestations and outcomes of influenza A and B among hospitalized children.

The demographic profile of our cohort was consistent with regional data, with a median age of hospitalization in the preschool years and a predominance of male patients [15]. Although the baseline demographic characteristics were similar between the two groups, a significantly higher proportion of children with influenza A had identifiable sick contacts compared to those with influenza B, particularly with other children. While the reasons for this are unclear, one possible explanation is differing transmission dynamics between influenza A and B during the study period. Nevertheless, household transmission from school-aged siblings remains a well-recognized route of pediatric influenza infection [1617].

The majority of hospitalized children in our study had no underlying comorbidities, consistent with findings from other published studies [181920]. This suggests that the burden of influenza-related hospitalization is largely driven by otherwise healthy children without pre-existing comorbidities and reinforces the importance of routine influenza vaccination in all children. Notably, only one child in our cohort had received influenza vaccination, minimizing the confounding effect of vaccination on clinical presentation and outcomes.

Fever, cough and rhinorrhea were common presenting symptoms in both groups, consistent with previous studies [212223]. However, seizures were significantly more frequent in children with influenza A, despite similar proportions of patients presenting with fever and comparable median temperatures on arrival. This suggests that the higher seizure incidence is unlikely to be solely attributable to fever. Some studies have reported that influenza A is associated with more neurological manifestations than influenza B [2425], whereas others have found no significant differences between the two virus types [26]. Our findings contribute to this body of evidence and underscore the need for further research to clarify potential subtype differences in neurotropism.

In contrast, influenza B exhibited a higher prevalence of lower respiratory tract signs and diarrhea, a pattern that has been observed by others [152527]. The higher prevalence of lower respiratory tract signs and diarrhea among children with influenza B could be explained by the broader cellular tropism of B/Victoria lineage viruses, which are capable of targeting both the lower airways and intestinal epithelium [28]. Although myalgia and musculoskeletal symptoms are often associated with influenza in older children and adults, they were infrequently reported in our cohort. This may be attributable to the young age of our cohort, with approximately one-third of patients under two years old and therefore too young to verbalize such symptoms. As a result, the prevalence of myalgia in our cohort may be underestimated. Some studies have also explored the utility of full blood count parameters to differentiate between influenza A or B or to predict disease severity [272930]. Although we observed some statistically significant differences in laboratory values, these differences were modest and unlikely to be of impactful significance.

Most studies have shown that clinical outcomes between influenza A and B are similar in hospitalized children, although Özkaya at al. noted that the proportion of PICU admissions was nearly twice as high for influenza B [313233]. In our study, children with influenza B demonstrated greater healthcare resource utilization compared to those with influenza A. This included more frequent use of non-invasive ventilation, intravenous fluid administration, along with a significantly longer hospital stay. The increased need for supportive care among those with influenza B can be explained by the higher proportion of patients presenting with lower respiratory tract signs and diarrhea. Although the difference in PICU admission rates between influenza A and B did not reach statistical significance (16.4% vs. 7.8%, P=0.078), the nearly twofold higher rate among influenza B patients may have clinical relevance. Our findings suggest that influenza B can present with considerable severity in children and underscore its potential burden in our local setting.

The influenza B cases observed in our study are likely from the B/Victoria lineage, as B/Yamagata has not been detected globally since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and is now considered potentially extinct [3435]. Although molecular sequencing was not performed to confirm lineage in our cohort, the absence of B/Yamagata globally during the study period supports this assumption. However, our study has several limitations. First, the lack of viral sequencing precludes definitive confirmation that all influenza B cases were of B/Victoria lineage and limits the ability to distinguish between influenza A subtypes such as A/H1N1 and A/H3N2, which may have different clinical manifestations and severity profiles. Second, the use of antigen based detection rather than polymerase chain reaction likely reduced diagnostic sensitivity, particularly for influenza B [36], and may have led to selection bias toward more clinically apparent or severe cases. Third, as this study was conducted at a single tertiary center, the findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare settings with different patient demographics or healthcare access. Lastly, the study was limited to a relatively short post-pandemic period and lacked comparative pre-pandemic data, restricting our ability to assess temporal trends or changes in clinical patterns over time. Nevertheless, this study offers valuable insights into the clinical distinctions between circulating influenza A and B infections in children during a period of post-pandemic influenza resurgence.

Although influenza A and B presented with overlapping clinical features, our study identified several notable differences in disease manifestations and healthcare needs. Influenza A was more frequently associated with neurological symptoms whereas influenza B showed a higher prevalence of lower respiratory tract signs and diarrhea. Children with influenza B required more supportive care including non-invasive ventilation, intravenous fluids and had longer hospital stays. These findings highlight the burden of both influenza types and reinforce the need for preventive strategies particularly routine childhood vaccination.
