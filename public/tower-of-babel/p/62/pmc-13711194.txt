Outcomes after conversion from mechanical to bioprosthetic valves for anticoagulation-related complications
Authors: Vincy Tam, Hartzell V Schaff, Kevin L Greason, Richard C Daly, Alexander Lee, Austin Todd, Kimberly Holst, Juan A Crestanello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConversion from mechanical to bioprosthetic valves is sometimes pursued for anticoagulation-related complications, yet outcomes after such conversion remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe analyzed 62 consecutive patients undergoing mechanical-to-bioprosthetic valve replacement owing to major bleeding, recurrent thromboembolism, or nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty between 2000 and 2005. Survival was estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis.ResultsThe median interval from mechanical valve implantation to conversion was 7 years (interquartile range, 1-17 years). The 5-year cumulative incidence of bleeding or thromboembolism was 27%, with 63% of events occuring while patients were receiving warfarin.ConclusionsConversion from a mechanical valve to a bioprosthetic valve for anticoagulation-related complications carries substantial early risk and frequently does not eliminate the need for anticoagulation. Recurrent bleeding and thromboembolic events remain clinically significant, underscoring the importance of careful patient counseling and shared decision making.Key Words: mechanical heart valve, bioprosthetic valve, redo valve surgery, anticoagulation, bleeding, thromboembolism, long-term outcomes

Bleeding and thromboembolism persist after valve conversion.

Central Message

Mechanical-to-bioprosthetic valve conversion for anticoagulation complications carries substantial early mortality and often does not eliminate the need for anticoagulation or recurrent events.

Perspective

In patients with mechanical prosthetic valves who develop anticoagulation-related complications, conversion to a bioprosthetic valve may be considered to avoid long-term warfarin therapy. However, our findings suggest that this strategy carries significant early risk and does not reliably eliminate the need for anticoagulation or prevent recurrent bleeding or thromboembolism.

Mechanical prosthetic valves provide excellent durability but require lifelong anticoagulation to mitigate the risk of valve thrombosis and systemic thromboembolism.1 Although warfarin remains effective in reducing thrombotic complications, chronic anticoagulation carries a well-recognized risk of major bleeding, labile anticoagulation control, and patient intolerance.2,3 In clinical practice, these competing risks can create a therapeutic dilemma, particularly in patients who experience chronic hemorrhage, thromboembolic events despite therapeutic anticoagulation, or the inability to maintain stable levels of anticoagulation.4

For selected patients with anticoagulation-related complications, conversion from a mechanical valve to a bioprosthetic valve may be considered. The rationale for such a conversion is to reduce or eliminate the need for long-term anticoagulation and thereby mitigate bleeding risk or to address recurrent thromboembolism presumed to be related to the mechanical prosthesis. This strategy is not without important limitations, however. Many patients undergoing conversion have underlying conditions that may independently necessitate continued anticoagulation even after implantation of a bioprosthetic valve. Furthermore, redo valve surgery in this patient population may carry increased operative risk, particularly in patients presenting urgently or with another significant comorbidity.5,6

Data describing outcomes after mechanical-to-bioprosthetic valve conversion are limited. Previous studies were small, heterogeneous, and often focused primarily on perioperative mortality without detailed characterization of long-term bleeding and thromboembolic risk.7,8 Moreover, the interplay between recurrent bleeding and thromboembolism following conversion remains poorly defined, particularly in high-risk patients with complex preoperative indications.

Consequently, we investigated early and long-term outcomes in a contemporary cohort of patients undergoing conversion from mechanical valves to bioprosthetic valves for anticoagulation-related complications. Specifically, we examined the need for continued anticoagulation after surgery, overall survival, and the cumulative incidence of postoperative bleeding or thromboembolic events, accounting for the competing risk of death. We hypothesized that although conversion may be pursued to mitigate anticoagulation-related complications, substantial early risk and persistent long-term event burden would remain.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

We investigated consecutive adult patients who underwent conversion from a mechanical valve to a bioprosthetic valve between January 24, 2000, and July 10, 2025. Patients were identified through institutional surgical databases and operative records. Study inclusion criteria were (1) prior implantation of a mechanical aortic, mitral, tricuspid, or multivalve prosthesis and (2) subsequent surgical replacement of that mechanical valve with a bioprosthetic valve. Patients were included if the indication for surgery was related to anticoagulation-associated complications, including major bleeding, recurrent thromboembolism, or nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty. Patients undergoing redo surgery for infective endocarditis were excluded.

This study received Institutional Review Board approval (IRB: 25-013068), with a waiver of individual informed consent owing to its retrospective nature.

Indications for Conversion

Indications for conversion were categorized as bleeding, thromboembolism, or nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty. These indications were not mutually exclusive. Preoperative bleeding included clinically significant hemorrhagic events necessitating hospitalization, transfusion, procedural intervention, or documented imaging-confirmed bleeding. Examples included chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and other major extracranial bleeding events. Preoperative thromboembolism included ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism, prosthetic valve thrombosis, and other clinically documented thromboembolic events occurring despite therapeutic or attempted therapeutic anticoagulation.

Nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty included patients with warfarin allergy, persistent inability to maintain therapeutic anticoagulation despite high-dose warfarin, severe medication-related side effects, or markedly fluctuating international normalized ratio (INR) associated with recurrent clinical decompensation, but without a qualifying major bleeding or thromboembolic event as the primary indication for reoperation.

Because many patients exhibited overlapping clinical features (eg, prior bleeding, thromboembolism), indications were recorded as present or absent for each category rather than mutually exclusive. Postoperative anticoagulation management was individualized based on patient-specific thrombotic risk factors. Anticoagulation status at hospital dismissal and during follow-up was obtained from clinical documentation.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint was time to first postoperative bleeding or thromboembolic event. A composite endpoint was used to reflect overall postconversion event burden owing to the heterogeneity of preoperative indications and the difficulty in distinguishing recurrence of the original pathology from new events. Death was treated as a competing risk in time-to-event analyses for bleeding or thromboembolism.

Postoperative bleeding

Postoperative bleeding was defined as any clinically significant hemorrhagic event occurring after valve conversion that necessitated hospitalization, transfusion, or procedural intervention or was confirmed by imaging or endoscopy. Qualifying events included gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, retroperitoneal hematoma, hemoptysis requiring admission, gross hematuria, postoperative hematoma, hemorrhagic shock, and other major bleeding events documented in the medical record. For patients with chronic or recurrent bleeding syndromes, the first clinically significant episode during follow-up was counted. Minor bleeding not requiring medical intervention was excluded.

Postoperative thromboembolism

Postoperative thromboembolism was defined as any objectively documented arterial or venous thrombotic event occurring after valve conversion. Qualifying events included ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism, prosthetic valve thrombosis, intracardiac thrombus, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. Patients could experience both bleeding and thromboembolic events during follow-up; however, only the first qualifying event contributed to the primary composite endpoint.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, overall survival, readmission within 30 days, and anticoagulation status at hospital dismissal and beyond 3 months.

Follow-Up

Clinical follow-up was obtained through review of institutional medical records and available outpatient documentation. The duration of follow-up was defined as interval from the date of valve conversion to the date of death or last clinical contact.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile ranges (IQR); categorical variables, as frequency and percentage. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The cumulative incidence of postoperative bleeding or thromboembolism was estimated using competing risk methodology, with death treated as a competing event. Cumulative incidence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported at 1, 3, and 5 years. All data analyses were performed with R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between 2000 and 2025, 62 patients underwent mechanical-to-bioprosthetic valve replacement owing to anticoagulation-related complications. The cohort had a median age at the time of surgery of 66.5 (IQR 55.3-73.1) years, and 25 (40.3%) were male.

Preoperative bleeding was documented in 31 patients (50.0%), thromboembolism in 37 (59.7%), and nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty in 5 (8.1%), with overlap among these indications. Prior cerebrovascular accident was recorded in 35.5% of patients, and atrial fibrillation or flutter in 35.5% as well. Hypertension (71.0%) and dyslipidemia (69.4%) were common comorbidities.

The year of original mechanical valve implantation was available in 61 patients. The median interval between mechanical valve implantation and conversion was 7.0 years (IQR, 1.0-17.0 years; range, 0-57 years). The patients’ baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Operative Characteristics

More than one-half (58%) of the procedures were performed on an urgent or emergent basis. Valve positions replaced included mitral (50.0%), aortic (38.7%), tricuspid (6.5%), and multivalve combinations (4.8%). Prior mechanical prostheses were predominantly bileaflet valves, most commonly St Jude, followed by Carbomedics across all valve positions (Table E1).

The median cardiopulmonary bypass time was 129.5 (IQR, 85.0-179.2) minutes, and the median aortic cross-clamp time was 87.5 (IQR, 62.2-125.8) minutes (n = 58). Intra-aortic balloon pump support was used in 10 patients (16.1%), and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was provided in 2 patients (3.2%).

Concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting was performed in 8 patients (12.9%). Annular enlargement was performed in 6 patients (9.7%), and concomitant aortic root replacement in 4 (6.5%); among 26 aortic valve operations, the rates were 5 (19.2%) and 4 (15.4%), respectively. Left atrial appendage occlusion was performed in 4 patients (6.5%), and the Maze procedure was performed in 2 patients (3.2%).

Four patients underwent isolated tricuspid conversion. These were selected redo cases with prior operations for complex right-sided valve disease, including endocarditis, rheumatic disease, arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia, and systemic inflammatory disease. One additional multivalve operation included the tricuspid position. Operative characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

Early Postoperative Outcomes

The median hospital length of stay was 8.5 (IQR, 6.0-14.8) days, and the median intensive care unit length of stay was 46.0 (IQR, 22.0-119.4) hours. Thirty-day readmission occurred in 9.6% of patients. In-hospital mortality occurred in 8 patients (12.9%).

Among the 54 patients discharged alive with available anticoagulation data, 38 (70.4%) were discharged on anticoagulation therapy. Warfarin was prescribed in 37 patients (68.5%), while 1 patient (1.9%) received an alternative anticoagulant. Sixteen patients (29.6%) were discharged without anticoagulation.

Among the 8 in-hospital deaths, 6 occurred after urgent or emergent operations and 2 occurred after elective operations. Death occurred on postoperative day 0 in 3 patients and between postoperative days 2 and 73 in the remaining 5 patients. The causes of death reflect the high acuity and complexity of the cohort and included low-output or right ventricular failure, myocardial infarction, perioperative bleeding with pulmonary compromise, respiratory failure after prolonged postoperative support, refractory shock with severe lactic acidosis, and multiorgan failure.

Compared with elective cases, urgent/emergent cases had more preoperative thromboembolism (72.2% vs 42.3%), more New York Heart Association class III/IV symptoms (78.1% vs 60.0%), greater intra-aortic balloon pump use (25.0% vs 3.8%), and higher hospital mortality (16.7% vs 7.7%). Among patients with available anticoagulation data at the time of event (n = 22), 63.6% were receiving warfarin therapy. Overall, 35 of 53 patients (66.0%) continued anticoagulation beyond 3 months after surgery. Documented indications for continued anticoagulation included atrial fibrillation, prior thromboembolism, intracardiac thrombus, hypercoagulable conditions (including antiphospholipid syndrome and factor V deficiency), and recurrent prosthetic valve thrombosis.

Antiplatelet data were abstracted only for patients with bleeding episodes and were not agent-specific. Among the 31 patients with preoperative bleeding indication, 22 (71.0%) had concomitant antiplatelet therapy documented. Among the 16 patients who experienced postoperative bleeding, 12 (75.0%) had antiplatelet therapy documented at the time of bleeding.

When stratified by preoperative indication, the 5-year cumulative incidence of postoperative bleeding or thromboembolism was 32.2% among patients with preoperative thromboembolism only, 29.2% among those with preoperative bleeding only, 10.0% among those with both bleeding and thromboembolism, and 25.0% among those with nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty only (Figure 3). Discussion

In this cohort of patients undergoing conversion from a mechanical valve to a bioprosthetic valve owing to anticoagulation-related complications, 3 central observations emerged. First, redo valve replacement in this setting carried a substantial early hazard, reflecting the acuity and complexity of the referral population. Second, conversion did not reliably achieve freedom from anticoagulation, as most survivors remained exposed to long-term therapy. Third, clinically significant bleeding and thromboembolic events continued during follow-up, indicating that conversion rarely provides definitive resolution of anticoagulation-related complications.

The early hazard observed in this study likely reflects the clinical context in which mechanical-to-bioprosthetic conversion is pursued. Patients often present after major bleeding, recurrent thromboembolism, prosthetic valve thrombosis, or inability to maintain stable anticoagulation. These conditions frequently coexist with substantial comorbidity, including cerebrovascular disease and atrial fibrillation.

When feasible, nonoperative measures were pursued before redo surgery, including treatment of the bleeding source, correction of coagulopathy, anticoagulation interruption or bridging, and medical management of prosthetic valve thrombosis or heart failure. Conversion was generally reserved for persistent bleeding, recurrent thromboembolism or prosthetic valve thrombosis, valve dysfunction, or clinical instability.

In contemporary redo valve series, a history of major bleeding and chronic kidney disease has been identified as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality.9 This finding is particularly relevant in our cohort, in which one-half of the patients underwent conversion for bleeding-related complications. The in-hospital mortality of 12.9% underscores the high-risk clinical setting in which these operations are performed. These findings support framing mechanical-to-bioprosthetic conversion as a high-risk, often salvage intervention in patients with limited alternatives rather than as a straightforward strategy to resolve anticoagulation-related complications.

A central message of this investigation is that valve selection and anticoagulation indication are not synonymous. Current guidelines recommend lifelong vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy for mechanical prostheses and a limited course of anticoagulation after surgical bioprosthetic implantation in selected patients.1,10,11; however, long-term anticoagulation still may be required for conditions independent of the prosthesis, including atrial fibrillation, prior systemic thromboembolism, intracardiac thrombus, venous thromboembolism, and high-risk thrombophilia.

Conversion removes the mechanical valve-specific requirement for lifelong VKA therapy but does not address systemic thrombotic conditions that often predate surgery. In contemporary practice, anticoagulation after surgical bioprosthetic valve implantation remains common, with registry and observational studies demonstrating substantial warfarin use at discharge and during early follow-up.12,13 In our cohort, however, anticoagulation beyond 3 months remained common, suggesting that therapy was driven not solely by postoperative valve-related considerations, but also by persistent patient-specific thrombotic indications.14 This distinction helps explain the continued occurrence of bleeding and thromboembolic events after conversion and reinforces that underlying thrombotic risk, rather than prosthesis type alone, determines long-term anticoagulation exposure.

Even after conversion, postoperative bleeding or thromboembolism remained clinically significant. The cumulative incidence of the composite endpoint approached 25% by 3 years and exceeded 25% by 5 years when accounting for the competing risk of death. Importantly, most events with documented anticoagulation status occurred while patients were receiving warfarin. This observation should not be interpreted as evidence that warfarin caused these events, however. Rather, it underscores the fundamental challenge in this population: patients who remain anticoagulated are often those with the highest baseline thrombotic risk and also may have increased bleeding susceptibility.15 This experience suggests that conversion to a bioprosthesis does not reliably eliminate the competing hazards of thrombosis and bleeding and thus postoperative management requires individualized assessment of thrombotic and hemorrhagic risk rather than an expectation of anticoagulation cessation.

When stratified by preoperative indication, the 5-year cumulative incidence of postoperative bleeding or thromboembolism was similar among patients undergoing conversion for thromboembolism alone and those doing so for bleeding alone. This pattern suggests that postoperative risk is not confined to a single presenting phenotype. The lower incidence observed in patients with both bleeding and thromboembolism should be interpreted cautiously, given the small subgroup size and competing mortality in this high-risk cohort. Similarly, estimates in the nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty subgroup are limited by small patient numbers, and thus these findings are best viewed as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Notably, a subset of patients experienced both bleeding and thromboembolic events during follow-up, further illustrating that anticoagulation in this population is both necessary and hazardous. This dual phenotype reinforces the concept that conversion modifies valve-related risk but does not eliminate systemic drivers of thrombosis and bleeding.

The nonbleeding/nonthromboembolic warfarin-related difficulty in this cohort should not be interpreted solely as medication failure. Rather, it likely reflects the broader clinical complexity of patients in whom thrombotic risk remains substantial despite prior bleeding or thromboembolic complications. Such conditions as atrial fibrillation and high-risk thrombophilia continue to mandate anticoagulation even after valve replacement; for example, long-term VKA therapy remains recommended in high-risk antiphospholipid syndrome.16 In such patients, conversion addresses the prosthesis but not the underlying prothrombotic condition, and thus anticoagulation exposure persists.

Structured risk assessment is essential in this setting. The HAS-BLED score estimates bleeding risk in anticoagulated patients with atrial fibrillation and incorporates prior stroke, renal dysfunction, prior bleeding, advanced age, and labile INR.17 Several of these factors were common in our cohort. However, HAS-BLED does not account for prosthetic valve–specific anticoagulation intensity requirements or high-risk thrombophilia.16,18 Therefore, for patients undergoing conversion, bleeding risk assessment must extend beyond conventional atrial fibrillation tools and integrate the strength of thrombotic indication, anticipated anticoagulation intensity, feasibility of monitoring, and patient preferences.

Conversion to a bioprosthetic valve may broaden anticoagulation options in selected patients whose remaining indication is atrial fibrillation. In this setting, direct oral anticoagulants can be considered beyond the early postoperative period, and a randomized controlled trial supports rivaroxaban as noninferior to warfarin in patients with atrial fibrillation and a bioprosthetic mitral valve.19,20 This flexibility applies only when anticoagulation is required solely for atrial fibrillation, however; in patients with high-risk antiphospholipid syndrome, long-term VKA therapy is recommended.16,21 Thus, for many patients in our cohort, the systemic thrombotic indication rather than the valve prosthesis determined ongoing therapy.

Clinical Implications

Our present findings have important implications for shared decision making. Conversion from a mechanical valve to a bioprosthetic valve for anticoagulation-related complications is associated with appreciable early operative mortality and morbidity, particularly in nonelective settings. It should not be presented as a definitive strategy to eliminate anticoagulation, as persistent systemic indications frequently remain. Finally, postoperative bleeding and thromboembolism remain clinically relevant long-term risks, and the postoperative complication profile may differ from the preoperative indication. Therefore, transparent counseling should emphasize realistic expectations and individualized assessment of ongoing thrombotic and bleeding risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and long study era introduced selection bias and evolving practice patterns in surgery and anticoagulation management. Event characterization was constrained by clinical documentation, particularly for chronic or recurrent processes, such as gastrointestinal bleeding with anemia. Our composite endpoint and time to first event approach were appropriate for a heterogeneous cohort but may underestimate the total event burden in patients with repeated episodes. Finally, the cohort size limited multivariable modeling and subgroup comparisons, and INR values at the time of bleeding or thromboembolic events were not consistently available.

Conclusions

Mechanical to bioprosthetic valve conversion for anticoagulation-related complications is associated with substantial early risk and does not reliably eliminate anticoagulation exposure. Persistent anticoagulation after conversion is common because many patients have systemic thrombotic indications that remain after the valve is replaced. These same comorbidities may also contribute to unstable anticoagulation control and bleeding vulnerability. Our findings support careful preoperative counseling that emphasizes the likelihood of ongoing anticoagulation, the continued risk of both bleeding and thromboembolic complications after conversion, and shared decision making aligned with patient goals.
