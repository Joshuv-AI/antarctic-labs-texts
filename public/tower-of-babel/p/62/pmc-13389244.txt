Paclobutrazol and planting density improved onion growth, reduced lodging and enhanced seed yield and quality in mid-hills of Himachal Pradesh
Authors: Kamrul Nissa, Rajender Sharma, Narender Kumar Bharat, Devinder Kumar Mehta, Priyanka Thakur, Arshi Sultanpuri, Anubhav Thakur, Nitin Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the increasing demand for Onion globally, enhancing seed production is crucial. In India, mid hill regions of Himachal Pradesh offer favorable conditions for onion seed production. The experimental site in Himachal Pradesh is located at an altitude of 1183 m above mean sea level in the mid-hill sub-humid temperate agro-climatic zone. The region is characterized by mild summers and cool winters, receiving an average annual rainfall of 1000–1300 mm. During the growing season, the maximum temperature ranges from 27.1 to 29.0 °C while the minimum temperature varies between 1.5 °C and 19.4 °C. However, frequent lodging of scapes due to western disturbances hampers seed yield and quality. A two-year field study (2022–2023 and 2023–2024) evaluated the efficacy of paclobutrazol (PBZ) bulb dipping as a solution in onion cv. Palam Lohit. Evidence from study indicated that bulb dipping in 100 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm (T12), yielded optimal outcomes. This treatment led to reduced seed scape height (71.42 cm), enhanced leaf greenness (83.39 SPAD), delayed flowering (131.67 days) and minimized lodging (18.92%). Moreover, it achieved the highest plot yield (156.00 g) and economic return with a benefit-cost ratio of 3.26:1. Thus, bulb dipping in 100 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm (T12) is recommended to enhance onion seed yield and quality under challenging ago- climatic conditions, after multi-location validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45980-6.Keywords: Bulb, Dipping, Height reduction, Onion, Physiology, Seed, Seed qualitySubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Onion is often honored as the “Queen of Kitchens” due to their vital role as a core ingredient in nearly every home kitchen1–4. The term “onion” comes from the Latin word unio meaning single or one, reflecting its characteristic of producing a solitary bulb5. In India, they stand as the second most consumed vegetable and carry significant political weight i.e., they are deeply woven into political fabric as a result dramatic price surges have triggered government collapses, prompting authorities to enforce price ceilings, limit storage and impose export restrictions to stabilize the market6,7. Hence, onion thrives only with premium quality planting material i.e., true botanical seed8. Currently, states like Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh produce the majority of onion seeds. With a share of over 29.55%, Maharashtra is in first place, followed by Madhya Pradesh (17.6%), Karnataka (10.2%), Gujarat (9.8%), Bihar (5.7%) and Rajasthan (5.2%)8,9.

Even though they make significant contributions, they are unable to satisfy the nation’s need for high-quality seed. Finding new areas that produce seeds has therefore become crucial. Potential exists in North India but yields there are adversely affected by a variety of constraints such as the micro-biotic nature of seeds, scape lodging and low storability8,10.

Onion is vital globally for nutrition and economics, yet scape lodging due to climatic disturbances hampers seed yield and quality. Regions like Ethiopia11, Brazil12 and Chile13 also grapple with scape lodging, inconsistent seed quality and climate-related stresses, highlighting the global relevance. Onion seed umbels develop at the top of long hollow stalks (scapes), often reaching heights of 0.9–1.2 m in varieties like Palam Lohit14. These scapes rise nearly perpendicular to the plant core, making them vulnerable to lodging especially under the strong winds common in North India during Western disturbance which often leads to stalk collapse and consequently compromises seed yield and quality15. To prevent unwanted vegetative growth without lowering plant production and offering any phytotoxicity risks plant growth retardants are employed in both horticultural as well as agronomic crops16.

Paclobutrazol, a compound consisting of a β-(4-chlorophenyl) methyl group attached to an α-(1,1-dimethyl)-substituted 1 H-1,2,4-triazole ring linked to an ethanol moiety is a major growth retardant which exists under multiple commercial names including PP 333, cultar, bonze, sadabahar, parley and clipper17–19. This compound has been used to decrease onion scape height and improve seed production20. PBZ targets ent-kaurene oxidase, an enzyme crucial for GA biosynthesis, by blocking the oxidation of ent-kaurene to ent-kaurenoic acid21,22. Beyond reducing plant height and preventing lodging, PBZ offers numerous benefits such as increased crop productivity, reduced evapo-transpiration and leaf area, all while altering the crucial balance of plant hormones like Gibberellic acid (GA), Abscisic acid (ABA) and cytokinins19,23. However, its application methods particularly bulb dipping under specific environmental conditions, remain underexplored.

Similarly, planting density influences both seed yield and quality by altering canopy structure, light interception and photosynthetic efficiency24. Adequately spaced onion plants in the field are essential to maximize the number of plants per unit area while simultaneously improving the quality of the seeds produced. Planting arrangement also significantly alters the microclimate of a given area25. While an increased plant population density (PPD) can negatively affect the growth and yield of individual plants but it has the opposite effect on yield per unit area26,27. It is based on the principle of intercepting over 95% of available photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) at the ideal plant population density (PPD). Increasing PPD beyond this point does not further enhance yield due to a decline in radiation usage efficiency28,29. Although importance of planting density in Onion is well established at a global scale, empirical studies that investigate the interaction between density optimization and growth regulator application in region-specific contexts remain limited. Hence, this integrated study was conducted to assess how these two factors together influence onion seed yield and quality offering insights applicability to similar climatic zones worldwide.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The research was carried out during the Rabi seasons of 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 at Dr. Yashwant Singh Parmar, University of Horticulture and Forestry, Nauni, Solan, Himachal Pradesh, India. The field trial was conducted at an experimental farm in Pandah (Nauni), located in the mid-hill zone of Himachal Pradesh, at an elevation of 1183 m above mean sea level (amsl). The geographic coordinates of the farm were recorded as 30.51 °N latitude and 77.09 °E longitude using a GARMIN GPS 12 Personal Navigator. The region has a sub-temperate and semi-humid climate, with harsh winters. Planting material, preparation and application of paclobutrazol (PBZ)

The bulbs of the variety Palam Lohit released by Chaudhary Sarwan Kumar Himachal Pradesh Krishi Vishvavidyalaya Palampur were used in the experiment. The selection of this variety was based on its long seed scape height which renders it more vulnerable to lodging under adverse climatic conditions. However, variety is characterized by its high-yielding attribute and moderate resistance to downy mildew along with the mid-hill climate of Himachal Pradesh being particularly favorable for its cultivation. Therefore, disease-free bulbs of medium size (2.5 cm in diameter) were used in the experiment. Initially, a stock solution of paclobutrazol (10000 ppm) was prepared by adding 43.5 ml of PBZ (Cultar 23 EC) to 1000 L of water. For each treatment, 50, 100 and 150 ml of the stock solution were added to 10,000 ml of water, resulting in paclobutrazol concentrations of 50, 100 and 150 ppm. The selected medium-sized, disease-free bulbs were submerged in these solutions for 6, 12–18 h and then shade-dried before being planted in the field at two spacing i.e., 45 × 30 and 30 × 30 cm together with dry bulbs as a control.

Experimental design

The field trial consisted of bulb dipping in paclobutrazol at various concentrations (50, 100 and 150 ppm) for three distinct periods (6, 12 and 18 h) and planting at two different spacing (45 × 30 and 30 × 30 cm). The experiment used a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with 20 treatment combinations (Table 1), each replicated three times and a plot size of 1.8 × 1.2 m. As a result, the wider spacing (45 × 30 cm) accommodated 16 bulbs per plot, while the closer spacing (30 × 30 cm) enabled 24 bulbs per plot. Following the field trial, seeds harvested were subjected to quality and antioxidant activity analysis in the laboratory, employing a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) with four replications.

Data acquisition

Data were collected from10 randomly selected uniform plants in a plot at the vegetative, bolting, flowering and harvesting stages (Table 2). At the harvesting stage when seeds inside the capsule had turned black and 25–30% of the seeds were exposed on the umbel, the umbels were carefully excised leaving 5–7 cm flower stalks. Seed extraction was conducted by gently beating and hand-rubbing the umbels. The seeds were then cleaned and dried for 3–4 days until the seed moisture content decreased to below 8%. These seeds were subsequently subjected to laboratory testing for seed quality and antioxidant activity.

Seed biochemical traits

Freshly harvested seeds were subjected to antioxidant activity assays to measure various enzyme activities. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assessed by monitoring the decrease in optical density of formazone formed by the superoxide radical and nitro-blue tetrazolium dye, with absorbance recorded at 560 nm as described by30. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that reduced absorbance by 50%. The specific activity of the enzyme was expressed as units g− 1 of fresh weight. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by monitoring the disappearance of H2O2 at 240 nm, as outlined by31, with specific activity expressed as mmol H2O2 reduced min− 1 g− 1 of fresh weight. Peroxidase (POX) activity in seeds was determined by observing the formation of tetraguaiacol from guaiacol at 470 nm, following the procedure of32, with specific activity expressed as tetraguaiacol formed µmol min− 1 g− 1 of fresh weight. Glutathione reductase activity was estimated based on the formation of a red-colored complex with 5,5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB), as described by33, with the increase in absorbance recorded at 412 nm. The specific activity was expressed as µmol min− 1 g− 1 of fresh weight.

Statistical analysis

The field experiment was structured using a randomized block design to control for variability among experimental units. IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan Multiple Range Test (DMRT) were used to compare treatment means. Microsoft Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) was used to create error bar graphs and descriptive statistics such as means and standard deviations. R software version 4.3.0 was used for additional studies, including the construction of boxplots and a correlation matrix to evaluate linear correlations among variables.

Results

Morphological traits

Figure 2 shows box plots of pooled data from both growing seasons, illustrating the effects of paclobutrazol (PBZ) application and planting density on key morphological traits. The shortest seed scape height (71.42 cm) was observed in T18 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm) which was statistically at par with T16 (72.17 cm) indicating that high PBZ combined with close spacing reduces plant height. The tallest scapes (100.17 cm) occurred in T19 (Control + spacing 45 × 30 cm) (p < 0.05) showing that wider spacing without PBZ promotes elongation. Maximum seed scape diameter (45.17 mm) was recorded in T17 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm), which was statistically at par with T18 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm) (44.22 mm) while the smallest (28.09 mm) was in T20 (Control + spacing 30 × 30 cm), highlighting the stronger effect of PBZ over spacing. The lowest number of scapes per plant (4.67) occurred in T18, whereas the highest (8.50) was in T19, suggesting PBZ suppresses reproductive growth under dense planting. Lodging was lowest (18.92%) in T17 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm) and highest (43.83%) in T20 (Control + spacing 30 × 30 cm) showing that PBZ especially at 150 ppm improves lodging resistance with wider spacing further enhancing the effect (p < 0.05).

Reproductive traits

The pooled analysis of reproductive traits showed that higher concentrations and longer dipping duration of paclobutrazol (PBZ) delayed both the initiation of bolting and 50% bolting in onion (Fig. 3). Planting density had no significant effect on these parameters. The longest delays were recorded in T18 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm) with 131.67 days to bolting and 148.50 days to 50% bolting which was statistically similar to T17 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm) (p < 0.05). Treatments with 150 ppm PBZ for short durations and 100 ppm PBZ for longer durations also delayed bolting but to a lesser extent. The earliest bolting occurred in the controls T19 and T20, confirming that PBZ primarily controls floral initiation. Analysis of umbel traits showed that both PBZ and planting density influenced umbel size and umbellet number. The largest umbels (10.75 cm) and highest umbellet count (666.17) were recorded in T15 (150 ppm PBZ for 12 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm), while the smallest umbels (7.52 cm) and lowest umbellet number (494.50) were in T20 (Control + spacing 30 × 30 cm) (p < 0.05). Wider spacing combined with higher PBZ improved umbel development in onion seed production.

Effect of paclobutrazol concentrations and planting density on reproductive characteristics: (a) days taken to initiation of bolting, (b) days taken to 50% bolting, (c) umbel diameter (cm) and (d) number of umbellets per umbel.

Physiological traits

Table 3 presents pooled data for physiological traits in Onion treated with different paclobutrazol (PBZ) concentrations, durations and planting densities over 2022–2024. PBZ significantly influenced all traits, while planting density had little effect. The highest chlorophyll content (SPAD 83.39) was recorded in T18 (150 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm) which was statistically at par with T15 (82.78) (p < 0.05). The lowest chlorophyll contents were in untreated controls T19 (56.67) and T20 (48.83), indicating that PBZ enhances leaf greenness. Transpiration rate (TR) decreased with higher PBZ with minimum in T18 (0.0026 mm H2O m−2 s−1) and maximum in T19 (0.0080 mm H2O m−2 s−1). Stomatal conductance (gs) followed a similar trend, lowest in T18 (0.106 mmol m−2 s−1) and highest in T19 and T20 (0.952 and 0.819 mmol m−2 s−1). Net photosynthetic rate (Pn) was highest in T11 (24.81 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1) and lowest in T18 (12.85 µmol CO2 m−2 s−1) (p < 0.05), suggesting that very high PBZ under close spacing may slightly reduce photosynthesis. Photosynthetic efficiency, measured by the Ci/Ca ratio, was highest in PBZ-treated plots (lowest ratio in T11, 0.46) and lowest in controls (T20, 1.05), confirming PBZ’s role in improving photosynthetic performance.

Productivity traits

The application of paclobutrazol (PBZ) combined with varied planting spacing significantly influenced key reproductive parameters. The lowest ovule-to-seed ratio (6.83), indicating higher seed recovery efficiency was observed in T15 (150 ppm PBZ for 12 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm) which was statistically comparable to T13 (7.00) and T11 (7.56) (Fig. 4). The highest ratio (11.00) occurred in the untreated control at T20 (Control + spacing 30 × 30 cm), while T19 (Control + spacing 45 × 30 cm) showed an improved ratio of 8.67. Seed setting percentage followed a similar trend; T15 achieved the highest value (71.50%), followed by treatments under wider spacing (T11, T13, T9, T17 and T7) (p < 0.05). The lowest percentage (48.50%) was recorded in T20, whereas T19 showed improved performance (57.33%). Maximum seed yield per plant (8.33 g) was observed in T11 (100 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm), statistically similar to T15 (8.25 g). The lowest yield (4.67 g) occurred in T20, while T19 showed a significantly higher yield (5.50 g) emphasizing the positive impact of wider spacing. Thousand-seed weight was highest in T15 (3.35 g) followed by T13 (3.14 g) and T11 (3.09 g), while the lowest (2.08 g) was recorded in T20. Closer spacing increased seed yield per plot, with highest yield in T12 (156.00 g) under close spacing, followed by T16 (153.40 g), T14 (148.00 g) and T10 (146.00 g). The lowest yield per plot (88.00 g) was observed in T19 which was significantly lower than T20 (112.00 g) (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that wider spacing improves seed yield per plant likely through enhanced thousand-seed weight, while closer spacing boosts yield per plot due to higher plant density and PBZ application enhances productivity under both spacing regimes (Fig. 4).

Seed quality traits

Figure 5 shows error graphs of pooled seed quality traits analyzed using one-way ANOVA at p < 0.05. The results demonstrate that paclobutrazol (PBZ) application combined with wider spacing significantly improved seed quality. Treatment T15 (150 ppm PBZ for 12 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm) recorded the highest germination percentage (90.38%), statistically similar to T13 (89.13%), T11 (88.88%) and T9 (87.13%) (p < 0.05). The lowest germination (80.13%) occurred in the untreated control (T20). Seedling length was greatest in T15 (15.50 cm), followed by T11 (14.94 cm) and T13 (14.78 cm), while the shortest seedlings (7.29 cm) were observed in T20. T19 (Control + spacing 45 × 30 cm) showed improved length (9.99 cm) over T20 (p < 0.05). Seedling dry weight was highest in T15 (2.96 mg), comparable to T11 (2.85 mg) and T13 (2.83 mg), whereas T20 recorded the lowest value (2.01 mg), with T19 showing moderate improvement (2.30 mg). Seed vigour indices I and II were also significantly enhanced by PBZ and wider spacing. T15 achieved the highest values (1400.88 and 267.87), statistically similar to T11 and T13 (p < 0.05). The lowest indices were in T20 (583.65 and 161.11), while T19 showed notably better results underscoring the benefits of wider spacing even without PBZ. These findings highlight the combined effect of PBZ treatment and optimal spacing in improving seed vigour and overall seed quality.

Seed biochemical traits

After assessing seed quality, the seeds were analyzed for antioxidant enzyme activity. The pooled data are shown as a scatter plot in Fig. 6, with significant differences marked based on one-way ANOVA at p < 0.05. The results show that enzyme activity increased with higher PBZ concentrations used for bulb dipping especially under closer spacing. Figure 6 presents the data on antioxidant enzymes. The highest superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (2.02 U g−1 FW) was recorded in T16, where bulbs were dipped in 150 ppm PBZ for 12 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm. This was statistically similar to T12 (2.00 U g−1 FW), which used 100 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm. Catalase (CAT) activity was also highest in T16 (1.74 mmol min−1 g−1 FW), closely followed by T12 (1.65 mmol min−1 g−1 FW). Peroxidase activity reached its peak in T16 (0.72 µmol min−1 g−1 FW) with comparable results in T12, T15, T10 and T11. Glutathione reductase (GR) activity was highest in T16 (0.37 µmol min−1 g−1 FW) and similar in T12, T14 and T18. The lowest enzyme activities were found in the control treatment T19 (Control + spacing 45 × 30 cm), with SOD at 1.00 U g−1 FW, CAT at 1.10 mmol min−1 g−1 FW, peroxidase at 0.49 µmol min−1 g−1 FW and GR at 0.23 µmol min−1 g−1 FW. These results suggest that higher PBZ concentrations combined with closer spacing enhance antioxidant enzyme activity possibly due to stress conditions in these treatments.

Economics of seed production

The economic analysis indicated that treatment T12 (100 ppm PBZ for 18 h + spacing 30 × 30 cm) was the most profitable, with highest gross return (USD 10400.04), net return (USD 7027.46) and benefit-to-cost ratio of 3.26. In contrast, T19 (Control + spacing 45 × 30 cm) recorded lowest gross returns (USD 5957.10), net returns (USD 3012.90) and benefit-to-cost ratios of 2.14 (Table 4).

Discussion

Influence of paclobutrazol bulb treatment and planting density on seed yield and associated yield parameters

Paclobutrazol (PBZ), a plant growth regulator derived from triazoles, effectively reduces plant height in a dose-dependent manner by suppressing the biosynthesis of gibberellins. This occurs through the blockage of the conversion of ent-kaurene to ent-kauronoic acid via cytochrome P450 monooxygenase inhibition19. Bulb dipping in 150 ppm PBZ (T18) resulted in the greatest suppression of seed scape height (p < 0.05), likely due to both PBZ application and closer plant spacing, which heightened competition for light, nutrients and water. PBZ reduces internode length and cell proliferation, contributing to compact growth34. At higher concentrations, PBZ limits the number of seed scapes, possibly redirecting resources to fewer healthier ones. Anatomically, PBZ-treated plants show increased stem thickness, enhanced epicuticular wax deposition and enlarged mesophyll and epidermal cells leading to sturdier stems. These structural changes reduce lodging by strengthening plant architecture. Comparable height-reducing effects of PBZ in onion have been documented by10.

Paclobutrazol (PBZ) application significantly delayed initiation and 50% bolting in onion independent of planting density due to gibberellins biosynthesis inhibition which suppresses floral primordia development. This delay was more pronounced at higher PBZ concentrations. The delay in bolting observed in this study aligns with similar research by35] and [10, who also reported delayed bolting in Onion treated with paclobutrazol. Concurrently, PBZ-treated plants exhibited increased umbel diameter, attributed to altered hormonal balance reduced gibberellins and enhanced cytokinins favoring reproductive over vegetative growth19. PBZ also influences DNA methylation during floral transition, enhancing flower development36. Consequently, treated plants showed improved resource allocation to fewer, larger umbels with greater flower density. Wider spacing (45 × 30 cm) further enhanced umbel diameter and umbellet number by reducing inter-plant competition. Treatments combining PBZ (100–150 ppm) with wider spacing (T15, T11 and T13) (p < 0.05) yielded the most pronounced improvements in reproductive traits.

The ovule to seed ratio in onion, set by umbels × flowers × ovules gauges seed recovery and overall productivity. Because each flower bears six ovules, full seed set is attainable only under favorable flowering stage conditions; low temperatures, heavy rain and high humidity impede fertilization and raise ovule to seed ratio by fostering diseases37. Plant spacing modulates these microclimatic risks: narrow 30 × 30 cm grids restrict airflow, intensify humidity and heighten pathogen pressure, whereas wider 45 × 30 cm spacing improves aeration, lowers disease incidence, and reduces resource competition, thereby decreasing ovule to seed ratio and boosting seed set percentage38. Paclobutrazol (PBZ) further enhances reproductive efficiency. By inhibiting gibberellins synthesis, PBZ shortens scapes, curtails lodging, enlarges umbels and increases umbellet number, collectively improving pollen delivery and stigma receptivity and pollinator activity10.

The T11 and T15 treatments, PBZ at 100–150 ppm combined with 45 × 30 cm spacing achieved the greatest seed yield per plant (p < 0.05) through synergistic reductions in vegetative growth and improved reproductive allocation. Nonetheless, high PBZ doses or prolonged bulb dips (150 ppm for 18 h) can depress yield. Conversely, closer spacing (30 × 30 cm) produced significantly higher seed yield per plot than wider spacing (45 × 30 cm) due to a greater number of plants per plot i.e. 24 versus 16, resulting in higher overall seed yield. Additionally, the positive impact of paclobutrazol on yield may also be linked to enhanced leaf photosynthesis, thousand seed weight and increased chlorophyll content39. Improved seed yield and thousand seed weight by enhancing photosynthesis, nutrient uptake and assimilate transport is shown in studies on Camelina sativa and Brassica napus40,41. The increase in thousand seed weight with wider plant spacing, due to reduced competition and enhanced nutrient availability is in agreement with the observations reported in onion by42] and [43.

Influence of paclobutrazol bulb dipping and plant spacing on physiological parameters

Paclobutrazol (PBZ) significantly increased relative chlorophyll content in onion leaves, primarily through cytokinin-induced chloroplast development and reduced gibberellins-mediated cell expansion. PBZ also promotes chlorophyll biosynthesis by enhancing phytol production and redirecting GGPP towards chlorophyll, carotenoids and tocopherol synthesis. Planting density had minimal effect on chlorophyll levels19,44. These results are consistent with previous findings in various crops including onion10, sunflower45 and peanut46. Moreover, paclobutrazol (PBZ) application significantly reduced transpiration rate and stomatal conductance, suggesting enhanced stomatal regulation and improved water retention47.

PBZ-treated plants also exhibited increased chlorophyll content, which is closely associated with greater light absorption and photosynthetic capacity, thereby enhancing net photosynthetic rate (Pn)48. Additionally, a lower Ci/Ca ratio observed in treated plants reflects more efficient CO2 fixation and stronger photosynthetic performance under limited gas exchange conditions49. Hence, the overall impact of PBZ aligns with previous findings that triazole compounds help plants reduce water loss and adapt better to drought stress50.

Effect of paclobutrazol bulb dipping and plant spacing on seed quality parameters

Seeds harvested from bulbs treated with PBZ exhibited superior characteristics, including higher 1000-seed weight, greater vigour and improved overall health, likely due to enhanced seed maturity and nutrient allocation under wider spacing. PBZ is known to influence assimilate partitioning and resource distribution, facilitating the accumulation of carbohydrates and proteins in developing seeds. This mechanism is supported by51, who observed increased assimilate translocation to reproductive organs in Brassica species. Additionally, PBZ enhances root and shoot development, leading to improved nutrient uptake and seed filling19. Its role in delaying leaf senescence further contributes to prolonged photosynthesis and effective remobilization of nutrients to seeds, thereby enhancing seed yield and quality52,53. The resulting seeds likely possessed elevated levels of metabolites and enzymatic activity, promoting efficient mobilization of stored reserves and contributing to the production of more vigorous seedlings with greater seedling length and dry weights. Moreover elevated SVI-I values with bulb dipping in 150 ppm PBZ for 12 h + spacing 45 × 30 cm were due to improved germination percentages and increased seedling lengths, indicating the production of seeds with enhanced potential to establish and grow under suboptimal conditions. Similarly, the higher SVI-II values reflected the positive influence of paclobutrazol which promoted better assimilate distribution, facilitating greater reserve accumulation in seeds, which translated into higher 1000-seed weight and more vigorous seedlings. These results align with the findings of10] and [38 who similarly observed improved seed vigour indices in onion.

Influence of paclobutrazol bulb dipping and plant spacing on antioxidant enzymes

In response to stress, plants produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can harm cellular components. To mitigate this damage, antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and glutathione reductase (GR) become active. Paclobutrazol (PBZ), induces moderate oxidative stress that stimulates these enzymatic defenses22. SOD initiates detoxification by converting superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, which CAT rapidly decomposes into water and oxygen, each CAT molecule can process up to 6 million H2O2molecules per second54. POD further detoxifies H2O2 and promotes lignin synthesis, reinforcing cell walls, while GR maintains redox homeostasis by recycling glutathione i.e., a process supported by PBZ-induced elevation of the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio55,56.

Furthermore, the increased competition for nutrients and resources in closer plant spacing generates mild abiotic stress, which synergistically amplifies the antioxidant enzyme response. Therefore, the concurrent application of PBZ and reduced spacing acts as a dual stimulus, significantly elevating antioxidant defences and enhancing the plant’s capacity to withstand oxidative damage. These findings are consistent with previous reports documenting similar increases in antioxidant enzyme activities under PBZ treatment and stress conditions in onion10 and pea54.

Conclusion

This study highlights the synergistic effect of paclobutrazol (PBZ) application and plant spacing in improving onion seed yield and quality under mid-hill agro-climatic conditions of Himachal Pradesh. The novel combination of bulb dipping in 100 ppm PBZ for 18 h with appropriate planting density significantly enhanced growth, reproductive attributes photosynthetic efficiency, seed yield and quality in the Palam Lohit cultivar. Wider spacing (45 × 30 cm) improved individual plant performance and seed quality traits, while closer spacing (30 × 30 cm) increased seed yield per plot and hectare as well as antioxidant enzyme activity contributing to better stress tolerance and economic viability. The proposed treatment offers a reproducible and cost-effective strategy. This innovative and practical approach offers farmers and seed producers a reproducible, low-cost solution to maximize productivity, paving the way for sustainable seed production in resource-limited environments.
