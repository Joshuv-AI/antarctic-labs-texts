Advancing nutritional quality in oilseed crops through genome editing: a comprehensive review
Authors: Asmamaw Menelih, Abayeneh Girma, Aleka Aemiro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenome editing has emerged as a powerful approach to enhancing the nutritional quality of oilseed crops. Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats case9 (CRISPR/Cas9) is the predominant editing tool, while transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs) and base editors are used less commonly. Key fatty acid desaturase genes such as FAD2 and FAD3 are prime targets because of their critical functions in fatty acid desaturation. This review summarizes recent progress in editing genes associated with oil composition and related traits across oilseed species. Visual data representations including, Sankey diagrams, heat maps, and crop-trait matrices illustrate shared editing priorities and emerging trait targets across crops. Despite its promise, genome editing still faces challenges in transformation efficiency, field-level validation, and regulatory acceptance. This review underscores the increasing impact of target gene editing on nutritional trait improvement and its potential to accelerate the development of healthier, more sustainable oilseed varieties.

Introduction

Oilseed crops are an important part of world agriculture as they contain not only edible oils which are used by humans but also proteins that are highly nutritious and essential micronutrients that are utilized as food, animal feed and industrial material.1 Some of the most significant oilseeds that are grown across the world include soybean (Glycine max), rapeseed (Brassica napus), sunflower (Helianthus annuus), peanut (Arachis hypogaea), safflower (Carthamus tinctorius), Camelina (Camelina sativa) and oil palm (Elaeis guineensis).2

Nevertheless, most oilseeds also have nutritional drawbacks, including an unbalanced fatty acid composition, the presence of antinutritional elements, and low concentration of the essential amino acids and vitamins.3 These restrictions do not only decrease the nutritional value of oilseeds but also restrict their further use in fighting malnutrition and addressing the increasing need of plants as a source of sustainable and plant-based protein.4

In the past, traditional breeding techniques have been crucial to the success of oilseed crop breeding efforts to improve nutritional value. These methods often involve laborious, sometimes inaccurate selection and cross-breeding procedures.5 Some of these approaches are limited by genetic diversity, making it difficult to select desirable traits that involve multiple genes related to complex nutritional factors.6 As a result, there is a growing need for more precise and efficient means to increase the nutritional value of these crops.

These problems highlight the necessity to explore new ways of breeding strategy, and genome editing technology, like CRISPR/Cas9, has become an effective solution to overcome the constraints of traditional breeding processes in oilseeds. This innovative approach allows researchers to make specific changes to genomes and alter traits such as fatty acid composition, protein quality, and micronutrients with unparalleled precision.7 For example, CRISPR/Cas9 can be used to increase the levels of desirable fatty acids, such as alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), in oilseed plants, which offers known health benefits.8 Additionally, genome editing can improve the resilience of oilseed crops to both biotic and abiotic stresses, increasing their productivity and sustainability to climate changes.9

Despite the potential implications of transforming oilseed crops, genome editing continues to face numerous obstacles.10 like regulatory hurdles, community acceptance, and the possibility of off-target effects are all areas that need to be addressed to encourage more widespread adoption of these innovations.11 Furthermore, ethical issues raised by genetic alterations such as transgenic techniques or more recent genome editing technologies are controversial among a range of stakeholders, including farmers, consumers, and policymakers.9

The present review work provides an integrated approach toward connecting the editing platforms with the enhancement of nutritional traits, field performance, and regulation elements. Specifically, it presents new visualization methods like gene-trait-crop functional maps and CRISPR impact matrices that reflect the priorities shared by all crops and innovations tailored to crops. This two-fold focus on technical innovations and translational avenues is to inform both the plant scientists and policymakers and industry stakeholders concerned with the prospective implementation of nutritionally enriched oilseed crops.

The Future of Oilseed Crop Improvement

The conventional methods of breeding have struggle to meet the rising demand of oil yield, quality and resistance to stresses. These traditional methods are limited by the narrow genetic pool, lengthy breeding depression, and polygenic nature of desirable traits (such as oil contents and disease resistance) which depend on environmental interactions.12 These factors are also exacerbated by the pressure of climate change, new pathogens, and sustainable agricultural approaches, which support more efficient and specific breeding strategies.13

The recent development of genomic technologies, such as next-generation sequencing, genome editing, such as TALENs, ZFNs, CRISPR/Cas systems, base editing and prime editing technologies, has introduced a new era in the optimization of oilseed crops.14;15 The breakthroughs allow the accurate identification and manipulation of genes that regulate important agronomic and nutritional traits, which increases the speed of producing cultivars with better quality oil, improved nutritional content, and increased resistance to biotic and abiotic stress.16;17 Furthermore, genetic variation and natural variation, especially in wild relatives, is a virgin source of crop resilience and productivity.18 Since the global demand for healthier oils and climate-smart farming is increasing, the integration of conventional breeding strategies with modern genomic technologies will be pivotal in shaping the future of oilseed crop improvement.

Limitations of Traditional Breeding and Emerging Needs

The conventional plant breeding of oilseed crops has significantly enhanced traits such as yield, oil content and resistance to diseases. However, its efficiency is limited by a number of factors such as long breeding patterns, dependence on the available natural variation, and genetic complexity of many economically valuable traits.19 These traits, such as oil content and resistance to pests, are controlled by many genes, so it is very difficult to obtain predictable results via conventional methods. Furthermore, the traditional breeding method is slow by nature, which limits its ability to quickly adjust to shifting market demands, changing environmental conditions, and the emergence of new diseases and pests.

The intensive selection of many oilseed crops, has narrowed their genetic base. Such non-diversification can result in a lower adaptive potential against both biotic and abiotic stressors.12 For example, the repeated cultivation of various high-yielding soybean cultivars has made this crop more susceptible to emerging diseases.20

The majority of the most desirable traits of oilseed crops, such as yield, oil content, and resistance to diseases, are polygenic, meaning that they are controlled by many genes. The complex inheritance of these traits limit their effective combination through traditional breeding methods.21 This could lead to unintended consequences, and one of them could be a loss in desirable traits when choosing others.

Furthermore, the expression of traits in oilseed crops is strongly influenced by genotype environment (G x E) interaction, which reduces predictability under conventional breeding. Such interactions may not be well captured by the conventional selection criteria, resulting in some varieties that may be very good under some conditions but not very good under others.22 This diversity may be detrimental to the effective development of crop resilient varieties that can perform well under a variety of conditions.

The traditional method of breeding is not only time-consuming and labor-intensive but also costly, requiring extensive resource to perform a long period of field testing to identify the suitable breeding lines,23 and also limit the progress of breeding programs, especially in the developing world.

Traditional breeding approaches, although historically effective, are generally slow and resource demanding. However, the dependence on these methods has led to the opposition to the adoption of modern breeding technologies. The appearance of such counterarguments may be an obstacle to the timely adoption of innovations aimed at improving efficiency, effective use of resources and reducing the time to achieve breeding goals.24

The drawbacks of conventional breeding have become more pronounced as climate change and increasing food demands have become more challenging for agriculture.25 These limitations underscore the urgent need for precision approaches that can target specific alleles, accelerate trait pyramiding, and reduce reliance on long introgression pipelines.

Integrating modern tools such as genomics-assisted breeding (GAB), speed breeding, and high-throughput phenomics helps to overcome these temporal and genetic limitations. For example, speed breeding (SB) has been shown to reduce generation time by three- to fivefold when combined with optimized light, temperature, and photoperiod regimes, enabling breeders to achieve many more generations per year under controlled conditions.26 High-throughput phenomics improves the accuracy and resolution of trait measurement, reducing environmental noise and enabling earlier or even nondestructive phenotyping, which in turn enhances selection efficiency.27

Genome editing, especially CRISPR-based platforms, also provides such an opportunity by directly modifying alleles controlling oil composition, protein quality, or anti-nutritional compounds in a single breeding cycle. These technologies offer a more pinpointed and even efficient means of targeting and integrating desirable traits and thus can eliminate the genetic and temporal limitations of conventional breeding.28 This combination of modern technologies based on methods of bioengineering and conventional technologies is increasingly needed to satisfy the needs of the sustainable production of oilseed crops.

Technologies for Editing the Genome of Oilseed Crops

Genome editing has emerged as a transformative approach in modern plant breeding, offering unparalleled precision in the modification of genetic sequences.29 Compared to traditional breeding that employs random recombination and prolonged selection mechanisms, genome editing allows the directed manipulation of specific genes that are linked to relevant agronomic and nutritional characteristics. These instruments have been utilized in oilseed crops to increase the quality of oil, resistance to pests and diseases, abiotic stress tolerance, and the production of better cultivars.30

Oilseed genomes can now be manipulated with genome editing tools such as ZFNs, TALENs, and CRISPR/Cas systems which have made precise manipulation of the genome of oil seeds possible. Although the feasibility of targeted mutagenesis was established by both ZFNs and TALENs, they were too complex and less efficient to be used on a large scale. In contrast, CRISPR/Cas platforms, with their simplicity, scalability, and ability to multiplex, have become the dominant choice for oilseed improvement. Base editing and prime editing have more recently made available a greater group of precision tools, allowing single-nucleotide alterations and small indels without breaking the two strands. They are especially applicable to nutritional characteristics in oilseeds where fine allelic adjustments (e.g. in FAD2 or raffinose synthase genes) can result in big changes in the quality and digestibility of the oil.

Zinc Ninger Nucleases (ZFNs)

ZFNs are genome editing tools that allow site-specific DNA cleavages using a site-specific double-strand break (DSB).31 The ZFNs are made up of a DNA-binding domain which contains several zinc finger motifs each specific to a three-nucleotide sequence combined with a FokI endonuclease cleavage domain that cuts the DNA.32 Zinc finger domains have a modular design that can be used to create highly specific nucleases that can target any genomic sequence of interest. After cleavage, the intrinsic repair pathways within the cell, either non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) or homology-directed repair (HDR), helps to disrupt, insert, or repair a gene.33

ZFNs were one of the first programmable nucleases used in genome editing and have been effectively utilized in plants, animals, and human cells. ZFNs have also been used in plants as targeted gene knockouts, as a tool to alter metabolic pathways, and enhance traits like resistance to disease and herbicides.34 In spite of these achievements, ZFNs are not without limitations such as technical complexity of designing zinc finger arrays, high costs and possibility of off-target effects.33 Since the introduction of CRISPR-based methods, ZFNs have been mostly replaced, but continue to play a significant role in the development of the specific technologies of genome editing.

Transcription Activator-Like Effector Nucleases (TALENs)

TALENs are customizable nucleases designed to make precise double-strand breaks at specified DNA sequences, consisting of arrays of customizable TALE repeat sequences with the FokI nuclease domain, followed by repair of these cleaves by non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) or homology-directed repair (HDR).36,37

In oilseeds crops TALENs have been effectively used to modify oil composition and quality, and other agronomically significant characteristics.38 As an illustration,39 knocked out paralogous FAD2-1A and FAD2-1B along with FAD3A in soybean with TALENs, resulting in the precedence of oleic acid, i.e. fatty acids containing 80% of all fatty acids, in soybean oil; in that same system, the TALEN editing system reduced the concentration of linoleic acid and 8-linolenic acid, both favorable to oxidative stability and health.39 The earliest report was in peanut (Arachis hypogaea), with FAD2 gene editing by TALENs causing mutant lines to increase oleic acid content in their seeds (to an average of 90.45) and decrease the percentage of linoleic acid by 319 in T2 seeds relative to wild-type. Also,37 applied TALENs to soybean in GmPDS11 and GmPDS18, obtained visible albino and dwarf phenotypes, which confirmed hereditary and consistent mutagenesis. These articles highlight both the potential and the technical challenges of TALEN applications in improving oilseeds: although oil quality traits have been altered quite successfully, the design, delivery, and screening of TALENs are more laborious than some other methods, and off-target effects and regulatory approvals are also issues of concern.

TALENs have also been used to create new varieties of all oilseed crops, which now have high oil contents and are resistant to pathogens.40 For example, modification of soybean via TALEN led to the generation of highly resistant soybean cyst nematodes.37

CRISPR-Based Tools

The genome editing field has developed over the last twenty years to include the first programmable nucleases and the most accurate CRISPR-based technologies. The original designs of ZFNs and TALENs were important proofs-of-concept demonstrating that targeted DNA modification was technologically feasible, albeit at high cost, but were constrained by potential technical complexity.33;41

CRISPR/Cas system

The release of the CRISPR/Cas9 system has transformed the scene by bringing an easy to use, RNA-guided nuclease which can effectively and easily be used to edit the genome of a wide range of organisms.42;43 The popularity of the CRISPR/Cas9 system has increased, and it has become one of the most favorable genome editing systems because it is simple and very successful.14 On the basis of this technology, we can change specific genes by subjecting them to double-strand breaks in the DNA, which can then be repaired via nonhomologous end joining or homology-directed repair.44

Base editing

Base editing is a new and very specific approach to traditional genome editing methods. Base editing also allows the direct chemical conversion of one nucleotide to another in contrast to classical CRISPR/Cas9-mediated methods, which use double-strand break (DSB) repair, which does not induce DNA strand separation and sufficient donor templates.15 This is commonly done by expressing a catalytically inactive Cas9 (nickase Cas9 or dead Cas9) fused to a deaminase enzyme, e.g. cytidine deaminase or adenine deaminase. The resulting cytosine base editors (CBEs) and adenine base editors (ABEs) can enable CA, TA (GG, TC) or AG, TC mutations at specific locations of the genome.49;50 Base editing minimizes chances of undesirable indels, chromosomal rearrangements or off-target integrations, which can be the consequences of normal genome editing due to the avoidance of double-strand breakage.

In addition to oil composition, base editing is also being considered to eliminate the presence of antinutritional factors like raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs) in soybean, which reduce the protein digestibility in human beings and livestock. Through the targeted addition of point mutations in the genes that regulate the production of RFO, the anti-nutritional content could be reduced without compromising seeds viability and energy storage capabilities.55

Base editing can also be used to improve yield and resistance to stress by allowing accurate editing of regulatory elements and transcription factor binding sites. As an example, specific modifications to flowering-time regulators, seed development genes or pathways of abiotic stress responses can also be made to optimally regulate expression without completely silencing gene activity, which can be used to boost productivity in less than ideal conditions.56 Critically, these gains can be realized in less breeding cycles and more predictably as opposed to traditional mutagenesis or introgression breeding.

Base editing can induce a specific mutation to enhance desirable oilseed crop phenotypes, such as oil quality and yield, safely without the risk associated with ordinary breeding or random mutagenesis.55

Prime editing

Prime editing is among the latest technologies in precise genome engineering, with more versatility than the traditional CRISPR/Cas9 or base editing technology. In contrast to other systems where double-strand breaks (DSBs) or donor DNA templates are required, prime editing allows the introduction of specific nucleotide substitutions, insertions, and deletions directly and in a predetermined and controlled way.51 The system makes use of the Cas9 nick using a reverse transcriptase nick enzyme, directed by a custom designed prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA). The pegRNA does not just guide the Cas9 nick to the site of interest, but also acts as a reverse transcription template with the desired genetic change, which becomes integrated into the genome. It is a design that produces a wide array of potential genome edits and minimizes the chances of unwanted indels, chromosomal rearrangement, or off-target integrations.57;58

Even though prime editing in oil seed crops is still in its infancy, it has got a lot to offer in its applications. In soybean, rapeseed, sunflower, and camelina prime editing can be used to precisely edit genes involved in fatty acid biosynthesis, including FAD2 and FAD3, to form alleles with healthier oils, increasing oleic acid and decreasing polyunsaturated fatty acids.55 Likewise, with prime editing, antinutritional factors (e.g., raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs) and allergenic proteins) can be removed using target deletions or replacements, which improves digestibility and nutritional safety.54 Moreover, prime editing would be able to increase resistance to abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and heat by editing cis-regulatory elements or promoter regions of stress-responsive genes.56 It is also appropriate in the creation of long-lasting resistance to fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogenesis by altering the susceptibility genes or expressing the resistance alleles directly into the genome due to its precision.53 Moreover, yield-associated characteristics, such as flowering period, seed size, and oil concentration, could be refined with the help of prime editing, which allows maintaining stable yields under fluctuating conditions.54

Even though prime editing in oilseeds has yet to be applied practically, the initial results in cereals like rice and wheat indicate that it is technically feasible and can be used with a wide range of crops.57;58 Prime editing, with its ability to make very precise edits without DSBs, is a revolutionary tool with vast potential to improve the quality of oil, its resistance to stress, and yield stability in oilseed crops.

Traits Targeted for the Improvement of Nutritional Quality in Oilseed Crops

The nutritional value of oilseed crops is an added value that has shifted the interest of consumers to healthier foods.59 Enhancing the nutritional value of oilseed crops is a multidimensional process targeting a variety of seed characteristics, including oil content and fatty acid composition, protein content, anti-nutritional factors, micronutrients, and specific bioactive compounds.

Improving the fatty acids composition can be used to enhance the health value of the oil and the ability of the oil to resist oxidation.60 Modifications in total oil content and fatty acid composition have been successfully targeted using CRISPR/Cas9 and TALEN-mediated gene editing of principal fatty acid biosynthesis genes.11;61

Although genome editing technologies such as TALNs, ZFNs and CRISPR/Cas9 have been successfully used to alter endogenous fatty acid biosynthesis genes like FAD2 and FAD3 to enrich oleic acid and control polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA).62, long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (LC-PUFA) like eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) in the oilseed crop have so far been produced using transgenic metabolic engineering strategies. It has been reported that by transferring desaturases and elongases of algae and marine organisms to oilseeds such as Camelina sativa and Brassica napus, there is massive accumulation of EPA and DHA.63 Though this approach is currently not yet using specific genome editing techniques, the current trends indicate the integration of CRISPR/Cas-regulated precursor flux and metabolic engineering to stabilize and optimize very long-chain PUFAs (VLC-PUFAs) biosynthesis in oilseed crops.

The nutritional value and quality of proteins play a vital role in human and animal nutrition, whereby there is an interest in enhancing the concentrations of essential amino acids such as lysine, methionine, and tryptophan as well as enhancing digestibility.64 Anti-nutritional substances, including glucosinolates, phytates, tannins, and protease inhibitors, have been diminished by precise base editing and CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of genes in the biosynthetic pathways, to increase nutrient bioavailability and palatability.61,65

Micronutrient and vitamin modification such as tocopherols and carotenoids have also been done with the use of CRISPR/Cas9 of storage and transport gene, which enhances antioxidant capacity and human health-related improvements.65,66 Other targets are phytosterols, polyphenols, and other bioactive compounds, the concentration of which can be improved with the help of genome editing and positively affect nutritional value and seed storage properties. Taken together, these new breeding and genome editing technologies are allowing to create oil seed crops with much better nutritional characteristics that can be used as food and also as the raw materials in the industry.61

Genetic Diversity and Natural Variations within Oilseed Species

Natural variation and genetic diversity in oilseed crops are also a rich source of resources to use in targeting genome editing. An example is the allelic variation in the FAE1 gene of Brassica napus that is known to regulate the erucic acid content, and some of the accessions have the naturally low-erucic acid alleles. High-erucic germplasm CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing (knock out of BnaA08.FAE1 and BnaC03.FAE1) has yielded almost zero-erucic acid lines with increased levels of oleic acid.67 Likewise natural variants that affect oleic, linoleic and linolenic acid content have been reported in Brassica carinata whereby the high-oleic and low-linolenic germplasm were used to enhance the traits.68 Diversity also goes to anti-nutritional compounds with the glucosinolates and phytic acid concentrations ranging across the Brassica taxa, which offers alleles that can lower these unwanted compounds in seed meals.69 The characteristic allelic variation can be explored and exploited by genome editing systems like CRISPR/Cas9 to precisely replicate or accelerate the introgression of desirable variants into elite cultivars to improve the quality of oil, nutritional value, and minimize anti-nutritional traits of oilseed crops.70

Natural variation and genetic diversity in oilseed crops are valuable resources for improving nutritional traits. Importantly, genome editing can be used to replicate naturally occurring beneficial alleles already identified in wild relatives or diverse germplasm, such as low-erucic FAE1 variants in Brassica. This strategy allows breeders to combine the precision of editing with the evolutionary advantages of natural variation, accelerating the introgression of desirable alleles without prolonged backcrossing.

Though most genome editing in oilseeds has aimed at lowering the levels of PUFAs to enrich oleic acid and stabilize the oil, a second nutritional goal of equal importance is to increase levels of health-promoting PUFAs, including omega-3 fatty acids.63 To illustrate, the engineered camelina accumulates eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) using metabolic engineering with algae genes of desaturase and elongase.84,85 This duality underscores the point that the nutritional enhancement approaches may be divergent in nature; either to lower the unstable fatty acids to improve the processing quality, to enhance essential PUFAs to gain health benefits.

The FAD2 genes have been the main target of editing in Camelina sativa. The deletion of all the three CsFAD2 homoeologs with CRISPR/Cas9 resulted in a rise in oleic acid levels to approximately 80%, but lowered plant fitness.71,73 Interestingly, partial knockouts supplemented oleic acid to a normal growth level of about 60%, which gives significant emphasis to gene dosage as one of the important factors in polyploid crops.

Further gene editing of CsTT8.73 and CsDGAT1/DGAT2.72 boosted triacylglycerol accumulation and total oil content, while knockouts of CsCRUC gene.74 indirectly influenced oil and protein composition, underlining the interconnectedness of seed metabolism.

Equivalent tendencies are also observed in peanut and soybean, where in addition to CRISPR, base editing and TALENs are also employed in those crops. Base editing.75,76 have reached an efficiency of up to 90.5% of the oleic acid content of peanut via the editing of FAD2A and FAD2B. For soybean, the first gene-edited food product to be commercialized was established.,39 with approximately 80% oleic acid having been edited in FAD2-1A and FAD2-1B. Such successes indicate a high degree of amenable traits to stable transformation and heritable traits of these crops.

In rapeseed, editing of BnFAD2 and BnFAE1 genes, has disrupted the synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acids, leading to an improved oleic acid content.77,78 Beyond nutritional enhancement,79 demonstrated that editing of BnaALDH10A9 can improve the seed aroma, highlighting that genome editing can expand the applications of oilseeds beyond their nutritional value.

Editing is also not as common as in sunflower due to transformation bottlenecks. However, FAD2-1 knockout using CRISPR/Cas9 was able to produce high-oleic phenotypic yields.80 Similarly, EgFAD2 and EgPAT have been edited in oil palm via embryonic callus transformation. Although phenotypic analyses are still limited due to its transformation inefficiency and long generation time, these studies demonstrate that genome editing is feasible even in technically difficult species.83

In the case of safflower, there are no published gene-editing studies that have so far been conducted. Nonetheless, FAD2, FAD3, and omega-3 desaturases have been identified to be candidate regulators of fatty acid characteristics by transcriptome analyses.81,82 (Wu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021), laying the groundwork for future editing to take use of safflower’s economic potential for linoleic and conjugated fatty acids.

Across species, FAD2 and FAD3 edits consistently yield high-oleic lines, though trade-offs such as stunted growth in Camelina highlight the importance of dosage-sensitive regulation. Beyond fatty acids, emerging applications include modification of seed storage proteins (CRUC), flavonoid regulators (TT8), and aroma-related genes (ALDH10A9), demonstrating that genome editing can extend oilseed improvement beyond conventional oil traits. These new targets represent the next frontier of nutritional engineering.

Genome Editing Efficiency

Oilseed crop genome editing shows how biological complexity, technological ability, and trait architecture influence the effectiveness of various interventions. Certain species like Camelina and rapeseed are easier to edit due to their shorter life cycles, known methods of transformation, and characterized gene targets like FAD2 desaturases. Recalcitrant species, including oil palm and safflower, are difficult to regenerate and transform, and their lengthy reproductive cycles make it difficult to verify the phenotypic, which is why there have been few reports of lines modified with these species.86

The polyploid crops pose unique challenges for editing since target genes are usually present in more than one homeolog and all homeologs need to be disrupted to attain the required phenotype. For instance, Camelina sativa is allohexaploid, and three FAD2 homeologs have to be edited to raise oleic acid content significantly.71 Partial knockouts may improve fatty acid composition but often yield incomplete or variable phenotypes. Such redundancy has been addressed by the development of germline-specific promoters and multiplex CRISPR techniques.83,87

One of the biggest limitations to the use of genome editing in a general way may be the possibility to effectively deliver editing reagents and to directly regenerate entire plants with transformed cells. Agrobacterium-mediated systems on Camelina, Brassica, and soybean have proven to be well-established, inducing faster success with the editing process, and recalcitrant legumes and perennials are still problematic.88,89 More recent developments such as in planta transformation and morphogenic regulator-mediated regeneration have already started to circumvent these challenges, but efficiencies are still dependent on the genotype.

There is also an effect of the biological simplicity of the desired trait on editing outcomes. There are genes such as FAD2 or FAD3 that directly regulate fatty acid desaturation and whose mutation yields predictable biochemical phenotype.90 On the other hand, characteristics controlled by polygenic nets or highly pleiotropic genes usually bring about unforeseen side effects. As an example, total FAD2 knockout in Camelina led to extremely high levels of oleic acid, but also inhibited the growth of plants.71

The Application of Genome Editing Technologies

The widespread use of CRISPR/Cas9 technology, which has been in use since 2014, characterizes the overall picture of genome editing in oilseed crops. When genome editing research in oilseeds first started, TALENs were employed to increase the amount of oleic acid, as in soybeans.39 This was a time when few editing tools were being used. However, since 2019, CRISPR/Cas9 has become a more popular tool due to its simplicity, accuracy, and ability to edit up to five genes in a multiplex.71,76,77, particularly in crops like rapeseed (BnFAD2), peanut (AhFAD2), and camelina (CsFAD2, CsTT8).

The widest range of genome editing work in oilseed crops was in 2022 and included base editing, TALENs and omics-based studies without direct editing. Base editing in peanut showed that the possibility of making accurate single-nucleotide changes without causing a double-strand break, especially in nutritional and allergenic characteristics in the future.75

Although older editing tools still like TALENs are used for oilseed genome editing, such as in soybean FAD2 knockouts for oleic acid enhancement.39,91, their use has remained rare due to the complexity of design compared with CRISPR systems. By 2023–2024, CRISPR/Cas system and its derivatives will be the new standard platform for multiplex editing of oilseeds. To illustrate, multiplex editing of BnFAD2 in rapeseed.77 or CsFAD2 in camelina.71 were performed using CRISPR/Cas9. Meanwhile, peanut (AhFAD2) base editing enhanced the precision editing without breaking strands.75 and omics-based research in safflower discovered new desaturase targets (FAD3, FAD7, FAD8) to be edited in the future.7 These developments, combined, emphasize the CRISPR/Cas9 platform core, supported by complementary tools depending upon crop, target gene architecture, and transformation feasibility needs as well as regulatory context.

Genome Editing Trends in Oilseed Crops

Genome editing has been used in the last ten years to greatly extend the research possibilities in oilseeds crops, allowing the manipulation of nutritional and agronomic characteristics (see Table 3 and Figure 2). Nonetheless, it has not yet been very successful in large-scale commercial applications, and most of the successes so far have been at the experimental or greenhouse level. The initiation of early development occurred when soybean achieved some success through TALEN-mediated editing of FAD2, resulting in increases in oleic acid content.39 Later, camelina, peanut, and rapeseed crops took up the systems of CRISPR/Cas9 and base editing to improve oil quality, nutritional profiles, and traits related to production. In particular, CsFAD2 and CsFAD3 editing in Camelina has had a striking effect on monounsaturated fatty acids, whereas CsTT8 and DGAT1/2 editing are directed at the same time, with the total oil content and seed flavonoid composition improved.71–73 Similarly, the CRISPR and base editing of AhFAD2A/B in peanut also led to an increase in the oleic acid proportion in the oil to 75–80%.75,76 Figure 2.Time series analysis of genome editing applications in oilseed crops.

The expanding range of genome editing targets in oilseed crops reflects a transition to the refinement of the oil composition to a more comprehensive level of nutrition and agronomic characteristics. The most recent applications are the alteration of fragrance by editing BnaALDH10A9 in rapeseed.79 the creation of compact plant architecture through the editing of EgPAT and EgFAD2 genes in oil palm.83 and the stress resistance stacked with herbicide resistance made by acetolactate synthases (ALS) editing in the sunflower.80 CRISPR/Cas9 is the most commonly used platform of genome editing because of its effectiveness and versatility. The findings of various studies reveal a similar pattern, with a noticeable increase in genome editing research in oilseed crops after 2020, propelled by cross-disciplinary cooperation, regulatory clarity, and technical maturity. Challenges in Transformation, Field Validation, and Regulation

Crop-Specific Transformation Protocols

The stable transformation and regeneration of plants continue to be a key technical milestone especially to oilseed crops with complicated genome or those which are ineffective in tissue culture.94 Even as Camelina sativa and Glycine max incorporate relatively efficient Agrobacterium-mediated transformation systems.39,71, difficulties remain a challenge to various other species like safflower, sunflower and oil palm. As an illustration, oil palm needs manual effort at bombarding particles into embryogenic Calli, very slow regeneration potential, and late protection assessment.83 Likewise, Carthamus tinctorius has no strong transformation method reducing practical verification of gene targets discovered in transcriptomics researches.81,82

Transformation is also complicated in most polyploid crops like rapeseed or peanut by gene redundancy and homeolog compensation, which need to be edited in multiplex to realize phenotypic effects.77,78 The absence of reproducible protocols at the species level limits the scalability and inter-cultivar and inter-environmental comparisons of editing results.

Field-Level Validation

Field-level validation is a familiar and important step in the process of taking genome editing results in oilseed crops from the laboratory or greenhouse and actually putting the results into practice in the field. The main objectives of field testing include verification of desired efficiency of targeted edits to offer desired phenotypic effects under agronomic conditions, yield and seed quality performance numerical data and investigation of potential unintended effects, e.g., possible pleiotropy, changes in composition etc. In addition, field validation is what can be used to supply the data necessary for regulatory approval as well as confidence to industry stakeholders.95

In field tests, appropriate designs like randomized block designs with many replications are used with non-edited lines as a control group. Important metrics go beyond the desired characteristic and frequently include traits related to seed quality, phenology, disease resistance, oil content, fatty acid composition, and yield components. Multi-location and multi-year experiments are essential for verifying the stability and robustness of altered traits since genotype-by-environment interactions can have a substantial impact on trait expression.

Field-level validation is also influenced by regulatory factors. Certain jurisdictions consider small alterations, including point mutations or short deletions, to be different than transgenic alterations, and create a less complicated regulatory system. One example is that in the United States and Canada, there are open-field experiments with genome-edited lines of camelina and canola with regulatory clearances, and in the European Union, open-field trials of edited lines are subject to genetically modified organism (GMO) legislation.95,96

Regulatory Issues of Genome Editing

The recent development of genome editing technology like TALENs, ZFNs, CRISPR/Cas systems, and base/prime editors has generated new opportunities in improving crops, and has also resulted in the emergence of new regulatory, ethical and legal issues. One of the key questions is whether genome-edited organisms (GEOs) are to be subject to the same regulatory frameworks as the traditional genetically modified organisms (GMOs), which generally entail transgenic strategies that imply the insertion of foreign DNA.100 In comparison to classical GMOs, most genome-edited crops contain small insertions, deletions, or substitutions which, theoretically, can be caused by mutation or conventional breeding.101 This difference has raised a lot of debate internationally as to how they should be regulated in terms of its development, commercialization and trade.

Regulatory issues surrounding genome-edited crops have been among the most important non-technical obstacles to their adoption, particularly in oilseed crops that may be internationally traded or used as food. Although a number of research.8, including the United States, Brazil, Argentina and Japan have established transparent policies excluding gene-edited organism which do not contain foreign DNA as being GMOs, other jurisdiction uses more stringent frameworks.11

The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) has stepped in with a fairly lax approach, exempting genome-edited products that do not involve introducing foreign DNA under the strict GMO guidelines.102 As an example, a CRISPR-edited mushroom without any sugar and high oleic soybean did not receive the same regulations as transgenic crops.103 The U.S. food and drug administration (FDA) and environmental protection agency (EPA) offers further regulation based on the purpose of the product and the effects of its use on the environment.

The European Union (EU) has a precautionary principle. In 2018, the European Court of Justice (ECJ) held that genome-edited organisms are covered by the same rigorous GMO Directive (2001/18/EC) as transgenic organisms, whether or not foreign DNA is incorporated.104 According to the decision of the ECJ, organisms created using genome editing methods, like CRISPR/Cas9, carry the same regulations as those produced by more traditional methods of GMOs when they are not products of conventional mutagenesis.105,106 This comprises obligatory environmental risk assessment, premarket authorization, traceability, and labeling, even when the unsettled state of any alien DNA has been left in the edited plant.

Latin American Countries, like Argentina, Brazil, and Chile, have taken a much more innovation-permissive stance, in which genome-edited crops do not have to be regulated as GMOs unless they contain any foreign DNA.107 Such a case-by-case assessment system introduced in Argentina was the first of its kind to be used in other countries, and has served as a template.

In 2019, Japan declared that foods that are genome-edited and contain no foreign DNA will not be regulated as GMOs, but they must be labeled and safety evaluated.108 However, China historically imposed strict measures against GMOs, which in 2022 allowed the release of draft guidelines to permit the genome-edited crops with less difficulty, making the policy less gradual.109

This level of regulation is very strict compared to more free ones and poses major difficulties to innovation, investments in public–private partnership, and alignment in trade.110 As in the case of genome-edited soybeans where yield is permitted to be commercialized in the U.S., whereas regulatory inconsistency prohibits importation of the same, despite the soybeans not containing transgenes.99

This disjoint leaves more complexity and expense in creating and putting oilseed crop species outsides of their genomes and it can retard advancements in circumstances that would benefit the most of the new developments in nutrition quality and climate stability.

The absence of harmonization between regulatory regimes creates a problem to international trade. A crop that is treated as conventional in one nation can be regarded as GMO in another one, which leads to the exposure to trade disputes and regulatory ambiguity.100 Scholars note that the regulation needs to be scientific, commensurate, and adaptive, which differentiates transgenic alterations and minor, precise edits that are available with natural mutations or traditional breeding.111

While several countries such as US, Argentina, Japan exempt non-transgenic genome-edited crops from GMO regulation, the EU maintains a precautionary stance by classifying them as GMOs. This lack of harmonization creates barriers to international trade; for example, genome-edited soybeans commercialized in the US cannot be freely imported into the EU. Such inconsistencies not only complicate global supply chains but also discourage adoption by smallholder farmers in export-dependent regions. A balanced, science-driven regulatory framework that distinguishes between transgenic and non-transgenic edits will be essential for equitable global adoption of nutritionally enhanced oilseeds.

Future Directions for Research and Deployment

CRISPR-Cas systems and specifically CRISPR-Cas9 systems have several obvious limitations regarding specificity and efficiency. One of the most widely discussed drawbacks is the occurrence of off-target effects, whereby the guide RNA (sgRNA) binds to sequences that are similar but not identical to the intended target, producing unintended double-strand breaks or mutations.52,112 Such off-target effects can cause gene loss, genomic repositioning, or unwanted expression of oncogenes.113

The other significant issue is that the on-target efficiency varies based on the choice of the sgRNA sequence composition, chromatin accessibility, delivery method, and cell type. Specifically, specific genome alterations based on homology-directed repair (HDR) is still inefficient in the majority of cells relative to non-homologous end joining (NHEJ).113 Other problems, including mosaicities in embryos or growing tissues, and the inefficient delivery of Cas9 components as well as cell-type-specific reactions to repair also decrease the consistency of editing.114

By considering the above limitations, in the future, the successful implementation of genome editing in oilseed crop improvement hinges on identifying and addressing remaining critical technical, regulatory, and translational challenges.

First, it is necessary to develop species-agnostic transformation platforms. A handful of innovations (in planta delivery, tissue culture-free methods, and better genome-editing delivery vectors) may enable genome editing to be applied to endangered crops, such as sunflower, safflower, and oil palm. It should lead to transformation bottlenecks being addressed to greatly scale the trait engineering in more commercial and orphan oilseed crops.

Second, it will require a systematic field validation of edited lines in various agroecological zones as desirable. Trials should be instituted that would not only assess the stability of edited traits but also would assess possible trade-offs in yield, fitness and stress tolerance using multi-location, multi-year trials. This will assist in mainstreaming genome editing into the breeding systems and allow the registration of cultivars.

Third, utilization of precision genome editing approaches, which include base editing and prime editing provides new chances to make subtle, SNP-level changes in eating plans without incorporating foreign genetic material or creating sites of twofold-stand break.51,55 The tools would be particularly useful to address health-related phenotypes like fatty acid compositions and allergenicity as in the case of base-edited peanut lines of AhFAD2A/B.75

Fourth, global commercialization of genome-edited oilseed crops requires regulatory harmonization. Whereas most of the world has introduced changes to exclude non-transgenic genome-edited crops under the regulations of GMOs, the European Union also implements strict restrictions through Directive 2001/18/EC.105,106 Such differences do not only hinder global trade but also dishearten investment in genome editing technology. Equitable access and innovation will be central to an approach to regulation, which achieves the balance between risk and proportionate regulation that is based on science.

Fifth, more multi-omics integration (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) will speed up the identification of functional genes related to nutritional quality, oil content, and stress resistance. Such knowledge can be used to undertake multi-trait genome editing, where multiple attributes can be simultaneously improved such as yield, oil profile, flavor and protein vector.

Lastly, the process should turn to the fact that people should be involved in clear communication of the safety, benefits, and non-GMO attributes of edited crops. This is especially needed where stringent regulations of the industry are not evident or the society is still dubious. Consumer trust will be an important factor toward the wide consideration of gene-edited, nutritionally enhanced oilseed products.

Conclusion

This review highlights the tremendous advancements in the utilization of genome editing, notably CRISPR/Cas9, with the aim of improving the nutritional value of oilseed crops. In a variety of species, this kind of directed gene alteration has universally led to increased oleic acid content as well as to enhanced oleic acid stability in oils. The most popular models are Camelina, peanuts, rapeseed, and soybean, which are prone to transformation and stable advancements in traits. New technologies such as base editing are increasing the resolution and range of traits, such as lower allergenicity properties and improved seed composition. The use of genome editing will become a game changer in achieving nutritionally improved health-benefiting types of oilseeds despite the technical and regulatory hurdles on the way. Further combinations of multitrait engineering, omics-dependent discovery, and enabling regulation will be important to accelerate the field.
