Evaluation of a holiday card retention strategy: a study within a trial (SWAT) embedded in a blood pressure reduction trial
Authors: Lesli E Skolarus, Devin L Brown, Ana Moraga, Sara Hassani, Mackenzie Dinh, William J Meurer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionParticipant retention is essential to minimize bias, preserve statistical power, and strengthen the validity of clinical trial findings. Although retention strategies are widely used, many lack rigorous evaluation. We evaluated whether sending a holiday card, compared with usual follow-up procedure, improves follow-up completion among clinical trial participants recruited from a resource-constrained emergency department.MethodsWe conducted a Study Within a Trial (SWAT) embedded within Reach Out, a 2 × 2 × 2 randomized trial of a mobile health-based behavioral intervention designed to reduce blood pressure among individuals with elevated blood pressure identified from a resource-constrained emergency department. All participants received standard retention strategies as part of the parent trial, with participants randomized to the SWAT intervention group additionally receiving a holiday card. The primary outcome was completion of the next available outcome assessment (6- or 12-month assessment). We estimated absolute differences with 95% confidence intervals in outcome assessment completion between participants who received a holiday card and those who did not, and then stratified by age, sex, and race.ResultsAmong Reach Out participants (N = 395), 198 were randomized to receive a holiday card and 197 to receive no card. Outcome assessment completion did not differ between participants who received a holiday card and those who did not (54.5% vs 58.4%; p = 0.43), and there was no evidence of effect modification by age, sex, or race.ConclusionIn this low-resource setting, receipt of a holiday card was not associated with higher outcome completion. These findings are consistent with prior studies and suggest that multicomponent interventions may be needed to increase retention, underscoring the value of pragmatic evaluations of retention interventions embedded within ongoing trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-026-09770-9.Keywords: Study within a trial, Trial retention, Holiday card, Embedded randomized trial

Participant attrition is a persistent challenge in clinical trials that reduces the available sample size, undermines statistical power, and limits the validity and generalizability of trial findings [1, 2]. Though attrition is a commonly reported challenge, and many strategies to reduce attrition have been reported, the effectiveness of these is rarely formally evaluated [3–5]. Studies within a trial (SWATs) provide a rigorous and efficient approach to testing retention strategies within ongoing trials, but individual SWATs are often small and context-specific, requiring replication across populations and settings to generate generalizable evidence [6].

Sending Christmas cards, Trial Forge SWAT 82 [7], to participants across eight trials did not improve retention [8]. However, those trial populations were predominantly White, female, and not recruited from acute care settings like the emergency department, limiting generalizability. As such, replication in more diverse populations and in settings with greater barriers to engagement is needed to better understand the effectiveness of this approach.

This SWAT was embedded within a behavioral blood pressure reduction trial that recruited participants from a resource-constrained emergency department, where participants often face substantial social, economic, and logistical barriers to sustained research participation. Retention challenges were identified early through routine trial monitoring, with loss to follow-up well exceeding the anticipated ~20% [9]. In response, this SWAT was conducted and, now that SWAT 82 has been published, serves as a replication and extension of prior work, testing whether sending holiday cards could improve retention among participants recruited from a safety-net emergency department. Guided by the COM-B framework,3 the intervention aimed to enhance social opportunity through expressions of appreciation to support ongoing engagement in the trial.

Methods

Design overview

We conducted an individually randomized Study Within a Trial (SWAT) embedded within Reach Out (NCT03422718), an ongoing randomized clinical trial.

Setting and trial population

The parent trial, Reach Out, was a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial, individually randomized, open-label trial examining the effects of behavioral intervention components on 1-year blood pressure among adults with hypertension. Reach Out consisted of three mHealth components, each with two levels: (1) healthy behavior text messaging (yes vs no), (2) prompted self-measured blood pressure (BP) monitoring and feedback (weekly vs daily), and (3) facilitated primary care provider appointment scheduling and transportation (yes vs no). Given the randomized factorial design, we expected attrition to be similar across intervention arms. Reach Out was conducted in Flint, Michigan, an urban, predominantly Black, low-income community. Adult participants were recruited from a safety net emergency department which are defined as providing care to a large proportion of patients who are underinsured or lack health insurance.

Individuals were eligible for Reach Out if they had at least 1 systolic BP ≥ 160 or a diastolic BP ≥ 100; at least 1 repeat BP remained systolic BP ≥ 140 or a diastolic BP ≥ 90; were expected to be discharged from the emergency department; and were able to receive text messages. Participants were randomized via block randomization stratified by age (<64 years, ≥65 years), self-reported sex, and whether they had taken an antihypertensive within the prior 6 months (yes versus no). Randomly permuted blocks of size 8 were generated using the blockrand package in R by the study statistician and were only accessible to the project manager at the time of randomization. Of the 488 participants randomized in Reach Out, 395 (81%) were active during the conduct of the SWAT. These participants were individually randomized within the SWAT to receive a holiday card or no holiday card (1:1 ratio), stratified by sex and age (<65 vs ≥65 years). These stratification factors were used in the parent trial (along with recent antihypertensive use) and are anticipated to be associated with study engagement and retention.

Trial retention

Reach Out retention strategies were grouped into 5 domains: study design, participant-focused, data collection, site and research staff-focused, and central study management [5]. Reach Out employed retention strategies across all 5 domains.

Study design

Reach Out was a community-engaged trial, with a Community Advisory Board (CAB) involved from trial inception through dissemination. The CAB provided ongoing input into study procedures, materials, and data collection instruments, supporting retention through shared ownership and relevance.

Participant-focused strategies

The CAB directly informed participant-level strategies by reviewing and revising trial materials prior to trial launch. Most notably, the CAB recommended the development of a comic book, co-designed with CAB members and a local graphic designer, to improve understanding of high blood pressure risks, the importance of blood pressure monitoring through the Reach Out program, and lifestyle changes to support blood pressure control [9].

At enrollment, participants were asked to provide multiple telephone numbers, including contact information for trusted family members or friends, and to grant permission for the study team to contact these individuals if the participant could not be reached directly. Participants were also provided nominal items designed to promote engagement and trial identity, including a pen and a bag with the Reach Out logo, as well as a Reach Out magnet displaying the date of the next scheduled outcome assessment.

Following enrollment, participants received regular newsletters that highlighted trial progress, introduced study staff through brief recruiter anecdotes, and included interactive content such as word puzzles or recipes and animated images of the research team. Newsletters also included reminders about upcoming outcome assessment timelines.

Prior to each outcome assessment, participants received reminder phone calls and text messages. If participants could not be reached using their primary contact information, the study team, with prior permission, contacted alternate phone numbers provided at enrollment to facilitate follow-up. Upon completion of outcome assessments, participants received USD 25 for the 6-month assessment and USD 30 for the 12-month assessment in appreciation of their time and effort.

Data collection

Outcome assessments were designed to be flexible and participant-centered. In addition to the participants’ homes, assessments were offered at the provider offices and emergency department, in addition to community settings including the Farmers Market, libraries, community centers, and through remote options. For remote outcome assessments, participants were asked to provide 3 BP measurements, each 1 min apart. Participants communicated these to the study team through phone calls or text messages. Participants were also asked to send a picture of their BP cuff on their wrists to confirm the correct orientation. Transportation assistance was provided to reduce barriers to completing outcome assessments.

Site and research staff strategies

Research assistants were intentionally recruited from the Flint community and reflected populations underrepresented in biomedical research, fostering trust and community concordance.

Central study management

A real-time study dashboard was used to track recruitment, retention, and outcome assessment completion and was reviewed weekly to support proactive follow-up and timely adjustment of retention strategies.

Trial outcomes

Participants completed either in-person or remote outcome assessments at 6 and 12 months. The trial began enrollment prior to the COVID 19 pandemic and completed follow up during the pandemic, which required a shift from in-person outcome assessments to predominantly remote outcome assessments for participant safety and to comply with research restrictions. Detailed methods and results were previously published [9, 10].

Intervention: Holiday card

Participants were randomized 1:1 to either receive a one-time holiday card in Winter of 2020 or no holiday card. Holiday cards were mailed in January 2020 by a member of the research team to participants’ home addresses and were addressed from the Reach Out Study Group. The printed card featured a simple winter design with a New Years greeting, with no religious or culturally specific imagery (Appendix 1). The holiday card thanked participants for taking part in the Reach Out study and acknowledged their efforts over the past year to focus on blood pressure and make healthy choices. No mailed holiday cards were returned to the study team as undeliverable. Outcome assessors were blinded to the SWAT intervention allocation.

Statistical considerations

Endpoint

The primary endpoint was retention, defined as completion of the next available outcome assessment (either the 6- or 12-month assessment). Specifically, this was the participant’s first follow-up occurring after January 2020, including either the 6- or 12-month timepoint. The SWAT sample size depended on Reach Out enrollment. Therefore, no formal sample size calculation was performed, which is in line with SWAT methodology.

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the trial population by SWAT intervention group assignment. The primary analysis compared completion of the next scheduled outcome assessment between participants sent a holiday card and those who were not, using chi-square tests. We estimated absolute differences in outcome assessment completion by group, with 95% confidence intervals, stratified by age, sex, and race. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27).

Results

The SWAT took place between January 2020 and June 2021, corresponding to the timing of participants’ 6- and 12-month outcome assessments following distribution of the holiday card in January 2020. All participants enrolled in the host trial at the time of the SWAT were randomized. Of the 395 participants, 198 were randomized to receive a holiday card and 197 were randomized not to receive a holiday card. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 47 years, 61.5% were female, and 54.9% of participants identified as Black.

Discussion

In this SWAT embedded within a behavioral blood pressure reduction trial that recruited participants from a low-resource emergency department setting, sending a winter holiday card was not associated with improved outcome completion. Although simple to implement, low-cost, and scalable, this low-intensity relational strategy alone did not increase participant retention. Our findings are consistent with prior literature, the initial SWAT82 was conducted in 2019 and published in 2021, after our SWAT was implemented and similarly found that Christmas cards have limited impact on participant retention [8, 11]. Any effect of sending cards, if present, is likely small and context dependent [12], particularly in trials such as Reach Out which are conducted in settings where participants face substantial competing demands [13] and where robust retention efforts are already in place [5]. In future studies, we recommend testing multicomponent retention strategies and considering the integration of retention and recruitment approaches, recognizing that engagement begins at recruitment, is dynamic, and may require longitudinal strategies.

Strengths include the randomized design and the pragmatic evaluation of a retention strategy within an active trial. Limitations include the context-specific nature of the trial setting, which may limit generalizability, and the potential influence of the COVID-19 pandemic. We also did not account for prior visit attendance or differences in retention across follow-up timepoints, which may influence retention and the effectiveness of the intervention. These factors would be most concerning if attendance patterns differed across study arms, as this could bias between-group comparisons. While the randomized design reduces the likelihood of such imbalances, it does not eliminate this possibility. Notably, the majority of outcome assessments (72.6%) occurred at the 6-month timepoint, which likely limits the impact of differential attrition over time on the primary findings.

SWATs are critical to building an evidence base to improve the design and conduct of clinical trials. Sending a holiday card was not associated with improved participant retention within a behavioral blood pressure reduction trial conducted in a low resource setting. Importantly, this study demonstrates the feasibility of embedding SWATs within behavioral intervention trials and contributes incremental evidence to the retention literature. Future SWATs should continue to test and accumulate evidence on alternative retention strategies with greater potential impact.
