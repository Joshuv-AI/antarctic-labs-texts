The impact of mobile nursing information systems on patient satisfaction with nursing care: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jianhua Qiu, Haibin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates the association between mobile nursing information systems (MNIS) and patient satisfaction with nursing care across diverse clinical settings.MethodsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to non-randomised quasi-experimental studies, we systematically searched eight electronic databases for literature published between January 2010 and October 2025 to identify eligible non-randomised controlled studies comparing MNIS with conventional nursing care. Randomised controlled trials were excluded to maintain methodological homogeneity for real-world implementation evidence. The primary outcome was patient-reported satisfaction with nursing care. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSix studies involving 4,617 patients were included in the final synthesis. Pooled results indicated that MNIS were associated with a modest elevation in patient satisfaction with nursing care (RR: 1.13, 95%CI: 1.07–1.20, P < 0.001), with substantial unexplained statistical heterogeneity across studies (I²=77.0%). Exploratory subgroup analyses detected positive correlational trends across outpatient infusion settings, inpatient wards and operating rooms. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the pooled correlational results.ConclusionsCurrent low-certainty evidence suggests that MNIS are associated with slightly higher patient satisfaction with nursing care across multiple clinical scenarios. Given the non-randomised study design, high unexplained heterogeneity and limited evidence quality, causal relationships cannot be established. Further rigorous research using standardised outcome measures is needed to strengthen the evidence base.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-05002-2.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of healthcare information technology (IT) has fundamentally transformed nursing practice and care delivery models globally. Mobile nursing information systems (MNIS), defined as portable digital platforms that enable real-time data capture, clinical decision support and workflow optimisation at the point of care, have emerged as critical tools for modernising healthcare operations [1]. These systems integrate various technological components, including mobile workstations, barcode scanning, electronic medication administration records and closed-loop management protocols, to support nursing activities across the care continuum [2]. The application of IT not only improves the standardisation and completeness of nursing documentation but also notably enhances nursing work efficiency through workflow optimisation. These improvements may contribute to a better patient experience and satisfaction with nursing care, but patient satisfaction is not equivalent to the technical quality of nursing care. However, the comprehensive association between these systems and patient satisfaction with nursing care remains incompletely understood, with empirical evidence remaining fragmented and inconsistent.

The imperative to adopt MNIS stems from multiple converging factors in contemporary healthcare. First, patient safety concerns remain paramount, with medication errors and adverse events continuing to pose substantial risks despite ongoing quality improvement efforts [3]. Traditional manual documentation and nursing workflow management are prone to information omission, unclear handovers and non-standardised operations. Second, healthcare systems worldwide face persistent challenges in nursing workforce retention and satisfaction, with burnout and workload pressures threatening the sustainability of care delivery [4]. Heavy documentation tasks consume substantial nursing time, reducing work efficiency and affecting job satisfaction. Third, patients increasingly expect healthcare experiences that match the technological sophistication they encounter in other service sectors, creating demand for more efficient, transparent and responsive care processes [5].

Recent evidence suggests that MNIS can address these challenges through several mechanisms. By automating routine documentation tasks and streamlining workflows, these systems may reduce nurses’ administrative burden, improve documentation quality and completeness and allow more time for direct patient care [6]. Through features such as barcode medication administration and closed-loop verification protocols, mobile systems can enhance medication safety and reduce preventable errors [7, 8]. These safety measures and efficiency improvements may increase patient trust and satisfaction with nursing services. Furthermore, real-time access to patient information and clinical decision support tools may improve care coordination and enable more responsive, personalised nursing interventions [9].

Despite these promising theoretical benefits, empirical evidence regarding the correlation between MNIS and patient satisfaction with nursing care remains fragmented and inconsistent. Although individual studies have reported improved patient satisfaction following implementation of mobile nursing technologies [10], others have noted minimal effects or identified implementation challenges that may limit benefits [11]. This heterogeneity in findings reflects several factors, including variations in system design, implementation contexts, measurement approaches and study methodologies. Moreover, most existing evidence originates from single-centre studies with relatively small sample sizes, limiting generalisability and statistical power to detect meaningful correlational effects.

Patient satisfaction with nursing care represents a critical patient-reported experience measure (PREM) in evaluating healthcare quality and system performance. Beyond its intrinsic value as an indicator of patient-centred care, it correlates with important clinical and operational outcomes, including treatment adherence, health outcomes and healthcare utilisation patterns [12]. Understanding how MNIS influence patient satisfaction with nursing care is essential for evidence-based technology adoption decisions. Furthermore, as healthcare systems globally invest substantial resources in health IT infrastructure, demonstrating tangible benefits in patient experience becomes increasingly important [13].

It is important to note that patient satisfaction with nursing care is a valued PREM but is not a direct or comprehensive measure of technical nursing quality. Satisfaction is influenced by interpersonal care, communication, environmental comfort and patient expectations, which extend beyond technical nursing performance. Therefore, this review focuses exclusively on patient satisfaction with nursing care as the outcome.

The nursing profession occupies a unique position in healthcare delivery, with nurses spending more time in direct patient interaction than any other healthcare professional group. Consequently, technologies that enhance nursing practice have the potential to substantially influence patient experience [14]. Mobile nursing information systems specifically target nursing workflows and processes, making patient satisfaction with nursing care an especially relevant outcome measure. However, the relationship between nursing informatics innovations and patient satisfaction is complex, mediated by system usability, workflow integration, nurse satisfaction and organisational implementation strategies [15–18].

Given the growing adoption of MNIS and the need for a rigorous synthesis of their correlational effects, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to non-randomised quasi-experimental studies to quantitatively synthesise available evidence on their association with patient satisfaction with nursing care. By pooling data across multiple studies and clinical settings, we aimed to provide more definitive estimates of effect sizes and explore potential sources of heterogeneity. This evidence synthesis can inform healthcare leaders, policymakers and frontline clinicians as they make decisions about technology adoption to optimise patient experience.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

The protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered on INPLASY (INPLASY202620046). The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guideline, which is a reporting guideline only and does not dictate study eligibility criteria. Two independent researchers systematically searched multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library for English-language publications, as well as Chinese databases, including China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, VIP and CBM. The search period spanned from January 2010 to October 2025.

The search strategy employed broad keyword searching (not limited to title/abstract) for systematic review reproducibility, combining controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) with free-text keywords to maximise retrieval. Full reproducible search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary File 1. Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were used systematically. No NOT filters were applied to avoid excluding relevant literature.

Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. All citations were exported to EndNote (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for deduplication and screening. Language was restricted to English or Chinese.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) the study population comprised hospitalised inpatients or outpatient/emergency department patients receiving nursing services; (2) study design was non-randomised quasi-experimental studies, including historical control and before-and-after comparative studies; (3) the intervention involved implementation of MNIS as the core component; and (4) the studies reported patient-reported satisfaction with nursing care as an outcome measure, with sufficient data to calculate risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) narrative reviews, expert opinions, conference abstracts, case reports or qualitative studies without quantitative data; (2) studies lacking a clear control group or intervention description; (3) studies without patient satisfaction with nursing care outcome data; (4) duplicate publications; and (5) randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

During screening, zero RCTs meeting the review scope were identified; RCTs were excluded to maintain methodological homogeneity, as randomisation of complex system-level interventions is often impractical or unethical in real-world settings. Quasi-experimental designs represent the most relevant evidence for routine clinical implementation.

Study selection process

The study selection process followed a systematic multistage approach. Initially, all search results were imported into EndNote, and duplicates were removed. Two independent reviewers conducted a two-stage screening process. In the first stage, titles and abstracts were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. For citations that could not be definitively excluded, full-text articles were retrieved for detailed evaluation.

Two reviewers independently assessed each full-text article and documented reasons for exclusion. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, with a third reviewer adjudicating if needed. The complete study selection process was documented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Ultimately, six studies met all inclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers used a standardised data extraction form to extract relevant information. Extracted data elements included (1) bibliographic information, including first author, year, country and journal; (2) study characteristics, including design, setting, sample size and patient demographics; (3) intervention details, including MNIS components, implementation and training; (4) control conditions, including usual care or conventional nursing management; and (5) outcome measures, including satisfaction scale, validation status, scoring criteria, data collection method and raw counts or percentages. When studies reported satisfaction as percentages without absolute numbers, the number of satisfied patients was calculated by multiplying the percentage by the group sample size. Rounding was performed to the nearest integer. Directly extracted and reconstructed values are clearly distinguished in Supplementary Table 1. Both reviewers independently extracted data; discrepancies were resolved by referring to the original publication, with a third reviewer adjudicating if needed.

Quality assessment

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (adapted NOS) was adopted for non-randomised studies, supplemented with criteria relevant to nursing informatics implementation research; ROBINS-I was not used due to its complexity for pragmatic implementation studies. This adapted approach has practical advantages but may not capture all biases in quasi-experimental designs. This framework evaluated three domains:

(1) selection: representativeness of source population, clarity of criteria and baseline comparability; (2) comparability: control for confounding via design or statistical adjustment; and (3) outcome assessment: validity and reliability of satisfaction scale, consistency of application and completeness of data.

Each rating was accompanied by explicit judgement criteria. Brief justifications for domain-level ratings are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Two independent reviewers applied this framework, rating each domain as ‘High’, ‘Moderate’ or ‘Low’. A calibration exercise was conducted prior to formal assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussion, with a third senior reviewer adjudicating if needed. Final consensus ratings were used to synthesise overall methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2) with the meta-analysis packages ‘meta’, ‘metafor’ and ‘dmetar’. For the dichotomous primary outcome of patient-reported satisfaction with nursing care (satisfied vs. not satisfied), RRs and 95%CIs were calculated as the effect size measure. Random-effects models using the DerSimonian–Laird method were specified a priori. Hartung–Knapp adjustment was applied to obtain more accurate CIs and p-values.

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. When significant heterogeneity was detected (P < 0.10 or I²>50%), the following were performed: (1) subgroup analyses by clinical setting and publication language (all subgroup analyses are exploratory and should not be overinterpreted as confirmatory evidence of consistency), (2) sensitivity analyses using a leave-one-out approach and (3) a Baujat plot to visualise heterogeneity contributions.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot visualisation and Egger’s regression test. With only six studies included, publication bias assessment is strictly exploratory and cannot be considered reliable. All hypothesis tests used two-sided p-values with alpha = 0.05 as the threshold.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for the association between MNIS and patient satisfaction with nursing care was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach (Supplementary File 1). As all included studies were non-randomised, the initial rating was ‘low’. Two reviewers independently assessed five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, reaching consensus.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic literature search identified 2,847 potentially relevant articles. After removing 652 duplicates, 2,195 unique articles underwent title and abstract screening. A total of 2,134 records were excluded, leaving 61 full-text articles for eligibility assessment. Upon full-text review, 55 articles were excluded due to the absence of control groups (n = 18), lack of patient satisfaction data (n = 21), inappropriate study design (n = 8), duplicate publication (n = 5) and other reasons (n = 3). Ultimately, six studies were included. Zero RCTs were identified during the screening process. The study selection process is depicted in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

The six included studies enrolled 4,617 patients (2,295 intervention, 2,322 control) across diverse clinical settings, and all studies were conducted in China. Table 1 presents the basic characteristics. All studies employed non-randomised quasi-experimental designs (before–after or historical control). Sample sizes ranged from 200 to 2,622 patients, and intervention durations ranged from 6 to 12 months.

The outcome assessment domain demonstrated relatively high methodological rigour. Two studies (33.3%) were rated ‘High quality’, as they utilised standardised, validated satisfaction scales with consistent timepoints and minimal missing data. Four studies (66.7%) were rated ‘Moderate quality’ due to the use of non-validated self-designed satisfaction scales or insufficient detailed description of measurement procedures. No study was rated ‘Low quality’ in this domain.

Variability was notable in the selection domain. No included studies achieved a high-quality rating for participant selection. Five studies (83.3%) were graded as ‘Moderate quality’, mainly due to minor ambiguities in sampling frame or participant recruitment details. The remaining study (16.7%) was rated ‘Low quality’, attributable to insufficiently described selection strategies and substantial risks of baseline imbalance across research groups.

Regarding the comparability domain, the majority of the studies showed marked limitations in controlling for potential confounders. Four studies (66.7%) were rated ‘Low quality’, with no reported confounder adjustment or baseline comparability handling. The remaining two studies (33.3%) were graded as ‘Moderate quality’ because they implemented only partial adjustment for limited demographic covariates and failed to fully balance all critical baseline confounding factors between intervention and control groups. No study achieved a high-quality rating in this domain.

For overall synthesis, no study was rated ‘Low quality’ across all three domains. The overall profile (Fig. 2A) indicates that although the evidence base is primarily derived from non-randomised studies with inherent limitations in comparability (most rated ‘Low’), the selection and outcome assessment domains were mostly rated ‘Moderate’ to ‘High’. This quality appraisal contextualises the subsequent meta-analytic findings, suggesting that although the pooled effect estimate indicates a positive association, the strength of causal inference is tempered by the substantial risk of confounding bias in the study design for most included studies (Supplementary Table 2).

Effect on patient satisfaction

Meta-analysis demonstrated that MNIS were associated with a modest increase in patient satisfaction with nursing care compared with conventional care (Fig. 3). The pooled RR was 1.13 (95%CI: 1.07–1.20, P < 0.001), indicating a 13% relative increase. Individual study RRs ranged from 1.08 to 1.26, with all studies showing a positive correlational trend favouring the intervention group. Statistical heterogeneity was substantial and unexplained (I²=77.0%, τ²=0.0021, P = 0.0006).

Subgroup analyses

All subgroup analyses in this section are exploratory, not confirmatory. Results from subgroups containing only a single study have limited statistical power and generalisability and must be interpreted with extreme caution.

Subgroup analysis by clinical setting (Fig. 4) showed the following. In outpatient infusion settings (four studies, 3,522 patients), the pooled RR was 1.14 (95%CI: 1.03–1.26, I²=76.7%, P = 0.005), indicating a positive association between MNIS and patient satisfaction with nursing care. The inpatient ward subgroup included only one single-centre study (855 patients, RR: 1.17, 95%CI: 1.12–1.22), and the operating room subgroup also contained only one study (240 patients, RR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.02–1.15). These two single-study subgroups are only for descriptive reference and cannot support setting-specific conclusions. The test for subgroup differences was not significant (χ²=4.14, df = 2, P = 0.1263).

Sensitivity and publication bias analyses

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Fig. 6) showed that the pooled RR remained consistently elevated, ranging from 1.12 (95%CI: 1.04–1.21) when omitting Yuan (2025) to 1.15 (95%CI: 1.07–1.23) when omitting Wu (2025). Confidence intervals excluded the null value in all iterations, demonstrating that no single study drove overall findings.

Certainty of evidence

Based on a structured consideration of the included studies and review methodology, the overall certainty of the evidence for the association between MNIS and improved patient satisfaction was assessed as low. This judgement was informed by the following considerations. Risk of bias (serious limitation, downgrade 1 level): The body of evidence consists exclusively of non-randomised studies with a serious risk of bias due to unmeasured confounding (from secular trends and co-interventions) and selection bias. This factor further reduced evidence certainty. Inconsistency (serious limitation, downgrade 1 level): Although the direction of association was consistent, substantial and largely unexplained statistical heterogeneity (I²=77%) was observed across studies. Variable effect magnitudes reduced the generalisability of the pooled estimate.

Indirectness (no serious concern, no downgrade): The population (patients receiving nursing care), intervention (MNIS) and outcome (patient satisfaction with nursing care) in the included studies directly address our review question. Imprecision (no serious concern, no downgrade): The meta-analysis included a substantial total sample size (n = 4,617). The 95%CI around the pooled estimate (1.07–1.20) excludes the null value, indicating acceptable precision. Publication bias (potential risk, no formal downgrade): Funnel plot asymmetry suggested possible publication bias, but the small number of included studies (n = 6) limited assessment reliability; this factor was noted but not used for formal downgrade.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides consistent evidence of a statistically significant association (not causal) between MNIS and modestly improved patient satisfaction across diverse clinical settings [19, 20]. The pooled analysis of six studies involving 4,617 patients demonstrated a 13% relative improvement in satisfaction, with benefits observed in outpatient infusion rooms, inpatient wards and operating rooms. This finding indicates an association with improved patient experience rather than definitive evidence of improved technical nursing quality [21–24]. A fundamental consideration is the nature of the primary outcome. Patient satisfaction is a subjective PREM and should not be equated with objective clinical quality indicators. The observed association, therefore, indicates a link to a more positive patient experience, a key goal in modern healthcare, rather than definitive proof of technical quality improvement across all dimensions.

Mechanisms of improvement

The improvement in patient satisfaction likely operates through multiple pathways. First, these systems streamline workflows and reduce administrative burden, freeing up nursing time for direct patient interaction and care delivery [25, 26]. Enhanced nurse–patient communication directly contributes to satisfaction [27]. Second, integrated safety features (e.g. barcode medication administration) reduce errors and adverse events [28, 29]. Beyond clinical benefit, visible safety protocols enhance patients’ perception of the quality of care and trust [30]. Third, features improving communication and information sharing (e.g. real-time displays, automated alerts) increase transparency, reduce uncertainty and empower patients [31, 32]. Fourth, gains in operational efficiency, such as reduced wait times and streamlined processes, directly address common sources of patient dissatisfaction [33].

Generalisability and contextual considerations

All six included studies were conducted in China, which represents a major limitation to international generalisability. Healthcare systems, staffing models, cultural determinants of satisfaction and implementation environments differ substantially across countries. The findings may be informative but cannot support confident global generalisability.

The consistency of benefits across clinical settings is exploratory only, as some settings were represented by single studies. Common elements such as mobile data access and barcode verification support potential generalisability, but the scope of setting-specific conclusions is limited.

Threats to validity and interpretation

Causal conclusions cannot be drawn due to the quasi-experimental designs (before–after, historical control) included. Observed changes cannot be isolated from the intervention due to potential confounding from secular trends, concurrent quality initiatives, co-interventions and selection bias. The pooled estimate likely reflects a composite of the technology effect and contextual changes.

Heterogeneity

Substantial and largely unexplained statistical heterogeneity was observed (I²=77%), representing a critical interpretive issue. Although all studies favoured the intervention, the effect magnitude varied widely. This reflects differences in intervention sophistication, implementation strategies, baseline satisfaction and heterogeneous satisfaction measurement tools. The pooled RR represents an average effect across diverse contexts rather than a uniform effect. High heterogeneity significantly limits the credibility and transportability of the pooled estimate [34, 35].

Magnitude of effect and measurement considerations

The RR of 1.13 indicates a modest average improvement in patient satisfaction. Assuming an 80% baseline satisfaction, this corresponds to an approximate 10-percentage-point absolute increase. Measurement heterogeneity is a major limitation: studies used varied, often non-validated instruments with different scoring thresholds, which may bias estimates or contribute to heterogeneity. A dedicated supplementary table (Supplementary Table 3) details the satisfaction measurement for each study, including instrument type, validation status, scoring criteria, data collection method and data transformation procedures.

Study design and quality

The finding that methodological quality did not significantly moderate effect sizes provides some reassurance. Sensitivity analyses excluding lower-quality studies yielded similar estimates, suggesting observed benefits are not mere artefacts of methodological flaws. However, all included studies were quasi-experimental, reflecting the practical and ethical challenges of randomising complex system-level interventions. Although providing valuable real-world evidence, caution is required in attributing benefits solely to the technology without considering contextual factors.

Generalisability of evidence base

The predominance of Chinese-language studies (4 of 6) raises questions about cross-cultural and health system generalisability. Subgroup analysis by publication language showed no significant difference, suggesting benefits may translate. The rapid adoption of health IT in China provides valuable implementation experience [36]. The scarcity of studies from other regions may reflect differences in research/publication practices rather than the absence of such systems.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the small number of included studies (n = 6) limits the power for publication bias assessment and meta-regression. Second, the exclusive use of quasi-experimental designs limits causal inference. Third, the patient satisfaction measurement tools were heterogeneous and non-validated across studies. Fourth, there was limited reporting of implementation details. Fifth, the follow-up periods were relatively short (6–12 months), with unclear long-term sustainability. Sixth, there was an absence of cost-effectiveness data. Seventh, all studies were conducted in China, severely limiting international generalisability. Eighth, the exclusion of RCTs (none identified during screening) may affect the comprehensiveness of the evidence base. Finally, the publication bias assessment is strictly exploratory and unreliable due to the small sample size.

Certainty of evidence

Based on a structured assessment using the GRADE approach, the overall certainty of the evidence for this association is low. This judgement is due to serious risk of bias from non-randomised designs, serious inconsistency (unexplained high heterogeneity), no serious concerns regarding indirectness or imprecision and the possibility of publication bias.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis of non-randomised quasi-experimental studies found that MNIS are associated with a modest elevation in patient-reported satisfaction with nursing care across multiple clinical settings. The effect size is small, evidence certainty is low, heterogeneity is substantial and all studies are from China; therefore, causal conclusions and broad generalisability are not supported. Future research should employ standardised, validated PREMS, conduct long-term follow-up, evaluate cost-effectiveness and include multinational settings to enhance generalisability.
