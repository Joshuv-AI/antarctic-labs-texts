Recent progress in anti-Chikungunya virus drug discovery: Focus on the viral life cycle and direct-acting antivirals
Authors: Chunjiao Liu, Shixiang Pan, Mingxue Gao, Yumin Zhang, Leike Zhang, Bin Lin, Xiangrui Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) belongs to the genus Alphavirus of the family Togaviridae. CHIKV infection generally causes severe clinical symptoms, including debilitating arthralgia, fever, hemorrhage and cutaneous rashes. In recent years, the transmission range of CHIKV has continued to expand, resulting in recurrent local outbreaks in densely populated and economically developed regions and posing a severe threat to public health. Accordingly, safe and effective anti-CHIKV therapeutics are urgently needed for the clinical treatment of infected patients. Nevertheless, no specific anti-CHIKV drugs have been approved for clinical use or advanced into clinical trials, and relevant research and development remain confined to the preclinical stage. This review systematically illustrates the key steps of the CHIKV life cycle, core viral components and vital functional domains, and summarizes the current research progress of anti-CHIKV agents. It comprehensively outlines the discovery strategies, structural optimization directions and activity evaluation approaches of anti-CHIKV small-molecule compounds, and further investigates their action targets. Notably, nsP2 and nsP4 represent promising targets for broad-spectrum anti-CHIKV even anti-alphavirus drug development due to their structural conservation across alphavirus species and druggable features. This work provides a solid theoretical basis and valuable reference for the future research and development of novel anti-CHIKV drugs.

Introduction

Chikungunya fever is an acute vector-borne infectious disease caused by the Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), primarily transmitted by Aedes albopictus and Aedes aegypti mosquitoes (Pialoux et al., 2007). CHIKV was first isolated in 1952 in Tanzania, an East African country, with the name “Chikungunya” originating from a local Tanzanian language term that vividly describes the contorted posture of patients due to severe joint pain (Lumsden, 1955; Pialoux et al., 2007). Influenced by globalization, climate warming, and the expanding distribution of mosquito vectors, CHIKV has subsequently caused multiple outbreaks across Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas, spreading to 119 countries and regions (WHO, 2025). Phylogenetic analysis based on the E1 gene sequence classifies CHIKV into three distinct genotypes: the Asian, the West African (WA) genotype, and the East/Central/South African (ECSA) genotype (Tsetsarkin et al., 2011). Among these, the ECSA genotype evolved into two key sub-lineages with enhanced transmission ability, namely the Indian Ocean lineage (IOL) and the ECSA-2 sub-lineage (Wahid et al., 2017). Key global transmission events of CHIKV are summarized in Table 1. In 1958, the first case in Southeast Asia was reported in Bangkok, Thailand (Tiawsirisup, 2011). In 1961, CHIKV was first documented in Cambodia (Cavalcanti et al., 2022). Between 1963 and 1973, successive outbreaks occurred across multiple Indian cities, including Calcutta and Chennai, resulting in more than 300,000 confirmed cases (Arankalle et al., 2007; Yergolkar et al., 2006). An outbreak in coastal Kenya, Africa, infected approximately 500,000 people in 2004 (Kumar et al., 2025). The epidemic spread to Reunion Island in the Indian Ocean, affecting over 260,000 individuals (34% of the island's population) from 2005 to 2006, with approximately 120 patients developing severe clinical manifestations, including neurological symptoms or hepatitis (Holmes et al., 2007; Schuffenecker et al., 2006). The IOL genotype caused this outbreak, and the E1-A226V mutation (alanine to valine) in the viral envelope glycoprotein enhanced transmissibility via Aedes albopictus (Schuffenecker et al., 2006; Sam et al., 2009); concurrently, outbreaks in 13 Indian states resulted in approximately 1.3 million infections (Arankalle et al., 2007). In 2007, Italy reported 205 autochthonous confirmed cases, marking the first known transmission event in a temperate zone (Rezza et al., 2007). In 2013, 658 severe cases emerged on Saint Martin Island in the Caribbean, with a case fatality rate of 1.76%; the epidemic rapidly spread to 45 countries and territories across North, Central, and South America, leading to over 2.9 million suspected cases (Henry et al., 2017; Yactayo et al., 2016). Clinical manifestations of CHIKV infection include fever, arthralgia, hemorrhage, and rash, and typically progress through three phases: acute, subacute, and chronic. The acute phase is characterized primarily by high fever, headache, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, rash, back pain, myalgia, and symmetric polyarthralgia with swelling and pain in the small joints of the hands and feet (Simon-Djamel et al., 2013). Follow-up studies of 368 patients in the subacute and chronic phases revealed that 20%–40% experienced persistent joint pain lasting months to years, approximately 60%–80% developed recurrent arthralgia (Singh et al., 2013), 58.9% reported fatigue or generalized exhaustion, with 2.4% experiencing symptoms persisting for over 1 year (Sánchez et al., 2019). A small proportion (approximately 0.3%) of patients exhibited atypical symptoms involving the cardiovascular, renal, respiratory, gastrointestinal, hepatic, adrenal, and central nervous systems (Gonçalves et al., 2025). Among 7315 patients with neurological disorders, CHIKV positivity rates were 27% for myelitis and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, 15% for Guillain–Barré syndrome, and 12% for encephalitis (Costa et al., 2024). Severe complications of CHIKV infection typically occur in patients with underlying conditions and in individuals aged 65 years and older.

Currently, no safe and effective specific anti-CHIKV drugs are available; clinical management relies primarily on symptomatic treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for arthralgia relief and corticosteroids for controlling chronic inflammation. However, these approaches cannot block the viral replication cycle and may carry the risk of adverse effects with long-term use (Simon et al., 2007, 2015). To date, only two vaccines, Ixchiq® and Vimkunya®, have received approval (Simone and Lienert, 2025; FDA, 2025). Ixchiq® was authorized in November 2023 under the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) accelerated approval pathway for individuals aged 18 years and older at high risk; however, a post-marketing safety monitoring report issued by the FDA in August 2025 identified a temporal association with 21 cases of severe adverse reactions and 3 deaths, leading to the suspension of its marketing authorization (Tindale et al., 2025; FDA, 2023). Although Vimkunya® received simultaneous approval from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the FDA in February 2025, substantial gaps remain in safety and immunogenicity data for immunocompromised populations and those with underlying conditions, thereby limiting its application in these special groups (Joyce et al., 2009; Marques and Burke, 2025). Given the safety of Ixchiq® and population coverage limitation of Vimkunya®, coupled with the scarcity of clinical antiviral agents, the development of highly effective and safe anti-CHIKV therapeutics is particularly urgent. This review systematically examines key stages of the CHIKV life cycle, critical viral elements, and important functional domains. It also compiles the in vitro and in vivo activities as well as the discovery processes of relevant small-molecule inhibitors, thereby providing essential insights and references for anti-CHIKV drug development.

CHIKV structure and replication cycle

CHIKV is a spherical enveloped particle virus approximately 65–70 nm in diameter, composed of glycoproteins, lipid bilayer envelope, and nucleocapsid (Fig. 1A) (Joyce et al., 2009). The core nucleocapsid is formed by the tight association of the capsid protein (CP) with the genomic RNA, exhibiting icosahedral symmetry and a diameter of about 30 nm (Joyce et al., 2009; Oliveira Lima et al., 2025). The CHIKV genome is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA of approximately 12 kb in length, structurally resembling messenger RNA: it features 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (5′ UTR and 3′ UTR), with the 5′ end capped by m7GpppAmp and the 3′ end polyadenylated, enabling direct translation initiation in host cells. The genome comprises two open reading frames (ORFs) (Fig. 1B): the 5′ ORF1 encodes nonstructural proteins (nsP1-nsP4), whereas the 3′ ORF2 encodes structural proteins (C, E3, E2, 6K, and E1) (Joyce et al., 2009; Kawashima et al., 2014; Solignat et al., 2009).Fig. 1The structure and genomic structure of the Chikungunya virus. The CHIKV replication cycle is illustrated in Fig. 2. CHIKV binds specifically to host cell surface receptors (such as AXL and Mxra8) via the E1-E2 glycoprotein heterodimer on its envelope, entering cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis (Holmes et al., 2020; Reis et al., 2021). In the acidic endosomal environment (pH 5.0–6.0), conformational changes in the E1-E2 complex expose the fusion loop (FL) of E1, which inserts into the endosomal membrane, mediating fusion between the viral envelope and the endosomal membrane and releasing the nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm, where it disassembles to liberate the genomic RNA (Kielian et al., 2010). The genomic RNA is translated into a nonstructural polyprotein precursor (nsP1-nsP2-nsP3-nsP4), which undergo proteolytic processing by nsP2 to form the viral replication complex responsible for synthesizing the negative-strand RNA (Joyce et al., 2009). This negative-strand RNA subsequently serves as the template for generating both 49S genomic progeny RNA and 26S subgenomic RNA. The 26S subgenomic RNA is translated into a structural polyprotein precursor (C-E3-E2-6K-E1), from which CP autocatalytically cleaves itself using its intrinsic protease activity and subsequently associates with the 49S positive-strand genomic RNA to assemble the nucleocapsid. The remaining structural proteins pE2-6K-E1 are cleaved by signal peptidase into pE2 (E3-E2 precursor), 6K, and E1 (Solignat et al., 2009; Tan et al., 2022). pE2 is further processed by a furin-like protease to yield mature E3 and E2 proteins (Curtis and Simons, 1988). Meanwhile, E1 and E2 form stable E1/E2 heterodimers that arrange orderly on the host cell membrane surface (Tan et al., 2022). The nucleocapsid migrates to the plasma membrane, associates with the envelope glycoprotein complexes. It is released from the cell surface via budding, producing mature CHIKV particles capable of infecting neighboring cells (Reis et al., 2021).Fig. 2Diagram of the replication cycle of the Chikungunya virus.

Progress in anti-CHIKV drug development

Several reviews have outlined the research trajectory in CHIKV inhibitors, offering valuable references for anti-CHIKV drug development (Li et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2026; de Souza et al., 2025). In recent years, further advances have been made in the study of small-molecule inhibitors targeting CHIKV. This review integrates and analyzes the latest research progress on CHIKV small-molecule inhibitors, thereby providing guidance and references for future anti-CHIKV drug discovery.

CHIKV structural protein inhibitors

Envelope glycoproteins E1, pE2, and their inhibitors

The CHIKV envelope glycoproteins E1, E2, and E3 coordinately regulate viral entry and immune recognition processes, with their structural assembly and functional division exhibiting strong interdependence: the type I transmembrane protein E1 initially forms a heterodimer with the precursor pE2, which subsequently oligomerizes into trimers; during transport to the plasma membrane, pE2 is cleaved by a furin-like protease into mature E2 and E3, ultimately assembling into icosahedrally symmetric spike structures on the virion surface (Oliveira Lima et al., 2025). The ectodomain of E1 consists of three β-sheet-rich domains (I, II, III), with the fusion loop (FL) located at the distal end of domain II serving as the critical functional region that mediates fusion between the viral envelope and host cell membrane (Kielian and Rey, 2006). Spatially behind the FL lies a conserved pocket structure (Pocket A) with a volume of approximately 592 Å3 and 52% hydrophobic residues, representing a viable binding site for small-molecule compounds (Battini et al., 2021). E2 is an entirely β-sheet protein whose spatial structure comprises three functional domains, A (N-terminal), B (central), and C (C-terminal), that form extensive interactions with E3 via a β-ribbon connector (Gaur et al., 2026; Li et al., 2010). In addition to mediating specific binding to host cell receptors, the core functions of E2 include modulation of post-infection inflammatory responses (Gaur et al., 2026; Li et al., 2010). Furthermore, E1 and E2 serve as the major antigenic determinants on the virion surface, harboring multiple immunodominant epitopes capable of eliciting protective host immune responses (Oliveira Lima et al., 2025). E3 plays central roles in regulating the maturation of the glycoprotein complex (E1-E2-E3), maintaining the stability of the surface spike architecture, suppressing premature E1-mediated membrane fusion activity, and ensuring proper initiation of envelope fusion with the host cell membrane (Oliveira Lima et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2008).

Utilizing computational chemistry approaches (such as molecular docking and virtual screening) combined with in vitro validation, researchers have identified several small-molecule compounds targeting CHIKV envelope glycoproteins (Fig. 3A). Passos and colleagues (Passos et al., 2020) discovered that the acrylamide derivative LQM334 (1) binds to domain A of E2 within the E3-E2-E1 glycoprotein complex, forming hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that block viral attachment and entry; at 40 μM, LQM334 exhibited 81% inhibitory activity against CHIKV and reduced the proportion of CHIKV-positive cells from 74.07% to 0.88%. Verma et al. (2022) performed virtual screening against two key binding sites on the CHIKV E1-E2 heterodimer (site 1: posterior to E1 FL; site 2: between E1 domain II and the E2 β-ribbon connector), identifying taxifolin (2), rutin (3), and ChemDiv 8015–3006 (4) as potential stabilizers of the E1-E2 interface that prevent viral entry, exhibiting dose-dependent inhibition of CHIKV with EC50 values of 3.6 μM, 88 μM, and 41 μM, respectively; Rashad and Keller (2013) identified compound 7 (5), which targets Pocket A near the FL at the E1-E2 heterodimer interface, displaying anti-CHIKV activity (EC50 = 9.3 μM, CC50 > 100 μM) (Battini et al., 2021); subsequent structural optimization by Battini et al. (2021) yielded the aminopiperidine compound 11 (6) with enhanced antiviral potency (EC50 = 1.6 μM, CC50 = 56 μM). Compound 11 demonstrated favorable solubility and stability in simulated gastric fluid, simulated intestinal fluid, and phosphate buffer, with half-lives (t1/2) exceeding 120 min in all media.Fig. 3Compounds targeting Chikungunya Virus structural proteins. Capsid protein (CP) and its inhibitors

The capsid protein (CP) is a multifunctional key protein in the viral life cycle, structurally comprising an N-terminal domain and a C-terminal domain. It shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm via intrinsic nuclear localization and nuclear export signals, thereby regulating multiple stages of the viral life cycle (Saijo et al., 2013). The N-terminal domain is a highly disordered region that enables specific binding to viral genomic RNA, facilitating RNA packaging and transport (Roques et al., 2016). The C-terminal domain possesses chymotrypsin-like serine protease activity, enabling autocatalytic cis-cleavage to liberate the capsid protein from the viral polyprotein precursor precisely; this domain also contains a conserved hydrophobic pocket that interacts explicitly with P404 in the cytoplasmic domain of E2 glycoprotein (cdE2), thereby modulating virion budding and release (Hahn et al., 1985; Krummenacher et al., 2012).

As a critical functional protein in the CHIKV life cycle, CP has been targeted by several small-molecule inhibitors (Fig. 3B). Thymoquinone (7), a natural product initially isolated from the essential oil of Nigella sativa L., exhibits anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and antitumor activities (Nainawat et al., 2025). Kumar et al. (2021) demonstrated through molecular docking and in vitro assays that thymoquinone binds to the hydrophobic pocket of CP, disrupting CP-cdE2 and CP-CP interactions to inhibit CHIKV assembly and budding. Activity testing further showed effective suppression of CHIKV replication, achieving a > 90% virus titer reduction (VTR) at 20 μM with low toxicity in Vero cells (EC50 = 4.5 μM, CC50 = 66 μM). Fatma et al. (2020) first applied structure-assisted drug repurposing, identifying P1, P4-di (adenosine-5′)tetraphosphate ammonium salt (8), eptifibatide acetate (9), and paromomycin sulphate (10) as post-entry inhibitors of viral replication via CP binding. These compounds showed EC50 values of 11 μM, 4.0 μM, and 23 μM, respectively, with eptifibatide acetate displaying the highest affinity for CP (KD = 0.4 × 10−3 M) and no notable cytotoxicity or interference with viral RNA synthesis.

6K protein and its inhibitors

The CHIKV 6K protein forms ion channels in the host endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane (Melton et al., 2002), modulating cellular calcium homeostasis and ER membrane permeability to induce cytopathic vacuoles, facilitate virion budding, and regulate transport of envelope glycoproteins such as E1 and pE2 to the plasma membrane; it thus serves as a key regulator of membrane-associated processes throughout the viral life cycle (Antoine et al., 2007). Small-molecule inhibitors targeting the 6K protein are shown in Fig. 3C.

Samy et al. (2019) found that amantadine (11) inhibits the ion channel activity, membrane fusion function, and permeability of the 6K protein, markedly altering the morphology of CHIKV virus-like particles (89.29% abnormal particles) and effectively suppressing CHIKV replication (77% virus titer reduction at 100 μM, IC50 = 30 μM). Lahiri et al. (2025) targeted the 6K-formed ion channel. They screened a 2839-compound repurposing library using three bacterial-based channel assays (negative, positive, and fluorescence-pH assays), identifying four effective blockers with average activity >50% against the CHIKV 6K channel at 100 μM: arterolane (12) (84%), 5-azacytidine (13) (64%), sulfabenzamide (14) (60%), and saroglitazar (15) (52%). Structural prediction and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that 6K functions as a trimer, forming an open funnel-shaped (SUS-type) ion channel architecture.

CHIKV nonstructural protein inhibitors

nsP1 and its inhibitors

CHIKV nsP1 possesses both methyltransferase (MTase) and guanylyltransferase (GTase) activities, catalyzing the formation of the 5′ Cap-0 structure (m7GpppA) on viral RNA (Yien et al., 2023). The nsP1 MTase transfers a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to GTP, forming m7GTP and the byproduct S-adenosyl-l-homocysteine (SAH), which is subsequently hydrolyzed by host SAH hydrolase. Under GTase activity, m7GTP forms a covalent m7GMP-nsP1 intermediate, and the m7GMP is ultimately transferred to the 5′ diphosphate end of viral mRNA to complete capping. This 5′ cap protects viral RNA from host endonuclease degradation, promotes efficient translation of viral mRNA, and plays a crucial role in evading innate immunity (Decroly et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2023; Roques et al., 2016; Wolfe and Borchardt, 1991).

Gigante et al. (2017) were the first to report that 3-aryl-[1,2,3]triazolo [4,5-d]pyrimidin-7 (6H)-one derivatives exhibit anti-CHIKV activity. Among these, MADTP372 (16) potently inhibited replication of the CHIKV 899 strain (EC50 = 2.6 μM, CC50 = 167 μM) and also showed activity against clinical isolates such as Venturini and Congo 95 (EC50 ranging from 0.75 μM to 4.9 μM). Delang et al. (2016) subsequently elucidated the mechanism, demonstrating that this class compound inhibits the GTase activity of CHIKV nsP1, thereby blocking viral RNA capping; resistance is associated with the nsP1-P34S mutation. Building on this, Gómez-SanJuan et al. (2018) performed structural modifications to yield compounds 9b (17) and 15e (18) with improved antiviral potency against CHIKV 899, Congo 95, and other clinical isolates (EC50 values of 0.12 μM–5.2 μM and 1.0 μM–8.9 μM, respectively). Further optimization by Gigante et al. (2017) produced oxime derivatives with enhanced solubility and target affinity, including compounds 8 (19) (EC50 values of 1.1 μM–5.2 μM), 18b (20) (EC50 values of 2.5 μM–12 μM), and 18f (21) (EC50 values of 3 μM–14 μM). The in vitro enzymatic assay confirmed inhibition of alphavirus nsP1 GTase activity, with 18f showing an IC50 < 2 μM. Kovacikova et al. (2021) performed docking of MADTP and CHVB compounds with CHIKV nsP1 and found these compounds bind at the SAM site within the capping domain, acting as competitive or non-competitive inhibitors. Other targeted inhibitors are shown in Fig. 5B. Feibelman et al. (2018) combined high-throughput screening with in vitro assays and identified the natural product lobaric acid (32), which competitively binds to the GTP-binding site of CHIKV nsP1 at low micromolar concentrations (Ki = 7.0 ± 0.6 μM), thereby inhibiting its guanylyltransferase activity. Lobaric acid effectively suppressed viral replication in both baby hamster kidney (BHK) cells and human hepatoma (Huh-7) cells, with EC50 values of 8.9 μM and 5.3 μM, respectively.

Drug repurposing represents an efficient strategy in antiviral development. Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), already approved by the U.S. FDA, have demonstrated antiviral activity against vesicular stomatitis virus, hepatitis C virus, Ebola virus, and dengue virus, among others (Johansen et al., 2013; Montoya and Krysan, 2018; Murakami et al., 2013; Tohma et al., 2019). Mudgal et al. (2022) evaluated three SERMs, 4-hydroxytamoxifen (33), tamoxifen (34), and clomifene (35), and found that all specifically inhibit both MTase and GTase activities of nsP1, blocking viral RNA capping and exerting anti-CHIKV effects (EC50 values of 0.93 μM–4.0 μM). Notably, tamoxifen suppressed CHIKV replication in infected mice and alleviated musculoskeletal lesions. 5-Iodotubercidin (36), an adenosine kinase inhibitor derived from toyocamycin (7-deazaadenosine), possesses antitumor and antitubercular properties (Zhang et al., 2017). Using a non-radioactive capillary electrophoresis assay, Mudgal et al. (2019) showed that 5-iodotubercidin targets CHIKV nsP1 GTase, inhibiting early replication and post-entry stages (IC50 = 11 μM, EC50 = 0.41 μM). Bhutkar et al. (2024) performed structure-based virtual screening of FDA-approved drugs and identified berbamine hydrochloride (37), venetoclax (ABT-199, 38), and ponatinib (39) as stable binders to CHIKV nsP1 MTase (KD ranging from 3.8 μM to 161.3 μM), exhibiting dose-dependent reductions in viral titer and mRNA levels with IC50 values of < 100 nM, ∼6.8 nM, and < 3.9 nM, respectively.

nsP2 and its inhibitors

The nsP2 protein of CHIKV consists of two domains: the N-terminal domain and the C-terminal domain. The N-terminal domain of nsP2 exhibits multiple enzymatic activities, including RNA helicase (nsP2 hel), nucleoside triphosphatase (NTPase), and 5′-triphosphatase (RTPase). It can hydrolyze all NTP substrates except deoxythymidine triphosphate; both NTPase and RTPase activities are strictly Mg2+-dependent, and the RTPase specifically removes the γ-phosphate from the 5′ end of RNA molecules (Karpe et al., 2011). The helicase activity harnesses ATP hydrolysis to unwind double-stranded RNA intermediates formed during viral RNA replication, providing single-stranded templates for subsequent synthesis (Law et al., 2019). The C-terminal contains a protease domain (nsP2pro) and a catalytically inactive methyltransferase-like (MTase-like) domain. The nsP2pro harbors a conserved Cys478-His548 catalytic dyad that specifically cleaves the viral nonstructural polyprotein (Nguyen et al., 2015; Strauss et al., 1992). CHIKV nsP2 translocates to the host cell nucleus, where it induces transcriptional shutoff by triggering polyubiquitination of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase II, thereby suppressing host mRNA synthesis (Ballista et al., 2024).

The protease, RTPase, and helicase activities of CHIKV nsP2 play essential roles throughout the viral life cycle, making nsP2 a prime target for drug development. To address the high reactivity of vinyl sulfone–based covalent warheads toward glutathione (GSH) and their susceptibility to species-dependent clearance mediated by GST catalysis, compound 5 (47) was identified as an inhibitor against CHIKV with a pIC50 of 6.3. Although it undergoes rapid metabolic clearance due to hepatic glucuronidation, structure–activity relationship studies have pinpointed the key sites responsible for its metabolic instability, providing guidance for subsequent structural modifications. Notably, this work also establishes the amino sulfonate acetamide class as a novel type of covalent inhibitor targeting nsP2 for the first time (Ghoshal et al., 2025). Utilizing the unique mechanism of the vinyl sulfone warhead, Metibemu et al. (2024) identified tetrahydroquinoline derivative J13 (48) as a covalent inhibitor of CHIKV nsP2pro. It exhibited potent anti-CHIKV activity in Vero cells (EC50 = 0.39 μM, CC50 > 50 μM) and was metabolically stable in human liver microsomes (HLM) and S9 fractions. J13 exhibited excellent oral bioavailability (95.6%) in mice; although it did not reduce viral titer, it markedly alleviated Chikungunya-associated footpad swelling with good safety (normal serum biomarkers, no apparent off-target effects), positioning it as a promising candidate for anti-CHIKV development. Semapimod (49), a tetravalent guanylhydrazone, targets the Toll-like receptor (TLR) chaperone gp96, inhibiting TLR signaling and reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby mitigating virus-induced inflammatory damage (Wang et al., 2016). Li et al. Temizer Keser et al. Jadav et al. (2015) synthesized and screened 20 arylidene derivatives of 1,3-thiazolidin-4-one, identifying compound 7 (51), which forms hydrophobic interactions with the S2/S3 pocket and hydrogen bonds with residues such as TYR1047, thereby inhibiting nsP2pro activity (IC50 = 0.42 μM, CC50 > 100 μM). Souza et al. (2023) used rational drug design to synthesize 1560 acrylamide derivatives and identified four competitive inhibitors: LQM330 (52), LQM333 (53), LQM336 (54), and LQM338 (55). All stably bind CHIKV nsP2pro via hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces, suppressing viral activity (IC50 values of 13 μM, 20 μM, 0.95 μM, and 15 μM, respectively). Mishra et al. (2016) designed and synthesized MBZM-N-IBT (56) based on 2-methylbenzimidazole and isatin-β-thiosemicarbazone scaffolds. At 200 μM, it reduced the formation of infectious CHIKV particles by 76.02% and inhibited nsP2 activity by over 97%, with EC50 values of 39 μM–60 μM and low cytotoxicity. Mechanistic studies revealed that it binds the C-terminal domain of nsP2, inhibiting protease activity while downregulating MAPK-, NF-κB-, and COX-2-mediated inflammatory pathways, thereby significantly decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. In 10–12-day-old C57BL/6 mice, oral administration of 15 mg/kg twice daily reduced serum viral titer by 93%, markedly lowered viral RNA and protein levels in muscle, liver, brain, and other tissues, ameliorated CHIKV-induced limb swelling and splenic damage, and achieved 100% survival with the twice-daily regimen. Additionally, MBZM-N-IBT inhibited 98% of CHIKV infection in monocyte-derived macrophages from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMC) at 100 μM (Saikat et al., 2022).

Ivanova, et al. (2021a) used molecular docking to identify 1,3-thiazole benzamide derivatives 10 (57) and 10c (58), both of which block the active site and catalytic dyad of CHIKV nsP2pro. In BHK cells, these compounds exhibited IC50 values of 13 μM and 8.3 μM, respectively, with compound 10 completely abolishing viral protein expression at 200 μM. Das et al. (2016) designed 12 compounds targeting CHIKV nsP2pro and found that stereochemistry significantly influences target binding. Compounds 8 (59) and 1c (60) (the trans isomer of compound 1) potently inhibited CHIKV activity (EC50 values of 1.5 μM and 50 μM, respectively) and showed concentration-dependent inhibition of nsP2pro (IC50 values of 50 μM and 100 μM). Using 1c as a lead, Ivanova et al. (2021b) optimized the structure and identified the S,S,S-configured D160d (61) as having the best antiviral activity (EC50 = 4.8 μM). Molecular dynamics simulations clarified that the core mechanism involves blocking the conformational changes required for nsP2pro substrate recognition, highlighting the critical role of stereochemistry and binding mode.

Several research teams have used drug repurposing to identify approved drugs targeting CHIKV nsP2pro: Tripathi et al. (2020) reported novobiocin (62) and telmisartan (63) as nsP2pro inhibitors (IC50 ∼ 2 μM and 5 μM, respectively), exerting antiviral effects by blocking viral proliferation (EC50 values of 20 μM and 45 μM). Rabelo et al. performed virtual screening against nsP2pro and identified the antiparasitic agent OBZ (64), which showed activity against both Asian (181/25) and ECSA (BRA/RJ/18) genotypes (EC50 values of 66 μM and 11 μM, respectively) (Kaduk et al., 2023; Rabelo et al., 2024). Saha et al. (2018) found that zinc acetate (ZnAc, 65) stably binds the catalytic dyad of CHIKV nsP2, blocking the active site and reducing viral load by > 3 logs (99.97%) in Vero cells in a dose-dependent manner. Li et al. (2025b) further demonstrated that zinc acetate inhibits full-length nsP2pro and its cleavage of nsP2/3 and nsP3/4 junctions (IC50 values of 0.19 μM, 0.69 μM, and 0.14 μM, respectively).

Several antiviral natural products targeting CHIKV nsP2pro have been identified. Rathore et al. (2024) reported withaferin A (66), a steroidal lactone from Withania somnifera, as an nsP2pro inhibitor; at 10 μM it achieved >80% inhibition in multiple cell lines (IC50 0.45 μM–0.51 μM). In C57BL/6 mice, twice-daily intraperitoneal administration of 5 mg/kg reduced viremia by 50% and eliminated joint swelling. Weber et al. (2015) demonstrated that epigallocatechin gallate (67) inhibits CHIKV infection (IC50 = 6.5 μg/mL), blocks entry of CHIKV-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors, and suppresses fusion with the cell membrane. Setyawati et al. (2024) used molecular dynamics simulations to show that epigallocatechin gallate binds catalytic residues of nsP2pro, altering the pocket conformation and inhibiting activity without disrupting the overall protein structure. Radicicol (68), a macrocyclic lactone antifungal antibiotic isolated from the fungus Monosporium bonorden (Delmotte and Delmotte-Plaque, 1953), was discovered by Nam et al. (2021) via high-throughput screening to target the methyltransferase-like domain of CHIKV nsP2, disrupting its interaction with host Hsp90β and dose-dependently inhibiting replication (EC50 = 0.04 μM, CC50 = 6.4 μM).

nsP3 and its inhibitors

CHIKV nsP3 consists of three main domains: the N-terminal macro domain (nsP3MD), the central alphavirus unique domain (AUD), and the C-terminal hypervariable domain (HVD). nsP3MD exhibits RNA-binding and ADP-ribose (ADPr) binding/hydrolysis activities, efficiently binding with monomeric ADPr and cleaving it from post-translationally modified proteins, making it a promising target for anti-CHIKV drug development (Aschenbrenner et al., 2024; Malet et al., 2009). The central AUD features a unique zinc-binding fold coordinated by four cysteine residues, playing a critical role throughout CHIKV RNA synthesis (Kuhn et al., 2019). The HVD is intrinsically disordered and contains multiple dispersed phosphorylation sites whose modification directly influences viral replication (Teppor et al., 2021). Ahmed et al. (2021) found that compound 1a (72) reduced viral titer by 2.5 logs at 10 μM (EC90 0.39 μM–1.5 μM) with low toxicity in normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDF; CC50 = 169 μM). As a key functional domain in the CHIKV replication cycle, nsP3MD has emerged as a major target for screening natural products and derivatives. Priya et al. The in vitro assay and docking revealed that 17d (75), 17f (76), and 18h (77) exert multi-stage antiviral effects by targeting both E protein and nsP3MD (IC50 0.95 μM, 1.95 μM, and 0.30 μM, respectively).

nsP4 and its inhibitors

nsP4 possesses RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and terminal adenylyltransferase (TATase) activities. Its C-terminal region contains the RdRp domain, and both full-length nsP4 (nsP4FL) and the isolated RdRp domain catalyze RNA polymerization. The N-terminal region includes an alphavirus-specific N-terminal domain (NTD) whose function remains unclear (Chen et al., 2017; Simon et al., 2007). The RdRp contains seven conserved motifs (G/F/A/B/C/D/E) from N to C terminus, forming a right-hand-like “palm-fingers-thumb” architecture, with the catalytic site in motif C featuring the conserved “GDD” sequence, a virus-specific target (Ganeshpurkar et al., 2019; Reyes-Gastellou et al., 2021). Chemical structures of nsP4 inhibitors.

Nucleoside analogs mimic natural nucleotides, incorporate into viral RNA during replication, and block viral replication. Ferreira et al. (2018) validated sofosbuvir (78) as an RdRp-targeted inhibitor in Huh-7 cells (EC90 1.0 μM–2.7 μM); it reduced replication and cell death in human iPSC-derived astrocytes (EC90 = 17 μM). In adult mice, 20 mg/kg/day alleviated paw edema; in neonates, 40–80 mg/kg/day improved survival and prevented neuromotor sequelae. Its mechanism of action lies in mimicking uridine analogs, binding to the RNA polymerase of the Chikungunya virus and terminating the elongation process of the RNA chain (Ferreira et al., 2018; Verwimp et al., 2025). Favipiravir (T-705, 79), a nucleoside analog approved in Japan in 2014 for influenza (Furuta et al., 2017), is intracellularly phosphorylated to its active ribofuranosyl triphosphate (T-705-RTP), which is recognized by nsP4 RdRp and inhibits polymerase activity (Furuta et al., 2005). Lacking base-pairing specificity, T-705-RTP induces A→G and U→C transitions, leading to lethal mutagenesis (Baranovich et al., 2013). Delang et al. (2014) confirmed that T-705 and its defluoro analog T-1105 (80) inhibit CHIKV replication in cell culture and mice (EC50 ranges from 0.7 μM to 13 μM), reducing mortality and brain viral load. Its mechanism of action is to simulate purine nucleoside analogs and bind to the RNA polymerase of the Chikungunya virus, thereby inducing lethal mutations (Delang et al., 2014; Verwimp et al., 2025). The combined use of sofosbuvir and T-705 acts on the RdRp of CHIKV through complementary mechanisms, resulting in a significant synergistic effect on human skin fibroblasts (Billis score = 14.3, Maximum Synergistic Area = 33.1). The in vivo verification of this finding has pointed out the key direction for subsequent research (Verwimp et al., 2025).

Zhang et al. found that VV261 (82) exhibits significant anti-CHIKV activity, showing potent inhibitory effects against both the CHIKV Asian strain and ECSA strain in various cell lines including BHK-21 and Huh-7, with EC50 values ranging from 99 nM to 326 nM. Mechanistic studies demonstrate that VV261 acts at the post-entry stage to block viral replication in a dose-dependent manner. Its antiviral effect is competitively antagonized by exogenous uridine and cytidine, consistent with action as a pyrimidine analog that inhibits viral RNA synthesis, without inducing lethal mutations in progeny viral genomes. In IFNAR-knockout (A129) mice, oral VV261 reduces tissue viral loads dose-dependently. At 10 mg/kg, viral titers in infected ankles fall below the limit of detection, matching the efficacy of 400 mg/kg T-705. VV261 also mitigates viremia, downregulates IL-6, TNF-α, and other inflammatory mediators, reduces paw swelling, and alleviates clinical signs including limb weakness and piloerection. The effective in vivo doses (2.5–10 mg/kg) are markedly lower than the toxic threshold (500 mg/kg) in SD rats. Pharmacokinetic modeling predicts that human oral dosing of 10 mg/kg VV261 yields peak 4′-FlU (81) levels ∼9000-fold above its anti-CHIKV EC50 (Zhang et al., 2026). Notably, VV261 is a double prodrug of 4′-FlU. Medicinal chemists systematically performed prodrug modifications on the ribose hydroxyl groups of 4′-FlU and the imide hydrogen at the N3 position of the uracil ring, and compared the physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties of various prodrugs. These efforts ultimately successfully addressed the poor chemical stability of 4′-FlU and identified VV261 as a broad-spectrum antiviral candidate. (Cheng et al., 2025).

Multiple teams have identified RdRp-targeted candidates through screening and validation: Agrawal et al. (2024) showed that lycorine (83) does not block entry but binds near the palm/fingers of CHIKV RdRp catalytic site, inhibiting negative-strand RNA synthesis in Vero, Huh-7, and A549 cells (IC50 0.21 μM–0.32 μM, CC50 > 50 μM); at 2.5 μM it completely blocked envelope protein expression. Wada et al. (2017) identified benzimidazole A (84), which targets M2295 in nsP4 to inhibit RdRp and suppresses multiple clinical isolates (EC50 values of 0.54 μM–1.3 μM, CC50 = 3.7 μM). Freire et al. (2022) reported that LabMol-309 (85) binds the nsP4 active site and confirmed activity in CHIKV replication systems (EC50 = 10 μM) and nanoluc reporter viruses (EC50 = 5.2 μM), providing key target features and a lead molecule. Pareek et al. (2022) validated piperine (86), 2-thiouridine (87), pyrazinamide (88), and chlorogenic acid (89) binding to the nucleotide site of CHIKV RdRp (KD 0.08 μM–9.9 μM) and anti-CHIKV activity (EC50 ranging from 6.7 μM to 54 μM). Yin et al. (2025) identified GAP-1173149 (90 and GAP-1146924 (91) via virtual screening as effective RdRp inhibitors (EC50 values of 9.3 μM–19 μM). Resistance studies have shown that nsP4-A350V confers resistance to GAP-1173149 and nsP4-Q542L to GAP-1146924; mechanistic differences include GAP-1173149 binding near the GDD motif with H-bonding to E553, whereas GAP-1146924 binds to the opposite side of the GDD motif with H-bonding to R593.

Summary and outlook

Discovery strategy for antiviral leads against CHIKV

To date, the current discovery of antiviral leads against CHIKV has formed a pattern of multiple pathways running in parallel and multiple technologies complementing each other. The main sources can be classified into the following five categories.(1)Structure-based virtual screening and computational chemistry-assisted discovery. Based on the three-dimensional structures of key proteins in the CHIKV life cycle, through computational chemistry methods such as molecular docking, ligand-receptor model construction, and virtual screening, potential ligands are enriched from the compound library, and then the activity is verified through in vitro experiments. For instance, Verma et al. conducted virtual screening on the two key binding sites of the CHIKV E1-E2 heterodimer, and discovered natural product inhibitors such as taxifolin (2) and rutin (3); Bhutkar et al. based on the structure of nsP1 MTase, conducted virtual screening of FDA-approved drugs, and screened out old drugs such as berbamine hydrochloride (37) and venetoclax (38) that can target this target; Zhang et al. through virtual screening combined with X-ray crystallography, discovered compounds bearing a 2-pyrimidinone-4-carboxylic acid core that can specifically bind to the ADPr binding site of nsP3MD.(2)Fragment-based drug discovery. Starting from low-molecular-weight fragment libraries, combined with techniques such as crystallography, surface plasmon resonance, and covalent fragment screening, fragments with interactions with the target were screened out, and then through fragment assembly and structure optimization, high-affinity lead compounds were obtained. For example, Merten et al. through covalent fragment screening, discovered a fragment with an ethylene sulfone warhead that specifically modified the catalytic cysteine (C478) of nsP2pro (RA-0002034, 44).(3)High-throughput screening and phenotypic screening. Using cytopathic effect, RNA replication level, or protein expression level after CHIKV infection as evaluation indicators, screening of large compound libraries was conducted to obtain lead compounds with cellular-level antiviral activity, without relying on target information. For example, Garzan et al. obtained thiazoleamide-based lead compounds through phenotypic screening, and after optimization, compound 26 (103) was obtained with significantly improved activity and metabolic stability.(4)Mining of natural products and their derivatives. Currently, the anti-CHIKV natural products cover various structural types such as flavonoids, steroidal esters, and macrolides: Thymoquinone (7) can target the hydrophobic pocket of CP to inhibit CHIKV assembly; Radicicol (68) blocks viral replication by targeting the methyltransferase-like domain of nsP2; Lycorine (83) can bind to the palm/finger domain of nsP4 RdRp to inhibit the synthesis of negative-strand RNA of the virus; withaferin A (66) has a strong inhibitory effect on nsP2pro, and can alleviate infection symptoms in mouse models. On this basis, researchers further improved the antiviral activity and drug-like properties of natural products through structural modification, such as the arylalkyne and 1,2,3-triazole modifications of α-mangostin derivatives.(5)Drug repositioning. Utilizing the advantages of sufficient safety data and short development cycle of marketed drugs, based on target homology, structural similarity, or cross-phenotypic activity, the potential of anti-CHIKV drugs was explored. To illustrate, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) such as 4-hydroxytamoxifen (33) and tamoxifen (34) can inhibit the MTase and GTase activities of nsP1; Antiviral drugs sofosbuvir (78) and favipiravir (79) exhibit broad-spectrum activity targeting RdRp across different viral families; Starting from ARB (107) and retaining the indole core structure, Scuotto et al. optimized the thiophenol substituent and ester group to obtain derivative IIc (110) with improved antiviral activity.

On this basis, researchers further improved the antiviral activity and drug-like properties of natural products through structural modification, such as the arylalkyne and 1,2,3-triazole modifications of α-mangostin derivatives.

(5)Drug repositioning. Utilizing the advantages of sufficient safety data and short development cycle of marketed drugs, based on target homology, structural similarity, or cross-phenotypic activity, the potential of anti-CHIKV drugs was explored. To illustrate, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) such as 4-hydroxytamoxifen (33) and tamoxifen (34) can inhibit the MTase and GTase activities of nsP1; Antiviral drugs sofosbuvir (78) and favipiravir (79) exhibit broad-spectrum activity targeting RdRp across different viral families; Starting from ARB (107) and retaining the indole core structure, Scuotto et al. optimized the thiophenol substituent and ester group to obtain derivative IIc (110) with improved antiviral activity.

Structural optimization direction of antiviral leads against CHIKV

The current structural optimization of compounds against CHIKV focuses on the goals of " enhancing antiviral activity, improving drug-like properties, and raising the barrier to drug resistance.”, and comprehensively employs structural-assisted drug design techniques and classic drug chemistry structure optimization methods (such as scaffold hopping, bioisostere, etc.) to carry out structural optimization. It mainly includes the following three optimization directions.(1)Enhance target affinity and antiviral activity. One common optimization strategy is to enable covalent interactions between the compound and the target. For targets containing cysteine residues, such as Cys478 of nsP2pro, electrophilic payloads (e.g., vinyl sulfone and acrylamide) are introduced to form C-S covalent bond between the compound and residue. Representative examples include RA-0002034 (44) and J13 (48), both of which exhibit IC50 values in the nanomolar range. A second approach focuses on enhancing non-covalent interactions: aromatic rings, heterocycles, halogens, and other functional groups are incorporated into key regions of the target pocket to strengthen hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces with amino acid residues. For instance, Battini et al. optimized compound 7 (5) to develop the amido-piperidine derivative compound 11 (6), which improved antiviral activity (EC50 decreased from 9.3 μM to 1.6 μM). The third strategy involves rigidifying the compound scaffold; conformational stability of binding is enhanced through ring formation, lactone modification, and polycyclic design. As an illustration, Muthu Ramalingam et al. modified lactopiperidine-type compounds into cyclo-sulfonamide inhibitors, reducing the IC50 of these compounds against nsP2 hel from greater than 100 μM to 500 nM.(2)Improve pharmacokinetics (PK) and physicochemical properties. Favorable in vivo pharmacokinetic and physicochemical properties are prerequisites for preclinical candidate drugs. Structural optimization reviewed in this article mainly focuses on water solubility, metabolic stability, and membrane permeability: first, improve water solubility by introducing polar groups (such as sulfonamide, amino, hydroxyl), adjusting the lipid-water partition coefficient (logP) to improve the water solubility of the compound, such as the oxime type derivatives optimized by Gigante, significantly enhancing the water solubility of the compound; second, improve metabolic stability, such as optimizing the covalent payload of RA-0002034 (44) by replacing the vinyl sulfone payload with an N-alkyl amino sulfonic acid ester payload to reduce its non-enzymatic binding with GSH, improving the half-life of compound 5 (47); third, improve membrane permeability by adjusting the hydrophobicity of the backbone, enhancing the cell penetration ability of the compound, such as the 6′-fluoro-arylmycin series designed by Yoon et al. (2019), enhancing intracellular absorption by optimizing the structure. fourth, optimize PK and physicochemical properties via prodrug modification, VV261 (82), a novel 4′-FlU (81) double prodrug with three isobutyryl groups on the ribose moiety and a nicotinoyloxymethyl group linked to the imide-nitrogen on the base moiety, exhibiting notable chemical stability and favorable PK properties.(3)Enhance the barrier to drug resistance, responding to viral mutations. First, target highly conserved sites of viral proteins, such as the GDD catalytic motif of nsP4 RdRp, the Cys478-His548 catalytic dimer of nsP2pro, and the conserved pocket of the E1 FL, designing compounds that are not prone to develop resistance, such as favipiravir (79) targeting the conserved site of RdRp, although the resistance mutation K291R is highly conserved in positive-strand RNA viruses, and these variants can still be inhibited by high-concentration drugs; second, design multi-target inhibitors, targeting multiple viral proteins or viral protein-homologous host factors, such as, Ahmed et al. discovered compound 8q (74), which can simultaneously target nsP3MD and human dihydroorotase, reducing the probability of virus resistance through a single target mutation; third, optimize the binding mode of the compound, guiding structural modification through molecular dynamics simulation to form multiple interactions between the compound and the target, enhancing the barrier to drug resistance, such as CHVB series compounds requiring simultaneous S454G and W456R double mutations in nsP1 to develop resistance.

Screening method for antiviral compounds against CHIKV

Because there are no approved, specific antiviral drugs for CHIKF, the current discovery of antiviral therapeutics against CHIKV based on multiple targets with multiple methods. Virtual screening and biochemical assays support active compound identification targeting certain proteins. Alternatively, cell-based screening for inhibitors is able to provide direct antiviral effect regardless of their targets, giving a rapid route for repurposing clinical drugs.(1)Virtual screening and biochemical assays. CHIKV replication relies on several pivotal viral non-structural proteins which act as specific enzymes, such as nsp4 (polymerase), nsp2 (helicase and protease), and nsp3 (macrodomain with ADP-ribosyl hydrolase activity). In addition, viral envelope glycoproteins E1-E2 can be also targeted to prevent viral attachment or membrane fusion. Technically, based on protein-ligand interaction principles, inhibitor screening for all these targets could be applied by virtual docking and then verified by affinity assays such as thermal stability measurements. Marjorie C et al. discovered a novel compound LabMol-309 (85) targeting CHIKV-nsp4 using computational project and differential scanning fluorimetry, proving that it has EC50 of 5.2 μM against CHIKV replicon in BHK-21 cells. In silico docking is also widely used for inhibitor identification against nsp2pro and nsp3MD, but generally, the activity of inhibitor output needs to be confirmed through enzymatic study. Therefore, enzymatic assays for identifying nsp2pro or nsp3MD inhibitors would more close to their actual functions. Enzymatic assays of nsp2pro rely on a synthetic peptide substrate that mimics the natural cleavage sites of the CHIKV polyprotein (typically the junctions between nsp1/nsp2, nsp2/nsp3, or nsp3/nsp4). The nsp2pro recognizes the specific amino acid sequence of this synthetic peptide and cuts it. Based on this mechanism, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) is the most widely used mechanism for high-throughput screening of nsp2pro inhibitors. It relies on the energy transfer between two light-sensitive molecules synthetized into substrates. If a test compound effectively inhibits nsp2pro, the enzyme fails to cleave the substrate, and the fluorophore remains quenched. A corresponding reduction in fluorescence signal thus indicates the presence of a potent inhibitor. For nsp3MD, similar fluorescence high-throughput methods include fluorescence polarization binding assays and AlphaScreen for inhibitor identification (Sawada et al., 2023; Mattila et al., 2021). However, since the macrodomain is both a “reader” (bind ADP-ribose) and an “eraser” (cleavage), screening assays for this target turns out more complex than that for nsp2pro.(2)Cytopathic effect (CPE) reduction assays. CPE reduction assays are among the most classic and intuitive methods for antiviral phenotypic screening. When CHIKV infects susceptible host cells in vitro (such as Vero or BHK-21 cells), it rapidly induces significant changes in cell morphology and large-scale cell death. The core of this assay lies in evaluating the extent to which a candidate drug can “rescue” infected cells. In practical applications, researchers typically combine this with cell viability dyes (such as MTT reagent or CellTiter-Glo luminescent assay) to quantify the proportion of surviving cells in a high-throughput manner. Rafidah et al. (2015) utilized a CPE reduction assay on CHIKV-infected Vero cells to evaluate the antiviral activity of flavonoids and successfully demonstrated that the compound silymarin significantly inhibited CHIKV-induced CPE at a concentration of 100 μg/ml. Similarly, Saha et al. (2023) employed a CPE inhibition assay to evaluate a series of small-molecule libraries against CHIKV. The researchers identified a 2,4-diaminoquinazoline derivative, DCR 137 (96), which significantly reduced CHIKV replication and associated cytopathic effects in a dose-dependent manner. CPE reduction assays can directly determine the EC50 and CC50 of a compound, thereby evaluating the selectivity index of a compound. Its advantage is that it simulates the complete viral life cycle, but it is relatively insufficient in identifying the specific target mechanism of the antiviral compound.(3)Replicon assays. Replicon assays utilize a genetically modified and highly safe subgenomic in vitro cell model. In the CHIKV replicon, genes encoding viral structural proteins (such as capsid proteins and E1/E2 glycoproteins) are artificially deleted and replaced with reporter genes or antibiotic resistance genes. Pohjala et al. (2017) developed a dual-reporter CHIKV replicon expressing both EGFP and Renilla luciferase to screen natural compound libraries and successfully identified specific flavonoids and other small molecules that inhibit CHIKV replication without host-cell toxicity. Lani et al. applied the same replicon system to validate that baicalein (101) inhibited the Rluc activity with an IC50 of 3.24 μg/ml. Due to the lack of structural genes, when this replicon is introduced into cells, it can autonomously carry out RNA replication and non-structural protein translation, but it is unable to assemble into infectious, mature progeny viral particles. Since there is no intact infectious virus, the experiment does not need to be conducted in a strict Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory; a standard BSL-2 laboratory is sufficient. Additionally, the pure replication and translation process makes it an ideal tool specifically for verifying whether non-structural protein inhibitors possess intracellular antiviral activity.(4)Reporter virus assays. Reporter viruses are fully infectious viruses (commonly requiring BSL-3 containment) that have been genetically engineered to express a reporter protein (such as mCherry, GFP, or nanoLuciferase) during infection. Unlike replicons, reporter viruses allow researchers to screen for inhibitors that target any stage of the viral life cycle, including viral entry, assembly, and release. With the reverse genetics’ technologies, reporter virus assays have greatly accelerated the screening process for antiviral drugs. This method uses genetic engineering techniques to insert a reporter gene such as green fluorescent protein EGFP, mCherry, or firefly luciferase into the fully infectious CHIKV genome. When this recombinant live virus infects host cells and begins to replicate, the reporter gene is expressed synchronously with viral proteins, producing fluorescent or luminescent signals that can be precisely quantified by instruments. Castro and Álvarez. (2024) used a reporter virus, CHIKV-ZsGreen, in a high-throughput screening and four molecules were identified as potential drug candidates against CHIKV. Zhang et al. constructed a reporter CHIKV 181/25 strain expressing nanoluciferase to rapidly screen active nucleotide analogs, such as VV261 (82). Compared to the traditional, time-consuming plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) or simple CPE observation, reporter virus assays convert the results into rapidly readable optical signals, making them highly suitable for high-throughput screening of large compound libraries. Simultaneously, they allow researchers to track the dynamics of viral infection and drug intervention in real-time within living cells.

The nsP2pro plays a pivotal role in CHIKV replication, as it mediates the proteolytic cleavage of the nsP1-4 polyprotein into mature nsP1, nsP2, nsP3, and nsP4 subunits. These processed proteins further assemble into the viral replication complex (VRC), a prerequisite for viral genome replication. Inhibition of the activity of nsP2pro thus acts at the “upstream” stage to abrogate VRC formation, thereby blocking CHIKV RNA replication in a targeted manner. Notably, three-dimensional structures of CHIKV nsP2pro (PDB IDs: 3TRK, 4ZTB) reveal that the catalytic site of nsP2pro is highly conserved across strains, featuring a canonical catalytic dyad composed of cysteine and histidine residues.

The Cys-His catalytic dyad contained within the catalytic site of nsP2pro is highly conserved in both primary sequence and structure between the Alphavirus genus (Narwal et al., 2018; Russo et al., 2006), and researchers have carried out numerous studies on anti-Togavirus drugs targeting this conserved site (Ghoshal et al., 2024, 2025; Ivanova et al., 2021a, Ivanova et al., 2021b; Merten et al., 2024; Metibemu et al., 2024). In addition, a recent study has revealed that beyond the conserved catalytic site, nsP2pro possesses an additional highly conserved, druggable allosteric regulatory pocket (Sears et al., 2025), which exhibits conserved structural and functional features throughout the alphavirus family. Given its conserved catalytic site and druggable allosteric pocket, the protease domain of nsP2 not only serves as a highly promising therapeutic target for anti-CHIKV drug development, but also represents an important candidate target for the research and development of broad-spectrum anti-alphavirus agents. Additionally, nsP4 harbors RdRp activity, which is another core component of the viral replication machinery. The catalytic core of RdRp contains a highly conserved GDD motif, which remains invariant across diverse CHIKV strains and strictly conserved among other Alphavirus genus members (Ghildiyal et al., 2019). This striking conservation across CHIKV strain and alphavirus species, combined with the indispensable role of nsP4 in viral replication, strongly highlights its considerable potential as a broad-spectrum antiviral target for anti-CHIKV and pan-alphavirus drug development.

According to the report released by the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2025, a total of over 500,000 cases of chikungunya fever (CHIKF) had been reported globally, involving 41 countries and regions, including China, which is a country that has not recorded CHIKF epidemic in recent years. The transmission range of CHIKV is continuously expanding. The research and reserve work on anti-CHIKV drugs is still in its infancy. In the event of a large-scale outbreak, patients' clinical treatment will face the predicament of having no drugs available. In recent years, researchers have conducted a large amount of research on the discovery of anti-CHIKV drugs, establishing a complete research system from the discovery of lead compounds, structure optimization, to activity evaluation. With the continuous development of structural biology, computational chemistry, and artificial intelligence technologies, the efficiency of lead compound discovery and the precision of structural optimization will be further enhanced, accelerating the clinical translation of antiviral candidate compounds against CHIKV.
