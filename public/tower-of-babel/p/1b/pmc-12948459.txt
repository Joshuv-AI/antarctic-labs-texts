Irreversible blindness induced by vitamin A deficiency in a child with avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder secondary to Noonan syndrome

A 4-year-old boy presented to our urgent ophthalmology clinic for photophobia and persistent eye rubbing. He had been born at 39 weeks via Cesarean delivery, without complications. Prenatal exposures included cigarette smoking, pyridoxine–doxylamine, and amitriptyline, and no reported recreational drug or alcohol use. The patient had hypoplastic male genitalia as well as bilateral cryptorchidism requiring orchidopexies at 8 and 12 months of age. His karyotype was 46,XY. Developmental delays were present, including walking at 18 months and forming sentences after age 3 years. Prior genetic and endocrinology evaluations ruled out hypogonadism or known syndromes.

At this visit, the orthoptist measured a visual acuity of 20/400 in both eyes. The patient’s colour vision could not be assessed as he was unable to sit still. When assessed by an ophthalmologist, he had minimal eye contact and was unable to fixate on distant objects. His mother reported that behaviours attributable to poor vision had started 2 years earlier. The patient would glance at objects from the side, hold items close to his face, display poor coordination, and struggle with utensils. A year earlier, the patient had seen an optometrist, who documented a normal ocular examination, with a visual acuity of 20/20 in both eyes. No family history of ocular problems was reported. Given the patient’s severe vision loss, our initial investigations focused on excluding a suspected intracranial mass. A week later, we performed an examination in our pediatric ophthalmology outpatient clinc, with the patient under anesthesia. We found marked conjunctival injection, reduced tear film, and superficial haze in the corneal stroma, with minimal vascularization in the inferior cornea of both eyes (Figure 1). The patient’s intraocular pressure readings were within normal limits. Refraction indicated an insignificant refractive error of +2.5 in both eyes. Fundus examination showed mild temporal optic nerve pallor, with anomalous retinal vessel bifurcation in both eyes (Figure 2). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head showed no acute intracranial abnormalities, though a Chiari I malformation was discovered incidentally. Follow-up spinal MRI showed syringohydromyelia. Following referral to neurosurgery, the patient reported no headaches, pain, stiffness, or weakness, and he continues annual follow-up. Since MRI was inconclusive, our investigations shifted toward alternative causes on the differential.

At 1-week follow-up in the ophthalmology clinic, given the prior corneal abnormalities, conjunctival injection, reduced tear film, and superficial haze with vascularization in the cornea, we considered vitamin A deficiency. Serum testing confirmed severe vitamin A deficiency (< 0.3 [normal range 1.2–2.8] μmol/L). Given the need for urgent treatment, the patient was admitted to the general pediatrics service for a comprehensive assessment by an interdisciplinary team.

Upon asking about the patient’s dietary history, we learned that he had a severely restricted diet, which had started at 1 year of age. Although he initially consumed a variety of purées as a baby, he began refusing most solid foods. Between the ages of 1 and 4 years, his diet consisted of yogurt, crackers, french fries, and apple juice. He avoided most foods owing to temperature and texture sensitivities. His mother’s attempts to introduce other foods and multivitamins were unsuccessful.

Aside from the patient’s ophthalmologic symptoms, he was asymptomatic from his nutritional deficiencies, with normal bowel movements and urination patterns. During admission, his vital signs were stable and appropriate for his age. His weight was 14.8 kg (19th percentile), height was 94 cm (0.07th percentile), and head circumference was 49.5 cm (25th percentile). Previous growth charts show weight tracking between the 3rd and 15th percentiles, with preserved height velocity. Physical examination showed brittle, spoon-shaped nails and follicular hyperkeratosis, consistent with vitamin A deficiency. Examination of cardiac, respiratory, abdominal, and neurologic systems was non-contributory.

During the admission, we re-consulted genetics and endocrinology. Given the patient’s short stature, developmental delays, and bilateral cryptorchidism, full exome sequencing was performed. Laboratory evaluations showed iron-deficiency anemia and vitamin D deficiency; all other micronutrient levels were within normal limits, except for the very low vitamin A level previously measured. Workup for secondary causes of vitamin A deficiency was unremarkable; celiac disease and cystic fibrosis were ruled out. Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) was diagnosed based on the patient’s restricted diet and sensory-based food aversions. His vitamin A deficiency was attributed to poor oral intake and ARFID.

A nasogastric tube was inserted to provide adequate nutrition. The general surgery service later inserted a gastrostomy tube, and the patient was discharged home on gastrostomy feeds. The gastrostomy tube was removed 3 years after insertion.

The patient was started on vitamin A supplementation (3 oral doses of 200 000 units), lubricating eye drops for ocular surface dryness, vitamin D3 supplementation (1500 IU daily), and ferrous sulfate. Despite normalized vitamin A levels and resolved xerophthalmia, his vision did not improve (Figure 3). Examination under anesthesia with electroretinography (ERG) showed normal rod and cone function, ruling out retinal dysfunction. Fundus examination showed persistent bilateral temporal optic nerve pallor.

Colour anterior segment photographs of both eyes showing substantial improvement in xerophthalmia following resolution of the vitamin A deficiency with nasogastric supplementation and a lubricating eyedrop regimen.

Full exome sequencing identified a pathogenic mutation in the LZTR1 gene, associated with Noonan syndrome and schwannomatosis. Noonan syndrome explained the patient’s developmental delays, short stature, bilateral cryptorchidism and ARFID.

The patient’s condition has progressively worsened on follow-up visits. At his most recent visit, he had severe, diffuse bilateral optic nerve atrophy on optical coherence tomography imaging of the retinal nerve fibre layer, confirming permanent vision impairment. We referred him to the Canadian National Institute for the Blind for support and resources.

Discussion

Vitamin A deficiency is a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness, affecting millions of children in areas of food insecurity. 1 In developed countries, vitamin A deficiency is rare and is primarily seen in individuals with restricted diets, malabsorption, or abnormal metabolism.

Vitamin A plays a key role in maintaining epithelial and mucosal integrity, supporting immune function, regulating bone growth, and hematopoiesis.1 It is also essential for retinal photo-transduction, where it is converted to 11-cis-retinal, a component of rhodopsin required for night vision.1 Vitamin A deficiency can cause nyctalopia (night blindness) from impaired rod function, xerophthalmia from epithelial breakdown, and, in rare cases, optic atrophy and fundus albipunctatus, all of which may lead to irreversible vision loss.1 Early treatment with vitamin A supplementation can prevent permanent ocular damage. However, without a high index of suspicion, vitamin A deficiency can be missed, particularly in individuals with restrictive eating patterns from conditions like anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or ARFID.2

We present a unique case of a 4-year-old boy with irreversible vision loss due to prolonged vitamin A deficiency from ARFID. His initial symptoms were vague; he presented with poor visual behaviour despite a normal eye examination. Maintaining clinical suspicion for vitamin A deficiency in children with unexplained vision changes, especially those with restrictive diets, is critical.

Optic nerve pallor and lack of improvement of vision despite resolving xerophthalmia point to optic neuropathy as the underlying cause. Normal ERG findings further support this presentation. A visual evoked potential (VEP) test could have clarified the diagnosis of optic neuropathy by assessing neural pathways from the eye to the brain. However, since a compressive etiology took diagnostic priority over a nutritional etiology, a VEP was not performed.

Vitamin A deficiency may also cause hyperostosis of the skull, leading to optic nerve compression and exacerbating the optic neuropathy; we did not observe that in this patient.3 Previous case reports have described optic neuropathy associated with vitamin A deficiency in older children (ages 9 to 12 yr); our case is notable for involving a substantially younger patient.3,4 Given his young age, disruption during the critical period of visual development likely caused amblyopia and permanent loss of vision.

Ophthalmological examination in children can be challenging because cooperation and communication may be limited. Unexplained vision loss and poor visual behaviour warrant referral to a pediatric ophthalmologist to ensure timely diagnosis and treatment.

Children with vitamin A deficiency may have normal-appearing eyes, with vision loss being the only symptom. In this case, poor visual behaviour predated the pathognomonic vitamin A deficiency–induced xerophthalmia, making recognition difficult. Diagnosing vitamin A deficiency is challenging in developed countries, where nutritional deficiencies are uncommon. Clinical suspicion should be heightened in at-risk groups, such as individuals with eating disorders or restrictive diets. This patient’s ARFID can be attributed to Noonan syndrome.

Noonan syndrome is an autosomal dominant condition affecting 1 in 1000 to 2500 live births, most often caused by mutations in the rat sarcoma–mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway.5 Common features include short stature, congenital heart defects, facial dysmorphism, and neurodevelopmental delay.5 Ocular manifestations include strabismus, nystagmus, amblyopia, cataracts, and optic nerve anomalies.6

Food aversions in Noonan syndrome are multifactorial, with gastroesophageal reflux, delayed gastric emptying, and feeding-skill difficulties as potential contributors.7 Autism spectrum disorder, present in 15% to 30% of those with Noonan syndrome, involves sensory sensitivities and emotional dysregulation, which predispose patients to restrictive diets and nutritional deficiencies.8,9 As recommended by the Noonan Syndrome Guideline Development Group, a feeding and swallowing assessment are essential to address feeding difficulties and prevent nutritional deficiencies.10

This case highlights the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion for vitamin A deficiency in children with unexplained vision loss. Early nutritional screening is crucial, particularly for at-risk populations.
