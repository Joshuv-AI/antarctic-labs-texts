Arthroscopic Anatomic Repair of the Triangular Fibrocartilage Complex Foveal Tears With Suture Anchors With Preservation of the Ligament Remnant
Authors: Yu Jiang, Wanxue Wang, Deixi Cui, Qinge Guo, Pengfei Yan, Jinli Chen, Xia Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe deep layer of the triangular fibrocartilage complex (distal radioulnar ligament) serves as the primary stabilizer of the distal radioulnar joint. Historically, the deep triangular fibrocartilage complex was reattached to the ulnar fovea using bone tunnel or suture anchor techniques to achieve isometric repair. However, with advances in anatomical research, a more refined approach has emerged, using suture anchors for anatomic repair of the deep triangular fibrocartilage complex on the radial wall of the ulnar fovea. Additionally, the introduction of ligament remnant preservation techniques has enhanced ligament‐to‐bone healing.

Triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) injury is a leading cause of ulnar‐sided wrist pain. It was previously believed that proper repair of the foveal avulsion required reattaching the deep fibers into the ulnar fovea. 1 However, a prior study has shown that isometric repair of the deep radioulnar ligament (RUL) at the foveal center is not optimal and may result in abnormal motion. 2 In contrast, inserting the suture anchor along the original trajectory into the anatomical center of the natural footprint on the radial wall is more conducive to achieving an anatomical reduction of the RUL, thereby restoring its natural insertion and orientation. Ligament remnant preservation techniques, commonly employed in anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction 3 and rotator cuff repair, 4 may offer new opportunities for clinical TFCC repair and warrant further investigation.

We describe a technique incorporating ligament remnant preservation, utilizing suture anchors inserted into the radial wall of the fovea for anatomical repair of the deep TFCC insertion.

The wrist is immobilized in a long‐arm cast with the forearm in supination for 4 weeks, followed by gradual range‐of‐motion exercises to restore wrist mobility. In this technique, the mid‐DRUJ portal 5 (Figure 1) is the most commonly used. Positioned midway between the traditional distal and proximal DRUJ portals, it lies directly beneath the radial attachment of the TFCC. The direct fovea portal (DF portal) (Figure 2), first described by Atzei et al., 1 is situated approximately 1 cm proximal to the 6U portaland and immediately anterior to the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve. To assess the stability and integrity of the deep TFCC structures, the hook test and trampoline test are performed (Figures 3 and 4). The 6R portal facilitates the placement of the probe tip into the prestyloid recess to complete the hook test.

Inspection of the Deep TFCC Structure Within the DRUJ Through the Mid‐DRUJ Portal

To improve the success rate of establishing the mid‐DRUJ portal, the following method has been developed: traction is moderately reduced (5‐7 pounds), 5 and under visualization through the 3‐4 portal, an 18‐gauge needle is inserted at the location of the 4‐5 portal, entering the DRUJ (Figure 5). The needle is then relocated 0.5 to1.0 cm proximal to the 4‐5 portal and reinserted into the DRUJ joint. After removing the hemostat, a 1.9 mm arthroscope (Smith & Nephew Medical Products International Trading (Shanghai)) is inserted into the DRUJ with the assistance of a self‐made switching stick (Figure 6).

Ligament Remnant Preservation and TFCC Foveal Insertion Preparation

A shaver is introduced through the DF portal to debride the proliferative synovium, fibrous scar tissue, and torn fibers of the deep TFCC within the DRUJ joint. Ligament remnants are preserved as much as possible, with minimal debridement to facilitate anchor insertion (Figure 7). A radiofrequency probe is then used through the DF portal to thermally shrink and stabilize the torn deep component of TFCC. An arthroscopic probe is subsequently employed to evaluate the injury extent of the deep TFCC insertion at the ulnar fovea (the palmar‐dorsal “footprint” zone). 5 Anchor placement is determined based on the injury extent of deep TFCC insertion, allowing for a personalized repair of the deep TFCC component. In accordance with the previous anatomical study on the deep TFCC component insertion, 2 it is recommended to place the anchor within the deep TFCC footprint zone, the radial wall of the ulnar fovea, rather than at the center of the ulnar fovea. Suture Anchor Insertion

At this step, if the ligament remnants are healthy and well‐preserved, only mild scoring of the residual tissue is required to meet the conditions for anchor placement. When inserting the anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL), a dedicated anchor insertion tool is used (Figure 9).

Specialized anchor insertion instrumentation, comprising a guide sleeve, drill bit, and preloaded bioabsorbable anchor, engineered for precise deployment in confined surgical fields. This figure highlights the advanced tools used to ensure accurate and secure anchor placement.

Suture Pass and Knot Tying

Suturing the Deep Component of the TFCC

An injection needle loaded with a 2‐0 Polydioxanone (Figure 10A) is used as a shuttle suture, as a shuttle suture, introduced through the DF portal, to penetrate the full thickness of the TFCC from its proximal (bottom) to distal (top) aspect. An absorbable suture anchor loaded with a single suture (2 strands) is used to repair each limb of the RULs (deep TFCC). First, the shuttle suture is used to pass 1 strand from the anchor through the dorsal aspect of the TFCC—at the junction between the TFCC and the capsule (Figure 11)—which is then retrieved via the 6R portal. Next, a suture loop is created using a Polydioxanone suture and a needle (Figure 10B), also introduced through the DF portal, to suture the volar aspect of the TFCC at the transition between the TFCC and the ulnocarpal ligaments (Figure 12). Both the loop and the previously passed suture strand are retrieved together through the 6R portal (via the same soft tissue tunnel). The suture strand is then threaded through the loop and pulled out through the DF portal, effectively returning to the DF portal along a U‐shaped path, thereby completing the first stitch. This stitch simultaneously repairs the volar and dorsal aspects of the TFCC.

The second stitch is completed in the same manner, suturing the middle aspect of TFCC from radial to ulnar (Figure 13), approximately 3 mm from its ulnar edge. Based on the modified Mason‐Allen suture technique, the 2 suture strands are interwoven in a cruciate pattern to reattach both the dorsal and palmar limbs of RULs (deep TFCC) to the ulnar fovea. Moreover, the second suture strand divides the repaired deep TFCC into volar and dorsal sections through this cruciate pattern to accomplish the ligament‐specific repair as proposed by Liu and Arianni 6 (Table 2).

The cruciate pattern secures the RUL bundles to the ulnar fovea, achieving ligament‐specific repair. (DRUJ, distal radioulnar joint; RUL, radioulnar ligament.)

Knot Tying and Fixation

Traction is then released, and the forearm is rotated into supination to optimize wrist positioning for DRUJ reduction. An assistant is instructed to reduce the DRUJ and apply pressure between the ulna and radius to maintain the reduction. A dedicated knot pusher is used to create a sliding knot, which is then pushed to the ulnar fovea. Additional standard knots are tied to further secure the sliding knot. During knot tying, a retractor is employed to protect the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve (Figure 14).

A retractor protects the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve from the portal site during this process. This figure emphasizes the importance of nerve protection during knot tying to minimize postoperative complications. (DF, direct fovea.)

Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

Active finger movements can commence immediately postsurgery. The forearm and wrist are secured in a long‐arm cast with the forearm in supination for 4 weeks. After 4 weeks, the cast is removed, and patients begin range‐of‐motion exercises for wrist flexion, extension, and rotation, which are progressively advanced until full wrist mobility is regained.

DISCUSSION

In contrast to previous arthroscopic anchor repair techniques for the deep TFCC, this approach does not place the anchor at the isometric point in the center of the ulnar fovea but rather on the radial wall of the fovea to achieve an anatomic repair and restore direct insertion of the deep TFCC. Additionally, in this technique, the remnant of the RUL is preserved, refraining from complete debridement of the bone bed to expose cancellous bone, which promotes tendon‐bone healing and enhances the stability of the reconstructed RUL.

Liu and Arianni 6 concluded from their cadaveric study that anatomic ligament‐specific repair restores the original foveal footprint, covering a larger area than the isometric zone and yielding a stronger, more robust repair compared with isometric techniques. Previous studies have confirmed that anatomical reconstruction of the ACL, which restores its native insertion, orientation, and geometry, can better restore normal knee kinematics. 7 , 8 , 9 Numerous basic studies have affirmed that preserving tendon or ligament remnants significantly enhances the tendon‐bone healing environment. 9 , 10 , 11

This surgical technique offers significant advantages in repairing deep TFCC injuries, yet its potential risks and limitations must be approached with caution. The preservation of ligament remnants and the achievement of anatomic reduction are 2 major strengths of this technique. By preserving the ligament remnants, we provide a superior biological environment for tendon‐bone healing, enhance postoperative stability, and reduce the risk of complications. Anatomic repair restores the natural insertion and orientation of the TFCC, thereby better restoring the normal kinematics and stability of the wrist joint. However, as with any surgical technique, certain risks are inevitable. In this technique, despite protective measures, there remains a risk of injury to the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve, which may lead to decreased sensation or numbness on the ulnar side of the hand. Moreover, improper anchor placement or premature postoperative strenuous activity by the patient may result in anchor loosening, thereby affecting the repair outcome.
