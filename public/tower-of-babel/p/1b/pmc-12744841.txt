Dissecting Oct4 enhancer function in pluripotent stem cells and mouse embryogenesis
Authors: Daniel A. Schmitz, Daiji Okamura, Masahiro Sakurai, Yi Ding, Seiya Oura, Emily Ballard, Yulei Wei, Leqian Yu, Yingying Hu, Jun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOCT4 is a master regulator of pluripotency, with expression restricted to pluripotent and germ cells. Its expression is controlled by two cis-regulatory elements: the distal (DE) and proximal (PE) enhancers. Although widely used as markers for pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), their biological roles have remained incompletely defined. Here, we generated PSC lines and mouse models with targeted deletions of the Oct4 DE and PE. Our findings reveal that the DE is dispensable for sustaining the primed pluripotent state but required for the naive state, whereas the PE is necessary for the primed state but not for the naive state. Notably, PE-deficient naive mouse PSCs retained the capacity to differentiate into somatic lineages in vitro and to contribute chimeras. In contrast, deletion of either enhancer in vivo resulted in early embryonic lethality. These models offer powerful genetic tools to dissect the regulation of Oct4 expression during pluripotency and early development.

Introduction

Pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) possess the unique ability to self-renew in vitro across distinct pluripotent states and differentiate into all adult cell types (Du and Wu, 2024). These properties make PSCs powerful tools for modeling development and disease, as well as for advancing regenerative medicine (Liu et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2022a; Wu and Fu, 2024). Despite decades of research, the molecular mechanisms regulating pluripotency remain incompletely understood. Elucidating these mechanisms is essential to fully realize the potential of PSCs in both fundamental and translational research.

Mouse PSCs can be stabilized in vitro to represent different stages of embryonic epiblast. Cells derived from the inner cell mass (ICM) of blastocysts give rise to naive mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) (Evans and Kaufman, 1981; Martin, 1981), while cells isolated from post-implantation epiblast generate primed mouse epiblast stem cells (mEpiSCs) (Brons et al., 2007; Tesar et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2015). Although both are pluripotent, they differ significantly in colony morphology, molecular signatures, and developmental potential (Du and Wu, 2024). Naive mESCs form dome-shaped colonies and robustly contribute to blastocyst chimeras, whereas mEpiSCs do not (Bradley et al., 1984; Huang et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2015). These states also rely on different signaling pathways: naive mESCs depend on LIF/STAT3, BMP4, and Wnt/β-catenin, while primed mEpiSCs require activation of fibroblast growth factor (FGF)/ERK and Activin/Nodal signaling and inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin (Chia et al., 2010; Najm et al., 2011). Despite these differences, both states are maintained by core transcription factors, most notably OCT4 (POU5F1), which is mostly expressed in pluripotent and germ cells during development (Pesce et al., 1998; Wu and Schöler, 2014; Zalc et al., 2021). OCT4 is essential for pluripotency: Oct4-null mouse embryos fail to form pluripotent ICM, and mouse PSCs lacking OCT4 quickly lose pluripotency (Hay et al., 2004; Nichols et al., 1998). Conversely, OCT4 overexpression can reprogram somatic cells into PSCs (Kim et al., 2009). In human PSCs, OCT4 also plays a central role; its loss triggers rapid spontaneous differentiation (Matin et al., 2004), and its forced expression, along with other transcriptional factors such as SOX2 and KLF4, is sufficient to reprogram somatic cells to PSCs (Takahashi et al., 2007). Thus, OCT4 is widely regarded as a master regulator of pluripotency.

In mouse pluripotent and germ cells, Oct4 expression is regulated by three cis-regulatory elements (Yeom et al., 1996; Choi et al., 2016): the proximal promoter (PP), proximal enhancer (PE), and distal enhancer (DE) (Figure 1A). The activity and utilization of the PE and DE are dynamically regulated during embryogenesis and germ cell development (Yeom et al., 1996; Choi et al., 2016; Pastor et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018). Either enhancer, together with the PP, is sufficient to drive Oct4 expression, but their usage varies by developmental stage (Choi et al., 2016; Yeom et al., 1996). The DE is primarily active in naive PSCs, the pre-implantation epiblast, and founder primordial germ cells (PGCs), while the PE is dominant in the post-implantation epiblast, primed PSCs, and migratory PGCs. During the naive-to-primed transition and in post-migratory PGCs, both enhancers are active (Chen et al., 2018; Choi et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2022b; Nazarov et al., 2014; Pastor et al., 2018; Yeom et al., 1996). The human OCT4 DE and PE show partial functional conservation with their murine counterparts (Buecker and Wysocka, 2012). As in mice, reporter assays indicate that the human DE is preferentially active in naive hPSCs, whereas the PE is active in primed hPSCs (Gafni et al., 2013; Theunissen et al., 2014). Although the dynamic regulation and epigenetic landscape of the Oct4 enhancers have been characterized in both species (Barnea and Bergman, 2000; Ben-Shushan et al., 1995; Buecker et al., 2014; Cai et al., 2015; Gu et al., 2005a, 2005b; Marin et al., 1997; Moon et al., 2023; Mulas et al., 2024; Park et al., 2007; Pikarsky et al., 1994; Schoorlemmer et al., 1994; Wu and Schöler, 2014), their functional necessity in maintaining specific pluripotent states, enabling transitions between them, or supporting differentiation remains largely untested.Figure 1Generation of ΔDE and ΔPE pluripotent stem cell lines(A) Schematic of Oct4 locus and developmental stages/cell types using each enhancer.(B) Schematic of Oct4 locus with regions targeted by sgRNAs. In this study, we took a loss-of-function approach to dissect the roles of the Oct4 PE and DE in pluripotent state maintenance, state transition, differentiation, and embryogenesis. Using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing, we generated enhancer-deletion PSC lines and animal models in human and/or mouse systems. Our results show that the PE is dispensable for maintaining the naive state and for differentiation but is required to stabilize the primed state. In contrast, the DE is essential for establishing the naive state but is not required for the primed state or for differentiation. Notably, deletion of either enhancer in vivo leads to embryonic lethality, highlighting a divergence between the requirements of cultured PSCs and the embryonic epiblast. Together, these findings demonstrate that the Oct4 enhancers function in a context-dependent manner, with distinct and non-redundant roles during specific pluripotency states and transitions.

Results

Generation of Oct4 ΔDE and ΔPE PSCs

The genomic locations of the mouse Oct4 DE and PE are well characterized, and their functions have been validated through in vitro and in vivo reporter assays (Yeom et al., 1996; Choi et al., 2016; Gafni et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2022b; Nordhoff et al., 2001) (File S1). Based on these studies, we designed two pairs of single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) to selectively delete each enhancer via CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing (Figure 1B). For the DE, one sgRNA pair targeted a 3.32-kilobase (kb) region (based on GOF-18ΔDE, from Yeom et al., 1996), generating a ∼3.30-kb deletion that removes most of conserved region 3 (CR3), all of CR4, and ∼98.4% of the DE, while leaving the PE intact (Figure S1A). To delete the PE, we designed sgRNAs targeting a 1.0-kb region (based on GOF-18ΔPE, from Yeom et al., 1996), resulting in a ∼953-bp deletion that eliminates CR2 and ∼95.1% of the PE sequence, while preserving the PP (based on GOF-18ΔPP, from Yeom et al., 1996) and the DE (Figure S1B). To generate ΔDE human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) using the RUES2-GLR line (Martyn et al., 2018), we targeted a 1.5-kb region of the human DE (based on the ΔPE-OCT4-GFP-2A-PURO expression reporter, from Gafni et al., 2013), resulting in a ∼1.48-kb deletion that removes CR2 and ∼99% of the human DE sequence (Figure S1C).

To improve the efficiency of generating large enhancer deletions, we used a dual-fluorescence strategy to enrich for cells co-expressing both sgRNAs required for deletion (Figure S1D). Each sgRNA, targeting either the 5′ or 3′ end of the enhancer, was co-expressed with Cas9 and either EGFP or mCherry. Transfection of both constructs followed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting of EGFP+mCherry+ population enabled the isolation of cells expressing both sgRNAs simultaneously. From these, we established three ΔPE mESC lines cultured in ground state conditions (Ying et al., 2008; 2iL medium containing CHIR99021, PD0325901, and LIF) (Figures 1C, 1D, and S1G), three ΔDE mEpiSC lines maintained in the primed state (Kinoshita et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2015) (AFX medium containing Activin A, FGF-2, and XAV939) (Figures 1E, 1F, and S1G), and three ΔDE RUES2-GLR hESC lines cultured in primed condition (Chen et al., 2011) (Essential 8 [E8] medium supplemented with FGF-2 and transforming growth factor β1) (Figures S1E–S1G).

During embryogenesis, the naive pre-implantation epiblast transitions through formative and primed pluripotency before undergoing gastrulation, where it differentiates into the three primary germ layers: ectoderm, endoderm, and mesoderm (Morgani et al., 2017; Bardot and Hadjantonakis, 2020; Yu et al., 2021a). To assess whether the Oct4 PE is required for this process, we induced random differentiation of ΔPE mESCs via embryoid body (EB) formation. After 14 days, immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that ΔPE EBs, like wild-type (WT) control, expressed lineage-specific markers for all three germ layers: TUBB3 (ectoderm), FOXA2 (endoderm), and cTNT (mesoderm) (Figure 2D). We further evaluated differentiation in vivo by teratoma formation (Figure 2E). ΔPE mESCs generated teratomas containing derivatives from all three germ layers (Figures 2F–2I), although the teratomas were consistently smaller than those formed by WT mESCs (Figure 2G). IF and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses confirmed differentiation into all three germ layers (Figures 2H and 2I). One potential explanation for the reduced teratoma size is increased apoptosis during differentiation, as evidenced by a significant rise in cleaved caspase-3-positive cells within ΔPE EBs (Figure 2J). Together, these results demonstrate that the Oct4 PE is dispensable for somatic differentiation from naive mESCs, despite an increase in apoptosis during this process.

A stringent assay for evaluating PSC developmental potential is their ability to contribute to blastocyst chimeras (Du and Wu, 2024). Naive mESCs can contribute to both somatic tissues and the germ line when injected into pre-implantation embryos. Given that ΔPE mESCs retained differentiation potential (Figures 2D, 2H, and 2I), we tested whether they could also contribute to chimeras. EGFP-labeled control and ΔPE mESCs (C57BL/6, black coat) were injected into ICR (CD-1, white coat) blastocysts (Figure 3A). At embryonic day 6.5 (E6.5), both control and ΔPE donor cells were detected in chimeric embryos (Figure 3B). However, IF analysis revealed reduced OCT4 expression in EGFP+ ΔPE donor cells when compared to surrounding EGFP-negative host cells and WT donor cells. Notably, ΔPE donor cells localized to the Brachyury (T)-positive primitive streak, demonstrating their exit of pluripotency (Morris et al., 2012) (Figure 3C). By E7.5, most EGFP+ ΔPE cells were lost, with only sparse donor cells remaining (Figure 3D). Of seven live-born pups from ΔPE-injected embryos, only one showed detectable donor cell contribution (Figures 3E and S2A), indicating a substantial drop in both chimera frequency and chimerism level compared to control (Figures S2A and S2B). Genomic PCR confirmed the presence of the ΔPE allele in this pup, verifying ΔPE mESC contribution (Figures S2C and 3F). Oct4 PE is essential for maintaining the primed pluripotency state

During embryogenesis, the naive pre-implantation epiblast transitions through a formative phase before acquiring the primed identity (Du and Wu, 2024). This progression can be recapitulated in vitro using stepwise culture condition adaptation (Hayashi et al., 2011; Yu et al., 2021a) (Figure 4A). To determine whether the Oct4 PE is required for this transition and for maintaining the primed pluripotent state, we subjected ΔPE mESCs to a two-step conversion protocol: 72 h in FA medium (FGF-2 + Activin A, formative-like state), followed by 5 days in AFX medium (Activin A, FGF-2, XAV939, primed state) (Kinoshita et al., 2021; Okamura et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2015) (Figure S3A). IF analysis at day 3 revealed comparable OCT4 expression between ΔPE and WT cells in FA medium (Figure 4B), and total cell numbers remained similar (Figures 4C and S3B). However, after 5 days in AFX medium, only WT cells formed characteristic primed colonies that are alkaline phosphatase (AP) positive, whereas ΔPE cells failed to do so (Figures 4D and 4E). In rare cases, ΔPE cells formed colonies under AFX condition, but these displayed colony morphologies inconsistent with mEpiSCs (Figure 4F). Further analysis revealed that these ΔPE colonies lacked OCT4 expression and instead expressed trophoblast-associated markers including Eomesodermin (Eomes), CDX2, and GATA3 (Figures 4F and S3C), suggesting trans-differentiation into trophoblast-like cells. This is consistent with previous reports showing that loss of OCT4 in PSCs promotes trophoblast differentiation (Hay et al., 2004; Niwa et al., 2000; Zafarana et al., 2009). Together, these findings demonstrate that, while ΔPE cells can transition through a formative-like state, the Oct4 PE is essential for maintaining the primed pluripotency state. We performed IF analysis of pluripotency-related transcription factors and found that both control and ΔDE hESCs retained robust expression of OCT4 and NANOG (Figure 5A). To evaluate whether the OCT4 DE is involved in in vitro differentiation, we performed an EB assay using control and ΔDE hESCs (Figure 5B). After 14 days of culture, both control and ΔDE EBs gave rise to cells expressing markers of all three germ layers: TUBB3 (ectoderm), α-SMA (mesoderm), and FOXA2 (endoderm) (Figure 5C), indicating that the DE is not required for multilineage differentiation in vitro. We next tested whether the OCT4 DE is necessary for acquiring the human naive pluripotent state. ΔDE hESCs were cultured in 5iLA medium (Figure 5B) (Theunissen et al., 2014), a defined condition for inducing naive pluripotency in conventional hESCs, containing PD0325901, IM12, SB590885, Y-27632, WH-4-023, LIF, and Activin A. In contrast to WT control, ΔDE hESCs rapidly lost OCT4 and SOX2 expression, as well as undifferentiated colony morphology after 5 days in 5iLA (Figure 5D). These results demonstrate that the OCT4 DE is dispensable for maintaining primed pluripotency and supporting differentiation in vitro but is essential for conversion to the human naive pluripotency state.

Generation of mouse lines harboring Oct4 ΔDE and ΔPE alleles

To investigate the roles of the Oct4 DE and PE in embryonic development, we generated mouse lines carrying the same enhancer deletions previously established in mESCs and mEpiSCs (Figure 1). Founder mice were produced using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing (Figure S4A), resulting in heterozygous animals with a single deleted allele of either the PE or DE. Sanger sequencing confirmed successful enhancer deletions (Figure S4B): the ΔPE allele carried a 959-bp deletion, eliminating ∼95.6% of the PE (Figure S4C), and the ΔDE allele contained a 3.27-kb deletion, eliminating ∼98.6% of the DE (Figure S4D). These models provide a valuable platform for dissecting the in vivo functions of the Oct4 enhancers and their roles in regulating Oct4 expression during development.

Embryonic malformations in homozygous ΔDE and ΔPE mouse embryos

Although ΔPE mESCs appeared normal and retained the ability to differentiate in vitro and contribute to chimeras, we sought to determine whether the PE is essential for embryonic development. To this end, we crossed heterozygous ΔPE+/− mice and analyzed E7.5 embryos. While WT and ΔPE+/− embryos showed normal morphology, homozygous ΔPE−/− embryos exhibited developmental defects, including disrupted anterior-posterior (A-P) axis formation reminiscent of Otx2-null phenotypes (Kimura et al., 2000) (Figure 6A). Cerberus (Cer-1), a marker of anterior visceral endoderm (AVE) (Perea-Gomez et al., 2002), remained expressed but was abnormally expanded to posterior regions (Figure S4E), indicating a failure in A-P axis patterning. At E6.5, OCT4 expression was diminished in ΔPE−/− embryos compared to ΔPE+/− embryos and was nearly undetectable by E7.5 (Figure 6B). By E8.5, both embryonic and extraembryonic malformations were apparent in ΔPE−/− embryos, although an elongated allantois extending toward the chorion, a key feature of embryonic-placental interaction, was still observed (Figure 6C). To assess the role of the Oct4 DE in vivo, we intercrossed heterozygous ΔDE+/− mice and analyzed the resulting embryos. At E3.5, homozygous ΔDE−/− embryos successfully formed blastocysts with identifiable ICM, comparable to WT and heterozygous ΔDE+/− counterparts (Figures 6D and S4F). Notably, OCT4 and SOX2 expression persisted in ΔDE−/− blastocysts despite the deletion of the DE (Figure 6E). However, by E7.5, ΔDE−/− embryos exhibited significant growth retardation and morphological defects, accompanied by a marked reduction in OCT4 expression compared to heterozygous littermates (Figures 6F–6H).

These findings show that both Oct4 enhancers are indispensable for normal embryogenesis: while OCT4 persists through pre-implantation, its expression is lost by E7.5 when either the PE or DE is deleted biallelically.

Embryonic lethality in mouse embryos homozygous for Oct4 ΔDE or ΔPE alleles

To determine whether homozygous ΔPE−/− or ΔDE−/− embryos can give rise to viable offspring, we genotyped pups from heterozygous intercrosses. Among 70 pups (no evidence of sex bias) from 15 litters, no ΔPE−/− animals were recovered (Figure 7A, S5A, and S5B). Similarly, no ΔDE−/− pups were detected among 20 offspring from ΔDE +/− intercrosses (Figures 7B and S5C). We also tested whether compound heterozygosity, possessing one functional copy of each enhancer, is sufficient for development. Crosses between ΔPE+/− and ΔDE+/− mice produced offspring in expected Mendelian ratios, including compound heterozygous (ΔPE+/−, ΔDE+/−), regardless of parental sex (Figures 7B–7D, S5D, and S5E). To further evaluate whether compound heterozygous mice are fertile and capable of producing functional germ cells, we performed reciprocal crosses with WT animals. Mating ΔPE+/−, ΔDE+/− males to WT females yielded offspring with both ΔPE+/− and ΔDE+/− genotypes (Figures 7D and S5F). Similarly, compound heterozygous females crossed with WT males produced 28 pups with equal distribution of ΔPE+/− and ΔDE+/− genotypes (Figure S5G). These results confirm that compound heterozygotes are fully fertile and capable of generating functional germ cells. Furthermore, neither enhancer is required for completing meiosis to generate haploid gametes during gametogenesis (Figure 7E).

In summary, these in vivo analyses reveal that, while complete loss of either Oct4 enhancer results in embryonic lethality, a single functional copy of each enhancer is sufficient to support both embryonic and germline development (Figure 7F).

Discussion

In summary, our enhancer-deletion strategy reveals distinct, context-dependent roles for the Oct4 DE and PE in pluripotency and embryonic development. We found that deletion of both PE alleles in mESCs does not disrupt maintenance of the naive pluripotent state and that deletion of the DE in hESCs and mEpiSCs does not compromise the primed state. Both ΔPE mESCs and ΔDE hESCs retained the ability to differentiate into all three germ layers, as evidenced by EB and teratoma assays. Notably, ΔPE mESCs also contributed to adult somatic tissues in chimeric mice. According to conventional criteria, these enhancer-deficient PSCs meet the definition of pluripotency. However, their ability to maintain pluripotency breaks down during state transitions: ΔPE mESCs could not stably adopt the primed state, while ΔDE hESCs failed to convert to the naive state. These results underscore a state-specific requirement for Oct4 enhancer activity and reveal a critical role for enhancer context in sustaining pluripotency across developmental states (Figure 7G).

Using ΔPE and ΔDE mouse models, we further demonstrate that both Oct4 enhancers are essential for embryonic development. Homozygous deletion of either enhancer led to a loss of OCT4 expression and embryonic lethality around E8.5 (Figure 7H). Notably, in ΔPE homozygous embryos, OCT4 levels began to decline by E6.5, with clear morphological defects emerging by E7.5 (Figures 6A and 6B). This temporal expression pattern closely mirrored our observation in blastocyst chimeras generated from ΔPE mESCs. Donor ΔPE mESC-derived cells robustly contributed to the epiblast at E6.5 but showed reduced OCT4 expression and were largely lost by E7.5 (Figures 3B–3D). The striking similarity between these two contexts highlights the essential role of the PE in sustaining Oct4 expression during early post-implantation development. Without it, OCT4 expression progressively diminishes, leading to developmental failure (Figure 6C) and loss of donor-derived cells (Figure 3D).

ΔPE homozygous embryos exhibited pronounced axial disorganization by E7.5 (Figures 6A and S4E), indicating a failure in proper anterior-posterior patterning. A similar phenotype has been reported in embryos with epiblast-specific conditional knockout of Oct4, where loss of OCT4 expression by E7.5 leads to defective axial formation and eventual embryonic lethality, likely due to compounded defects in epiblast specification and patterning (Mulas et al., 2018). This parallel further underscores the critical role of OCT4 in the post-implantation epiblast and highlights the importance of the PE in sustaining OCT4 expression during this developmental window.

An unexpected observation emerged from our analysis of ΔDE embryos. At E3.5, ΔDE homozygous embryos exhibited normal morphology (Figures 6D and S4F) and maintained OCT4 and SOX2 expression within the ICM (Figure 6E), indicating that Oct4 expression is preserved during pre-implantation development despite complete deletion of the DE. This finding contrasts with earlier predictions (Choi et al., 2016; Yeom et al., 1996) and suggests that the DE is not essential at this early stage. Phenotypic abnormalities became apparent only by E7.5, when ΔDE embryos began to show developmental delays and mild morphological defects (Figure 6F), suggesting that the requirement for the DE becomes critical post-implantation. This temporal discrepancy between in vivo and in vitro findings (Figures 1E and 1F) suggests the existence of compensatory mechanism(s) that maintain Oct4 expression during early development. One possibility is that, in the absence of the DE, the PE may transiently compensate to sustain Oct4 expression in pre-implantation embryos. However, this hypothesis remains speculative, as no direct evidence currently supports functional redundancy between the two enhancers. Another explanation is that maternally inherited OCT4 persists through the pre-implantation stages and compensates for the loss of DE-driven OCT4 expression (Yeom et al., 1996; Palmieri et al., 1994; Wu and Schöler, 2014). Clarifying this possibility would require experiments involving maternal Oct4 knockout embryos.

While our in vivo studies focused on mouse embryos, it is important to recognize species-specific differences in OCT4 function. In particular, OCT4 may be required earlier in human development than in mouse (Wu et al., 2013; Frum et al., 2013; Fogarty et al., 2017). In contrast to mice, OCT4 expression is observed in TE cells in human blastocysts and blastoids (Yu et al., 2021b, 2023). In addition, the human OCT4 locus contains a fourth cis-regulatory element, referred to as the naive enhancer, which is absent in mouse and has been implicated in human germline formation (Chen et al., 2018). Future investigations using human embryo models derived from ΔDE and ΔPE human PSCs may help elucidate these evolutionary distinctions and clarify the enhancer-specific regulation of OCT4 in human development (Yu et al., 2021b; 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Oura et al., 2025).

Another puzzling observation is that, while ΔPE mESCs fail to transition to the primed pluripotent state in vitro (Figure 4), they are nonetheless able to contribute to the epiblast in E6.5 chimeras (Figure 3). One might expect that cells incapable of acquiring primed identity would either fail to integrate or undergo apoptosis before or during gastrulation. Surprisingly, ΔPE mESCs contribute to the E6.5 epiblast at levels comparable to WT mESCs. A possible explanation is that, although OCT4 expression is reduced in ΔPE cells, the downregulation may occur gradually, allowing transient survival and partial progression toward differentiation, including mesodermal commitment, as indicated by Brachyury expression (Figure 3C). However, by E7.5, the cumulative loss of OCT4 likely compromises epiblast identity and cell viability, resulting in apoptosis and the disappearance of ΔPE mESC-derived cells (Figures 3D and S5G).

Of particular interest is the potential contribution of these cells to the germ line, as OCT4 expression become restricted to the germ cell lineage during and after gastrulation (Yeom et al., 1996). Future studies should investigate whether ΔPE and ΔDE PSCs retain the ability to differentiate into primordial germ cell-like cells (Chen et al., 2018; Hayashi et al., 2011; Pastor et al., 2018). Such work would offer critical insights into the functional roles of the Oct4 PE and DE in germline specification, particularly given the established requirement for Oct4 in the development of PGC precursors (Okamura et al., 2008).

The ΔPE and ΔDE mouse models also offer valuable tools for biotechnological applications. Conventional enhancer-based reporter constructs (e.g., Oct4-DE-GFP and Oct4-PE-GFP) do not faithfully recapitulate endogenous OCT4 expression, for example, in transgenic ovaries (Choi et al., 2016). In contrast, allelic tagging of the endogenous Oct4 locus in ΔPE and ΔDE backgrounds with distinct fluorescent reporters (e.g., GFP and RFP) would enable precise, dual-color monitoring of enhancer activity in vivo. Additionally, stem cell lines such as mESCs and mEpiSCs derived from these models could serve as powerful platforms for dissecting enhancer function in vitro. This dual-reporter system would facilitate high-resolution, spatiotemporal analysis of OCT4 regulation, yielding insights into enhancer dynamics across pluripotency maintenance, germline specification, and lineage commitment.

Together, our results provide strong evidence that the Oct4 PE and DE serve distinct and indispensable roles in both embryonic development and PSC biology. The mouse and stem cell models generated in this study offer a versatile platform for further investigation into the context-specific regulation of OCT4 throughout development.

Methods

Generation of ΔDE and ΔPE PSC lines

To generate ΔPE mESCs, mESCs derived from C57BL/6 embryos were electroporated using a NEPA21 type II super electroporator (Bulldog-Bio) using the manufacturer’s recommended parameters. 2 × 106 mESCs were mixed with 5 μg of each DNA vector (U6-5’sgRNA, Cas9-EGFP; U6-3’sgRNA, Cas9-mCherry) in 100 μL of Opti-MEM (Gibco). ΔDE mEpiSCs (C57BL/6) were generated the same way, except using AFX medium. ΔDE RUES2-GLR hESCs were also generated the same way, except using E8 medium. The media compositions are described in "Cell Culture".

Annotation of the PE, DE, and PP

The sequence for the mouse Oct4 locus was obtained from NC_000083.7, Reference GRCm39 C57BL/6J (NCBI). Additional upstream and downstream genomic sequences were identified using Ensembl (Ensembl.org). The locus pertaining to GOF-18 was identified by locating the HindIII and EcoRI restriction sites from Yeom, Y. et al. (1996), and corresponded to a ∼17.2 kb genomic sequence. Using GRCm39 as a reference, the second HindIII restriction site upstream of the Oct4 start codon was identified as the beginning of GOF-18, and the second EcoRI restriction site downstream of the Oct4 start codon was identified as the end of GOF-18. The DE, PE, and PP were annotated as described in Yeom, Y. et al. (1996). The DE (3,320 bp) begins with the second BamHI restriction site upstream of the start codon and ends with the first BamHI restriction site upstream of the start codon. The PE (1,002 bp) begins with the first BamHI restriction site upstream of the start codon and ends with the first BstEII restriction site upstream of the start codon. The PP begins with the first BstEII site upstream of the start codon and ends with the first AvrII site upstream. An annotated sequence of mouse GOF-18 is included in the supplemental information.

Naive-to-primed conversion

Naive mESCs were stepwise converted to primed mEpiSCs by inducing mEpiLCs (Hayashi et al., 2011). 1 × 105 mESCs were plated in each fibronectin-coated 12-well plate (16.7 μg/mL) in N2B27 medium plus Activin A (20 ng/mL), FGF-2 (12 ng/mL), and 1% KSR (FA medium), as described (Hayashi et al., 2011). Medium was refreshed daily. On day three, mEpiLCs were collected and counted. 1,000 cells were plated into a Matrigel-coated 6-well plate in AFX medium and cultured for 5 days, refreshing medium daily (Okamura et al., 2021).

Primed-to-naive conversion

To induce naive pluripotency, primed RUES2-GLR human ESCs were transitioned to 5iLA medium as described (Theunissen et al., 2014). Cells were dissociated with TrypLE, plated onto irradiated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) in 12-well plates at 4 × 104 or 8 × 104 cells/well in 5iLA medium (N2B27, PD0325901, SB590885, IM-12, WH-4-023, Y-27632, human LIF, and Activin A). Medium was replaced daily. Naive conversion was assessed at 5 days by colony morphology and markers OCT4, CDX2, and GATA3 via IF.

EB assay

Mouse EBs were generated using AggreWell 400 microwell plates (STEMCELL Technologies) as instructed. Mouse EBs were formed in 2iL medium for 48 h and then transferred to ultra-low attachment 24-well plates (Corning) in EB medium (DMEM/F12, 10% FBS, GlutaMAX, NEAA, and 2-mercaptoethanol). After 48 h, EBs were moved to Matrigel-coated dishes and cultured 2 weeks before used for analysis. EB medium was changed every 3 days. For ΔPE mESC viability and hESC differentiation, EBs were formed in EB medium with 1 μM Y-27632 in 6-well suspension culture plates pretreated with anti-adherence solution. Mouse EBs were cultured for 4 days and then fixed for cleaved caspase-3 IF. Human EBs were cultured for 5 days and then transferred to Matrigel-coated dishes and differentiated using EB medium.

Blastocyst chimera generation

CD-1 females (8 weeks or older) were mated with CD-1 males, and blastocysts were harvested at E3.5 by flushing uterine horns. Single-cell naive mESC suspensions were placed in a droplet of KSOM-Hepes with recipient blastocysts. Cells were then collected into a micropipette (15 μm diameter), and a piezo micro manipulator (Prime Tech) created an opening in the zona pellucida and trophectoderm. Ten mESCs were injected into each blastocoel, and blastocysts were cultured in mKSOMaa. Embryos were then transferred to 8- to 12-week CD-1 surrogates mated with vasectomized males to induce pseudopregnancy. Ketamine (30 mg/mL)/xylazine (4 mg/mL) and Buprenorphine SR-LAB (1 mg/mL) were used for surgery. Embryos were transferred to the uterine at E2.5, using fifteen to thirty blastocysts transferred within 20–30 min per surrogate. At birth, chimeras were identified by coat color.

IF staining

Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 min at room temperature (RT), permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h at RT, and then washed 3× with PBS and incubated in blocking buffer (6% donkey serum and 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer, applied to the samples, and incubated overnight at 4°C. Then, samples were washed 3× in 0.1% Tween 20 (PBS-T) and incubated with fluorescent antibodies in blocking buffer. Finally, samples were washed 3× with PBS-T, counterstained with 300 nM DAPI for 20 min at RT, and washed again before analysis. Samples were imaged with a fluorescence microscope (Echo Laboratories, CA). See Table S1 for antibody information.

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN) as instructed. cDNA was synthesized using iScript reverse transcription supermix (Bio-Rad) and amplified with HotStart 2× SYBR Green qPCR master mix (APExBIO) on a Touch Thermal Cycler Real-Time PCR machine (C1000, Bio-Rad). GAPDH and β-actin were used as controls. See Table S1 for primer sequences.

Genomic DNA extraction and genotyping

For cell line genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted with QuickExtract DNA extraction solution (LGC Biosearch Technologies). 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL extraction solution, incubated at 68°C for 15 min and 95°C for 10 min, and then centrifuged 15 min at 17,000g. Supernatant was diluted 1:30, and 1 μL was typically used for PCR using CloneAmp HiFi PCR premix (Takara). For mouse tail and embryo genotyping, a DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (QIAGEN) was used following manufacturer’s instructions. See Table S1 for primers.

Cell culture

RUES2-GLR hESCs were a gift from Ali H. Brivanlou (Rockefeller University). hESCs were cultured on Matrigel (BD Biosciences)-coated plates in Essential 8 (E8) medium (made in-house, as previously described) (Chen et al., 2011). hESCs were passaged at 80% confluence at a 1:6 split using Versene or TrypLE and cultured in E8 + 10 μM Y-27632 for first day after passage or freezing (E8 + 10 μM Y-27632 + 10% DMSO). C57BL/6 mESCs and mEpiSCs were generated as described (Wu et al., 2015, 2017). C57BL/6 mESCs were cultured feeder free on Matrigel in 2iL medium (DMEM/F12 + Neurobasal 1:1, 0.5× N2, 0.5× B27, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1× NEAA, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 10 ng/mL rhLIF, 3 μM CHIR 99021, and 1 μM PD0325901). mEpiSCs were cultured feeder free in AFX (DMEM/F12 + Neurobasal 1:1, 0.5× N2, 0.5× B27, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1× NEAA, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 20 ng/mL Activin A, 12.5 ng/mL FGF-2, and 2 μM XAV939). Mouse PSCs were dissociated using TrypLE. All cell lines tested negative for routine PCR-based mycoplasma testing.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD. Experiments were repeated at least three times, unless otherwise noted (repeat number was indicated as “n” in figure legends). Differences between groups were evaluated by Student’s t test (two-sided). p values are shown in the figure captions. Graphic analyses were done using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) and Microsoft PowerPoint and Excel (Microsoft 365).
