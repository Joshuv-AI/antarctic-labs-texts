Enabling Continuous Intracranial Pressure Monitoring During MRI: A Technical Note on the Development of an MR‐Safe Support Frame
Authors: Marcelo P Martins, Malin Ringholm, Jacob Engström, Anders Tisell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring during magnetic resonance (MR) imaging is often discontinued due to safety concerns, as commonly used devices are not MR‐conditional for in situ operation. Parenchymal probes must be disconnected, and external ventricular drainage (EVD) systems are typically closed or cannot be connected to MR‐conditional transducers, thereby limiting diagnostic opportunities in unstable neurocritical care patients. MR examinations in neurocritical care are often deferred—or performed with ICP monitoring paused—because commonly used devices are not MR‐conditional for in situ operation: parenchymal probes must be disconnected and EVD systems are typically closed or cannot be connected to MR‐conditional transducers. This technical note describes the development of a custom‐made, MR‐safe support frame that enables continuous ICP monitoring and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage during scanning. The device complies with MR‐conditional requirements and allows for hydrostatic levelling to ensure accurate pressure readings.Relevance to Clinical PracticeThis innovation may improve MR access for unstable patients by enabling earlier and repeated imaging, potentially allowing for earlier detection of secondary brain injury.Keywords: critical care nursing, intracranial pressure, magnetic resonance imaging, patient safety

Background

During neurocritical care, intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring is often required in patients with conditions such as subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH), intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) or traumatic brain injury (TBI), as elevated ICP can lead to secondary brain injury, compromised cerebral perfusion and poor neurological outcomes. Continuous monitoring allows timely detection and management of pressure fluctuations, thereby guiding therapeutic interventions to protect the injured brain [1, 2]. External ventricular drainage (EVD) systems are primarily used in patients who require therapeutic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion. When closed, the EVD system also allows for real‐time ICP monitoring. Alternatively, ICP can be continuously monitored using a dedicated parenchymal pressure probe [3].

Neuroimaging is a cornerstone in the management of neurocritical care patients, guiding treatment decisions and assessing disease progression. However, in most hospitals, repeated imaging with Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance (MR) imaging requires intrahospital transport from the controlled environment of the Neurocritical Care Unit (NCCU) to the radiology department.

Intrahospital transports pose substantial risks to critically ill patients. The complication rate can reach up to 70%, with physiological instability during transport contributing to increased ICP and the risk of secondary brain injury [4, 5, 6, 7]. These concerns have led to the development of adapted workflows for bedside or near‐bedside MR imaging in NCCU settings [7, 8, 9, 10].

However, real‐time ICP monitoring during MR scanning remains challenging due to MR safety constraints and equipment limitations. Firstly, although MR‐conditional parenchymal pressure probes are available, manufacturer guidelines require that all external cables, wires and monitors be disconnected prior to MR scanning. Consequently, continuous in situ ICP monitoring cannot be performed during the examination.

Secondly, conventional EVD systems have a proximal catheter segment of less than 10 cm, which is insufficient to reach the pressure transducer contact board in the MR‐conditional monitoring system that needs to be placed at a safe distance from the MR‐bore. This limitation is particularly problematic when patients must lie flat during MR scanning, which can further compromise ICP. As a result, the EVD system is typically closed during scanning [11, 12]. This not only interrupts therapeutic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage but also prevents continuous ICP monitoring, leaving potential pressure elevations undetected during the examination.

To overcome this limitation, we designed a solution enabling continuous ICP monitoring during MR imaging.

Aim

This technical note aims to describe the development of a customised support frame designed to enable safe and accurate intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring during magnetic resonance (MR) imaging. By positioning the pressure transducer and its contact board in a stable location outside the MR‐scanner bore, the solution addresses current limitations in EVD‐based ICP monitoring in neurocritical care settings.

Methods

At Linköping University Hospital, Sweden, ICP monitoring is performed using the Philips Invivo Expression MR400 system (Philips, Orlando, FL, USA), which is MR‐conditional under the limitations of a maximum static magnetic field of 0.5 T and a specific absorption rate (SAR) of 4 W/kg [13]. For invasive pressure monitoring, the manufacturer advises that neither the pressure transducer nor its contact board and cable should be placed within the 0.5 T line, and that they should not be in direct contact with the patient. Simultaneously, the manual refers users to the specific labelling of the pressure transducer for guidance.

However, the pressure transducer's contact board provided with the system (two‐channel CODAN Xtrans) was not labelled with specific MR safety information. As a precaution, the MR‐conditional specifications of the entire monitoring system were adopted when using the transducer. Due to the limited length of the EVD catheter tubing, it has not been possible to connect the EVD system to the contact board while ensuring placement outside the 0.5 T line. As a result, ICP monitoring has typically been discontinued during MR scanning, with the EVD system closed.

To resolve this issue, the manufacturer (CODAN Medizinische Geräte GmbH & Co KG, Lensahn, Germany) was contacted directly, and the Instructions for Use (IFU) for the Xtrans pressure transducers were obtained. According to the IFU, these transducers are MR‐conditional up to 7 T but must not be positioned inside the MR bore during scanning. This latter restriction is in place to ensure measurement accuracy according to the European standard EN 60601‐2‐34, which specifies an acceptable error margin of ±4% or ±4 mmHg [14].

Given that MRI of the brain is the most common neuroimaging modality in the NCCU, the development of a custom support frame made of MR‐safe plexiglass was undertaken to enable continuous ICP monitoring during brain MRI while ensuring compliance with safety and measurement accuracy standards. Plexiglass—polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)—was chosen to provide structural stability and visual transparency while maintaining MR safety [15].

The frame was placed at the foot end of the MR table, just outside the MR bore but within the practical range of the EVD catheter. This position enables hydrostatic alignment of the pressure transducer with the patient's foramen of Monro while keeping the transducer within the MR‐conditional requirements, thus enabling accurate pressure measurements throughout the scan. The system preserves the integrity of the pressure waveform, ensures continuous monitoring and allows the EVD system to remain open when clinically indicated.

The Support Frame

The bottom plate (A) (Figure 1) is designed to be inserted underneath the mattress of the MR table, utilising the patient's body weight to enhance stability. In this setting (3‐T Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), the table surface is flat, which facilitates stable placement. However, the dimensions or shape of the bottom plate may require adjustments depending on the design of the specific MR table used.

Custom‐built plexiglass support frame designed to hold the ICP pressure transducer in a stable position outside the MR scanner bore. The frame allows vertical adjustment to maintain hydrostatic levelling with the patient's foramen of Monro.

The vertical arm of the support frame includes three custom‐designed, 3D‐printed plastic components. The first is a holder for the pressure transducer's contact board (B) (Figure 1), which can be freely adjusted in height to enable hydrostatic levelling with the patient's foramen of Monro. The second component (C) (Figure 1) is a shaped holder designed to securely accommodate the proximal part of the EVD system, including the drainage syringe (model 51‐0002D, Neuromedex GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). This holder is also vertically adjustable, which enables continuous cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage during MR scanning, if clinically indicated. In the latest version of the vertical arm, a millimetre mercury (mmHg) scale has been engraved to allow precise height adjustment for accurate drainage settings. Lastly, a curved top hook (D) (Figure 1) serves as a suspension point for the drainage bag, ensuring appropriate positioning throughout the examination.

All components of the support frame, including the mounted accessories, are assembled using non‐metallic plastic screws to maintain MR safety and allow easy disassembly for cleaning or modification. Importantly, no part of the support frame or its attached components—such as the pressure transducer, drainage tubing or collection bag—comes into direct contact with the patient (Figure 2), thereby preventing radiofrequency‐induced hazards [16]. This setup complies with MR safety standards and is consistent with manufacturer recommendations while scanning the brain, including the MR‐conditional guidelines for the Philips Expression MR400 system and Codan transducers, which specify that certain components must remain outside the 0.5 T line and not be in contact with the patient during scanning.

Photograph of the support frame in clinical setup, showing integration with the EVD system and MR‐conditional monitoring equipment. No part of the frame, transducer or drainage components comes into direct contact with the patient during MR scanning.

Future Implications

The implementation of the support frame enables continuous intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage during MR imaging—capabilities that were previously limited by safety and technical constraints. As a result, MR imaging can be performed earlier in the course of neurocritical care, even in patients with unstable ICP. This may reduce reliance on bedside CT, thereby avoiding unnecessary exposure to ionising radiation and improving diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, the ability to safely repeat MR examinations throughout the patient's stay facilitates closer monitoring of disease progression and may increase the likelihood of detecting secondary injuries, such as ischaemic complications, at an earlier stage. Overall, this technique may contribute to more individualised and optimised neurointensive care.

The device has been used in more than 50 MR scans with continuous ICP monitoring, and no safety issues have been reported, supporting its feasibility and clinical utility. As a subject for further research, ongoing studies are evaluating ICP fluctuations during MR examinations in patients monitored with the support frame, compared to those in whom ICP monitoring is temporarily paused during scanning. These studies may provide additional insight into the clinical benefits of continuous ICP monitoring during MRI and inform future workflow optimisations.

Given its simple design and adaptability, this method has the potential to be implemented in other centres where MR imaging plays a central role in neurocritical care.
