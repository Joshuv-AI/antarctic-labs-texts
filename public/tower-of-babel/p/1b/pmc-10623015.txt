Visual event boundaries restrict anchoring effects in decision-making
Authors: Joan Danielle K. Ongchoco, Robert Walter-Terrill, Brian J. Scholl
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNumbers can be unexpectedly powerful. Suppose you must provide the last two digits of your social security number (SSN), after which you are asked how much you are willing to pay for a bottle of wine. Though your SSN digits are irrelevant to your valuation judgment, they nonetheless influence numerical estimates, such that lower SSN digits lead to lower valuations and higher SSN digits, to higher valuations. Such “anchoring” effects are extremely prevalent and powerful. Here, we demonstrate how a feature of low-level visual perception—the spontaneous segmentation of experience at event boundaries, as when you simply walk through a doorway—can restrict or even eliminate anchoring effects across economic valuations, factual questions, and legal judgments.

It can be tempting to think that we reason or make decisions only by implementing principles of logic or by calculating expected value—but decades of research suggest that reasoning and decision-making are often instead guided by (useful but fallible) heuristics and biases (1). One of the most robust and well-studied of these biases is numerical anchoring, in which arbitrary task-irrelevant numbers influence numerical decision-making (1). Suppose you must provide the last two digits of your social security number (SSN) and then must say how much you are willing to pay for a bottle of wine. Despite your SSN digits being irrelevant to this judgment, the resulting estimates tend to vary with the extremity of those digits—with lower SSN digits (such as “21”) leading to lower valuations and higher SSN digits (such as “89”) leading to higher valuations (2). Such anchoring effects are pervasive (3), occurring in domains including questions of general knowledge (1) (“What is the percentage of African countries in the United Nations?”), economic valuations (2) (“How much would you pay for this bottle of wine?”), probability estimates (4) (“What is the likelihood of a nuclear war?”), forecasts (5) (“How likely do you think it is that this athlete will score in the game?”), and legal judgments (6, 7) (“How many months in prison does this crime deserve?”). These anchoring effects are especially robust when people make an explicit comparison to the anchor values (e.g., “Are the digits greater or less than the price of a bottle of wine?”), but they also occur (though perhaps less robustly) even when the numerical digits are incidental (8–12), as when they appear on an advertisement of a meal whose price you are asked to estimate (12).

Although anchoring estimates to pertinent values may often be adaptive, such a heuristic can also easily become maladaptive in specific circumstances when the values are no longer relevant to the judgments at hand. Yet, previous work exploring the mechanisms and mediators of such effects suggests that anchoring (especially in its explicit form) is remarkably difficult to overcome (3). Anchoring effects persist despite people’s expertise and experience (13), regardless of incentives to avoid them (11, 13), despite explicit warnings of their nature (11, 13), and in some cases perhaps even over long durations in an impoverished form (14).

Past research has explored the impact (or lack thereof) of many forms of cognition on anchoring effects but has rarely made contact with the study of how we perceive the world in the first place. This may be because how we think and how we perceive the world seem intuitively different, lying on different sides of a great theoretical and sociological divide. Theoretically, it has been argued that there are stark limits on how much perception and cognition interact (15, 16), and that “cognition does not affect perception” (17). Sociologically, this divide seems even greater, with perception and cognition often studied in different journals and conferences. The current experiments attempt to bridge such divides.

A growing body of work in perception has explored the nature and power of event segmentation. Though the input to perception is a continuous wash of light and sound over time, what we experience are discrete events. And just as objects in space are segmented from each other by particular cues such as connectedness (18) and closure (19), so too event boundaries in time are triggered by cues such as motion onsets/offsets (20), changes in motion speed (21), and disruptions of visual statistics (22, 23). This type of event segmentation has consequences for attention and memory, e.g., with event boundaries leading to lapses in target detection (24) and increased inattentional blindness (25). Images are also better remembered when they are presented at event boundaries (26), and long-term memory performance correlates with how we segment continuous streams of activity (27). Perhaps the most remarkable effect of event segmentation involves the influence of walking through a doorway on working memory. After memorizing items from a list and then walking across a room, people are worse at remembering these items when they pass through a doorway during their walk than when they do not—equating time and distance traveled (28–30), and this also holds for memory of visual stimuli such as objects or faces (31–33).

Such effects have been explained by appeal to adaptive memory “flushing” (as when one flushes a cache in a computer program) (20). Though we typically think of forgetting as a limitation (due to decay or interference), some forgetting may be adaptive (34). In particular, holding onto information may be maladaptive once that information becomes obsolete—as when previous statistical patterns are no longer applicable to the current state of the world. In this case, it may be best to weaken memories of such information, and this adaptive forgetting may be especially critical at event boundaries, since these are when the statistics of our local environments tend to change most dramatically (35).

The present study combines anchoring and event segmentation: just as event boundaries flush information in visual working memory, perhaps they could also weaken the anchors, thus leading to more effective judgments and decisions. And while of course event boundaries come in many varieties [from scene cuts in films (36) to musical phrases in auditory sequences (37)], here we ask whether a mere incidental (and completely task-irrelevant) visual event boundary may suffice to trigger the restriction or elimination of previously encountered anchors.

We first showed people numerical anchors in a way that (unlike some other incidental anchoring studies) required engagement, yet was still maximally inconspicuous (and indeed didn’t even seem to be a part of the experiment): Before starting an online study, subjects completed a “CAPTCHA” request (“to verify that you are an actual human”) in which they had to reproduce two blurred digits (Fig. 1A). Across subjects, the CAPTCHA digits could either be relatively low (e.g., “29”) or high (e.g., “92”). As in traditional anchoring tasks, subjects were later asked to make a numerical judgment (such as an item valuation; Fig. 1D). Our critical manipulation of event segmentation occurred between the presentation of the anchor and the later judgment: Subjects simply watched an immersive 3D animation simulating a virtual “walk” down a long room (Fig. 1 B and C). Half of them passed through a doorway as the event boundary (Doorway condition; Fig. 2A), while the other half did not (No-Doorway condition; Fig. 2B)—equating time, distance, and paths traveled. As expected, the CAPTCHA induced anchoring effects, but these were restricted or even eliminated in the Doorway condition—and this effect was due to the presence of event boundaries per se, rather than to superficial visual properties (such as the visual complexity of rooms with or without doorways). We explored this in seven experiments, across domains involving economic valuations, general knowledge, and legal judgments—and in the context of both incidental and explicit anchoring.

Results

Incidental Anchoring in Item Valuations.

We first tested for incidental anchoring effects in a classic item valuation judgment (2), in which subjects estimated how much an item (either a piece of luggage, or a tote bag) in the room was worth. Collapsing across items, in the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor in the initial CAPTCHA gave significantly higher value estimates compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [$103.46 (SD = 57.88) vs. $90.11 (SD = 53.79), t(398) = 3.38, P < 0.001, d = 0.24, 95% CI (5.60, 21.11)]. Remarkably, anchoring was eliminated in the Doorway condition [$91.42 (SD = 55.36) vs. $96.48 (SD = 55.12); t(398) = 1.29, P = 0.196, d = 0.09, 95% CI (–12.72, 2.61), BF10 = 0.18], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 929) = 6.57, P = 0.011, np2 = 0.007]. (Plots with the full distributions of responses across conditions—for all experiments reported here—are depicted in Dataset S1.)

For the piece of luggage: In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor in the initial CAPTCHA gave significantly higher value estimates compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [$112.22 (SD = 54.23) vs. $98.82 (SD = 52.13), t(398) = 2.52, P = 0.012, d = 0.25, 95% CI (2.95, 23.86)]—but this anchoring effect was eliminated in the Doorway condition [$103.81 (SD = 53.75) vs. $106.32 (SD = 52.36); t(398) = 0.47, P = 0.636, d = 0.05, 95% CI (–12.95, 7.92), BF10 = 0.12], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 796) = 4.49, P = 0.034, np2 = 0.006].

For the tote bag: In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor gave significantly higher value estimates compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [$94.70 (SD = 60.19) vs. $81.40 (SD = 54.14), t(398) = 2.32, P = 0.021, d = 0.23, 95% CI (2.05, 24.55)]—but this anchoring effect was eliminated in the Doorway condition [$79.05 (SD = 54.28) vs. $86.64 (SD = 56.19), t(398) = 1.38, P = 0.170, d = 0.14, 95% CI (–18.46, 3.26), BF10 = 0.28], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 796) = 6.90, P = 0.009, np2 = 0.009].

Incidental Anchoring in Item Valuations (Controlling for Visual Complexity).

The Doorway and No-Doorway conditions also differed in that the former included a bare wall (Fig. 2A), making the frames leading up to the doorway less visually complex than in the latter (where the furnishings beyond the wall were fully visible; Fig. 2B). To confirm that these results were not due to differences in complexity, we constructed new animations in which the Doorway condition was more visually complex (Fig. 3 A and C) than the No-Doorway condition (Fig. 3 B and D). Subjects were asked to estimate how much a piece of luggage in the room was worth. In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor gave significantly higher value estimates compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [$90.30 (SD = 44.18) vs. $81.39 (SD = 42.10), t(398) = 2.07, P = 0.039, d = 0.21, 95% CI (0.43, 17.40)]—but this anchoring effect was eliminated in the Doorway condition [$83.18 (SD = 40.85) vs. $89.94 (SD = 40.26), t(398) = 1.67, P = 0.096, d = 0.17, 95% CI (–14.73, 1.21), BF10 = 0.42], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 796) = 7.01, P = 0.008, np2 = 0.094].

Incidental Anchoring in Factual Questions.

We also replicated the effect in one of the most common forms of anchoring—answers to factual questions. Subjects were asked to estimate how many days of rain the town of Dubuque, Iowa gets on average per year. (And to ensure that effects in this study were not specific to the particular room layouts in the videos, we created new animations for this experiment.) In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High CAPTCHA anchor gave significantly higher estimates compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [76.11 d (SD = 39.63) vs. 66.34 d (SD = 40.60), t(398) = 2.44, P = 0.015, d = 0.24, 95% CI (1.88, 17.66)]—but this anchoring effect was eliminated in the Doorway condition [67.80 d (SD = 40.65) vs. 71.06 d (SD = 41.29), t(398) = 0.80, P = 0.426, d = 0.08, 95% CI (–11.32, 4.79), BF10 = 0.15], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 796) = 5.17, P = 0.023, np2 = 0.006].

Incidental Anchoring in Legal Judgments.

We also replicated the effect in a legal domain. Subjects were told that a student had been caught vandalizing a high-school gym and were asked to estimate how many hours of community service the student should complete. In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor recommended significantly greater community service hours compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [12.62 h (SD = 9.98) vs. 10.71 h (SD = 8.88), t(398) = 2.03, P = 0.043, d = 0.20, 95% CI (0.06, 3.77)]—but this anchoring effect was eliminated in the Doorway condition [11.10 h (SD = 9.99) vs. 11.88 h (SD = 9.29), t(398) = 0.81, P = 0.419, d = 0.08, 95% CI (–2.68, 1.12), BF10 = 0.15], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 796) = 3.98, P = 0.046, np2 = 0.005].

Incidental Anchoring in Legal Judgments (Larger Replication).

We directly replicated the effect in the previous experiment with double the sample size. In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor again recommended significantly greater community service hours compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [12.96 h (SD = 10.96) vs. 11.46 h (SD = 10.02), t(398) = 2.03, P = 0.042, d = 0.14, 95% CI (0.05, 2.97)]—but this anchoring effect was eliminated in the Doorway condition and if anything was almost reliable in the opposite direction [12.32 h (SD = 11.20) vs. 13.83 h (SD = 12.37), t(398) = 1.80, P = 0.072, d = 0.13, 95% CI (–3.14, 0.14), BF10 = 0.38], with a significant interaction between these conditions [F(1, 796) = 7.27, P = 0.007, np2 = 0.005].

Explicit Anchoring in Item Valuations.

Inspired by the strength of the results in the previous experiments, we also investigated whether such subtle visual event boundaries might even be able to modulate anchoring via explicit comparisons (which yield especially robust and persistent effects). Instead of completing an incidental CAPTCHA before watching the animation, subjects were shown an image of a bag and were asked whether its price was greater or less than an anchor value. After watching the animation, they then provided a numerical value estimate, as in the first three experiments. In the No-Doorway condition, subjects who viewed the High anchor gave significantly higher value estimates compared to subjects who viewed the Low anchor [$79.37 (SD = 39.16) vs. $47.48 (SD = 28.31), t(498) = 10.44, P < 0.001, d = 0.93, 95% CI (25.89, 37.90)]. This explicit anchoring effect was also present in the Doorway condition [$73.19 (SD = 36.00) vs. $52.37 (SD = 27.13), t(498) = 7.30, P < 0.001, d = 0.65, 95% CI (15.22, 26.43)] but was significantly attenuated [F(1, 996) = 7.01, P = 0.008, np2 = 0.007].

Discussion

Visual event segmentation has not been previously discussed (much less employed as a manipulation) in studies of decision-making. Nevertheless, we consistently observed the same connection between these two domains across seven independent experiments. We observed robust incidental anchoring effects from the initial CAPTCHA digits (perhaps magnified by the subjects having had to actually enter the digits themselves), but these effects were restricted or even eliminated when subjects experienced an event boundary (walking through a doorway) while traversing a virtual room—despite equating time, distance traveled, and brute visual complexity. Of course, we suspect that such effects would generalize beyond doorways (and beyond vision), given the sheer variety of possible types of event boundaries (38). But the current results seemed especially striking, given that the event boundaries involved mere passive viewing of an entirely incidental and task-irrelevant visual stimulus. These effects generalized across several of the most prominent examples of anchoring, with mere visual event boundaries eliminating incidental anchoring effects for economic valuation, general knowledge, and legal judgment—and restricting anchoring effects induced with explicit comparisons, which are especially robust. We interpret this in terms of event segmentation leading to active forgetting in working memory (28, 35): the doorway serves as a trigger that previously acquired information may have become obsolete in the context of the new event, and so, even though that memory trace still exists (since subjects generally had no trouble explicitly recalling the anchor value after the fact; see Dataset S1), that value nevertheless ceases to be available as an automatic guide to online behavior.

These results seem intriguing not only because some anchoring effects are notoriously difficult to combat (11, 13), but also because the relevant manipulation (of an incidental low-level visual transition) seems so intuitively unconnected to the higher-level process (of decision-making) being measured. Of course, perception has ubiquitous influences on decision-making in an entirely pedestrian sense, since we often think thoughts and make decisions about the objects that are currently in view. (Your decision to drive through an intersection will obviously depend on the color of the traffic light.) Here, in contrast, the link between perception and decision-making seems both subtler and deeper—with a particular incidental (and entirely task-irrelevant) visual cue spontaneously exerting a surprisingly powerful and specific influence on downstream decisions. In this way, the current study forges a new and unexpected link between visual perception and higher-level judgment. (And while anchoring effects may often be adaptive, the current results suggest a practical solution when they operate as a cognitive bias: the next time you have to make an important decision, you may want to walk through a doorway first!)

Materials and Methods

Preregistrations.

The preregistered methods and analyses for each experiment can be viewed at https://aspredicted.org/blind.php?x=ey3sz4 (Experiments 1 and 2), https://aspredicted.org/blind.php?x=x8wh6t (Experiment 3), https://aspredicted.org/blind.php?x=np7kz3 (Experiment 4), https://aspredicted.org/blind.php?x=8hz2ap (Experiment 5), https://aspredicted.org/blind.php?x=gp7ts9 (Experiment 6), and https://aspredicted.org/JT6_87N (Experiment 7).

IRB Review.

All experimental methods and procedures were approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board, and all subjects confirmed that they had read and understood a consent form outlining their risks, benefits, compensation, and confidentiality, and that they agreed to participate in the experiment.

Experiment 1: Anchoring in Item Valuations (with a Piece of Luggage).

Participants.

Eight hundred unique nonexcluded subjects (200 per condition), recruited using the Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) online platform, participated for monetary compensation. Prescreening criteria required subjects to be based in the United States, have an MTurk task approval rate of at least 95%, and have previously completed at least 500 MTurk tasks. This sample size was chosen before data collection began, was preregistered, and was fixed to be identical for experiments 1 to 5.

Apparatus.

Subjects completed the experiment on either a laptop or desktop computer (as opposed to a phone or tablet) in a web browser, with stimulus presentation and data collection controlled via custom software written using a combination of Hypertext Markup Language (HTML), Cascading Style Sheets (CSS), JavaScript, PHP, and the JsPsych libraries (39). To discourage multitasking, subjects completed the experiment with their browser in fullscreen mode. Since the experiment was rendered on subjects’ own displays and browsers, viewing distance, screen size, and display resolutions could vary dramatically, so we report stimulus dimensions below using pixel (px) values.

Stimuli.

All text, across the instructions and prompts, was presented in black “Open Sans” 18 px font on a white background. For our “anchor”, we created a custom CAPTCHA request, displayed at the center of the browser window (Fig. 1A). This CAPTCHA consisted of a blurred checkerboard image (300 px by 300 px), superimposed with two digits in different font sizes (randomly selected from 80 px to 120 px), font families (one Arial, the other Georgia), different weights (one regular, the other bolded), and variable degrees of blurring (either 0.5 px, 0.6 px, or 0.7 px). The two-digit number could either be a low value (“37”) or high value (“97”). Below the checkerboard image was the prompt, “To verify that you are an actual human, please enter the digits above.” Below this prompt was a text input box (190 px by 45 px) with “Captcha” as an input placeholder. Beside this input box was a green rectangular button box (90 px by 45 px) with the text, “Verify”.

Video animations were created using Sweet Home 3D. We designed a 40.5 by 20 foot room, populated with furniture and objects from built-in models in the Sweet Home 3D library. Floor layouts (as depicted in Fig. 2 A and B) were identical in both “Doorway” and “No Doorway” conditions, except for the dividing wall (with doorway) that bisected the room in the former condition. The animation of the “virtual walk” was created by using the software’s camera and “Create a 3D video” function. The path of the camera was set by identifying key positions where the camera would pass by during the 3D simulation. This path started with the camera view facing a computer screen, as if the observer were seated by a desk. The camera would then pan as if the observer were standing up, turning, and then moving across the room. The path ended with the camera facing a view of the other end of the long room. (For camera paths, see Fig. 2 A and B. The Doorway and No Doorway animations can be viewed in Movies S1 and S2, respectively.) Each animation lasted for 20 s and was rendered within Sweet Home 3D into 1,280 px by 720 px videos at 25 frames/second. These files were converted into .mp4 files to increase web browser compatibility, and the displayed width of the video was set to be 93% of the subject’s monitor width at fullscreen.

Procedure and design.

There were 4 trial types: 2 room types (Doorway or No-Doorway) × 2 anchor values (High or Low), and each subject was randomly assigned to one of these four conditions. Subjects were first presented with the CAPTCHA request and were only allowed to proceed if they reported the correct digits. After completing the CAPTCHA, subjects watched a video animation (Fig. 1 B and C). At the start of the animation, instructions were presented within a white rectangular box (630 px by 410 px): “You are about to watch an animation where you will be situated in a room. The scene will then gradually change as if you are virtually walking through the room. Just try your best to place yourself in the room, and imagine you were actually walking through it.” To begin the animation, subjects pressed a key. At the end of the virtual-walk animation, subjects were prompted with the image of an item, and estimated its monetary value. The response prompt was a white rectangular box (880 px by 530 px) overlaid on the background at 90% opacity (so that subjects could still see the room in the background; Fig. 1D): “Below is a close-up of the luggage beside the couch. Without knowing any more details other than what is in the photo, how much in US dollars do you think this luggage is worth?”. Below the text and image was a text box (190 px by 45 px, just above a 60 px × 20 px “Submit” button) where subjects could input an amount. Subjects then completed a debriefing questionnaire, which included questions asking about how well they paid attention (on a scale of 1 to 100), and whether they were familiar with anchoring effects.

Data analysis.

Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 50/100 or reported knowledge of the anchoring effect [n = 258; data from these subjects (in this and all subsequent experiments) were not recorded and were automatically discarded and so are not included in the online datasets], 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 28), and 3) extreme response values (below 10, above 350, or more than 1.5 SDs from the mean; n = 44). The dependent measure was subjects’ numerical estimates of how much they estimated the piece of luggage to be worth. In each of the room conditions (Doorway and No-Doorway), a two-tailed between-samples t test was used to determine whether the estimates in the High and Low anchor conditions reliably differed. We then used a 2 × 2 ANOVA to determine whether there was a reliable interaction between the anchor and room conditions.

Experiment 2: Anchoring in Item Valuations (with a Tote Bag).

This experiment was identical to experiment 1 except as noted. Eight hundred new subjects were recruited. The response prompt box contained the text, “Below is a close-up of the bag on the couch. Without knowing any more details other than what is in the photo, how much in US dollars do you think this bag is worth?”. Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria, in the following order: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 50/100 or reported knowledge of the anchoring effect (n = 280), 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 38), and 3) extreme response values (below 10, above 350, or more than 1.5 SDs from the mean; n = 67).

Experiment 3: Anchoring in Item Valuations (Controlling for Visual Complexity).

This experiment was identical to experiment 1 except as noted. Eight hundred new subjects were recruited using the Prolific online platform. We computed the frame-by-frame complexity of the video animations (reduced in resolution to account for textured walls) from experiments 1 and 2 using a lossless compression algorithm (40). This resulted in complexity values of 0.372 for the Doorway animation, and 0.409 for the No-Doorway condition, where a lower proportion reflects greater redundancy, and thus, lower complexity. To rule out this potential confound of visual complexity, new video animations were constructed by reducing the overall visual complexity for both conditions by removing some furnishing from the rooms (Fig. 3 A and B) and also increasing visual complexity in the Doorway condition by including additional furnishings along what had previously been a bare wall (Fig. 3 C and D). As a result of these manipulations, the complexity values reversed, with 0.328 and 0.285 for the Doorway and No-Doorway conditions, respectively. (The Doorway and No Doorway animations can be viewed in Movies S3 and S4, respectively.) The CAPTCHA numbers could either be a low value (“29”) or high value (“92”). After subjects gave their estimate, they completed a final memory question: “Earlier, you completed a CAPTCHA by typing in a two-digit number. What was the two-digit number that you typed in a while ago?” Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 80/100 or reported knowledge of the anchoring effect (n = 255), 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 20), and 3) extreme response values (below 10, above 350, or more than 2 SDs from the mean; n = 50).

Experiment 4: Anchoring in Factual Questions.

This experiment was identical to experiment 3 except as noted. Eight hundred new subjects were recruited. New video animations with new wall colors and furnishings were constructed, while still equating overall time and paths traveled from experiments 1 to 3. (The Doorway and No Doorway animations can be viewed in Movies S5 and S6, respectively.) The CAPTCHA numbers could either be a low value (“28”) or high value (“82”). The response prompt box contained the text: “Please answer the following question—without looking up the answer, since we’re interested in your intuitive guess: how many days of rain do you think that the town of Dubuque, Iowa gets on average per year?”. Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 80/100 or reported knowledge of the anchoring effect (n = 313), 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 18), and 3) extreme response values (above 365, or more than 2 SDs from the mean; n = 52).

Experiment 5: Anchoring in Legal Judgments.

This experiment was identical to experiment 3 except as noted. Eight hundred new subjects were recruited. New video animations that reversed the complexity of the animations from experiment 4 (such that the Doorway animation was more complex than the No-Doorway animation, via the same types of manipulations that varied between experiments 1/2 and 3) were constructed. (The Doorway and No Doorway animations can be viewed in Movies S7 and S8, respectively.) The CAPTCHA numbers could either be a low value (“18”) or high value (“81”). The response prompt box contained the text: “A male student has been caught vandalizing the high school gym (by spray-painting their initials behind the bleachers). Without any further information about this behavior, how many hours of community service should this student complete?”. Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 80/100 or reported knowledge of the anchoring effect (n = 208), 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 24), and 3) extreme response values (more than 1.5 SDs from the mean; n = 44).

Experiment 6: Anchoring in Legal Judgments (Larger Replication).

This experiment was identical to experiment 5 except as noted. First, 1,600 new subjects were recruited (with this preregistered sample size arbitrarily doubling that of experiment 5, to increase power). Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 80/100 or reported knowledge of the anchoring effect (n = 435), 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 63), and 3) extreme response values (more than 1.5 SDs from the mean; n = 61).

Experiment 7: Anchoring via Explicit Comparison.

This experiment was identical to experiment 2 except as noted. First, 1,000 new subjects were recruited. Instead of a CAPTCHA, subjects first saw a prompt: “Below is an image of a tote bag. Without knowing any more details other than what is in the photo, do you think that the price of this bag is greater or less than” a price that could either be a low value (“29”) or high value (“92”). Per the preregistered exclusion criteria, subjects were excluded from the analyses (with replacement) for the following criteria, in the following order: 1) self-reported attention ratings below 80/100 (n = 17), 2) response times more than 2 SDs from the mean (n = 26), and 3) extreme response values (below 10, above 350, or more than 1.5 SDs from the mean; n = 79).
