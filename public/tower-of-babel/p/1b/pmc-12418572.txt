Neurophysiological characteristics accompanying runners with Achilles tendinopathy and their influence in triceps surae performance (PhD Academy Award)
Authors: Gabriel Lima Fernandes
Published: 2025

What did I do?

My PhD aimed to explore the influence of cortical and corticospinal mechanisms of motor control and their influence in plantarflexor performance in runners with Achilles tendinopathy and whether it affected each muscle of the triceps surae differently (figure 1). Alongside neurophysiological measures, I also measured plantarflexor peak isometric torque, using isokinetic dynamometry, and endurance, as the maximal number of single-leg heel raises (SLHR). The first study focused on the brain’s role in motor control, where I investigated short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) to the triceps surae. In the second study, I shifted attention to α-motoneurons, specifically motor units, which consist of a group of muscle fibres innervated by the same α-motoneuron. This study explored the central nervous system’s independent control of each triceps surae muscle during isometric contractions and examined whether these control strategies differ between individuals with and without Achilles tendinopathy. Finally, I investigated a potential strategy to selectively increase lateral gastrocnemius activation of the by positioning the foot inwards during isometric plantarflexion.

Why did I do it?

Achilles tendinopathy is a highly prevalent injury among runners and often becomes chronic, with many patients failing to respond to conservative treatment.1 At the time, existing evidence2 suggested that the soleus was the primary muscle affected in Achilles tendinopathy and responsible for deficits in plantarflexor strength and endurance reported in this population. Force production results from multiple inhibitory and facilitatory mechanisms controlled by the central nervous system. This motivated me to investigate some of these mechanisms of motor control (cortical and corticospinal) and their impact in triceps surae performance. Advancements in neurophysiology now allow non-invasive techniques to assess motor unit firing characteristics, reflecting muscle activity during contraction. Furthermore, these techniques enable the investigation of individual muscles separately. These methods provided an opportunity to investigate the mechanisms potentially affecting soleus strength and endurance in runners with Achilles tendinopathy, helping to clarify the neurophysiological factors underpinning it.

How did I do it?

Building on a study that investigated SICI in athletes with patellar tendinopathy,3 I used transcranial magnetic stimulation, to assess SICI to the triceps surae in chronic Achilles tendinopathy. In studies 2 and 3, I used surface high-density electromyography (HD-EMG), a non-invasive technique that uses multiple EMG electrodes in a single surface grid (eg, 64 channels), allowing the recording of a sample of motor units, and the analysis of their characteristics, such as the rate at which they fire during a contraction. The analysis of firing rates from motor units recorded from the soleus and lateral and medial gastrocnemius provided insight into how the central nervous system recruited each muscle during a contraction (ie, neural drive). This method allowed the investigation of whether runners with Achilles tendinopathy exhibit selective muscle weakness, as suggested by previous studies, and which muscles were affected. Study 2 investigated motor unit firing rates from individual muscles of the triceps surae during an increase in isometric force intensity and compared these against matching controls. Study 3 explored an efficient strategy to selectively increase the neural drive to the lateral gastrocnemius in a healthy population, which had never been trialled in a clinical population such as individuals with Achilles tendinopathy. Therefore, I measured motor unit firing rates and the level of muscle activity from lateral and medial gastrocnemius during standing isometric plantarflexion in two conditions, with the feet in a neutral position and with the feet pointing inwards, and compared runners with and without Achilles tendinopathy.

What did I find?

The results of study 14 demonstrated that individuals with Achilles tendinopathy have increased inhibition (greater SICI) to the triceps surae. In addition to this increased inhibition, the Achilles tendinopathy group also presented bilateral reduction in plantarflexor endurance (lower number of SLHR repetitions), even in individuals with unilateral Achilles tendinopathy. Motor units typically increase their firing rate to adjust to higher force demand. In study 2,5 I found that the lateral gastrocnemius of runners with Achilles tendinopathy did not increase their firing rates in response to force adjustments, suggesting a lower neural drive. In contrast, both the soleus and medial gastrocnemius demonstrated similar responses in both groups, showing an increase in motor unit firing rates to adjust the greater force demand. Study 36 demonstrated that positioning the feet inwards during plantarflexion may be an effective strategy to enhance lateral gastrocnemius activation in runners with and without tendinopathy.

What is the most important practical application?

Collectively, my research demonstrated that the central nervous system mechanisms of motor control appear to inhibit the triceps surae in runners with Achilles tendinopathy, particularly the lateral gastrocnemius, through cortical and possible corticospinal inhibitory mechanisms. Furthermore, the plantarflexor deficit observed in our sample of runners with Achilles tendinopathy across all studies was specific to endurance rather than maximal force. A key finding of study 1 was that alongside the increased intracortical inhibition in the Achilles tendinopathy group, associated with a reduced plantarflexor endurance. This reduced endurance was bilateral even in cases with unilateral tendinopathy presentation, outlining the influence of the central nervous system. Study 2 identified the lateral gastrocnemius as the main muscle affected in this population. Finally, study 3 showed that positioning the feet inwards during plantarflexion can selectively increase the activation of the lateral gastrocnemius, emerging as a promising treatment strategy for runners with Achilles tendinopathy.
