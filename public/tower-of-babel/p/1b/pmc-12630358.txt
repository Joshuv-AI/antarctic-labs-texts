Integrative multi-omics analysis identifies genetically supported druggable targets for inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Si-Chun Gu, Si-lu Zeng, Wei Zhang, Chang-Yi Shen, Can-Xing Yuan, Yu Song, Qing Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Comprehensive multi-omics identifies novel druggable targets for IBD.•Strong genetic support enhances target validity and clinical potential.•Causal inference from SMR and colocalization bolsters findings.•Phenome-wide MR confirms target safety profiles.•Drug prediction and docking reveal strong candidate binding.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), represents a set of chronic, immune-mediated gastrointestinal disorders 1, 2. The incidence and global burden of IBD have shown a consistent upward trend over the past decades, posing escalating social and healthcare challenges worldwide 3.

The management of IBD is multifaceted, aiming to induce and maintain remission, reduce disease-related complications, and enhance the quality of life for affected individuals. Standard therapies include 5-aminosalicylic acid, corticosteroids, immunomodulators (e.g., azathioprine), and biologics (e.g., anti-TNF-alpha agents) 4, 5, 6. However, some patients do not respond optimally, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic targets 7.

Advances in genetics research focusing on 'druggable' genes presents a significant opportunity to pave the way for innovative drug development, potentially transforming the treatment landscape for a multitude of complex diseases 8, 9. Large-scale integrative omics analyses, especially those leveraging genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping, enable a deeper investigation of potential molecular mechanisms for complex disease 10, 11, 12, 13, 14.

Thus, we adopted a systems genetics approach by integrating methylation-QTL (mQTL), expression-QTL (eQTL), and GWAS data to identify IBD-associated genes through summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) 15. Key findings were validated using independent data from the FinnGen cohort 16, 17, 18, 19, and further refined by Bayesian colocalization to distinguish true causality from linkage 20. To assess pleiotropic implications and predict therapeutic relevance, we conducted phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) and employed computational drug prioritization pipelines coupled with molecular docking simulations. These integrative analyses collectively highlight a panel of genetically anchored candidate targets for IBD, paving the way toward rational drug development and precision therapeutics.

Methods

Study design

The overall workflow of the study was schematically summarized in Fig. 1. We performed a multi-omics SMR analysis to examine causal relationships between genetic regulation of candidate druggable genes and IBD risk. The International Inflammatory Bowel Disease Genetics Consortium (IIBDGC) 16 served as the primary discovery dataset (Table 1). We extracted cis-QTLs for gene methylation and expression from publicly available resources 17, 18, 19. Replication was performed in FinnGen 19, and tissue-specific validation used GTEx eQTL data (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/index.html). Colocalization analysis distinguished shared causal variants 20. In the final phase, a series of downstream investigations including PheWAS, computational drug prioritization, and molecular docking simulations were conducted to explore the translational potential of the identified genetic targets. Identification of druggable genes

Druggable genes were compiled from two complementary sources: the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb v4.2.0; https://www.dgidb.org/) and research report by Finan et al. 21. The DGIdb integrates information from literature, biological databases, and web-based repositories to annotate gene–drug relationships. We extracted the “Druggable Categories” subset and mapped entries to Entrez gene identifiers. Additionally, we incorporated the gene list from Finan et al., which systematically linked GWAS loci to druggable genome elements, providing a foundation for therapeutic prioritization 9 (Table 1, Supplementary table 1).

Multi-Omic QTL resources and Integration Strategy

QTLs were employed to reveal associations between single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). We integrated multi-layer QTL datasets linking genetic variants to epigenomic and transcriptomic traits. MQTL data were obtained from a large-scale blood-based study by McRae et al., comprising 1,980 individuals of European ancestry 17. EQTL data were sourced from the eQTLGen consortium, encompassing 31,684 individuals 22. In addition, tissue-specific eQTLs were retrieved from the GTEx v8 portal 23, which contains data from 17,382 samples across 52 human tissues. For disease-relevant resolution, we specifically extracted cis-eQTLs from intestinal tissues (Table 1).

IBD GWAS summary statistics

GWAS summary-level data for IBD, CD, and UC were primarily derived from IIBDGC, covering 25,042 IBD cases and 34,915 controls 16. The sub-phenotypes included 12,194CD cases and 12,366 UC cases. The sub-phenotype analyses for CD and UC utilized distinct, non-overlapping control sets to ensure the independence and validity of the sub-phenotype associations. Furthermore, we clarified that the diagnostic criteria for all cases were uniformly based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) definitions (ICD-9 and ICD-10), ensuring a high degree of phenotypic homogeneity and comparability across the study. For replication, we employed GWAS results from the FinnGen consortium 19, which comprised a distinct sample set (Table 1).

Summary-Data-Based Mendelian randomization

We used SMR (v1.3.1) 13 to robustly infer the causal associations between the genetic regulation of druggable genes (via methylation and expression) and the susceptibility to IBD and its subtypes. The SMR method systematically integrates genetic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic data to provide a statistically rigorous framework for causal inference, thereby offering deeper mechanistic insights into disease etiology. For each gene, we selected the lead cis-QTL within ± 1000 kb using a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−8). SNPs with allele frequency discrepancies > 0.2 between QTL, outcome, or linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference datasets were excluded.

Colocalization analysis

To comprehensively assess the associations between the regulation of druggable genes and IBD across different biological levels, we classified causal candidate genes as those that demonstrated causal associations with IBD and its subtypes (i.e., gene-IBD, gene-CD, and gene-UC associations) at both methylation and expression levels. For these candidate genes, colocalization analyses were conducted to identify shared causal variants between two traits (Trait 1: expression of each validated candidate druggable gene, Trait 2: IBD-related traits) using the 'coloc' package (https://github.com/clagiamba/moloc) 20, 24. Five posterior probabilities are reported, corresponding to the following hypotheses: H0: no association for both traits, H1: the association only for Trait 1 with the genetic variant, H2: the association only for Trait 2 with the genetic variant, H3: both traits are associated with the genetic variant, but the associations are caused by different genetic variants, and H4: both traits are associated with the genetic variant, and the associations are caused by the same genetic variant. The colocalization region was defined as ± 1000 kb around each gene. For trait-specific associations, prior probabilities were set at 1e-4, while for shared variants, the prior was set at 1e-5. Since the H4 hypothesis suggests that both traits are influenced by the same causal variant, we prioritized results where the H4 assumption held. A posterior probability for H4 (PPH4) greater than 0.75 was considered strong evidence of colocalization 25. These findings identified therapeutic targets for IBD and its subtypes, providing robust support for causal relationships.

Statistical power and sensitivity analyses

To rigorously assess the robustness and validity of the SMR findings, we incorporated a multi-faceted approach to statistical power and sensitivity analyses. First, the statistical power of the SMR analysis was systematically calculated post-hoc for all identified associations. Power was estimated based on the observed effect sizes, the variance of the exposure (QTL) and outcome (IBD GWAS) data, and the sample size of the GWAS. We ensured that the study possessed adequate power (typically exceeding 80 %) to reliably detect the reported causal effects at the nominal significance level (ɑ = 0.05). Second, the heterogeneity in dependent instrument (HEIDI) test was employed as a primary sensitivity measure to detect potential pleiotropy, with a stringent threshold of P-HEIDI < 0.01 used to exclude associations where the causal variant was likely distinct from the QTL. Third, the robustness of the false discovery rate (FDR) threshold (ɑ = 0.05) was examined. Only associations with both FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 and P-HEIDI > 0.01 were retained in SMR analyses. Finally, to validate these therapeutic targets for IBD and its subtypes, we conducted SMR analysis of the causal associations between therapeutic targets blood cis-eQTL and IBD and subtypes in FinnGen study datasets. Furthermore, colocalization analyses were also replicated between therapeutic targets blood cis-eQTL and IBD and subtypes in FinnGen study datasets. Moreover, we validated therapeutic targets for IBD and its subtypes with use of intestine-specific cis-eQTL from the GTEx v8 dataset.

Phenome-Wide causal inference

To assess pleiotropic effects and potential off-target implications, we conducted PheW-MR using data from Zhou et al. 26, who applied the SAIGE framework to over 1,400 binary traits across 408,961 individuals of European ancestry in UK Biobank (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). SMR analyses were extended to 780 non-IBD traits, using publicly available GWAS summary statistics derived from SAIGE (https://www.leelabsg.org/resources).

Tissue expression profiling

To characterize tissue-level expression patterns of prioritized druggable targets, we queried the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). This resource includes RNA and protein expression data for over 15,000 genes across 44 human tissues. Cross-tissue profiling of therapeutic targets was used to infer their physiological relevance and potential systemic effects.

Candidate drug prediction

The Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) (https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) was utilized to predict candidate drugs for druggable genes that exhibited strong colocalization with IBD signals.

DSigDB is a comprehensive database containing 22,527 gene sets and 17,389 unique compounds, covering 19,531 genes. For the identified druggable genes, we searched DSigDB to predict potential candidate drugs and evaluate their pharmacological activity. We retrieved pharmacologically active candidate drugs for each gene to support downstream prioritization.

Molecular docking simulation

To evaluate the binding energies and interaction patterns between candidate drugs and their respective targets, we performed molecular docking analyses. This process helped prioritize drug targets for further experimental validation and guided the refinement of prospective candidate drugs. Structural data for the drug compounds were obtained from the PubChem Compound Database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in SDF format and subsequently converted to Protein Data Bank (PDB) format using OpenBabel 2.4.1. Protein structural data were sourced from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking was conducted using the AutoDock 4.2.6 software (https://autodock.scripps.edu/) on the top candidate drugs and the proteins encoded by their target genes. For AutoDock simulations, grid box dimensions were set to 60 × 60 × 60 points with a spacing of 0.375 Å. The grid center was positioned at the predicted active site of each protein structure based on ligand binding pocket prediction using fpocket software. For binding site selection, we selected the highest-ranked pocket based on druggability score and volume. The docking results, including binding affinities and interaction patterns, were visualized using PyMol 3.0.2 (https://www.pymol.org/).

Colocalization analysis

Due to their significant causal associations with IBD and its subtypes at both mQTL and eQTL levels, THBS3, CTSS, RORC, and TNFRSF25 were identified as candidate druggable genes. Genetic colocalization analyses were performed to evaluate the likelihood of shared single causal variants between IBD, UC, and CD loci and the blood cis-eQTLs of THBS3, CTSS, RORC, and TNFRSF25. The results revealed that THBS3 showed strong colocalization with significant results in IBD (PPH4 = 0.96), UC (PPH4 = 0.92), and CD (PPH4 = 0.77) datasets (Fig. 4A-C). For RORC, strong colocalization was observed for UC (PPH4 = 0.97) and CD (PPH4 = 0.90), with a lower probability in IBD (PPH4 = 0.25) (Fig. 4D-F). Sensitivity analyses

We explored the causal associations of candidate druggable gene expression with IBD, UC, and CD in intestinal tissues. Due to the lack of instruments for CTSS, RORC, and TNFRSF25 in the intestine eQTL data, these genes were not included in the analysis. Genetically predicted expression levels of THBS3were significantly associated with increased risks of IBD (OR: 1.21, 95 % CI: 1.10–1.32, FDR-corrected P for SMR = 0.001), UC (OR: 1.20, 95 % CI: 1.08–1.33, FDR-corrected P for SMR = 0.006), and CD (OR: 1.24, 95 % CI: 1.10–1.39, FDR-corrected P for SMR = 0.008) in intestinal tissue (Supplementary table 11).

In the replication phase using eQTL-IBD data from the FinnGen study, the majority of associations maintained a consistent direction with the discovery stage results (Fig. 5). Phenome-wide MR analysis of IBD therapeutic targets

This analysis aimed to assess whether the expressions of the three IBD-associated druggable genes (THBS3, RORC, and TNFRSF25) in blood circulation might have beneficial or harmful effects on other phenotypes. The results revealed no significant associations between these genes and other traits at the genome-wide significance level (FDR-corrected P < 0.05, supplementary Fig. 1). These findings supported the validity of targeting THBS3, RORC, and TNFRSF25 therapeutically, with minimal risk of adverse drug reactions or unintended pleiotropic effects.

IBD therapeutic target expression in different tissues

We used data from the Human Protein Atlas to identify the expression patterns of THBS3, RORC, and TNFRSF25 across human tissues. THBS3 was predominantly expressed in the endometrium, colon, and thyroid gland (supplementary Fig. 2A), while RORC showed high expression in the liver and skeletal muscle (supplementary Fig. 2B). TNFRSF25 was most notably expressed in the tonsil, appendix, small intestine, and colon (supplementary Fig. 2C). These tissue-specific expression patterns may provide insights into the target tissues and potential pathways for these therapeutic genes.

Discussion

Our study employed an integrative multi-omics framework to identify and validate three drug target genes (THBS3, RORC, and TNFRSF25) with IBD, UC, and CD. The phenome-wide MR analysis revealed no potential adverse effects for these target genes, reinforcing their viability for therapeutic development.

THBS3 is a matricellular protein involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis, macrophage polarization, and immune cell recruitment. It interacts with matrix components such as collagen and fibronectin to maintain tissue architecture, and regulates macrophage phenotype and iron metabolism in mucosal repair. THBS3 also influences T cell subset dynamics, including Th1/Th2 balance and cytokine secretion 27, 28, 29. Our genetic findings demonstrated a strong association between THBS3 expression and increased IBD susceptibility, particularly in the colon, reinforcing its potential as a therapeutic target. Drug docking analyses identified AM580 and Dasatinib as promising THBS3 modulators. DSigDB identified AM580 (a RARα agonist) and Dasatinib (a broad-spectrum tyrosine kinase inhibitor) as compounds with strong binding 30. Given that THBS3 is a crucial ECM protein, changes in ECM regulation induced by AM580 could potentially alter THBS3 levels 31, 32. Dasatinib is a broad-spectrum tyrosine kinase inhibitor utilized primarily in the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia and acute lymphoblastic leukemia 33. It targets multiple kinases, including Breakpoint Cluster Region-Abelson tyrosine-protein kinase (BCR-ABL) and Src family kinases (SFK), which are pivotal in regulating cell proliferation, survival, and migration. Dasatinib's inhibition of SFK can influence various signaling pathways associated with cell migration, adhesion, and ECM dynamics 34, 35. Since THBS3 plays a role in ECM regulation, Dasatinib's modulation of these pathways could potentially impact THBS3 expression or activity indirectly. Further research is imperative to elucidate the specific interactions between these compounds and THBS3, which could provide valuable insights into their therapeutic potential and effects on IBD and its subtypes.

Our study identified RORC as a crucial player in IBD. RORC encodes the transcription factor RORγt, which drives T helper 17 (Th17) cell differentiation 36. Th17 cells produce key pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-17) that have been implicated in the initiation and progression of IBD 37, 38. Elevated RORγt and IL-17 levels in IBD intestinal mucosa highlight the pathogenic significance of Th17-mediated responses 39, 40. Animal experiments further confirm that RORγt inhibitors reduce Th17 cell development and diminish colitis severity 41, 42, 43. In line with these findings, our MR and colocalization analyses provided replicated evidence supporting a causal role for RORC in IBD. Additionally, 25-Hydroxycholesterol (an oxysterol) and MPA emerged as potential RORC-targeting agents. By binding to or downregulating RORC/RORγt, these compounds may suppress Th17 cells and their pro-inflammatory cytokines 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49. These findings align with prior functional studies and underscore the therapeutic rationale for RORC-targeted interventions.

TNFRSF25 modulates T cell proliferation, cytokine production, and regulatory T cell function. It also augments Th17-related pathways that drive IBD-associated inflammation 50, 51, 52, 53. TNFRSF25 is highly expressed in inflamed intestinal tissues of both IBD patients and mice with experimental colitis, indicating a dual immunological role but predominantly pro-inflammatory activity in chronic disease 52, 53. Our results demonstrated that genetically higher TNFRSF25 expression correlates with increased IBD risk, underscoring its therapeutic potential. The current research also provided new insights by suggesting that DIM might treat IBD through modulation of TNFRSF25. DIM is a bioactive compound derived from the digestion of indole-3-carbinol, found in cruciferous vegetables like broccoli and Brussels sprouts. DIM has gained attention for its anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and immunomodulatory properties 54. Research indicated that DIM could modulate immune responses by affecting cytokine production and T cell differentiation in IBD 55, 56. Specifically, DIM has been shown to inhibit Th17 cell differentiation and reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 and TNF-α 57. Given that TNFRSF25 influences Th17 cell activity, DIM may indirectly affect TNFRSF25-mediated pathways by suppressing Th17 responses. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms by which DIM may influence TNFRSF25 expression or signaling in the context of IBD. Hesperidin is a flavonoid abundantly found in citrus fruits, known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and vaso-protective effects. In the context of IBD, hesperidin has demonstrated protective effects in experimental models by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and improving intestinal barrier function. However, its specific mechanism remains not fully understood. Our study indicated that hesperidin might exert its therapeutic effects by acting on TNFRSF25 58. The underlying biological principle might involve hesperidin's anti-inflammatory properties, particularly its inhibition of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways, which are crucial for the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes 59. By suppressing NF-κB activation, hesperidin might reduce the expression of cytokines that activate TNFRSF25, thereby potentially modulating its activity indirectly. Though mechanistic links remain to be validated experimentally, these compounds present promising leads for further exploration.

This study presented several strengths. First, it was the first to systematically integrate methylation and expression QTL data for druggable gene prioritization in IBD using summary-level MR and colocalization approaches. Second, by anchoring the analysis in pre-defined druggable targets, we enhanced the translational relevance of findings. Third, the use of large-scale, multi-ancestry GWAS datasets provided sufficient statistical power to detect robust associations. The phenome-wide MR framework added an additional safety layer, identifying targets with minimal off-target effects. Finally, drug prediction and docking analyses advanced potential therapeutic hypotheses supported by both genetic and structural evidence.

Nonetheless, limitations should be acknowledged. First, limited protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data restricted direct protein-level investigation. Second, our GWAS data were primarily derived from European ancestry cohorts, which might constrain generalizability. To enhance the generalizability, future efforts should integrate multi-ethnic GWAS data to validate the causal relationships across diverse populations. Third, the identified targets represented novel therapeutic avenues distinct from current IBD medications, as no approved therapies directly targeted THBS3, RORC, or TNFRSF25. While this highlighted innovation potential, the lack of existing clinical experience and experimental validation with these specific targets increased developmental uncertainty compared to established therapeutic mechanisms. Future in vitro and in vivo models are essential to confirm the functional role of these genes in intestinal inflammation and IBD pathogenesis, which include conducting targeted gene knockdown or knockout experiments in relevant cell lines and IBD animal models to assess the impact on inflammatory pathways, immune cell differentiation, and overall disease phenotype. Fourth, the clinical applicability assessment of candidate drugs revealed translational gaps. While computational drug repurposing and molecular docking provided initial hypotheses, these predictions required rigorous experimental validation. The pharmacological activity of predicted compounds against target proteins must be confirmed through biochemical binding assays and cellular functional studies. Moreover, safety concerns significantly constrain repurposing potential for several candidates. Dasatinib carried established risks including pleural effusion occurrence, gastrointestinal adverse effects, and myelosuppression that pose substantial concerns in immunologically compromised IBD populations 33, 60. AM580 exhibited retinoid-related teratogenicity and hepatotoxicity 61, 62. For compounds with more favorable preclinical safety profiles including 25-hydroxycholesterol, medroxyprogesterone acetate, DIM, and hesperidin, clinical efficacy data in IBD patients remained absent 63, 64. Critical knowledge gaps persist regarding optimal therapeutic dosing, bioavailability from various formulations, pharmacokinetic optimization, and potential drug interactions. The convergence of genetic evidence supporting target validity and predictions of drug-target interactions provides valuable direction for therapeutic development, yet substantial preclinical and clinical investigation is essential before clinical translation can be realized.

Conclusions

In summary, our integrative analysis identified THBS3, RORC, and TNFRSF25 as promising druggable targets for IBD. Candidate therapeutic agents including AM580, Dasatinib, 25-Hydroxycholesterol, MPA, DIM, and Hesperidin were prioritized through computational drug repositioning and structure-based docking. These findings provided a translational framework for accelerating drug discovery in IBD and offer mechanistic insight into target-disease-drug relationships.
