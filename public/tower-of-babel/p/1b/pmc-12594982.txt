Theoretical aspects and simulation with the application of a new two parameter distribution
Authors: Ahlem Ghouar, Sule Omeiza Bashiru, Halim Zeghdoudi, Arwa M Alshangiti, Mahmoud E Bakr, Oluwafemi Samson Balogun, Alexis Habineza, Eslam Hussam, Ahmed M Gemeay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research introduces a new two-parameter distribution (NTPD). The probability density function of the NTPD exhibits distinctive characteristics, making it a valuable tool for modelling various real-world phenomena. Several key statistical properties of the distribution, including the mode, reliability function, hazard function, moments, moment generating function, Rényi entropy, fuzzy reliability, stochastic ordering, and quantile function, are derived explicitly. The model parameters are estimated using sixteen classical methods, and their performance is assessed through a comprehensive simulation study. Applications to datasets with complex behaviors, such as high skewness and peakedness, from environmental science, biomedical science, and reliability engineering demonstrate that the NTPD provides a superior fit compared to several established distributions, including the Lindley, XLindley, modified Lindley, sine Lindley, and exponentiated inverse Rayleigh distributions.Keywords: Lindley distribution, Maximum product of spacings, Environmental science, Biomedical science, Reliability engineeringSubject terms: Scientific data, Software, Statistics

Introduction

Recent developments in lifetime modeling and statistical inference have explored novel distributions and advanced estimation techniques under censoring and complex data structures. For example, Panahi24 investigated reliability inference for nano-droplet rebound data using comparative Kumaraswamy populations under joint progressive censoring. Similarly, Asadi et al.4 proposed an improved adaptive progressive censoring method for estimating the parameters of the Burr type III distribution, with applications in surface coating reliability. In the context of goodness-of-fit testing, Laio13 discussed Cramér-von Mises and Anderson-Darling tests for extreme value distributions with unknown parameters. Furthermore, A. L. Sobhi1 introduced the inverse-power logistic-exponential distribution and applied it to insurance data, highlighting its flexibility and inferential power.

Models and data related to the lifetime or durability of processes or items are crucial in many practical areas, including finance, engineering, and medicine. Scientists have developed various probability distributions to model lifetime data in fields such as engineering, biology, medical sciences, economics, and environmental sciences. Among these distributions, the Lindley distribution19 has garnered significant attention due to its simplicity and flexibility. It was generated as a combination of an exponential and gamma distribution, and its statistical properties were analyzed by Ghitany et al.14.

To overcome the constraints of the Lindley distribution and enhance flexibility, academics have suggested several extensions and generalizations. Shanker et al.28 proposed a two-parameter Lindley distribution that encompasses the original Lindley distribution as a special case, while improving flexibility via the integration of exponential and gamma distributions. Alternative models, such as the gamma Lindley distribution23 and the quasi-Lindley distribution27, offer additional flexibility for modeling various types of lifespan data.

Enhancing the flexibility of current models via the incorporation of supplementary factors or the amalgamation of distributions has been a pivotal focus of study. Combinations of exponential, gamma, and Lindley distributions have led to significant generalizations, including the compound Lindley–Weibull distribution5, which effectively characterizes various failure rates, encompassing unimodal, decreasing, increasing, and bathtub-shaped patterns. Additional significant instances include the XLindley distribution10, the new XLindley distribution17, the Zeghdoudi distribution20, and the Xgamma distribution26, which further enhance the capacity of statisticians and scientists to model intricate lifetime datasets proficiently. Surveys on generalization approaches, including those by6,7,12,29, provide significant insights into the development of these models.

Recently, there has been a resurgence of interest in the Lindley distribution and its generalizations, driven by its capacity to describe favorably skewed data applicable in dependability, economics, and health sciences. Chesneau et al.9 conducted an extensive investigation into the characteristics and applications of the Lindley distribution, highlighting its significance in contemporary statistical modeling. Tomy and Gillariose30 presented a novel interpretive viewpoint, highlighting the theoretical foundations and flexibility of the Lindley framework. Recently, Gillariose et al.15 presented the extended Slash Modified Lindley distribution, which is notably adept in modeling economic variables that display asymmetry. These studies underscore the ongoing efforts to enhance the adaptability and relevance of the Lindley family. Our proposed new two-parameter distribution (NTPD) advances this study by enhancing shape flexibility, providing closed-form formulations for fundamental functions, and demonstrating superior performance across various datasets and estimation techniques.

Rényi entropy

Entropy describes the level of uncertainty in a random variable’s distribution. The Rényi entropy has many applications in statistics, computer science, and econometrics. A high entropy value indicates a high level of uncertainty. The Rényi entropy is derived here to assess the information-theoretic complexity of the proposed NTPD distribution. As a generalization of the Shannon entropy, the Rényi entropy quantifies the level of uncertainty or dispersion within a distribution, depending on a tunable parameter . This measure is especially useful in various applied fields such as information theory, decision sciences, reliability analysis, and machine learning, where understanding the spread or concentration of data is essential. The availability of a closed-form expression for the Rényi entropy of the NTPD provides deeper insight into the distribution’s structural properties and facilitates entropy-based comparisons with other models. Remark 3

Although our primary focus is on the empirical performance of the estimators through simulation, it is important to note that the MLEs for the parameters and of the NTPD distribution are consistent under standard regularity conditions. Specifically, the log-likelihood function is continuous and differentiable, the parameter space can be assumed compact, and the model is identifiable. As such, classical results from maximum likelihood theory (e.g., Wald’s consistency theorem) guarantee that the MLEs converge in probability to the true parameter values as the sample size increases. This provides a theoretical foundation for the estimators used in our analysis.

Real data evaluation

This section applies the newly developed probability distribution (NTPD) to three real-world datasets from diverse fields, including Environmental Science, Biomedical Sciences, and Reliability Engineering. The performance of the NTPD is compared with seven well-known probability distributions: Lindley Distribution (LD) by19, X-Lindley Distribution (XLD) by10, Modified Lindley Distribution (MLD) by8, SS Lindley Distribution (SSLD) by11, Sine Modified Lindley Distribution (SMLD) by31, Half Logistic Inverse Rayleigh Distribution (HLIRD) by2, and Exponentiated Inverse Rayleigh Distribution (EIRD) by25. To evaluate the goodness of fit, statistical criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Corrected Akaike Information Criterion (CAIC), Hannan-Quinn Information Criterion (HQIC), Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test, Anderson–Darling (AD) test, and Cramér–von Mises (CVM) test, along with their associated p-values, are employed. These metrics will provide a rigorous comparison of model performance and the suitability of each distribution in modeling the datasets.

The distribution with the lowest AIC, BIC, CAIC, HQIC, KS, AD, and CVM values is considered the best fit for model selection. Additionally, the model with the highest p-values for the KS, AD, and CVM tests is deemed the most appropriate for the dataset under consideration. Several generalizations of the Lindley distribution, such as the XLindley, modified Lindley, ss Lindley, and sine modified Lindley distributions, have been introduced to overcome specific limitations of the classical Lindley model. While each contributes unique strengths, many suffer from structural or computational drawbacks, such as the absence of closed-form expressions for quantile or moment-generating functions. Although no single model can universally outperform all others across every dataset or application, comparative studies still provide valuable insights. In this study, the NTPD model demonstrated superior performance over a diverse and representative subset of competitive models, including those widely recognized for their flexibility. This suggests that the NTPD strikes a desirable balance between theoretical tractability and empirical accuracy. In particular, the NTPD not only includes the XLindley as a special case but also extends shape flexibility through its two-parameter structure, allowing both decreasing and unimodal functions. Furthermore, it provides closed-form expressions for key statistical measures, supports multiple classical estimation methods, and consistently yields strong goodness-of-fit results across various real-world datasets. These advantages justify its introduction as a robust and practical addition to the family of lifetime distributions.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced and thoroughly examined the NTPD, a two-parameter model with a tractable mathematical form for its probability density function. This enabled us to derive and analyze several important statistical properties, including moments, entropy, quantile function, incomplete moments, and hazard rate behavior. We employed sixteen distinct parameter estimation techniques and conducted a simulation study to evaluate the consistency and reliability of these estimators. All methods yielded reliable results, with the MPSE proving to be the most robust in terms of bias and MSE performance. The model was then applied to three real-world datasets from environmental science, biomedical research, and reliability engineering, each exhibiting high skewness and peakedness. Across all applications, the NTPD demonstrated remarkable flexibility and a superior fit compared to several well-established models. These findings confirm that the NTPD is a powerful and versatile alternative for modeling complex data. The practical implications of the NTPD are noteworthy. Its closed-form expressions and broad shape adaptability make it especially suitable for applications involving lifetime data, reliability analysis, medical survival times, and risk modeling in finance and insurance. Its ability to accommodate different data patterns, including heavy tails, skewness, and peakedness, enhances its utility across various scientific and engineering domains.

A limitation of this study is that the NTPD is a two-parameter model, which makes its flexibility more restricted than multi-parameter distributions. Future work could explore modifying the distribution to include additional parameters to enhance its flexibility.

Several avenues remain for future research. First, a Bayesian estimation framework could be developed for the NTPD, incorporating prior information and using MCMC techniques for posterior inference. This would enhance inference, especially in small samples or hierarchical modeling contexts. Second, further theoretical exploration, such as proofs of asymptotic normality, analysis of robustness properties, and construction of likelihood-based test statistics, would provide additional inferential tools. Third, extending the NTPD to regression models, multivariate frameworks, or survival models with covariates could significantly broaden its applicability. Finally, broader validation across a wider range of competing models and more diverse datasets is recommended to fully assess the general applicability and performance of the proposed distribution.
