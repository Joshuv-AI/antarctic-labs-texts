Methadone treatment utilization and overdose trends among Medicaid beneficiaries in New Jersey before and during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: James Lloyd, Peter Treitler, Jamey J Lister, Molly Nowels, Stephen Crystal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the traditional mode of methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) delivery through the imposition of lockdowns and social distancing measures. In response, policy makers granted flexibilities to providers delivering MMT to change their practices to maintain patient participation while accommodating the measures imposed to prevent the spread of COVID-19. This study examines the utilization of MMT and overdoses of patients receiving MMT during the COVID-19 pandemic in one mid-Atlantic state.Materials and methods:We analyzed Medicaid claims data for 2018–2020, calculating weekly trends for starts, discontinuations, and medically-treated overdoses for beneficiaries receiving MMT who had been continuously enrolled in Medicaid for the previous 12 months, to account for changes in the composition of the Medicaid population following the COVID-19 public health emergency (PHE). We completed data analyses from January to June 2022.Results:We observed countervailing trends in new starts, which experienced an immediate, non-significant dip of −22.47 per 100,000 Medicaid beneficiaries (95%CI, −50.99 to 6.04) at the outset of the pandemic followed by an increasing upward trend of 1.41 per 100,000 beneficiaries per week (95%CI, 0.37 to 2.46), and in discontinuations, which also experienced an immediate dip of −3.23 per 1000 MMT enrollees (95%CI, −4.49 to −1.97) followed by an increasing upward trend of 0.14 per 1000 MMT enrollees per week (95%CI, 0.09 to 0.19). The net result of these shifts was a stable, slowly increasing rate of MMT treatment of 0.02 % per week before and after the PHE. We also found no statistically significant association of the PHE with medically-treated overdoses among beneficiaries enrolled in MMT (trend change = 0.02 overdoses per 10,000 MMT enrollees, 95%CI, −0.05 to 0.09).Conclusions:New Jersey achieved overall stability in MMT treatment prevalence following the pandemic’s onset, while some changes in treatment dynamics took place. This outcome may reflect that the extensive flexibilities granted to providers of MMT by the state and federal government successfully maintained access to MMT for Medicaid beneficiaries through the pandemic without increasing risk of medically-treated overdose. These findings should inform policy makers developing the post-COVID-19 legal and regulatory landscape.Keywords: COVID-19, Opioid use disorder, Methadone, Overdose, Methadone maintenance treatment, Opioid treatment program, New Jersey, Policy

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted delivery systems for the treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD) (Amram et al., 2022). In response, the federal government (Proclamation No. 9994, 2020) and the State of New Jersey (Executive Order No. 103, 2020) enacted emergency measures granting flexibility to health care providers to allow their patients to continue receiving or engaging in the care they need despite pandemic disruptions. The pandemic and the regulatory changes enacted to address it had a major impact on the delivery of methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) in New Jersey, and this study examines the impact that those changes had on trends in MMT receipt and overdoses among MMT patients during this critical time period. While other modalities of medication for OUD have expanded in the last decade, most notably buprenorphine and naltrexone, this study focuses on MMT given the unique impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on service delivery in opioid treatment programs (OTPs).

MMT is a “gold standard” treatment approach for people with OUD that involves regular dosing of methadone through phases of induction, stabilization, and maintenance, and is effective in reducing opioid overdose and other opioid-related consequences (Wakeman et al., 2020). OTPs administer MMT services in the United States, where patients attend in person, often daily, to receive a dose of methadone alongside psychosocial counseling in group or individual settings and a regimen of drug testing to ensure safe administration of methadone and reduce the likelihood of accidental overdose (Federal Opioid Treatment Standards, 2015). The facilities in which counseling, drug testing, and dosing services are delivered are often crowded – delivery methods that conflicted with infection control guidelines released during the COVID-19 public health emergency (PHE).

Before the PHE, some MMT patients could receive doses of methadone to use at home (Take-homes) based on a strict regulatory scheme and only after a period of sustained drug use abstinence. The strict scheme was designed to address concerns that patients could abuse the medicine by taking it more frequently than intended, or “divert” the medication to people not engaged in MMT, thereby exposing patients and others to risk of methadone overdose. The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) modified federal guidelines limiting Take-homes at the onset of the PHE, granting much greater flexibility to providers in determining the number of Take-homes for which patients are eligible (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2020). On March 24, 2020, the New Jersey Department of Human Services, Division of Mental Health and Addiction Services (DMHAS) issued additional guidance to OTPs with greater detail on practices permitted under SAMHSA’s temporary regulations (e.g., 28-day Take-homes for patients deemed stable) (Division of Mental Health and Addiction Services, 2020).

As a result of the changes in 2020, OTPs implemented a variety of approaches to protect the health of their patients and staff, including providing more Take-homes, reducing the frequency of urine toxicology screens, and incorporating more telehealth (Treitler et al., 2022). As COVID-19 moves from pandemic to endemic and the need for social distancing wanes, policymakers must still answer questions about the extent to which temporary flexibilities imposed during the public health emergency ought to be extended indefinitely. Proposed rules from SAMHSA would extend several aspects of the COVID-19 flexibilities, but the proposed Final Rule leaves states the option to impose stricter limits and grants individual providers at OTPs discretion to determine the stability of patients for Take home dosing (Medications for the Treatment of Opioid Use Disorder, n.d.). Prior research suggests potential benefits of the regulatory flexibilities (beyond social distancing during the pandemic), including improved retention (Gomes et al., 2022; Hoffman et al., 2022), fewer barriers to care, and greater patient satisfaction (Saloner et al., 2022). Although studies are limited to this point, they have found mixed evidence of increases in harms such as overdoses or acute care utilization (Amram et al., 2022; Brothers et al., 2021; Jones et al., 2022; Kaufman et al., 2023; Kleinman & Sanches, 2023). Despite the potential benefits of more flexible approaches to care, some potential downsides of practice changes (e.g., additional Take-homes, increased use of telehealth) have been noted, such as difficulty building rapport, concerns over diversion, difficulty of monitoring patients, and reduced clinical contacts (Goldsamt et al., 2021; Saloner et al., 2022; Treitler et al., 2022).

The aim of this study was to assess changes in MMT utilization and overdose trends in the NJ Medicaid population before and during the pandemic. Findings have the potential to inform programmatic and service decisions made by treatment authorities and individual MOUD providers. The results here reflect the impact of regulatory changes that are being contemplated by SAMHSA as part of permanent changes to 42C.F.R. Part 8, and will provide critical evidence for policymakers implementing state regulations for MMT and drafting communications to OTPs when that Final Rule is issued (Medications for the Treatment of Opioid Use Disorder, n.d.). The evidence can also inform practice at OTPs and for individual providers, who under the proposed regulations are tasked with determining whether patients are stable enough to receive larger amounts of Take homes than under the previous regulations.

Materials and methods

Data and sample

These analyses used New Jersey Medicaid claims data from 2018 to 2020, which include beneficiary enrollment and demographic characteristics, services received, and diagnoses. The Rutgers Biomedical and Health Sciences Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the study protocol (Protocol: Pro2020000641). The study waived informed consent because the analysis used only deidentified secondary data initially collected for non-research purposes. The study completed data analyses from January to June 2022.

The study cohort’s inclusion criteria included 1) all beneficiaries with continuous Medicaid enrollment and no dual Medicare enrollment during the current month and prior 12 months, and 2) at least one OUD diagnosis during this period, determined by ICD-10 code F11. For the first criterion, we required continuous Medicaid and no Medicare enrollment for 12 prior months to limit the cohort to individuals enrolled in Medicaid before COVID-19-related changes in eligibility and recertification requirements went into effect. For the second criterion, we required an OUD diagnosis to limit the sample to individuals eligible for MMT.

These analyses aggregated data to the weekly level and calculated trends with weekly start dates from 1/1/2018 to 12/21/2020. Analyses defined the PHE start date as 3/16/2020, consistent with SAMHSA and NJ guidelines for OTPs issued on that date (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2020; Division of Mental Health and Addiction Services, 2020). Except as noted below, the pre-PHE period ranged from 1/1/2018 to 3/15/2020, and the post-PHE period from 3/16/2020 to 12/27/2020.

Outcome measures

The research team performed interrupted time series analyses to examine the association between the COVID-19 PHE and MMT utilization and overdose among MMT enrollees.

This study’s analyses use 4 variables as outcome measures: 1) MMT receipt, 2) MMT new starts, 3) MMT discontinuation, and 4) medically-treated opioid overdoses. Individuals were positive for MMT receipt if they had methadone supply on at least one day during the week, identified using HCPCS codes H0020, Z2006, and Z3356. For weekly bundled OTP services (billed using code H0020HF26), individuals were coded as having methadone supply on the date of the claim and the following 6 days. For all other codes (i.e., non-bundled services for methadone dispensed per diem), individuals were coded as having methadone supply for the same number of days as units of service on the claim, starting with the claim date. For example, if a patient had a claim using HCPCS code H0020 with 5 units of service on 1/1/2018, the study coded them as having methadone supply from 1/1/2018–1/5/2018. For the MMT receipt outcome, the analysis calculated the weekly percentage of Medicaid beneficiaries with methadone supply among all beneficiaries meeting eligibility criteria described above (i.e., continuous, non-dual Medicaid eligibility; OUD diagnosis). Using the same denominator, this study calculated the MMT new starts outcome as the number of individuals per 100,000 Medicaid enrollees with OUD who started an MMT episode of care in the week, defined as having new methadone supply after 30 or more days without methadone supply. The study calculated new starts in weeks from 2/5/2018 to 12/21/2020, to allow a 30-day lookback period for no methadone supply and to align the 7-day time periods with the PHE declaration on 3/16/2020. The analysis assessed the MMT discontinuation outcome by calculating the number of individuals who discontinued treatment per 1000 individuals with any MMT receipt during the week. In addition to the above enrollment criteria, individuals included in discontinuation analyses were required to be enrolled in Medicaid but not Medicare in the month following the month in which discontinuation occurred to ensure there was not a new claim for methadone in the following 30 days. The date of discontinuation was the last day of methadone supply before 30 or more days without supply. Individuals who received buprenorphine within 30 days following MMT discontinuation were excluded, as these represent medication transitions rather than treatment discontinuation. The analysis calculated discontinuations in weeks starting from 1/1/2018 to 11/23/20 to allow 30 days follow-up time to identify individuals without methadone supply.

Finally, to assess changes in overdose risk among individuals enrolled in MMT, we calculated the weekly number of medically-treated opioid overdoses per 10,000 active MMT enrollees. Overdoses were identified using ICD-10 codes for opioid poisoning T40.0, T40.1, T40.2, T40.3, and T40.6, excluding those with 6th character of “5”, which represent adverse effects due to opioids (Green et al., 2017). Overdoses were included only if they occurred on a day when the individual had methadone supply (see specifications above for determining days’ supply) and were recorded in the Medicaid claims data used in this study; we did not capture overdoses not resulting in Medicaid claims. Units of analysis used in calculating rates are based on their frequencies and differ across outcomes to facilitate interpretation. For all outcomes, individuals could be included in the study for more than one treatment episode (e.g., the same individual could experience more than one start or discontinuation provided they met outcome criteria each time).

Statistical analysis

This paper presents interrupted time series analyses using segmented regression and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models to account for seasonal and non-seasonal trends in the data. We used the auto.arima function from the R package forecast (Hyndman et al., 2023) to select the best-fitting ARIMA model, and ruled out remaining autocorrelation and seasonality by performing Ljung-Box tests and examining autocorrelation function and partial autocorrelation function plots (Beard et al., 2019). Models included a constant, a slope term to account for secular pre-PHE trends, and terms to estimate a change in level and a change in slope following PHE declaration, as well as any autoregressive or moving average effects selected by the auto. arima() function. The analyses were conducted using R statistical software (The R Foundation, n.d.).

Of these, 17,925 (23.2 % Black, 10.3 % Hispanic, 60.2 % White, 48.4 % female, 55.5 % aged 25–44) received MMT for at least one day (Table 1). Discussion

The disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic dramatically impacted the delivery of MMT; overall MMT enrollment, however, remained stable in NJ. The initial drop in starts (−22.47 per 100,000 Medicaid enrollees), though not statistically significant, suggests that new MMT patients were not unlike the rest of the population in reducing their utilization of healthcare as the PHE began (Mehrotra et al., 2021). The drop occurred during the early stages when the threat of the pandemic was acute, particularly in light of the fact that New Jersey was a hot spot early in the pandemic, and slowly built back up over the course of the year. Other research has suggested that some OTPs suspended starts during the PHE (Joudrey et al., 2021), but earlier research also suggests that MOUD providers in New Jersey initially moved aggressively to protect their patients and staff and gradually removed some restrictions as the summer of 2020 brought with it lower COVID-19 case and fatality rates (Treitler et al., 2022).

Notably, discontinuations followed a similar trajectory to starts, with an initial decrease followed by a climb back to pre-PHE trends. As patients stopped attending OTPs in person, the analyses found fewer discontinuations, suggesting that the OTPs were still delivering medication to their patients. This could be the result of several factors. First, the expansion in the number of Take-home doses most patients received and increased use of telehealth may have kept them from missing appointments that they otherwise would have without the flexibility (Treitler et al., 2022). Second, a reduction in urine toxicology screening of MMT patients may have created fewer prompts for OTPs to discontinue services with patients with continued drug use that raised safety concerns. Third, if higher-risk patients were less likely to pursue care during the PHE, the remaining population of patients would be less likely to discontinue. Some research has shown that MMT patients had positive experiences with using the flexibilities (Hoffman et al., 2022), and some providers have stated that patients’ utilization of services was made easier by the flexibilities (Madden et al., 2021). Other studies have similarly shown that COVID-era flexibilities may have reduced treatment dropout and improved retention (Medications for the Treatment of Opioid Use Disorder, n.d.; Gomes et al., 2022).

These analyses found that overall trends in weekly methadone utilization rates were unchanged before and after the PHE, which may reflect efforts by regulators and providers in New Jersey to maintain access despite the disruptions (Saloner et al., 2022; Treitler et al., 2022), including strong regulatory support in fully implementing flexibilities in Take-homes permitted by federal regulatory flexibilities (Division of Mental Health and Addiction Services, 2020), and a willingness among OTPs to implement these flexibilities (Treitler et al., 2022). Although new MMT starts briefly declined during the early months of the pandemic, this was offset by fewer discontinuations, resulting in stable enrollment during this period. These findings contrast with those of a Wisconsin study that found larger disruptions among Medicaid beneficiaries in MMT associated with the pandemic, with services receipt decreasing substantially early in the pandemic, followed by a partial recovery (Tilhou et al., 2022). It is important to note, however, that measurement of services receipt differed in this study, and may have underestimated services if patients were issued more Take-home doses. Additional explanations for these disparate findings may include differences in the guidance issued by the respective states to OTPs or variations in the degree to which flexibilities were implemented by OTPs in each state.

The relative stability of rates for overdose found in the Medicaid claims data (no significant trend pre- to post-PHE) also potentially speaks to the safety of New Jersey’s efforts to maintain patients’ engagement in MMT through use of waivers offered by SAMHSA during the PHE. New Jersey was one of a handful of states where overdose rates remained relatively stable during 2020 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, National Center for Health Statistics, n.d.), and the trends reflected in medically-treated overdoses among MMT patients are no different. The lack of a significant change in medically-treated overdose rates suggests that the additional flexibilities granted to MMT patients by OTPs (more Take-homes, reduced urine toxicology screens) does not appear to have contributed to increased overdose rates. These findings are consistent with those of other studies, which show no increased risk among MMT patients issued more Take-homes during the PHE. Gomes et al., 2022, examined the impact of increasing numbers of Take-homes in Ontario, Canada during the pandemic and found that the risk of discontinuation and treatment interruption were lower for individuals who received more Take-homes during the early phases of the PHE, and found lower rates of opioid-related overdose for those who were previously receiving daily in-person doses. Welsh et al., 2022, compiled data from the U.S.’s national poison data system and found a small increase in methadone exposures during the first year of the PHE, but no increase in the severity of the exposures. Amram et al., 2022, examined outcomes from a single OTP that implemented increased Take-home doses, and also saw no increase in numbers of overdoses during the PHE. Other studies have found an increase in methadone-related overdose deaths during the first year of the pandemic, but those studies included OUD patients not in MMT and were national in scope, including patients across the United States, which experienced a general increase in overdoses higher than the relatively flat rate seen in New Jersey during 2020 (Kaufman et al., 2023; Kleinman & Sanches, 2023). New Jersey’s successful experience in maintaining access to MMT during acute COVID-19 disruptions, in an early COVID-19 epicenter where pandemic disruptions were especially severe, has major implications for future policy to support delivery of MOUD. The flexibilities introduced may have helped avoid an increase in MMT discontinuations despite shutdowns and other restrictions imposed as part of the PHE, successfully maintaining access to MMT and retention of patients in MMT. These findings support consideration of permanent extension of federal flexibilities in Take-homes, urine drug screening, and other MMT policies, supporting modification of a high-threshold, highly supervised model that has maintained these strict requirements for patient surveillance for multiple decades. Additionally, the lack of association between the regulatory flexibilities and any increase in medically-treated overdoses supports the idea that flexibilities introduced did not increase risk of overdose for patients engaged in MMT, and suggest that flexibilities can be continued after the PHE without increasing overdose risk to patients. Many scholars and practitioners have criticized the strict regulatory regime governing the delivery of MMT (Joudrey et al., 2020), and these results provide further evidence on the potential impact of changing those policies, with high relevance for policy decisions on how to retain flexibilities post-PHE (Lister & Lister, 2021).

This analysis provides evidence on MMT enrollment, new starts, discontinuations, and methadone overdoses during the initial stages of a pandemic that turned out to span multiple years and may have a lasting impact on OUD service delivery. Further research is needed to track changes both in clinic policies and in enrollment dynamics and recovery outcomes over time. Leveraging the heterogeneity across OTPs within and across states in the extent to which flexibilities were maintained following the acute initial phases of the pandemic (Andraka-Christou et al., 2021; Cantor & Laurito, 2021) will allow for extension of these natural experiments to understand how OTP practice impacts patients. Valuable research could be done examining how utilization and overdoses changed as the PHE evolved (e.g., states lifting of stay-at-home orders, the introduction of COVID-19 vaccines, subsequent waves of COVID-19 variants), and how these trends change in the years beyond the end of the PHE. Potential shifts in the medications used for OUD treatment (buprenorphine and naltrexone, notably) should also be examined during the pandemic period, given the different delivery modalities involved with those medications. The substantial variation between OTPs with respect to how they implemented the flexibilities (Figgatt et al., 2021; Mehrotra et al., 2021; Treitler et al., 2022), suggests that extending this analysis to a clinic level, along with collecting data on how individual clinics handled the flexibilities, would allow us to see even more clearly how the policies impacted outcomes. And better understanding the experiences of patients by including patient voice and perspective in research is critical to inform policymakers and practitioners as they implement the post-PHE delivery system.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although New Jersey is a state with a large, diverse population, this single-state study was not able to compare patterns with other states. Future research should examine how our trends compare to states with similar demographic, drug use, and MMT supply, who adopted COVID-19 adaptations differently. The study further looked only at those Medicaid enrollees with enrollment before the PHE and does not include the large numbers of people who enrolled in Medicaid during the PHE. These analyses also could not investigate the extent to which telehealth was used by providers to facilitate the delivery of MMT, whether through online or telephonic counseling or supervised dosing of Take-homes, due to the limitations of New Jersey claims data, which during the PHE did not include a flag indicating the use of telehealth. Nor could these analyses directly measure utilization of Take-homes; future research with the ability to monitor Take-home utilization would provide stronger evidence for the impact of regulatory flexibilities. This analysis assumes that patients with claims for weekly bundled methadone received 7 days’ supply, but it is possible that patients did not receive medication on each day. Identifying individuals with OUD using ICD-10 codes is also a limitation, as those without a formal diagnosis cannot be identified. Similarly, we could only identify overdoses that generated Medicaid claims, so overdoses recorded in other datasets or that were not medically-treated are not included in our analysis. The impact of the PHE and the flexibilities implemented with it are confounded by the COVID-19 pandemic itself, both in terms of the health effects of individuals who contracted the virus, and changing patterns of people’s behavior outside of the policy restrictions in response to the threat of COVID-19. Last, this study was not powered to detect subpopulation patterns based on the geographic place of treatment (e.g., rural-urban differences), by race, or by sex. Future research should examine whether the patterns we identified vary by subpopulation.

Conclusions

This study documents that continuity of MMT treatment in New Jersey was successfully maintained despite pandemic disruptions. The initial decrease in new starts was offset by the decrease in discontinuations, and overall levels of MMT receipt remained stable throughout the study period. Moreover, there was no association between the PHE and increased medically-treated overdoses among people enrolled in MMT. Policy makers are now engaged in defining the future of MOUD delivery, and the PHE has created a natural experiment where major changes were implemented in how MOUDs were delivered to patients. This study’s findings add to growing research suggesting that permanent extension of pandemic-era regulatory changes is warranted, and can improve treatment engagement without increasing risk of adverse patient outcomes.
