How does personal utility depend on clinical setting? Evidence from 3 cohorts

ABSTRACT
PurposeEvidence of personal utility of genetic testing is critical to clinical care, funding, and policy decisions. We aimed to understand how patient-oriented values and preferences for genetic testing vary across clinical settings to inform the development of a personal utility index.MethodsParticipants were recruited from 3 clinical settings: pediatric clinical genetics, pediatric oncology, and prenatal care. For each cohort, a preliminary set of domains and elements of utility were generated from the literature. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with parents to understand the meaning of personal utility and relevance of preliminary domains and elements. Deductive coding identified shared and unique elements of utility across cohorts.ResultsA total of 63 parents were interviewed. Personal utility domains that resonated with participants included cognitive, medical management, affective, behavioral, and social. Common elements included increased understanding about the cause of their child’s condition and contributing to scientific knowledge. Unique elements were identified in each cohort: identifying support services in clinical genetics, understanding cancer risks in oncology, and pregnancy decision making in prenatal care.ConclusionWe identified shared and unique elements of personal utility across cohorts from 3 clinical settings, suggesting the need to tailor utility assessment to the clinical setting and patient population.

Introduction

Genetic testing is playing an increasingly critical role in medicine, enabling definitive diagnosis, identifying health risks, and tailoring management plans.1, 2, 3 Apart from these elements of clinical utility, which can be ascertained from medical records4, 5, 6 or clinicians’ perspectives,7,8 genetic test results have significant informational value for patients and families.9 A standardized approach to ascertaining the patient and family perspective is essential to patient-centered care, and important to policymakers and payors when deciding when and for whom emerging technologies should be available.10,11

Personal utility refers to how patients subjectively orient to the value of genetic information (ie, positively or negatively) and encompasses both health and non-health-related outcomes.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 The construct of personal utility was originally defined by Kohler et al17 to include 4 domains of utility: cognitive, affective, behavioral, and social. Building on this work, the construct has since been expanded to include medical management as a fifth domain, representing the value of clinician-directed activities from the patient perspective.14,15,18

Although personal utility has been broadly defined, the degree to which domains and elements of utility are applicable across a range of clinical settings is unclear. For instance, prenatal genetic testing may help to inform pregnancy-related decision making,19,20 whereas in oncology, somatic and germline testing may allow for targeted treatment options or identify familial cancer risks.21, 22, 23 Because of the breadth of clinical indications for genetic testing, setting-specific approaches to defining and measuring personal utility may be warranted. The purpose of this study was to better understand which domains and elements of personal utility appear to be commonly valued, and which may be unique to distinct clinical settings. Our findings contribute to fine-tuning a definition and measurement strategy for personal utility across the range of clinical settings to which genetic and genomic technologies apply.

Materials and Methods

In collaboration with a parent advisory committee (PAC) comprising 8 parents with lived experience, we report herein on a descriptive qualitative interview study following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist,24 where applicable. Informed consent was obtained locally before study participation.

Sample and recruitment

Eligible participants included (1) parents and caregivers of children (<18 years) who had genetic testing through pediatric clinical genetics for a range of indications (eg, intellectual disability, hearing loss, and epilepsy), (2) parents and caregivers of children (<18 years) who received cancer-related germline and/or tumor genetic testing, and (3) prospective parents who received prenatal genetic testing, triggered by a positive serum screen, ultrasound finding, or known family history. In all cohorts, individuals were invited to participate if they received genetic test results (ie, diagnostic results, non-diagnostic results, or variants of uncertain significance) in the previous 12 months. Participants were excluded if their preferred language was not English, and interpreter services were unavailable. Participants were identified through medical chart review by members of the study team. Consecutive sampling of eligible participants occurred at 5 clinical sites in Ontario, Canada and 1 site in British Columbia, Canada. Once a member of the care team verified that it was appropriate to invite an eligible participant (ie, no extenuating or distressing circumstance known), participants were notified of their eligibility for the study by a member of their care team and consented locally. Once consented, potential participants were emailed to schedule the interview, with up to 3 reminder emails sent over a 6-week period.

Data collection

One-hour semi-structured interviews were conducted between February 2022 and June 2023. Interviews were conducted by experienced qualitative researchers and individuals trained in genetic counseling and/or medical genomics. No prior relationship existed between the interviewers and participants. Interviews took place over Microsoft Teams and were audio recorded. To inform the interview guide, we drew upon work related to defining and measuring the construct of personal utility,13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 literature relevant to the utility of genetic testing in each clinical cohort,19, 20, 21, 22, 23 and the lived experience of our PAC. The first section of the interview guide included open-ended questions about participants’ motivations for and experiences with genetic testing. As is typical in the early stages of measurement development wherein the construct of interest warrants input from the intended users of the measure,25 the second section included questions about the relevance and comprehensibility of 5 domains of utility (ie, cognitive, medical management, affective, behavioral, and social)14,15 and specific elements of utility within each domain. All domains were explored across the 3 cohorts. Some elements were common across the 3 cohorts, and some were specific to each setting. For example, we asked participants from the prenatal cohort to reflect upon how the results may have affected their pregnancy decision making, and we asked participants from the oncology cohort to reflect upon their understanding of cancer risks. The interview guide was reviewed by the study team and PAC to ensure relevance and comprehensiveness (Supplemental Information 1).

Demographics of the parent participants and clinical medical history of the index case were collected at the end of each interview. Interviews were conducted until saturation was reached for each clinical cohort, as determined by cohort-specific analyses.26

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and coded using NVivo 12.7.0. A deductive, domain-based coding structure was developed by the study team and was applied to all transcripts. Coding began with data generated from section 2 of the interview. For each cohort, participant feedback on the meaning and relevance of each domain-specific element of utility derived from the literature,15,17,19, 20, 21, 22, 23 was coded. Section 1 data were then reviewed to capture content that would assist the team’s understanding of the meaning and relevance of each element coded in section 2. For example, a participant’s motivation to pursue genetic testing to inform a pregnancy-related decision (section 1) and their explanation that their prenatal test result guided decision making (section 2) were grouped, underscoring the relevance of this element of utility. If, however, a motivation for testing was not realized, the motivation itself was not considered to provide support for a specific element. Coding discrepancies were resolved through team discussion. After coding, data were compared across cohorts to identify similarities and differences in relevant elements of utility. From this comparative analysis, we identified elements that were core, or generalizable to the construct of personal utility, and elements that were unique to specific clinical settings. Elements of utility were determined to be core if they were endorsed as relevant by a majority of participants from all 3 cohorts. Elements of utility were considered setting specific, if (1) the element was endorsed as relevant by a majority of participants from one specific cohort, and (2) the element was identified to be of limited or no relevance, in other cohorts.

Cognitive

Participants across all cohorts expressed that gaining knowledge about their child’s health concerns was a primary motivator for pursuing genetic testing. In particular, understanding the cause of the condition was a valued outcome.“And then this kind of deep dive genetic test actually gave us all the answers on what exactly is causing the [condition]…a specific gene that is responsible for that was just recently identified in a very small number of people worldwide. So it was extremely helpful, because now we know exactly what’s going on.”—CG008, clinical genetics, diagnostic.

“And then this kind of deep dive genetic test actually gave us all the answers on what exactly is causing the [condition]…a specific gene that is responsible for that was just recently identified in a very small number of people worldwide. So it was extremely helpful, because now we know exactly what’s going on.”—CG008, clinical genetics, diagnostic.

Some participants who received non-diagnostic results explained that the results were still useful because they helped them to understand that the condition may not be genetic:“The tests came back that there was no genetic link or predisposition, which was helpful to my husband and I. And the second test where it looked at over 800 genes, something did come up, wasn’t cancer related. And we thought OK, that’s good to know…[child’s cancer] was one of those fluky environmental things.”—ON006, pediatric oncology, non-diagnostic.

And we thought OK, that’s good to know…[child’s cancer] was one of those fluky environmental things.”—ON006, pediatric oncology, non-diagnostic.

In addition to understanding causation, participants valued learning about the inheritance and the possibility of ruling out previously suspected conditions.

Medical management

Elements of utility such as referrals to specialists and accessing additional medical tests were pertinent to participants across cohorts. Specifically, access to additional or updated genetic testing for their child, themselves, or other family members was also seen as beneficial by participants:“It will likely lead to further genetic testing for other family members. Specifically, my sister…I’m sure that would be something that she will also pursue if she has a second pregnancy.”—PR014, prenatal, non-diagnostic.

“It will likely lead to further genetic testing for other family members. Specifically, my sister…I’m sure that would be something that she will also pursue if she has a second pregnancy.”—PR014, prenatal, non-diagnostic.

For some, receiving a negative result helped them to access advanced genetic testing that may have previously been unavailable:“We just recently got called back…and gave a new blood sample for testing called…long-read sequencing, it reads the DNA in longer sections, and it can allow us to detect changes that would have been missed in previous testing.”—CG015, clinical genetics, non-diagnostic.

Affective

In the affective domain, participants across cohorts expressed a mix of emotions after receiving their genetic test results. Positive emotions were more frequently described than negative emotions. Many participants described the results as providing a sense of relief, peace of mind, hope for the future, and empowerment:“I think that I feel stronger to be able to talk about it now and bring awareness for others. Participating in this study, even though it’s really difficult, I feel empowered to do it and to share, because I know it’s important for others.”—PR001, prenatal, diagnostic.

“I think that I feel stronger to be able to talk about it now and bring awareness for others. Participating in this study, even though it’s really difficult, I feel empowered to do it and to share, because I know it’s important for others.”—PR001, prenatal, diagnostic.

However, feelings of worry and uncertainty were expressed by many participants, especially among those who received negative or uncertain results. The emotional burden that testing had on their mental health was also commonly described, particularly among participants in the clinical genetics and prenatal cohorts. Although these feelings were described in relation to the test results received, participants’ reflections on their experience with testing suggest that these feelings were also germane to navigating their child’s needs in general. For example:“There’s been a lot of anxiety since she was diagnosed. Some days, the testing results are useful for helping us to sort of look, dispassionately, at the situation. Some days, overall, I think it’s too much.”—ON011, pediatric oncology, diagnostic.

“There’s been a lot of anxiety since she was diagnosed. Some days, the testing results are useful for helping us to sort of look, dispassionately, at the situation. Some days, overall, I think it’s too much.”—ON011, pediatric oncology, diagnostic.

Behavioral

Within the behavioral domain, discussion surrounding the utility of genetic testing primarily focused on 2 elements: family planning and communication. Participants across cohorts noted that their genetic test results were useful when deciding if and how they wanted to have more children:“If we hadn’t had these results then we would probably have been pregnant by now with natural conception. So now, we don’t want to do that, so it’s changed completely.”—ON014, pediatric oncology, diagnostic.

“If we hadn’t had these results then we would probably have been pregnant by now with natural conception. So now, we don’t want to do that, so it’s changed completely.”—ON014, pediatric oncology, diagnostic.

Information from the genetic test results was also found to be helpful to extended family members contemplating their own family planning:“My brother… He’s trying to have a baby now and may think more because they’re under 40…I don’t know if the risk is in our genes, but they’re cautious now.”—PR002, prenatal, diagnostic.

“My brother… He’s trying to have a baby now and may think more because they’re under 40…I don’t know if the risk is in our genes, but they’re cautious now.”—PR002, prenatal, diagnostic.

Participants also found that the results helped them to have more targeted conversations with health care providers, and with family and friends about their child’s or their own health concerns and needs.

Social

Across cohorts, participants described that genetic testing was valuable in helping them feel connected to and supported by their friends, family, and community. Specifically, many participants described connecting with an online community, support group, or engaging with others who have similar lived experiences:“I found one person… She has a child with the same syndrome as my little one. I found it on the Facebook group, but we have lots of people in [countries]. So we’ve been connecting online and we are making a group of discussion about it, just to help each other. I think this is so important to have someone talk to.”—CG011, clinical genetics, diagnostic.“I got to talk to another mother that went through something that was the same that I went through… You get to connect on a different level, and it really helps you talk it through and understand what’s going on. Because at the end of the day, a lot of women, when they get a diagnosis of something’s wrong with their child, and they need to make a decision, basically a life-altering decision, obviously it’s difficult, but it’s good to know that you’re not alone. Because a lot of women actually do feel very alone.”—PR020, prenatal, diagnostic.

Additionally, participants were generally altruistic and saw value in contributing to scientific knowledge by having undergone genetic testing, regardless of result type or whether testing was done in a clinical or research setting.

Unique elements of utility

Unique elements of utility, by clinical cohort, are described below (Figure 1, Supplemental Information 3). Within the clinical genetics cohort, many participants described their child’s diagnostic odyssey and how their focus has shifted to long-term planning for their child’s needs. In pediatric oncology, participants were more focused on understanding cancer recurrence risk and ensuring their child was receiving the best medical care. In prenatal, participants described the urgency surrounding decision making and the immediate impact of genetic testing on their pregnancy.

Clinical genetics

Within the clinical genetics cohort, we identified that unique elements of utility primarily focused on ideas related to planning for the future and locating specific community or government-based support services for their child. For example:“For me, knowing is allowing us to prepare…otherwise, we would have been like, oh, there’s a speech delay at a year and a half and trying to get services. Now we know that there’s a high chance that there could be, it’s better that we’re in the process of getting everything [in place].”—CG006, clinical genetics, diagnostic.

“For me, knowing is allowing us to prepare…otherwise, we would have been like, oh, there’s a speech delay at a year and a half and trying to get services. Now we know that there’s a high chance that there could be, it’s better that we’re in the process of getting everything [in place].”—CG006, clinical genetics, diagnostic.

Although imminent access to supports was highly valued, parents in the clinical genetics cohort also described the importance of learning prognostic information that could help guide planning for their child’s needs later in life, such as educational supports and future living arrangements.

Pediatric oncology

Within pediatric oncology, unique elements of utility were most frequently identified in the cognitive and medical management domains. Understanding the risk for cancer in the family was a prominent element in the cognitive domain. One parent explained that receiving a non-diagnostic germline result clarified cancer recurrence risk for their affected child and the risk for cancer in their other children:“It was reassuring to know that…the chance of him getting anything like this, again, is minimal… It was also very reassuring to know that my other two children aren’t at an increased risk, they have the same risk as any other kids.”—ON005, pediatric oncology, non-diagnostic.

“It was reassuring to know that…the chance of him getting anything like this, again, is minimal… It was also very reassuring to know that my other two children aren’t at an increased risk, they have the same risk as any other kids.”—ON005, pediatric oncology, non-diagnostic.

Additionally, another parent whose child received a diagnostic germline result described the importance of learning about cancer risk for other family members:“So yeah, just another piece of news that they [husband's family] had to receive throughout the whole diagnosis, like oh it’s not just random, it’s actually affecting us completely genetically, especially for my husband. His family and my family now, has to worry about him more as well, because he’s susceptible to other cancers.”—ON014, pediatric oncology, diagnostic.

“So yeah, just another piece of news that they [husband's family] had to receive throughout the whole diagnosis, like oh it’s not just random, it’s actually affecting us completely genetically, especially for my husband. His family and my family now, has to worry about him more as well, because he’s susceptible to other cancers.”—ON014, pediatric oncology, diagnostic.

In the medical management domain, some participants found that the genetic test results enabled or altered surveillance and monitoring plans for their child. In some instances, family members were advised to begin routine cancer screening earlier in life than recommended by population guidelines. Lifestyle modifications resulting from the genetic test, such as reduced sun exposure, were also discussed by parents in this cohort.

Prenatal

In prenatal, unique elements of utility were most frequently observed in the behavioral and affective domains. Participants discussed that their test results helped them to decide when, with whom, and how much information about their pregnancy they wanted to share. However, informing decision making about their current pregnancy was the most prominent element in the behavioral domain. Although pregnancy decision making could be broadly encompassed by the core element of family planning, our participants focused on the unique value of having results available to support their immediate decision making. One participant described that their results helped to reaffirm their decision to interrupt their pregnancy:“I felt that it gave me closure in my termination and we can make a decision faster, so that was also more beneficial to my health and my mental wellness.”—PR003, prenatal, diagnostic.

“I felt that it gave me closure in my termination and we can make a decision faster, so that was also more beneficial to my health and my mental wellness.”—PR003, prenatal, diagnostic.

For another participant, their genetic test results helped them decide to continue their pregnancy:“I think when we found out that my husband had the same gene mutation, that completely changed things for us, because we were like, ‘Well this baby deserves the same chance.’ I never would have interrupted the pregnancy unless I knew a lot of supporting evidence that something was very wrong.”—PR004, prenatal, variant of uncertain significance.

“I think when we found out that my husband had the same gene mutation, that completely changed things for us, because we were like, ‘Well this baby deserves the same chance.’ I never would have interrupted the pregnancy unless I knew a lot of supporting evidence that something was very wrong.”—PR004, prenatal, variant of uncertain significance.

Within the affective domain, participants who decided to continue their pregnancies felt that they could bond with their baby more after receiving their genetic test results. Moreover, many also felt they could experience their pregnancy more fully after receiving their results.

Discussion

We sought to understand how personal utility manifests across a range of clinical settings, both positively and negatively. Within the 3 clinical settings explored, all domains of personal utility resonated with participants. We identified core elements of utility within each domain that spanned clinical settings, including elements such as feeling empowered, understanding the cause of condition, and guiding communication. We also identified unique elements of utility within each cohort, demonstrating that some aspects of personal utility may be setting specific. Additionally, we identified elements of utility that were neither core nor fully unique, such as sense of closure and hope for new tests or treatments. In situations in which elements of utility are shared across some but not all clinical settings, we speculate that other contextual factors may be more aligned in some settings than others, such as motivations for testing and life stage. For example, motivations to end the diagnostic odyssey for participants in the clinical genetics cohort and to explain prenatal findings may have contributed to feeling a sense of closure. Conversely, motivations in the oncology cohort were more frequently aligned with seeking therapeutic opportunities. Further exploration of these elements may help to provide a better understanding of when and for whom these elements of utility matter. Informed by the lived experience of participants from 3 clinical settings, we have further refined elements of utility previously reported in the literature.15

Several measures of personal utility have emerged in recent years.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 The Personal Utility (PrU) measure for genomic results consists of 3 factors: self-knowledge, reproductive planning, and practical benefits.28 Although originally developed as a measure for adults, Turbitt et al29 have since adapted the PrU for pediatric indications. The GENEtic Utility (GENE-U) scale, a measure for pediatric genomic sequencing, consists of 2 subscales: Informational Utility and Emotional Utility.30 A second version of the GENE-U has been developed for adult screening populations.31 The emergence of the PrU and GENE-U highlights the demand for measures of personal utility for genetic testing, in both pediatric and adult populations. These tools were developed with participants from large sequencing projects, in which a broad range of indications for testing were included. We recognize that this is essential when developing a tool intended to have broad applicability. When considering the factors and subscales of the PrU and GENE-U, we see strong overlap between them and the 5 domains of personal utility considered in our study. The PrU self-knowledge factor largely encompasses the cognitive and affective domains, the reproductive planning factor mirrors the family planning aspects of the behavioral domain, and the practical benefits factor encompasses components of the behavioral and social domains. Notably, and aligned with the conceptual work that guided the development of the PrU, the medical management domain is absent from this tool.17 With respect to the GENE-U, the Information Utility subscale largely encompasses the cognitive, medical management, behavioral, and social domains, whereas the Emotional Utility subscale overlaps with the affective domain. Additionally, both the PrU and GENE-U contain many items that overlap with the core elements of utility we have identified, such as gaining a better understanding of the condition and contributing to scientific knowledge.

Unique setting-specific elements of utility identified herein may not be reflected in tools designed to be broadly applicable. For example, including items about pregnancy decision making (ie, termination or continuation) would not be relevant in nonprenatal settings. Similarly, understanding future cancer risk may be inappropriate to ask in situations in which the indication for testing is unrelated to cancer. As such, a tool that is intended for use across a range of clinical settings may not be sensitive to the nuanced value of testing in distinct clinical settings. To our knowledge, only 1 tool has emerged that has taken a setting-specific approach to personal utility. Specifically, Hamilton et al32 developed a measure of perceived utility of secondary germline findings in cancer patients who received tumor sequencing. The measure captures many core elements of utility and includes setting-specific items of utility, such as, My tumor genomic profiling can help my doctor treat my cancer.

This study indicates that measuring the personal utility of genetic testing may include core elements common to disparate settings and also require setting-specific elements. In other areas of measurement science, it is not uncommon to develop population-specific measures of a construct to accompany a generic version of a tool.33,34 For example, the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory is a well-established measure of health-related quality of life for children and adolescents.34, 35, 36 The Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory Generic Core Scale can be used alone or in combination with disease-specific modules to achieve greater measurement sensitivity.37 We believe that this approach could be applied to personal utility, for which a core scale would allow for comparison across clinical populations, and setting-specific adaptations could increase tool relevance and sensitivity. While existing personal utility measures for genomic medicine have led the charge on much needed patient-reported outcome measures in genomics,28, 29, 30, 31, 32 our work sets the stage for a comprehensive measurement system for personal utility that is sensitive to unique aspects of genetic testing in a range of clinical settings. Our ongoing work aims to develop a personal utility measure, called the Patient-reported Genetic testing Utility InDEX, which will consist of core items, applicable across clinical contexts, and unique items that are setting specific.

Strengths of this study included the diversity of our team and the contribution of our PAC to the design and implementation of the study. Additionally, we recruited from multiple sites across Canada, which provided a broad range of perspectives and experiences. Finally, similar to the GENE-U, we asked participants to consider both the utility and disutility of genetic testing across domains and similar to both the GENE-U and PrU, will reflect the bidirectional impact of testing in emerging measures to ensure the full spectrum of patient experiences with genetic testing is represented. Although existing literature characterizes generally favorable patient/parent-reported outcomes related to genetic testing,13, 14, 15 patients and parents have reported challenges related to how, when, and with what support results have been disclosed.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 As such, a balanced measure of utility is warranted to support patient-centered care and to convey, through published evidence, the range of patient preferences and values to decision makers.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, most of our participants were female (85.7%), of European ancestry (61.9%), and college educated (95.2%). Although a minority of our participants primarily spoke a language other than English at home (22.2%), this is reflective of the Canadian population.38 We acknowledge that results from this study may not be reflective of the general population or transferable to all groups. Further work with underrepresented communities is underway to improve our understanding of how these elements of personal utility relate to their experiences with genetic testing. Second, we acknowledge that the 3 distinct cohorts selected for this study do not represent all clinical settings in which genetic testing occurs. Although our participants received indication-based genetic testing, our findings may not be applicable to healthy populations who receive newborn or cancer risk screening, for example. Third, because our cohorts reflected on prenatal and pediatric settings, further work on how utility changes across the lifespan (eg, adolescent, adult) is warranted and underway. Fourth, although we achieved a balanced sample across result types, further work to investigate how result type affects utility is warranted. Finally, we did not assess the relative importance of domains or elements across clinical settings.

Conclusion

Drawing upon 3 distinct clinical cohorts, we identified core elements of personal utility and unique, setting-specific elements of utility that are important to patients and families. To effectively integrate evidence of personal utility into clinical care and policy decision making, measurement strategies need to consider when, why, and for whom genetic testing is ordered. Ultimately, our findings contribute to a broader understanding of personal utility across a range of clinical settings and support further work to establish a personal utility measurement system that is tailored to specific clinical settings and applications of genetic testing.
