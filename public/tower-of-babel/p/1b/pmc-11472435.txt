Accessory gallbladder with partial encasement by esophageal adenocarcinoma: A rare case report
Authors: Arushi Sangwan, Gina Bladuell, Rajendra Kedar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accessory gallbladders are rare congenital malformations of the hepatobiliary system. They are often incidentally diagnosed preoperatively or intra-operatively. We report a case of a 52-year-old man with an accessory gallbladder that was partially encased by esophageal adenocarcinoma. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an accessory gallbladder diagnosed concurrently with esophageal adenocarcinoma. It is important for clinicians and radiologists to be cognizant of this anatomical malformation to avoid misdiagnoses and intra-operative complications.

Introduction

Accessory gallbladders (AGBs), also known as duplicated gallbladders, double gallbladders, or multiple gallbladders, are rare anatomical anomalies that occur in approximately 1 person per 4000 to 5000 people [1,2]. The exact incidence of AGBs is difficult to estimate, as they often go undiagnosed, and most existing calculations have been deduced from cadavers [1]. It has been postulated that AGBs affect both males and females equally, however, a higher frequency of gallbladder disease in females may lead to a higher rate of diagnosis in females [3]. Although often asymptomatic, some patients with AGBs can present with biliary colic and cholecystitis associated with symptoms such as nausea, loss of appetite, and abdominal pain [2].

An accessory gallbladder (AGB) is a rarely reported diagnosis. An extensive literature review revealed very few reported cases of AGBs draining near the confluence of the left and right hepatic ducts. No instances of an accessory gallbladder occurring concurrently with esophageal adenocarcinoma and being infiltrated by it were found in the current literature.

Case report

A 52-year-old male presented with a 5-month history of dysphagia. The patient was previously diagnosed with an esophageal stricture at another healthcare institution. Over the last 1 week, the patient reported worsening of the dysphagia with complete intolerance to solid food. Vomiting foamy substances after drinking and a feeling of fluid getting stuck in the throat was also reported.

A gastrointestinal consult was obtained. An esophagogram and endoscopy were performed. The esophagogram showed a distal esophageal stricture. Endoscopy showed fluid and retained secretions in the esophagus with a malignant-appearing esophageal stenosis. The scope had to be downsized to traverse through the stenosis, which was then dilated to about 15 cm to improve the stenosis. Moderate mucosal tear was noted. A biopsy was performed and histopathology revealed human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) positive adenocarcinoma of the esophagus.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed a distal esophageal mass extending into the gastroesophageal (GE) junction. A 5.3 × 3.5 × 3.7 cm fluid-filled structure was noted extending medially into the gastrohepatic space, suspicious of an accessory gallbladder. The structure demonstrated a duct draining to the distal left hepatic duct near the confluence of the right and left hepatic ducts. Medially, the structure was noted to be partially encased by the distal esophageal mass. The true gallbladder was noted in its usual location with multiple calcified gallstones (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Discussion

An AGB was first reported as an anatomical anomaly during an autopsy in 1674 by Blasius [1]. The first case of an AGB in a living patient was reported by Sherren in 1911 [1].

In terms of embryological development, an AGB is hypothesized to occur during the third and fourth weeks [1]. The most common classification systems for AGBs are the ones put forth by Boyden in 1926, and Harlaftis in 1977 (Table 1, Table 2) [4,5]. The H-type accessory gallbladder is known as the most common anatomical variant (approximately 48% of cases) [1]. Various locations of AGBs have been reported: intrahepatic, subhepatic, and within the gastrohepatic ligament [6]. There are no specific signs or symptoms associated with AGBs [7,8]. Patients with AGBs often remain asymptomatic and are often diagnosed when patients present with diseases involving the true gallbladders [7,8]. AGBs may also be diagnosed with cholelithiasis and cholecystitis [8]. Only a few cases of AGBs associated with carcinoma have been reported in the literature [2,9,10]. Other concurrent conditions reported with AGBs include heterotopic gastric mucosa, biliary cirrhosis, duodenojejunal diverticulum, and duodenal atresia [7]. Our patient's AGB was an incidental diagnosis, it was visualized while he was undergoing imaging for esophageal adenocarcinoma.

AGBs can often be missed on routine imaging and diagnosed incidentally intra-operatively [3,11]. It has been reported that ultrasonography and computed tomography do not provide adequate visualization of the biliary system [1,2]. Ultrasound is the most common imaging modality for patients presenting with biliary symptoms, but a major disadvantage is it is operator-dependent [7]. Other entities may also mimic AGBs on ultrasound, including but not limited to choledochal cysts, gallbladder diverticulum, hepatic cysts proximal to the true gallbladder, and Phrygian cap [7]. Invasive procedures such as ERCP can help with detailing the anatomy of AGBs, although it has a high rate of false negativity [7,12]. MRCP is the preferred imaging modality in cases of suspected AGBs [7,13]. MRCP was utilized to further visualize the AGB in our case as well. Imaging procedures such as oral cholecystography have been shown to miss around 30% to 60% of AGBs [1]. Intraoperative cholangiography is considered advantageous and superior [1,2].

In symptomatic patients, surgery is the management of choice [7]. When an AGB is diagnosed intra-operatively during a cholecystectomy of the true gallbladder, excision of both gallbladders is recommended [1,7,12]. AGBs can be associated with the same pathologies and complications as the primary gallbladder, including cholecystitis, torsion, papilloma, and rarely, cholangiocarcinoma [1,7,8]. Surgically, AGBs can be removed laparoscopically or with open surgery [7,11]. H-type AGBs are associated with a higher risk of intraoperative complications such as damage to the biliary tree [12]. Hence, it is important to identify and classify the AGB to determine the type of surgical approach for patients [12]. In our patient, the focus was first put on oncologic management since the AGB continued to remain clinically dormant.

AGBs continue to be a rarely reported anomaly. Due to the paucity of cases and patient data regarding AGBs, a standardized mode of management has not yet been established. An overall increase in the reporting of AGBs would contribute to improving the diagnostic and management methods, benefitting potential patients in the future.

Conclusion

Accessory gallbladders are rare anatomical malformations. They often go undiagnosed or are diagnosed incidentally and intra-operatively. Knowledge and accurate visualization of ectopic positions of the gallbladder are important to prevent potential iatrogenic injuries to biliary structures. There is a scarcity of cases of accessory gallbladders associated with carcinoma, and further studies regarding AGBs are required to determine the sensitivity and specificity of imaging modalities, as well as the preferred mode of management.
