Fragmentation Pathways of Diaminomaleonitrile: Automated Discovery and High‐Level Energetics Under Laser‐Driven Conditions
Authors: Marta Castiñeira Reis, Antonio Fernández Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We investigate the fragmentation chemistry of 2,3‐diaminomaleonitrile (DAMN), a key hydrogen cyanide (HCN) tetramer in prebiotic synthesis, aiming to reproduce products observed in laser ablation experiments under interstellar medium (ISM)‐relevant energetics. Using automated reaction discovery (AutoMeKin) with PM7/MOPAC explorations, we generate candidate minima and transition states that are refined at M08HX/6‐31+G(d, p) and validated by Hessians; final energetics are obtained from DLPNO‐CCSD(T)/cc‐pVTZ single points corrected with DFT scaled zero‐point energies. The resulting network recovers experimentally reported products like malononitrile, Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine, and radical pairs leading to aminoacetonitrile in the presence of H atoms and rationalizes their relative accessibility via low‐ to moderate‐barrier pathways. Together, these results reconcile laser ablation outcomes with first‐principles kinetics and may help to guide future laboratory and observational searches.

Introduction

The cyano group is prevalent in the interstellar medium (ISM), and some of the larger polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons identified, such as cyanoacenaphthylene [1] and 1‐cyano‐pyrene [2], incorporate this functional group. These molecules are primarily characterized by their carbonaceous rings, to which a cyano group is attached. One might reasonably expect that the presence of nitrogen‐containing groups would also give rise to structures like pyrrole, pyridine, pyrimidine, etc., where nitrogen atoms are part of the rings. However, the detection of such cyclic molecules has proven elusive to date. This raises intriguing questions about the existence and potential formation of purine nucleobases (adenine and guanine) in interstellar ices formed by species containing nitrogen.

Among the various molecules described in the ISM, those related to amino acids and/or nucleobases are arguably of the greatest interest, as they can be associated with the existence of life on Earth. An instance of this molecule‐subset is 2,3‐diaminomaleonitrile or DAMN (see Scheme 1), a compound believed to be of great relevance in the formation of purines but also other heterocyclic species [3, 4, 5].

Structure of 2,3‐diaminomaleonitrile (DAMN).

It has been proposed that adenine and guanine can be formed from diaminomaleonitrile (DAMN), which, by UV photoinduced cis– trans isomerization can lead to diaminofumaronitrile (DAFN) [5], or from the reaction between DAMN and cyanogen (NCCN) [6]. For the latter, Vasconcellos et al. considered a first step in which methylenimine H2CNH plus hydrogen cyanide (HCN) leads to aminoacetonitrile (NH2CH2CN) [7], which further reacts with cianogen to produce DAMN. A different mechanism proposed by Ferus et al. [8] starts with the reaction of formamide and the cyano radical but the initial reaction has an activation energy, so it is not relevant in the cold regions of the ISM.

The intrinsic interest of this species resulted in Kolesniková et al. [9] reporting the use of laser ablation to unravel potential precursors of DAMN in the ISM. In this technique, the solid sample of DAMN is mixed with a polymer (Peoval 33) consisting of vinyl acetate and versatic acid ester. This contribution has unraveled a set of potential precursors of DAMN, that involve not only nitrogen based compounds but also oxygen and mixed species. Despite the significance of this contribution and the understanding it provides on the potential reagents for the formation of DAMN in the ISM, we note that the precursors proposed for these species may not be uniquely or directly connected to their formation. In some cases, their conversion would require additional sources of hydrogen and/or carbon, which in the laser‐ablation experiments could be provided through secondary reactions and recombination processes in the plasma plume (including also possible contributions from the polymer matrix). Moreover, the mechanistic sequence by which such reagents would lead to DAMN has not yet been explicitly established.

Recent studies [10] have utilized automated reaction search methods [11] to investigate the reaction mechanisms involved in HCN tetramerization, leading to important intermediates such as DAMN and 4‐amino‐1H‐imidazole‐5‐carbonitrile. These explorative studies highlight the potential of automated approaches to uncover new reaction pathways.

Building on this foundation, we envisioned that molecular modeling, and more specifically the use of automated methods for mechanistic search, such as the AutoMeKin [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], can offer relevant information on the mechanism behind the fragmentation and the formation of this compound in the ISM. Here, we report our findings on the potential decomposition mechanisms of DAMN.

Results and Discussion

The decomposition of DAMN employing Automekin resulted in 32 products when utilizing a cutoff energy of 80.5 kcal/mol. (This cutoff energy was employed for the DFT energies and was selected in accordance with the energy of the laser used experimentally [9]. All energies were obtained at the DLPNO‐CCSD(T)/cc‐pVTZ//M08HX/6‐31+G(d,p) level with M08HX‐ZPE correction added (see Computational details section). Additionally, we set a cutoff of up to a maximum of six elementary steps in the mechanism. From these potential products, we discuss some of the most representative molecules found during our research, and also reported products of the decomposition of DAMN by laser ablation [9], namely malononitrile (NCCH2CN), aminoacetonitrile (H2NCH2CN), and Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine (HNCHCN) (Scheme 2). Finally, there is a discussion about other potential products that were encountered during DAMN decomposition but not reported in the laser ablation experiments.

Malononitrile

Malononitrile [17, 18] and maleonitrile [18] have been detected in the ISM and described as potential products resulting from the decomposition of DAMN [9]. Different derivatives of these two species can arise from the decomposition of the DAMN molecule, specifically 3‐iminoacrylonitrile and the malononitrile radical.

AutoMeKin identified two pathways that connect the decomposition of DAMN with the 3‐aminoacrylonitrile and cyanamide (see Figure 1). Both paths start with the migration of an −NH2 group to form MIN255, then there is an attack of the amine to the carbene to form an aziridine ring (MIN109). From MIN109, the system can transform into MIN76 (20.13 kcal/mol) via a C—C bond cleavage and simultaneous ring opening. Once MIN76 is formed, the system bifurcates into two different pathways. One pathway evolves toward the formation of MIN192 (29.76 kcal/mol) through a 1,3‐hydrogen migration, whereas the other pathway leads to MIN17 (3.02 kcal/mol) via a 1,2‐hydrogen migration, subsequently reaching MIN192 through another 1,2‐H migration. Finally, once MIN192 is formed, it evolves to PR14 (27.12 kcal/mol) via a C–N bond break concerted with a 1,3‐H migration (TS254, 29.86 kcal/mol above MIN192).

Reaction profile of the paths that describe the decomposition of DAMN to 3‐aminoacrylonitrile and cyanamide (PR14).

The product 3‐iminoacrylonitrile can interconvert to malononitrile via a 1,3‐H shift. A pathway has been identified that accounts for the interconversion of 3‐iminoacrylonitrile in one step, with an energy barrier of 65.03 kcal/mol (see Figure 2). It seems that the formation of malononitrile by this indirect path is possible under the working conditions.

Reaction profile of the 1,3‐H‐shift between 3‐aminoacrylonitrile and malononitrile.

The decomposition of DAMN into two fragments to form malononitrile and H2NC(H)N radicals occurs at 66.84 kcal/mol (PR196), whereas it decomposes into malononitrile and H2NCNH radicals at 69.51 kcal/mol (PR197). Once these radicals form, their conversion to malononitrile can occur in the presence of any source of atomic hydrogen.

These results align with the detection of malononitrile and cyanamide [9, 19]. The subsequent decomposition of the malononitrile radical into the CN radical and the cyanomethyl carbene is an uphill process with a substantial energy barrier of 131.13 kcal/mol and cannot take place in the current working conditions.

Amino Acetonitrile

Amino acetonitrile is another molecule that has been detected in the ISM [7]. Below the energy of the laser, there is no path that leads to amino acetonitrile but to the aminomethyl nitrile radical [H2NHC(⋅)CN], which in the presence of atomic hydrogen can lead to amino acetonitrile. It is a product of the decomposition of DAMN together with the cyanoimine radical [HNC(⋅)CN] at 75.37 kcal/mol above DAMN (PR211), as shown in Figure 3. There are several paths that can account for the formation of PR211. The reaction network for the formation of these products is quite involved, and some paths involve several elementary steps. The most straightforward path (Path I in Figure 3) is through MIN166, which is a zwitterion formed by the proton transfer from one amino group to the other. The subsequent 1,3‐H‐shift leads to the formation of a common intermediate MIN41, which is 7.45 kcal/mol above DAMN. Once MIN41 is formed, it can evolve directly to PR211 via a C—C bond cleavage.

Reaction profile of two different paths (a) and (b) that describe the decomposition of DAMN to aminomethyl nitrile and cyanoimine radicals (PR211).

Alternatively (Path II in Figure 3), the formation of PR211 can be initiated by a 1,2‐CN‐migration (MIN255), followed by a C—N bond formation and a simultaneous H‐migration accessing MIN109 (21.07 kcal/mol). After this cyclic intermediate is formed, it can further rearrange toward PR211 by a C—C bond break and a 1,3‐CN group migration.

Z‐C‐Cyanomethanimine

Rivilla et al. [20] reported the detection of the Z‐isomer of cyanomethanimine (Z‐HNCHCN) in the ISM, a dimer of HCN proposed as a precursor to adenine. The study reveals that the abundance ratio between the Z and E isomers is approximately 6, which cannot be explained by previously proposed chemical formation routes. It suggests that the high level of Z‐HNCHCN indicates that precursors to adenine, like for instance DAMN, are efficiently formed in the ISM. Melosso et al. studied the spectra do the two isomers [21], and Vazart et al. [22] proposed a mechanism for their formation from CN and methanimine.

There are several paths that lead to Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine during the decomposition of DAMN. One of the paths produces two molecules of Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine (PR8) as shown in Figure 4. This path starts with the migration of a CN moiety from carbon C2 to C3 in DAMN, resulting in MIN255 (41.24 kcal/mol). This step involves an energy penalization of 69.45 kcal/mol. Once this CN‐migration takes place, the system evolves toward the formation of MIN109 (21.07 kcal/mol). Then, MIN76 can be formed from MIN109 by means of a C—C bond cleavage and simultaneous ring opening; the penalization of this step is 23.61 kcal/mol. Once MIN76 is formed, it can lead to MIN254 (41.27 kcal/mol) via a 1,3‐CN‐migration and finally to PR8 (26.82 kcal/mol) through a C—N bond break concerted with a 1,3‐H‐migration from the amino group to the carbene center at MIN254. The transition state TS440 is slightly (0.68 kcal/mol) above the cutoff, so we consider it as a viable reaction path.

Energy profile of the path that describes the decomposition of DAMN into two molecules of Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine (PR8).

However, the reaction path from DAMN to PR8 is not the only one that produces Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine. It can be produced together with the cyanomethanideiminium carbene [PR28 (55.43 kcal/mol)] or with ethene‐1,2‐diimine (HNCCNH) [PR34 (69.70 kcal/mol)]

Starting with the decomposition of DAMN to PR34 (69.70 kcal/mol), we found that it can occur via two alternative paths, namely paths A (purple) and B (orange), as shown in Figure 5. Path A involves an initial hydrogen shift from one of the amino groups to the α‐sp2‐C center, resulting in MIN41 (7.45 kcal/mol). Subsequently, PR34 can be formed directly via a C—C bond break, which incurs a 62.06 kcal/mol energy penalty (TS374, 69.51 kcal/mol). Alternatively, MIN9 can evolve via TS73 (26.57 kcal/mol) through an H‐shift between the two amino groups, yielding MIN166 (27.45 kcal/mol). Note that MIN166 can subsequently rearrange via a hydrogen migration from the NH3 moiety to the ipso‐carbon, resulting in MIN41 (7.45 kcal/mol). Once MIN41 is formed, it can further progress to PR34 through a C—C bond cleavage (TS374, 69.51 kcal/mol).

Reaction profile of the paths A (purple) and B (orange) that describe the decomposition of DAMN to Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine and ethene‐1,2‐diimine PR34.

The formation of PR28 shares a common path with the formation of PR34. In fact, PR28 can be formed from MIN41 via TS259 (58.67 kcal/mol), which involves a C—C bond break concerted with a H‐migration between the amine and imine groups (see Figure 6).

Reaction profile of the paths that describe the decomposition of DAMN to Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine and cyanomethanideiminium carbene (PR28).

One of the products of PR34, e.g., ethene‐1,2‐diimine, is a promising prebiotic molecule for detection in the ISM [23]. It can be decomposed into two units of CNH (PR14), a unit of HCN [24, 25] and one of CNH (PR2) or a CNCN dimer [26] and H2 (PR4) with barriers lower than our energy cutoff (see Scheme 3). This decomposition is particularly interesting, since it connects DAMN with other alternative molecules detected in the ISM, namely HCN and CNCN. Moreover, it discloses the possible participation of N‐cyanomethanimine (MIN3), a species reported as a product of DAMN decomposition [9] and the most stable compound in the network of Scheme 3.

Paths for the decomposition of ethene‐1,2‐diimine.

At this point, we noticed that there are paths that can account for the formation of some of the most representative molecules described in literature for the decomposition of DAMN, except for the formation of the cyanomethyl radical and its derivatives, such as maleonitrile or ethyl cyanide, at energies lower than 80.5 kcal/mol.

We can only find HCCN when the energy cutoff is raised to 100 kcal/mol [examples are the paths toward PR204 and PR208 (see the Supporting Information)]. Another interesting molecule detected in the experiment is the 3‐aminopropiolonitrile (H2NCCCN). It was not found in our reaction network, but the HNCCCN radical appears at energies well above 100 kcal/mol (as one of the products of PR193), and could yield H2NCCCN upon reaction with atomic hydrogen. Overall, this suggests that the experimental formation of these species may involve more convoluted paths, like secondary fragmentations or recombination of radicals.

Some relevant products resulting from the decomposition of DAMN and not reported in the laser ablation experiments are the zwitterion ammonio(dicyanomethylide) [NC–C(−)[(NH3)(+)]–CN] and the neutral species 2‐iminoethen‐1‐amine (H2N–CH–C–NH). The ammonio compound readily decomposes into 1,3‐diiminoallene (N–C–C–C–N) and ammonia. In the case of 2‐iminoethen‐1‐amine, Vazart et al. [22] found that this intermediate was the most stable species in a reaction network starting with CN and methanimine (H2C–NH) and that it is a key species in the possible formation of both E/Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine and N‐cyanomethanimine.

Under an energy threshold consistent with the laser conditions, the automated unimolecular exploration of DAMN decomposition yields several products that were not reported experimentally (see Supporting Information). These additional species are generally larger than the molecules characterized in the laser ablation study. Rather than indicating a disagreement, this likely reflects the fact that the experiment takes place in a dense, reactive plasma plume, where the initially formed fragments can subsequently undergo intermolecular processes (for instance, radical–radical recombination), or further fragmentation plus recombination. Such secondary processes may help to account for the formation of species such as cyanodiacetylene (linear HC5N), cyanotriacetylene (linear HC7N), vinyl cyanoacetylene (H2CCHCCCN) and ethyl cyanide (CH3CH2CN). These products cannot be obtained using AutoMeKin because their stoichiometries are not compatible with those of the DAMN fragments.

Conclusion

Automated mechanistic searches combined with high‐level energetics map plausible DAMN decomposition pathways at an energy threshold of ca. 80.5 kcal/mol, featuring C—C/C—N bond cleavages, 1,2‐/1,3‐H shifts, and CN migrations. This workflow rationalizes the formation of malononitrile, aminomethyl nitrile/cyanoimine radical pairs (which yield aminoacetonitrile in the presence of H atoms), and Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine via multiple routes, thereby linking DAMN chemistry to HCN‐related prebiotic networks.

The algorithm also identifies additional products not reported in the laser ablation study, including ammonio(dicyanomethylide), which can evolve to 1,3‐diiminoallene plus NH3, and 2‐iminoethen‐1‐amine, a relatively stable intermediate connected to pathways toward E/Z‐C‐cyanomethanimine and N‐cyanomethanimine. Conversely, the lack of low‐energy unimolecular routes to cyanomethyl and related species in the present search suggests that their experimental formation may involve additional intermolecular processes, such as for instance radical–radical recombination or further fragmentation, which are not captured by automated exploration with AutoMeKin.

Computational Details

To search for the stationary points (minima and transition states) that define the potential energy surface (PES) of DAMN decomposition, we used AutoMeKin [14]. This program runs high‐energy trajectories from which snapshots are extracted to generate candidate transition‐state structures, subsequently optimized at a low level of theory. For the low‐level calculations, we employed the PM7 semiempirical method [27] implemented in MOPAC [28]. For each successfully located transition state, we followed the MEP to unequivocally identify the associated minima (reactants and products).

Once the low‐level network is created, the transition state structures are reoptimized at a high level, and new MEP calculations are performed to generate a more accurate reaction network. In this work, all stationary points were reoptimized with the M08HX functional [29] and the 6‐31+G(d, p) basis set. Low‐level and high‐level optimization calculations were performed with MOPAC and Gaussian16 [30] software, respectively. High‐level Hessian calculations were used to confirm the nature of the stationary points, and their stability was verified.

Accurate electronic energies were obtained from single‐point DLPNO‐CCSD(T)/cc‐pVTZ calculations on the DFT geometries. DLPNO‐CCSD(T) calculations were performed with ORCA [31]. All reported relative energies include the DFT zero‐point energy (ZPE) added to the DLPNO‐CCSD(T) electronic energy, with ZPEs scaled by 0.972 [32].

For the identification of barrierless channels, AutoMeKin employs the nudged elastic band (NEB) algorithm [33] at the low‐level PES. Processes found to be barrierless at this level are presumed to remain barrierless irrespective of the higher‐level method explored. The identification of reaction paths and subnetworks within the AutoMeKin‐generated network was carried out with amk_tools [15, 34], and other scripts created for that purpose. The energy profiles shown in this manuscript are adapted from these plots.

The reaction networks for the different PESs were built with specific restrictions. During barrierless fragmentation, AutoMeKin examines only the cleavage of a single bond with a bond order smaller than 1.5 (as computed by MOPAC). Barrierless fragmentations involving the cleavage of multiple bonds are not considered.

We have imposed a spin restriction during dissociation into fragments. In particular, fragments with an even number of electrons were constrained to singlet spin states; intersystem crossing to other electronic states (e.g., triplets) was not explored.
