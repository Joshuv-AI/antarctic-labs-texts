NETs accelerate aortic valve calcification by promoting M1 macrophage polarization through the TLR9 signaling pathway
Authors: Luyao Liu, Zhenzhen Lan, Xincan Liu, Yun Chen, Zhihao Chen, Li Cheng, Ting Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are positively correlated with the severity of calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD). This study aims to elucidate the mechanism by which NETs contribute to CAVD. The CAVD mice model was established by calcification-promoting diets, and NETs formation was modulated via intraperitoneal injection of Cl-amidine. We observed the effect of NETs on Raw264.7 cells by regulating NETs and TLR9 in vitro. Concentrations of TNF-α, MPO-DNA complex, and IL-10 were measured using ELISA. NETs formation was assessed through immunofluorescence assay citrullinated histone H3 (citH3). Expression levels of BMP2, RUNX2, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TLR 9 were analyzed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting, while flow cytometry was used to assess the expression of CD86 and CD206 on Raw264.7 cells. Results indicated that compared to the vehicle group, the CAVD group exhibited significant valve thickening and increased calcium deposition, as well as elevated levels of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β, NET-related markers MPO-DNA complexes and citH3, ossification factors BMP2 and RUNX2, and TLR9. Conversely, IL-10 levels were significantly reduced. Cl-amidine intervention in early CAVD mice significantly improved valve thickness and reduced calcium deposition, inflammatory factors, NETs-related markers, ossification factors, and TLR9 levels, while increasing IL-10 levels. Cl-amidine may delay CAVD progression in mice by reducing NETs. In vitro studies confirmed that serum from CAVD mice induced NETs, promoting the polarization of Raw264.7 cells to the M1 phenotype via TLR9 signaling pathway, thereby releasing pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), and inhibiting M2 polarization and IL-10 expression. In summary, our findings suggest that NETs promote Raw264.7 cell polarization to M1 through the TLR9 signaling pathway, contributing to the inflammatory response in CAVD. This study proposes a novel therapeutic strategy targeting NETs to delay CAVD progression.

Introduction

Calcified aortic valvular disease (CAVD) is the most common cardiac valvular disease in the elderly population, which is mainly characterized by valve thickening, fibrosis, and leaflet mineralization. Clinical manifestations include chest tightness, shortness of breath, and palpitations, which in severe cases can lead to heart failure, arrhythmia, and sudden death, causing a huge health burden on an aging society. CAVD is one of the three major causes of cardiovascular disease, with a spectrum of diseases ranging from aortic valve sclerosis to calcified aortic stenosis (AS), which can cause severe left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Aortic valve sclerosis occurs before aortic stenosis, and approximately, 9% of aortic valve sclerosis progresses to aortic stenosis within 5 years [1]. Population-based cardiovascular health studies have shown that 1–2% of aortic valve sclerosis progress to AS every year, and that 75% of these AS patients develop heart failure and are treated with valve replacement surgery or die within 2–5 years [2–4]. According to statistics, the disease burden of CAVD is increasing in the elderly population [5, 6]. Therefore, it is urgent to explore the pathogenesis of CAVD and develop drug therapeutic programs that can effectively delay or prevent its progression.

The formation mechanism of CAVD is very complex and involves endothelial injury, inflammatory cells and lipid invasion, release of inflammatory factors, proliferation of valve interstitial cells (VICs), remodeling of extracellular matrix, and calcification. Among them, the inflammatory response mediated by inflammatory cells is present throughout the development of CAVD and is the main mechanism for the formation and continued progression of CAVD. Macrophages are an important inflammatory cell type mediating the inflammatory response [7]. Macrophages can be categorized into 2 phenotypes: M1 and M2, and different macrophage subtypes may secrete and regulate different inflammatory factors [8]. Among them, M1 is the main type of macrophage that exerts pro-inflammatory effects and the main inflammatory cell that mediates the inflammatory response in CAVD [9]. It has been demonstrated that pleiotropic cytokines produced by M1 macrophages, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), are associated with increased expression of bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) [10, 11], which promotes mineralization of VICs through BMP2 expression [12, 13], BMP2 also promote osteogenic differentiation through activation of Runx2/Cbfα1 or Wnt signaling pathway. In addition, inflammatory mediators released by macrophages can activate NF-κB receptor activator ligand (RANKL)-related signaling pathways, thereby inducing osteogenic differentiation [14]. In summary, the release of inflammatory cytokine mediated by M1 macrophage is the key to CAVD formation.

Neutrophils are the most abundant immune cells in the blood and are among the first immune cells to be recruited into inflamed tissues in the presence of infection or sterile injury [15]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are a kind of reticular DNA structure released into the extracellular space after neutrophil activation. NETs are mainly composed of nuclear histones and neutrophilic proteins, such as citrullinated histone H3 (citH3), myeloperoxidase (MPO), and neutrophil elastase (NE). The process of production and release of NETs, called NETosis, is a specific type of cell death. In addition to their antimicrobial function, NETs also have a strong inflammatory function [16, 17], which can promoting the recruitment of monocytes and triggering the release of reactive oxygen species and pro-inflammatory cytokines from macrophages, aggravating the inflammatory response [18, 19]. More and more studies have confirmed that NETs are involved in atherosclerosis and thrombosis [20, 21]. NETs can aggravate inflammation by activating NF-κB signaling in macrophages, thereby aggravating atherosclerosis [22]. Cl-amidine is a peptide arginine deiminase (PAD) inhibitor, can block the formation of NETs, thereby decreasing atherosclerotic lesions in mice [21]. Neutrophils and NETs are emerging as important mediators of pathogenic inflammation [23], and playing an important role in the development of atherosclerosis. The pathogenesis of aortic valve calcification is similar to atherosclerosis, with the presence of inflammatory cells infiltration and the release of inflammatory factors [24, 25]. Therefore, NETs may also become an important mediator involved in the progression of CAVD. Studies have demonstrated that the presence of NETs in stenotic valves in patients with AS is positively correlated with the severity of the disease [26]. However, the mechanism of NETs in CAVD is not clear. In this study, we proposed to establish a CAVD mice model to investigate the inflammatory mechanism of NETs–macrophages involved in CAVD by detecting NETs-related markers, inflammation and calcification indexes in aortic valves.

Materials and methods

In vivo experiments

Male C57BL/6J wild-type mice were administered a diet conducive to calcification over a period of 20 weeks to develop an early-stage murine model of CAVD [27]. This study was conducted in the Animal Experimentation Center of Henan University of Chinese Medicine and approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Henan University of Chinese Medicine (Approval Nos. IACUC-202302016, IACUC-202307029). All operations were performed according to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health, USA). The animals were housed in SPF-grade environments, with good indoor ventilation, temperature, and humidity, in separate cages under 12 h of cyclic light, with 48 h of change of bedding and feed, and with ad libitum access to water. The PAD inhibitor Cl-amidine (20 mg/kg/d/each) was administered by intraperitoneal injection for 4 weeks during the early stages of CAVD. Blood was taken from the eyeballs and the entire aortic valve of the mice was collected.

Neutrophil isolation and treatment

Mice peripheral neutrophils were isolated by Mice Peripheral Blood Neutrophil Isolation Solution Kit (Tianjin Hao Yang Biological, LZS1100). A 10-ml sterile siliconized centrifuge tube was taken and 3 ml of isolate 1 was added, followed by a slow addition of 1.5 ml of 80% concentration of the isolate solution to form a gradient interface. Preparation of blood samples: 6-week-old female C57BL/6-J mice with blood taken from the eyeballs. Anticoagulant and erythrocyte sedimentation solution were mixed in a 2:1 ratio. Carefully layer the separation solution on top of the gradient interface and then centrifuge at 800 g for 25 min. After centrifugation, two circular milky white cell layers will appear below the plasma layer in the centrifuge tube, the upper cell layer is the mononuclear cell layer and the lower cell layer is the neutrophil layer, aspirate the neutrophil layer in the centrifuge tube, and transfer it to a new centrifuge tube. To the centrifuge tube containing neutrophils, add 5 times the volume of wash solution, mix the cells, centrifuge at 250 g for 10 min, and discard the supernatant. Add 8 times the amount of erythrocyte lysate as appropriate according to the number of erythrocyte residuals and lysed the erythrocytes until the erythrocytes were completely lysed. Then, PBS was added for washing, the precipitate was resuspended, the supernatant was discarded after centrifugation, and the cells were resuspended by adding complete medium and set aside.

The neutrophils obtained from the isolation were counted and inoculated in cell culture 6-well plates at 106 cells/well.

The cells were treated according to the following groups: resuspended in mice peripheral blood neutrophil complete medium containing 10% serum from the vehicle group (Qisai Biologicals, QS-M238A); resuspended in mice peripheral blood neutrophil complete medium containing 10% serum from the CAVD model group, with or without the PAD inhibitor Cl-amidine (200 uM), Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (30 ng/ml); cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. After incubation, the supernatant and cells were collected separately.

Raw264.7 cells treatment and culture

RAW264.7 cell culture medium: high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Qisai Bio, QS-S011) + 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (GIBCO, 10099-141) + 1% (Penicillin–Streptomycin Solution) (Qisai Bio, QS-S402).

The RAW264.7 cells were removed from liquid nitrogen and rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath. The cryotube was gently shaken to dissolve the cryopreservative solution. Once thawed, the cells were transferred to a centrifuge tube containing 5 ml of medium and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature to collect the cells. The supernatant was then discarded and the cells were suspended in complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, inoculated into Petri dishes, gently blown and mixed, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 saturated humidity.

When the density of the cells reached 80%, the cells were passaged: the medium was discarded and washed with PBS. 2 ml of complete medium was added, and the cells were gently blown to make a single-cell suspension, which was passaged at a ratio of 1:4 and cultured for expansion at 37 °C with 5% CO2 saturated humidity.

The Raw264.7 cells in logarithmic growth phase with good growth status were taken and inoculated into cell culture 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well and then cultured overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Mice peripheral neutrophils containing 10% vehicle group serum, containing 10% CAVD model group serum or treated with PMA, were incubated for 24 h. Their cell supernatants were treated with Raw264.7 cells for 24 h. The cells were incubated for 24 h with or without the TLR9 antagonist ODN2088 (1 μM, InvivoGen), and the TLR 9 agonist ODN1826 (1 μM, InvivoGen) was incubated for another 24 h, respectively.

Echocardiography

Mice were tested by echocardiography before modeling, at 16 weeks of modeling, and at 20 weeks of modeling.

M-mode echocardiography and color Doppler ultrasound were used to detect left ventricular function and aortic stenosis, respectively.

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) were measured on the parasternal long-axis views in M-mode. Aortic valve peak flow velocity (Vav), aortic valve transvalvular pressure gradient (AVPG), and aortic valve effective orifice area (AVA) were measured using color Doppler in the aortic arch and apical 5-chamber views.

The ultrasound probe angle correction range was 40–50°. To minimize inter-individual variation the entire procedure was performed by 1 person alone. All procedures and results were performed under the supervision of the ultrasound instructor. The angle correction range of the ultrasonic probe is 40–50°. In order to reduce the difference between individuals, the whole operation process is completed by one person alone. All the operation methods and test results were completed under the guidance of the ultrasound teacher.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

After 20 weeks of modeling, blood was taken from the eye to a 1.5 ml EP tube, followed by immediate opening of the chest cavity and removal of the heart, which was placed in pre-cooled PBS and washed 3–5 times. The heart was removed along with a portion of the aorta, dried on filter paper, placed in 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, then subjected to 4 μm serial sectioning after dehydration and paraffin embedding, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin staining for 2–8 s. The stained tissue was observed under the microscope and the aortic valve leaflet thickness was quantified.

Von Kossa staining

The obtained 4 μm valve sections were deparaffinized, hydrated, and treated with silver nitrate for 5–30 min until the tissue darkened and then the nuclei were stained with tender red or Hematoxylin for 1–5 min. The stained tissue was observed under a microscope and the area of positive calcium salt deposits in aortic valve tissue was quantified (Image-Pro).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The expression level of NETs-related marker citH3 was determined in aortic valve tissues from all groups of mice and in peripheral neutrophils from mice treated with different sera using IF staining. The tissues (cells grown on coverslips) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% TritonX-100 for 5 min at room temperature, and diluted normal goat serum (BOSTER, AR1009) and closed for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with a primary antibody specific for citH3 (Abcam, ab219407, 1:100) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG (BOSTER, BA1032, 1:100) for 1 h at 25 °C. All slides were observed under a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus Corporation).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Quantification of NETs-associated myeloperoxidase MPO-DNA complexes in mice serum and conditioned media derived from mice peripheral neutrophils was performed by the Cell Death ELISA Kit (enzyme linked, YJ361041). After blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin, serum samples or cell culture supernatants were added to 96-well plates of mice MPO-DNA incubated with peroxidase-labeled anti-DNA monoclonal antibody. Zeroing with blank wells, the absorbance (OD value) of each well was measured sequentially at 450 nm.

Western blot (WB)

Total proteins were extracted by lysing mice aortic valve tissues or Raw264.7 cells with RIPA lysis solution (Servicebio, G2002). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (Guangzhou Jiebes Biotechnology Co., Ltd., G3522). Protein extracts were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to a PVDF membrane. 5% skimmed milk powder in Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) (blocking solution) soaked PVDF membrane for 2 h at 4 °C with anti-citH3 (Abcam, Ab1791), BMP2 (Servicebio, GB11252), RUNX2 (Affinity, AF5186), TLR9 (Abcam, Ab134368), TNF-α (Affinity, AF7014), and IL-10 (Servicebio, GB11108) monoclonal antibodies were incubated overnight, and then the membrane was incubated with the TBST dilution of the corresponding HRP-labeled secondary antibody 1:10,000 dilution, so that the PVDF membrane was immersed in the secondary antibody incubation solution and incubated on a shaker at room temperature for 2 h. Detection was carried out using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Servicebio, G2014). β-actin (Affinity, T0022) was used as an internal reference for protein expression standardization.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mice aortic valve tissues or Raw264.7 cells using Trizol reagent (Ambion, 15596-026) according to the instructions and then reverse transcribed to cDNA using the Prime-Script RT kit (VAZYME, R233). cDNA was extracted using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (TOLOBIO, 22204) rapid real-time PCR system: pre-denaturation (95 °C, 10 min), followed by denaturation (95 °C, 10 s) and annealing (60 °C, 60 s) for a total of 40 cycles. mRNA expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

After the treatment of Raw264.7 cells in logarithmic growth phase with good growth status, the cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. Each group of cells was divided into two parts, one of which was resuspended with 100 μl of PBS containing 0.5% BSA, 1 μg of CD86 antibody (BioLegend, 105109) was added, and the other was incubated at 4 °C and protected from light for 30 min. The other was added with 1 ml of Fix/ Perm working solution, mixed and incubated at 4 °C for 50 min, then washed twice with Perm/wash working solution, then resuspended by adding 100 μl of Perm/wash working solution, added 0.5 μg of CD206 antibody (BioLegend, 162505), and incubated at 4 °C for 40 min away from light. Cells after incubation with antibodies were washed twice with PBS containing 0.5% BSA. After resuspension with 0.2 ml of PBS containing 0.5% BSA, they were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used for statistical analysis. The measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and one-way analysis of variance (LSD method) was used for normally distributed and variance-aligned data. Nonparametric rank sum test was used for non-normally distributed or variance-uniform data. p < 0.05 difference was statistically significant. Immunofluorescence and protein blotting images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ.

NETs upregulate TLR9 signaling pathway expression

As shown in Fig. 5A, J, NETs were significantly increased in the CAVD group compared with the vehicle group, and its TLR9 signaling pathway expression was also significantly increased. On the contrary, NETs were significantly decreased in the CAVD+Cl-amidine group and its TLR9 signaling pathway expression was also significantly decreased compared with the CAVD group. Therefore, NETs may participate in the inflammatory response of CAVD by up-regulating the TLR9 signaling pathway. In addition, as shown in Fig. 8H, the peripheral neutrophils of mice cultured with CAVD mice serum were added with PMA to further induce the formation of NETs, and whose cell supernatants were co-cultured with Raw264.7 cells, showed a significant decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokine release from Raw264.7 cells after addition of the TLR9 inhibitor ODN2088 to the culture. In contrast, the addition of the TLR9 agonist ODN1826 to the culture showed a significant increase in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from Raw264.7 cells. In summary, our results show that NETs participate in the inflammatory response of CAVD by up-regulating TLR9 signaling pathway.

PMA can cause further increase of NETs in the above serum (CAVD+NETs group) and lead to further increase of CD86-positive cells, TNF-α mRNA, IL-1β mRNA, IL-6 mRNA, and TLR9 mRNA expression and further decrease of CD206-positive cells and IL-10 mRNA expression. The expression of TLR9 mRNA decreased, the proportion of CD86-positive cells and the levels of TNF-α mRNA, IL-1β mRNA, and IL-6 mRNA decreased. Discussion

The results of this study showed that the formation of NETs was closely related to the formation of CAVD in mice, and the PAD inhibitor Cl-amidine delayed the formation of CAVD in mice by inhibiting the formation of NETs and reducing the expression of the TLR9 signaling pathway, inflammatory factors, and ossification factors. To elucidate the mechanism of action, we extracted neutrophils from peripheral blood of normal mice and resuspended them in medium containing serum of vehicle mice and CAVD mice, respectively. We found that the serum of CAVD mice could induce the formation of NETs, and the formation of NETs further activated the TLR9 signaling pathway, which induced the polarization of Raw264.7 cells toward M1 and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and inhibited the polarization of Raw264.7 cells toward M2 and the release of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10.

CAVD is the most prevalent cardiac valvular disease in the world, and its symptomatic CAVD has the characteristics of high morbidity and high mortality. Although interventional therapy has improved the mortality of symptomatic CAVD in recent years, not all patients are suitable for interventional therapy, especially the elderly with complications. The pathogenesis of CAVD is very complex [28] and inflammation plays a key role in the development of CAVD [6]. NETs are a special way of death of neutrophils, which are induced by a variety of infectious and non-infectious stimulants and participate in a variety of inflammatory diseases through the secretion of matrix metalloproteinases and pro-inflammatory cytokines [29–31]. Previous studies have shown that NETs are present in the serum and aortic valves of AS patients and are positively correlated with the severity of the disease [26]. In the present study, the formation of NETs was increased in the valve and serum of CAVD mice, which was consistent with previous studies [26]. On this basis, we explored the effects of NETs on CAVD in mice. Inhibition of NETs formation by PAD inhibitor Cl-amidine can delay the disease progression of CAVD mice. Although studies have found that NETs are involved in the pathological process of CAVD, the mechanism of action of NETs in CAVD was not clarified.

In recent years, a large number of studies have demonstrated that NETs, as a novel mediators of neutrophil–macrophage communication, can induce the activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages and promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α through the TLR 9-related pathway [23, 32–34]. At the same time, the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 released by activated macrophages will in turn further promote the formation of NETs, forming a vicious cycle [35]. In addition, macrophages are one of the important cellular components involved in CAVD, especially M1 cell phenotype, and inflammatory factors release a large number of inflammatory mediators and ultimately leading to calcification of the aortic valve [36, 37]. As mentioned above, we hypothesized that NETs may promote the polarization of macrophages to M1, release pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhance valve inflammation, and promote valve thickening, injury and calcification by activating the TLR9 signaling pathway. We extracted peripheral neutrophils from normal female mice and resuspended them in the medium containing the serum of the vehicle group and the serum of the CAVD group mice. We found that the serum of the CAVD mice could induce the formation of NETs, which may be related to the inflammatory factors in the serum of the CAVD mice [35, 38]. The formation of NETs was further increased after we added PMA treatment. In contrast, the addition of Cl-amidine treatment further decreased the formation of NETs, which is consistent with previous studies [39, 40]. In order to clarify the mechanism, we co-cultured NETs induced by CAVD mice serum with Raw264.7 cells, with or without the addition of the TLR9 inhibitor ODN2088, and the TLR9 agonist ODN1826 selected. We found that after adding ODN2088, NETs mediated Raw264.7 cells, CD86 cells decreased significantly, CD206 cells increased significantly, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 mRNA expression decreased, and the IL-10 mRNA expression increased. On the contrary, after adding ODN1826, NETs mediated Raw264.7 cells, CD86 cells increased significantly, CD206 cells decreased significantly, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 mRNA expression increased, and the IL-10 mRNA expression decreased. Our results show that NETs mediate the polarization of macrophages to M1 phenotype through TLR9 signaling pathway, release TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, inhibit the polarization of macrophages to M2 type, and inhibit the expression of IL-10.

The limitations and shortcomings of this study include, for example, the fact that our studies were based on the mice and cellular level and did not involve the human level, and the role of this mechanism in human diseases needs to be explored further in future; furthermore, the sample size of this study was small and limited to a specific strain of mice, and therefore, large-scale animal and clinical trials are needed in future to validate this finding. Nevertheless, our study demonstrates the potential of inhibiting NETs in the treatment of CAVD and provides a new direction for the clinical application of CAVD.

In summary, we suggest for the first time that NETs are involved in the inflammatory mechanism of CAVD, and inhibition of NETs can delay the formation of CAVD in mice. Our data reveal that NETs induced by CAVD mice serum induce pro-inflammatory macrophage activation through the TLR 9 pathway in vitro, release pro-inflammatory cytokines, and indicate that inhibiting the formation of NETs may be a potential treatment for CAVD.
