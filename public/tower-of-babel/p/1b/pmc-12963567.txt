Targeting SLC6A8 suppresses tumor growth and enhances ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Wenfang Bao, Yang Yu, Guofeng Yu, Jingde Chen, Yandong Li, Yanan Hai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) persists as a therapeutic challenge owing to restricted treatment alternatives and the dearth of effective biomarkers. The creatine transporter SLC6A8 has been documented to participate in the progression of various malignancies. However, the role that SLC6A8 plays in HCC remains poorly characterized. Hence, this study aimed to investigate the function of the SLC6A8 in HCC and evaluate its candidacy as a therapeutic target.MethodsBioinformatics analysis, qPCR on tissue specimens, and immunohistochemistry on tissue microarrays were utilized to assess the expression and clinical significance of SLC6A8 in HCC. In vitro assays including cell viability, colony formation, and cell migration, as well as an in vivo subcutaneous xenograft model, were employed to explore the impacts of both genetic silencing of SLC6A8 and pharmacological blockade of SLC6A8 on HCC tumor growth. The anti-tumor effect of SLC6A8 inhibition in combination with the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 in HCC was also examined in cellular and animal models.ResultsOur findings revealed that SLC6A8 is notably upregulated in HCC tissues and is associated with a poor prognosis. SLC6A8 knockdown suppressed cell proliferation and migration of HCC cells. RGX-202 also demonstrated potent antitumor efficacy in HCC cells both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, SLC6A8 inhibition augmented cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis and significantly enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 in xenograft models of HCC.ConclusionThese findings underscore SLC6A8 as a promising therapeutic target, and its inhibitor combined with ferroptosis inducers may serve as an innovative treatment strategy for improving the therapeutic effect in HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-026-06442-8.Keywords: SLC6A8, Hepatocellular carcinoma, RGX-202, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks as the sixth most frequently diagnosed cancer, with an estimated 865,000 new cases each year. It also stands as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality, resulting in over 750,000 deaths worldwide (Bray et al. 2024). The main types of primary liver cancer are hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which accounts for 75%-85%, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), comprising 10%-15%. Chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) is responsible for 21%-55% of HCC cases globally (Rumgay et al. 2022). The mainstay treatment options for advanced HCC encompass tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and combination with or without anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy (Zheng et al. 2025). The distinct characteristics and a lack of definitive biomarkers of HCC constrain the efficacy of current therapies, urgently driving the request for novel therapeutic modalities and predictive biomarkers.

Solute carrier family 6 member 8 (SLC6A8), also known as “CT1” or “CRTR”, mapped to Xq28, is a creatine transporter (Salomons et al. 2001). SLC6A8 enables cells to import phosphocreatine, which functions as a swiftly mobilizable energy reserve for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) regeneration, in a Na+/Cl- dependent manner (Stary and Bajda 2023). Emerging evidence has implicated that SLC6A8 is involved in the malignancy progression of various cancers by regulating the key signaling pathways or tumor microenvironment. SLC6A8 promotes tumor progression through the Notch pathway in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (Feng et al. 2021) and mediates the oncogenic effect of VTN in colorectal cancer (CRC) (Yu et al. 2025). In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), SLC6A8 is transcriptionally upregulated via p65/NF-κB cascade and then activates the AKT-ERK1/2 cascade to enhance tumor cell survival (Li et al. 2021).

Ferroptosis represents a form of iron-dependent programmed cell death propelled by excessive lipid peroxidation. (Dixon and Olzmann 2024). An increasing body of evidence emphasizes the pivotal role of ferroptosis in HCC. Sorafenib, the first TKI approved for first-line standard therapy in advanced HCC, has been shown to trigger ferroptosis (Li et al. 2024; Louandre et al. 2013). Additionally, several regulatory axes have been identified to modulate ferroptosis susceptibility in HCC, such as the LIFR/NF-κB/LCN2 (Yao et al. 2021), PIAS3/TGF-β/TXNIP (Bao et al. 2023), and Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 (Yang et al. 2024a) axis. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis in HCC. Nevertheless, the influence of SLC6A8 on ferroptosis in HCC has not yet been experimentally verified.

In the current investigation, we identified that SLC6A8 was upregulated in HCC tissues and is correlated with poor prognosis. SLC6A8 knockdown suppressed the progression of HCC cells in vitro and in vivo. Besides, an oral small-molecular SLC6A8 transporter inhibitor, RGX-202, has demonstrated notable anti-tumor activity in both cellular and animal models. Subsequent investigations reveal that inhibiting SLC6A8 promotes ferroptosis vulnerability of HCC, suggesting a promising synergistic treatment strategy for HCC.

Materials and methods

Human HCC tissue samples

A human HCC tissue microarray (TMA) (#ZL-LVC1801), containing 90 paired tumor and adjacent normal specimens, was acquired from Shanghai Zhuohao Biotech (Shanghai, China). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was carried out with an anti-SLC6A8 antibody (dilution 1:50; 20299-1-AP; Proteintech, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Two blinded pathologists independently evaluated the immunostaining results, scoring each sample according to staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells. SLC6A8 staining was scored based on intensity as follows: - = none, + = weak, ++ = moderate and +++ = strong. Thirty paired HCC tumor specimens and their corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues were procured from patients at Shanghai East Hospital. All the collected samples were immediately subjected to snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until subsequent analysis. All participants provided written informed consent and the study was granted approval by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine.

Cell culture, reagents, and antibodies

Huh7 and HCC-LM3 were obtained from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Corning, USA), which was supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Corning, USA) and 100units/mL penicillin/streptomycin. The cultivation was carried out in a humidified incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. RSL3 and Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) were obtained from MedChemExpress (NJ, USA). Deferoxamine (DFO) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA (Germany). RGX-202 was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (TX, USA). SLC6A8 (20299-1-AP) and ACSL4 (22401-1-AP) antibody was obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). GPX4 (ab125066) antibody was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Cell transfection

Non-specific control (NC) and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) with 2’-O-methyl modification that target SLC6A8 were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transient transfection was conducted with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. The sequences were as follows:

siNC: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’;

siSLC6A8-1: 5’- CGCCACCAGUAUCAAUUGUdTdT-3’;

siSLC6A8-2: 5’- CACCAUGAGUGCUCACUAAdTdT-3’;

For stable knockdown of SLC6A8, lentiviral particles expressing shRNA based on the siSLC6A8-1 sequence was used. HCC cells were transduced using the lentiviruses at the exposure of 4 µg/mL polybrene. Subsequently, these cells were subjected to selection with 2.5 µg/mL puromycin to establish stably infected cell lines.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell viability was assessed with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) according to protocols. In brief, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 per well in triplicate. Following the appropriate incubation period, each well was added with 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 75 min. Absorbance was then determined by spectrophotometer. at a wavelength of 450 nm.

For the clonogenic assay, 1 × 103 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates and cultured with medium changes every four days. After 14–21 days, the resulting colonies were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde. Then they were stained with 0.5% crystal violet, subjected to imaging, and quantitatively analyzed.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA), iron and reactive oxygen species (ROS)

MDA levels were quantified with the MDA Assay Kit (A003-4-1, Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) and iron levels were quantified with the Iron Assay Kit (A039-2-1, Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intracellular ROS levels were detected with a Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (S0033S, Beyotime, China) employing the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. The probe was diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium, which was then applied to the cells after removal of the original culture medium. After 30-minute incubation at 37 °C in the dark, the cells were rinsed using phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Imaging was performed with an inverted fluorescence microscope. In flow cytometric analysis, cells were similarly incubated using diluted DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 50 min and analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer. Data processing was conducted with FlowJo (FlowJo, LLC).

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was assessed with a Transwell assay (Corning, USA). Briefly, 80,000 HCC cells resuspended in 400µL of serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, whereas the lower compartment was added with 800µL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Following 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were carefully removed. While, migrated cells on the lower membrane were fixed, stained using crystal violet, photographed under a microscope and then quantified.

Western blotting analysis

Whole cell lysates were lysed using RIPA buffer (Merck Millipore, USA) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Germany). The proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electro-transferred onto a methanol-activated PVDF membrane. The PVDF membrane was then blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at a room temperature, followed by incubation with primary and secondary antibodies. The results were detected by Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells or tissues with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA with the Hifair® AdvanceFast 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). qRT-PCR was performed using Hieff UNICON® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative mRNA expression levels of detected genes were computed via the 2−ΔΔCT method, and human β-actin serves as the endogenous control. The primer sequences used are listed below:

SLC6A8-F: 5’- TGATCGCCCTGGTTGGA-3’;

SLC6A8-R: 5’- GATCACCATGGAGGCGTAG-3’;

β-actin-F: 5’- AGAGCCTCGCCTTTGCCGATCC-3’;

β-actin-R: 5’- CTGGGCCTCGTCGCCCACATA-3’.

Animal models

4 weeks old male athymic nude mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and used for establishing xenograft models for HCC. HCC-LM3 cells stably infected with shSLC6A8 or NC were injected subcutaneously into each mouse. Tumor size was measured twice a week and volume was computed with the formula: (Length × Width²) / 2. Approximately four weeks later, the mice were euthanized and then tumors were harvested, weighed, and photographed.

To assess the influence of RGX-202 therapy on tumor growth in vivo. About one week after HCC-LM3 cells were injected, all mice were randomly allocated into distinct groups (n = 5) and administered with vehicle control or RSL3 (10 mg/kg, via intraperitoneally injection) every other day or RGX202 (500 mg/kg, via oral gavage) once a day or combo treatment for three weeks, respectively. Tumor size was measured twice a week and volume was computed with the formula: (Length × Width²) / 2. Approximately 3 weeks later, the mice were euthanized, after which the tumors were harvested, weighed, and photographed. Animal studies were carried out according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals from the National Institutes of Health (NIH). All experiments were approved by Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital, Tongji University.

Bioinformatics analysis

GEPIA (Tang et al. 2017) (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) was used to analyze the mRNA expression levels of SLC6A8. The prognostic significance of SLC6A8 mRNA in HCC was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier plotter (Győrffy 2024) (http://kmplot.com).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± SD. The differences between groups were evaluated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc and contingency tables were analyzed via the Fisher-exact test to detect significant associations among diverse variables, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. The statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Results

SLC6A8 is upregulated in HCC and correlated with poor prognosis of HCC patients

Analysis of TCGA data through GEPIA online tool revealed a significant elevation in SLC6A8 mRNA expression in HCC tumor tissues (n = 369) compared to normal samples (n = 160) (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that high SLC6A8 expression was correlated with poorer 5-year overall survival in HCC patients (Fig. 1B). To further validate these findings, qRT-PCR was performed on paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues from 30 HCC patients at our institution. The results confirmed that SLC6A8 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (P < 0.01), with over half of the cases (56.7%, 17/30) exhibiting a fold change greater than 1.5 (Fig. 1C). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining of the HCC TMA demonstrated stronger SLC6A8 protein expression in tumor tissues relative to adjacent normal controls (Fig. 1D–F). Importantly, elevated SLC6A8 levels were associated with reduced overall survival (OS) among HCC patients (Fig. 1G). These results suggest that SLC6A8 is overexpressed in HCC tissues and may represent a promising biomarker.

SLC6A8 knockdown suppresses growth of HCC cells in vitro

To investigate the functional role of SLC6A8 in HCC, we initially assessed its influence on cell proliferation and migration in vitro. After transfection with siRNAs against SLC6A8 in Huh7 and HCC-LM3 cells, the interference efficiency was verified by western blotting (Fig. 2A). Subsequent CCK-8 assays demonstrated that silencing SLC6A8 with two independent siRNAs (siSLC6A8-1 and siSLC6A8-2) markedly suppressed cellular growth compared to the negative control (Fig. 2B). In line with this, colony formation assays using SLC6A8-knockdown cells demonstrated a substantial decrease in clonogenic capacity (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, Transwell migration assays indicated that downregulation of SLC6A8 significantly impaired the migratory capacity of HCC cells (Fig. 2D).

SLC6A8 knockdown suppresses HCC cell growth in vitro. A The efficiency of siSLC6A8 transfection in HCC cells assessed by Western blotting. B CCK-8 assays for HCC cells transfected with siSLC6A8-1, siSLC6A8-2 or siNC. C Colony formation assays for HCC cells transfected with siSLC6A8-1, siSLC6A8-2 or siNC. D Transwell migration assays for HCC cells transfected with siSLC6A8-1, siSLC6A8-2 or siNC. Scale bars, 100 μm. * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01

SLC6A8 inhibitor RGX-202 exhibits potent antitumor effects in HCC cells

RGX-202 (β-guanidinopropionic acid) blocks cellular creatine uptake by inhibiting cell surface SLC6A8 receptors and has been reported to suppress CRC progression (Kurth et al. 2021). To assess the antitumor effects of RGX-202, we conducted cell growth curve and colony formation assays in Huh7 and HCC-LM3 cells (Fig. 3A–B). The results demonstrated a clear concentration-dependent suppression of cell viability and clonogenicity following RGX-202 treatment.

Targeting SLC6A8 suppresses tumor growth of HCC in vivo

To further validate the impact of SLC6A8 on HCC in vivo, we established HCC cells with stable knockdown of SLC6A8. Western blot analysis confirmed successful silencing of SLC6A8 expression (Fig. S1A). HCC-LM3 cells (2 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into mice to generate xenograft tumors. The results revealed a significant reduction in tumor growth upon SLC6A8 knockdown (Fig. 4A). In parallel, a subcutaneous tumor model was generated using wild type HCC-LM3 cells, which were divided into RGX-202-treated and control groups. RGX-202 administration markedly suppressed tumor progression (Fig. 4B). These data demonstrate that both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of SLC6A8 effectively attenuates in vivo tumor growth in HCC.

SLC6A8 inhibition sensitizes HCC cells to ferroptosis

Creatine mitigates oxidative stress by suppressing mitochondrial activity and reducing intracellular ROS levels, and has been reported to confer resistance to ferroptosis (Chen et al. 2024; He et al. 2025). Since SLC6A8 mediates creatine transport, we investigated whether it influences ferroptosis in HCC cells. CCK-8 assays (Fig. 5A) presented that inhibition of SLC6A8 enhanced the sensitivity of HCC cells to RSL3-induced growth suppression an effect that was rescued by Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor (Miotto et al. 2020), supporting a promoting ferroptosis role of SLC6A8 loss. Furthermore, silencing SLC6A8 significantly enhanced RSL3-triggered production of malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation (Figs. 5B and S1B), which was also reversed upon Fer-1 treatment. Additionally, elevation of the intracellular iron overload was observed, which was reversed after treatment with the iron-chelator DFO (Fig. S2C–D). Consistent with the above findings, total intracellular ROS levels evaluated with an oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe were markedly increased upon SLC6A8 knockdown, as observed through both fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 5C) and flow cytometry (Figs. 5D and S1E). As a creatine transporter, the knockdown of SLC6A8 reversed the creatine-mediated inhibition of ferroptosis in HCC cells, which is in line with prior reports (Fig. S2A–B). Finally, in vivo validation of the combination therapy with RGX-202 and RSL3 revealed an enhanced anti-tumor efficacy compared to either monotherapy (Fig. 5E). Besides, the crucial ferroptosis-related markers were evaluated. With exposure to RSL3, the expression levels of GPX4 (a ferroptosis-suppressor) were more significantly reduced in SLC6A8 knockdown cells compared to control group, while the expression levels of ACSL4 (a ferroptosis-driver) in SLC6A8 knockdown cells exhibited opposite expression patterns (Fig. S1F). In summary, these data supported that inhibition of SLC6A8 strengthens ferroptosis in HCC.

Discussion

SLC6A8, one member of the sodium-dependent SLC6 transporter family, encodes CRT1, which stands as the sole known creatine transporter (Diep and Lipshutz 2025). This transporter mediates the cellular uptake of creatine phosphate, a high-energy compound that is essential for maintaining normal function in highly metabolic activities of tissues (Ostojic et al. 2025). SLC6A8 has been found to be upregulated in multiple cancer types, including NSCLC (Kuempers et al. 2024; Fan et al. 2022), CRC (Kurth et al. 2021), and breast cancer (Li et al. 2021), and associated with poor prognosis, implying its potential as a biomarker across malignancies. Nevertheless, the contribution of SLC6A8 to HCC is not yet fully understood. In the present study, SLC6A8 was notably elevated in HCC and associated with unfavorable outcomes. Further functional experiments implicated that knockdown of SLC6A8 inhibited HCC cell proliferation, migration, and in vivo tumor growth, supporting its potential as both a biomarker and therapeutic target in HCC. In the meantime, we introduced RGX-202 to further assess the effectiveness of pharmacologically inhibiting SLC6A8 in HCC. RGX-202 is a pioneering small-molecule inhibitor targeting SLC6A8. This inhibitor causes the depletion of intracellular phosphocreatine and ATP, ultimately inducing cell apoptosis (Kurth et al. 2018). RGX-202 has entered several clinical trials for CRC and shown objective antitumor activity with no observed dose-limiting toxicities in the relapsed/refractory CRC patients (Bendell et al. 2020; Hendifar et al. 2022). In our preclinical models of HCC, RGX-202 consistently exhibited anti-tumor efficacy, supporting further clinical evaluation in advanced HCC patients as a second-line or beyond treatment.

Ferroptosis, a novel modality of regulated cell death propelled by the accumulation of lipid peroxidation, potentially serves as a promising therapeutic approach for HCC. (Ajoolabady et al. 2023). Compared to other tumors, HCC cells exhibit heightened oxidative stress (Gensluckner et al. 2024), likely because of the unique metabolic features such as enhanced glycolysis (the Warburg effectYang et al. 2024b). Emerging reports have illustrated that creatine can reduce ferroptosis. In intestinal cells, ferroptosis could be attenuated by creatine via AMPK activation and subsequent phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (He et al. 2025). Creatine supplementation also reduces intracellular iron levels, oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thus restraining ferroptosis in ectopic endometrial stromal cells (Chen et al. 2024). In this study, knockdown of SLC6A8, inhibitor of creatine uptake, potentiated ferroptosis induced by RSL3 in HCC cells, and RGX-202 combined with RSL3 to enhance anti-tumor responses. Thus, co-targeting SLC6A8 and ferroptosis may represent an effective approach for HCC treatment.

Notably, TKIs recommended for advanced HCC as first-line therapies, including Sorafenib (Wang et al. 2024) and Lenvatinib (Chen et al. 2025), have been shown to induce ferroptosis in HCC. This provides a rationale for combining RGX-202 with Sorafenib and Lenvatinib in clinical settings. The initial clinical step would be a Phase Ib dose-escalation and expansion study of RGX-202 combined with either sorafenib or lenvatinib in patients with advanced, treatment-naïve HCC patients and incorporate prospective biomarker analysis like SLC6A8. This strategy also holds promise for overcoming TKI resistance and could serve as a foundation for future triplet regimens that include RGX202, TKI and immune checkpoint inhibitors.

However, our study is subject to limitations. The mechanisms underlying the upregulation of SLC6A8 in HCC were not elucidated. Further investigation is warranted to explore, including whether there is a transcriptional regulation by upstream regulatory factors or alterations in epigenetic modifications. The mechanism reported by He et al. shows that creatine activates AMPK to phosphorylate ACC, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and suppressing ferroptosis (He et al. 2025). Our study reveals that SLC6A8 downregulation blocked creatine-mediated ferroptosis inhibition and affected ferroptosis-related markers. Yet, how SLC6A8 mechanistically regulates ferroptosis in HCC has not been fully explored and merits further study.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrated that both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of SLC6A8 suppresses progression of HCC both in vitro and in vivo and sensitizes HCC cells to ferroptosis. This work provides a mechanistic foundation for evaluating RGX-202 or combination strategies with ferroptosis inducers such as sorafenib or Lenvatinib in advanced HCC.
