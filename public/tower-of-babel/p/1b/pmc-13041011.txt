Background

This case report documents marked QT prolongation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia temporally associated with NDP in a young woman with metastatic serous carcinoma. The uniqueness resides in the comprehensive electrocardiographic documentation of drug-induced long-QT syndrome with polymorphic ventricular tachycardia during NDP infusion. Clinicians should recognise QT prolongation as a rare but potentially fatal complication of anticancer therapy and adopt systematic ECG monitoring during nedaplatin-based regimens for early detection of malignant arrhythmias.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old Asian woman was referred to our institution with abdominal pain, diarrhoea and vomiting. She had experienced untreated, recurrent abdominal pain, diarrhoea and vomiting for ten years. The abdominal pain intensified and was accompanied by diarrhoea, vomiting and dyspnoea one year earlier; on one occasion she lost consciousness for 1–2 min.

The patient had no known comorbidities and no documented hypersensitivity to drugs or food. There was no history of illicit drug use, unusual dietary exposure or clinical evidence of an alternative gastrointestinal infection. Ten years earlier she had been diagnosed with hyperthyroidism. Following ¹¹³I ablation she developed hypothyroidism and has since received levothyroxine 50 µg once daily (Table 1). There was no personal history of cardiac disease and no familial history of malignant arrhythmia.

On admission, severe anaemia (Hb 64 g/L) was noted; electrolyte concentrations and serum albumin were within normal limits. The admission ECG revealed sinus rhythm at 84 bpm with a QTc of 444 ms calculated by Bazett’s formula (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced CT and PET-CT revealed multi-organ involvement, including the small-bowel wall of the lower abdomen, the rectouterine pouch, the peritoneum, and the abdominal lymph nodes. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy of the retroperitoneal mass was performed under local anaesthesia. The procedure was successful, and two tissue specimens were obtained for histopathological examination. Percutaneous biopsy confirmed high-grade serous carcinoma with extensive metastatic dissemination. She sequentially received PD-1 inhibitor (Tislelizuma, 14:56 − 16:17), paclitaxel (16:18 − 16:48) and NDP (commenced 16:52). Twenty minutes after NDP infusion began, she developed abdominal pain, palpitations (heart rate 234 bpm), nausea and hypotension (68/39 mmHg) (Table 2). ECG disclosed marked QT prolongation, polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and Mobitz type I second-degree atrioventricular block.

The differential diagnosis for her abdominal pain and palpitations included progressive serous carcinoma and drug-related hypersensitivity. Clinical history made acute tumour progression unlikely. Agents had been administered at staggered intervals, permitting temporal attribution. Continuous ambulatory ECG (Holter) recorded ST-segment depression, T-wave inversion, severe QT prolongation, polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and Mobitz type I second-degree atrioventricular block, all manifesting 20 min after NDP infusion. QT intervals spontaneously normalised and targeted genetic analysis excluded pathogenic variants associated with congenital long-QT syndrome.

We therefore consider NDP the most likely precipitant of both QT prolongation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. This attribution is supported by the temporal relationship: symptoms emerged 20 min after the first dose and resolved promptly upon discontinuation of the agent.

Advanced life-support measures were instituted immediately (Table 2), comprising intravenous adrenaline, dexamethasone, aggressive volume resuscitation and standard anti-anaphylaxis protocols. Sinus rhythm and QTc within normal limits were restored within 40 min; haemodynamic stability was achieved after 60 min. Serum calcium and troponin levels have normalized (Table 3).

During 48 hours of monitoring after withdrawal of the implicated agent and substitution with carboplatin, the patient remained asymptomatic. She was discharged home 7 days later with no clinical or electrocardiographic evidence of cardiac dysfunction. Monthly outpatient reviews have remained unremarkable, with persistent sinus rhythm and a QTc within normal limits; long-term surveillance is planned.

Discussion

NDP, a cisplatin analogue, exhibits potent antineoplastic activity and is widely used for solid malignancies including squamous-cell carcinoma, small-cell lung cancer and non-small-cell lung cancer [1]. Compared with cisplatin, NDP is associated with reduced nausea, vomiting and nephrotoxicity. Documented adverse effects include hypersensitivity reactions, myelosuppression and renal impairment [2]. Nevertheless, QTc prolongation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia have not previously been attributed to NDP [3].

Long-QT syndrome (LQTS) is a multifactorial disorder of cardiac ion channels characterised by QT-interval prolongation on the ECG and an increased risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmias that can precipitate syncope or sudden death. LQTS is classified as congenital or acquired; diagnostic thresholds are QTc ≤ 0.44 s in men and ≤ 0.46 s in women [4–7]. Congenital LQTS arises from pathogenic variants in genes encoding cardiac sodium, potassium or calcium channels. Mutations in KCNQ1, KCNH2 and SCN5A are well-established substrates for LQTS [8, 9]. With the exception of acute events, serum electrolytes (including potassium and sodium) remained within normal reference ranges.

Acquired LQTS may be precipitated by drugs, electrolyte derangements, bradyarrhythmias, hypothermia, toxins, endocrine disorders or systemic inflammation. Numerous agents, most notably antiarrhythmics, antimicrobials, antifungals, antipsychotics and antihistamines, impair ventricular repolarisation and thereby prolong the QT interval [8].

Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia is the archetypal life-threatening arrhythmia associated with QT prolongation [8]. Clinical presentation ranges from palpitations or syncope to seizures or sudden death; occasionally it is asymptomatic. Spontaneous termination may occur when runs are brief. Notably, QT prolongation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia appeared only after NDP initiation; no arrhythmic events were observed during the preceding 90-min infusion of PD-1 inhibitor and paclitaxel.

Hypocalcaemia prolongs the plateau phase of the cardiac action potential and thereby predisposes to ventricular arrhythmias [10]. Consequent prolongation of calcium-channel opening facilitates early after-depolarisations that may trigger tachycardia. Thus, ventricular arrhythmia is a recognised complication of hypocalcaemia. In our patient, serum calcium fell from 2.40 mmol/L to 1.60 mmol/L only after NDP infusion. Acute hypocalcaemia is typically transient, and the associated channel-open time is brief. We therefore postulate that hypocalcaemia was secondary to an acute hypersensitivity reaction to NDP.

Because NDP is considered safe, clinicians may underestimate its adverse effects, potentially delaying diagnosis and first-line management. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report attributing isolated QT prolongation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia to NDP. Official prescribing information for each agent administered contains no reference to QT-interval prolongation. PD-1 inhibitors are associated with hypersensitivity, electrolyte disturbances (hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia) and supraventricular arrhythmias, but not with QT prolongation. Paclitaxel may provoke hypersensitivity and non-specific arrhythmias, yet QT prolongation is undocumented. The regimen was consequently modified to PD-1 inhibitor, paclitaxel and carboplatin. This combination was well tolerated and no further adverse events occurred.

Several limitations of this case report should be acknowledged. First, plasma nedaplatin concentrations were not measured, precluding direct pharmacokinetic confirmation of exposure; magnesium levels were not assessed, obscuring the relationship between hypocalcemia, malignant arrhythmias, and magnesium homeostasis. Second, being a single-case observation, our findings require validation in larger cohorts before causality can be inferred. Third, ethical considerations prohibited rechallenge, so the de-challenge information remains the main pillar of causality assessment. Finally, the current follow-up duration is limited; consequently, the long-term cardiac safety of nedaplatin remains to be established.

Accordingly, this report adds to the safety literature by linking NDP with severe cardiac events including drug-induced LQTS and potentially lethal arrhythmias. We acknowledge that plasma nedaplatin concentrations were not measured, and thus direct pharmacokinetic confirmation is unavailable. Although plasma NDP concentrations were not available, exclusion of alternative aetiologies supports a causal association.

Conclusions

Although NDP is generally regarded as safe and effective, its use may be complicated by drug-induced long-QT syndrome and potentially fatal arrhythmias. We therefore recommend systematic ECG surveillance during NDP therapy, even in the absence of cardiac symptoms, because drug-induced QT prolongation can precipitate polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, cardiac arrest and sudden death.
