The experiment of the Mozart effect revisited: Modulations of the functional network according to EEG correlations☆
Authors: Dulce María Guerrero-Tánori, Antonieta Martínez-Guerrero, Markus F. Müller, Paola V. Olguín-Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Mozart effect has been the subject of lively but also controversial debate in literature during the last decades. Positive as well as negative results have been reported, using different musical stimuli as well as cognitive tasks. In addition, only a few studies included EEG recordings in the experimental design, and when they did, they focused exclusively on spectral power densities. A more complete experimental design is presented considering different stimuli as well as three different cognitive tasks including EEG measurements. Furthermore, we propose a novel strategy for the analysis of the functional network of brain dynamics, based on deviations from a universal stationary cross-correlation pattern.•An experimental design of the Mozart effect is presented, considering different cognitive tasks and two different musical stimuli, besides a control group•The presence of a stationary cross-correlation pattern that is nearly independent of the physilogical brain state is illustrated.•It is demostrasted that that deviations from the stationary pattern serve much better for the distinction of different task settings

Background

For several years, it has been speculated that music has effects on cognitive processes. In 1985, the article [8] was published, in which 60 boys and 60 girls between the ages of 9 and 14 participated. They were organized into three groups of 40 subjects each: (a) musically gifted children with the ability to compose/improvise, (b) musically gifted children without the ability to compose/improvise, and (c) a control group. The results suggest that musical ability is significantly related to spatial orientation in both sexes and to spatial visualization in boys.

In 1993, the article [1] entitled “Music and Spatial Task Performance”, was published. In this study, 36 students were recruited and divided into three groups: (a) subjects who listened to Mozart’s Sonata K.448, (b) subjects who listened to relaxing acoustic stimuli and, (c) a control group without music stimulation. Afterwards, all three groups were asked to complete the Paper Folding and Cutting subtest of the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scale, which measures spatial intelligence. The results showed that those who listened to Mozart obtained higher scores than those who only listened to relaxation techniques or simply remained in silence.

This increase in intelligence quotient due to musical stimulation was called the “Mozart Effect”, although this effect is not exclusively due to a particular piece composed by Mozart. In [9] the authors present a modification of the Mozart effect; after training on the tasks, the participants listened to Brahms' Hungarian Dance No 5 to compare their response to the task. The authors raise the question of whether certain pieces of music foster specific cognitive processes.

However, the effect was not considered long-lasting; rather, it was speculated to persist for approximately 10–15 min after exposure to the musical piece. Subsequently, many studies were published in which the original experiment was repeated, or in which experiments were conducted with different musical rhythms and different cognitive tasks. The results turned out as varied as the experimental designs. Positive as well as negative results have been reported for similar experiments to that of Rauscher, as well as for different musical stimuli and a variety of cognitive tasks. A comprehensive meta study can be found in the supplementary material of [10]. Besides, the study by Rauscher encountered vivid criticism, particularly their interpretation of the results [11].

Furthermore, the vast majority of experiments focused exclusively on participants' behavior and the quantitative results of cognitive tasks. The few papers included EEG measurements essentially focused on the power spectral densities in different frequency bands, but we are not aware of a study considering the functional network of brain dynamics during task performance.

Here we present an experimental routine that, including electroencephalography, probes two different acoustic stimuli and three qualitatively different cognitive tasks probing different features of intellectual performance. We further propose a novel idea to analyze modulations of the functional network that is based on dynamical system theory.

Hence, we propose an experimental protocol that integrates different elements used in former studies, namely different musical stimuli as well as three different cognitive tasks and, most important, we propose a novel strategy of the analysis of EEG recordings.

Method details

Psychological tests

To ensure that the results were homogeneous, all participants completed several neuropsychological assessments, namely the Shipley-2 Test, the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), the Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II), and the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. These psychological tests were standardized for the Mexican population.

Beck Anxiety Inventory. This inventory is used to measure the level of anxiety of a person up to one week prior. Each answer has a score between 0 and 3. All participants obtained mild or less [12].

Beck Depression Inventory. This inventory is used to measure the level of depression of a person up to one week prior. Each answer has a score between 0 and 3. All participants obtained mild or less [12].

Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. This inventory was used to assess handedness preferences by asking participants about their use of the right or left hand for various activities, ranging from writing to brushing their teeth. Responses were scored as −1 or −2 for left-hand use and +1 or +2 for right-hand use. These values were then converted into a laterality score ranging from −100 to +100 [13].

Shipley-2 test, version B. This test is used to assess an individual’s intelligence quotient (IQ), with a maximum administration time of 25 min. In this test, all participants scored 90 points, or above which corresponds to level average or above [13].

Experimental design

The 36 participants were Mexican male, between 21 and 30 years old, right-handed, with at least 12 years of schooling, and no history of neurodegenerative diseases or neurological disorders. Here we focus on male subjects only to homogenize the sample. The authors of [14] provided evidence that functional networks of acoustic information processing are different for men and women. Furthermore, neural activity is altered during the menstrual cycle [15] and in particular for the version of the Stroop test considered in the present study it was shown that the performance of females depends on the hormone cycle [16]. During the experiment, participants did not take any medications that affect the central nervous system.

The participants were divided into three groups of twelve and placed into groups designated randomly in the Mozart Group, Brahms Group or Control Group.

First, baseline brain electrical activity was recorded with two minutes of open eyes and closed eyes respectively. For the open eyes setting, participants were instructed to fixate on a cross displayed on the center of the screen. After a rest period, the Mozart group listens to Mozart's Sonata for Two Pianos in D major, K. 448, and the Brahms group listens to Brahms' Hungarian Dance No 5. All musical stimuli had the same duration of 515 s. It should be noted that participants in the control group also observed a fixation cross on the screen for the same duration as those with musical stimuli (silence condition). Tasks

The selected tasks were chosen not only for their ease of computerized administration, but also because they are well-established paradigms associated with different cognitive demands, including visuospatial processing, processing speed/visual perception, and executive attention. Their theoretical grounding and low motor requirements make them appropriate for evaluating whether the proposed EEG analysis can distinguish condition-dependent patterns across cognitive contexts while minimizing motor contamination in the recordings.

Symbol search: This task is part of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and it evaluates information processing speed and visual perception. It consists of 60 symbols that must be answered within a maximum of 120 s. The participant must decide whether one of the symbols shown on the left is in the list of symbols on the right, as shown in Fig. 1. The task ends when the 120 s are up. The symbols employed were sourced from the following webpage: https://wais-iv-symbol-search-f568d4.netlify.app/

The Paper Folding and Cutting: This task was included because it evaluated visuo-spatial performance and it was presented in the original experiment by Rauscher. This is part of the Stanford-Binet test. It consists of 9 exercises as shown in Fig. 1. At the top is a rectangle representing a piece of paper, which is then folded along the double lines and cut along the dotted lines. The participant must mentally visualize what it will look like after the folds and cuts, and then select the correct unfold pattern. This task is completed with a time limit of 40 s with a break of 3 s.

Stroop effect: The variation is called the Color-Word Matching Stroop Task, which is broadly considered as an executive attention task. This task is divided into two parts: training and the task itself and is completed without a time limit. In the first part, participants must decide whether the color of XXX at the top matches the word at the bottom. In the second part, they must decide whether the color of the word at the top matches the word at the bottom [20].

These tasks were programmed using Psychopy software version 2023.1.0 and presented on a ViewSonic VS18522 monitor approximately 50 cm from the participants' eyes. The tasks Symbol search and The Paper Folding and Cutting were completed using keypads built with an Arduino Leonardo board and connected to the computer via a USB microcontroller. This keypad had two buttons representing "YES" and "NO" (keypad 1) [21]. The third task was completed using a similarly constructed keypad (keypad 2) with five buttons representing "A", "B", "C", "D", and "E". Data acquisition

Electroencephalographic recordings were obtained using electrocap caps with tin-based conductive material. The international 10–20 system was employed, including electrodes Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, T3, T4, T5, T6, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, Fz, Cz, and Pz. In addition, two electrodes were used to monitor cardiac activity (EKG−, EKG+), and two electrodes were placed to record saccadic eye movements (V−, V+). The reference was set to A1 electrode, in addition the signal A2 was also recorded. All electrode impedances were maintained below 10kΩ. EEG signals were recorded using a NeuroScan LT system with a montage that included a 60 Hz notch filter and a sampling frequency of 250 Hz. Task onsets were marked using event markers corresponding to the actions performed by the participant, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

EEG preprocessing

At first, the EEG data were visually inspected and then Independent Components Analysis (ICA) was applied to remove eye movement and muscle artifacts using EEGLAB [17]. The EEG data was filtered in the range 1−35Hz using a 4th-order Butterworth and the after signals were re-reference to the median to minimize deformations of the functional network [18]. A total of 20 non-overlapping windows of 10 s each were analyzed. The full EEG segment was used without distinguishing between correct and incorrect responses.

Numerical analysis

The numerical analysis is based on the fundamental observation of a distinct, extremely stable cross-correlation pattern in EEG recordings that covers the entire scalp and is almost independent of the physiological state. This pattern was first reported to be present during the peri-ictal transition of focal onset seizures [2], thereafter in healthy subjects during sleep or adults with open and closed eyes [3]. It turns out that this pattern is universal in the sense that it is (almost) subject independent [2,3,5]. This permanent, strongly correlated activity was interpreted as the dynamics on (or close to) a stable attractor in phase space that contains all those dynamical states necessary to maintain the brain in an efficient operational mode. Using a simultaneous EEG-fMRI measurement it was shown in [4] that this pattern is equivalent to resting state networks. Using the tiny fluctuations around this correlation structure as a predictor for the General Linear Model of fMRI activity, the default mode, executive control and salience network as assessed by Independent Component Analysis were predicted with a specificity of over 95 %. Thus, the pronounced stationary EEG pattern and fMRI resting state networks are two different expressions of the same phenomenon.

Consequently, transient dynamics like e.g. the response to an external stimulus or cognitive processes should be encoded in deviations from the stable cross-correlation pattern. This hypothesis was confirmed for the peri-‑ictal transition of focal onset seizures, distinguishing pre-ictal, ictal and post-ictal states, different sleep stages of healthy subjects, open and closed eye conditions [3] as well as acoustic stimuli [19] and attentional processing to faces in children [7]. Possibly, changes of the functional network during cognitive processes are more subtle. Here we probe for the first time this hypothesis.

Hence, in the first step we estimate the average correlation matrix separately for each of the physiological states Cijps and for each subject. Then we represent the stationary correlation pattern SCPij as the average over all correlation matrices of all physiological states. In this article we consider 5 physiological conditions: resting state, listening to the music as well as the three cognitive tasks (Eq. (1)):(1)SCPij=15∑ps5Cijps.

Thereafter, we estimate the difference matrices Dijps=SCPij−Cijps separately for each condition. To confirm the presence of a stationary pattern we estimate the correlation between the different Cijps hypothesizing that structural differences are minor, which justifies the average over all conditions. To this end we sort the nondiagonal elements of Cijps in a sequence of numbers, which is then treated as a time series to estimate the Pearson coefficient between different Cijps. Thereafter we applied the same procedure to the difference matrices Dijps.

The Picture changes qualitatively when comparing the difference matrices for each task (Fig. 4, panels D, E, F). Now the Pearson coefficients are distributed over a much larger range (±0.6 setting without music, [−0.6,0.2] Mozart and [−0.8,0.4] Brahms). In this case there appear pronounced anticorrelations for the pairwise comparisons as well as coefficients close to zero, which clearly indicate a better distinction between the different task conditions, and thus confirming the hypothesis outlined above.

In summary, we confirmed the presence of a task and subject independent distinct stationary cross-correlation pattern covering the whole scalp. We further show that deviations from the stationary pattern are quite different for different settings and allow their discrimination. Both results speak in favor of the experimental design of our study and the novel proposal for data analysis.

Limitations

Only linear bivariate measures for data analysis are used. Univariate as well as nonlinear measures might be relevant. In addition, the use of musical stimuli is quite limited. It could be interesting to probe for e.g., different music genres as well. Another limitation of the present study is that we solely considering male subjects, for reasons already expressed in the main text.
