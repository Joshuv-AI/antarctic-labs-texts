Prognostic value of stress hyperglycemia ratio in critically Ill patients with acute kidney injury: a machine learning-driven retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Yichun Shuai, Yan Liu, Xiahong Yang, Zhe Chen, Qiaoqian Wan, Jie Zhao, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a serious complication in critically ill patients, contributing to high morbidity and mortality. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), defined as the ratio of admission blood glucose to estimated average glucose derived from HbA1c, has been previously linked to outcomes in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions, but its role in predicting short-term mortality in ICU patients with AKI remains unclear. This study explores the association between SHR and short-term mortality, and developed machine learning models to improve prognostic accuracy. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 5,555 ICU patients with AKI from the MIMIC-IV (v3.1) database. Patients were divided into SHR quartiles. Primary outcomes were 30- and 90-day all-cause mortality. The association between SHR and mortality was assessed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling, Cox regression, and Kaplan–Meier analysis. Feature selection was performed using the Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression. Twelve machine learning models were developed and systematically compared for 30-day mortality prediction. Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics, and the best-performing model was further assessed using decision curve analysis and calibration analysis. SHAP analysis was applied to interpret the contributions of individual features. Results: A U-shaped relationship was found between SHR and mortality, with higher SHR levels significantly increasing the risk of both 30-day and 90-day death (HR > 1, p < 0.01). Subgroup analyses confirmed SHR's predictive reliability across multiple populations. Among the models, LightGBM demonstrated the best overall predictive performance (AUC = 0.864), outperforming traditional ICU scoring systems. The model showed good calibration and a favorable net clinical benefit. Conclusion: SHR is an independent, nonlinear predictor of short-term mortality in ICU patients with AKI. Machine learning models incorporating SHR, especially LightGBM, significantly improve risk stratification, offering valuable support for early identification and personalized ICU management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13755-026-00443-0.Keywords: Stress hyperglycemia ratio, Acute kidney injury, Mortality, MIMIC-IV database, Boruta algorithm, LASSO regression, Machine learning

Introduction

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) is a prevalent and severe complication in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), characterized by a sudden decline in renal function, high morbidity, high mortality, and poor long-term prognosis [1, 2]. According to the Kidney disease: improving global outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, AKI is diagnosed based on an acute increase in serum creatinine levels or a decrease in urine output. Despite advances in critical care management, AKI remains a major clinical challenge due to its complex pathophysiology and multifactorial etiology. ICU patients are particularly susceptible to AKI due to hemodynamic instability, sepsis, ischemia–reperfusion injury, drug toxicity, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), all of which contribute to renal hypoperfusion, inflammation, and oxidative stress [3]. Previous studies have reported that the incidence of AKI among ICU patients varies between 20 and 50% [4, 5]. Alarmingly, even transient AKI episodes are not entirely reversible. Even after hospital discharge, patients remain at risk for long-term deterioration of renal function, potentially accelerating the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD), end-stage renal disease (ESKD), and CKD-related cardiovascular disease (CVD) [2, 6]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk AKI patients in the ICU, accurate prediction of their short and long-term prognosis, and individualized interventions are crucial.

Stress hyperglycemia has recently emerged as a prevalent metabolic disturbance among ICU patients, attracting growing clinical attention. It refers to a transient but significant elevation in blood glucose levels during acute illness, even in individuals without pre-existing diabetes. It is considered an adaptive response to physiological stress, mediated by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, sympathetic activation, insulin resistance, and increased oxidative stress [7]. Despite its clinical significance, conventional blood glucose measurements cannot distinguish between stress-induced hyperglycemia and pre-existing diabetes related hyperglycemia, limiting their utility in risk stratification. To address this issue, the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), an emerging metabolic index, has been developed. SHR is derived by normalizing blood glucose levels to glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), offering a more precise measure of hyperglycemia due to acute stress. This index has been increasingly utilized to evaluate the stress response and prognostic risk in critically ill patients [8]. An increasing body of evidence supports the clinical utility of SHR in predicting mortality among critically ill ICU patients, as well as in conditions such as cardiovascular disease, stroke, and sepsis [8–11]. However, while the association between SHR and these conditions has been explored, its specific relationship with AKI occurrence, progression, and prognosis remains unclear. Considering the severity of AKI as a complication in ICU patients and its profound impact on short- term outcomes, evaluating the predictive value of SHR in this context is of great importance. Therefore, this study sought to investigate the association between SHR and adverse outcomes in patients with AKI, and further to develop and compare multiple SHR-based machine learning models to improve the early identification of short-term mortality risk among ICU patients with AKI, thereby providing evidence for clinical risk stratification and individualized management.

Methods

Data source

Patient data for this retrospective analysis were obtained from MIMIC-IV (v3.1), a publicly available critical care database. The database was developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in partnership with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) and contains clinical data of patients from 2008 to 2019. The dataset comprises information on vital signs, laboratory test results, therapeutic procedures, diagnostic outcomes, and medication records [12]. Access to the database required certification from the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI), which was obtained by researcher Yichun Shuai.

Study population

A total of 61,741 patients diagnosed with acute kidney injury (AKI) were initially identified from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1). To ensure cohort homogeneity and minimize intra-individual correlation, the analysis was restricted to first ICU admissions, resulting in 41,761 eligible patients. The diagnosis of AKI was based on the KDIGO criteria[13]:

Stage 1: Serum creatinine (Scr) increase of 0.3 mg/dL (26.5 μmol/L) or 1.5–1.9 times from baseline, or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12 h.

Stage 2: Scr increase of 2.0–2.9 times from baseline, or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 12 h.

Stage 3: Scr increase of ≥ 3 times from baseline, ≥ 4.0 mg/dL (353.6 μmol/L), or requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT); or urine output < 0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥ 24 h, or anuria for ≥ 12 h.

The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Age < 18 years. (2) Missing blood glucose or HbA1c measurements in the first laboratory test. (3) Implausible survival time records. (4) ICU stay < 24 h. Following the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 5,555 patients were retained for the final analysis and categorized into four quartile-based SHR groups (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Data were extracted using PostgreSQL (version 17.2), and the variables obtained encompassed the following categories: (1) Baseline characteristics: age, gender, race, weight, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Charlson Comorbidity Index and AKI stage. (2) Interventions: mechanical ventilation therapy, renal replacement therapy (RRT), vasopressor therapy, and sedative therapy. (3) Comorbidities: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure (HF), hypertension, atrial fibrillation, stroke, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), renal disease, liver disease, and malignancy. (4) Vital signs: mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate, temperature, and respiratory rate. (5) Laboratory parameters: white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin, platelet, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, chloride, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), lactate, creatinine, pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PO₂), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO₂), BNP tested, troponin tested, Creatinine kinase tested. (6) Hospital and ICU-related data: admission and discharge dates, ICU admission and discharge dates, and date and time of death. All variables were collected within the first 24 h of hospital admission. SHR was derived using the following formula:

The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was calculated as the ratio of admission glucose to estimated average glucose (eAG). The eAG was derived from HbA1c using the formula: eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × HbA1c − 46.7, as proposed by the A1c-Derived Average Glucose study [14]. This study primarily focused on 30-day and 90-day mortality as the key clinical outcomes. All analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2. In this study, multiple imputation was performed using the MICE (Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations) package in R to handle missing data, with the number of imputations set to five. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching. Binary variables were imputed using logistic regression models, unordered categorical variables using multinomial logistic regression, and ordered categorical variables using proportional odds models. All variables included in the analysis were incorporated into the imputation model [15].

Statistical analysis and modeling

According to SHR quartiles, baseline variables were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using different statistical methods: the chi-square test for categorical variables, one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed data. To compare groups, categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, normally distributed continuous variables with one-way ANOVA, and non-normally distributed variables with the Kruskal–Wallis H test. The algorithm evaluates the relevance of original features by contrasting their importance with that of randomly created shadow features, thereby identifying the most informative predictors of the target variable. Features with significantly higher importance than shadow features were classified as Confirmed. Features with lower importance than all shadow features were classified as Rejected. Features with intermediate importance remained in a Tentative state and required further evaluation [16]. The selected variables were incorporated into Cox proportional hazards regression models to assess the impact of SHR on 30-day and 90-day mortality. Three adjustment models were developed:

Model 1: No covariates were adjusted.

Model 2: Adjusted for age, gender, weight, COPD, CAD, HF, hypertension, stroke, renal disease, liver disease, and malignancy.

Model 3: Further adjusted based on Model II by incorporating SOFA score, SAPS II, Charlson comorbidity index, MV therapy, Vasopressor therapy, Sedative therapy, MAP, Heart rate, Temperature, Respiratory rate, WBC, Hemoglobin, Platelet, Sodium, Potassium, Bicarbonate, Chloride, BUN, Lactate, Creatinine, pH, PO2, PCO2, BNP tested, Troponin tested, Creatinine kinase tested and AKI stage. In addition, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of SHR on mortality in AKI patients, and differences between groups were examined using the log-rank test. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model with four knots was used to explore the nonlinear association between SHR and mortality. We also conducted subgroup analyses to assess the effect of SHR across subgroups defined by age, sex, vasopressor therapy, T2DM, and hypertension. The interaction effects of SHR were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests and interaction term analyses.

Machine learning modeling

To enhance predictive accuracy, this study employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression for feature selection in machine learning models. LASSO is a regularized regression technique used for feature selection and overfitting prevention. Its core mechanism is L1-norm regularization, which automatically selects the most relevant variables by shrinking some regression coefficients to zero. The variables identified through feature selection were then incorporated into machine learning models for subsequent analysis. A random split of the dataset was performed, allocating 70% for training and 30% for validation. This study applied 11 different machine learning models to estimate 30-day mortality risk in AKI patients, including: Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost, Extra Trees, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Neural Network, Naive Bayes. To ensure model stability, tenfold cross-validation was performed to evaluate performance, and hyperparameter tuning was conducted during model training. In addition, the predictive performance of different machine learning models for 30-day mortality in AKI patients was evaluated using multiple metrics. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), recall, and F-beta score were calculated for each model in the validation set. Based on these metrics, a radar plot was constructed to visually compare model performance across multiple evaluation dimensions. The model demonstrating the best overall and most balanced performance was selected for subsequent analyses. After identifying the optimal model, its clinical utility and predictive reliability were further assessed. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to examine the net benefit across different risk thresholds, and calibration curves were generated to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes in the validation cohort. To enhance the interpretability of the machine learning models, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was applied in this study. SHAP quantifies the importance of each feature to the model's predictions by calculating its marginal contribution for individual samples, based on the Shapley value framework from game theory. We first ranked the input features according to their SHAP values and generated a feature importance plot to visually demonstrate the average impact of each variable on the prediction outcomes. Subsequently, a SHAP summary plot was used to further illustrate how variations in feature values influence the predicted risk [17]. After model development and validation, the best-performing LightGBM model was deployed as a web-based interactive application using the Shiny framework in R. The interface allows users to enter relevant clinical variables and generate real-time predictions of 30-day mortality risk.

Result

Baseline characteristics

Among 61,741 patients diagnosed with AKI in the MIMIC-IV database, 41,761 were retained after restricting the analysis to first ICU admissions. After excluding patients with missing glucose data (n = 9) or HbA1c data (n = 35,824), abnormal survival time (n = 4), and ICU length of stay less than 24 h (n = 369), a total of 5,555 patients were ultimately included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The mean age of the study population was 68 years, with 2,179 women and 3,376 men. Based on SHR quartiles, patients were categorized into four groups: Q1 (SHR < 0.9, n = 1,335), Q2 (0.9 ≤ SHR < 1.09, n = 1,393), Q3 (1.09 ≤ SHR < 1.33, n = 1,416), and Q4 (SHR ≥ 1.33, n = 1,411).

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are presented in Tables 1, 2, 3. Patients in the Q4 group exhibited significantly worse disease severity, greater need for therapeutic interventions, higher comorbidity burden, and poorer clinical outcomes compared to the other groups. Specifically, the SOFA score (5.28) and SAPS II score (37.76) were significantly higher in the Q4 group, indicating a more critical condition. As shown in Table 1, the distribution of AKI stages differed significantly across SHR quartiles (p < 0.01). The fourth quartile (Q4) group exhibited the highest proportion of patients with AKI stage 3 (24.81%), significantly higher than that in the Q1 group (20.97%). Conversely, patients with AKI stage 1 accounted for the lowest proportion (24.81%) in the Q4 group. These findings suggest that elevated SHR levels may be associated not only with mortality risk but also with increased AKI severity. Regarding therapeutic interventions, the rates of mechanical ventilation therapy, renal replacement therapy, vasopressor therapy, and sedative therapy were significantly higher in the Q4 group, reflecting an increased demand for intensive treatment. Similarly, in terms of comorbidities and underlying diseases, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus, heart failure, renal disease, and liver disease was markedly higher in Q4 than in the other groups, suggesting a greater systemic disease burden in patients with higher SHR levels. For laboratory parameters, patients in the Q4 group exhibited higher white blood cell counts, serum creatinine, and lactate levels, further suggesting that they may suffer from more severe inflammatory and metabolic disorders. Regarding clinical prognosis, the Q4 group demonstrated notably higher mortality rates at both 30 days (20.06%) and 90 days (24.03%) relative to the lower SHR quartiles, supporting the association between high SHR and elevated short-term mortality.

Clinical outcomes

In this study, the Boruta algorithm was employed for feature selection to identify key variables influencing the clinical outcomes of patients with AKI (Fig. 2). Green-labeled features represent important variables identified by the Boruta algorithm, orange-labeled features indicate potentially relevant variables, and red-labeled features are considered to have an insignificant contribution to model predictions. To gain deeper insight into the link between SHR and clinical outcomes in AKI patients, we performed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis (Table 4). Cox regression analysis demonstrated that patients in the Q4 group (SHR ≥ 1.33) had a significantly higher risk of mortality at both 30-day and 90-day follow-ups, using the Q1 group (SHR < 0.9) as the reference. This association remained statistically significant after adjusting for multiple confounders (HR > 1, p < 0.01). To further explore the impact of AKI severity on the relationship between SHR and mortality, patients were stratified according to the KDIGO staging criteria, and separate multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed for each stage (Table 5). Among patients with AKI stage 1, those in the fourth quartile (Q4) of SHR had a significantly higher risk of 30-day mortality compared to those in the first quartile (Q1) (HR = 3.73, 95% CI: 1.88–7.39, p < 0.001). The risk of 90-day mortality was also significantly elevated in Q4 (HR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.21–3.60, p = 0.008). In patients with AKI stage 2, SHR in Q4 remained significantly associated with both 30 day and 90 day mortality. This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for confounding factors in Model 3, suggesting that SHR has a stable and independent predictive value in patients with moderate AKI. However, in patients with AKI stage 3, although SHR in Q4 was significantly associated with mortality risk in the univariate and partially adjusted models (Model 1 and Model 2), the association was no longer statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 3). This may suggest that mortality in severe AKI is influenced by more complex clinical factors, which may confound or attenuate the independent predictive value of SHR. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to assess the impact of SHR on 30-day and 90-day mortality among patients with AKI (Fig. 3). The results demonstrated that the mortality rate in patients with higher SHR (Q4 group) increased significantly over time, and their survival curves exhibited a marked downward trend, indicating that higher SHR was closely associated with poorer survival outcomes.

Restricted cubic spline

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted to explore the nonlinear association between SHR and mortality risk in AKI patients, with adjustments made for all covariates included in multivariable Cox regression Model 3. The analysis demonstrated that the RCS curves for both 30-day and 90-day mortality (Fig. 4) revealed a significant U-shaped association between SHR and the risk of mortality (nonlinear p < 0.01). When SHR was approximately 1.09, the mortality risk ratio was close to 1. The risk of mortality increased significantly with rising SHR levels, supporting the notion that higher SHR may be linked to worse outcomes.

Restricted cubic spline analysis of the association between SHR and 30-Day and 90-day mortality (A all-cause death in 30 days, B all-cause death in 90 days, the vertical dashed line denotes the reference SHR value (1.09), at which the hazard ratio was set to 1.0)

Subgroup analysis

In this study, Fig. 5 illustrates the relationship between SHR and 30- and 90-day mortality across subgroups stratified by age, sex, vasopressor administration, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension. In the overall analysis, the Q4 group (SHR ≥ 1.33) was significantly associated with both 30 day and 90 day mortality (HR > 1, p < 0.001). Further subgroup analyses demonstrated that SHR in the Q4 group was consistently and strongly associated with an increased risk of mortality across all subgroups, regardless of patient age (< 65 years vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (male vs. female), treatment with vasoactive medications, or the presence of type 2 diabetes mellitus or hypertension. This association remained statistically significant in all subgroups (HR > 1, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that higher SHR levels are strongly associated with an increased risk of 30-day and 90-day mortality in patients with AKI, regardless of baseline characteristics. Additionally, a statistically significant interaction between age, vasopressor therapy and SHR was observed in the subgroup analysis of 90-day mortality. The results of this study further support SHR as an independent predictor of mortality risk in patients with AKI and highlight its broad applicability across diverse patient populations with varying baseline characteristics.

Machine learning

In this study, we assessed the performance of different models in predicting 30-day mortality risk in AKI patients using multiple machine learning methods and compared their predictive performance using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves (Fig. 7). The results demonstrated that machine learning-based predictive models significantly outperformed traditional scoring systems (SOFA score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and SAPS II) in overall predictive accuracy. To further compare the predictive performance of different models, multiple metrics were evaluated in addition to receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, including accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), recall, and F-beta score (Supplementary Table 1). A radar plot was constructed to visually compare model performance across these metrics (Fig. 8). LightGBM demonstrated relatively balanced performance across AUC, accuracy, PPV, and F-beta score, indicating strong discrimination and predictive capability. Based on these results, LightGBM was selected for subsequent analyses. Calibration curves were generated to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Fig. 9). Both the apparent and bootstrap-corrected curves were close to the ideal reference line, particularly within the low- to intermediate-risk range, indicating good calibration for 30-day mortality prediction. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the LightGBM model across different risk thresholds (Fig. 9). Within a threshold probability range of 0.1–0.5, the model provided greater net benefit than the treat-all and treat-none strategies, supporting its potential clinical utility in this range.

Model interpretation using SHAP

To interpret the prediction mechanism of the LightGBM model for 30-day mortality in critically ill patients with AKI, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis was performed. The SHAP feature importance ranking is presented in Fig. 10. The Charlson Comorbidity Index, SAPS II score, and stroke were identified as the most influential predictors, followed by stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), weight, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and AKI stage 3. Notably, SHR ranked among the top features, highlighting its contribution to mortality prediction. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 10) further illustrates the direction and magnitude of each feature’s contribution to the model output. Higher SHR values were associated with positive SHAP values, corresponding to an increased predicted risk of mortality. Conversely, lower SHR values were primarily located in the negative SHAP value range, indicating a reduced predicted risk.

Clinical application via web deployment of the LightGBM model

Based on the final LightGBM model, a web-based interactive interface was developed using the Shiny framework in R to demonstrate its application in predicting 30-day mortality among critically ill patients with AKI (Supplementary Fig. 1). Users can input SHR and other relevant clinical variables to automatically generate individualized mortality risk predictions. The web application is available at: https://hosdelaypredict.shinyapps.io/ICUAKI/ (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

Association between SHR and mortality in AKI patients

This retrospective study demonstrated that SHR is an independent predictor of 30 and 90 day mortality in ICU patients with AKI. Notably, SHR demonstrated a U-shaped association with mortality, with values exceeding 1.09 significantly increasing hazard ratio for mortality. These findings support the role of SHR as a potential biomarker for predicting short-term mortality in individuals with AKI. This study further performed a stratified analysis of AKI patients based on KDIGO staging and found that the positive association between SHR and short-term mortality was most pronounced in patients with AKI stages 1 and 2. Particularly in AKI stage 1, SHR in Q4 was significantly associated with increased risks of both 30-day and 90-day mortality, suggesting that SHR may have a substantial impact on prognosis even in the early stages of AKI. In contrast, in patients with AKI stage 3, the association between SHR and mortality was no longer statistically significant after full adjustment for confounding factors, implying that mortality in severe AKI may be influenced by more complex clinical variables. This finding highlights the importance of early identification of elevated SHR, particularly in patients with mild to moderate AKI, where timely intervention may offer greater prognostic benefits. To further evaluate its clinical utility, we assessed SHR’s prognostic robustness across various subgroups, including age, gender, vasopressor therapy, T2DM, and hypertension. The results demonstrated SHR’s broad applicability in ICU patients, reinforcing its relevance across diverse clinical populations. Moreover, a machine learning model incorporating SHR outperformed traditional scoring systems in mortality risk prediction, suggesting that SHR enhances predictive accuracy and facilitates more precise risk stratification. Despite the strong predictive performance of the machine learning models in this study, their limited interpretability may hinder widespread clinical adoption. To address this limitation, SHAP analysis was applied to provide an in-depth interpretation of the predictions generated by the LightGBM model. The results indicated that SHR is among the most influential variables contributing to mortality prediction. Collectively, these findings support SHR as a valuable biomarker for early risk assessment and individualized AKI management in ICUs.

Comparison with previous studies

Traditionally, blood glucose measurements in critically ill patients have relied on arterial blood gas (ABG) testing, which does not adequately account for baseline glycemic status, potentially limiting its predictive accuracy. In contrast, SHR, as a novel marker of stress hyperglycemia, adjusts for HbA1c levels, thereby providing a more accurate measure of acute hyperglycemia [18].

SHR has demonstrated significant prognostic value in various critical illnesses, including sepsis, stroke, and cardiovascular disease. In a multicenter study of 7,662 patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), Zeng et al. (2023) reported that An elevated SHR was significantly linked to a higher risk of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, and unplanned revascularization, supporting SHR as an independent predictor of long-term prognosis in ACS patients [19]. Similarly, in a MIMIC-IV-based study, a higher SHR was linked to increased 30- and 90-day all-cause mortality in patients with acute ischemic stroke, with a stronger correlation observed in non-diabetic patients [20]. Furthermore, an analysis of 1,835 septic patients from Ruijin Hospital and Xinhua Hospital (Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine) identified SHR as an independent predictor of in-hospital, 30-day, and 60-day mortality, further substantiating its clinical value in critically ill patients [21].

Despite the extensive study of SHR in ACS, stroke, and sepsis, its predictive value in AKI patients remains unclear. This study provides the first evidence linking SHR to mortality outcomes in ICU patients with AKI, identifying SHR as an independent predictor of short-term death risk. Additionally, using the Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression for feature selection, we confirmed that SHR plays a significant role in predicting 30-day mortality in AKI patients. Consistent with our findings, Yavuz et al. (2023) reported in a prospective observational study that daily blood glucose variability exceeding 70 mg/dL was significantly associated with mortality in ICU patients. They found that greater blood glucose variability had an even greater impact on patient outcomes than sustained hyperglycemia alone. This finding suggests that acute stress-induced glycemic instability may be a key factor influencing prognosis in critically ill patients [22]. Additionally, in a study involving patients with in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), Visneci et al. (2025) identified the glucose-to-potassium ratio (GPR) as an independent predictor of 30-day mortality. Research indicates that patients with IHCA experience significant alterations in blood glucose and potassium levels due to the intense stress responses triggered by hypoxia and ischemia [23]. This finding further demonstrates that metabolic dysregulation under stress conditions, particularly imbalances in glucose metabolism, serves as a significant predictor of mortality risk in critically ill patients. Furthermore, machine learning-based predictive models incorporating SHR outperformed traditional ICU scoring systems in mortality risk prediction. Our results indicate that SHR could serve as a useful marker for early risk stratification, potentially contributing to the development of personalized management strategies in ICU patients with AKI.

Potential mechanisms

Although this study identified the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) as an independent predictor of short-term mortality in patients with AKI, the underlying biological mechanisms remain speculative due to the retrospective design. However, based on our findings and previous literature, SHR may reflect a complex interplay of stress-related pathophysiological processes that adversely affect patient outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that critically ill patients often exhibit hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation, resulting in increased secretion of cortisol, epinephrine, and glucagon, which promote gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance, ultimately leading to stress hyperglycemia [24].

In a hyperglycemic state, excessive glucose uptake by renal tubular cells leads to mitochondrial overactivation and increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately inducing apoptosis and necrosis of renal tubular cells [25, 26]. Hyperglycemia also triggers endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, leading to protein misfolding and aggregation, which increases cellular burden. Prolonged ER stress exacerbates renal tubular injury by activating apoptotic pathways through CHOP and JNK signaling [27–29].

Additionally, in a high-glucose environment, glucose undergoes non-enzymatic glycosylation with proteins, lipids, and amino acids, forming early glycation products that undergo oxidation and rearrangement to generate advanced glycation end products (AGEs). AGEs accumulate in tissues, promoting endothelial dysfunction, and their receptor (RAGE) is often upregulated in AKI patients. This interaction amplifies inflammatory responses and exacerbates renal injury through RAGE signaling activation [30, 31].

Hyperglycemia also enhances inflammatory responses by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, increasing the expression of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which promote tubulointerstitial fibrosis and exacerbate renal tissue damage [32, 33].

The kidney is highly sensitive to microcirculatory damage, and hyperglycemia impairs endothelial barrier function, resulting in tubulointerstitial edema and increased pressure load. Additionally, hyperglycemia inhibits nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, promoting vasoconstriction and reducing renal blood perfusion, ultimately placing the renal tubules in a hypoxic state and increasing the risk of ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) [34, 35]. Patients with stress hyperglycemia also exhibit a higher propensity for thrombosis, which may lead to microvascular embolism, further exacerbating renal ischemia–reperfusion injury [36, 37].

In summary, elevated SHR may contribute to the progression of AKI through multiple mechanisms, including metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and microcirculatory dysfunction, thereby adversely impacting patient prognosis.

Clinical applicability and interpretability of machine learning model

In this study, SHR was incorporated into multiple machine learning models for risk prediction. Among these models, LightGBM demonstrated balanced performance in discrimination, calibration, and clinical decision analysis, and showed better overall predictive performance than traditional ICU scoring systems. This difference may be related to the ability of machine learning approaches to integrate multiple clinical variables and capture nonlinear relationships among them. In contrast, conventional scoring systems generally rely on fixed weighting schemes or linear assumptions and may therefore be limited in representing complex clinical conditions. Notably, SHAP analysis showed that SHR ranked among the top features in the model and that higher SHR values were generally associated with increased predicted mortality risk. This finding is consistent with our regression results and suggests that SHR maintains a relatively stable predictive contribution even within a multivariable and complex modeling framework. To enhance clinical applicability, the LightGBM model was deployed as a web-based tool, allowing clinicians to generate individualized risk predictions without the need for specialized computational or programming expertise.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective cohort study based on the MIMIC-IV database, unmeasured confounders may still be present despite adjustments for multiple variables and the application of machine learning-based feature selection. Second, retrospective studies inherently carry the risk of selection and information bias, which cannot be entirely eliminated. Therefore, causal inferences require further validation through prospective cohort studies or randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Finally, although the MIMIC-IV database includes a large ICU patient cohort, most data were derived from a single medical center. Future studies should validate the applicability of SHR in AKI prognosis using larger, multicenter datasets to enhance its clinical utility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically examined the association between the SHR and short-term mortality in ICU patients with AKI, establishing SHR as an independent predictor of 30- and 90-day mortality. The study also identified a U-shaped relationship between SHR and mortality, with a significantly increased risk of death observed when SHR exceeded 1.09. Subgroup analysis confirmed the predictive stability of SHR across various patient subgroups, regardless of age, sex, vasopressor therapy, T2DM, or hypertension. In addition, stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was incorporated into multiple machine learning approaches for 30-day mortality prediction in critically ill patients with AKI. Following model comparison, LightGBM demonstrated balanced performance in discrimination, stability, and clinical utility. Higher SHR values were associated with increased predicted mortality risk. The integration of SHR into the prediction framework may enhance short-term mortality risk identification and serve as a complement to existing clinical risk stratification tools. While our results are compelling, large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are still warranted to validate the prognostic value of SHR in AKI and assess its potential in guiding timely interventions in the ICU.
