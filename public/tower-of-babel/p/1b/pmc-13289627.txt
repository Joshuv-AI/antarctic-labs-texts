Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Nikhil Choudhary, Naveen Seervi, Amita Bhargava, Shubhakaran Khichar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesEmerging evidence indicates a complex relationship between glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and motor symptom severity in Parkinson’s disease. Both elevated (>6.1%) and low (<5.3%) HbA1c have been linked to increased disability. This study examined the association between HbA1c levels and motor symptoms in patients with Parkinson’s disease from Jodhpur, India.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted at the department of Neurology, Mathura Das Mathur Hospital, Jodhpur between March 2023 and February 2024. One hundred patients with Parkinson’s disease patients fulfilling the UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank (UKPDSBB) criteria were enrolled. Participants underwent HbA1c testing, motor evaluation with the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS III) in ON and OFF states, and cognitive screening with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Patients were categorised into three groups based on HbA1c: ≤4.9% (Low), 5.0–5.9% (Normal-to-Prediabetic), and ≥6.0% (High). Disease duration and medication history were also recorded.ResultsNo significant group differences were found for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), or disease duration, between the three groups, based on HbA1c levels. UPDRS and MoCA scores differed significantly across HbA1c categories (P<0.001). Higher HbA1c strongly correlated with worse motor outcomes (UPDRS ON: Spearman’s ρ=0.90; OFF: ρ=0.84) and poorer cognition (MoCA: ρ= –0.79).Interpretation and conclusionsElevated HbA1c levels were significantly associated with greater motor impairment and cognitive dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease. These findings highlight the potential role of metabolic dysregulation in progression of Parkinson’s disease and underscore the need for longitudinal studies to clarify causal links and therapeutic implications.

Parkinson’s disease is a neurodegenerative disorder characterised by dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra, resulting in bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, and postural instability.1 Emerging evidence links metabolic dysregulation, particularly hyperglycaemia, to Parkinson’s disease progression through oxidative stress, inflammation, and microglial activation.2,3 Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) reflects average blood glucose levels over 2–3 months and serves as a stable marker of glycaemic control. Previous studies have reported that HbA1c levels >6.1% associate with higher Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) scores, whereas levels <5.3% may increase the risk of reaching Hoehn and Yahr stage 3.4-6 A cross-sectional study also demonstrated that elevated HbA1c correlates with greater motor impairment on the Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III.7 Impaired glucose tolerance in Parkinson’s disease may reflect underlying dopaminergic dysfunction.8

No study has examined the association between HbA1c and motor symptom severity in Indian patients with Parkinson’s disease, a population with high diabetes prevalence and considerable genetic diversity. This study aimed to evaluate HbA1c level in patients with Parkinson’s disease from Northern India and determine its association with motor outcomes assessed by UPDRS Part III in both ON and OFF medication states, and cognitive performance evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).

Methods

Study design and Settings

We conducted a hospital-based cross-sectional study between March 2023 and February 2024 in the department of Neurology, Mathuradas Mathur (MDM) Hospital, Jodhpur, a tertiary care teaching hospital affiliated with Dr. S.N. Medical College, Jodhpur, Rajasthan, India. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Dr. S.N. Medical College, Jodhpur. All participants provided written informed consent after being informed of the study’s purpose, and their right to withdraw. Patients were recruited consecutively from the neurology outpatient clinic during routine clinical consultations.

Participants and eligibility criteria

We enrolled 100 patients diagnosed with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease based on the full UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank criteria, including mandatory features (bradykinesia + tremor/rigidity/postural instability), exclusion criteria (e.g., history of repeated strokes, cerebellar signs, supranuclear gaze palsy, early severe dementia, exposure to dopamine-blocking drugs), and supportive criteria (e.g., unilateral onset, persistent asymmetry, excellent levodopa response, levodopa-induced dyskinesias, progression of symptoms).9

Demographics

Data including age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) were recorded using a structured clinical proforma. Retrospective data on disease duration (from symptom onset and first diagnosis) and detailed medication histories, including specific drug types (e.g., levodopa, dopamine agonists, metformin, monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) inhibitors and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) inhibitors) were also collected for all participants to account for potential confounding effects.

Clinical and cognitive assessment

Motor severity was assessed using UPDRS Part III in both ‘ON’ (2 hr post-levodopa dose) and ‘OFF’ (12 h post-medication withdrawal) states by a trained neurology resident to evaluate treatment effects and baseline function. Cognitive function was assessed using the validated Hindi version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA 7.1). The standard education adjustment of +1 point was applied for participants with ≤12 years of formal education, as per MoCA guidelines.10 Based on prior Parkinson’s disease studies, patients were categorised by HbA1c levels: ≤4.9% (low), 5.0–5.9% (normal-to-prediabetic), and ≥6.0% (high).5 We collected demographic data (age, gender, BMI), disease duration, and detailed medication histories, including levodopa, dopamine agonists, metformin, and other drugs, to adjust for potential confounders.

Biochemical assessment

HbA1c was measured via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using the Bio-Rad D-100 System at a certified clinical laboratory.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated a priori to detect a correlation between HbA1c and UPDRS motor scores. The only prior study reporting this association,11 found a significant positive correlation but did not report the exact coefficient. We therefore conservatively assumed a medium-to-large effect size of ρ = 0.50. Using the standard formula for a two-tailed test of a single correlation12 with α=0.05 and power of 80%, this yielded n ≥ 38.We planned to recruit 100 patients to (i) allow stable multivariable regression with up to 12 covariates, (ii) ensure ≥10 patients in the low-HbA1c group (≤4.9%), and (iii) achieve >99% power for the observed correlations (ρ=0.84–0.90). Post-hoc power with n=100 and the observed ρ=0.90 exceeded 99.99 % (G*Power 3.1.9.7).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Jamovi (Version 2.6; Sydney Australia). No data were missing. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Normally distributed variables (e.g., age, BMI) are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD), non-normal variables (e.g., HbA1c, UPDRS) as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA for parametric data, Kruskal-Wallis tests for non-parametric data, and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Bivariate associations between HbA1c and clinical outcomes (UPDRS ON, UPDRS OFF, MoCA) were analysed using Spearman’s correlation because these variables showed non-normal distributions (P<0.05). Multivariable linear regression was then applied to determine the independent association of HbA1c with motor and cognitive outcomes after adjusting for age, BMI, disease duration, diabetes status, and medication use (levodopa, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors, and multivitamins). Despite mild deviations from normality, regression analysis was justified as it is robust to moderate non-normality with larger samples such as the present study (n=100). Residual and standardised residual plots confirmed approximate normality and homoscedasticity, supporting the validity of the models. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study included 100 patients with Parkinson’s disease (71% male, mean age 66.0±4.5 years, mean BMI 23.8±3.5 kg/m2, mean disease duration 5.5±2.5 years). When stratified by HbA1c into low (≤4.9%, n=10), normal-to-prediabetic (5.0–5.9%, n=28), and high (≥6.0%, n=62) groups, no significant differences were observed in age (P = 0.982), sex distribution (P=0.136), BMI (P = 0.953), or disease duration (P=0.786). The median HbA1c levels were 4.76% [Interquartile range (IQR) 4.33–4.90] in the low HbA1c group (≤4.9%, n=10), 5.44% (IQR 5.32–5.60) in the normal-to-prediabetic group (5.0–5.9%, n=28), and 7.38% (IQR 6.89–7.90) in the high HbA1c group (≥6.0%, n=62). All 16 patients with type 2 diabetes were in the high HbA1c group, underscoring the overlap between elevated HbA1c and overt diabetes (P<0.001).

Motor severity, assessed using the UPDRS III, was significantly higher in patients with elevated HbA1c. In the ON state, the low HbA1c group had the lowest scores (median 40, IQR 39–44), followed by the normal-to-prediabetic group (median 47, IQR 44–50), while the high HbA1c group had substantially higher scores (median 61, IQR 58–67; P<0.001). A similar gradient was observed in the OFF state, where median scores rose from 44 (IQR 36–52) in the low HbA1c group to 54 (IQR 47–58) in the normal-to-prediabetic group, and 68 (IQR 62–73) in the high HbA1c group (P<0.001).

Cognitive performance, assessed using MoCA, declined progressively with increasing HbA1c. Patients in the low HbA1c group had the best cognitive scores (median 14, IQR 13–16), followed by those in the normal-to-prediabetic range (median 13, IQR 11–15), whereas patients in the high HbA1c group demonstrated the poorest cognition (median 7, IQR 5–10; P<0.001).

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s correlations revealed strong positive associations between HbA1c and UPDRS ON (ρ=0.90, P<0.001) and OFF (ρ=0.84, P<0.001) scores, indicating greater motor impairment with elevated HbA1c. A strong negative correlation was observed with MoCA scores (ρ= –0.79, P<0.001), suggesting worse cognitive performance ( Figs. 1A and B, 2A and B, and 3A and B). Outlier assessment was performed through visual inspection of scatterplots and by examining standardised residuals and Cook’s distance values. All standardised residuals were within ±3 SD and Cook’s distance values were <1, confirming the absence of influential outliers.

Regression analysis

To determine whether HbA1c levels independently predict motor and cognitive outcomes beyond the influence of potential confounding variables, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted. While initial bivariate correlations (e.g., Spearman’s ρ) demonstrated significant associations between HbA1c and clinical outcomes, these unadjusted correlations do not account for other relevant patient factors such as diabetes status, medication use, BMI, or disease duration. Therefore, multivariate models were employed to isolate the unique contribution of HbA1c to both motor (UPDRS ON and OFF) and cognitive (MoCA) outcomes, while controlling for these potential covariates.

Motor outcomes (UPDRS)

Higher HbA1c levels were independently associated with worse motor outcomes. In the model predicting UPDRS ON scores, HbA1c emerged as a significant predictor (β=0.86, 95 % CI: 0.77, 0.96, P<0.001). Similarly, in the model predicting UPDRS OFF scores, HbA1c was significantly associated (β=1.05, 95% CI: 0.83, 1.27, P<0.001). In addition, diabetes status was significant for UPDRS ON (β=–0.31, 95% CI: –0.57, –0.05, P=0.018), indicating that non-diabetic patients had better motor performance. Moreover, multivitamin use was significantly associated with lower UPDRS OFF scores (β= –0.34, 95% CI: –0.62, –0.06, P=0.016). The overall model fit was strong, with R2=0.835 for UPDRS ON and R2=0.741 for UPDRS OFF (Table).

Cognitive outcome (MoCA)

Higher HbA1c levels were independently associated with lower cognitive performance as measured by the MoCA (β=–0.72, 95% CI: –0.96, –0.48, P<0.001). No other covariates (age, BMI, Parkinson’s disease duration, or medication use) were significantly related to MoCA scores. The model accounted for 38.0% of the variance in MoCA scores (R2=0.380; Table).

Medication history

Detailed medication histories were obtained for all participants. The most commonly used Parkinson’s disease medications were Levodopa/Carbidopa (administered to 98% of patients), followed by Pramipexole (45%), Ropinirole (38%), Rasagiline (25%), Selegiline (15%), and Entacapone (10%). All participants with type 2 diabetes were receiving metformin therapy. None of the patients in the low or normal-to-prediabetic HbA1c groups had diabetes or were on any anti-diabetic medications. On average, patients were taking 2.5±1.0 additional medications, including Amlodipine (used by 40% of patients) for hypertension and atorvastatin (used by 30% of patients) for dyslipidaemia. Multivitamins were reported in 20% of patients ( Table).

Discussion

This study identified a significant association between elevated HbA1c levels and worse motor and cognitive symptoms in Parkinson’s disease patients from Northern India. Higher HbA1c correlated strongly with increased UPDRS scores and reduced MoCA scores, consistent with prior studies linking poor glycaemic control to Parkinson’s disease severity.4-7 As one of the first studies in an Indian cohort, where diabetes prevalence is high (16% in our sample vs. 10% globally), these findings highlight the importance of metabolic factors in Parkinson’s disease management within high-risk populations.

Impaired glucose metabolism, a potential Parkinson’s disease risk factor, may precede motor symptoms and contribute to disease onset and progression.13 Inadequate glycaemic control has been linked to worse motor symptoms,14 and while diabetes’s role in Parkinson’s disease risk remains debated, elevated HbA1c is associated with neuroaxonal damage and cognitive impairment.6,15,16 Experimental studies support this relationship, as mitochondrial toxins such as rotenone and MPTP reproduce Parkinson’s disease -like pathology.17-19

Elevated HbA1c may exacerbate Parkinson’s disease pathology through interconnected molecular pathways. Chronic hyperglycaemia induces advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) that activate neuronal and glial receptors for AGEs (RAGE), triggering oxidative stress, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signalling, and microglial-driven neuroinflammation. AGEs can also modify α-synuclein, enhancing its aggregation, while upregulation of thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) under high-glucose conditions impairs the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway, promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and dopaminergic neuronal loss. These mechanisms collectively link impaired glucose metabolism with neurodegeneration and symptom progression in Parkinson’s disease, consistent with prior evidence implicating oxidative stress, impaired dopamine signalling, and reduced autophagy in hyperglycaemia-related neuronal loss.20-26

The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Parkinson’s disease progression could influence glucose metabolism, as reduced physical activity or autonomic dysfunction in advanced Parkinson’s disease may elevate HbA1c.27 Although confounders such as disease duration and medication use were adjusted for, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporality. The cohort’s high diabetes prevalence and elevated HbA1c may reflect selection bias from a tertiary-care setting that often attracts patients with more severe disease or comorbidities.28 The finding that diabetic patients had slightly better UPDRS ON scores may suggest a neuroprotective effect of metformin,26 while the association between multivitamin use and lower UPDRS OFF scores hints at nutritional influences, both warranting further investigation.

Although the correlations between HbA1c and clinical outcomes were statistically robust, their magnitude should be interpreted with caution. These values may partly reflect the limited sample size and the relatively homogeneous population recruited from a single tertiary-care centre. Unmeasured factors such as nutritional status, disease clustering, or treatment adherence may also have accentuated these associations. Local dietary patterns, lifestyle habits, or genetic variations in the Jodhpur population could further influence the observed metabolic–neurodegenerative link. Scatterplots confirmed linear trends without significant outlier distortion, yet validation in larger, multi-centre cohorts is necessary.

Despite adjustment for key confounders, residual effects from physical activity, dietary glycaemic load, or glucose variability cannot be excluded. Elevated HbA1c levels were strongly associated with greater motor impairment and cognitive decline, emphasising the role of glycaemic status in Parkinson’s disease severity. Very low HbA1c levels (≤4.9%) may also reflect malnutrition, frailty or autonomic dysfunction, conditions common in Parkinson’s disease. Although this subgroup showed the most favourable outcomes, its small size precludes firm conclusions, underscoring the need for larger studies examining both extremes of glycaemic control.

From a public-health perspective, these findings support integrating metabolic assessment into Parkinson’s disease care, especially in regions with high diabetes prevalence. Recent population-based evidence also highlights that both prediabetes and diabetes increase the likelihood of developing Parkinson’s disease,29 reinforcing the need for early metabolic screening and lifestyle interventions. Routine HbA1c screening and patient education on glycaemic control could complement motor and cognitive rehabilitation. Collaboration between neurologists and endocrinologists, and inclusion of metabolic risk assessment in clinical guidelines, may improve disease outcomes and quality of life in Parkinson’s disease.
