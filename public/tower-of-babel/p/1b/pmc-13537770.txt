Kaempferia galanga cream accelerates wound healing through modulation of tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-10 in an excision wound model
Authors: Siska Siska, Fujianti Fujianti, Tahyatul Bariroh, Supandi Supandi, Islamudin Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraditionally used to treat inflammatory diseases, Kaempferia galanga L. includes bioactive substances with anti-inflammatory properties, such as eugenol and ethyl p-methoxycinnamate. Its potential as a topical treatment for wound healing is yet unclear, though. This study aims to assess the ability of K. galanga cream (KGC) in accelerating wound healing and to observe its effects on excisional wound models based on plasma levels of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Six groups (n = 5) were created from thirty male Wistar rats. After creating a 1.5-cm full-thickness wound on the dorsal side, without treatment as a control, a base cream as a negative control, 10% povidone–iodine cream as a positive control, or KGC (5%, 10%, or 15%) was applied topically every day for 14 days. On days 7 and 14, the ImageJ software was used to quantify the wound area. Plasma TNF-α and IL-10 levels were measured on day 15 using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and statistical evaluation was conducted via one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test. KGC markedly improved wound healing versus the negative control. The 10% formulation produced the greatest effect, reducing wound area by 65.3% on day 14 (P < 0.001). It also markedly decreased TNF-α levels by 38.7% (P < 0.001) and increased IL-10 levels by 42.5% (P < 0.001) compared with the negative control. The 10% KGC demonstrated the most effective wound healing activity, likely through modulation of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. These findings support its potential as a topical herbal formulation for wound management.

INTRODUCTION

The largest organ in the human body, the skin, acts as the main defense against microbiological, chemical, and physical harm. A complicated wound healing process that includes hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling is triggered when this barrier is broken by cuts, burns, or lacerations. Disruption or prolongation of the inflammatory phase can delay healing, increase susceptibility to infection, and contribute to chronic wound formation, making the development of safe and effective topical therapies an important clinical priority. Conventional wound-care agents, such as antiseptics, often show cytotoxic effects on regenerating tissues, highlighting the need for alternative treatments with superior biocompatibility and anti-inflammatory activity.

Medicinal plants have gained considerable scientific attention as a source of wound healing compounds due to their diverse pharmacological properties. Kaempferia galanga L.(Zingiberaceae), commonly known by the local Indonesian name “kencur,” has long been used in Southeast Asian ethnomedicine for managing inflammation, pain, and skin disorders. Phytochemical analyses consistently identify ethyl p-methoxycinnamate (EPMC) and eugenol as major constituents of its rhizomes, along with kaempferol, methyl cinnamate, and other bioactive compounds.[12] EPMC has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory actions by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase pathways and suppressing leukotriene B4 production in inflammatory models.[34] In addition, EPMC significantly downregulates tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) expression through inhibition of NF-κB activation, indicating its potential role in regulating early inflammatory events in wound healing.[5] Eugenol, another active compound in K. galanga, has been shown to reduce oxidative stress and attenuate inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and interleukin (IL)-6, which further supports its potential relevance in tissue repair.

Several recent studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of K. galanga extracts on wound-related conditions. For example, the alcoholic extract of K. galanga enhanced wound contraction, epithelialization, and breaking strength in excision and incision wound models in rats.[6] In addition, pharmaceutical gel formulations containing diterpene compounds isolated from K. galanga enhanced fibroblast migration and viability in vitro, suggesting improved tissue regeneration,[7] and also the application of a mouth gel formulation from K. galanga for the treatment of aphthous ulcers and oral stomatitis.[8] Furthermore, a gel containing kaempulchraol compounds exhibited enhanced cell migration and anti-inflammatory effects relevant to wound healing assays.[9] Topical application of K. galanga ethanol extract accelerated healing of chemically induced oral mucosal ulcers and reduced inflammatory scores, supporting its traditional use in skin and mucosal injuries.[10] Nevertheless, the wound-healing activity of K. galanga in full-thickness excisional wound models remains underexplored, particularly regarding its effects on key cytokines such as TNF-α and interleukin-10 (IL-10). TNF-α is a central pro-inflammatory mediator whose overexpression prolongs inflammation and delays tissue regeneration, while IL-10 counteracts this process by promoting anti-inflammatory responses, encouraging re-epithelialization, and supporting tissue remodeling.

Examining K. galanga’s effects in a topical cream formulation may offer important insights into its therapeutic potential for wound treatment, given its promising pharmacological profile and active components. Thus, this study aimed to assess the effectiveness of K. galanga cream (KGC) at different doses in a full-thickness excisional wound model and to look into its modulatory effects on TNF-α and IL-10 levels. This study supports the development of K. galanga as a natural topical treatment to improve wound healing and provides scientific confirmation for its traditional use.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Every chemical utilized was either pharmaceutical or analytical quality, and every additional reagent was obtained from reputable vendors and was of analytical grade. The rhizomes of K. galanga L. were acquired from Kebun Biofarmaka, IPB University, Bogor, West Java, Indonesia. The plant was recognized using the determination specimen number B-12/IV/D1.01/1 at The Research Center for Biology, Indonesia Institute of Sciences (LIPI), Cibinong, West Java, Indonesia. The rhizome was pounded into a coarse powder after being dried in the shade.

Plant extraction

Five kilograms of dried powder were macerated for 3 days with 70% ethanol and then condensed using a vacuum evaporator (under reduced pressure). Then the macerate was fractionated with n-hexane using a column. The final n-hexane fraction of K. galanga was made in cream form for application to the wounded skin to evaluate the wound healing potency.

Preparation of Kaempferia galanga cream

KGC is made with an oil–water-based cream. The oil phase consists of stearic acid and paraffin liquid, and the water phase consists of triethanolamine and hot water, with nipasol and nipagin as preservatives. KGC was made at a dosage variation of 5% (1.25 g KGF in 23.75 g base), 10% (2.5 g KGF in 22.5 g base), and 15% (3.75 g KGF in 21.25 g base), as shown in Table 1. Base cream does not contain the K. galanga n-hexane fraction as a negative control. The positive control group was treated with 10% povidone–iodine cream. The creams were stored at room temperature for subsequent testing.

Evaluation of cream formulations

The creams were evaluated for pH (using a calibrated digital pH meter), Viscosity (Brookfield viscometer, spindle No. 64, 10 rpm), spreadability, adhesivity, stability (phase separation, color change, odor, and consistency), and homogeneity.

Detection of ethyl p-methoxycinnamate and eugenol

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was used to detect EPMC and Eugenol. The TLC test uses a stationary phase silica Gel plate 60 F254 (Merck, Germany) and a mobile phase using toluene: ethyl acetate (95:5). The comparison compounds were ethyl methoxy-p-cinnamate and eugenol standard (Merck, Germany). The results were viewed at 254 and 366 nm wavelengths.

Experimental animals

Thirty male Wistar rats weighing between 180 and 250 g and aged between 8 and 10 weeks were utilized. The animals were kept in controlled environments (22°C ± 2°C, 55% ± 10% humidity, 12-h light–dark cycle) with unlimited access to normal chow and water. The Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Universitas Muhammadiyah Prof. Dr. Hamka, Jakarta, approved all experimental protocols (Ethical Clearance No. 03/22.08/02002).

Wound-size measurements

In the development of wound healing in each treatment group, images were collated to determine the progress in wound healing. Images of the wound from the skin were obtained in the 1st and 2nd weeks. All the areas of the wound were measured using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) after calibration with a ruler scale.

Blood collection and cytokine analysis (tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-10)

Before being used, rat plasma samples were gathered and kept at −20°C. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (BT Laboratory, China) was used to quantify TNF-α and IL-10 levels in plasma in accordance with product instructions. A microplate reader (Biorad, America) was used to determine absorbance at 450 nm after samples were examined in duplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical evaluation was performed using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test in GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Group differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.0001.

Characteristic evaluation of the Kaempferia galanga cream formula

Over 14 days, all cream formulations had acceptable physicochemical qualities [Table 2], with a uniform appearance and no phase separation. Their viscosity stayed constant, and their pH values (6.1–6.8) fell within the physiological range of the epidermis. These findings verify that K. galanga extract was successfully incorporated into a stable O/W cream base. The cream evaluation was performed in triplicate.

Thin-layer chromatography profile

The n-hexane fraction of K. galanga was subjected to TLC profiling utilizing eugenol and EPMC as reference standards. A complicated composition of nonpolar metabolites was shown by the chromatograms at 254 nm and 366 nm [Figure 1], which showed that the n-hexane fraction created multiple unique spots. The presence of an EPMC-like ingredient was confirmed by a significant band in lane 2 that showed Rf equivalency to the EPMC norm. This band exhibited intense blue–green fluorescence below 366 nm, which is in line with previously documented properties of EPMC and related chalcone-type compounds. In contrast, eugenol (lane 3) presented fewer and less intense spots, reflecting its simpler phenylpropanoid structure.

Effect of Kaempferia galanga cream on wound closure

Based on Figure 2, it can be seen that there was a decrease in wound area in the 1st and 2nd weeks in all treatment groups. Topical administration of KGC accelerated wound closure significantly compared with the negative control. On day 7, the formulation of 10% KGC demonstrated the highest wound reduction (41.8%), followed by 15% KGC and 5% KGC. By day 14, wound closure in 10% KGC reached 65.3%, significantly greater than the negative control (28.1%) and comparable with the povidone–iodine (positive control) group. The 10% formulation consistently outperformed 5% and 15%, suggesting an optimal therapeutic concentration.

Figure 3 shows that TNF-α levels were lower in all cream-treated groups compared with the negative control, with the KGC 10% group showing the greatest reduction (38.7%, P < 0.001). KGC 5% and KGC 15% also significantly decreased TNF-α, though less than KGC 10%. The positive control produced a moderate decrease. DISCUSSION

Topical KGC significantly improved wound closure and modulated inflammatory cytokines in a full-thickness wound model, with the KGC 10% cream showing the strongest effect. This aligns with the known anti-inflammatory properties of K. galanga and its traditional use for wound-related conditions.[111213] The application of KGC 10% in a rat model with open wounds is considered to accelerate the healing process by modulating the balance of inflammatory cytokines, specifically by reducing levels of TNF-α and increasing levels of IL-10. TNF-α is the primary pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in the early stages of inflammation. High and persistent levels of TNF-α can impair wound healing by increasing oxidative stress and tissue damage. Meanwhile, IL-10 acts as an anti-inflammatory cytokine that suppresses TNF-α production and induces the transition of macrophages to the anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype, thereby accelerating tissue regeneration.[14] Bioactive compounds in K. galanga, such as EPMC, are known to exhibit anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, which contributes to a decrease in TNF-α and an increase in IL-10.[4511] These cytokine shifts support a regenerative wound environment. Previous studies on K. galanga extracts and isolated compounds likewise reported improved healing, enhanced cell migration[9], and increased collagen content,[1516] reinforcing the present findings. Thus, the therapeutic effect of galanga cream on open wounds in rats is likely mediated by its immunomodulatory properties, which suppress excessive inflammatory responses (TNF-α) and enhance anti-inflammatory responses (IL-10), thereby synergistically accelerating the wound healing process.

The KGC 10% cream’s greater effectiveness might be the result of an ideal ratio between the bioactive concentration and the characteristics of the cream matrix. While lesser doses may not deliver enough active chemicals, too high concentrations can change physicochemical properties, impede absorption, or cause mild discomfort. The significance of formulation optimization in topical phytopharmaceuticals is shown by this nonlinear dose–response.

The study has limitations despite the encouraging results. Molecular markers like COX-2, vascular endothelial growth factor, and oxidative stress indicators like malondialdehyde, as well as histopathological evaluations like collagen deposition (e.g., Masson’s trichrome), were not assessed. These results should be included in future research with bigger sample sizes to elucidate the underlying causes.

CONCLUSION

Topical KGC accelerated wound closure in rats, with the 10% formulation showing the most pronounced effect. This improvement was associated with reduced plasma TNF-α and elevated IL-10, indicating modulation of inflammatory cytokines. The findings support the potential of KGC as a topical herbal formulation for wound management, particularly through its influence on inflammatory regulation. Nevertheless, further studies including tissue-level histology and molecular markers are required to confirm its effects on other phases of wound healing and to establish a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding.
