Multi-omics integration identifies associations between large high-density lipoprotein and lung function, asthma, and COPD: evidence from genetic and population-based analyses
Authors: Ming Zhang, Quanhong Liu, Liangliang Ju, Yucao Zheng, Kexin Dou, Weihong Chen, Yanjun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolomic signatures of lung function and chronic inflammatory respiratory diseases have been associated with potential early disease processes. We aimed to investigate the genetic correlations and causal relationships of plasma metabolites with lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases.MethodsLeveraging large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary-level data and individual-level data from UK Biobank, we applied linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) for genetic correlation analysis, Mendelian randomization (MR) for causal inference, cross-trait meta-analysis for identifying shared genetic loci, and transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) for gene-level association analysis to examine the genetic and observational associations, causal relationships and shared genes of metabolites with lung function, asthma, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).ResultsHigh-density lipoprotein (HDL) profiles consistently showed significant genetic correlations with lung function measurements, asthma, and COPD, especially large HDL profiles (L-HDL- [cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, total lipids, particles, and phospholipid]). The genetic correlations (rg) of large HDL profiles with forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) ranged from 0.12 to 0.20, and with asthma and COPD ranged from − 0.27 to -0.10 (P < 0.05/249). In MR analysis, genetically elevated large HDL profile levels (per unit) showed a significant association with increased FEV1 (mL, β coefficient ranged from 11.81 to 14.64, P < 0.05/6) and decreased risks of asthma and COPD (odds ratio ranged from 0.94 to 0.95, P < 0.05/6). Six shared genes at chr17q12 were identified in priority metabolites with lung function, asthma, and COPD.ConclusionsOur findings revealed significant associations of HDL profiles with lung function, asthma, and COPD, and provided evidence supporting potential causal relationships of HDL size and large HDL profiles in explaining these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03562-0.

Background

Chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), are airway disorders characterized by progressive lung function decline and persistent inflammation [1]. The early death and years lived with disability from respiratory damage greatly contribute to the global disease burden, highlighting the importance of investigating disease onset and progression [2]. Evidence suggests that the complex pathogenesis of these diseases arises from the interplay of multiple factors, with metabolomics offering a promising approach for elucidating underlying mechanisms. Most recently, metabolic pathways, including cellular energetics, glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, mitochondrial beta-oxidation, fatty acids metabolism, and lipid metabolism were reported to be implicated in the occurrence and development of chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases [3–7]. As downstream molecular phenotypes with substantial heritable components, metabolites provide a useful insight for investigating genetic associations with respiratory system injury.

While metabolomic studies have revealed disturbances across diverse biological pathways, lipid metabolism has emerged as a particularly tractable focus for clinical investigation, owing to its well-characterized subclasses. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), best known for its cardiovascular protective effects, has shown intriguing but contradictory associations with lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases. A study including six cohorts (N = 32,350) showed that elevating HDL-C levels were associated with a rapid decline in lung function [8], while other studies showed that serum concentrations of HDL-C were positively correlated with forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) either in subjects with atopy and asthma or in general populations from the USA and South Korea (Ntotal = 52,592) [9, 10]. Additionally, a positive relationship between the HDL-C levels and the risk of COPD was observed in a Norway cohort study with 344,556 participants [11]. These studies have reported inconsistent results, possibly due to differences in sample size and population, variations in covariate adjustment, and the lack of consideration for how HDL particle size affects its functional properties, which is partly attributable to technical constraints. Metabolomic analyses provide more detailed information on HDL particle size and lipid subclasses, which may help explain the inconsistent associations with respiratory outcomes reported in previous studies.

Genetic correlation analysis and Mendelian randomization (MR) utilize germline genetic variation to investigate shared genetic architecture and infer potential causal relationships, respectively [12]. Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) link genetically predicted gene expression to complex traits to prioritize candidate genes. These complementary genetic approaches may help reduce confounding and reverse causation and provide insights into potential therapeutic targets. Previous research has identified genetic correlations between triglycerides (TG) and HDL-C with lung function, with TG negatively associated with forced vital capacity (FVC), and HDL-C positively associated with FVC [13]. However, few studies have investigated the genetic associations of metabolites with lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases, with limited attention to lipid particle size subclasses and gene-level evidence from GWAS data [13–15].

In the current study, large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data of 249 metabolite biomarkers and biomarker ratios from UK Biobank were used to investigate the genetic associations of metabolites with lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases [16]. By leveraging genetic methods, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC), MR, cross-trait GWAS analysis, and transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) we aimed to identify the robust association of metabolite levels with lung function alternation and the prevalence of chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases, and provide novel insights into metabolic influences on respiratory health. Furthermore, we validated these associations using individual-level data from the UK Biobank.

Methods

Study design

An overview of the study design was shown in Fig. 1. We first utilized LDSC analysis and generalized summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (GSMR) analysis to identify the genetically significant metabolites with lung function parameters, asthma, and COPD. Genetically significant metabolites were further examined through sensitivity analyses, MR Bayesian model averaging approach (MR-BMA), and colocalization to strengthen causal inference. To explore potential shared mechanisms, cross-trait meta-analysis and TWAS were conducted. Finally, validation of the identified metabolites was performed using individual-level data from the UK Biobank (N = 242,129).

GWAS summary-Level data resources

The GWAS summary statistics for 249 plasma metabolites were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk), comprising data from 115,078 individuals [17]. All metabolite biomarkers were quantified using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of non-fasting baseline EDTA plasma samples (Table S1) [18]. Four lung function parameters, including FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, and peak expiratory flow (PEF) were extracted.

The GWAS summary-level data for lung function parameters were obtained from a meta-analysis of UK biobank and SpiroMeta Consortium (N = 321,047) [19]. The GWAS summary-level data for asthma (Ncases = 38,369, Ncontrols = 458,631) and COPD (Ncases = 13,530, Ncontrols = 483,470) were comprised from the UK Biobank and FinnGen, which were available in the National Bioscience Database Center (NBDC) human database (https://humandbs.biosciencedbc.jp/en/) [20]. Details for all the summary statistics were provided in Table S2.

Genetic correlation analysis

We conducted LDSC analysis to evaluate the genetic correlations (rg, ranging from − 1 to 1) of metabolites with lung function, asthma, and COPD. LDSC used precomputed LD scores derived from approximately 1.2 million common and retained single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the HapMap3 reference panel [21]. The human leukocyte antigen region was excluded from the LD Score regression analysis. All analyses were performed using LDSC software (version 1.0.1; https://github.com/bulik/ldsc) [22]. We performed Bonferroni adjustment for all 249 metabolites in the primary analysis to identify significant metabolites. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis using Bonferroni correction within stratified metabolite groups (definitions provided in Table S2) to account for potential correlation arising from structural similarity among metabolites. All statistical tests were two-sided.

Mendelian randomization analysis

We used GSMR to evaluate the causal relationships between metabolites and asthma, COPD, and lung function. Instrumental variables were selected using clumping (P < 5 × 10− 8 and LD r2 < 0.05) and filtered via HEIDI-outlier to remove pleiotropic SNPs [23]. The odds ratio (OR) of per unit change in the metabolite levels indicated the instrumental estimates of metabolites with asthma and COPD. The instrumental estimates of metabolites with lung function were interpreted as β coefficient of per unit change in the metabolite levels. In reverse causal analysis, we multiplied the causal estimates by 0.693 (loge2) to represent the average change in metabolites per doubling (2-fold increase) in the odds of asthma and COPD, as asthma and COPD are binary variables [24]. All analyses were conducted using genome-wide complex trait analysis software (GCTA v1.91.7) [25], with Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/number of tests, two-sided tests).

Multivariable Mendelian randomization analysis

Given the structural similarities among NMR-quantified metabolites (particularly lipoprotein subclasses), we employed MR-BMA to prioritize causal candidates. MR-BMA calculates posterior probabilities (PP) for all possible metabolite combinations, then determines each metabolite’s importance through its marginal inclusion probability (MIP) which is the sum of the PP of all models [26]. Conservative causal estimates are derived from model-averaged effects (MACE). In our study, instrumental variables were first selected using clumping (P < 5 × 10− 8 and LD r2 < 0.05), consistent with the primary Mendelian randomization analyses. We then used the SNPs shared across the significant metabolites as the genetic variants in MR-BMA. We used Q-statistic to identify invalid instruments (Q > 10) and the Cook distance to quantify the genetic variants in the best model with PP > 0.02. The details of MR-BMA were described previously and the R code is provided at https://github.com/verena-zuber/demo_AMD [26].

Colocalization analysis and sensitivity analysis for Mendelian randomization

To verify the robustness of our genetic causal inferences, we performed LD clumping (r2 ≤ 0.1 within 1000 kb windows for variants in the same locus) in PLINK v1.9 to retain genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) [27]. Then, we performed the colocalization analysis of genetic associations with significant metabolites and asthma, COPD, and lung function, extracting SNPs within 500 kb windows of exposure and outcome GWAS loci. We performed gene annotation for variants with strong evidence of shared causality (PPH4 > 0.8) [28].

The clumped SNPs were also selected as IVs to perform the sensitivity analyses. We conducted sensitivity analyses using inverse variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, simple median, weighted mode, and Mendelian randomization based on Egger regression (MR-Egger) implemented in the R package TwoSampleMR [29].

Cross-trait meta-analysis

We applied cross-trait meta-analysis using cross-phenotype association (CPASSOC) to investigate shared genetic loci between the priority metabolites identified by MR-BMA and lung function, asthma, and COPD [30]. CPASSOC is an approach that integrates evidence of association across multiple GWAS studies using summary-level data and identifies loci shared among traits. Given the heterogeneity in our GWAS summary-level data, we used the SHet test for the analysis, as it is robust to heterogeneous genetic effects across traits. The suggestive significant SNPs of each trait (P < 1 × 10− 5) with CPASSOC significance (PSHet<5 × 10− 8) were defined as shared signals. Details of the software are available at https://hal.case.edu/~xxz10/zhu-web/.

Transcriptome-wide association analysis

We conducted TWAS to identify shared expression-associated genes for the priority metabolites, lung function, and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases. TWAS was performed using the FUSION (TWAS / FUSION), which integrates GWAS summary-level statistics with precomputed gene expression prediction models derived from GTEx v8 multi-tissue expression data [29]. FUSION evaluates multiple predictive models, including elastic net, LASSO, BLUP, and top-SNP models, and selects the best-performing model for each gene-tissue pair. LD was estimated using the 1000G European reference panel. We applied Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons in identifying significant expression-trait associations across approximately 200,000 gene-tissue pairs. Significant associations were defined by PBonferroni<0.05.

Individual-level validation of discovered metabolites in UK biobank

We further validated our genetic findings using baseline phenotypic data from the UK Biobank. We included 242,129 UK Biobank participants with available NMR metabolomics data, spirometry measurements, and complete disease information for analysis. Metabolite levels were log-transformed and standardized to z-scores, and effect sizes are reported per 1 SD increment. Linear regression models assessed associations between identified metabolites and lung function parameters, using the highest values from at least two reproducible measurements according to the UK Biobank protocol. Concurrently, logistic regression evaluated metabolite associations with asthma (ICD-10 J45-J46) and COPD (ICD-10 J40-J44). All models were adjusted for age, sex, height, weight, ancestry, education, smoking status (never/former/current), alcohol consumption (never/previous/current), medication for cholesterol (yes/no), Townsend deprivation index (TDI), family history of cardiovascular disease or COPD (yes/no), and phases of NMR data released in UK Biobank (Phase I/II). Significant associations were defined by P < 0.05/test. To evaluate whether the associations between metabolites and lung function differed across key demographic and clinical factors, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by age, sex, smoking status, and ancestry.

Results

Genetic correlations of metabolites and lung Function, Asthma, and COPD

Among 249 metabolites, 40 significant genetic correlations of FEV1, 122 significant genetic correlations of FVC, 9 significant genetic correlations of FEV1/FVC, 41 significant genetic correlations of asthma, 47 significant genetic correlations of COPD, while no significant genetic correlation for PEF was observed after multiple test adjustment (Table S3). The metabolites of HDL profiles (including HDL and its subclasses) showed consistently significant associations with lung function, the prevalence of asthma or COPD (Fig. 2A). HDL profiles consistently showed significant negative correlations with asthma, COPD, and FEV1/FVC, and positively correlated with FEV1 and FVC. HDL-size was negatively correlated with FEV1/FVC (rg = -0.10, P = 8.97 × 10− 5), asthma (rg = -0.18, P = 1.84 × 10− 6), and COPD (rg = -0.27, P = 2.61 × 10− 6) and was positively correlated with FEV1 (rg = 0.13, P = 1.38 × 10− 8) and FVC (rg = 0.20, P = 7.80 × 10− 19). Large HDL profiles (mean diameter is 12.1 nm), including L-HDL-[cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, total lipids, particles and phospholipid](L-HDL-[C, CE, L, P, and PL]), were negatively correlated with FEV1/FVC and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases (rg ranged from − 0.26 to -0.15, P < 0.05/249) and was positively correlated with FEV1 and FVC (rg ranged from 0.12 to 0.20, P < 0.05/249).

The causality relationships between metabolites with lung function, asthma and COPD

We utilized bidirectional GSMR to investigate the potential causality of each metabolite with lung function, asthma, and COPD (Table S4). According to the genetic correlation results, we focused on the causality of biomarker concentrations, including HDL-size and L-HDL-[C, CE, L, P, and PL], that were significantly associated with lung function parameters or chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases, to identify vertical pleiotropic effects (Fig. 2B and C). In GSMR results, these metabolites were nominally significantly associated (P < 0.05) with FEV1, FEV1/FVC, asthma, and COPD and showed protective effects. After Bonferroni adjustment (PBonferroni = 0.05/6), there were significant effects of genetically determined increased HDL-size with the elevation of FEV1 (mL, β coefficient [95% confidence interval] 14.6 [6.17, 23.03]/unit of HDL-size, P = 6.87 × 10− 4), FVC (mL, 12.17 [3.56, 20.78], P = 5.58 × 10− 3), and FEV1/FVC (%, 0.02 [0.01, 0.03], P = 7.68 × 10− 6). Genetically determined L-HDL- [C, P, and PL] levels were positively associated with FEV1 (mL, β coefficient ranged from 11.81 to 14.64, P < 0.05/6). Increased HDL-size was genetically predicted associated with the decreased prevalence of asthma (odds ratio [95% confidence interval] 0.95 [0.92, 0.97]/unit of HDL-size, P = 1.7 × 10− 4). Genetically determined increased L-HDL- [C, CE, L, P, and PL] levels were associated with the decreased risk of asthma (odds ratio ranged from 0.947 to 0.951, P < 0.05/6) and increased L-HDL- [CE, L, and P] levels were associated with the decreased risk of COPD (odds ratio ranged from 0.935 to 0.937, P < 0.05/6). Reverse MR analysis suggested weak genetic causal effects of lung function on large HDL profiles (Fig. 2D). Reverse MR results of HDL-size and large HDL profiles with asthma showed no significance, while the reverse MR results of FEV1/FVC were significant after Bonferroni adjustment. Both FEV1 and FVC had significant genetic effects on L-HDL-P.

In MR-BMA analysis, we prioritized the role of large HDL profiles on lung function, asthma, and COPD (Table 1 and Table S5). L-HDL-CE was the top-ranked candidate for asthma and COPD among large HDL profiles (MIPasthma = 0.194, PPasthma = 0.161; MIPCOPD = 0.21, PPCOPD = 0.161). L-HDL-PL showed the highest rank for FVC and FEV1/FVC (MIPFVC = 0.199, PPFVC = 0.159, MIPFEV1/FVC = 0.353; PPFEV1/FVC = 0.189). The top-ranked factor for FEV1 was L-HDL-P, with a MIP of 0.19 and PP of 0.167. We also identified the top models and potential invalid SNPs of large HDL profiles with lung function, asthma, and COPD (Table S6-S7).

Colocalization analysis and sensitivity analysis

We utilized colocalization analysis to identify the shared causal SNPs (PPH4 > 0.8) of significant metabolites with lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases (Fig. 3.Table S8). Notably, some shared genetic SNPs were observed in multiple phenotypes and mapped to specific genes, including SLC39A8, FADS2, FADS3, MYRF, POLR1C, RAB3IL1, and BAG3. To validate the robustness of GSMR results, we used multiple MR methods for sensitivity analysis and the estimates of other MR methods were similar to the GSMR (Table S9). In addition, Steiger filtering indicated that the selected genetic instruments explained more variance in the metabolites than in the respiratory outcomes. This supported the assumed direction of the forward MR analyses (Table S9).

Cross-trait meta-analysis

We conducted cross-trait meta-analysis to explore the shared locus between the priority metabolites and lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases. We identified 14 shared loci in L-HDL-CE and asthma, 3 shared loci in L-HDL-CE and COPD, 32 shared loci in L-HDL-P and FEV1, 25 shared loci in L-HDL-PL and FVC, and 20 shared loci in L-HDL-PL and FEV1/FVC. We found that 9 loci (chr1p34.3, chr4q31.21, chr4q24, chr6p21.31, chr7p15.1, chr8p23.1, chr10q22.3, chr16p13.12, chr17q21.31) were shared cross multiple traits. Fifteen of them may be candidate loci for lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases, but have not been reported in previous GWAS (Table S10).

Transcriptome-wide association studies

To elucidate the complex relationships between genetic variations and gene expression, we used TWAS by using gene expression data and GWAS summary statistics to link specific genes to phenotypes. There were 42 overlap TWAS genes in L-HDL-CE and asthma, 28 overlap TWAS genes in L-HDL-CE and COPD, 140 overlap TWAS genes in L-HDL-P and FEV1, 143 overlap TWAS genes in L-HDL-PL and FVC, and 114 overlap TWAS genes in L-HDL-PL and FEV1/FVC found in TWAS (Fig. 4 and Table S11-15). We further overlapped the results of TWAS and CPASSOC and identified 61 shared genes that were both significant in TWAS and CPASSOC. Among them, ERBB2, PGAP3, PNMT, PPP1R1B, STARD3, and TCAP at chr17q12 were significant genes that were shared of L-HDL-CE, L-HDL-P, L-HDL-PL, lung function, and asthma from tissues. In addition, PPIEL, FADS1, FADS2, and MAPKBP1 were also identified in colocalization analysis as genes shared causal for large HDL profile, lung function, and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases.

Phenotypic validation of large HDLs associations

Baseline characteristics of the validation population are presented in Table 2. Our analysis revealed significant protective associations between large HDL particles (excluding TG) and both FEV1 and FVC after Bonferroni correction (all P < 0.05/7). Notably, observational results demonstrated a detrimental effect of L-HDL-TG on lung function that was not replicated in our genetic analysis (Table 3 and Table S16-17). Subgroup analyses showed generally consistent associations across age, smoking status, and ancestry, with evidence of effect modification by sex (Tables S18-19).

Discussion

In this study, we reported the association and causal analysis of plasma metabolites with lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases in a large-scale population. The genetic associations of large HDL profiles with lung function parameters, asthma or COPD were observed. Further multi-omics analysis revealed novel pleiotropic loci and candidate genes that may underlie the shared genetic architecture between lipid metabolism and respiratory health.

HDLs are involved in multiple biochemical pathways, including lipid metabolism, immune regulation, and antioxidant in organism [31]. The HDL hypothesis has been challenged in recent years, with conflicting evidence emerging [32]. In the present study, the genetic analysis results reflected systematic alterations in metabolites and indicated HDL profiles as “good” roles in respiratory system, consistent with findings from epidemiological studies. Higher levels of HDLs were reported to be associated with better lung function, less severe airflow obstruction, and lower risk of asthma [33]. The diverse functionality of HDL depends on lipid/protein compositions and the size of the particles. In the respiratory system, HDLs are the primary source of vitamin E for alveolar type II cells and play a crucial role in the oxidative reaction of lung [34]. In addition, published literature have shown that HDLs can participate in the regulation of inflammation [31]. HDLs exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the secretion of proinflammatory markers like TNF-α, IL-1α, and IL-6 [35]and inhibiting the oxidant of LDL which decreases the activation of monocyte and inhibits the expression of adhesion molecules mediated by cytokine on endothelial cells [36].

Cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, free cholesterol, phospholipid, and triglycerides are the main lipid compositions in HDL [37]. The results of present study indicated that cholesterol, phospholipid, and cholesteryl esters in large HDL showed significant causal associations with beneficial to lung function, and reduced risk of asthma or COPD. The availabilities of phospholipid and cholesterol were strongly correlated with pulmonary function by affecting the component and availability of pulmonary surfactants [38]. Phospholipid is the predominant consistent of pulmonary surfactants and cholesterol can increase the membrane viscosity by embedded in the lipid bilayer of surfactants [39, 40]. Cholesteryl ester is not directly involved in the composition of pulmonary surfactants, but it is located in the reserve fluid and undergoes hydrolysis to become cholesterol adsorbed into the surfactant layer, impacting the effectiveness of the surfactants [40, 41].

While current studies predominantly focused on the different components in lipoproteins, the different particle sizes of lipoprotein have also been shown to play exclusive roles in the development of diseases. Leveraging NMR technology, which quantifies lipoprotein subclasses by size, provides novel insight in the function of lipoprotein particles. It has been demonstrated that HDL size and large HDL are predictors of cardiovascular diseases with higher levels of large HDL being negatively associated with cardiovascular risk [42, 43]. Recent advance in NMR metabolomics has revealed that VLDL subclasses exhibit inverse genetic associations with COPD and asthma, suggesting potential protective effects [44]. An NMR measurements study found that HDL-size and the concentration of large HDL were positively correlated with FEV1 in atopic subjects with asthma [9], similar effects were observed in our results. Large and buoyant HDL particles may load more lipids than small HDL and medium HDL and primarily carry cholesterol in circulation [42]. The present study extended the evidence of the potential roles of HDL size and large HDL in lung function decline and the prevalence of chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases. However, the protective mechanisms or pathways of HDL particles in the respiratory system require further investigation.

Our findings suggested genetic associations between HDL profiles and both lung function and chronic respiratory diseases. However, the presence of weak reverse effects implied that these relationships might involve more complex biological mechanisms. In subsequent genetic analyses, we identified multiple shared loci between large HDL metabolites and respiratory phenotypes, suggesting their broad regulatory roles in both lipid metabolism and respiratory function. Evidence has identified STARD3 as a crucial regulator of intracellular cholesterol transport [45].It also appears to modulate mitochondrial function in pulmonary cells [46]. A mechanistic study revealed that exogenous cholesterol potentiates cigarette smoke extract-induced airway inflammation by disrupting cholesterol homeostasis via the STARD3-MFN2 signaling pathway [47]. These findings collectively suggest that STARD3-mediated cholesterol dysregulation may play a pivotal role in pulmonary pathophysiology. The human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER) family, including ERBB2, has been demonstrated to be closely associated with inflammatory responses and airway epithelial cell injury in asthma pathogenesis [48–50]. FADS1 and FADS2 encode fatty acid desaturases critical for polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) biosynthesis, influencing cellular lipid composition and inflammation regulation [51, 52]. In our study, PPIEL and MAPKBP1 were found to play potentially regulatory roles in metabolic and signaling pathways. However, direct experimental evidence regarding the specific role of PPIEL in metabolic signaling pathways remains unknown. MAPKBP1 is a scaffold protein in cells that belongs to the MAPK family. MAPK family has been shown to be important for embryonic development, adult tissue homeostasis, and various pathological processes, particularly in inflammatory pathways [53]. The genes identified in our study may be involved in the synthesis of lipid metabolites, metabolite-associated signaling, and inflammation or oxidative stress during disease development. These findings suggested potential molecular pathways through which lipids might contribute to lung function decline and the progression of respiratory diseases, and highlight possible novel targets for clinical intervention. However, these mechanisms require further experimental validation.

In our cross-sectional analyses, L-HDL-PL was associated with increased risk of COPD at the nominal significance, but this association disappeared after multiple testing correction. It contrasted with the results from our genetic analyses. Additionally, L-HDL-TG exhibited a negative association with lung function in the observational analyses, but this was not observed in the genetic analyses. Several factors may explain these discrepancies. Firstly, although we adjusted for potential confounders in the observational models, residual bias may still exist due to unmeasured or unavailable confounders. Secondly, the cross-sectional design cannot establish causality and may be affected by reverse causation. Thirdly, the observational cohort included a small proportion of non-European participants (4.6%), while the genetic analyses were restricted to Europeans, which could lead to differences in effect estimates across populations. Furthermore, we noted that the number of IVs for L-HDL-TG (n = 169) was smaller than that for other large HDL profiles (n > 200), which may limit the statistical power of the MR analysis to detect significant effects.

We conducted a systematic analysis of the associations between 249 metabolites and asthma, COPD, and lung function, investigated metabolite characteristics, identified the distinct functions of HDL subclasses, providing insights into potential physiological and pathological mechanisms. However, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The individuals in our genetic study are of European ancestry, which limited the generalizability of the findings to other populations. The MR methods we conducted are limited because the genetic variants we used may have a negligible effect on most risk factors, and potential sample overlap between exposure and outcome datasets could introduce bias. Larger non-overlapping GWAS summary statistics will be needed to validate our findings in future studies. Regions with complex LD may complicate the interpretation of colocalization results. In the observational study, although we adjusted for many covariates, residual confounding cannot be fully accounted for (e.g., the lack of family history of asthma). The metabolites in our study do not encompass the full spectrum of metabolites in the human body, and the limitations of NMR technology precluded a more detailed structural classification of lipids. While our multi-omics approach identified several candidate genes linking large HDL metabolism to respiratory function, these findings require experimental and clinical validation.

Conclusion

We performed a large-scale genetic analysis to identify the metabolites with significant genetic associations of lung function and chronic respiratory inflammatory diseases. Our findings indicate that HDL profiles, particularly large HDL particles, are genetically associated with increased lung function and reduced risk of asthma and COPD. These results support a potential protective role of HDL profiles in respiratory health, warranting further mechanistic and interventional studies.
