EBANO study: real-world data from patients with locally advanced/metastatic urothelial carcinoma (la/mUC) in Northern Spain
Authors: R. Fernández Rodríguez, N. Sagastibeltza, E. Pujol Obis, N. Lainez Milagro, R. Sánchez-Escribano, M. Martínez Kareaga, J.A. Verdún Aguilar, M. Arruti Ibarbia, M. Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited real-world data exist on managing locally advanced/metastatic urothelial carcinoma (la/mUC), crucial for predicting outcomes and integrating new treatments. This study explores la/mUC in Northern Spain.MethodsRetrospective observational analysis from 16 hospitals across 12 provinces. The study population included adult patients diagnosed with la/mUC (1 January 2007-31 December 2019). Median overall survival and progression-free survival were determined using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsA total of 1230 patients were included, 83% were males, median age was 68, and 70% had a smoking history. The most common primary tumor was bladder (89.2%) followed by renal pelvis (6.4%), ureter (4.3%), and urethra (0.1%) and most of them (91%) were pure urothelial tumors. Cystectomy was the predominant treatment of localized disease (69%) and 30% received perioperative chemotherapy. Twenty-four percent of la/mUC patients never received systemic therapy and out of 934 patients treated with first-line (1L) therapy, 55% were fit for cisplatin. Progression to further lines of treatment was poor, only 53% (n = 492) progressed to receive a second line and 22% (n = 209) to a third line. Median overall survival (95% confidence interval) was 12.2 (11.3-12.9) months for the entire cohort and 14.5 and 10.8 months for patients who received first-line cisplatin or carboplatin-based chemotherapy, respectively. No unexpected toxicity was reported.ConclusionsThis Spanish real-world data analysis echoes previous findings and highlights unmet needs, including the proportion of patients not receiving systemic treatment and the limited progression to subsequent lines of therapy, hampering access to new effective treatments.

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma, previously known as transitional cell carcinoma, originates from the urothelium lining the renal pelvis, ureters, bladder, and urethra. About 90% of urothelial carcinomas arise in the bladder, with 75% being pure urothelial carcinoma and 25% variant histologies like squamous and adenocarcinoma. Bladder cancer is classified by invasion depth into non-muscle-invasive (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive (MIBC) forms. NMIBC, confined to the urothelium and lamina propria, represents nearly 75% of organ-confined bladder cancer with a generally good prognosis. MIBC involves the muscle layer or beyond and may progress to advanced disease in about half of the cases, with <10% presenting as newly diagnosed metastatic urothelial carcinoma.

Age is the primary risk factor, with a median diagnosis age of 73 in the USA and 71 in the UK. Long-term exposure to carcinogens, especially tobacco smoke, accounts for >50% of cases, followed by occupational exposure to aromatic amines, benzene, and ionizing radiation. Men are diagnosed three to four times more frequently than women, with lifetime risks of 1.1% in men and 0.27% in women. There are notable geographical differences, with higher frequencies in Western Europe and North America compared with Asia or Eastern Europe. In the USA, bladder cancer is the sixth most common cancer, representing 4.2% of all new cases, with 82 290 new cases and 16 710 deaths estimated for 2023.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 In Spain, 22 097 new diagnoses are expected in 2024, making it the fifth most common cancer overall and the fourth in males.7,8

MIBC management typically involves neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy, pelvic lymph node dissection, and urinary diversion, although about 50% of patients are unfit for cisplatin. Alternatives include chemoradiation and partial cystectomy in rare cases. Despite optimal treatment, MIBC is aggressive, with ∼50% progressing to advanced disease and a median overall survival (OS) of 13-15 months with platinum-based chemotherapy, and <10% surviving beyond 5 years.9, 10, 11

For locally advanced/metastatic urothelial carcinoma (la/mUC) patients, first-line therapy has traditionally involved platinum-containing chemotherapy, but new drug combinations are emerging. Several immune-oncology (IO) agents targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) are approved as second-line treatments in the US and Europe for patients previously treated with platinum-based chemotherapy. These drugs are also first-line options for cisplatin-ineligible patients whose tumors express PD-L1 or those ineligible for any platinum-based chemotherapy.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 The PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab combined with the antibody–drug conjugate enfortumab vedotin (EV) received accelerated Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for cisplatin-ineligible la/mUC patients and full approval for first-line la/mUC regardless of platinum eligibility, recommended by ESMO since March 2024.17, 18, 19

For la/mUC patients refractory to platinum-based chemotherapy and IO, treatment options were limited. In December 2019, EV was approved in the USA for adults with la/mUC previously treated with a PD-1/L1 inhibitor and platinum-based chemotherapy, later receiving European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval.20 EV is included in major guidelines.21,22 Erdafitinib, an fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitor, is also recommended after platinum for patients with susceptible FGFR3 or FGFR2 alterations based on phase II and III trials.23 Before these agents, vinflunine was approved by the EMA in 2009 (though not by the FDA) for la/mUC after platinum therapy failure and remains a marginal treatment option in Europe.24

Despite the multiple treatment options currently available, real-world data (RWD) about patient access to therapies, treatment outcomes, and progression across different lines of therapy are limited. The aim of the present study was to describe the clinical characteristics, treatments, and health outcomes of a large cohort of patients with la/mUC in Northern Spain.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study includes adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with la/mUC between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2019 (study inclusion period). All consecutive patients in the study inclusion period were included from selected sites. Clinical data were gathered from electronic and archived health records. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of all the participant centers. Informed consent was obtained whenever required by the Spanish Law.

Data collection

Epidemiological (demographic characteristics: age, gender, performance status, risk factors), histological type, tumor characteristics, sites of metastasis, treatment information (type of treatment received, efficacy, safety), and outcome (progression and survival) data were also collected.

Study objectives

The primary objective was to describe the clinical and pathological characteristics and the therapeutic management of patients with la/mUC in Northern Spain. Secondary objectives included determining: (i) progression-free survival (PFS) to the different treatments received in the first, second, third, and following lines of treatment; (ii) median OS of the overall population and of some specific subgroups; and (iii) OS rates at 3 and 5 years of the overall population and of some specific subgroups.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ demographics and other study characteristics have been analyzed using descriptive statistics for categorical and quantitative (continuous) variables. Continuous variables have been described using the mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, as well as the lower and upper quartiles. Categorical variables have been described using their frequency distribution, as well as the 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the variables related to the main objective of the study. Median OS and PFS were determined using the Kaplan–Meier method. The statistical analysis has been carried out using the statistical package IBM SPSS version 26.

A total of 1239 patients from 16 centers located in Northern Spain were enrolled. Patients’ characteristics

Table 1 summarizes baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. Out of 1230 patients, 83% were male and the median age at diagnosis was 68 years (range 35-97 years). Primary tumor locations followed a typical distribution, with bladder representing the most common (89.2%), followed by renal pelvis (6.4%), ureter (4.3%), and urethra (0.1%). The most frequent predominant histology was urothelial (91.5%) and among variants squamous cell carcinoma was the most common with 29 patients (2.4%). Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS) was available from 901 patients. The majority were ECOG 0-1 (10% and 56%, respectively); 26% were ECOG 2, and 6.5% were ECOG 3. Regarding the patient’s medical history, the majority (74%) had ≥1 comorbidity with hypertension (48%), diabetes (22.5%), coronary artery disease (13%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (12%), and peripheral artery disease (6%) as the most frequent. Eight hundred and fifty-nine patients (70%) referred exposure to tobacco (current or past smokers), with smoking as the most prevalent risk factor in our series. Other risk factors such as exposure to toxics in working environment or previous radiation to the bladder were minimally represented with 2% and 0.7%, respectively. About a third of the patients (32.5%) had a concomitant diagnosis of prostate cancer. When analyzing the characteristics of the metastatic disease, the distribution of metastatic sites followed a common pattern, with lymph nodes as the most frequent site of dissemination (63.7%), followed by lung (32.6%), bone (26.4%), liver (19.8%), and peritoneum (6.0%). In our series, 27.2% of patients (n = 114) had exclusive nodal metastasis. Treatment characteristics

Localized disease

A total of 868 patients (70.7%) received any form of local treatment either as a primary therapy or as a consolidation after systemic therapy. Surgery was by far the preferred approached (820 patients; 94.5%). Specifically, 68.7% underwent radical cystectomy, 13.3% underwent nephroureterectomy (with 20% of these patients also having some form of cystectomy), and <1% underwent ureterectomy. The remaining patients underwent transurethral resection and other techniques. Just around 7% of patients in our series received radiation as part of a local treatment. Nearly 30% received perioperative treatment.

Metastatic disease

Out of 1230 la/mUC patients, 296 (24%) never received systemic therapy. Regarding lines of treatment, 934 patients (75.8%) received first-line systemic treatment. Almost half of the patients (48.1%) received carboplatin + gemcitabine, followed in frequency by cisplatin + gemcitabine (36.8%) and immunotherapy [3.1%, most of them were treated with atezolizumab (65.5%)]. With regards to response to first-line treatment (cisplatin + gemcitabine and carboplatin + gemcitabine), response rate was 44.2% (95% CI 39.0% to 49.5%) and 40.8% (95% CI 36.4% to 45.4%), respectively (44 patients, 12.8%, and 34 patients, 7.5% had complete response, respectively). Only 3% of the patients were enrolled in clinical trials although this proportion increased with progression to further lines of therapy. Four hundred and ninety-two patients (53%) received any form of treatment beyond first line; of those the majority (473; 96%) were treated with systemic therapy, vinflunine being the most frequent treatment (39.1%), followed by immunotherapy [24.9%, mostly atezolizumab (89.8%)], the combination of carboplatin and gemcitabine (12.1%), and taxanes (9.5%). Surgery, radiation, and a combination of these treatments were also reported in second line. Twenty-two patients (4.5%) were enrolled in clinical trials in this setting. With regards to third line, 209 patients progressed to this setting and received treatment; of these, 198 patients received systemic therapy, vinflunine (30.2%) being the most frequent treatment, followed by taxanes (22.6%) and immunotherapy [22.1%, most of them treated with atezolizumab (84.1%)]. Thirteen patients (6%) were enrolled in clinical trials. Some 81 patients reached fourth line and 74 received fourth line systemic treatment, mostly taxanes (24%), immunotherapy (18.9%), vinflunine (17.6%), and carboplatin + gemcitabine (12.2%). Only 16 patients in our series were treated in fifth line, 14 received systemic therapy with taxanes (50%) followed by immunotherapy (14%) as the preferred options. Finally, three patients reached further lines of treatment (three, sixth and one, seventh) two of them receiving chemotherapy (vinflunine and platinum + gemcitabine) and one radiation. With regards to the 24% of patients who did not receive treatment, we identified several differential factors, including age, ECOG PS, creatinine clearance, and the Galsky score, compared with those who were treated. The median age in the group of patients who did not receive treatment was 72 (range: 79-97) versus 66 (range: 72-86) in those who received treatment. A higher ECOG score was observed in patients who did not receive treatment, with 19% having an ECOG score ≥3. Creatinine clearance also varied: 50% of patients who did not receive treatment had a creatinine clearance <60, compared with 27% of those who did receive treatment. In addition, 59% of untreated patients were considered unfit, compared with 41% of treated patients.

As for toxicities associated to treatment, the most common toxicities leading to dose reduction were hematologic toxicity in ∼40% of cases, asthenia in 13%, and impaired renal function in 10.8%. Dose reductions due to febrile neutropenia occurred in 9.9% of cases. Treatment interruption was required for 17% of the treated patients: 20.4% of those treated with carboplatin + gemcitabine, 16.6% of those treated with cisplatin + gemcitabine, and 6.9% of those treated with checkpoint inhibitors. The most frequent reasons for treatment interruption were hematologic toxicity (thrombocytopenia in >40%, anemia and neutropenia in ∼30%), renal toxicity in 13%, and asthenia in 8.8%. Febrile neutropenia necessitated treatment interruption in 8.8% of treated patients.

Outcomes

The median follow-up for all patients was 9.2 months (3.9-16.6 months), while the median for the treated and non-treated patients were 11.6 and 2.2 months, respectively. The median OS in the entire population was 12.2 months (95% CI 11.3-12.9 months) (Figure 3). With regards to the subgroup analysis, patients who received cisplatin–gemcitabine had a median OS of 14.5 months (95% CI 13.4-15.5 months), while those who received carboplatin–gemcitabine combination had a median OS of 10. 8 months (95% CI 9.9-11.6 months). Regarding patients with lymph node-only metastasis within the study period (2016-2019), due to the limited sample size treated with immunotherapy, the data available are insufficient to draw robust conclusions regarding OS in this specific subgroup. The 3-year OS rates were 8.9% (95% CI 6.2% to 11.6%) for patients treated with carboplatin and gemcitabine, and 12.9% (95% CI 9.2% to 16.7%) for those treated with cisplatin and gemcitabine. The 5-year OS rates were 2.8% (95% CI 1.2% to 4.4%) for carboplatin and gemcitabine, and 1.9% (95% CI 0% to 4.1%) for cisplatin and gemcitabine. Discussion

RWD on treatment patterns and outcomes in patients with la/mUC in the routine clinical setting in Spain are limited. These data analyses provide relevant information regarding practice patterns, barriers to care, adverse events, and real-life outcomes helping to bridge the efficacy versus effectiveness gap.25 Numerous factors may influence this gap, including patient age, comorbidities, organ function, fitness for treatment, and clinician experience. In addition, the genetic heterogeneity reported in bladder cancer is now known to contribute to progression, relapse, and poor outcomes, and real-world patient cohorts may be much more heterogeneous than previously believed.26,27 Embracing a multidisciplinary approach based on rigorous evidence-based guidelines and recognizing real-world barriers will advance patient outcomes.

The aim of the present study was to describe the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and health outcomes of patients with la/mUC in Northern Spain. Patients’ characteristics, with regards to distribution of age, gender, and histology were comparable to other RWD series already published and to those enrolled in clinical trials giving strength to the current analysis. We acknowledge that many patients in our study who received local therapy underwent cystectomy, which might suggest a selection bias. This is partly because our patient cohort was composed predominantly of individuals deemed fit for surgery. We would, however, like to clarify that our participating centers are not exclusively referral centers for surgery, and our patient population does not entirely consist of individuals referred specifically for cystectomy. Moreover, it is important to note that the low rate of bladder preservation in our study reflects our institutional practices and patient preferences rather than the fitness level of the entire non-metastatic population. Interestingly, 30% of patients with localized disease received no local therapy, which is a notably high percentage. This can be attributed to selection bias, as the study primarily aimed to include patients with metastatic disease. The first-line treatment regimens utilized were also consistent with international guideline recommendations.28,29 Although cisplatin-based chemotherapy remained the preferred first-line treatment of patients with advanced bladder cancer, many patients (45%) were unable to receive cisplatin based on their clinical characteristics [ECOG PS ≥2 and renal insufficiency, creatinine clearance (CrCl) <60 ml/min] reflecting a situation that the physician will confront frequently in the clinic in the real-life setting compromising administration of the most efficacious treatments.30

In the present study, the median PFS and median OS in patients who received first-line treatment were comparable with historical data with 5 and 12.2 months, respectively.31,32 The most common second-line treatment in our study was single-agent vinflunine (39.1%), followed by immunotherapy [24.9%, mostly atezolizumab (89.8%)], retreatment with platinum-based combinations (12.1%), and taxanes (9.5%). These findings reflect treatment trends due to drug access during the inclusion period of the study. Although vinflunine has now a marginal use, it was recommended in the past an alternative for patients progressing after platinum-based combination chemotherapy for metastatic disease in Europe.28

Immunotherapy became available in the second-line setting only in the last 2-2.5 years of our recruiting period, and this is reflected in the percentage of patients receiving IO, and perhaps partly explained why only half of our patients progress to second line. The widespread use of immunotherapy has changed our daily practice, nowadays being one of the standards of care in second line and also being utilized occasionally in chemo-naive patients. Effective new treatments are quickly reaching neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and advanced first and further lines as the field develops. While treatment options are evolving, real-world patient characteristics are unlikely to change, so we will need to modify our approaches and conduct new clinical trials accordingly.

One of the major strengths of this work is the sample size and the lack of numerous similar reports in the literature in our environment. There is very limited evidence regarding RWD studies in la/mUC in Spain. Most of the studies published in the literature included small series analyzing outcomes of a particular drug rather than a large descriptive cohort.

There are also some limitations with the present study. Data were collected in a retrospective fashion and were based on investigator assessment with no central review of imaging, efficacy, or safety assessment data. Treatment information was collected in an aggregated fashion and therefore it is not possible to analyze the efficacy of different treatment sequences. In addition, although safety data were collected, there is no detailed information regarding patients’ reported outcomes or quality of life. More importantly, the data collection cut-off was restricted to December 2019 and therefore this limits the number of patients treated in first line with IO-based therapies, given the limitations of access to these compounds as previously stated, hampering analysis of efficacy and/or safety of these populations.

In summary it can be concluded from this RWD analysis regarding the management of la/mUC in Northern Spain that the results are comparable in epidemiological, efficacy, and safety terms to series published in other geographical regions of the world. Our data also highlight unmet needs such as the proportion of patients who never receive systemic treatment and the notable decrease in the number of patients progressing to second and subsequent lines of therapy, hindering the access to new effective treatments developed in this context. The information generated from real-world studies should be considered when designing future trials for this population.
