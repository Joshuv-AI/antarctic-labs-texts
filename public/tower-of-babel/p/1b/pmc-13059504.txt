The Haunting Echoes of Omitted Care: intensive care unit nurses’ experiences of missed nursing care, a phenomenological study
Authors: Reza abdollahi, Rahim Baghaei, Yaser Moradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMissed nursing care, defined as the omission or delay of necessary nursing tasks, poses significant risks to patient safety and nurse well-being in the high-stakes environment of Intensive Care Units (ICUs). Despite its prevalence, the subjective experiences of ICU nurses encountering this phenomenon remain underexplored, particularly in high-acuity settings.AimThis study aimed to explore the lived experiences of ICU nurses regarding missed nursing care, focusing on their perceptions, emotions, and the contextual factors influencing care delivery.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological approach was employed. Fifteen ICU nurses from three tertiary care hospitals in Urmia, Iran, were purposively sampled. Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted between January 2024 and June 2025. Data analysis followed Colaizzi’s phenomenological method. Trustworthiness was ensured through member checking, triangulation, and adherence to COREQ guidelines.FindingsThe overarching theme, “The Haunting Echoes of Omitted Care,” encapsulates the pervasive impact of missed care on nurses’ professional and emotional lives. Four subthemes emerged: Relentless Systemic Pressures, Emotional Erosion Over Time, Moral Quagmires of Compromise, and Fragile Threads of Adaptation. These reflect the interplay of systemic, emotional, and ethical factors shaping missed care experiences.ConclusionMissed nursing care in ICUs is a complex phenomenon driven by systemic constraints and mitigated by nurses’ resilience. The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, such as acuity-based staffing and ethics support, to reduce care omissions and support nurse well-being. Future research should explore longitudinal and cross-cultural perspectives to enhance care quality in critical settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

In the high stakes environment of the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), nurses serve as the backbone of patient care, navigating complex clinical demands, time sensitive interventions, and emotional challenges. The ICU is a unique healthcare setting where critically ill patients require constant monitoring, advanced technological support, and meticulous care to survive life-threatening conditions [1]. Despite the critical role ICU nurses play, the phenomenon of missed nursing care defined as the omission or delay of necessary nursing tasks has emerged as a significant concern in healthcare delivery [2]. Missed nursing care can compromise patient safety, prolong recovery, and increase morbidity and mortality rates. Understanding the lived experiences of ICU nurses regarding missed nursing care is essential to uncovering the factors contributing to this issue and developing strategies to enhance care quality [3].

Missed nursing care is not a new concept in healthcare research, but its implications in the ICU setting are particularly profound due to the fragility of patients and the intensity of care required [4]. Tasks such as administering medications, turning patients to prevent pressure ulcers, or providing emotional support may be inadvertently overlooked due to competing priorities, staffing shortages, or resource constraints. These omissions can have cascading effects, leading to adverse patient outcomes and increased stress among nurses. While quantitative studies have identified the prevalence and types of missed nursing care, there is a gap in understanding the subjective experiences of ICU nurses who encounter this phenomenon in their daily practice [5]. A phenomenological approach offers a valuable lens to explore these experiences, capturing the essence of nurses’ lived realities and shedding light on the contextual and systemic factors at play [6].

The purpose of this phenomenological study is to delve into the lived experiences of ICU nurses concerning missed nursing care, focusing on their perceptions, emotions, and the situational factors that influence their ability to deliver comprehensive care [7]. By adopting a phenomenological framework, this study seeks to uncover the meaning nurses ascribe to missed care, their coping mechanisms, and the impact of these experiences on their professional and personal well-being. This approach is particularly suited to the ICU context, where nurses operate in a high-pressure environment that demands both technical expertise and emotional resilience. Understanding these experiences can inform healthcare policies, improve resource allocation, and foster supportive work environments that mitigate missed care [8].

The significance of this study lies in its potential to bridge the gap between clinical practice and healthcare system improvements. ICU nurses are often at the forefront of patient care, yet their voices are underrepresented in discussions about care quality and patient safety. Missed nursing care is not merely a reflection of individual performance but a symptom of broader systemic issues, such as inadequate staffing, high patient acuity, and competing administrative demands [9]. By exploring nurses’ lived experiences, this study aims to provide a nuanced understanding of how these systemic factors manifest in daily practice and contribute to care omissions.

Phenomenological research emphasizes the exploration of lived experiences through the participants’ own perspectives, making it an ideal methodology for this study [10]. By engaging ICU nurses in in-depth interviews, this research seeks to capture the essence of their experiences with missed nursing care, including the emotional, ethical, and professional dilemmas they face. The ICU environment, characterized by rapid decision-making and life-or-death stakes, amplifies the significance of even minor care omissions [11]. For instance, failing to perform timely oral care or repositioning can lead to ventilator-associated pneumonia or pressure injuries, both of which have severe consequences for critically ill patients. Understanding how nurses navigate these challenges and the factors that lead to missed care can provide critical insights into improving care delivery [12].

This study is timely given the increasing pressures on healthcare systems worldwide, particularly in the wake of global health crises that have strained ICU resources and highlighted the critical role of nurses. The COVID-19 pandemic, for example, exposed vulnerabilities in healthcare delivery, with ICU nurses facing unprecedented workloads and emotional burdens. These conditions likely exacerbated instances of missed nursing care, making it imperative to explore nurses’ experiences in this context [13]. By focusing on the ICU setting, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature, as most research on missed nursing care has focused on general medical-surgical units rather than specialized, high-acuity environments [14].

this phenomenological study aims to illuminate the complex and multifaceted experiences of ICU nurses with missed nursing care. By centering the voices of nurses, the research seeks to uncover the interplay of individual, organizational, and systemic factors that contribute to this phenomenon. The insights gained from this study have the potential to inform targeted interventions, improve nurse well-being, and enhance patient safety in the ICU. As healthcare systems strive to deliver high-quality care in increasingly challenging environments, understanding and addressing missed nursing care is a critical step toward achieving excellence in critical care nursing.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive phenomenological approach, guided by the philosophical underpinnings of Husserl, to explore the lived experiences of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) nurses regarding missed nursing care. Descriptive phenomenology was chosen as it seeks to describe the universal essence of a phenomenon by bracketing the researcher’s preconceptions, thereby allowing the participants’ lived experiences to emerge without prior theoretical framing [15]. This approach is particularly suited to uncovering the fundamental structures and meanings of missed care as directly perceived and described by the nurses who encounter it. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines to ensure rigor and transparency [16].

Researcher reflexivity

The primary interviewer (R.A.) is a male registered nurse with seven years of prior clinical experience in medical-surgical and emergency units, but no direct experience working in an ICU. This background provided familiarity with clinical nursing contexts while minimizing assumptions specific to ICU culture. Two co-investigators (Y.M. and R.B.) are senior academic nurses with extensive research experience in patient safety and critical care. None of the researchers had supervisory or prior collegial relationships with the participants. To maintain reflexivity, the primary interviewer kept a reflective journal to document personal reactions, biases, and insights after each interview. Regular peer debriefing sessions were held with a qualitative research expert external to the project to challenge interpretations and ensure the findings remained grounded in the data.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted in the ICUs of three tertiary care, public teaching hospitals affiliated with Urmia University of Medical Sciences in Urmia, Iran. In these settings, a total patient care model (or case method) is typically employed, where one nurse is responsible for the comprehensive care of assigned patients. The official nurse-to-patient ratio in these ICUs is 1:2, but nurses reported frequent deviations to ratios of 1:3 or higher due to staffing shortages. Nurses worked fixed 8-hour shifts (morning, evening, night) and were contracted for 192 working hours per month. Compensation followed the standardized public sector salary scale in Iran, with no specific performance-based incentives for ICU work [17].

Participants were ICU nurses purposively sampled to capture a range of experiences. Inclusion criteria were: being a registered nurse with a minimum of one year of full-time ICU experience, direct involvement in patient care, and fluency in Persian. Exclusion criteria included administrative roles or being on extended leave. Recruitment continued until data richness was achieved, which was determined when analysis of three consecutive interviews yielded no new substantive codes or thematic insights. This point was reached after the 13th interview; two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation, resulting in a final sample of 15 nurses.

Data collection

Data were collected through single, semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted between January 2024 and June 2025 by the primary researcher (R.A.). A single interview per participant was deemed sufficient based on the study’s phenomenological aim to capture the essence of the experience, not its evolution over time. To achieve the necessary depth within one session, several strategies were employed: (1) Prolonged engagement: Each interview lasted 45–75 min, allowing time for detailed narration and reflection. (2) Reflective probing: The interviewer used techniques such as silent pauses, paraphrasing, and prompts like “Can you describe that moment in more detail?” to encourage deep exploration of experiences and emotions. (3) Detailed field notes: Non-verbal cues and contextual observations were recorded immediately after each interview to enrich the textual data.

The interview guide (Table 1) was developed based on a literature review. All interviews were conducted in a private room at the hospital or via a secure online platform, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim in Persian within 48 h. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment due to logistical constraints and concerns about participant identification; however, a rigorous member checking process was implemented whereby a summary of the emergent themes and illustrative quotes was shared with a subset of five participants. They confirmed the accuracy and resonance of the interpretations, enhancing the credibility of the findings.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Colaizzi’s (1978) seven-step phenomenological method, as delineated by Morrow et al. [15]. This structured approach was selected for its rigorous process of moving from individual descriptions to a synthesized universal description of the phenomenon. The analysis was conducted manually, with support from MAXQDA software (v.10) for data organization.

Trustworthiness

To ensure trustworthiness, the study adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants, member checking, and triangulation of data sources (interviews and field notes). Transferability was supported by providing thick descriptions of the context and participant characteristics. Dependability was ensured through a detailed audit trail of methodological decisions and data analysis processes. Confirmability was achieved by maintaining reflexivity through researcher journals and peer debriefing with a qualitative research expert not involved in the study [18].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Urmia University of Medical Sciences (IR.UMSU.REC.1403.060). Participants provided written informed consent prior to interviews, which included information about the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw without consequences. Anonymity was ensured by assigning pseudonyms to participants and removing identifiable information from transcripts. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored on a secure, password-protected server, accessible only to the research team.

Findings

The phenomenological analysis of the nurses’ lived experiences revealed a singular overarching theme: The Haunting Echoes of Omitted Care. This fundamental structure captures the essential essence of missed nursing care as it is lived not as discrete events, but as a pervasive, embodied, and temporally lingering presence that reverberates through the nurse’s professional being-in-the-world. Participants described missed care as an existential burden: an omission that is not merely forgotten but somatically inscribed in the body (e.g., as tension, fatigue, or insomnia), unfolds across shifting temporal horizons (from immediate crisis to enduring regret), and disrupts relational threads with patients, families, colleagues, and one’s own moral self. The “haunting echoes” reflect this temporal sedimentation past omissions resound in the present and project into future shifts as anticipatory dread while embodying the nurse’s compromised capacity for holistic caregiving in a high-stakes lifeworld.

Four subthemes surfaced as constitutive elements of this haunting: Relentless Systemic Pressures, Emotional Erosion Over Time, Moral Quagmires of Compromise, and Fragile Threads of Adaptation. These subthemes were derived through Colaizzi’s method, involving bracketing preconceptions, horizontalization of transcripts, cluster formulation, and validation via member checking. Quotations from participants (identified by pseudonyms and participant number) illustrate the essence of each subtheme, highlighting the nurses’ raw, embodied encounters with missed care.

Relentless systemic pressures

In this dimension, the ICU lifeworld is embodied as an overwhelming, chaotic spatial-temporal field dominated by alarms, monitors, and competing demands. Nurses experience their bodies as stretched and fragmented, unable to extend fully toward routine yet essential caring acts (e.g., oral hygiene or repositioning). Temporally, the present moment contracts under urgent crises, relegating preventive or relational care to an ever-deferred “later” that rarely arrives. Relationally, this pressure isolates the nurse from patients and families, reducing intersubjective connection to instrumental tasks.

Emotional erosion over time

Here, missed care manifests as a temporal process of gradual, embodied wearing-down. The repeated postponement of caring acts accumulates as a somatic and emotional residue guilt felt in the body as restlessness or heaviness, burnout as progressive numbing. Temporally, the experience stretches across shifts and years, with past omissions echoing forward into insomnia and anticipatory anxiety about future failures. Relationally, this erosion severs the nurse’s compassionate attunement to patients and erodes self-relation, fostering detachment as a protective but hollowing adaptation.

Every missed turn or unheld hand chips away at you. I go home feeling like a failure, replaying what I didn’t do, and it keeps me up at night. (P11)

Moral quagmires of compromise

This subtheme reveals the deeply relational and ethical disruption at the core of the lived experience. Nurses embody a fundamental dissonance between their oath-bound self (as advocate and compassionate presence) and the constrained, compromised self forced to ration care. Temporally, the moment of compromise is lived as an extended moral suspension—knowing the right action yet being unable to enact it, with the ethical wound lingering as moral injury. Relationally, omissions fracture the intersubjective bond with patients (experienced as betrayal) and with one’s professional identity.

I swore an oath to do no harm, but when I delay pain assessment because of a new admission, am I harming? It’s a lie I tell myself to sleep. (P9)

Fragile threads of adaptation

Amid the haunting, nurses actively weave fragile relational and temporal lifelines to reclaim agency. These adaptations are embodied micro-practices (e.g., quick peer handoffs or voice notes) that momentarily restore connection and temporality reclaiming small futures of care within the pressured present. Relationally, they reaffirm intersubjectivity through peer solidarity and fleeting patient/family contacts, though their precarity underscores the fragility of resilience in an unsustainable world.

We huddle quick ‘Hey, can you reposition mine while I chart yours?’ It’s patchwork, but it stitches back some humanity into the shift. (P14)

Discussion

The findings of this phenomenological study illuminate the profound and multifaceted experiences of ICU nurses with missed nursing care, encapsulated in the overarching theme of “The Haunting Echoes of Omitted Care.” This theme reflects the persistent, almost spectral impact of care omissions on nurses’ professional identities, emotional states, and ethical frameworks. Through the four subthemes Relentless Systemic Pressures, Emotional Erosion Over Time, Moral Quagmires of Compromise, and Fragile Threads of Adaptation the study reveals how missed care is not merely an operational shortfall but a deeply human phenomenon intertwined with systemic constraints and personal resilience. These insights align with and extend existing literature on missed nursing care, particularly in high-acuity settings like the ICU, where the stakes of omission are amplified by patient vulnerability and the intensity of interventions.

The subtheme of Relentless Systemic Pressures highlights how external factors such as staffing shortages, high patient acuity, and resource limitations compel nurses to prioritize tasks, often at the expense of essential but less immediately critical activities like oral hygiene or family communication. This resonates with quantitative studies that have quantified the prevalence of missed care in ICUs, reporting rates as high as 88% for certain tasks (e.g., ambulation or turning patients). For instance, Kalisch et al.‘s model of missed nursing care posits that antecedents like labor resources and material availability directly influence care omissions, mirroring participants’ descriptions of “drowning in alarms” and rationing care as a survival mechanism [19]. In the ICU context, where patients often require ventilator support or continuous monitoring, these pressures are exacerbated, as evidenced by research during the COVID-19 pandemic, which documented surges in missed care due to overwhelming workloads [20]. Our findings add a qualitative depth, portraying these pressures not as abstract statistics but as a “battlefield” that normalizes omission, thereby perpetuating a cycle of compromised care.

Emotional Erosion Over Time emerges as a chronic consequence, with nurses reporting cumulative guilt, burnout, and emotional detachment that extend beyond the workplace. This subtheme echoes the concept of compassion fatigue in critical care nursing, where repeated exposure to high-stress scenarios leads to emotional exhaustion [21]. Participants’ accounts of insomnia and “hollowing out” align with studies linking missed care to increased nurse burnout and turnover intentions, with one meta-analysis indicating that missed care correlates with a 1.5-fold increase in emotional distress among ICU staff [22]. Unlike prior research focused on general wards, this study underscores the unique ICU amplification: the life-or-death nature of omissions (e.g., delaying pain assessments) intensifies emotional tolls, fostering a desensitization that participants described as building “walls” to cope. This phenomenological lens reveals the temporal dimension erosion “over time” suggesting that missed care contributes to long-term psychological wear, potentially explaining high attrition rates in ICU nursing (up to 20–30% annually in some regions) [23].

The Moral Quagmires of Compromise subtheme delves into the ethical conflicts arising from care omissions, where nurses grapple with violations of their oaths to “do no harm” and advocate for patients. This internal dissonance is akin to moral distress, a well-documented phenomenon in nursing ethics literature, defined as knowing the right action but being constrained from taking it [24]. In ICU settings, where omissions can lead to tangible harms like ventilator-associated pneumonia or pressure ulcers, participants’ experiences of “moral injury” and “betrayal” extend Corley’s framework of moral distress in critical care, which links it to systemic issues like inadequate staffing [25]. Our findings innovate by framing compromise as a “quagmire,” emphasizing the sticky, inescapable nature of rationalizing omissions for “the greater good,” which leaves a lasting residue. This is particularly relevant post-pandemic, as studies have shown heightened moral distress among ICU nurses facing resource rationing [26], highlighting the need to address ethical burdens as a core component of missed care.

Finally, Fragile Threads of Adaptation illustrates nurses’ resilience through informal strategies like peer delegation and technological workarounds, offering glimmers of agency amid adversity. This subtheme aligns with resilience theories in nursing, such as the ability to “bounce back” via adaptive coping [27], but portrays these threads as “fragile,” underscoring their precariousness in unsustainable systems. Literature on missed care often overlooks positive adaptations, focusing instead on deficits; however, our findings parallel interventions like team-based care models that reduce omissions by fostering collaboration [28]. Participants’ “patchwork” approaches, such as voice-noting family updates, suggest bottom-up innovations that could inform formal protocols, bridging a gap in phenomenological explorations of agency in high-pressure environments.

Collectively, these subthemes and the 12 emergent concepts (e.g., alarm fatigue, cumulative guilt, ethical rationalization) provide a holistic structure to the phenomenon, as distilled through Colaizzi’s method. While prior studies have predominantly used surveys to measure missed care, this phenomenological approach captures the “essence” of lived experiences, revealing missed care as a haunting echo that reverberates through individual, interpersonal, and systemic layers.

Reflexivity and contextual considerations

As researchers embedded within the Iranian healthcare context, we acknowledge that our professional backgrounds and the specific cultural and organizational environment of Urmia’s public teaching hospitals have shaped the interpretation of these data. The primary interviewer’s (Y.M.) clinical experience outside the ICU allowed for a perspective that was both familiar with nursing realities yet intentionally distanced from ICU-specific assumptions. The co-investigators’ (R.A., R.B.) expertise in patient safety oriented the analysis towards systemic factors. The pervasive theme of systemic pressure, for instance, may be particularly acute in a resource-constrained setting with reported staffing deviations. Furthermore, cultural norms regarding professional hierarchy, communication, and the expression of moral distress could influence how participants narrated their experiences. We have sought to bracket these preconceptions through reflexive journaling and peer debriefing, aiming to foreground the nurses’ authentic voices. This reflexive stance strengthens the credibility of the findings by making the interpretive process transparent.

Transferability of findings

While this study is situated within the specific socio-cultural and organizational context of Iranian ICUs, the core phenomenon the haunting psychological and ethical burden of necessary care left undone is likely transferable to other high-acuity, resource-pressured environments globally. The subthemes of systemic pressure, emotional erosion, moral conflict, and fragile adaptation resonate with international literature on nursing work. However, the manifest expression and relative weighting of these factors may vary across healthcare systems with different staffing norms, funding models, or cultural attitudes towards authority and error. For instance, the intensity of “relentless systemic pressures” might be more pronounced in settings with severe nursing shortages, while “moral quagmires” could be framed differently in cultures with distinct ethical paradigms. Thus, the findings offer a conceptual framework that can be explored and refined in diverse contexts, rather than a universally fixed description.

Collectively, these subthemes provide a holistic structure to the phenomenon. While prior studies have predominantly used surveys to measure missed care, this phenomenological approach captures the “essence” of lived experiences, revealing missed care as a haunting echo that reverberates through individual, interpersonal, and systemic layers.

The study’s findings have significant implications for enhancing ICU care delivery. At the practice level, recognizing the haunting impact of missed care underscores the need for interventions that alleviate systemic pressures, such as implementing acuity-based staffing models. To address moral quagmires, ethics rounds or debriefing sessions could provide forums for nurses to process dilemmas. From a policy perspective, the insights advocate for systemic reforms, including minimum staffing ratios in ICUs. In education, nursing curricula should incorporate insights into critical care training, emphasizing emotional resilience and ethical decision-making.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. The sample was drawn from three hospitals in Urmia (with potential generalizability issues noted in demographic reporting), which may limit transferability to other cultural or healthcare contexts, such as those in Western countries with different staffing norms. Although purposive sampling ensured diversity, the small sample size (N = 15) is inherent to phenomenological designs but may not capture all variations in experiences. Additionally, self-reported data via interviews could introduce recall bias, though member checking and triangulation enhanced credibility. The focus on Persian-speaking nurses fluent in the interview language may exclude non-fluent perspectives, and the study’s timing (post-COVID-19) might reflect pandemic-specific influences not fully disentangled from general ICU dynamics.

Recommendations and future research

To mitigate missed care, we recommend pilot testing adaptive strategies identified here, such as peer huddles and tech workarounds, in ICU settings to evaluate their efficacy in reducing omissions. Hospitals should conduct regular audits of missed care using mixed-methods approaches to monitor trends and intervene early. Future research could expand this phenomenological inquiry to comparative studies across ICU subspecialties (e.g., neonatal vs. cardiac) or international contexts to explore cultural variations. Longitudinal designs tracking nurses’ experiences over time would elucidate the progression of emotional erosion and moral injury. Additionally, integrating quantitative measures (e.g., burnout scales) with phenomenology could provide a fuller picture, while intervention studies testing system-level changes (e.g., AI-assisted prioritization) would translate findings into actionable improvements.

Conclusion

This phenomenological study has illuminated the complex, lived reality of missed nursing care in the ICU, framed by the overarching theme of The Haunting Echoes of Omitted Care. Beyond cataloguing tasks left undone, our findings contribute to a deeper theoretical understanding of missed care as an existential, systemic, and moral phenomenon intrinsically woven into the fabric of critical care nursing. The essence of this experience is not captured merely by incidence rates but through the resonant, persistent impact on the nurse’s being where omissions transcend operational failures to become sources of enduring emotional and ethical distress.

The four identified subthemes construct a theoretical framework that elucidates the dynamic interplay between external constraints and internal conflict. Relentless Systemic Pressures theorize the environment not as a passive backdrop but as an active, coercive force that normalizes rationing. Emotional Erosion Over Time conceptualizes the cumulative psychological attrition as a form of professional weathering, distinct from, yet contributing to, burnout. Moral Quagmires of Compromise advances the concept of moral distress by framing it as a chronic, insidious negotiation that leads to a compromised professional identity. Finally, Fragile Threads of Adaptation theorizes resilience not as a fixed trait but as a precarious, ongoing process of reclaiming agency within a limiting structure.

Therefore, this study’s primary contribution lies in reconceptualizing missed nursing care from a problem of practice to a phenomenon of experience. It provides a vivid, context-rich narrative structure that explains how systemic failures are internalized, morally navigated, and somatically borne by nurses. This theoretical lens underscores that interventions must address not only the structural drivers but also the profound human dimensions the haunting echoes that sustain the cycle of omission. Future initiatives, whether in staffing, ethics support, or education, must be informed by this holistic understanding to foster environments where comprehensive care is not an ideal to be mourned but a sustainable reality.
