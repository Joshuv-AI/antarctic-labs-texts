Optimized DNA affinity purification sequencing determines relative binding affinity of transcription factors
Authors: Katharina Schiller, Anja Meierhenrich, Sanja Zenker, Lennart M Sielmann, Bianca Laker, Andrea Bräutigam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDAP-seq is an in vitro method to analyze the relative binding affinity of transcription factors to DNA. It is a fast and scalable method and its application to plant transcription factors with a binary bound/not bound readout was first published in 2016 by O’Malley and colleagues. Since DAP-seq only requires the transcription factor protein and genomic DNA of a species, it can easily be applied to any species with DNA extraction protocols and available genome sequence resources. We present an optimized DNA Affinity Purification sequencing (DAP-seq) protocol for the relative quantification of protein–DNA interactions and a practical guide for data analysis. The desired transcription factor is expressed in vitro and fused to a tag, such as a HaloTag. Genomic DNA is fragmented and adapters are ligated, added to the purified TF::HaloTag protein, and unspecifically bound DNA is washed away. After the bound DNA is recovered, we add a quantification step which homogenizes library size and improves reproducibility. The expanded downstream bioinformatic analysis identifies transcription factor binding sites in the genome followed by analyses of replicate robustness by comparing three different peak height measures, control characteristics, and relative binding affinity.Keywords: transcription factor, DNA-binding, regulation, affinity

Introduction

Transcription is a highly regulated process. Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that can bind directly to specific DNA sequences and thereby regulate gene expression. While general TFs are not sequence-specific and enable or support the process of transcription in general, sequence-specific TFs activate or repress transcription at specific target genes [1]. The binding ability, the position of binding relative to the transcription start site, and the binding of other TFs in the vicinity contribute to transcriptional control [1]. Both, in vitro and in vivo methods have been developed to characterize TF binding sites (TFBSs) [2, 3].

In vitro methods like SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential Enrichment) and protein binding microarrays (PBMs) are high-throughput methods [4]. In SELEX, synthetic double-stranded oligonucleotide libraries are used to detect TF binding specificities [4]. In protein binding microarrays, short immobilized oligonucleotides are employed to examine TF binding motifs in vitro [5]. Both methods quantify the relative affinity of proteins to DNA; SELEX indirectly by following the enrichment of bound sequences through several rounds, PBMs directly by quantifying bound protein at an oligomer. Both methods characterize the binding event outside of the genomic context. At the other end, in vivo, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) examines TFBSs in the genomic context, in a particular tissue, at a time of day, under particular conditions. It captures the in vivo complexity of TF binding and is influenced by all layers of regulation including potential interaction partners and co-factors, accessibility of chromatin, potential interaction with histones and histone flags, and three-dimensional genome configuration. ChIP-seq requires either a very efficient and specific antibody to the TF under investigation or genome engineering to introduce a tagged TF. ChIP-seq results may vary widely depending on tagged TF abundance and sampling context since it captures the full variation in a biological system [6].

DNA Affinity Purification sequencing (DAP-seq) is an in vitro method to analyze the binding of a TF to DNA within the genomic context. It does not require genome engineering tools, which in principle makes it applicable to all sequenced species. Since the publication of the method in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, DAP-seq has been applied to a variety of plant species [7–16]. DAP-seq will capture the potential TFBSs in a genome without capturing the layered information available with ChIP-seq. This is at the same time a blessing and a curse; since DAP-seq is not tissue and condition dependent, it will in theory be able to capture the full set of potential TFBSs. DAP-seq also yields data for protein complex binding as it successfully analyzes homodimers [17], and was recently adapted to heterodimer analysis [16]. As a high-throughput method, DAP-seq can uncover the TFBSs of large sets of TFs from a variety of different species to give an insight into the (epi-)cistrome [17], the evolution of TF families [15], and the evolution of TFBSs [18, 19]. Generally, a single motif is reported as the result of a DAP-seq experiment [14, 15, 17, 20] with no gradient of binding reported. In some publications an additional secondary motif is also reported [16]. Peaks of varying height over background yield slightly different motifs [21]. Ideally, a method for examining DNA-protein interactions should yield both, information on TF binding within a genomic context and information on the relative affinity of the TF to DNA.

ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5; AT5G11260) of A. thaliana was used for method development. It is a member of the bZIP TF family which binds as a homodimer [22]. HY5 has multifaceted roles in growth and development [23] and has been reported to bind thousands of target genes directly as determined by ChIP-seq and DAP-seq [6, 17, 24]. HY5 is known as a crucial regulator for light-responsive growth and induces complex downstream regulation (reviewed by [25]). Despite its crucial role, ChIP-seq and other analyses report HY5 binding to a core ACGT motif, to the G-box (CACGTG), the E-box (CAATTG), and others [23, 26]. DAP-seq reports its binding motif as ACGTCA with minor preferences in the bases surrounding the core motif (retrieved from http://neomorph.salk.edu/dev/pages/shhuang/dap_web/pages/browse_table_aj.php; [7, 27]).

It is currently unclear to which degree the qualitative (detectable TF binding motif and TFBSs) data varies with different sequencing depths and library preparations. Variable results for DAP-seq experiments in terms of sequencing depth are reported. Sequence read yield of a TF from a pooled library is highly variable for different TFs analyzed using the same experimental and bioinformatic pipeline [17]. Different iterations of the method differ slightly in sample preparation, notably in the number of amplification cycles of the purification eluates [9, 13, 16]. DAP-seq enables the comparison of TF binding motifs and, to a limited degree, of TFBSs between different TFs [15, 17]. The robustness of the fold-change over background measure for relative affinity remains unexplored [21]. To overcome the current limitations and reduce overamplification artifacts often introduced using the original method, we developed an improved protocol and downstream bioinformatic pipeline for analyses.

To study a TF with DAP-seq, the TF of interest is fused to a tag, expressed, and purified. The TF::tag fusion protein is coupled to specific beads and unbound protein is washed away. In parallel, the genomic DNA library is prepared (Fig. 1). The genomic library can be used as is, i.e. with all DNA methylations for the DNA binding assay or it can be amplified to remove methylation of the DNA fragments. The addition of this amplification step is referred to as ampDAP-seq and generates a methylation-free library. For the DNA binding assay, the genomic DNA library and TF::tag fusion protein are incubated together and the TF binds to its preferred DNA fragments. Unbound DNA is washed away and the protein complex is denatured by heat which releases the bound DNA. A final amplification step is necessary to increase the sample concentration for sequencing as well as incorporation of sequencing indices (Fig. 1). We have observed that using the published protocol with a fixed number of amplification cycles [7] often leads to the introduction of artifacts during this final amplification step before sequencing. These “bubble artifacts” are heteroduplexes composed of partially homologous library fragments and are a result of either too much input material or too many PCR cycles [28]. These artifacts impede accurate library quantification [28]. Barcoded primer pairs in this final amplification step allow for pooling of several samples for sequencing. After pooling, a size selection step is performed to remove residual primers and fragments are sequenced. The raw reads are demultiplexed. Reads are trimmed and mapped to the reference genome. Quality filtering and formatting steps prepare the mapped reads for peak calling where the coverage file is analyzed for occurrence of regions where the short reads accumulate and pile up relative to a background. Peak calling was initially conducted without controls [17, 29] but now usually includes a library control [14, 18, 19]. These peaks denote parts of the DNA that are bound by the analyzed TF in vitro. Peak height is frequently reported as fold-change over background [17–19]. Sequences of these peaks can be used by motif detection tools such as MEME-ChIP to search for a common DNA motif in these bound regions which is then called the TF binding motif.

In this study, we present a series of adjustments to the method and a practical guide for data analysis. Changes in the final amplification protocol avoid the formation of bubble artifacts by adjusting the cycle number specifically for each DAP-seq sample. We recommend a qPCR step to estimate the optimal number of cycles for the final amplification of bound DNA fragments. Although this requires additional steps for each individual sample, the method retains its original throughput. This method avoids bubble artifacts and results in more equal read distribution across pooled samples. We demonstrate that independent DNA libraries have less variation in peak height for a TF compared to the same library sequenced in ideal and overamplified conditions. The consequences of changes to sequencing depth and of using an extraneous control library are explored and robustness is analyzed. A saturation analysis tests whether peak detection saturates and shows that, as expected, peak detection does not saturate with increasing sequencing depth for a TF with a low complexity core binding motif of ACGT. We introduce adjustments to the bioinformatic pipeline and a different measure of peak height, namely read pile-up under the summit instead of fold-change over background or reads under peak. This measure yields high correlation at R2=0.91 between DAP-seq experiments of the same TF on independently constructed libraries and is thus a robust measure of relative affinity. The high correlation only holds for read pile-up under the summit but not for the frequently used measure of fold-change over background. We include an easy to reuse bioinformatic pipeline to transfer the analysis to other samples.

Material and methods

Plant material and DNA isolation

Above ground plant material of 4-week-old A. thaliana Col-0 was harvested and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. DNA was isolated using the Nucleobond HMW DNA isolation kit (Macherey Nagel) according to manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was eluted in elution buffer (EB; Qiagen, Netherlands) and stored at 4°C until use.

ampDAP-seq DNA library preparation

The library preparation was adjusted from Galli et al. [21]. All steps were carried out in DNA LoBind reaction tubes (Eppendorf SE, Germany). The DNA library was constructed using a total of 5 µg of isolated genomic DNA. The DNA was sheared to a fragment length of 200 bp in a microTUBE AFA Fiber Screw-Cap with 50 µl sample volume per burst using the Covaris M220 sonicator with the following settings: 50 W peak incident power, 20% duty factor, 200 cycles per burst, 175 s treatment time at 20°C. Fragment sizes were verified using the Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA). The fragmented DNA was purified using two volumes of AMPure XP bead-based reagent (Beckman Coulter, UK) according to manufacturer’s instructions and eluted in 85 µl EB. Ends were repaired with NEBNext End Repair Module (New England Biolabs, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The end repair reaction was purified with two volumes of AMPure XP beads and eluted in 32 µl with EB. The A-tailing reaction was assembled with 32 µl end-repaired DNA, 1x NEBuffer2 (NEB, USA), 200 µM dATP and 15 U Klenow Fragment (3’-5’Exo 5 U/µl; NEB, USA) to a 50 µl reaction volume and incubated for 30 min at 20°C. The DNA was cleaned with two volumes AMPure XP beads and eluted in 30 μl EB. The truncated Illumina TruSeq Strand A adapter (5’-ACA CTC TTT CCC TAC ACG ACG CTC TTC CGA TCT) and Strand B adapter with a 5’ phosphate group (5′-P-GAT CGG AAG AGC ACA CGT CTG AAC TCC AGT CAC-3′) were combined to a final concentration of 30 µM per oligo. The mixture was incubated at 96°C for 2 min and cooled down to 20°C to anneal the adapters. For the ligation reaction, 30 µl A-tailed DNA, 1x T4 DNA ligase reaction buffer (NEB, USA), 6 µM annealed Y-adapter and 2000 U T4 ligase (NEB, USA) were assembled and incubated at 20°C for 16 hours. Finally, the ligation reaction was purified using one volume of AMPure XP beads, and the DNA library was eluted in 31 µl EB. The concentration of the DNA library was quantified with Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using dsDNA High Sensitivity (HS) assay kit according to manufacturer’s instructions. The fragment sizes were verified by on-chip electrophoresis using Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA).

The DAP-seq DNA library was amplified to obtain an ampDAP-seq DNA library by assembling 15 ng of the DNA library as template, 2 U Phusion High Fidelity (HF) DNA polymerase (NEB, USA), 500 µM dNTPs, 1x Phusion HF buffer (NEB, USA), 0.5 µM forward primer (5’-ACA CTC TTT CCC TAC ACG ACG CTC TTC CGA TCT) and 0.5 µM reverse primer (5’-GTG ACT GGA GTT CAG ACG TGT GCT CTT CCG) for a 50 µl PCR reaction. The amplification was performed using the following protocol: 2 min at 95°C, 30 s at 98°C, 11 cycles of 98°C for 15 s, 60°C for 30 s and 72°C for 1 min and final extension of 10 min at 72°C. The resulting ampDAP-seq library was purified using one volume of AMPure XP beads and eluted in 31 µl EB. Concentration and fragment size distribution were evaluated using Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA), respectively.

DAP-seq assay

The DAP-seq assay was adapted from Bartlett et al. [7] and all steps were carried out in twin.tec PCR Plate 96 LoBind (Eppendorf SE, Germany). The coding sequence of HY5 from A.thaliana was C-terminally cloned in frame with the HaloTag sequence in the pFN19A HaloTag T7 SP6 vector (Promega) using Gibson assembly [30]. The control sample was prepared using a pFN19A HaloTag T7 SP6 vector with the barnase removed leading to synthesis of a HaloTag without any protein fused to it. The protein was expressed in TnT® SP6 High-Yield Wheat Germ Protein Expression System (Promega Corporation, USA) for two hours at 25°C using manufacturer’s instructions with 2 µg of pFN19A-Halo-HY5 plasmid or control vector DNA as template for expression in 50 µl. Magne HaloTag beads (Promega Corporation, USA) were washed three times with a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 8 g/l NaCl, 0.2 g/l KCl, 1.44 g/l Na2HPO4, 0.24 g/l KH2PO4, pH 7.4) + NP40 [0.005% (v/v)] solution and resuspended in 95% volume with PBS+NP40 solution. To bind the proteins to the beads, 40 µl of the protein expression reaction, 30 µl PBS+NP40 solution and 10 µl washed Magne HaloTag beads were combined and incubated for one hour at 20°C in rotary motion, end-over-end with the Rotator Genie (Scientific Industries, USA). The bound proteins were washed three times with 85 µl PBS+NP40 solution and resuspended in 40 µl PBS+NP40 solution.

For quality control, a standard western blot protocol using 5 µl of protein expression and 8 µl of the supernatant after protein binding verified expression of a fusion protein with the expected size and its binding to the beads in a qualitative manner. The HaloTag was detected with an anti-HaloTag monoclonal antibody (Promega Corporation, USA) as the primary antibody in a 1:5000 dilution, and with Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam, UK) at 1:7000 dilution.

For DNA binding to the TF, 50 ng of the ampDAP library and 1 µg of salmon sperm DNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were added to the bound proteins. The volume was filled up to 80 µl with EB. The assay was incubated for one hour at 20°C in rotary motion. A series of four washes with 85 µl PBS+NP40 solution was then performed, with the last wash in a new well. To recover the bound DNA, the beads were resuspended in 30 µl EB, incubated at 98°C for 10 min and placed on ice for 5 min. The resulting eluate containing the released DNA was stored at -20°C until further use.

To avoid overamplification in the subsequent steps, a qPCR was used to quantify bound DNA: 1 µl equivalent to 3.3% of recovered DNA eluate of each sample was used for testing. This DNA along with the truncated Illumina TruSeq Strand A primer (5’-ACA CTC TTT CCC TAC ACG ACG CTC TTC CGA TCT), the i7 8 bp index primer (5’-CAA GCA GAA GAC GGC ATA CGA GAT ATCTTAGT GTG ACT GGA GTT CAG ACG TGT), and the Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (NEB, USA) were assembled for qPCR reactions in a reaction volume of 10 µl according to manufacturer’s instructions. The two-step qPCR was performed using a lightcycler with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 39 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 55°C for 30 s. To calculate the Ct value, the threshold was set to half-maximum fluorescence of the HY5 sample. The final amplification was assembled with 25 µl recovered DNA, 2 U Phusion HF DNA polymerase (NEB, USA), 500 µM dNTPs, 1x Phusion HF buffer (NEB, USA), 0.5 µM i5-primer and 0.5 µM i7-primer for a 50 µl PCR reaction. The Illumina TruSeq DNA 8 bp indexes were used by combining the i5 and i7 adapters to form unique primer pairs. The following amplification protocol was performed: 2 min at 95°C, 30 s at 98°C, adjusted number of cycles of 98°C for 15 s, 60°C for 30 s and 72°C for 1 min and the final extension of 10 min at 72°C. The numbers of cycles were adjusted for each sample as calculated from the qPCR result as described above. Additionally, replicates of each sample were run with 15 cycles for comparison [9, 16]. Amplification and fragment sizes were verified by gel electrophoresis using 5 µl of each PCR reaction.

For size selection, 60 µl of pooled sample was loaded to 1% (w/v) Low Range Ultra Agarose (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) gel stained with 1:10 000 SYBR® Safe nucleic acid gel stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A gel extraction of fragments between 200 and 400 bp length was carried out using the Monarch® DNA Gel Extraction Kit (NEB, USA). On-chip electrophoresis was performed using Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) to validate fragment size distribution. The concentration of the extracted DNA was measured using Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the DNA was sequenced as single-end reads with a length of 85 bp on the NextSeq2000 (Illumina, USA). The empty control was treated the same way with the exception that the HaloTag only was used for the DAP assay.

Computational analysis of DAP samples

Raw reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.39 [31] and the parameters SE, -phred33, ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq3-SE.fa:2:30:15 LEADING 30 TRAILING 30 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36. Trimmed reads were mapped to the A.thaliana reference genome TAIR10 [32] excluding mitochondrial and plastid DNA. Mapping was performed with the aligner bowtie2 v2.5.4 [33] using default parameters and the flag “—no-unal.” Mapped reads were quality controlled and filtered by applying the samtools [34] and sambamba [35] commands: (i) samtools sort converts SAM to BAM filetype and sorts, (ii) sambamba markdup—remove-duplicates removes duplicated reads, and (iii) sambamba view -h -f bam -F “mapping_quality >=30” filters for mapping quality. To adjust for sequencing depth, reads were randomly subsampled using the command shuf followed by extracting the mappings of these reads with samtools view. To produce replicates of subsampling, a subsample random source file is produced from the genome FASTA file. Reads were subsampled in triplicates (with three different subsample random source files) to yield read amounts between 1000 and 5 000 000 reads. Peaks were called from the quality-controlled and subsampled BAM file using MACS3 v3.0.2 [36] and the callpeak feature with the parameters -gsize 1.2e8—nomodel—keep-dup auto—call-summits using the library-specific control. With this feature enabled, MACS3 evaluates peaks in comparison to the control. For analyses without library-specific control, the parameter—no-lambda was set.

To determine binding motifs, DNA sequences from the reference genome were extracted from peak summit regions ±250 bases using the BEDtools v2.30.3 [37] sub-command “getfasta.” Motifs were determined from the 501 bp peak sequences with the option meme-chip from MEME Suite v5.5.5 [38] including a background model with parameter -bfile and applying default conditions. MEME Suite parameters -searchsize and -seed were set to 100 000 and 0 respectively, to randomly sample the input for the motif search. For relative affinity analysis, the peaks were sorted according to read pile-up and partitioned into 10% quantiles. Motifs were determined from the 501 bp peak sequences with the option meme-chip from the MEME Suite [38] as described above. The background model was analyzed based on the reference genome with the option fasta-get-markov and the parameter -m 2. To compare two samples, the feature mergePeaks from HOMER2 v5.1 (RRID: SCR_010881) was used on the MACS3 output with parameters -d 100 -gsize 1.3e8.

Visualizations were created with python v3.12.2 using matplotlib [39] and with R v4.4.1 using ggplot2 [40] and motifs were displayed with the R-package universalmotif [41]. Read pile-ups in the genome were visualized with the integrative genomics viewer (IGV; [42]). Reads in peaks were counted using deeptools.countReadsPerBin [43] on MACS3 narrowPeak files for the respective samples and visualized as a scatterplot for shared peaks. The pipeline is provided as a FAIR digital object in form of an Annotated Research Context (ARC; link available upon publication) and in GitLab (gitlab.ub.uni-bielefeld.de/blaker/dap-seq_workflow; release v1.0.0).

Results

The objective of this study is to modify the DAP-seq method [7, 17] to avoid overamplification artifacts, test reproducibility, evaluate background library effects, and generate a bioinformatic pipeline that measures relative affinity. Overamplification is evaluated using a modified DAP-seq protocol. The DAP-seq assay is performed with the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) HY5 (AT5G11260) of A.thaliana. The empty HaloTag/pFN19A barnase deletion vector served as control. Two DAP-seq libraries were constructed from independent genomic DNA isolates. To exclude any impact of differential methylation in independent DNA libraries, the DNA library was amplified to generate an ampDAP library.

The HY5::HaloTag fusion protein and HaloTag only protein were detected at the expected molecular weights of 53.8 kDa for the HY5::HaloTag fusion protein and 33.6 kDa for the HaloTag protein in a western blot (Fig. 2). Following the binding of the proteins to the magnetic HaloTag beads, a reduced but detectable quantity of protein was observed in the supernatant (Fig. 2), indicating that unbound protein remained and was thus available for homodimer formation as bZIP TFs are reported to act as homodimers.

Sequencing of quantitatively amplified libraries resulted in 10.8 and 12.7 million raw reads for the two HY5 samples, and 13.4 and 10.9 million raw reads for the two empty vector controls. Raw reads were trimmed and quality filtered for subsequent analyses.

Processing three seeds of four million subsampled mapped reads of empty vector control library A resulted in 16 832, 16 858, and 16 788 called peaks, of empty vector control library B in 13 572, 13 610, and 13 590 called peaks, of HY5/library A in 19 888, 20 034, and 19 922 called peaks, and of HY5/library B in 21 462, 21 350, and 21 438 called peaks. The fraction of reads in peaks was 0.16 for HY5/library A and 0.23 for HY5/library B explaining the different peak numbers for the libraries. Inspection of the four experiments with IGV demonstrated that unspecific peaks largely overlap in the two independent libraries across the four experiments if a region without genuine HY5 binding sites was chosen (Fig. 4a). A peak calling without control will identify all the peaks and hence lead to a larger number of peaks called in HY5 binding (Fig. 4b) that includes the control peaks (Fig. 4a). Including the library-specific control reduces the number of peaks called from 19 867 to 8808 of which only 190 are not shared with the initial peak calling without control (Fig. 4b). The additional peaks called with control were in the expected range of variation in peak calling induced by different seeds (see above). Using a control avoids false positives while introducing only very few potential false positives (Fig. 4b). Two control libraries were generated from two independent DNA isolations to test whether a generic control library or an experiment-specific control library is necessary (Fig. 4c). Both control libraries shared 7014 peaks and contained 9787 and 6535 specifically called peaks (Fig. 4c). However, when peaks were cross-called using HY5 data experimentally generated with library A and library B as the control and vice-versa, both control libraries performed equally well (Fig. 4c). The data suggests that a generic control library is suitable for peak calling. Peak calling even with either control left 368 peaks called in all libraries, control libraries A and B, HY5 generated with library A controlled with A and B, respectively, and HY5 generated with library B controlled with A and B, respectively, indicating that either peaks of controls overlap with true HY5 binding or controls in peak calling are limited (Fig. 4c). While most peaks (6304) were shared between the two independent experiments, peaks indicating HY5 binding were also specific to the two independent experiments (2195 and 274, respectively) or specific to one experiment on a particular control (290–1097 peaks) (Fig. 4c) pointing to substantial variation between experiments.

Next, we explored the consequences of different sequence read depths for a TF with a known core binding motif of four bases and tested for a lower limit of sequencing depth that still yields a motif. Five million raw sequencing reads of HY5 on library A DAP-seq sample were randomly down-sampled in 500k read intervals from five million reads down to the extreme case of only 1000 filtered reads, and the corresponding peaks were called as previously described. Peak calling yielded four or five peaks when using 1000 raw reads and increased non-linearly to 10 077–10 126 peaks when using five million raw reads (Fig. 5a). The number of called peaks did not saturate, indicating that deeper sequencing will likely result in more called peaks (Fig. 5a). At a depth of four and two million reads, peak overlap was assessed and it showed that at lower depth only a subset of peaks of the four million read sample was detectable with a jaccard index of 0.61 between the two subsets (Fig. 5b). As demonstrated previously, a small subset of peaks was only called in the small read subset (Fig. 5b). It is expected that at some point, the peak calling algorithm can no longer identify small peaks over the control. Indeed, when read pile-ups were inspected in IGV, this case was observed (Fig. 5c). With half the reads, peak sizes decreased, and a smaller peak called in the four million read sample was no longer detected in the two million read sample (Fig. 5c). To test which read depth is necessary to reliably identify a binding motif, peaks from all down-sampled sets were tested with MEME-ChIP. All but the smallest read depth of 1000 reads yielded the known HY5 motif when using all available peaks for prediction (Fig. 5d). Taken together, the results indicate that, at least for TFs with a core 4-mer binding motif, such as the bZIPs, sequencing depth will influence the number of binding sites called in a genome. Binding affinity is likely represented in the data, and with increasing depth, more low affinity targets can be detected.

To generate a measure of relative affinity, three different quantification methods were tested, reads in peaks, and fold-change over background as done previously [11, 17, 19, 44] and read pile-up under the summit with the expectation that a robust method yields comparable results in two independent experiments. When comparing fold-change over background as the peak height measure in two independently prepared A.thaliana genomic DNA libraries (library A and library B), the peak height of the shared peaks yielded a Pearson correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.72 (Fig. 6a). Comparing the number of reads under peak for the shared peaks yields a Pearson correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.87 which is more robust than fold-change over background (Fig. 6b). When comparing read pile-up under the summit as the peak height measure, the peak heights of the shared peaks were comparable with a Pearson correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.91 and a linear regression with a slope of 1.09 (Fig. 6c). The comparison shows that, provided the read depth is sampled to an even number of reads, read pile-up under the summit is the preferable measure for relative binding affinity quantification over fold-change over background and reads in peaks (Fig. 6a–c). Absolute peak height quantified as read pile-up under the summit is affected by sequencing depth. When the same sample with different amounts of mapped reads that were each randomly subsampled was compared, the pile-up under the summit (peak height) correlates very well (R2 = 0.92). However, the slope of the linear regression is 0.48, so the peak heights are roughly half the height when using half the number of reads (Fig. 6d). While the relative quantification is robust with regard to correlation, halving the number of reads from four to two million resulted in missed peak calls (Fig. 5b). Taken together, the results indicate that read pile-up under the summit is an appropriate, robust measure of relative affinity, and that sequencing depth affects absolute peak height and peak detection. When comparing two samples from the same library, which were either overamplified (15 cycles) or more quantitatively amplified according to DNA amount eluted after DAP (in this case 7 cycles), correlation of peak height is 0.89 (Fig. 6e). Variation in peak height due to overamplification is slightly higher compared to variation in peak height between independent libraries (Fig. 6c and e).

To analyze whether peak height defined as read pile-up under the summit and motif structure are related, motifs were called along the gradient of peak height. Four million mapped reads were used for the analysis. All peaks were sorted by peak height, split in ten quantiles, and were processed by MEME-ChIP to detect motifs for each quantile (Fig. 6f). The motif for the top 10% peaks with a median read pile-up under summit of 124 is complex (gccACGTcatc). The core ACGT motif occupies nearly 100% for all bases and the bases surrounding the core are distinguished (Fig. 6f). Along the gradient of peak height, the leading A of the core motif ACGT loses distinction while the bases surrounding the core (gcc….catc) lose distinction until they are barely discernable when the bottom 10% of peaks are queried. The motifs in the interval between the top and bottom quantiles show intermediate motif complexities (Fig. 6f). For HY5, a high-affinity binding motif defined as the top quantile of peaks is distinguishable from a low-affinity binding motif in the bottom decile of peaks (Fig. 6g). Using different read depths as the base for the experiment will likely keep the high-affinity motif (Fig. 5d), but change the low-affinity motif, as low-affinity peaks have a reduced likelihood of being called (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

In this study, we describe an optimized protocol for DAP-seq and provide an easy-to-use, summarized data analysis guide. We used the TF HY5 from A.thaliana to demonstrate the improvements in the modified protocol. DAP-seq was performed with HY5 fused to a HaloTag, and the HaloTag alone served as a control. Further, we compared two independently prepared DNA libraries, used two different final amplification strategies, and analyzed the effect of sequencing depth by random subsampling. HY5 is a TF that operates as homodimer [22]. Therefore, immunoblotting was used to check whether sufficient amounts of HY5 were present to allow formation of homodimers on the magnetic HaloTag beads without being impeded by the beads as previously observed by O'Malley et al. [17]. The immunoblot shows sufficient expressed HY5::HaloTag fusion protein for protein to be still present in the supernatant (Fig. 2), which suggests that sufficient protein was available to allow homodimer formation on the beads. While we have not titrated the protein amount down to the point of assay failure, we recommend performing such a western blot to ensure that the protein is present in sufficient excess during binding to the beads.

The qPCR of DAP-seq samples recovered from the bead-protein–DNA complexes revealed different amounts of DNA recovered across the different samples (Fig. 3a). The Ct values were similar for the HY5::HaloTag samples across both DNA libraries indicating comparable DNA amounts, but were higher for the HaloTag only samples across both libraries. As expected, less DNA was recovered from the complexes without a TF present (Fig. 3a). After amplification with a cycle number tailored to the DNA yield, the recovered fragments were in the expected size range of 200 to 400 bp (Fig. 3b). In contrast, amplification with a fixed cycle number, such as 15, resulted in a smear of larger sized, so called bubble artifacts [28], which were observable in all samples amplified with 15 cycles (Fig. 3b). The smear was most pronounced in the samples with higher DNA starting amounts (Fig. 3b) as expected for amplification artifacts. The original amplification protocol recommends 20 cycles [7, 17] which was amended to 15 cycles in later iterations, which, when used with the amounts of protein and DNA library applied here, almost certainly leads to overamplification. Since both, motif complexity [15] and the number of motif occurrence in a genome [24] vary, different TFs will likely yield highly variable amounts of DNA recovered from the protein–DNA complexes. The non-standardized protein input will also likely lead to differences in recoverable DNA. Before sequencing, bubble artifacts can result in less accurate fluorescence-based quantification, and therefore inaccurate loading of the flow cell. By adjusting the number of amplification cycles, more similar DNA amounts can be achieved across samples (Fig. 3b). Therefore, all samples can be pooled by using equal volumes for balanced read depth. In addition, overamplification has a slight but detectable impact on the robustness of relative binding affinity quantification (Fig. 6).

Controls have a major impact on peak calling and thus on the resulting data (Fig. 4). Initial DAP-seq data was made without explicitly reported controls [17], but newer reports [14, 18, 19] include controls. We chose a negative control in which the HaloTag is expressed from the empty vector (without a TF fused to the tag), coupled it to the magnetic beads, and let the DNA library bind to it (Fig. 1). Using the coverage of the reads from a control sample in the peak calling of a TF::HaloTag fusion protein allows to filter for unspecific peaks that may originate from the library itself, or from DNA fragments that bind to the HaloTag, or even to the magnetic beads. Incorporating this control in the peak calling drastically reduces the number of called peaks (Fig. 4b) and filters out peaks where the coverage is similar in the empty vector controls (Fig. 4a). We compared two empty vector controls generated from independent DNA libraries and show that the two DAP-seq samples of the HaloTag have very distinct peak patterns (Fig. 4c). HY5 on library A yields comparable data with peak calling on either the control library A or B indicating that a generic control library is suitable. As MACS3 uses a local background model for peak calling (see methods) and not a simple subtraction method, the overall patterns in the independent libraries are likely similar enough to make a generic control feasible. This makes FAIR data in the public repositories of ENA and NCBI amenable to re-analysis with a generic control library, as long as a control library for the species is available.

Sequencing depth has a strong impact on peak detection (Fig. 5a and b) but not on the motif recovered (Fig. 5d). This indicates that high affinity motif detection is possible even at low sequencing depth, while the number of genes identified as targets will vary with different sequencing depths. More reads lead to more detected peaks (Fig. 5a and b) and therefore more potential target genes. Visual inspection of read pile-ups in a genome browser demonstrates that peak height of smaller, low affinity peaks will become limiting for peak calling (Fig. 5c). This observation is confirmed by a comparison of peak height measured by read pile-up under the summit (Fig. 6c). In contrast, saturation in the peak number is not observed with five million mapped reads for the HY5 TF (Fig. 5a). For HY5, the core motif is only the 4-mer ACGT ([23]; Fig. 5d). This 4-mer occurs 228 199 times in the A.thaliana nuclear genome [33] resulting in potentially 228 199 peaks at extremely high sequencing depth explaining why the number of peaks did not saturate at five million mapped reads.

Relative affinity can be quantified at a defined sequencing depth (Fig. 6). Comparing fold-change over background [7, 17, 19], reads under peaks [17], and read pile-up under the summit, we identified read pile-up under the summit as a more robust measure (Fig. 6c). To analyze the motif present at different peak heights, motifs were called for ten quantiles (Fig. 6f). When determining the common motif of the top 10% peaks (highest peaks), a motif of four core bases with six adjacent enriched bases was identified (gccACGTcatc) (Fig. 6f). Decreasing peak heights led to a less specific motif including a loss of specificity for the adenine in the four base core of ACGT (Fig. 6f). The importance of low-affinity binding sites for gene regulation is not well studied in plants, but research in microorganisms and animals has shown that low-affinity sites are functionally relevant [45]. They have been shown to be critical for efficient switches in the phage lambda, for graded gene expression from the Gal4 promoter, and for tissue-specific expression during Drosophila melanogaster and Caenorhabditis elegans development (summarized in [43]). Low-affinity binding sites evolve by fewer mutational steps and can be easily replaced by other low-affinity sites, which facilitates the evolution of enhancers with clusters of low-affinity sites [46]. Information on binding affinity including low affinity binding is thus an important measure for understanding gene regulation [45]. The in vitro high throughput ampDAP-seq method determines motifs with different relative affinities for a TF (Fig. 6f), and it directly determines genome positions within these different relative affinity classes. This removes the additional step required in SELEX or PBMs, where motifs of different affinity are mapped back to the genome with a potential for errors. For quantile motifs of HY5, the considerable variation in bound motifs in each quantile, as represented by the decreasing height of letters in the logo (Fig. 6f), makes an assignment of binding class based on the logo itself impossible. However, peak height defined as read pile-up under the summit (Fig. 6c) or fold-change of background or reads in peaks (Fig. 6a and c and [17–19]) can be determined for each genome location.

The workflow presented here is one example of possible workflows. Alternatives include enzymatic fragmentation of the genomic DNA for the construction of the DNA library, replacing the sonication procedure performed here and in the original publication [7, 20]. Also, some studies have successfully used different protein tags such as the FLAG-tag [47] or the SBP-tag [16]. Expression of the fusion protein can be performed in reticulocyte lysate instead of wheat germ extract [16, 19]. Size selection after pooling of the DAP samples for sequencing can also be done by bead-based purification such as AMPure XP bead-based system (Beckman Coulter, UK). The qPCR step, the matched controls, the subsampling of reads for comparative analyses beyond the core motif, and the read pile-up under the summit as the peak height and therefore proxy for relative binding affinity are compatible with these alternative workflows.

We provide an easy-to-use, fully reproducible, modular and scalable pipeline for downstream data analysis, starting from raw reads to motif detection. By using Snakemake, this pipeline is able to parallelize data processing, thereby reducing computation time which is particularly beneficial for high-throughput sequencing data such as DAP-seq data. Due to the pipeline’s modular design, most of the programs and tools used can be exchanged for alternatives, if desired. After trimming, reads are aligned using bowtie2 in the pipeline, but other publications have also used BWA-MEM [14, 48]. An alternative to samtools and sambamba, which we use for PCR duplicate filtering, is picard [49]. For peak calling, MACS3 and GEM have both been used on DAP-seq data [7, 19, 50]. Both have the option to include a control file in the peak calling for background calculation. There are also various motif detection tools that can be used to find common motifs from peak sequences. Here, we show MEME-ChIP results, but HOMER findMotif represents an alternative method for motif discovery, which was already used in ChIP-seq data analysis.

Taken together, we describe an optimized protocol for DAP-seq. We show that a quantification step reduces overamplification artifacts, that a generic control is sufficient, that relative affinity determined by peak height is a robust measure, and that variations in peak calling seeds and changes in sequencing depth yield changes in the number of called peaks.
