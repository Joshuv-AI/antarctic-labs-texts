Whole genome sequence analysis of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol across 246 K individuals
Authors: Margaret Sunitha Selvaraj, Xihao Li, Zilin Li, Eric Van Buren, Sara Haidermota, Darina Postupaka, Whitney Hornsby, Joshua C Bis, Jennifer A Brody, Brian E Cade, Ren-Hua Chung, Joanne E Curran, Scott M Damrauer, Lisa de las Fuentes, Paul S de Vries, Ravindranath Duggirala, Barry I Freedman, MariaElisa Graff, Xiuqing Guo, Bertha A Hidalgo, Lifang Hou, Ryan Irvin, Renae Judy, Rita R Kalyani, Tanika N Kelly, Iain R Konigsberg, Brian G Kral, Lydia Coulter Kwee, Daniel Levy, Changwei Li, Ani W Manichaikul, Lisa Warsinger Martin, May E Montasser, Alanna C Morrison, Take Naseri, Kari E North, Jeffrey R O’Connell, Nicholette D Palmer, Patricia A Peyser, Alex P Reiner

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRare genetic variation provided by whole genome sequence datasets has been relatively less explored for its contributions to human traits. Meta-analysis of sequencing data offers advantages by integrating larger sample sizes from diverse cohorts, thereby increasing the likelihood of discovering novel insights into complex traits. Furthermore, emerging methods in genome-wide rare variant association testing further improve power and interpretability.ResultsHere, we conduct the largest meta-analysis of whole genome sequencing for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), a therapeutic target for coronary artery disease, analyzing data from 246 K participants and integrating 1.23B variants from the UK Biobank and the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) program. We identify numerous rare coding and non-coding gene associations related to LDL-C, with replication across 86 K participants in All of Us. Our findings are based on single-variant analyses, rare coding and non-coding variant aggregation tests, and sliding window approaches. Through this comprehensive analysis, we identify 704 novel single-variant associations, 25 novel rare coding variant aggregates, 28 novel rare non-coding variant aggregates, and one novel sliding window aggregate.ConclusionsThis study provides a meta-analysis framework for large-scale whole genome sequence association analyses from diverse population groups, yielding novel rare non-coding variant associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03698-0.

Background

The discovery and characterization of rare highly penetrant protein-coding variants contributing to lipid perturbations have yielded important broad insights into cardiovascular disease (CVD), the leading cause of premature death [1]. For example, the molecular characterization of LDLR dysfunction among individuals with familial hypercholesterolemia informed fundamental understandings between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and CVD, as well as LDL-C regulation [2–4]. Additionally, the discovery that PCSK9 loss-of-function variants lead to reduced LDL-C and reduced risk of CVD led to a successful therapeutic hypothesis, now with multiple pharmacologic inhibitors in clinical practice [5].

In recent years, the advent of population-based whole genome sequencing (WGS) has enabled allelic characterization across the full frequency spectrum, allowing for the detection and study of both common and rare genetic variants. Prior analyses in diverse individuals have identified common variants and rare variant aggregates that are associated with lipids [6–8]. Prior studies reported genome-wide significant rare non-coding variants in Mendelian lipid genes and lncRNA variant sets significantly associated with blood lipid levels [9, 10]. This effectively expanded the allelic spectrum across different population groups to clinically important rare non-coding variants linked to genes like LDLR, PCSK9, as well as lncRNA for lipids. These findings suggested that rare variant association studies may be biologically informative beyond coding sequences. Furthermore, they provided further evidence that the gene nearest to the array-based genome-wide association studies (GWAS) index SNP frequently serves as the functional gene influencing blood lipid levels [11].

However, beyond expanding the genetic architecture framework, sample sizes for the discovery of novel rare variant non-coding aggregates remained relatively modest. Now, with continually expanding sample sizes, power for novel discovery of new loci has improved. In the current study, we performed a WGS analysis of LDL-C using two WGS cohorts, NHLBI TOPMed (N = 72 K) and UK Biobank (N = 173 K) via meta-analysis, now several fold larger than prior such efforts [12]. We further replicated the genome-wide significant findings in a third WGS cohort, All of Us (N = 86 K). In this study, we expand our understanding of associations of rare non-coding variants with LDL-C beyond Mendelian lipid genetic loci.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, we included 246,157 participants from the NHLBI TOPMed consortium and the UK Biobank (UKB) 200 K WGS with LDL-C levels (see Additional file 1: Table S1) for the discovery analysis. The TOPMed cohort exhibited greater diversity, comprising 31,527 (43.68%) White, 18,589 (25.76%) Black, 14,717 (20.39%) Hispanic, 4,609 (6.39%) East Asian participants, 220 (0.30%) South Asian participants, 1165 (1.61%) Samoan, and 122 (0.17%) Native American participants. Among the 72,175 TOPMed participants, 43,815 (60.71%) were female, and the mean (standard deviation, SD) age of the cohort was 55 [16] years. Population characteristics based on each of the contributing cohorts in TOPMed are provided in Additional file 1: Table S2. The UKB cohort consists of 163,621 (94.04%) White, 2612 (1.50%) Black, 1217 (0.70%) East Asian participants, and 2951 (1.70%) South Asian participants. Among the 173,982 UKB participants, 95,089 (54.65%) were female, and the mean (SD) age of the cohort was 57 [8] years. The participants prescribed lipid-lowering medication in the discovery cohorts varied (i.e., TOPMed: 16,506 (22.87%); UKB: 27,494 (15.80%)). The mean (SD) LDL-C concentration was slightly higher in the UKB cohort at 137.79 (33.46) mg/dl than in the TOPMed cohort at 120.81 (38.18) mg/dl. In total, 571 M and 660 M variants from TOPMed and UKB, respectively, were used for the meta-analysis; overall, up to 1.23B variants were integrated through this analysis.

In the replication cohort, All of Us (AOU), we included 86,540 participants, out of which 54,078 (62.49%) were White, 14,358 (16.59%) were Black, and 2472 (2.86%) were Asian individuals, comprising both East and South Asian participants. Among the total replication cohort, 52,736 (60.93%) were females, the mean (SD) age of the cohort was 56 [16] years, and the mean (SD) LDL-C concentration was 99.07(34.98) mg/dl.

Single variant associations with LDL-C

In the discovery cohorts, we conducted a meta-analysis of individual variants with a combined minor allele count (MAC) greater than 20, amounting to 92,583,608 variants. We applied a threshold of 5 × 10−09 to identify genome-wide significant hits, resulting in the identification of 21,657 significant variants. To uncover novel loci and variants, we compared our results with summary statistics from the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (GLGC) [8], employing two distinct approaches (Fig. 1A). In the first approach, we used the summary statistics from our individual variant GWAS and defined genomic risk loci using FUMA [13] (see Methods section – secondary analysis for details) and compared our risk loci to the loci reported in the full GLGC summary statistics (up to 52 M variants) for both LDL-C and TC. We observed that 127 of our 128 genomic risk loci were significant in the GLGC study (Additional file 1: Table S3). We identified a novel variant associated with LDL-C within the locus that included the LPL gene (rs2119690 [8:20,002,028:G:A]; MAF = 0.290; beta = − 0.651 mg/dL; p-value = 3.57 × 10−09). This variant was previously reported in the GWAS Catalog as a locus for cholesterol levels in small HDL, total lipids ratio in small LDL [14], and triglyceride levels [8]. Given the comparatively smaller size of our study in comparison to the common variant GWAS conducted by the GLGC consortium, the identification of very few novel loci using individual variant meta-analysis was expected. However, given the use of WGS in the present study relative to genotyping arrays in GLGC, in the second approach, we identified variants unique to WGS data that were not reported in GLGC. In total, 704 significantly associated variants across the genome (3.25% of genome-wide significant SNPs) were not captured in GLGC array data (see Additional file 2: Fig. S1); detailed information on these variants is provided in Additional file 1: Table S4.

Rare variant associations with LDL-C

We conducted rare-variant aggregates testing using both gene-centric and region-based approaches. We performed this analysis in 3 stages. In Stage 1, we scanned for all the genes/regions on the genome for rare variants in different functional categories, which we define as masks/windows, respectively. In Stage 2, we filter aggregates and perform conditional analysis by accounting for common variants. Any aggregate that passes the conditional analysis at genome-wide significance is replicated in an independent cohort in Stage 3. This 3-stage process, along with the number of aggregates passing each stage, is shown in Fig. 1B.

Gene-centric: coding

We conducted rare-variant aggregate tests for variants with a combined MAF < 1% across approximately 20,000 genes. These variants were aggregated based on the 5-coding mask categories: putative loss-of-function (pLoF), putative loss-of-function and disruptive missense (pLoF-ds), missense, disruptive missense, and synonymous. At the genome-wide significant threshold (MetaSTAAR-O p-value ≤ 5 × 10−07), we identified 70 gene-centric coding aggregates as significant (Additional file 1: Table S5). Among the most significant aggregates were APOB:pLoF, PCSK9:pLoF-ds, and PCSK9:missense (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A). We then conducted conditional analysis, accounting for genome-wide significant common variants associated with LDL-C and total cholesterol (TC) from GLGC summary statistics. Following this analysis, 39 coding aggregates remained genome-wide significant (Additional file 1: Table S6). A total of 31 aggregates (44.3%) that were initially genome-wide significant lost their significance after accounting for the common significantly associated variants. The MetaSTAAR p-values before and after conditional analysis are depicted in Fig. 2A.

Numerous new gene aggregates exhibited genome-wide significance, including MARK4:missense/pLoF-ds and A1CF:missense. However, after conditioning on significant single variant associations, many of these rare variant aggregate sets were rendered insignificant, indicating these results were driven by low-frequency or common significant variants. Notably, many Mendelian lipid genes, such as APOB, PCSK9, LDLR, and APOE, remained genome-wide significant even after conditional analysis. We proceeded to replicate the 39 aggregates that passed conditional analysis in the AOU cohort. Using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (p-value ≤ 0.001), we successfully replicated 25 gene-centric coding aggregates (see Additional file 1: Table S7). To identify any novel coding aggregates, we compared our replicated results with the studies conducted by Hindy et al. [11] and Selvaraj et al. [9], which involved whole-exome and whole-genome analysis of lipids, respectively.

Among the 10 genes that exhibited rare coding signals, only ABCA6 (chr17 (q24.2-q24.3; Uniprot Id: Q8N139)) was not previously reported to be significant in lipid genetic studies [11]. Though multiple common variant associations are reported in the GWAS Catalog, genome-wide significant rare variant aggregate sets were not reported in previous lipid genetic association studies. We identified two aggregate sets within the ABCA6 gene at genome-wide significance: the first set (ABCA6-pLoF-ds) comprises putative loss-of-function and disruptive missense variants, and the second set (ABCA6-missense) comprises missense variants. In the discovery cohort, ABCA6-plof-ds contained 478 variants (MetaSTAAR-O p-value: 1.07 × 10−11; combined-MAC: 5496), while ABCA6-missense contained 990 variants (MetaSTAAR-O p-value: 2.89 × 10−07; combined-MAC: 14,749). Based on STAAR methodology, pLoF-ds are a combination of pLoF or predicted disruptive missense variants, and missense variants include missense variants regardless of predicted effect. The ATP-binding cassette sub-family A member 6 (ABCA6) gene spans 39 exons, encoding a protein structure of 1617 amino acids with two ABC transporter domains and multiple transmembrane domains (Fig. 3A). The modeled protein structure of the gene (AF-Q8N139-F1) was obtained from the AlphaFold repository (version v2.0) [15]. AlphaFold assigns a per-residue model confidence score (pLDDT), which can gauge the confidence of the protein model structure. The ABCA6 protein structure exhibited a high pLDDT score for 90% of the modeled region making it a high-quality protein model to carry out downstream investigation. Utilizing the ABCA6 model structure, we analyzed the most important rare variants from ABCA6-plof-ds and ABCA6-missense aggregate sets. Annotation scores (aPCs) from the rare variants were employed to identify crucial variants (i.e., phred > 40) from both aggregate sets (Additional file 1: Table S8). We identified 39 SNPs in ABCA6-pLoF-ds and 30 SNPs in ABCA6-missense, with a few variants (rare disruptive missense) mapping to both aggregate sets (Fig. 3B). Among the rare disruptive missense SNPs, few had high mappability aPCs that identify variants uniquely mapped to sequencing reads. This includes 5 variants in a stretch on the ABC transporter 1 domain (p.Thr510Met [rs144484556], p.Ala511Val, p.Leu513Gln [rs754019110], p.Gly514Cys/Val [rs777830583/rs1054480788], p.Ser516Arg [rs753065335] (Fig. 3C)). Next, there were few rare disruptive missense variants with high protein-aPCs directly reflecting the protein function. But these variants were spread across the protein structure and had missense mutations comprising of cysteine and tryptophan amino acid changes (Additional file 1: Table S9). Through this analysis of highly scored aPCs, we narrowed down to a set of variants predominantly located in one ABC transporter domain and another set more spread throughout the ABCA6 gene.

Gene-centric: non-coding

In the gene-centric non-coding rare-variant analysis, we utilized a similar combined MAF < 1% cut-off, accounting for seven non-coding (UTR (untranslated region), upstream, downstream, enhancer-CAGE (Cap Analysis of Gene Expression), enhancer-DHS (DNase hypersensitivity sites), promoter-CAGE, and promoter-DHS). To our surprise, non-Mendelian genes topped the list, including BCAM:downstream, IGSF23: enhancer/promoter-DHS, and SMARCA4: enhancer-DHS (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B). Out of approximately 140 K genes:mask combinations analyzed, 111 gene:masks were genome-wide significant at a Bonferroni corrected MetaSTAAR-O p-value ≤ 3.57 × 10−07 (Additional file 1: Table S10). After adjusting for common variants, 44 non-coding aggregates were genome-wide significant. One example of aggregates becoming insignificant after adjustment is for IGSF23 aggregates (Fig. 2B). We observed many lipid genes to be genome-wide significant for multiple non-coding masks, and along with them, many non-Mendelian genes were also genome-wide significant, including ILF3, SMARCA4, RELB, TOMM40, BCAM, and BSND (Additional file 1: Table S11).

We replicated 28 non-coding aggregates that passed conditional analysis at the Bonferroni corrected threshold (p-value ≤ 0.001). We compared our non-coding rare variant results with a prior smaller but overlapping study from TOPMed WGS data[9] to identify novel aggregates. Our comparison revealed that in addition to the published results of non-coding signals from Mendelian genes (LDLR, PCSK9, APOB), we now identify non-Mendelian genes that were replicated in an independent cohort. This includes ILF3:enhancer-DHS/UTR, QTRT1:enhancer/promoter-CAGE, RELB:UTR, SMARCA4:enhancer/promoter-CAGE/UTR, and TOMM40:enhancer/promoter-DHS/UTR (Additional file 1: Table S12). We also observed that most of these reported novel non-coding aggregates were on chromosome 19. Therefore, we conducted a secondary analysis by merging results from the region-based approach to identify an independent region on the chromosome that harbors rare variant non-coding signal apart from the LDLR gene region.

Before conditional analysis accounting for common variants, many genes had at least one genome-wide significant coding and non-coding rare variant signal. These included Mendelian lipid genes like APOB, PCSK9, LDLR, APOE, ABCG5, and ABCG8, as well as non-Mendelian genes like NECTIN2, SMARCA4, VATIL, PVR, MARK4, USP24 (Fig. 2C). Few genes had significant signals only through rare coding aggregates such as NKPD1, NPC1L1, APOC3, ABCA6, and DOCK6, and few only from non-coding aggregates like RELB, BCAM, ILF3, TOMM40 (Fig. 2C). But after adjusting for known variants, the aggregates from ABCA6, ABCG5, APOB, APOC3, APOE, LDLR, NPC1L1, PCSK9, ILF3, QTRT1, RELB, SMARCA4, SPC24, TOMM40, and ZNF441 (Additional file 1: Tables S7 & 12) remained genome-wide significant and were replicated.

Sliding-Window

In the region-based approach, we examined a 2.6-Mb region on the genome using the sliding-window approach, where variants with MAF < 1% in each region were aggregated and tested for LDL-C association. We employed a Bonferroni-corrected MetaSTAAR-O p-value threshold of 1.88 × 10−08 to determine significant associations. Before conducting the conditional analysis, we identified 994 significant aggregates, and after adjusting for common variants, we identified 247 aggregates to still be genome-wide significant (Additional file 1: Tables S13 & 14). Before replicating these regions in an independent cohort, we compared the region-based results with both gene-centric coding and non-coding results. Any genome-wide significant sliding-window region outside the extended gene boundaries (+/− 500 kb) of significant gene-centric aggregates was analyzed in the replication cohort. This step aimed to eliminate regions that had already been captured and analyzed through gene-centric approaches. We identified only two regions that passed this criterion, and only one region replicated in the AOU cohort with nominal significance (p-value < = 0.05). The one region that passed the replication was from chromosome 16 (16:72,511,001:72,513,000) and was proximal to the ZFHX3 gene (Additional file 1: Table S15).

Unraveling significant non-coding results through sliding window analysis:

We identified multiple rare non-coding aggregates that reached genome-wide significance, including Mendelian lipid genes previously reported in our research [9]. Furthermore, our gene-centric non-coding test revealed additional non-Mendelian genes on chromosome 19. This raises the question of whether an independent rare non-coding signal, apart from LDLR, contributes to LDL-C association. To investigate this, we leveraged the results from region-based sliding-window analysis, first, we filtered a genomic region of +/− 1 MB around each Mendelian lipid gene (PCSK9, APOB, LDLR), which were genome-wide significant and mapped all significant non-coding aggregates to that region. We also identified the most significant annotations that are contributing to each of the aggregate sets.

In the PCSK9 region on chromosome 1, the majority of significant aggregate signals mapped to PCSK9 exonic and intronic segments, with the top aggregate set showing elevated FATHMM (Functional Analysis Through Hidden Markov Models) scores (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A, top panel), suggesting higher functional relevance. While a few sliding-window aggregates near USP24 and TMEM61 also reached significance in the region-based analysis—driven primarily by local annotation scores—these loci did not achieve genome-wide significance in the gene-centric non-coding analysis and were therefore considered less compelling (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). Similarly, in the region surrounding APOB on chromosome 2, associations were predominantly driven by the exon regions of APOB, with the significance driven by CADD scoring pattern (Additional file 2: Fig. S3B). This analysis of PCSK9 and APOB regions was in line with our previous study [9]. It suggests that the associations with LDL-C are predominantly influenced by rare coding and non-coding PCSK9 variant sets. These variant sets map to different introns and exons, where the sliding window mapping the exons are the most significant associations followed by introns with non-coding associations at genome-wide significance. For APOB, all the sliding-window signals come from exons and the association is most significant through the different protein functional scoring systems.

Next, we proceeded to examine genes on chromosome 19, where we observed two regions harboring non-coding signals. Region 1 contained LDLR alongside ILF3, QTRT1, SMARCA4, and SPC24. The most notable signal stemmed from LDLR exon 5 and intron 4, driven respectively by protein-function and epigenetic-active scoring systems (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). Another independent region (chr19:region2) emerged, comprising genome-wide significant non-coding signals from APOE, RELB, and TOMM40 genes. In our previous study, we had investigated the rare variant associations of Mendelian lipid genes and showed that APOE had a strongly significant rare coding signal, but did not harbor a significant non-coding signal [9]. We now identify both significant rare coding (disruptive-missense, missense, plof-ds) and non-coding (promoter-DHS/CAGE, enhancer DHS) signals after conditional analysis of common variants (Fig. 2A and B). Even though chr19:region2 included APOE, the foremost signal originated from RELB, with top aggregates influenced by transcription-factor and protein-function scoring systems (Additional file 2: Fig. S5). In addition to these genome-wide significant hits, several other region-based aggregates were highly significant, but those signals did not pass conditional analysis (IGSF23) or replication (BCAM). Through the integration of sliding-window findings and gene-centric non-coding data from our present investigation, we uncovered an additional region comprising the RELB, APOE, and TOMM40 genes, where the RELB signal was predominant, which has not been reported earlier for rare non-coding associations and is located 1 MB beyond LDLR, that exhibits a non-coding signal associated with LDL-C.

Discussion

Through the current study, we have carried out the largest WGS meta-analysis for blood lipids, including 246,157 participants from diverse population groups and integrated ~ 1.23B variants from TOPMed Freeze10 and UKB200K cohorts. We have extended our previous efforts to show robust rare non-coding associations of both Mendelian and non-Mendelian genes for LDL-C. We have confirmed known rare non-coding associations in the LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9 genes, as previously reported, and identified novel rare coding associations in the ABCA6 gene and rare non-coding associations in the RELB gene. We have demonstrated an efficient method to integrate results from gene-centric and region-based approaches, highlighting the utility of aPC scores in identifying crucial rare variants.

Several key takeaways emerge from our findings. Firstly, while there are fewer samples with WGS than with genotyping array data, the former provides a significantly more comprehensive view of genetic variation across the entire genome, enabling the detection of rare coding and non-coding variants that may have significant effects on the traits. Secondly, replication of rare variants poses several challenges compared to common variants. Unlike common variants, which may be observed more consistently across populations, rare variants, specifically the singletons and doubletons, are subject to greater variation across populations and may not be reliably detected without sufficient sample sizes. For example, the number of rare variants forming aggregates in the discovery and replication cohorts was significantly different. The APOB:enhancer-DHS aggregate set in the discovery cohort consisted of 2224 variants, but the replication cohort had only 1546 variants (Additional file 1: Tables S11&12). Similarly, we identified multiple rare variants in the discovery cohort that could be potentially novel but were not successfully replicated. Examples include ATG4D:enhancer-DHS, BCAM:promoter-DHS in chromosome 19 and BSND:promoter-DHS, ANGPTL3:promoter-DHS on chromosome1. Therefore, identifying the same rare variants in multiple WGS biobanks is a challenge and annotations play a critical role in identifying significant aggregates, with different annotations exhibiting varying degrees of efficacy across cohorts. Thirdly, the sliding window approach is shown to be a valuable tool for delineating functional regions within the genome, thereby facilitating downstream investigations. This approach not only enhances the resolution of functional annotation but also provides valuable insights into the spatial organization of gene regions and their annotations. In our previous analysis, we have effectively accounted for known common and rare coding variants in nearby genes while identifying non-coding aggregates. In the current meta-analysis framework, we implemented a distance parameter of 1 MB. This approach helps to account for the potential influence of closely located rare variants, ensuring more accurate and specific results in our genetic analyses. Furthermore, in the secondary analysis, we have teased apart multiple regions on the genome by integrating gene-centric and region-based aPC scores to identify hot spots on the regions.

Our study provides evidence for several novel genome-wide significant rare variant associations that were not captured by previous efforts. One such example is the ABCA6 rare coding aggregates. ABCA6 gene is ubiquitously expressed with the highest mRNA levels in liver, lung, heart, brain, and potentially involved in macrophage lipid homeostasis [16]. The gene is reported to be associated with blood lipids in Dutch population [17] where the missense variant rs77542162 (MAF = 0.034) is associated to LDL-C and TC, but the role of rare variants aggregates is not studied. In our discovery cohort, the reported variant is genome significant (chr17:69,085,137:A:G; MAF = 0.018; p-value = 1.13 × 10−62; beta = 2.74 mg/dL) and additionally, we also identified rare missense and plof-ds coding aggregates at genome-wide significance. The discussion surrounding ABCA6 and its implications in lipid biology merits further exploration, considering its potential relevance in understanding lipid metabolism and associated diseases. Moreover, investigating the interplay between ABCA6 and other lipid-related genes may uncover novel regulatory pathways governing lipid metabolism, thereby enhancing our understanding of complex biological processes underlying cardiovascular health and disease. The second example is RELB, a proto-oncogene subunit of NF-kappaB which is a pleiotropic transcription factor present in almost all cell types and is involved in many biological processes [18]. This gene, along with BCAM (basal cell adhesion molecule), TOMM40 and APOE, is present in the independent region on chromosom19 that is associated to LDL-C. The functional relevance of this region and its independence from LDLR association require further investigation.

There are some limitations in our study. First, there are operational and computational constraints associated with performing large-scale WGS meta-analyses in a federated cloud-based research environment. Biobanks increasingly store data within individual trusted research environments, hindering harmonization efforts. Transferring or centralizing such massive datasets is often not feasible due to policy constraints. As a result, cross-cohort meta-analysis must be conducted in a distributed manner, requiring significant coordination, infrastructure compatibility, and computational resources. Secondly, the population group distributions of our cohorts are uneven as TOPMed comprises greater ancestral and self-reported diverse groups than UKB. Although AOU is diverse, policy restrictions on data downloads prevented its use as a discovery cohort, relegating it to a replication cohort instead.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have successfully implemented the WGS meta-analysis workflow on two big cohorts for studying the genetic associations of LDL-C in diverse populations and replicated the findings. We have extended our understanding of Mendelian lipid genes and have gained new insights into non-Mendelian genes and their importance in LDL-C genetics.

Methods

Overview

We conducted a meta-analysis of WGS data from the TOPMed and UK Biobank (UKB) datasets, focusing on LDL-C (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Our discovery cohorts were TOPMed Freeze10 cohorts and the UKB 200 K release data, comprising 72,175 and 173,982 participants, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S1). The TOPMed Freeze10 cohort encompasses samples from 23 contributing studies (Additional file 1: Table S2). LDL-C measurements were collected from each contributing study, and we performed harmonization and normalization of phenotypes as outlined in the “Methods” section.

For the WGS meta-analysis, we employed the MetaSTAAR pipeline [12], involving three main steps. Step 1 encompassed the preparation of various files in each of the two contributing cohorts, including the phenotype file and sparse genetic relatedness matrix (GRM) to create the null model. In Step 2, we generated summary-level data comprising score statistics and covariance matrix from the two discovery cohorts, also annotating variants using the Functional Annotation of Variants – Online Resource (FAVOR) database [19]. Step 3 involved the meta-analysis at the individual variant level and at the rare variant aggregate level, utilizing different annotation masks and genomic regions.

Subsequently, we performed conditional analysis using common variants from the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (GLGC) [8] to account for previously identified lipid-associated variants. We replicated genome-wide significant findings after conditional analysis in an independent cohort consisting of 86,540 participants from All of Us (AOU) study. Finally, we conducted secondary/sensitivity analysis on aggregates that were not reported in other studies and were associated with LDL-C to gain insights into lipid biology.

Dataset

Contributing studies

Discovery cohort

The discovery cohort included samples from TOPMed freeze10 and the UKB 200 K WGS release. In TOPMed, we included 72,157 participants, where the cohort comprised samples from 23 contributing studies. In UKB, we included 173,982 participants with LDL-C measurements. The UKB data included volunteer residents of the UK aged 40–69 years and were recruited between 2006 and 2010. Consent was previously obtained from each participant regarding the storage of biological specimens, genetic sequencing, access to all available electronic health record (EHR) data, and permission to recontact for future studies. All UKB participants gave written informed consent per the UKB primary protocol. In total, the discovery cohort comprised 246,157 participants with LDL-C measurements (Additional file 1: Table S1). The TOPMed cohort was ancestrally diverse, whereas the UKB cohort comprised individuals of European ancestry. Self-reported race and ethnicity data from cohort participants were used to define population groups for phenotypic modeling. For example, individuals categorized as East Asian participants in TOPMed were primarily Americans of East Asian descent. Detailed information on the contributing TOPMed studies and the UKB cohort is provided in Additional file 3.

Replication cohort

We used All of Us (AOU – version 7) as the independent replication cohort, which included 86,540 participants with LDL-C measures (Additional file 1: Table S1). The All of Us Research Program recruited individuals across the USA, primarily within healthcare institutions, with a goal to improve the engagement of populations typically underrepresented in biomedical research [20]. The first release of genomic data included approximately 98,000 individuals who completed electronic consent modules and health questionnaires upon enrollment. In the current study, we used version 7 data, which included 250 K WGS participants. The AOU cohort is diverse, and the self-reported population group information of the participants was curated from the AOU cohort builder data repository. The AOU self-reported population groups include participants identified as White, Black, or African American, Middle Eastern, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and Asian, where the East Asian and South Asian participants are combined into one group. As reported in the discovery cohort, the population groups were used in the phenotypic modeling. All analyses of All of Us data were carried out in the AOU Researcher Workbench.

Phenotypes

We independently curated LDL-C measurements in the TOPMed and UKB cohorts. LDL-C was either directly measured or calculated by the Friedewald equation (Additional file 1: Table S1). Given the average effect of lipid-lowering medicines observed in placebo-controlled randomized controlled trials, when such medicines were present, we adjusted LDL-C by dividing by 0.7 [6].

For the TOPMed cohort, we obtained phenotype data from the 23 contributing studies, harmonized the data across studies, and performed inverse rank normalization of the residuals of each race within each cohort scaled by the standard deviation of the trait, adjusting for covariates [9]. We included age, age2, sex, the first 10 principal components (PC1–10), study groups, and the Mendelian founder lipid variants APOB p.R3527Q and APOC3 p.R19X for the Amish cohort [21, 22] as covariates.

For the UKB cohort, we curated the first instance of LDL-C (data field numbers:30780). The lipid measurements in mmol/L were converted to mg/dL by multiplying LDL-C measurements by 38.67. We removed unconsented samples from our dataset and normalized LDL-C, as stated above, adjusting for age, age2, sex, and PC1–10 as covariates.

For the AOU cohort, we curated LDL-C measurements and other covariates through the cohort-builder feature available in the AOU Research Workbench (RWB) following the process described in our previous study [23]. The phenotype was adjusted for statin usage and normalized following the same procedures as above.

Genotypes

For the TOPMed cohort, WGS with a goal of > 30X coverage was conducted at seven centers: Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, Northwest Genomics Center, New York Genome Center, Illumina Genomic Services, PSOMAGEN (formerly Macrogen), Baylor College of Medicine Human Genome Sequencing Center, and McDonnell Genome Institute (MGI) at Washington University. Comprehensive details regarding the central quality control on WGS data for TOPMed have been previously described [24]. The Variant Call Format/Binary Call Format (VCF/BCF) files were converted to Genomic Data Structure (GDS) format [25]. The sparse genetic relatedness matrix (GRM) containing relatedness information for the samples in the TOPMed cohort was computed centrally using the FastSparseGRM R package [26] (version: 1.02). Both datasets were downloaded from the dbGap TOPMed Exchange Area.

For the UKB cohort, we processed the 200 K WGS VCF files using VCF to GDS workflow [27]. This workflow begins with individual VCF files available in the UKB Researcher Analysis Platform (RAP) environment, trims (vcf_trimmer), and merges (vcf_merger) them per chromosome. Subsequently, we utilized the vcf2gds function to obtain GDS files. All these steps were executed on the UKB RAP, and the generated data were stored on the same platform; no individual-level data was downloaded from the UKB RAP. Next, we generated the sparse GRM for UKB array genotypes using the FastSpareGRM pipeline [26]. First, we imposed few quality control metrics (–maf 0.001; –geno 0.05; –mind 0.05; –hwe 1e-06) and pruning measures (–indep-pairwise: window-size:50; step-size:5; r2:0.1) to filter samples and variants from UKB array genotypes. We also removed related individuals at this stage and the filtered data were used to generate ancestry divergence estimates which were used to obtain sparse GRM using FastSpareGRM pipeline.

For the AOU cohort, we utilized the variant data structure (VDS) files centrally generated by the AOU team. The Hail (version 0.2) pipeline [28] was employed to narrow down to the region on the genome/gene we aimed to replicate, and the genotype file was saved in Plink format [29]. All these procedures were conducted on the AOU Researcher Workbench (RWB), and the generated data were stored on the same platform; no individual-level data was downloaded from the AOU RWB.

Statistical methods

MetaSTAAR pipeline

We conducted a WGS meta-analysis using the MetaSTAAR (version v0.9.6.3) pipeline [12] for the discovery cohort. MetaSTAAR, an R package, facilitates meta-analysis of rare variant aggregate sets by utilizing curated annotation information from the FAVOR database [19]. FAVOR is an open-access portal for functional annotation of variants and indels from whole genome and whole exome sequencing (WGS/WES) data (https://favor.genohub.org/). It integrates annotation information from a wide range of public databases to support comprehensive variant interpretation. The MetaSTAAR method efficiently integrates variant information, handles large datasets from sequencing projects, and employs a sparse matrix approach to store summary statistics [12]. Notably, the method considers relatedness and population structure, incorporating functionally important variant annotations from various resources as scores to enhance the power of rare variant analysis. It utilizes a sparse GRM and permits the inclusion of study-specific covariates when fitting the null Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM), ensuring computational efficiency while preserving accuracy.

MetaSTAAR employs the MetaSTAAR-worker to independently generate summary-level data for each cohort within their respective secure environments. During the meta-analysis stage, only score statistics and the sparse variance–covariance matrix from each study are utilized for downstream analysis. The MetaSTAAR pipeline also offers the option to integrate FAVORannotator, another R package, for annotating the merged variant list from the discovery cohorts using the FAVOR database. Finally, this method is utilized to conduct individual variant and rare variant aggregate meta-analysis by incorporating multiple functional annotations in the weighting scheme, utilizing the STAAR framework as described below [30, 31]. The STAAR pipeline aggregates gene-centric rare variants into five coding and seven non-coding masks based on its annotations. In the meta-analysis stage, MetaSTAAR integrates these outcomes and generates MetaSTAAR-O p-value for each of the variant sets.

STAARpipeline

We conducted WGS replication analysis using the STAARpipeline (version v0.9.7) [31] within the All of Us (AOU) cohort. STAARpipeline is an R package employed for performing individual variant and rare variant aggregate tests in individual sequencing cohorts. The variant set analysis in the STAARpipeline encompasses both gene-centric and non-gene-centric (region-based) analyses of rare variants. The gene-centric coding analysis in STAARpipeline provides insights into five genetic categories: putative loss of function (pLoF), missense, disruptive missense, pLoF plus disruptive missense, and synonymous variants. The gene-centric noncoding analysis offers seven genetic categories: promoter or enhancer overlaid with annotations from Cap Analysis of Gene Expression (CAGE) or DNase hypersensitivity sites (DHS) sites, UTR (untranslated region), upstream and downstream. The region-based analysis includes both sliding window analysis with fixed sizes and dynamic window analysis with data-adaptive sizes. The method in the STAARpipeline conducts aggregate testing for each variant set and calculates the STAAR-O p-value. This omnibus test aggregates multiple annotation-weighted burden tests, SKAT (Sequence Kernel Association Test), and ACAT-V (Adaptive Combined Association Test for Variants) within the STAAR framework [30].

WGS meta-analysis

In Step 1, we prepared data files for the MetaSTAAR implementation (see Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Phenotypic data were curated from each discovery cohort (TOPMed Freeze10, UKB 200 K), and necessary files for the meta-analysis, including genotypes in GDS format, sparse GRMs, and null models for both cohorts, were generated. In Step 2, we used the MetaSTAAR-worker to generate score statistics and the sparse variance–covariance matrix independently for each cohort. For the UKB 200 K data, these files were generated in the UKB-RAP. Once the summary-level data were independently generated, we merged the variants from both cohorts and utilized the FAVOR annotator to create annotated-GDS files, annotating merged variants with information from different repositories, as documented in the FAVOR database [19]. This merged list of variants, along with the summary-level information from each cohort, was used in Step 3 for conducting the WGS meta-analysis.

Individual variant meta-analysis

For the individual variant meta-analysis of common and low-frequency variants across the genome, we utilized a combined minor allele count (MAC) greater than 20 (MAC > 20). The function Individual_Analysis_meta() within the MetaSTAAR pipeline was employed to account for single variants throughout the genome. We applied a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of MetaSTAAR-O p-value ≤ 5 × 10−09 to identify genome-wide significant variants. Results from this study were compared with those from the latest published Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (GLGC) array-based GWAS [8] complete summary statistics to identify novel loci and variants unique to the WGS meta-analysis.

Rare variant aggregate meta-analysis

For the rare variant aggregate set meta-analysis, we used a combined minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 1% (MAF < 1%). In the gene-centric approach for approximately 20 K genes, variants mapping to the five coding masks and the seven non-coding masks were used as aggregate sets for the analysis, as specified by the STAARpipeline. The functions Gene_Centric_Coding_meta() and Gene_Centric_Noncoding_meta() were employed for the meta-analysis. A Bonferroni-corrected threshold accounting for the number of masks for both coding and non-coding gene-centric approaches (Coding [0.05/(5-mask*20 K-genes)] MetaSTAAR-O p-value ≤ 5 × 10−07; Non-coding [0.05/(7-mask*20 K-genes)] MetaSTAAR-O p-value < = 3.57 × 10−07) was applied to identify genome-wide significant rare variant aggregate sets. In the region-based sliding-window approach, approximately 2.6 M regions were scanned statically on the entire genome of the merged discovery cohort, with a 2-kb window and 1-kb overlap size. A Bonferroni-corrected threshold [0.05/(2.6 × 1−06)] of MetaSTAAR-O p-value ≤ 1.88 × 10−08 was used to identify region aggregate sets that were genome-wide significant. Additionally, a nominal significance threshold (p-value ≤ 0.05) was applied for the three statistical tests (SKAT, burden, ACAT-V) used in the MetaSTAAR pipeline to filter spurious hits driven by one or two extremely rare variants.

Conditional analysis

We conducted conditional analysis, accounting for known common variants in both individual variant and rare variant genome-wide significant results obtained from MetaSTAAR. We used all the genome-wide significant variants from the published GLGC summary statistics for LDL-C and total cholesterol in our conditional analysis [8]. From the GLGC locus, we defined a +/− 500-kb window and filtered all the variants from the current study mapping to that region. Those variants were defined as known signal, and the rest were defined as novel variants in the individual variant analysis. For rare variants, we filtered all genome-wide significant aggregates from coding, non-coding, and sliding-window-based approaches. For these filtered aggregates, summary and covariance information were generated from both the discovery cohort while accounting for genome-wide significant variants from GLGC. The function generate_MetaSTAAR_cov_cond() was used for this step of the conditional analysis. In the conditional analysis step, MetaSTAAR uses the list of known variants and generates the score statistic vector and creates a variance–covariance matrix of these variation and the rare variants in the aggregate sets. Next the method computes adjusted score statistics by accounting for the covariance between the rare and known variants. Subsequently, we ran the MetaSTAAR conditional step using the functions Gene_Centric_Coding_meta_cond(), Gene_Centric_Noncoding_meta_cond(), and Sliding_Window_meta_cond() for their respective categories. After the conditional analysis, we used the genome-wide significant p-value threshold mentioned above to filter aggregates that passed the conditional analysis.

Replication in an independent cohort

We employed All of Us (AOU) as the replication cohort, utilizing the version 7 release of the data, which included approximately 86 K WGS samples with LDL-C measured for the current study. All aggregate sets resulting from both gene-centric coding and non-coding approaches that successfully passed the conditional analysis were brought forward to replication. For the sliding-window approach, replication was limited to the regions that were distinctive and not encompassed by the gene-centric approach. The STAARpipeline was implemented for various masks and regions, applying a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (0.05/number of aggregates tested) STAAR-O p-value ≤ 0.001 for gene-centric replication and a nominal significance threshold was employed for the replication of sliding-window aggregates.

Secondary analysis

From the genome-wide significant individual variant results, we used the FUMA pipeline [13] to identify novel genomic loci. Summary statistics from the single-variant GWAS were used to determine lead variants (i.e., variant with the smallest p-value at a locus). As a reference dataset, we employed the 1000 Genomes (Phase 3-ALL) [32], applying linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (r2 ≥ 0.6 within 250 kb) to define independent signals. The resulting set of variants was used to map genomic risk loci via the FUMA web interface (see Additional file 1: Table S3). These genomic risk loci were compared with the list of loci from GLGC to identify novel hits.

From the rare variant genome-wide significant results, we determined specific genes and regions that had strong association to LDL-C and integrated results from both gene-centric and region-based results. We examined annotation scores (including annotation principal components (aPC)) [19, 30] and identified the most significant p-values from these scores in relation to regions on the genes. The aPC are derived by applying principal component analysis (PCA) to a matrix of functional annotations associated with genetic variants. Each aPC represents a linear combination of original annotation features, capturing distinct patterns of variation in the functional annotation landscape. To compute aPC, a comprehensive set of functional annotations each capturing a specific aspect of variant biological function, including epigenetics, conservation, protein function, local nucleotide diversity, distance to coding, mutation density, transcription factors, mappability, distance to TSS/TES, and micro RNA was assembled. In the constructed matrix where each row corresponded to a variant and each column to an annotation feature. PCA was then performed on this matrix to extract principal components that summarize the variance in the annotation data. The resulting aPCs serve as composite scores that encapsulate the combined information from multiple annotations. The utility of the aPCs was assessed by incorporating them into rare variant association analyses. In the current study, we used the aPC scores to pinpoint hot-spot regions and variants associated with LDL-C. Additionally, we used external resources such as the Genomics 2 Proteins (G2P) portal [33] and utilized protein model structures from AlphaFold (version v2.0 pipeline) [15] to delve into the important residues that emerged as significant through the aPC scoring system.

Individual-level whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data and harmonized lipid phenotypes for TOPMed are available via restricted access through the TOPMed dbGaP Exchange area portal. The dbGaP accession numbers for each of the TOPMed cohorts are provided in the Additional File 3. UK Biobank (UKB) WGS data can be accessed through the UKB Research Analysis Platform (RAP) following approval via the UKB access system (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk). Whole-genome sequence data from the All of Us (AoU) program are accessible through the AoU Researcher Workbench (https://www.researchallofus.org). The code for implementing MetaSTAAR is publicly available at https://github.com/xihaoli/MetaSTAAR, with additional STAAR workflows provided at https://github.com/xihaoli/STAAR and https://github.com/xihaoli/STAARpipeline. FAVOR database is available at https://favor.genohub.org/.
