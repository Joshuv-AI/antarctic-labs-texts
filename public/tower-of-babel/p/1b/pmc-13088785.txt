Early clinical outcomes of standard and 3D-planned pre-bent hook plates versus suture-button fixation for acute Rockwood IIIB–V acromioclavicular dislocation with a Bigliani type II acromion: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jun Lin, Shenggui Xu, Weizhong Guo, Chengshou Lin, Chun Chen, Feng Li, Qilong Huang, Dong Zhou, ShiQing Lei, Shuai Zhou, Qingshan Xu, Zhenbao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical management of acute high-grade (Rockwood IIIB–V) acromioclavicular joint dislocation (ACD) remains controversial. Standard clavicular hook plating (CHP) provides reliable reduction, but subacromial irritation/osteolysis and routine plate removal remain important concerns, particularly in patients with a Bigliani type II acromion. Three-dimensional (3D) planning may improve implant fit, whereas suture-button (SB) fixation represents a minimally invasive alternative. We compared Standard CHP, 3D-planned pre-bent CHP (3D-CHP), and SB fixation with respect to early recovery, subacromial morbidity, and secondary procedure burden.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 125 patients with acute high-grade ACD and a Bigliani type II acromion treated at a single center (CHP, n = 49; 3D-CHP, n = 33; SB, n = 43). The primary endpoint was VAS pain score at 3 months, and the key secondary endpoint was Constant–Murley Score (CMS) at 3 months. Additional outcomes included shoulder range of motion, coracoclavicular distance (CCD) and related radiographic events, subacromial erosion, complications, and secondary procedures within 24 months.ResultsBaseline characteristics were broadly comparable across groups. At 3 months, 3D-CHP and SB showed lower VAS pain scores and higher CMS than Standard CHP (both P < 0.05). By 24 months, pain, function, and radiographic maintenance were comparable across groups (all P > 0.05). Subacromial erosion was less frequent after 3D-CHP and absent after SB than after Standard CHP (P < 0.001). Planned plate removal accounted for most secondary procedures in the hook-plate groups, whereas unplanned reoperation was rare and did not differ significantly among groups (P = 0.144). SB subgroup outcomes were similar between Rockwood IIIB and pooled IV/V injuries.ConclusionsAll three approaches achieved comparable 24-month functional and radiographic outcomes. The main between-technique differences appeared to concern early postoperative recovery, hook-related subacromial morbidity, and implant-related secondary procedure burden. Introduction

Acromioclavicular joint dislocation (ACD) accounts for approximately 9%–12% of all shoulder injuries [1] and predominantly affects physically active young men and manual workers [2]. It typically results from a direct blow to the lateral shoulder, disrupting the acromioclavicular joint and the coracoclavicular ligament complex and causing combined vertical and horizontal instability [3]. Treatment is commonly guided by the Rockwood classification: types I–II are usually managed nonoperatively, whereas types IV–V generally warrant surgical stabilization because of marked displacement [4]. Type III injuries remain controversial, particularly type IIIB with clinically relevant horizontal instability, and recent studies increasingly support operative stabilization to reduce persistent pain and functional limitation [5, 6].

A wide range of surgical techniques has been described for ACD [7], which can be broadly categorized into rigid and elastic fixation. Clavicular hook plating (CHP) remains widely used because it is technically straightforward and provides reliable reduction [8]. However, subacromial impingement and bony erosion of the acromion or distal clavicle are well-recognized postoperative concerns [9, 10]. In addition, hook-plate fixation usually requires planned implant removal, increasing the secondary procedure burden [11]. Because the risk of impingement appears to be anatomy-dependent, acromial morphology has attracted increasing attention [12]. The Bigliani classification is commonly used to describe acromial shape [13], and a type II acromion has been associated with a higher incidence of subacromial impingement syndrome and subacromial osteolysis after standard CHP [14, 15]. Against this background, three-dimensional (3D) preoperative planning has been adopted as a pragmatic refinement to facilitate patient-specific contouring of the hook plate. By improving conformity between the hook tip and the undersurface of the acromion, this approach may help reduce implant-related subacromial irritation [16]. In parallel, suture-button (SB) fixation has emerged as an elastic alternative that restores vertical stability using a suture-and-button construct across the coracoclavicular interval. It typically requires smaller incisions, provides extra-articular fixation, and avoids routine implant removal [17]. Whether such an elastic construct can maintain durable stability under high loads, particularly in high-grade injuries with combined vertical and horizontal shear forces, remains uncertain [18].

Multiple comparative studies have shown that both SB fixation and CHP can achieve satisfactory mid- to long-term clinical outcomes with acceptable radiographic stability [19]. Evidence regarding early recovery, however, remains mixed. Some studies suggest that SB may provide earlier pain relief and faster functional improvement [20], whereas others have reported only modest or inconsistent differences between techniques [21]. Several factors continue to limit interpretation of the current evidence. Many studies are underpowered and include heterogeneous injury patterns, with substantial variation in Rockwood grade distribution. Reported outcomes have often focused on function and radiographic measures, whereas secondary procedures and treatment burden have been less consistently assessed. In addition, the practical implementation of 3D preoperative planning has not been well characterized. Severity-stratified data for SB, particularly in higher-grade injuries, also remain limited, leaving uncertainty as to whether treatment effects are consistent across injury subgroups. In routine practice, Standard CHP therefore remains widely used because of its technical simplicity and mechanical predictability, whereas 3D-assisted CHP and SB are more selectively applied [22].

In this context, we conducted a single-centre retrospective cohort study of patients with acute high-grade ACD and a Bigliani type II acromion. We compared Standard CHP, 3D-planned pre-bent CHP (3D-CHP), and SB fixation with respect to early clinical recovery, radiographic maintenance, implant-related subacromial morbidity, and secondary procedure burden. Within the SB group, we also prespecified a severity-stratified analysis comparing Rockwood type IIIB injuries with pooled type IV/V injuries. We hypothesised that all three techniques would provide comparable 24-month functional and radiographic outcomes, while differing mainly in early recovery, implant-related subacromial morbidity, and implant-related secondary procedure burden.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because data were anonymised prior to analysis. The study was designed and reported following the STROBE guidelines [23]. Eligible patients with acute high-grade acromioclavicular joint dislocation (ACD) were identified from the institutional surgical database.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria

(1) age 18–70 years; (2) unilateral acute ACD with an injury-to-surgery interval ≤ 3 weeks; and (3) imaging confirmation of high-grade ACD (Rockwood types IIIB–V) with a Bigliani type II acromion.

Exclusion criteria

(1) chronic dislocation with an injury-to-surgery interval > 3 weeks; (2) prior surgery on the affected shoulder or substantial pre-injury functional impairment; (3) concomitant ipsilateral fractures requiring surgical fixation (e.g., clavicular shaft, scapular, or proximal humeral fractures); (4) pathological dislocation related to tumour or infection; (5) incomplete medical records or inability to complete the prespecified follow-up schedule, defined as postoperative follow-up < 24 months.

Acromial morphology classification

Bigliani acromial type was determined on standard shoulder radiographs according to the contour of the acromial undersurface, with type II defined as a curved acromion. CT was reviewed when radiographic morphology was equivocal. Two attending orthopaedic surgeons, blinded to treatment group and follow-up outcomes, independently assessed acromial type, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability for Bigliani type II classification were not prospectively collected.

Grouping

Between January 2019 and December 2023, 172 patients with acute high-grade ACD and a Bigliani type II acromion were screened. After application of the eligibility criteria, 30 patients were excluded (6 chronic dislocations, 8 severe concomitant injuries, 3 prior ipsilateral shoulder surgery or marked pre-injury functional limitation, and 13 incomplete records), and 17 were lost to follow-up before 24 months. The final cohort comprised 125 patients: 49 treated with Standard CHP, 33 with 3D-CHP, and 43 with SB (Fig. 1). Treatment allocation was not randomized. Procedure selection reflected routine clinical decision-making based on injury characteristics, local anatomy, implant/workflow availability, and patient-specific factors. Most procedures were performed by a single senior surgeon. Patients were grouped by fixation strategy rather than by implant brand. The three techniques were applied in parallel during the study period rather than in mutually exclusive eras.

Surgical techniques

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the beach-chair position. Prophylactic intravenous antibiotics were administered preoperatively.

Standard CHP

A curvilinear incision (approximately 6 cm) was made from the lateral clavicle to the acromion to expose the acromioclavicular joint. After debridement and open reduction, a pre-contoured clavicular hook plate was inserted beneath the acromion and secured to the clavicle with screws. In the hook-plate groups, the implant used was the Clavicle Hook Locking Plate II (Double Medical Technology Inc., Xiamen, China), a 3.5-mm titanium clavicular hook plate compatible with 3.5-mm locking and/or cortical screws, with available hook depths of 15 mm and 18 mm. Reduction and implant position were confirmed fluoroscopically, and the wound was closed in layers. Coracoclavicular ligament repair was not routinely performed.

3D-CHP

Thin-slice CT of both acromioclavicular joints (slice thickness 0.625 mm) was obtained. DICOM data were imported into Mimics 21.0 for 3D reconstruction. A contralateral mirrored model was used to generate the target reduction anatomy. Virtual planning was performed using STL models of the same commercially available hook plate described above. In each case, the virtual STL model corresponded to the implant ultimately used intraoperatively. After optimization of implant position beneath the mirrored Bigliani type II acromion, measured prebending parameters were used to contour the plate before implantation. Intraoperative exposure, reduction, and fixation steps were otherwise consistent with the Standard CHP procedure, with fluoroscopic confirmation.

SB fixation

Two small incisions (approximately 2 cm each) were made, one near the acromioclavicular joint and another near the coracoid projection. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a targeting guide was used to drill a tunnel from the clavicle to the base of the coracoid. A commercially available suture-button device, CanLoop™ Adjustable Strap Titanium Plate (Zhejiang Canwell Medical Co., Ltd., Jinhua, China), was then passed through the tunnel, with the inferior button seated under the coracoid base and the superior button resting on the clavicle. After confirming anatomic reduction, the sutures were tied to restore the coracoclavicular distance, followed by fluoroscopic verification and closure in layers. No supplementary acromioclavicular ligament repair or Kirschner wire augmentation was performed. The surgical workflow and representative intraoperative fluoroscopic images are shown in Fig. 2.

Postoperative rehabilitation

All patients followed a standardised rehabilitation protocol. The operated arm was immobilised in a sling for 4–6 weeks. During this period, gentle pendulum exercises were encouraged and motion of the elbow, wrist, and fingers was permitted. Passive shoulder mobilisation started in the first postoperative week, and unloaded active forward flexion and abduction were initiated after 2 weeks. From weeks 4–6 onward, active range-of-motion exercises and progressive strengthening were advanced, with a gradual return to activities of daily living by approximately 12 weeks.

In the hook-plate groups, implant removal was planned through the original incision at 9–12 months. In the SB group, routine device removal was not planned and was considered only for persistent symptoms or device-related complications.

Outcome measures

Assessments were performed at five prespecified time points: preoperative (T0), immediate postoperative (T1), and postoperative 3 months (T2), 12 months (T3), and 24 months (T4). Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), injured side, injury mechanism, Rockwood classification (types IIIB–V), and injury-to-surgery interval. Clinical outcomes, radiographic measurements, and adverse events were collected according to the prespecified follow-up schedule.

Primary outcome. VAS pain score at 3 months (T2).

Key secondary outcome. Constant–Murley Score (CMS) at 3 months (T2).

Secondary outcomes. (1) ROM (forward flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation) at T2 and T4; (2) VAS and CMS at T3 and T4; (3) radiographic outcomes—CCD at T0, T1, and T4, CCD change (T4–T1), and loss of reduction, defined as a CCD increase of ≥ 5 mm from T1 to T4 (< 5 mm was considered physiological settling [24]); (4) clinical complications, including subacromial impingement, wound problems, infection, and overall complication rate; (5) secondary procedure outcomes within 24 months, including unplanned reoperation, defined as any non-protocol secondary surgery for persistent symptoms, complications, fixation failure, or recurrent instability, and planned hook plate removal in the CHP and 3D-CHP groups as a protocol-mandated procedure inherent to the treatment strategy; and (6) perioperative metrics, including operative time, blood loss, and incision length.

A prespecified exploratory subgroup analysis within the SB group (Rockwood IIIB vs. pooled IV/V) evaluated whether recovery and radiographic maintenance differed by injury grade. Radiographic measurements were performed independently by two blinded assessors, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python (statsmodels). Because of the retrospective design, no formal sample size calculation was performed; all eligible patients were included, and comparisons for uncommon adverse events were interpreted cautiously. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and were compared using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, with Tukey’s HSD or Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests as appropriate. Two-group comparisons used the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Cohen’s d was calculated for between-group differences in VAS and CMS at T2. Longitudinal VAS and CMS from T2 to T4 were analysed using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for time, group, and time × group interaction, and a random intercept for participant. Planned hook-plate removal and unplanned reoperation were analysed separately; because planned removal was protocol-mandated in the hook-plate groups, it was not treated as a complication-driven comparative outcome. Unplanned reoperation was compared descriptively and with Fisher’s exact test. Relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs were calculated for selected binary outcomes versus the Standard CHP group, when estimable.

Prespecified exploratory analyses included an SB subgroup comparison (Rockwood IIIB vs. pooled IV/V) and multivariable linear regression for 24-month CMS including age, sex, BMI, injury-to-surgery interval, Rockwood classification (IV/V pooled), and treatment group, with Standard CHP as the reference. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Perioperative metrics and postoperative clinical outcomes

All procedures were completed as planned without major intraoperative complications. Compared with hook-plate fixation, SB required longer operative time but a smaller incision and less blood loss (all P < 0.001). CMS and ROM were not recorded preoperatively because of pain-limited function.

VAS at T2 was lower in the 3D-CHP and SB groups than in the Standard CHP group (P < 0.001). CMS at T2 also differed among groups (P = 0.003), favoring 3D-CHP and SB, with small differences in selected ROM measures. Standardized effect sizes at T2 showed a larger between-group effect for VAS than for CMS (Cohen’s d, Table 2). Differences in VAS and CMS diminished at T3 and were not statistically evident at T4. In mixed-effects models, the time×group interaction was significant for VAS (P < 0.001) but not for CMS (P = 0.195). At 3 months, Cohen’s d was 2.12 for VAS in 3D-CHP vs. CHP and 2.60 in SB vs. CHP; the corresponding CMS values were 0.62 and 0.65.

Radiographic outcomes

Following the early clinical comparisons, we assessed radiographic maintenance and implant-related changes (Table 3). CCD decreased from T0 to T1 in all groups. CCD at T0, T1, and T4, as well as ΔCCD (T4 − T1), did not differ among groups, and loss of reduction (ΔCCD ≥ 5 mm) was uncommon and comparable (all P > 0.05). In contrast, subacromial erosion showed a clear gradient—most frequent after Standard CHP, less common after 3D-CHP, and absent after SB (P < 0.001). ΔCCD was not correlated with 24-month CMS (r = − 0.013, P = 0.890; Fig. 3).

Complications and secondary procedures

Complications and secondary procedures are summarised in Table 4. Subacromial impingement occurred in 10/49 (20.4%) patients in the Standard CHP group, 2/33 (6.1%) in the 3D-CHP group, and 0/43 (0%) in the SB group (P = 0.003). Overall complication rates also differed among groups (P = 0.018). Planned plate removal was performed in 44/49 (89.8%) Standard CHP and 29/33 (87.9%) 3D-CHP patients according to the postoperative protocol; no routine implant removal was planned in the SB group. Unplanned reoperation occurred in 2/43 (4.7%) SB patients and in no patients in the Standard CHP or 3D-CHP groups, with no significant between-group difference (P = 0.144). Total secondary procedure rates differed significantly among groups (P < 0.001). Patient-level details of all recorded clinical complications are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

In this anatomy-selected cohort of acute high-grade ACD with a Bigliani type II acromion, 3D-planned pre-bent hook-plate fixation was associated with fewer hook-related subacromial problems than Standard CHP. Subacromial impingement occurred less often after 3D-CHP (6.1% vs. 20.4%, P = 0.003), and subacromial erosion/osteolysis was also less frequent (9.1% vs. 32.7%, P < 0.001). A plausible explanation is improved hook–acromion congruence achieved through virtual implantation and patient-specific prebending, which may reduce focal contact pressure during abduction. However, causal inference is limited by the retrospective design, and the existing literature supports the technical feasibility of digital matching more strongly than definitive clinical superiority. These findings suggest that 3D-guided prebending may represent a mitigation-oriented refinement of hook-plate fixation, although this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating given the retrospective design.

Beyond implant–acromion congruence, the three strategies showed distinct early recovery profiles. The injury-to-surgery interval was comparable among groups (P = 0.115), making it less likely that the early differences were attributable to systematic variation in surgical timing. Consistent with prior comparative reports [25, 26], Standard CHP showed less favorable early pain and functional recovery compared with 3D-CHP and SB, although these differences diminished over time and outcomes converged by the final follow-up. At 3 months, the between-group separation was more pronounced for pain than for function: VAS was approximately 1.4–1.6 points lower after 3D-CHP and SB than after Standard CHP, whereas the CMS difference was more modest (approximately 3 points). Standardized effect sizes at T2 were also larger for pain than for function (Cohen’s d: 2.12 for VAS in 3D-CHP vs. CHP and 2.60 in SB vs. CHP; corresponding CMS values, 0.62 and 0.65). Although a pain minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of approximately 1.4 points has been reported in broader shoulder outcome literature, its direct applicability to acute acromioclavicular dislocation is uncertain. Therefore, the observed VAS difference should be viewed as potentially, rather than definitively, clinically meaningful [27, 28]. In contrast, the approximately 3-point CMS advantage at 3 months, although statistically significant, appears clinically modest. In a prospective study of acute high-grade acromioclavicular joint separation, Stein et al. reported an AC-specific MCID of 16.6 points for the Constant score [27], which is substantially larger than the between-group difference observed in our cohort. This suggests that the early functional advantage detected here was statistically detectable but likely small in clinical magnitude. At the same time, that threshold was derived in a different AC-separation cohort and follow-up context, so its application to 3-month recovery should remain cautious. By 24 months, pain, function, ROM, and radiographic stability were broadly comparable across groups. This interpretation was further supported by an exploratory adjusted analysis, in which between-technique differences in 24-month CMS remained limited after accounting for baseline covariates. Accordingly, the main distinctions among these procedures appear to relate to early recovery and differences in complication profiles and secondary procedure burden rather than in mid-term functional outcome.

Beyond early recovery, the between-group contrast was also reflected in complication profiles and implant-related secondary procedure burden. Overall clinical complication rates differed among groups (P = 0.018), with fewer events after 3D-CHP and SB, although only selected pairwise differences remained significant after multiplicity adjustment. Subacromial impingement showed the clearest gradient, occurring in 20.4% of Standard CHP cases, 6.1% of 3D-CHP cases, and no SB cases (overall P = 0.003). In contrast, the marked difference in total secondary procedures was driven largely by routine hook plate removal, which is inherent to hook-plate fixation and therefore reflects treatment strategy rather than complication-related failure. When the analysis was restricted to unplanned reoperations, event rates were low and no significant between-group difference was observed. Even so, the need for a routine second operation may remain clinically relevant because it adds treatment burden, may interrupt recovery, and may increase resource use; in this respect, avoiding routine implant removal may still offer practical advantages for a typically young and active population [29–31].

Radiographically, all three fixation methods achieved similar maintenance of reduction. CCD did not differ among groups at any time point, nor did ΔCCD from T1 to T4 or the proportion meeting the prespecified threshold for loss of reduction, consistent with prior reports [32]. Although a subset of patients showed mild CCD settling by 24 months, this change was not associated with functional outcome; the correlation between ΔCCD and 24-month CMS was negligible (r = − 0.013, P = 0.890). Together, these findings suggest a degree of radiographic–clinical dissociation, in which limited late displacement on imaging may not translate into meaningful functional deterioration [33]. Clinically, these findings may support a balanced surgical objective: restoring stability while preserving soft-tissue integrity, rather than increasing dissection or additional procedures solely to optimize radiographic alignment. In line with Guo et al. [34], careful soft-tissue handling and minimization of surgical trauma may be more relevant to recovery than marginal radiographic gains, while also reducing implant-related secondary procedure burden. Longer follow-up is needed to determine whether reduction settling progresses beyond 24 months or has delayed functional consequences.

In the prespecified stratified analysis within the SB group (Rockwood IIIB vs. pooled IV/V), outcomes appeared broadly similar across follow-up (Table 5). As expected, IV/V injuries showed greater baseline vertical displacement, reflected by a larger preoperative CCD than IIIB injuries (20.5 ± 6.4 mm vs. 15.3 ± 2.8 mm; P = 0.010). However, postoperative CCD at T1 and T4, as well as ΔCCD from T1 to T4, did not differ significantly between subgroups. Likewise, VAS and CMS at T2 and T4 were similar (all other P > 0.05). These findings did not provide clear evidence of injury-grade–dependent variation in SB performance within 24 months. However, given the small sample size, pooling of Rockwood IV and V injuries, and the absence of multiplicity adjustment, this analysis should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory and warrants validation in larger prospective cohorts with longer follow-up [35].

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study without random treatment allocation, this analysis remains susceptible to residual confounding and selection bias despite broadly comparable baseline characteristics and multivariable adjustment. Because treatment allocation was pragmatic rather than protocol-driven, confounding by indication cannot be excluded, particularly given the lack of consistent data on hand dominance, occupational demand, baseline activity level or sports participation, and patient preference regarding a planned second procedure. Second, because the study spanned 2019–2023, temporal variation in technique use and possible learning-curve effects, particularly for 3D-assisted planning and SB fixation, may have influenced the results. Third, case-level documentation of specific hook-plate brands and size variants was not consistently available. Fourth, loss to follow-up and the 24-month follow-up duration may have affected the estimates and may not capture later sequelae. Fifth, generalizability is limited because the cohort was restricted to acute high-grade ACD with a Bigliani type II acromion. Finally, the Rockwood-stratified SB subgroup analysis was underpowered, and inter- and intraobserver reliability for Bigliani type II acromion classification were not prospectively collected, which is important because this classification defined cohort eligibility. Future multicenter prospective studies with longer follow-up, standardized assessment of horizontal AC stability, prospective reliability assessment of acromial morphology, and more detailed implant-specific recording are needed. Resource use and patient-centered endpoints may further clarify the roles of 3D-guided hook-plate contouring and SB fixation.

Conclusions

In acute high-grade ACD with a Bigliani type II acromion, Standard CHP, 3D-CHP, and SB approaches produced broadly comparable 24-month functional outcomes and radiographic maintenance. Differences were mainly observed in early symptoms, implant-related subacromial morbidity, and secondary procedure burden. Standard CHP provides reliable mechanical support but is more frequently associated with subacromial irritation and usually necessitates planned implant removal. 3D-CHP retains the structural characteristics of hook-plate fixation and was associated with improved implant–acromion conformity in this cohort. This finding may reflect better implant–acromion congruence after measurement-guided prebending, but should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design and the limited external evidence establishing definitive clinical superiority of 3D planning. The lower number of secondary procedures after SB should be interpreted primarily in light of the absence of routine implant removal, whereas unplanned reoperations were uncommon across all groups. Therefore, procedure selection may be individualized based on patient priorities (e.g., return-to-work/sport), willingness to undergo a planned second operation, and local anatomical considerations.
