Perioperative changes in diaphragm thickening fraction in cardiac surgery patients
Authors: Gülfem Yildirim, Serkan Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Diaphragm ultrasonography has been proposed as a bedside tool to assess diaphragm function.MethodsIn this prospective study, 133 patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery were evaluated. Diaphragm thickening fraction (TF) was measured using ultrasound one day before and one day after surgery. The perioperative change in TF (ΔTF) was also calculated. Introduction

Cardiac surgery carries an inherent risk of perioperative mortality and morbidity depending on the characteristics of the patients, their initial condition, and the type of surgical intervention [1, 2]. Various prediction models have been created in the preoperative period to estimate the surgical risk in patients. For successful extubation after surgery, the patient’s clinical and hemodynamic status should be good, and the patient’s respiratory data and hemodynamic imbalances should also be controlled. One of the most difficult situations in the postoperative period is to separate patients from mechanical ventilation, and diaphragm dysfunction is one of the most important reasons for this [3].

The diaphragm is one of the largest muscles in humans and serves as the primary respiratory muscle [4]. Diaphragmatic contraction directs changes in intrathoracic pressure during inspiration and expiration. The diaphragm thus acts as a respiratory pump, increasing systemic venous return and regulating left ventricular (LV) afterload. This continuation of respiration is essential for both the systemic and pulmonary venous systems. Diaphragm dysfunction is a major contributor to poor recovery from mechanical ventilation (MV), longer ICU stay, and increased ICU mortality, and is a predictor of prolonged MV [5].

Various imaging modalities can be used to assess diaphragm function. Chest X-rays are used to assess the borders and position of the diaphragm and are often the preferred initial assessment of diaphragm dysfunction by clinicians. Other imaging modalities used for suspected diaphragm dysfunction include fluoroscopic evaluation, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging. In addition to imaging modalities, inspiratory muscle strength measurements such as maximal inspiratory pressure (Pimax) can be used to assess the degree of diaphragm dysfunction [6]. Ultrasound (US) is a cost-effective and practical method that provides data on diaphragm function, excursion, thickness and thickening and can also be used during movement. Therefore, it is frequently preferred for bedside evaluations today. Decreased diaphragm thickness in ultrasound evaluation is an indicator of diaphragm atrophy. Studies have shown that ultrasonographic detection of the diaphragm is feasible, reproducible and quite reliable. Recently, US measurement of the diaphragm has been suggested to be valuable in predicting the optimum time for extubation in patients undergoing mechanical ventilation.

Mechanical ventilator support is the most effective method for respiratory support in the postoperative period, as the process prolongs, diaphragmatic dysfunction may develop, and it may become increasingly difficult for patients to be weaned from mechanical ventilation. This may suggest diaphragm dysfunction in patients who extubate late after cardiac surgery [7]. If diaphragmatic dysfunction develops after cardiac surgery, complications such as respiratory distress, pneumonia, atelectasis, and decreased exercise capacity may develop in the long term. To reduce this risk, early mobilization of patients should be ensured, and the process should be supported with physical therapy and pulmonary rehabilitation. Thus, complications are tried to be prevented by strengthening the diaphragm muscles [8]. In a study by Cammarota et al. (2019), preoperative diaphragm thickness measured via ultrasonography was found to be associated with difficult weaning and longer ICU stay following major thoracic surgery [9]. However, such data are limited in the context of cardiac surgery, where the impact of diaphragmatic dysfunction on postoperative recovery has not been fully elucidated. Our study was designed to evaluate whether preoperative ultrasonographic assessment of diaphragm thickness could provide a non-invasive surrogate marker of diaphragmatic function and predict postoperative outcomes such as ICU length of stay and recovery profile in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.”

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective study was conducted between March 2021 and October 2021, and data analysis was completed in March 2023. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the relevant Institutional Ethics Committee on November 19, 2021 (Approval No: 2021/017), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 133 consecutive patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery were included in the study. Patients younger than 18 years, those undergoing emergency surgery, those with diaphragmatic eventration detected on preoperative chest X-ray, a history of pneumothorax, phrenic nerve injury, or neuromuscular disease, those undergoing redo surgery, pregnant patients, those who developed postoperative pneumonia, and those who had undergone segmentectomy, lobectomy, or pneumonectomy were excluded.

Due to the exploratory nature of the study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and all eligible patients during the study period were included consecutively.

Surgical procedure and perioperative management

All patients were operated on by four experienced cardiac surgeons. The left internal thoracic artery, saphenous vein, and radial artery were routinely used as grafts for coronary artery bypass grafting when indicated. Myocardial protection was achieved using blood cardioplegia administered via antegrade, retrograde, or combined routes. Topical cooling was not used.

Patients were managed under mild hypothermia (32–35 °C) and received standardized anesthetic care with propofol and remifentanil infusions. Mechanical ventilation was provided using tidal volumes of 5–7 mL/kg and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 cmH₂O. After ICU admission, ventilator settings were adjusted according to arterial blood gas values.

Data collection and clinical variables

Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and perioperative clinical data were recorded. Extubation time (minutes), ICU length of stay, and total hospital length of stay were documented.

Extubation time was analyzed as a continuous variable. Due to the low number of patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation, categorical analysis using predefined thresholds was not performed.

The primary outcome of the study was ICU length of stay.

Diaphragm ultrasound assessment

Diaphragm thickness measurements were performed by a single experienced pulmonologist one day before surgery and one day after surgery using ultrasonography. Measurements were obtained using a high-frequency linear probe (7–18 MHz).

The probe was positioned at the anterior axillary line between the eighth and tenth intercostal spaces. The diaphragm was identified as a three-layered structure consisting of pleural and peritoneal membranes with an intervening hypoechoic muscle layer.

Patients were instructed to inhale to total lung capacity and exhale to residual volume during measurement. Thickness at end-inspiration (TEI) and thickness at end-expiration (TEE) were recorded.

Measurements were obtained preoperatively and postoperatively in all patients. The perioperative change in TF (ΔTF) was defined as the difference between postoperative and preoperative values.

All ultrasound measurements were performed by a single experienced pulmonologist with expertise in diaphragm ultrasonography. Although the operator was not blinded to clinical data, a standardized measurement protocol was strictly followed to minimize variability. Interobserver variability was not assessed; however, the use of a single operator ensured internal consistency.

Definition of variables

Diaphragm thickening fraction (TF) was analyzed as a continuous variable. Diaphragm dysfunction was not used as a predefined primary outcome. However, for exploratory purposes, diaphragm dysfunction was defined as TF < 20% based on previous literature, and subgroup analyses were performed accordingly.

ICU length of stay was analyzed as a continuous variable.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages.

To evaluate independent associations between diaphragm ultrasound parameters and ICU length of stay, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed. Variables included in the model were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature and included preoperative TF, postoperative TF, cardiopulmonary bypass time, cross-clamp time, body mass index, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and preoperative ejection fraction.

In secondary analyses, the perioperative change in diaphragm thickening fraction (ΔTF) was also evaluated.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of diaphragm thickening fraction parameters for ICU length of stay. The area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence intervals, and optimal cut-off values (Youden index) were calculated.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Preoperative diaphragm measurements showed a mean thickness at end-expiration (TEE) of 3.31 mm and a mean thickness at end-inspiration (TEI) of 5.4 mm. Postoperative measurements demonstrated a decrease in diaphragm thickening fraction compared to preoperative values.

In unadjusted analyses, diaphragm thickening fraction was not significantly associated with extubation time, body mass index, smoking status, preoperative ejection fraction, or ICU length of stay. Similarly, no significant correlation was observed between diaphragm thickening fraction parameters (preoperative, postoperative, or ΔTF) and extubation time (p > 0.05 for all).

The mean duration of hospitalization was 5.47 ± 3.02 days. In unadjusted analyses, diaphragm thickening fraction showed a tendency toward longer cardiopulmonary bypass time and hospital stay; however, these associations were not statistically significant.

In unadjusted analyses, diaphragm thickening fraction showed significant correlations with cardiopulmonary bypass time and cross-clamp duration; however, these associations were not retained after multivariable adjustment.

In multivariable linear regression analysis, cardiopulmonary bypass time and cross-clamp time remained independently associated with ICU length of stay (p < 0.05), whereas neither preoperative nor postoperative diaphragm thickening fraction, nor the perioperative change (ΔTF), showed an independent association after adjustment for confounding variables (Table 2).

In subgroup analysis using a diaphragm dysfunction threshold (TF < 20%), patients with lower diaphragm thickening fraction showed a tendency toward longer ICU stay; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance and was not confirmed after multivariable adjustment.

In an exploratory analysis, patients with a higher perioperative change in diaphragm thickening fraction (ΔTF ≥ 76%) had a significantly shorter ICU stay compared to those with lower values (2.79 vs. 3.89 days, p = 0.013).

Discussion

The diaphragm is the principal muscle involved in respiration and contributes to approximately 70% of tidal volume during inspiration. Due to its anatomical proximity to the pericardium and internal mammary arteries, the phrenic nerves are particularly susceptible to injury during cardiac surgery, especially during internal mammary artery harvesting or myocardial cooling [10].

Diaphragmatic dysfunction following cardiac surgery has been reported with a wide incidence ranging from 1% to 60% and is associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation and extended ICU stay [11, 12]. In a study by Lerolle et al., diaphragm excursion was significantly reduced in patients with severe diaphragmatic dysfunction after cardiac surgery, highlighting the clinical relevance of impaired diaphragm function in postoperative recovery [13]. Although diaphragm dysfunction is often defined using TF thresholds in the literature, our study primarily evaluated diaphragm thickening fraction as a continuous variable, which may better reflect physiological variation in this population.

Ultrasonographic assessment of diaphragm function has been proposed as a practical bedside tool to evaluate respiratory mechanics and extubation readiness. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis suggested that diaphragm thickening fraction measured by ultrasound may be useful in the context of extubation outcomes [14, 15]. However, in our cohort, diaphragm thickening fraction was not associated with extubation time. This may be explained by early extubation practices and the relatively low variability in extubation duration.

Similarly, diaphragm ultrasound parameters were not independently associated with ICU length of stay after adjustment for confounding variables. The lack of association observed in adjusted analyses suggests that previously reported relationships may be influenced by surgical and clinical factors such as cardiopulmonary bypass duration and cross-clamp time. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for confounding variables when evaluating the prognostic value of diaphragm function.

Although a threshold of TF < 20% has been proposed to define diaphragm dysfunction, its clinical applicability in cardiac surgery populations remains uncertain. In our study, this threshold was not associated with ICU length of stay. In addition, an exploratory analysis using a higher perioperative change in diaphragm thickening fraction (ΔTF ≥ 76%) demonstrated a shorter ICU stay; however, this finding was not confirmed after multivariable adjustment and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Previous studies have demonstrated that diaphragm dysfunction is associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation and increased ICU mortality. For example, Demoule et al. reported that patients with persistent diaphragm dysfunction required longer mechanical ventilation and had higher ICU mortality [16]. In our study, similar trends were observed in unadjusted analyses; however, these associations were not maintained after adjustment. This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient populations, surgical complexity, and perioperative management strategies.

Diaphragm ultrasound is a practical, non-invasive, and widely accessible imaging modality that allows real-time assessment of diaphragm function without radiation exposure [17]. In our study, all measurements were performed by a single experienced operator using a standardized protocol, which ensured consistency but limited the assessment of interobserver variability.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, which may limit generalizability. Second, diaphragm dysfunction was not confirmed using a gold standard diagnostic method such as phrenic nerve stimulation. Third, interobserver variability was not assessed. Additionally, preoperative and postoperative measurements were obtained under different physiological conditions, which may have influenced diaphragm thickening fraction values. Finally, the exclusion of patients with postoperative pulmonary complications may have introduced selection bias. In addition, no formal sample size or power calculation was performed, which may limit the statistical strength of the findings.

Despite these limitations, our study contributes to the growing body of evidence on diaphragm ultrasonography in cardiac surgery. While diaphragm ultrasound may provide useful physiological insights, its role as an independent prognostic tool appears limited based on the present findings. Further studies with larger populations, standardized measurement conditions, and inclusion of higher-risk patients are needed to better define its clinical utility.

Conclusion

In our cohort, diaphragm ultrasound parameters were not independently associated with ICU length of stay after adjustment for confounding factors. These findings suggest that diaphragm thickening fraction may primarily reflect surgical complexity and perioperative physiological stress rather than serve as an independent prognostic marker.

A decrease in diaphragm thickening fraction was observed in the early postoperative period compared with preoperative values; however, this change was not independently associated with clinical outcomes.

Diaphragm ultrasonography may provide useful physiological information in the perioperative setting; however, its role as a prognostic tool appears limited based on the present findings. Further studies with larger populations, standardized measurement conditions, and inclusion of higher-risk patients are needed to better define its clinical utility.
