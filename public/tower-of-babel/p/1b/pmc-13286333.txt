Optimizing cardiovascular health to mitigate cognitive decline: Physical activity as a primary modifiable target
Authors: Yue Wei, Bo Yang, Jingyi Wang, Wenzhuo Zhao, Weijie Dai, Rui Xie, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular disease has been consistently associated with cognitive impairment; however, the relationship between Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), a novel composite metric of cardiovascular health (CVH), and cognitive function in older adults remains inadequately characterized. This study aimed to investigate the cross-sectional association between LE8 scores and cognitive function among United States older adults and identify the relative contributions of individual LE8 components. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2011 to 2014 cycles. A total of 2108 participants aged ≥ 60 years with complete cognitive assessment and LE8 data were included. Cognitive function was evaluated using 4 validated neuropsychological tests: Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer Disease Word Learning and Delayed Recall, animal fluency test, and Digit Symbol Substitution Test. Composite and domain-specific z-scores were computed. Weighted multiple linear regression models were employed to examine LE8-cognition associations, adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates. Weighted quantile sum regression was applied to determine the relative contribution weights of individual LE8 components. In fully adjusted models, each 10-point increment in LE8 score was associated with a 0.09-unit increase in composite cognitive z-score (95% confidence intervals: 0.04–0.14, P < .001). Compared with low CVH (LE8 < 50), high CVH (LE8 ≥ 80) demonstrated significantly higher cognitive z-scores (β = 0.41, 95% confidence intervals: 0.25–0.57, P < .001). Weighted quantile sum regression identified moderate physical activity as the predominant contributor (weight = 0.56) to cognitive performance, followed by blood glucose (0.19) and blood pressure (0.14). Higher LE8 scores are significantly associated with better cognitive function in older adults, with moderate physical activity emerging as the most influential modifiable factor. These findings underscore the potential of integrated CVH optimization as a strategy for cognitive preservation in aging populations.

1. Introduction

The global demographic transition toward population aging has precipitated an unprecedented public health challenge. By 2050, the number of individuals living with dementia is projected to reach 139 million worldwide,[1] imposing substantial burdens on healthcare systems, families, and societies. In the absence of disease-modifying therapies, identifying modifiable risk factors for cognitive decline has become an urgent research priority.[2]

Cardiovascular health (CVH) represents a promising target for dementia prevention. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that cardiovascular disease and its risk factors (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking, and physical inactivity) are independently associated with accelerated cognitive aging and incident dementia.[3,4] The American Heart Association (AHA) introduced Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) in 2010 as a composite metric encompassing 7 CVH components: smoking, body mass index, diet, physical activity, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and total cholesterol.[5] In 2022, the AHA expanded this framework to Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) by incorporating sleep health as an eighth component, reflecting emerging evidence linking sleep disturbances to cardiovascular and neurocognitive outcomes.[6]

The LE8 framework has demonstrated predictive validity for multiple health outcomes, including cardiovascular events, cancer prognosis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and chronic kidney disease.[7–9] However, its relationship with cognitive function in older adults remains incompletely characterized. Prior investigations have yielded inconsistent findings regarding which CVH components exert the strongest influence on cognition, and whether integrated CVH metrics outperform individual risk factors in predicting cognitive performance.[10,11] Several mechanistic pathways may underline the LE8-cognition association. Hypertension contributes to cerebral small vessel disease and white matter hyperintensities.[12] Diabetes accelerates brain aging through microvascular dysfunction, insulin resistance, and advanced glycation end-product accumulation.[13] Physical inactivity reduces neurotrophic factor expression and hippocampal neurogenesis.[14] Sleep disruption impairs amyloid-β clearance and synaptic homeostasis.[15] Notably, these risk factors frequently cluster and interact, supporting the utility of composite metrics such as LE8 for risk stratification.

Despite these advances, significant knowledge gaps persist. First, most prior studies examined LS7 rather than the updated LE8 framework. Second, the relative contributions of individual LE8 components to cognitive function have not been systematically quantified. Third, the generalizability of findings to diverse older adult populations requires further validation. To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive cross-sectional analysis of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011 to 2014 data to: characterize the association between LE8 scores and multidomain cognitive function in United States (U.S.) older adults, identify which LE8 components contribute most substantially to cognitive performance using weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, and examine domain-specific cognitive associations to inform targeted intervention strategies. We hypothesized that higher LE8 scores would be positively associated with cognitive function, with physical activity and vascular risk factors (blood pressure, glucose) emerging as primary contributors.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the NHANES 2011 to 2012 and 2013 to 2014 cycles. NHANES is an ongoing program of studies conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, employing a complex, multistage probability sampling design to obtain nationally representative health and nutritional data from the noninstitutionalized U.S. civilian population.[16] All NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

We included participants aged ≥ 60 years who completed cognitive assessments and had available LE8 component data. Exclusion criteria were: age < 60 years (n = 16,299); incomplete cognitive function data (n = 698); missing LE8 component data (n = 627); and missing covariate data (n = 199). The final analytical sample comprised 2108 participants. 2.2. Cognitive assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using 4 validated neuropsychological tests administered by trained interviewers: Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer Disease (CERAD) Word Learning: Participants were presented with 10 unrelated words across 3 consecutive learning trials (with different word orders). Immediate recall was assessed after each trial, with the final score calculated as the mean across 3 trials (range: 0–10). [18] CERAD Delayed Recall: Approximately 5 minutes after completion of intervening tests, participants were asked to recall the 10 words without cueing, assessing short-term and delayed verbal memory (range: 0–10).[18] Animal fluency test: Participants were given 60 seconds to generate as many animal names as possible, assessing semantic fluency and executive function. Digit Symbol Substitution Test: Participants matched symbols to numbers according to a coding key within 120 seconds, with the total number of correct substitutions recorded. This test assesses processing speed, executive function, and working memory.[19] For each test, we calculated age-adjusted z-scores. A composite cognitive z-score was computed as the mean of the 4 domain-specific z-scores.

2.3. LE8 score calculation

LE8 comprises 4 health behaviors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health) and 4 biological metrics (body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, blood pressure). Each component was scored from 0 (poor) to 100 (ideal) according to AHA criteria,[6] with the overall LE8 score calculated as the unweighted mean of the 8 components. For interpretability, LE8 scores were divided by 10, yielding a 0–10 scale. CVH categories were defined as: low (< 50), moderate (50–79), and high (≥ 80). Physical activity assessment merits particular attention given our findings. Moderate physical activity was defined as engaging in moderate-intensity activities causing light sweating or slight to moderate increases in breathing or heart rate for at least 10 minutes continuously. Vigorous activity was defined as activities causing heavy sweating or large increases in breathing or heart rate for at least 10 minutes continuously. Scoring incorporated both frequency and duration according to AHA guidelines.[6]

2.4. Covariates

Demographic variables included age (continuous), sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, other), education level (less than high school, high school graduate, college or above), and marital status (married/cohabiting vs living alone/never married). Socioeconomic status was assessed using the family income-to-poverty ratio, categorized as low (< 1.0), middle (1.0–3.0), and high (≥ 3.0). Clinical covariates included: alcohol consumption (≥ 12 drinks/year vs < 12 drinks/year); depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 score ≥ 10)[20]; self-reported stroke history; and serum creatinine (mg/dL) as a marker of kidney function.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated NHANES sampling weights (2-year mobile examination center weights divided by 2 for combined cycles) to account for the complex survey design and generate nationally representative estimates. Standard errors were estimated using Taylor series linearization. Baseline characteristics were compared across CVH categories using continuous variables: weighted means ± standard errors; group comparisons performed using survey-weighted linear regression with CVH category as the independent variable, reporting F-statistics and P values from Wald tests. For categorical variables: weighted percentages; group comparisons performed using survey-weighted chi-square tests (Rao–Scott chi-square). Primary analyses employed survey-weighted multiple linear regression to examine associations between LE8 (continuous and categorical) and cognitive outcomes (composite z-score and subdomain scores). Three hierarchical models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, education, and income-to-poverty ratio; Model 3: Additionally adjusted for alcohol consumption, depression, stroke history, and creatinine. Regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and P values are reported. Secondary analyses utilized WQS regression to estimate the mixture effect of LE8 components on cognitive function and quantify individual component contributions.[21] The WQS model constructs a weighted index of correlated exposures, estimating an overall mixture effect while apportioning weights to individual components. We randomly split data into training (40%) and validation (60%) sets (seed = 2023), ranked each LE8 component into quartiles, executed 1000 bootstrap iterations in the training set, constrained the effect direction to be positive (based on hypothesized beneficial effects of higher LE8 scores), and validated weights in the holdout sample. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).[22,23] Statistical significance was set at 2-tailed P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

The final sample comprised 2108 participants representing approximately 41.2 million U.S. older adults. The weighted mean age was 68.95 ± 0.27 years; 47.08% were male, and 80.24% were non-Hispanic White. By CVH status, 461 participants (21.9%) had low CVH, 1455 (69.0%) had moderate CVH, and 192 (9.1%) had high CVH. Participants with higher CVH were more likely to be non-Hispanic White, married/cohabiting, college-educated, and in higher income brackets (all P < .01). 3.2. Association between LE8 and cognitive function

Continuous LE8 analysis: In unadjusted models, each 10-point LE8 increment was associated with a 0.20-unit increase in cognitive z-score (95% CI: 0.14–0.26, P < .001). In fully adjusted Model 3, each 10-point LE8 increment was associated with a 0.09-unit increase in cognitive z-score (95% CI: 0.04–0.14, P < .001) (Table 2). Categorical LE8 analysis: In fully adjusted models, moderate CVH (β = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.03–0.29, P = .018) and high CVH (β = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.25–0.57, P < .001) demonstrated significantly higher cognitive z-scores compared with low CVH, with evidence of dose-response (Ptrend < 0.001). Domain-specific analyses: LE8 was significantly associated with all 4 cognitive domains in fully adjusted models: CERAD-Immediate Recall Test (β = 0.07, P = .005), CERAD Delayed Recall (β = 0.05, P = .03), animal fluency test (β = 0.10, P = .002), and Digit Symbol Substitution Test (β = 0.07, P = .01) (Table 2). High CVH showed significant associations with all domains compared with low CVH, whereas moderate CVH associations were generally nonsignificant.

4. Discussion

In this nationally representative cross-sectional study of U.S. older adults, we demonstrate that higher LE8 scores are significantly associated with better cognitive performance across multiple domains. Using WQS regression, we identify moderate physical activity as the predominant contributor to this association, accounting for 56% of the mixture effect, followed by glycemic control (19%) and blood pressure management (14%). These findings extend prior research by quantifying the relative importance of individual CVH components within the updated LE8 framework and highlight physical activity as a critical modifiable target for cognitive preservation.

Based on the NHANES 2011 to 2014 database, our study investigated the association between LE8 and cognitive function among older adults. The findings were consistent with 2 recent similar studies,[24,25] all demonstrating that higher LE8 scores were significantly associated with better cognitive performance. Compared with existing literature, this study made substantial methodological improvements. We adopted age-adjusted z-scores, and we innovatively applied WQS regression to explore the combined effects and independent contribution weights of each LE8 component, addressing the limitation of prior studies that only focused on total scores while ignoring the specific effects of individual components. In addition, we included depression, stroke history, and renal function markers in covariate adjustment and strengthened the robustness of the results through hierarchical regression models and bootstrap validation. Overall, our study used a more rigorous and comprehensive methodological design, thus providing more reliable evidence for the relationship between LE8 and cognitive function in older adults.

4.1. Comparison with prior literature

Our findings align with and extend several recent investigations. Samieri et al[3] demonstrated that higher LS7 scores were associated with lower dementia incidence in the 3-City Study, with effect sizes comparable to our estimates. Ferreira et al[26] recently reported that optimal LE8 was associated with slower cognitive decline over 8-year follow-up, with effect modification by age, sex, and race: effects that were most pronounced in older, male, and Black participants. Our cross-sectional findings are consistent with this longitudinal evidence and provide complementary mechanistic insights through component-specific analyses. Neuroimaging studies provide biological plausibility for our observations. Zhou et al[27] demonstrated in the United Kingdom Biobank that higher LE8 scores were associated with larger total brain and hippocampal volumes, as well as lower white matter hyperintensity burden. These structural differences likely mediate, at least partially, the cognitive associations we observe.

Our WQS findings regarding physical activity are particularly noteworthy, consistent with prior observations of protective effects against dementia-related mortality in active older adults.[28] Our results extend these mortality-focused findings to cognitive performance and identify moderate (rather than vigorous) physical activity as the optimal target: potentially more feasible for older adult populations.

4.2. Mechanistic considerations

The predominance of physical activity in our WQS model likely reflects its pleiotropic effects on brain health. Exercise-induced upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor promotes hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity.[29] Physical activity also enhances cerebrovascular function, increases cerebral blood flow, and reduces neuroinflammation through modulation of microglial activation.[30] Animal models demonstrate that exercise attenuates neuroinflammatory responses and improves spatial learning in aging mice.[31]

The secondary contributions of glucose and blood pressure control underscore the importance of vascular health for cognitive function. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes advanced glycation end-product accumulation, microvascular dysfunction, and blood-brain barrier compromise.[13] Hypertension accelerates cerebral small vessel disease, white matter degeneration, and lacunar infarction.[12] The relatively modest contributions of diet, sleep, and lipids in our model may reflect measurement limitations, collinearity with other components, or true weaker independent effects in this population.

4.3. Clinical and public health implications

Our findings carry important translational implications. First, they support the integration of cognitive outcomes into CVH promotion programs. The LE8 framework provides a clinically actionable structure for risk assessment and intervention prioritization. Second, the identification of physical activity as the dominant modifiable factor suggests that exercise prescription should be central to dementia prevention strategies in older adults: even modest increases in moderate activity may yield substantial cognitive benefits. Third, the graded association between CVH categories and cognition supports a “higher is better” approach rather than threshold-based targets.

Future research priorities include: longitudinal validation of LE8-cognition associations in diverse populations, randomized controlled trials of LE8-optimized interventions with cognitive endpoints, neuroimaging substudies to elucidate structural and functional mediators, and implementation science research to optimize LE8 improvement in real-world clinical and community settings.

4.4. Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the large, nationally representative sample; validated multidomain cognitive assessment; rigorous survey-weighted analyses; and novel application of WQS regression to quantify component-specific contributions. Limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference: reverse causation (cognitive impairment influencing health behaviors) and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Longitudinal studies are essential to confirm temporal relationships. Second, cognitive assessments, while validated, were brief and may not capture subtle cognitive changes or domain-specific deficits with equivalent sensitivity. Third, the NHANES sample, while nationally representative of the U.S., may not generalize to other countries or healthcare systems. Fourth, LE8 components were assessed at single timepoints, potentially misclassifying long-term exposure status. Fifth, the WQS model assumes all components contribute in the same direction; while appropriate for LE8 (where higher scores uniformly indicate better health), this constraint should be considered in interpretation.

5. Conclusions

In this nationally representative study of U.S. older adults, higher LE8 scores were significantly associated with better cognitive performance across multiple domains. Moderate physical activity emerged as the predominant contributor to this association, followed by glycemic and blood pressure control. These findings support the integration of comprehensive CVH optimization (including physical activity promotion) into dementia prevention strategies for aging populations. Longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to establish causality and inform clinical implementation.
