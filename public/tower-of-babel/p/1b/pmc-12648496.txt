ATP6V0D2 drives triple-negative breast cancer progression and inhibits cisplatin sensitivity by promoting PHLPP2 lysosomal degradation
Authors: Jingyu Zhang, Congbo Cao, Hongyu Yang, Liu Yang, Meiqi Wang, Xiaohan Liu, Xin Hao, Yuanyuan Zhang, Xiaohan Wang, Zhenchuan Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) faces therapeutic challenges due to limited treatment options and frequent chemoresistance. In this study, we identify ATP6V0D2 as a key factor upregulated in TNBC, where its expression correlates with poor prognosis. Transcriptomic and functional analyses linked ATP6V0D2 to PI3K/AKT signaling and platinum drug resistance. Mechanistically, ATP6V0D2 enhanced lysosomal function to promote degradation of the tumor suppressor PHLPP2, thereby sustaining AKT phosphorylation. Functional assays showed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown increased cisplatin sensitivity both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase with bafilomycinA1 sensitized TNBC patient-derived organoids to cisplatin. Clinically, our analysis of 91 TNBC patients who received cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy found that high ATP6V0D2 expression was inversely correlated with pathological complete response (pCR), whereas PHLPP2 levels showed positive association, supporting the proposed mechanistic link. Together, these findings highlight the impact of ATP6V0D2 on TNBC progression and cisplatin sensitivity, nominating ATP6V0D2 as a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) amplification, accounts for 15%–20% of all breast cancers and is characterized by aggressive clinical behavior, including early metastasis, high recurrence rates, and limited therapeutic options.1,2 Unlike hormone receptor-positive or HER2-enriched subtypes, TNBC lacks actionable targets for endocrine or antibody-based therapies, leaving cytotoxic chemotherapy as the primary treatment modality. Despite advances in chemotherapy and immunotherapy, drug resistance remains a major hurdle for many patients.3 Cisplatin, a platinum-based DNA-damaging agent, is widely used in neoadjuvant and metastatic TNBC regimens. However, over 60% of patients develop intrinsic or acquired resistance, resulting in low pathological complete response (pCR) rates and poor prognostic.4 This stark clinical reality underscores the urgent need to dissect the molecular drivers of TNBC progression and cisplatin refractoriness, which may identify novel therapeutic targets to improve outcomes for this high-risk population.

ATPase H+ transporting V0 subunit d2 (ATP6V0D2), a critical subunit of the vacuolar ATPase (V-ATPase) complex, is widely distributed across a variety of intracellular membrane structures within eukaryotic cells.5,6 Beyond its canonical role in maintaining cellular pH homeostasis7 and regulating osteoclast fusion and activity,8,9 ATP6V0D2 also plays a crucial role in regulating lysosomal autophagy.10 As a key factor of autophagy process, ATP6V0D2 can enhance the degradation of bacteria and proteins through lysosomes.10,11 Recently, emerging evidence underscores different roles of ATP6V0D2 in cancer progression, including its ability to inhibit cancer cell apoptosis in esophageal cancer, reverse sunitinib resistance in renal cancer, and maintain survival pathways in lung adenocarcinoma.12,13,14 In breast cancer, our prior work identified ATP6V0D2 overexpression in clinical specimens, where it correlates with advanced tumor stage and poor survival. Functional studies demonstrated that ATP6V0D2 knockdown suppressed TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and inhibits tumor growth in xenograft models.15 Despite evidence of ATP6V0D2’s pro-tumorigenic role in TNBC, the molecular mechanisms linking its lysosomal regulatory function to TNBC aggressiveness and drug sensitivity remain elusive.

The PI3K/AKT pathway, a central driver of TNBC progression, is tightly controlled by a balance of kinase and phosphatase activities. As a member of PHLPP family of Ser/Thr phosphatases, PH domain and leucine-rich repeat protein phosphatase 2 (PHLPP2) has emerged as a master negative regulator of PI3K/AKT signaling through direct dephosphorylation of AKT at Ser473.16,17 This phosphatase is widely classified as a tumor suppressor regulating the cell cycle and inhibiting oncogenic signaling.18,19,20 While PHLPP2 loss drives cisplatin resistance in gastric and esophageal cancers,21,22 its functional and mechanistic role in TNBC remains undefined. Notably, PHLPP2 degradation mechanisms exhibit tissue specificity, as it undergoes ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation in hepatocellular carcinoma but is cleared via autophagic-lysosomal pathways in gastric cancer.23,24 The precise mode of PHLPP2 degradation in breast cancer cells, however, remains an open question in the field.

In this study, through integrative analysis of datasets, we identified elevated ATP6V0D2 expression in TNBC, which was inversely associated with patient overall survival (OS). Mechanistically, ATP6V0D2 facilitates the autophagic-lysosomal degradation of PHLPP2, resulting in hyperactivation of the AKT pathway. These findings elucidate the role of ATP6V0D2 in driving TNBC progression. Notably, genetic knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of ATP6V0D2 sensitized TNBC cells and patient-derived organoids (PDOs) to cisplatin, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in TNBC.

Results

ATP6V0D2 is upregulated in TNBC and correlates with poor outcome

Initial analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data revealed significant dysregulation of ATP6V0D2 expression in tumor tissues compared to normal counterparts across multiple cancer types, including TNBC (Figure 1A). Then, we assessed ATP6V0D2 mRNA levels using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from the Kaplan-Meier plotter database. Consistent with TCGA results, ATP6V0D2 expression demonstrated significant upregulation in breast cancer tissues compared to both unpaired and paired normal controls (Figures 1B and 1C). To specifically evaluate ATP6V0D2 expression in TNBC, we performed an integrated analysis of GEO breast tissues datasets. Survival analyses demonstrated that high ATP6V0D2 expression was associated with shorter OS in breast cancer patients (Figure 1E). Notably, this negative correlation was particularly pronounced in TNBC cohorts, where elevated ATP6V0D2 levels strongly predicted poor OS (Figures 1F and 1G). Collectively, these results establish ATP6V0D2 as a TNBC-associated gene with prognostic significance, showing both marked overexpression in TNBC tissues and a robust inverse correlation with patient survival outcomes.

ATP6V0D2 promotes TNBC proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro

To elucidate the biological role of ATP6V0D2 in TNBC cells, we selected two TNBC cell lines, MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468. We manipulated these cell lines to either knockdown or overexpress ATP6V0D2 using small interfering RNA (siRNA) and the plasmid pEZ-M02-ATP6V0D2, respectively. Negative control siRNA (si-NC) and an empty vector plasmid served as controls. RT-qPCR confirmed efficient ATP6V0D2 knockdown and overexpression (Figures S1A–S1D). CCK8 assays demonstrated that knockdown of ATP6V0D2 significantly reduced cell viability compared to si-NC (Figures 2A and 2C), while overexpression of ATP6V0D2 markedly increased cell viability relative to controls (Figures 2B and 2D). These findings were further supported by colony formation assays, which demonstrated that ATP6V0D2 knockdown led to a significant reduction in colony numbers, whereas its overexpression resulted in a pronounced increase in colony formation (Figures 2E and 2F). Transwell assays revealed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown impaired both migration and invasion (with and without Matrigel), whereas overexpression augmented these capabilities (Figures 2G and 2H). Similarly, wound healing assays confirmed that ATP6V0D2 overexpression accelerated wound closure, while knockdown delayed it (Figures 2I and 2J). ATP6V0D2 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway and induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition

To investigate the mechanism underlying ATP6V0D2-driven TNBC progression, we performed RNA-seq in ATP6V0D2-silenced MDA-MB-231 cells, identifying 2,229 differentially expressed genes (1,010 upregulated and 1,219 downregulated; Figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in cancer-related pathways, most notably the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, and identified direct associations with platinum resistance (Figure 3B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis linked ATP6V0D2 to signal transduction, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, with additional enrichment in lysosomal/endoplasmic reticulum membranes and identical protein binding (Figure 3C). Western blotting confirmed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown suppressed PI3K/AKT pathway activation, resulting in reduced p-AKT and p-GSK3β, whereas overexpression amplified phosphorylation of these markers (Figures 3D and 3E; Figures S1E and S1F). Intriguingly, PI3K phosphorylation levels remained unchanged. Given the association of ATP6V0D2 with migration and ECM regulation, we assessed epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers, revealing that ATP6V0D2 knockdown reduced N-cadherin, vimentin, and snail expression while elevating E-cadherin, whereas ATP6V0D2 overexpression produced the opposite effects (Figure 3F; Figures S1G and S1H). ATP6V0D2 activates the AKT-GSK3β pathway by targeting PHLPP2

Since ATP6V0D2 lacks intrinsic kinase and phosphatase activity, we proposed that it regulates AKT phosphorylation indirectly. Through a literature search of PI3K/AKT pathway regulators, we screened out three candidate mediators: PRR15,25 INPP4B,26 and PHLPP2.16 Our transcriptome sequencing analysis did demonstrate a regulatory relationship between ATP6V0D2 and PHLPP2 (Figure 4A), with their expression levels exhibiting a significant inverse correlation (Figure 4B). Further, western blot analysis confirmed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown robustly upregulated PHLPP2 protein expression, whereas its overexpression conversely suppressed PHLPP2 levels (Figure 4C; Figures S2A and S2B). None of the other candidates displayed significant changes under these experimental conditions (Figures S2C and S2D). PHLPP2 is known to reduce the phosphorylation level of AKT at Ser473 through dephosphorylation. To investigate whether ATP6V0D2-dependent AKT activation was ascribed to PHLPP2-induced dephosphorylation of AKT, we performed rescue assays. The results demonstrated that PHLPP2 knockdown in ATP6V0D2-silenced cells rescued p-AKT and p-GSK3β levels (Figure 4D; Figures S2E and S2F). To further investigate the relationship between the AKT pathway and EMT, we treated AKT pathway-activated TNBC cells with the AKT pathway inhibitor MK-2206 (10 μM) and found that it suppressed EMT characteristics (Figures S3A–S3C). Furthermore, rescue experiments targeting PHLPP2 demonstrated that knockdown of PHLPP2 in ATP6V0D2-silenced cells rescued the expression of EMT markers (Figures S3D–S3F), as well as restored the proliferation (Figures 4E and 4F; Figures S3G and S3H), migration, and invasion capabilities of TNBC cells (Figures 4G–4J; Figures S3I–S3L). Independent PHLPP2 knockdown in TNBC cells similarly elevated p-AKT and p-GSK3β (Figure 4K; Figures S3M and S3N) and enhanced malignant phenotypes (Figures S4A–S4J), further confirming its role as a tumor suppressor. ATP6V0D2 knockdown impairs autophagic-lysosomal degradation of PHLPP2

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays revealed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown significantly extended the half-life of PHLPP2 in TNBC cells (Figures 5A–5D), indicating that ATP6V0D2 depletion stabilizes PHLPP2 by blocking its degradation. To identify the degradation pathway, we treated MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 or the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ). While MG132 had no effect on PHLPP2 levels, CQ treatment markedly increased PHLPP2 levels (Figures 5E and 5F; Figures S5A–S5D), pinpointing the autophagic-lysosomal pathway as the primary route for PHLPP2 degradation in breast cancer cells. Strikingly, CQ failed to further elevate PHLPP2 in ATP6V0D2 knockdown cells, suggesting that ATP6V0D2 is essential for lysosomal targeting of PHLPP2. To directly link ATP6V0D2 to lysosomal function, we treated cells with bafilomycin A1 (BafA), a V-ATPase inhibitor and lysosomal inhibitor. We observed that BafA treatment elevated both PHLPP2 and p62 levels in control cells but failed to induce further accumulation in ATP6V0D2-silenced cells (Figures 5G and 5H; Figures S5E and S5F), demonstrating that ATP6V0D2 inhibition compromises lysosomal degradation efficiency. We next explored whether ATP6V0D2 interacts with PHLPP2. The results demonstrated that FLAG-tagged ATP6V0D2 combined with PHLPP2 (Figure 5I; Figure S5G). Finally, LysoTracker staining and immunofluorescence revealed significantly reduced colocalization of PHLPP2 with lysosomes in ATP6V0D2-silenced cells (Figure 5J; Figure S5H). Together, these results suggested that knockdown of ATP6V0D2 suppresses autophagic-lysosomal degradation of PHLPP2.

ATP6V0D2 knockdown enhances cisplatin sensitivity in TNBC cells, in xenograft, and PDOs

KEGG pathway analysis highlighted a close association between ATP6V0D2 and cisplatin resistance. Functional experiments showed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown reduced the cisplatin IC50 in TNBC cells, while overexpression increased it (Figures 6A and 6B; Figures S5I–S5L), indicating that ATP6V0D2 diminishes cisplatin efficacy. Conversely, PHLPP2 knockdown elevated the cisplatin IC50 (Figure 6C; Figures S5M and S5N), supporting its role as a cisplatin sensitizer. In xenograft models, ATP6V0D2 knockdown enhanced cisplatin-induced tumor suppression without inducing systemic toxicity (Figures 6E–6J). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed elevated PHLPP2 levels, decreased KI67 expression, and suppressed AKT pathway activation, as indicated by reduced p-AKT and p-GSK3β, in ATP6V0D2-silenced tumors (Figures 6K and 6L).

ATP6V0D2 and PHLPP2 expression was correlated with cisplatin response in TNBC patients

To further investigate the clinical relevance of ATP6V0D2 and PHLPP2, we analyzed pretreatment biopsy samples from 91 TNBC patients treated with cisplatin-based neoadjuvant therapy. We stratified patients into ATP6V0D2-high (n = 33) and ATP6V0D2-low (n = 58) groups and PHLPP2-high (n = 44) and PHLPP2-low (n = 47) cohorts based on IHC staining (Figures 7A and 7B). pCR rates, assessed using the Miller-Payne grading system, differed markedly, with only 6 of 33 (18.2%) ATP6V0D2-high patients achieving pCR, compared to 49 of 58 (84.5%) in the ATP6V0D2-low group (Figure 7C). Conversely, 38 of 44 (86.4%) PHLPP2-high patients attained pCR, compared to 17 of 47 (36.2%) in the PHLPP2-low cohort (Figure 7D). Discussion

Despite therapeutic advances that have improved outcomes in TNBC, this aggressive malignancy remains a major clinical challenge. While multiple molecular drivers of TNBC progression have been identified,27,28,29 few have been translated into actionable therapeutic targets. Here, we uncover a previously unrecognized ATP6V0D2-driven regulatory mechanism that promotes TNBC pathogenesis and inhibits cisplatin sensitivity by orchestrating autophagic-lysosomal degradation of the tumor suppressor PHLPP2, leading to hyperactivation of the oncogenic AKT-GSK3β pathway (Figure 7E). Our work not only establishes a causal link between ATP6V0D2-dependent PHLPP2 elimination and sustained AKT-GSK3β pathway activation but also elucidates a crucial role of ATP6V0D2 in therapeutic response for TNBC, filling a critical gap in understanding how ATP6V0D2 exacerbates disease progression, and proposes its targeting as a strategy to restore cisplatin sensitivity.

Tumor metastasis accounts for nearly 90% of cancer-related deaths, underscoring its central role in cancer lethality.30 The AKT signaling pathway, a pivotal regulator of metastatic dissemination, fuels malignant progression across diverse cancer types through aberrant activation,31,32,33 while EMT represents a critical biological process enabling cancer cells to acquire metastatic capabilities.34,35 In this study, by combining RNA-seq with functional assays, we revealed that ATP6V0D2 knockdown suppresses AKT signaling, providing the first evidence of its regulation in pathway activation. In addition, GO analyses linked ATP6V0D2 to ECM organization and cell adhesion, with experimental validation confirming its capacity to modulate EMT marker expression, a phenotype consistent with established ECM-mediated EMT regulation.36 Critically, we mechanistically connected AKT hyperactivation to EMT induction, revealing ATP6V0D2 promotes the EMT process by activating the AKT pathway. In the latest study by Ganesan et al., it was found that ononin inhibits TNBC lung metastasis via suppression of the AKT pathway and downregulation of EMT.37 Our study further established ATP6V0D2 as a central regulator integrating the AKT oncogenic signaling pathway and EMT to drive metastatic dissemination. Thus, targeting ATP6V0D2 represents a potential therapeutic strategy for inhibiting TNBC metastasis.

As a pivotal regulator of lysosomal function, ATP6V0D2 dynamically modulates lysosomal degradation to shape disease-specific molecular networks.10,11 Notably, ATP6V0D2 exhibits context-dependent duality in tumor microenvironments (TMEs). In lung adenocarcinoma and renal clear cell carcinoma (RCC), ATP6V0D2 is primarily expressed in tumor-associated macrophages or tumor cells, respectively, where it functions by lysosomal degradation of HIF2α to inhibit VEGF signaling, demonstrating conserved tumor-suppressive effects.12,14 Conversely, in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, tumor cell-intrinsic ATP6V0D2 promotes EMT-mediated progression,13 highlighting its microenvironment-dependent functional switch. In this study, we systematically deciphered ATP6V0D2’s pro-tumorigenic mechanism in TNBC, where tumor cell-enriched ATP6V0D2 regulates the PHLPP2-AKT-GSK3β axis to promote malignant progression. Although PHLPP2 acts as a tumor suppressor across malignancies,38,39 its degradation mechanisms exhibit tissue-specific heterogeneity, featured by ubiquitin-proteasome system dominance in hepatocellular23 and lung carcinomas,40 while autophagic-lysosomal primacy in gastric cancer24 and, as we show here, in TNBC. Through pharmacological lysosome inhibition and high-resolution confocal imaging, we demonstrated that ATP6V0D2 knockdown attenuates lysosome-dependent PHLPP2 degradation, and stabilizes PHLPP2 to suppress AKT signaling, thus solidifying its role as a key lysosomal regulator. These findings unveil the “molecular duality” of ATP6V0D2 in cancer biology, where it exerts tumor-suppressive effects by degrading oncoproteins, such as HIF2α, but can also promote tumorigenesis by eliminating tumor suppressors, like PHLPP2. This functional bifurcation may originate from substrate selectivity dictated by tissue microenvironment. The pH within the TME profoundly affects V-ATPase function and lysosomal activity. RCC and TNBC exhibit different stromal microenvironments. Renal cells inherently maintain acid-base balance and likely possess different baseline lysosomal pH compared to mammary epithelial cells. Hyperglycolysis in TNBC cells can increase lactic acid, leading to a more acidic extracellular TME.41 Compared to RCC, this acidity in TNBC may alter the assembly, activity, or localization of ATP6V0D2-containing V-ATPase complexes in TNBC, impacting substrate degradation efficiency or specificity. Furthermore, immune infiltration may differ between TNBC and RCC. Tumor-immune cell crosstalk may affect lysosomal function and influence specific V-ATPase subunits (like ATP6V0D2) in immune evasion or antigen presentation. Our future work aims to define the specific interactome and degradation targets of ATP6V0D2 in TNBC models for direct comparison with its HIF2α regulatory role in RCC. Beyond elucidating TNBC-specific PHLPP2 degradation mechanisms, our work provides a bidirectional paradigm for lysosomal dysfunction in cancer, offering actionable insights for therapies tailored to ATP6V0D2’s contextual roles.

Cisplatin remains a cornerstone of neoadjuvant therapy for TNBC, yet its efficacy is substantially compromised by acquired resistance.42 Multiple factors contribute to cisplatin resistance, including epigenetic alterations at the molecular and cellular levels and the hypoxic TME.43 Thus, developing innovative strategies to overcome drug resistance has garnered considerable interest in the fight against TNBC. Our KEGG pathway enrichment analyses identified ATP6V0D2 as a key player in platinum resistance, with its overexpression directly diminishing cisplatin sensitivity in TNBC. While previous studies linked PHLPP2 downregulation to AKT-driven cisplatin resistance,21,22 we revealed for the first time that ATP6V0D2 acts as an upstream negative regulator of PHLPP2, establishing the ATP6V0D2-PHLPP2 axis as a critical driver of reduced sensitivity to cisplatin chemotherapy. Intriguingly, this mechanism exhibits molecular parallels with the recently identified ATP6V0D2-HIF2α-VEGF axis attenuating sunitinib resistance in renal carcinoma,14 suggesting ATP6V0D2 may serve as a universal modulator of therapeutic response across cancers. To further explore these findings, we tested V-ATPase inhibitors in TNBC PDOs and observed enhanced cisplatin sensitivity. However, their broad-spectrum toxicity limits clinical applicability. Clinical validation in cisplatin-treated TNBC cohorts confirmed the axis’ prognostic relevance. ATP6V0D2-high patients showed significantly lower pCR rates compared to ATP6V0D2-low cases, while PHLPP2-high patients achieved superior pCR rates. IHC staining revealed an inverse ATP6V0D2-PHLPP2 correlation, consistent with xenograft model findings. These results position ATP6V0D2 not only as a resistance biomarker but also as a co-predictor with PHLPP2 for cisplatin responses in TNBC, offering a dual-component framework for personalized therapy. Our findings carry significant translational potential that extends beyond the mechanistic insights described above. Its prognostic value could be harnessed for improved patient risk stratification, guiding more personalized treatment decisions. Furthermore, the functional role of ATP6V0D2 in inhibiting cisplatin sensitivity nominates it as a compelling therapeutic target. Developing strategies to inhibit ATP6V0D2 may therefore provide a novel means to re-sensitize tumors to standard chemotherapy, a hypothesis that merits vigorous investigation in future translational studies.

In conclusion, our study reveals that ATP6V0D2 modulates TNBC progression and cisplatin sensitivity by mediating lysosomal degradation of the tumor suppressor PHLPP2 to activate AKT-GSK3β pathway. These findings position ATP6V0D2 as a translational target of high clinical promise, while reshaping our understanding of lysosome-mediated low drug sensitivity in TNBC. By bridging mechanistic discovery to therapeutic strategy, this work provides a blueprint for targeting ATP6V0D2 to enhance cisplatin sensitivity and improve clinical outcomes in this recalcitrant malignancy.

Limitations of the study

While our findings provide critical insights into ATP6V0D2’s role in TNBC progression and cisplatin sensitivity, several limitations merit consideration to contextualize their implications. First, the precise molecular mechanisms through which ATP6V0D2 drives lysosome function remain incompletely elucidated. Second, the clinical generalizability of our findings is constrained by the single-center cohort size and the absence of in vivo pharmacodynamic validation of PHLPP2’s regulatory role in cisplatin sensitivity. Despite these limitations, our study identifies ATP6V0D2 and PHLPP2 as dual-purpose biomarkers for predicting cisplatin response and prognostic outcomes.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (Procell Life Science & Technology, Wuhan, China) and MDA-MB-468 (ETHEPHON, Shanghai, China) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. All cell lines described here were mycoplasma-free and have been authenticated using STR profiling within the last 3 years.

Mice

Twenty 5-week-old female BALB/c nude mice, procured from Huafukang Bio-technology (Beijing, China), were allocated into 4 distinct groups. They were housed in a pathogen-free environment for a week. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval No.20240734).

Patients and clinical specimens

Ninety-one patients diagnosed with triple-negative breast cancer, admitted to the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University between 2019 and 2023, provided tissue samples for our study. All study participants were Asian and of Han Chinese ethnicity, the majority group in China. Each participant underwent cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy without prior treatment. The ethical considerations of this human research were rigorously reviewed and approved by the hospital's Ethics Committee (approval No.2022KY036).

Method details

Database acquisition

Pan-cancer expression profiles of ATP6V0D2 were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and analyzed using TIMER2.0. The expression of ATP6V0D2 in breast cancer tissues and adjacent tissues were analyzed by KM-plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). We further verified its prognostic significance based on clinical data from KM-plotter.

Four breast tissue gene datasets were analyzed from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE29044 (35 normal samples), GSE42568 (17 normal samples), GSE58812 (107 TNBC patients), and GSE83937 (121 TNBC patients). Batch effect correction was performed using the R package “sva”, followed by quantile normalization with “preprocessCore”. Integrated analysis was conducted to compare ATP6V0D2 expression levels between TNBC and normal breast tissues. Prognostic significance was assessed using log-rank tests, with Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrating the association between ATP6V0D2 expression and TNBC patient outcomes.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the TRUEscript RT MasterMix Kit (Aidlab, China), following manufacturer protocols. Quantitative PCR was performed with Go Taq® SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Promega, China) on a 20 μl reaction system. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and analyzed via the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

DNA plasmids, siRNA construction and transfection

A human DNA fragment encoding ATP6V0D2 was synthesized and cloned into the pEZ-M02 vector to generate a transient overexpression plasmid, and the corresponding empty vector was used as a control. The work was completed by GeneCopoeia, FulenGen Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Small interfering (si)RNA targeting ATP6V0D2 (siATP6V0D2) was from Shanghai GenePharma Co. (Shanghai, China). Flag-tagged ATP6V0D2 (GeneID: 152565; vector: pcDNA3.1) was purchased from BAIDAI Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). PolyFect (Qiagen GmbH) and GP-transfect-Mate (GenePharma) were used for plasmid transfection and siRNA transient transfection, respectively, under the instructions of the manufacturer. The sequences of the DNA plasmids and siRNAs are provided in Table S2.

Cell viability and clonogenicity

For cell viability assays, TNBC cells (2×103) per well were seeded into 96-well plates. Cell viability and proliferation were detected using the CCK8 kit (MCE, Shanghai, China) every 24 h. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Group, Ltd.).

In colony expansion experiments, 2×103 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates for different treatment conditions and cultured for a period of 10 day. Cell clusters (>50 cells) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained using a 1% crystal violet solution. The colonies were subsequently examined and counted.

Transwell assay

For cell migration and invasion assays, 4×104 cells suspended in serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber covered without or with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) respectively. A total of 0.6 ml normal medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. After 1-2 day, the inserts with cells that had migrated or invaded were processed by fixation, staining. Subsequently, the cells were observed and counted with a microscope.

Wound healing assay

TNBC cells were seeded at a concentration of 4×105 cells/well in 6-well plates. Upon achieving 80% confluence, straight wounds were made in the wells with a sterile 10 μl pipette tip. After that, the cells were incubated in medium without serum, with photographs taken at the 0 h and 24 h time points. The wound closure rate (%) was determined using a specific formula.: [(wound width at 0 h - wound width at 24 h) / (wound width at 0 h)]×100%.

RNA-seq and GO, KEGG analysis

Total RNA was isolated from three sets of control MDA-MB-231 cells and those treated with siRNA against ATP6V0D2 to prepare sequencing libraries. Prior to next generation sequencing, the integrity of the RNA was assessed. Subsequently, a comprehensive transcriptome landscape was conducted by Annoroad Gene Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). For validation purposes, genes exhibiting a significant differential expression, defined by an absolute |log2FoldChange| ≥ 0.585 and a p-value below 0.05, were identified and selected. The differentially expressed genes are shown in Table S3. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases, utilizing the “clusterProfiler” R package. A significance threshold was set at an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05 to determine enrichment.

Immunoblotting and antibodies

The cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Beijing China) containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. To ensure uniform sample loading, the protein lysate of different groups was quantified by a BCA kit. The proteins were then separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 60 min. Last, the membranes were detected by a luminescent imaging machine (Bio-Rad, USA) with ECL kit. Blot intensities were measured using ImageJ software (RRID:SCR_003070) and normalized to the GAPDH levels.

CHX chase and MG132 assays

After a 48 h post-transfection period, the protein's half-life was ascertained through cycloheximide (CHX)-based tracing. Cells were exposed to CHX (MCE, Shanghai, China) at concentration of 100 μg/ml, and collected at specific intervals for subsequent immunoblotting analysis. To elucidate the pathways involved in protein degradation, various cell populations were subjected to treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (MCE, Shanghai, China) at concentration of 20 μM, as well as the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) (MCE, Shanghai, China) at concentration of 10 μM, over a period of 12 h. Following this treatment, protein extraction was performed, and the samples were subsequently evaluated using western blot analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Different groups of TNBC cells were plated onto glass slides at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well and left to incubate overnight for optimal adhesion. Subsequently, the cells were stained with LysoTracker Red DND-99 dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), diluted to 1:1000 (1 μM) in serum-free DMEM, and incubated at 37°C under the dark for 30 min. Post staining, the cells were rinsed with sterile PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, made permeable with 0.3% Triton X-100, and then blocked with 5% BSA. The cells were then incubated with anti PHLPP2 antibody overnight at 4 °C. Alexa Fluor® 488-labeled secondary fluorescent antibody was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstained for 3 min, and finally anti-fluorescence quench agent was added. Images were obtained using a confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany).

Immunoprecipitation

For co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays, cells were lysed using M-PER buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein lysate were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C with either IgG antibody or Flag-tag antibody. Immune complexes were then captured by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h at 4°C with rotation. Following three washes with lysis buffer, bound proteins were boiled then subjected to Western blotting.

Cell cytotoxicity assay

Cisplatin sensitivity in TNBC cells was evaluated via CCK8 assay. Cells (6×103/well) were plated in 96-well plates overnight for adhesion, followed by 72 h cisplatin treatment (0-100 μM). After adding CCK8 reagent (1 h incubation), absorbance at 450 nm was measured (Tecan microplate reader). Viability (%) was calculated relative to untreated controls. Dose-response curves (semilog plots) and IC50 values were derived from viability-drug concentration relationships.

Animal model studies

5 × 106 MDA-MB-231 cells with stable ATP6V0D2 knockdown and their control counterparts were injected subcutaneously into the mammary fat pad of each mouse (shATP6V0D2 or shNC; n=5/group). The MDA-MB-231 cells with stable ATP6V0D2 knockdown were constructed using a lentivirus harboring shATP6V0D2 and maintained in complete medium supplemented with 1 μg/mL puromycin.15 Cisplatin (3 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered every three days upon tumors reaching 100 mm3,44 while the control group received PBS injections. Tumor volume was tracked via caliper measurements (0.5 × length × width2). When tumors in the control group expanded to a maximum diameter of 1.2 cm, the mice were euthanized. Tumors were processed for weight, imaging, and protein analysis.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) model studies

Tumor tissues from three treatment-naive TNBC patients were obtained per approved protocol. Fresh specimens were processed within 2 h of resection. Mechanically dissected tissues underwent collagenase II digestion (37 °C, 30-40 min), filtered through 100 μm strainers. After centrifugation, the pre-cooled Matrigel gel mixed with cells was collected by pipetting gun. The cell suspension was dispensed into a preheated 24-well plate (50 μl/well). Incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, organoids were maintained in commercial medium (D1Med, Hangzhou, China) with biweekly passaging at a ratio of 1∶2 to 1∶4.

For drug assays, passage 3 organoids were plated in 96-well format. After 48 h preculture, BafA pretreatment (500 nM, 96 h) preceded cisplatin exposure (0-100 μM, 72 h). Viability was quantified via AOPI (Nexcelom CS2-0106) staining-derived green fluorescence area. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval No.2022KY036).

IHC

Mice tumor tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm). Both human and mouse sections underwent xylene deparaffinization and endogenous peroxidase removing (5% H2O2). After antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated with 5% goat serum, exposed to primary antibodies (4°C overnight), followed by secondary antibodies. DAB chromogen and hematoxylin counterstaining were applied. Two blinded pathologists evaluated protein expression by grading the mean intensity for each marker and the percentage of cells that stained positively. The scoring criteria were consistent with our previous research.15

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 and SPSS 25.0 software. Cellular and molecular experiments included three independent biological replicates. Quantitative results were depicted as the mean ± SD, while categorical variables were shown as frequencies (%). Between-group comparisons used Student's t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (≥3 groups). Nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney) were applied to non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed by chi-square test. Survival differences were evaluated through Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank test. All of the statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends. Statistical significance was determined at a P < 0.05. (∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ns, no significance).
