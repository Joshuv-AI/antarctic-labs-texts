Women’s decision making autonomy on maternal and neonatal health care utilization and associated factors in central Ethiopia: a community based cross-sectional study
Authors: Ayele Sahile Abdo, Yohannes Fikadu Geda, Mesfin Difer Tetema, Mangistu Abera, Aynalem Belay, Derese Eshetu, Wubshet Estifanos, Abera Mersha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen’s decision-making autonomy is an important driver of maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization. However, in developing countries, women have limited autonomy over decisions related to their own and their newborns’ health. Limited autonomy hinders the utilization of maternal healthcare services and contributes to maternal-neonatal morbidity. This study assessed the levels and factors associated with women’s decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare service utilization in central Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Guraghe zone, central Ethiopia, from March 15, 2023, to April 15, 2023. A simple random sampling technique was used to select 466 study participants. The data were gathered through structured pretested interviews with mothers. The data were entered into EpiData 3.1 and exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27.0 for analysis. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to assess factors related to women’s decision-making autonomy. A p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence level was used to establish statistical significance in multivariable analysis.ResultsThe overall women’s decision-making autonomy was found to be 71.5% (95% CI, 67.0% − 76.0%). Age ≥ 35 years [AOR = 4.24 (1.85, 9.71)] and between 24 and 35 years [AOR = 2.78 (1.31, 5.91)], husband primary educated [AOR = 2.56 (1.33, 4.94)] and secondary and above educated [AOR = 3.66 (1.79, 7.47)], higher wealth index [AOR = 0.47 (0.24, 0.91)] and medium wealth index [AOR = 0.38 (0.19,0.76)], husband involvement [AOR = 6.19 (3.56, 10.74)], Women’s knowledge of neonatal danger signs [AOR = 2.07 (1.22, 3.49)], Women who were knowledgeable [AOR = 2.726 (1.33,5.56)] and moderately knowledgeable about maternal healthcare services [AOR = 2.81 (1.32,5.97)] were identified as significant factors.ConclusionsAlthough all women have the right to participate in decisions regarding their own and neonatal healthcare, more than one-quarter (28.5%) had no role. These findings highlight the need to strengthen health education to improve women’s knowledge on neonatal danger signs and maternal healthcare services, which is essential to empower women to participate in decisions about their healthcare. Lastly, encouraging husbands’ education and involvement in maternal and neonatal healthcare services will enhance women’s decision-making autonomy.Keywords: Autonomy, Women’s decision-making autonomy, Maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization, Central Ethiopia

Background

Autonomy is a multifaceted concept, which is difficult to quantify. It is the ability to gather information and make judgments regarding matters of personal concern [1]. Women’s autonomy refers to their ability to make decisions about their own and their children’s health, freedom of movement and financial control, without seeking permission from others [1–5]. The provision of maternal and neonatal health requires a multi-sector approach, which in turn requires the women’s strong decision-making autonomy to reverse barriers at the household level [6, 7]. Women’s autonomy plays a crucial role in the utilization of maternal healthcare (MHC) services and, consequently, contributes to the reduction of morbidity and mortality in developing countries [8–10].

Globally, an estimated 260,000 maternal deaths were reported in 2023, with approximately 92% occurring in low- and lower-middle-income countries [11], and 2.3 million neonatal deaths in 2022 [12]. Women’s decision-making autonomy is the current focus of global development efforts because it is a vital determinant of women’s health and well-being and is associated with lower fertility, greater contraceptive use, and a smaller ideal family size [13–15]. The UN proclaimed that women’s autonomy is a fundamental human right and must be fully recognized as an essential component of the framework for sustainable development in order to uphold human dignity [16]. Worldwide, approximately 55% of married women have the autonomy to make decisions about their sexual and reproductive health [17]. In developing countries, women play a vital role in the welfare of their families; however, they are also viewed as primarily as mothers and homemakers [18]. They have limited autonomy in deciding whether to visit health facilities for maternal health services [19], which directly results in poor utilization of maternal health services [20, 21]. Studies conducted in Africa reveal that 38.9% of women in Nigeria [20], 82.7% in Egypt [21], and 75% in Ghana [22] have high decision-making autonomy regarding health matters. According to the 2017 Senegal Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), only 6.3% of women reported having autonomy over their own health decisions. This reflects traditional gender roles, where men hold decision-making power, and women face economic dependence, limited education, and restrictions shaped by religious or cultural norms [23].

In Ethiopia, the number of maternal deaths decreased from 871 to 401 as reported in 2017 [24] and a Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR) increased from 29 to 33 deaths per 1,000 live births [25] owing to low healthcare utilization. According to the 2019 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), 43%, 34%, and 48% of women had ≥ 4 Antenatal Care (ANC) visits, postnatal checkups, and gave birth at a health facility, respectively [25]. Regarding their healthcare decision-making autonomy, only 11–18% of women make decisions alone, according to the 2016 EDHS report [26]. Studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia revealed that 41.4% of the Bale zone [3], 55.6% of Ambo town [27], 75.1% of Debretabor [28], and 58.4% of the Wolaita and Dawro zones [29] had decision-making autonomy in their healthcare issues. Improving maternal and neonatal health is one of the government’s concerns, both nationally and internationally, and is part of the third and fifth Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) [30]. The existing sociocultural, religious, and tribal structures that define gender roles often determine the circumstances under which women would have autonomy in healthcare decision-making [31]. Assessing the level of women’s decision-making autonomy in maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization and associated factors greatly guides health professionals, local health departments, program managers, health policymakers, and Ethiopia’s Health Sector Transformation Plan (HSTP-II), which focused on enhancing health in all policies and strategies, to identify indicators of women’s decision-making autonomy and apply appropriate measures to empower women in maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the level of women’s decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization and associated factors in central Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, period and setting

A community-based, cross-sectional study design was used to meet the study objectives. This study was conducted in Central Ethiopia, Guraghe Zone, from March 15, 2023, to April 15, 2023. Guraghe is a diverse ethnic group found in Central Ethiopia and is an administrative zone in Central Ethiopia. The district, known locally as a woreda in Ethiopia, comprises 29 kebeles—the smallest administrative units—and is located 198 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia; Ezha woreda is bordered to the south by Gumer, to the west by Cheha, to the north by Kebena and Muhor Na Aklil, and to the southeast by the Silt’e Zone and Meskan. The zone has four hospitals and 72 health centers. All hospitals provide maternal healthcare services, including antenatal care, labor and delivery, and postpartum care.

Population and eligibility criteria

The source populations were all married women who gave birth in a one-year period in Ezha Woreda, Guraghe zone, Central Ethiopia. The study population consisted of all married women who gave birth in a one-year period and resided in selected kebeles during the data-collection period. During the study period, married women who had an infant and had lived in the area for ≥ 6 months before data collection were included. Women who were seriously or mentally ill during the data-collection period were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size determination

The sample size for the first objective was determined using a single population proportion formula based on the following assumptions: a proportion of women’s decision-making autonomy on maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization and other socioeconomic aspects of 75.1% [28], 95% level of confidence, and 5% margin of error.

Where n is the required sample size, α is the level of significance, z is the value from the standard normal distribution curve corresponding to a 95% confidence level (1.96), p is the proportion of women’s decision-making autonomy in household and maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization, and d is the margin of error. By considering for a 10% nonresponse rate to minimize errors and a design effect of 1.5, the final calculated sample size was 476 participants.

Sampling techniques and procedures

A simple random sampling method was used to select the study participants. In the first stage, the kebeles in Ezha Woreda were stratified as urban or rural to make them more representative. Next, the nine rural kebeles were chosen at random by lottery from the 28 rural kebeles, and the urban kebele was chosen. The list of all women who had given birth in the past year was obtained by reviewing health post-registration books (family folders) from each selected kebele. The registration book was reviewed prior to sampling with the assistance of health extension workers. To ensure representativeness, the calculated sample size was allocated proportionally to draw study participants from each kebele. The sampling frame was then designed by numbering a list of women using the health extension worker registration books. Finally, study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique with a random number generated by a computer.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using structured, pretested, interviewer-administered questionnaires. This tool was adapted from previous studies [3, 26, 28, 29]. The questionnaire was first prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and then back-translated into English by an independent language expert to ensure consistency. Pretest conducted on 5% of the total calculated sample in Cheha woreda, which was not selected for the actual study. The questionnaire contained six sections: part one, sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics; part two, reproductive history and maternity healthcare services; part three, women’s decision-making autonomy indicators; part four, mothers’ knowledge of neonatal danger signs; part five, mothers’ knowledge of MHC services, and part six, mothers’ attitudes toward MHC services.

Data collection was conducted by five BSc-level recently graduated and three female diploma midwives to ensure participants’ safety and confidentiality. Two health officers served as supervisors.

Before commencement of data collection, data collectors and supervisors were given two days of training on data collection tools, interviewing techniques, and the objective of the study. The purpose, duration, and benefits of the study were explained to the participants. The mothers were encouraged to feel free, the confidentiality of their responses was assured, and no information was shared with anybody without the investigator. Women willing to participate signed an informed consent document and were interviewed in a private setting. The data were collected through house-to-house visits. Participants who were not contacted at the time of the survey were revisited twice to reduce the nonresponse rate.

Data quality control

To maintain the quality of the data, the questionnaire was first prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and then back-translated into English by an independent language expert to ensure consistency. Prior to data collection, a pretest was conducted. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were estimated for the knowledge and attitude of mothers toward maternal healthcare utilization, resulting in internal reliabilities of 0.76 and 0.81 respectively. Data collectors and supervisors were properly trained on how to interview, fill in the questionnaire, the objectives of the study, and maintain the confidentiality of the study subjects. The principal investigator and supervisors closely followed the data collection process. The collected questionnaires were checked daily for completeness and consistency by the principal investigator and supervisors, and timely corrections were made. Data were cleaned, coded, entered, and rechecked during data entry into computer software before analysis.

Data processing and analysis

The data were thoroughly cleaned, coded, and carefully entered into EpiData version 3.1 and exported to SPSS Windows version 27. PCA was performed to determine the household wealth status. From the 37 items included in the analysis, seven factors with 19 items were created, each with an eigenvalue ≥ 1, which explain 63.24% of the total variance. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentages, mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range were computed. For inferential statistical significance, tests were applied to reflect the association between1the dependent and independent variables. A bivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the association between each independent variable and the dependent variable. To control for potential confounders, variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable binary logistic regression model. Variance inflation factor (VIF) > 10 and Tolerance 0.1 were used as an indicators of multi-collinearity; however, no multi-collinearity was found during the analyses. The regression model’s fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, which was statistically insignificant (p-value = 0.411), indicating a good fit of the model to the data. The strength of association is presented using the Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with a 95% Confidence Interval (CI). Variables with a P value < 0.05, in multivariable analysis, were considered to be significantly associated. Finally, the results were presented in tables, graphs, and figures.

Measurements

Women’s decision-making autonomy: dichotomized into higher decision-makingautonomy and lower decision-making autonomy, which were coded as ‘‘1” and ‘‘0”, respectively. For each question, responses were coded as 2 if the woman decided alone, 1 if decisions were made jointly, and 0 otherwise. Total scores ranged from 0 to 18. Accordingly, those women who scored the mean and above the mean were considered as high decision-making autonomy, and those who scored below the mean were considered as of low decision-making autonomy [5, 28, 32].

Knowledge of neonatal danger signs (NDS): mothers who mentioned at least three neonatal danger signs among the 12 WHO neonatal danger signs without prompt were considered to have good knowledge, and those who mentioned less than three were considered to have poor knowledge [33, 34].

Husband involvement in MNCH services: the summative score of variables designed to assess husbands’ involvement with a score above the mean was considered to be involved, and below the mean was considered as not involved in MNCH-related activities [28].

Knowledge of women on maternal healthcare: Assessed by considering knowledge of the components of maternal healthcare, including ANC, delivery, and PNC services, as well as danger signs. After a composite score was determined, mothers who scored above 84% were considered highly knowledgeable, if they scored between 50 and 84% as moderately knowledgeable, and those who answered < 50% were considered poorly knowledgeable mothers [3].

Women’s attitude toward MHC service: a woman’s overall positive or negative evaluation of MHC service was measured using positively stated statements on attitudes toward MHC with possible responses on a five-point Likert scale. Participants who responded to attitude questions with a mean score above the mean value were considered to have a favorable attitude, while those who scored below the mean value were considered to have an unfavorable attitude [3].

Wealth index: households were assigned scores based on the number and types of consumer goods they owned: assets including domestic animals, durable goods, productive tools, and housing characteristics [26]. Each item was coded as 0 = No and 1 = Yes, and then principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The scores are then ranked in three quintiles so that each household receives their quintile category [29].

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

This study included 466 mothers with a response rate of 97.9%. The mean age and standard deviation of the respondents were 30.63 (± 5.28) years. More than half, 266 (57.1%) participants were aged 25–34 years. Out of the total participants, 399 (85.6%) resided in rural areas. The majority of participants, 255 (54.7%), were Orthodox religion followers, 452 (97.0%) were in monogamous marriage, and 372 (79.8%), lived in a nuclear family structure. The median family size was 4.0, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 2; 246 (52.8%) respondents had fewer than five family members, and 432 (92.7%) respondents were exposed to media (Table 1).

Women’s decision-making autonomy on MNHC utilization

Overall, women’s decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization was 71.5% (95% CI: 67.0%, 76.0%) (Fig. 1). Nearly three-quarters, 331 (71%), women made decisions regarding their own health care to visit the health facilities when they became sick jointly with their husbands. Concerning MHC service utilization, more than half, 274 (58.8%), of the study participants decided together with their husbands. A total of 332 (71.2%) and 339 (72.7%) respondents made joint decisions with their husbands about the health of the newborn and having an additional child respectively (Table 4).

Among the autonomous women, 228 (70.2%) had four or more ANC visits, 310 (93.1%) had postnatal checkups, 308 (92.5%) delivered at a health facility, 266 (79.9%) had knowledgeable about neonatal danger signs, 198 (59.5%) had knowledgeable toward MHC services, and 198 (59.5%) had favorable attitude towards MHC services.

Factors associated with women’s decision-making autonomy

In the multivariable analysis, age, husband’s educational status, household wealth index, husband’s involvement in MNCH-related activities, knowledge of NDS, and knowledge of MHC services were statistically associated with women’s decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization. Women aged ≥ 35 years and 25–34 years were 4.24 and 2.78 times more likely to have had higher decision-making autonomy on maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization as compared to those women aged 18–24 years, (AOR = 4.24; 95% CI:1.85,9.71) and (AOR = 2.78; 95%CI:1.31,5.91) respectively. The likelihood of women’s decision-making autonomy on maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization was 3.66 and 2.56 times higher among women whose husbands were educated above secondary and primary education than those who had no formal education, (AOR = 3.66; 95% CI, (1.79, 7.47) and (AOR = 2.56; 95% CI:1.33, 4.94), respectively.

Women from higher and medium household wealth quantiles had a 53% and 62% lower chance of participating in decision-making on maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization than those from lower wealth index households (AOR = 0.47; 95%CI:0.24,0.91) and (AOR = 0.38; 95%CI:0.19,0.76) respectively. The odds of having high decision-making autonomy for maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization were 6.19 times higher for women who had husband involvement in MNCH-related activities than in those who did not have husband support (AOR = 6.19; 95%CI:3.56, 10.74). Women who were knowledgeable and moderately knowledgeable about MHC services were 2.72 and 2.81 times more likely to have had higher decision-making autonomy on maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization, respectively, than their counterparts (AOR = 2.72; 95%CI:1.33,5.56) and (AOR = 2.81; 95%CI:1.32, 5.97), respectively. Similarly, the likelihood of women’s decision-making autonomy was twice as high among women who had adequate knowledge of NDS than among their counterparts (AOR = 2.07; 95%CI: 1.22, 3.49) (Table 5).

Discussion

Promoting women’s decision-making autonomy is an important driver of MNHC utilization. Among the characteristics assessed in this study, age, husband’s educational status, household wealth index, husband’s involvement in MNCH-related activities, knowledge of NDS, and knowledge of MHC were significantly associated with women’s decision-making autonomy in MNHC. Overall, women’s decision-making autonomy regarding their neonatal healthcare utilization in this study was 71.5% (95% CI: 67.0%, 76.0%). The findings of this study are consistent with those of studies conducted in northwest Ethiopia (75.1%) [28] and Ghana (75%) [22]. The result, however, is lower than the analysis of the 2016 EDHS report (81.6%) [26] and DHS conducted in Egypt (82.7%) [21]. This inconsistency might be due to differences in the measurement of outcome variables, sample size and the sociodemographic characteristics of the study populations. Another possible explanation might be this study was conducted in a specific geographic and demographic context, whereas the results from Egypt and EDHS were obtained from a national demographic health survey.

The results of this study were higher than those of other studies conducted in Nigeria (38.9%) [20], Senegal (6.26%) [23], and Ethiopia (41.4% [3], 55.6% [27], and 58.4% [29]). This discrepancy might be due to differences in the time gap and methodological and sociodemographic characteristics of the study populations. Women’s decision-making autonomy in this study could be attributed to the fact that, over time, there has been a strong commitment and effort made by the Ethiopian government on maternal health, women’s education, and the expansion of health services. In this study, nearly three-quarters (69.3%) of the study population were educated; educated women are more aware of their right to free choice and might be more capable of exercising their will as far as healthcare decisions are concerned [35, 36]. This study included women from both rural and urban areas. Urban women tend to have relatively better education and economic status, along with active engagement with digital technologies such as mobile phones, the internet, and social media, which facilitates exposure to a wide range of health information and ultimately strengthens their autonomy in health-related decision-making.

This study also showed that women’s age was significantly associated with their decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare service utilization. Accordingly, maternal age greater than 35 years and between 25 and 34 years was more likely to have had high decision-making autonomy in healthcare utilization compared to those aged between 18 and 24 years. This finding is supported by a study conducted in Nigeria [37] and two studies conducted in Ethiopia [28, 29]. This might be because, as age increases, their educational status and participation in income-generating activities also increase women’s economic independence and challenge the traditional belief of male dominance in decision making, thereby improving spousal communication in household decision-making [38]. Another possible explanation might be that women’s positions in society are socially constructed and their status varies depending on their age.

Women whose husbands had primary and secondary education status were more likely to have had higher odds of high decision-making autonomy than those who did not. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Nigeria [5], Ghana [22], and Ethiopia [29]. This might be because the more educated a husband is, the more he accepts gender equality and believes in equal participation in decision-making and more aware of human and reproductive rights, leading them to support women’s autonomy and free choice in healthcare decisions, particularly regarding neonatal healthcare utilization [35].

Husband involvement in MNCH-related activities is a significant factor in women’s decision-making autonomy. Women who had husbands involved in MNCH-related activities were found to have increased odds of having high decision-making autonomy compared to women who did not have husband support. This study is congruent with a study done in Ethiopia [28]. This may be due to the fact that most of the husbands who are involved in MNCH activities are more aware and more concerned about the health of the family. Further, women whose husbands are encouraged to participate in maternal health services help relieve the worry, discomfort, and anxiety related to pregnancy and childbirth problems, which will increase the certainty of the women, in turn increasing shared decisions. In addition, information missed by mothers about their neonatal health might be addressed by their husbands. Studies have shown that husband involvement encourages women’s use of skilled maternal and neonatal health services and participation in socioeconomic-related activities [39–41]. The World Health Organization also advocates for shared decision-making between partners as a means to promote respectful maternity care and empower women with greater autonomy during the maternal period.

Recently, household wealth status has emerged as a significant factor in women’s decision-making autonomy in maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization. Women from higher and medium household wealth quantiles had a lower chance of participating in decision-making on maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization than those with lower wealth index households. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Nepal [35], Egypt [42], and Ethiopia [29]. This indicates that as households become richer, they are less likely to participate in decision-making. The negative influence of a higher household economy on the maternal and neonatal healthcare decision-making autonomy of women could be the consequence of less involvement of women in household assets as a result of limited participation in household income. In this study, nearly half (41.6%) of the study participants were housewives who did not participate in income-generating activities, so in turn they depend on their husbands’ income; hence, their decision-making autonomy is restricted, supporting the idea that women who work for an income are more likely to be exposed to a wider range of ideas and social roles, which may change their views about what they should be able to do [43]. This study was contradicts two other studies from Ethiopia [3, 28]. A possible reason for this variation might be that previous Ethiopian research focused on average monthly income to measure the relative socioeconomic status of households. Another explanation may be the differences in autonomy measurement dimensions and sample sizes.

Women who were knowledgeable about neonatal danger signs were more likely to have higher decision-making autonomy than those who had poor knowledge. This finding was in line with studies conducted in Ethiopia [3, 28]. This might be because being knowledgeable about neonatal health empowers women to challenge their husbands because comprehending the seriousness of the illness leads to increased shared decisions. Furthermore, this might also be due to the enabling factor of the husband’s involvement in MNCH-related services [28, 34].

Women who were knowledgeable and moderately knowledgeable about MHC services were more likely to have higher decision-making autonomy than their counterparts. This study was supported by a previous study conducted in Ethiopia [3]. This might be due to the fact that those women who have knowledge of MHC services could easily influence and convince their husbands about the importance of utilizing MHC services. The expansion of Health extension workers may facilitate behavioral changes in the utilization of maternal health services, increase health literacy, and support women’s autonomy in health-related decision-making [44].

Conclusion

This study showed that nearly three-quarters of women had high decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization. Advanced maternal age, educated husband, lower household wealth index, husband involvement in MNCH-related activities, being knowledgeable about MHC services, and NDS were significantly associated with decision-making autonomy regarding maternal and neonatal healthcare utilization. Even in households with adequate resources, limited female participation in healthcare decision-making leads to low uptake of maternal healthcare services. Therefore, targeted strategies aimed at enhancing women’s autonomy through education, male involvement, and health literacy may lead to more immediate and sustainable improvements in maternal and neonatal health outcomes. We wish to recommend to the Woreda Health Office and healthcare providers that investing maximum effort to make women knowledgeable about NDS and MHC services is a double advantage, through designing proper health education via different programs, such as regular women’s conferences within health facilities, and delivering information via local media to improve decision-making autonomy. Additionally, creating community awareness and formulating supportive policies to encourage male involvement in MNCH-related activities may further enhance maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study was a community-based study with a simple random sampling technique. The authors used the multi-dimensional measurement of women’s decision-making autonomy. Because the instrument’s reliability, validity, and sensitivity are high, the study allows for results comparison. However, there are limitations to this study. The recall period for some mothers was quite high, may not accurately recall the experiences of visiting health care services. As a result women’s decision-making autonomy is subject to potential recall bias. The study may be subject to social desirability bias due to the use of interviewer-administered. Since data on covariates and the outcome variables were collected at the same point in time, no causal interpretation can be made of the relationships between variables. Additionally, the generalizability of the findings may be limited in settings that differ culturally, socially, or economically from the study area.
