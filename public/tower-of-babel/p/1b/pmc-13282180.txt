Studying When to Add Biological Therapy to Cytotoxic Therapy in Advanced Cancer Care Using Real World Data: How to Avoid Design Choices That Generate Immortal Time
Authors: Xabier Garcia‐Albeniz, Julia Vila‐Guilera, Manuel Zamparini, Helena Oliveres, Vicente Alonso‐Orduna, Jaime Feliu, Ana Fernández‐Montes, Marta Martín‐Richard, Elisa Gálvez‐Munoz, Ana Ruiz‐Casado, Alfonso Yubero‐Esteban, Jorge Aparicio, Julia Alcaide‐Garcia, Javier Gallego Plazas, Carlos Fernandez‐Martos, Rosa Gallego‐Sanchez, Mónica Guillot Morales, Rubén Leno, Francis Esposito, Sandra Lopez‐Prades, Míriam Cuatrecasas, Barbra A Dickerman, Joan Maurel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSince real‐world data lack information on treatment assignment (usually the only information available is treatment prescription/administration) and early outcomes can happen in advanced cancer, some design choices may introduce immortal time due to selection or due to treatment strategy misclassification.ObjectiveTo illustrate design choices that would biassedly introduce immortal time and propose unbiased alternatives, using as a case study the estimation of the effects of different monoclonal antibody (mAb) sequencing strategies on overall survival in colorectal cancer patients.MethodsWe specified a target trial to estimate the effect on overall survival of initiating mAb within 8 weeks of starting first line chemotherapy versus initiating mAb with second line chemotherapy. The first biased design choice would be requiring the initiation of second line chemotherapy for eligibility. This was avoided by aligning eligibility with the initiation of first line chemotherapy. The second biased design choice would be excluding from the first line mAb strategy those who die during the grace period without starting mAb. This was avoided separately via cloning, censoring and weighting and by sequential emulation.ResultsThere were 1014 eligible patients in GEMCAD 1401. The CCW approach estimated a 4‐year survival difference (second—first line mAb) of 4.3% (95% CI) (−3.3%; 10.0%) and a risk ratio (RR) of 0.95 (0.88; 1.04). Sequential trial emulation estimated a survival difference of −2.8% (−7.5%; 1.2%) and a RR of 1.04 (0.98; 1.10). Biased analyses that introduced immortal time estimated an implausible early survival benefit for mAb initiation with second‐line (6‐month RR of 0.13 (0.03; 0.28)).ConclusionsOur results are compatible with no effect on survival by mAb sequencing. Misalignment of time zero, treatment assignment, and eligibility introduced immortal time.Keywords: clone censor weighting, immortal time, sequential emulation, target trial emulation

Introduction

Treatment for patients with advanced cancer involves receiving drugs in sequences as “lines of treatment”, consisting of one or several drugs received for a pre‐specified duration or until tumor progression, toxicity, or a decision to stop treatment is reached. When a drug with a new mechanism of action is available as an add‐on for cytotoxic therapy, the optimal sequencing may be unknown because pivotal clinical trials usually evaluate its efficacy vs. the standard of care, rather than comparing early use (e.g., to achieve an early control of the tumor) vs. later use (e.g., to delay toxicity). Such is the case for the use of monoclonal antibodies (mAb) for the treatment of advanced colorectal cancer: their effect on improving cancer outcomes compared with the standard of care is well‐established [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], but the added value of administering them earlier vs. later is unknown.

In the absence of clinical trials to guide decisions on mAb sequencing, causal inference can rely on real‐world data (RWD), for example, by emulating a target trial [6]. Causal inference from RWD can be challenging due to features inherent to the data (e.g., unmeasured confounding or measurement error) and due to features related to some design choices, which is the focus of this article. Since RWD lack information on treatment assignment (usually the only information available is treatment prescription or administration) and early outcomes can happen in advanced cancer, some design choices may introduce immortal time due to selection or due to treatment strategy misclassification [7]. Here we use sequencing of mAb in advanced colorectal cancer as a case study to illustrate biased design choices that introduce immortal time, followed by a proposal and implementation of solutions. The manuscript is structured as follows. First, we articulate the research question by specifying the target trial. Second, we identify two design choices that, due to the nature of the research question, would biasedly introduce immortal time. Third, we use RWD (an observational registry of patients with advanced colorectal cancer) to emulate the target trial avoiding the introduction of immortal time and compare the results with those obtained using other biased design choices.

Eligibility Criteria

Diagnosis of mCRC between June 2014 and June 2018, treatment‐naïve for metastatic disease, no previous history of cancer in the last 3 years, an ECOG performance status of 0–2, no history of metastatic resection, and a candidate to start chemotherapy (e.g., FOLFOX, CAPOX, FOLFIRI or FOLFOXIRI).

Treatment Strategies

The strategies to be compared would be (1) addition of mAb within 8 weeks (grace period) of initiating first line chemotherapy (provided there is no contraindication, defined as disease progression, worsening of ECOG PS to 3–4, hospital admission, onset of treatment toxicity or adverse events, or a decision to stop cytotoxic therapy during the grace period), (“first line mAb strategy”) and (2) deferral of initiation of mAb concomitantly with second line chemotherapy, (“second line mAb strategy”). In both strategies, the duration of mAb treatment would be at the discretion of the treating physician.

Treatment Assignment

Patients would be randomly assigned to a strategy at baseline and be aware of the strategy they have been assigned to.

Outcomes

Death due to any cause, grade 3–4 toxicity, hospitalization for any cause.

Follow‐Up

Patients would be followed from the time of treatment assignment (baseline) to the earliest of death, last contact with the registry, or June 2021 (administrative end of study).

Causal Contrast

The per‐protocol effect of adhering to the specified treatment strategies throughout the study follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

For each outcome, the per‐protocol effect would be estimated by fitting a pooled logistic regression model containing an indicator of treatment strategy, a flexible function of follow‐up time (restricted cubic splines with knots at times 3, 34, 80, and 169 weeks) and a product term between treatment strategy and follow‐up time. This model would be fitted to the data after censoring patients if and when they deviate from their assigned treatment strategy [8]. Specifically, patients in the first line mAb strategy would be censored at the end of the grace period if they have not started mAb by then (in the absence of a contraindication defined as disease progression, worsening of ECOG PS to 3–4, hospital admission, onset of treatment toxicity or adverse events, or a decision to stop cytotoxic therapy during the grace period) and patients in the second line mAb strategy would be censored at the earliest of the following: initiation of mAb before initiating second‐line chemotherapy or initiation of second line chemotherapy if mAb is not started the same week. Time‐varying non‐stabilized inverse‐probability weights would be used to adjust for baseline and time‐varying confounding [9]. The inverse probability weights would be a function of baseline (age, ECOG performance status, Charlson Comorbidity Index, RAS mutational status, BRAF mutational status, microsatellite instability status, sidedness of primary tumor, primary surgery, number of organs affected by metastasis, number of lesions and size of metastasis in liver, number of lesions and size of metastasis in lung, peritoneal involvement, node involvement, LDH level within normal range) and time‐varying covariates (ECOG performance status, Charlson Comorbidity Index, toxicity, LDH level within normal range). The predicted values from these models would be used to estimate the 48‐month risk of each outcome under each treatment strategy. The estimated weights would be truncated at the 99th percentile to avoid undue influence of outliers. Risk of grade 3–4 toxicity and of hospitalization would be estimated without elimination of competing events [10]. Effects would be estimated as risk differences and ratios, bounded by percentile‐based 95% confidence intervals computed using bootstrapping with 500 samples.

Emulation of the Target Trial

We emulated this target trial using the GEMCAD 1401 registry database (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02254941 [11]) which collected data prospectively from patients with advanced colorectal cancer initiating first‐line treatment from 47 Spanish centers from June 2014 to June 2018 (1106 patients enrolled). GEMCAD 1401 was designed with several objectives, one of them being the evaluation of different sequences of treatment. The registry collected tumor samples and recorded the following information at baseline and over follow up: demographic and clinical characteristics, tumor response, treatment toxicities (the occurrence of grade 3–4 toxicity, whether it prompted stopping or switching treatments and, if so, the organ affected), hospitalizations and reasons for stopping treatment.

Due to the nature of the specified treatment strategies, the emulation of the target trial can be at risk of creating immortal time due to selection and due to treatment strategy misclassification [7]. We elaborate on these challenges in the corresponding components of the emulation of the target trial.

Eligibility Criteria

Same as for the target trial. The time of the application of the eligibility criteria is key to avoid the first challenge, which is the potential for immortal time due to selection. In our target trial emulation, patients assigned to the strategy “initiation of mAb concomitantly with second line chemotherapy” will only have the opportunity to initiate mAb if they have initiated second line chemotherapy (i.e., they have documented disease progression and are fit enough to continue treatment). If we (incorrectly) included in the analysis only those who initiated a second line of treatment, the period between initiating first line chemotherapy (baseline) and initiating second line of treatment would be immortal. The solution to avoid introducing immortal time by design is aligning the application of eligibility criteria with treatment assignment and the beginning of follow‐up. In our application, this means that patients who, within the study period, do not start neither mAb in the first line grace period nor a second line of treatment because they die or never present an indication to do so, are compliant with the second‐line mAb strategy and should be included and contribute follow‐up from the study baseline.

Treatment Strategies

Same as for the target trial.

Treatment Assignment

Given that GEMCAD 1401 does not contain information on treatment assignment decisions, only on treatment administered, patients need to be classified into treatment strategies according to the latter. This opens the door for the second challenge, which is the introduction of immortal time due to misclassification into treatment strategies. In the real world there can be a waiting (or grace) period from the time a treatment decision is made to the time a treatment is administered, for several reasons. For example, in our case study, mAb may not be initiated at the exact same time as first‐line chemotherapy because of administrative delays or a need for approval by payers, for additional testing, or to accommodate further consideration by patients (the length of the grace period, 8 weeks, was proposed by practicing oncologists in the research team based on usual care; Figure S1 shows the distribution of time from initiation of first‐line chemotherapy to initiation of mAb). In our target trial, the grace period to add mAb to first‐line chemotherapy is 8 weeks, which implies that treatment strategies are not distinguishable at time zero. A form of misclassification of treatment assignment that would introduce immortal time would consist of considering those patients who die during the grace period without initiating mAb as non‐compliant with the first line mAb strategy. This would effectively grant immortal time to the remaining patients in the first line mAb strategy, that is, the time it took them to initiate mAb within the grace period.

For the current emulation, we apply two possible solutions to avoid the introduction of immortal time due to misclassification when using a grace period: cloning‐censoring‐weighting (CCW) [12, 13] and changing the causal question to make the treatment strategies distinguishable at time zero. CCW consists of assigning patients to all treatment strategies their baseline data are compatible with (in our case, effectively creating two copies of each individual). The second approach consists of comparing treatment strategies that are distinguishable at time zero by reformulating the first‐line mAb strategy as “Initiate mAb concomitantly with first line chemotherapy”, without a grace period. Therefore, individuals who initiate mAb concomitantly with first line chemotherapy are classified into the first line strategy, and into the second line strategy otherwise. In both approaches, as in the target trial, individuals are censored when they deviate from their assigned strategy and time‐varying non‐stabilized inverse‐probability weights are applied to adjust for this artificial censoring (see Statistical Analysis).

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Same as for the target trial.

Causal Contrast

Observational analogue of the per‐protocol effect, that is, the effect under complete adherence to the treatment strategies.

Statistical Analysis

For the analysis in which the research question involves an 8‐week grace period to initiate mAb as first line therapy, the analysis is the same as in the target trial except that we created two individuals (clones) per eligible patient and assigned one to each treatment strategy. For the analysis that reformulates the first line mAb treatment strategy to make it distinguishable at baseline (eliminating the grace period), we emulated a series of trials starting at each of the 8 weeks following the initiation of first‐line chemotherapy, applying eligibility criteria and classifying individuals into treatment strategies based on information from the corresponding baseline week. The 8 cohorts are pooled for the outcome analysis [14]. Time to event probabilities are standardized to the joint distribution of the baseline covariates. See Supporting Methods for details on the computation of weights and outcome models.

Sensitivity Analyses

We assessed the robustness of our estimates to several analytic decisions, including: (1) changing the length of the grace period of the CCW analysis to 4 weeks (vs. 8 weeks in the primary analysis), (2) stabilizing the weights of the CCW analysis to emulate a uniform initiation of mAb during the grace period (i.e., the proportion of eligible patients initiating mAb each week of the grace period is the same [12]), (3) emulating a series of trials starting at each of the 56 days (instead of 8 weeks) following the initiation of first‐line chemotherapy and classifying patients into treatment strategies based on information on the baseline day, and (4) truncating weights at the 99.5 and 97.5 percentiles (vs. the 99th percentile in the primary analysis).

Biased Analyses Introducing Immortal Time

To visualize the impact of immortal time due to selection bias and due to treatment strategy assignment misclassification on the estimated risk of death in this application, we used a Kaplan–Meier estimator to plot the overall survival of the cohort from eligibility (natural history) and compared it to that in a selection of patients who initiated second‐line chemotherapy and in a group of misclassified patients where only those who initiated mAb within the grace period were classified into the first‐line mAb strategy (i.e., omitting deaths that prevented first‐line mAb initiation). Additionally, we compared the survival of the selection of patients who initiated second‐line chemotherapy and mAb concomitantly versus those who received mAb with first‐line chemotherapy by adjusting for baseline variables via standardized parametric survival analysis as described above. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1. Code is available at https://github.com/jvilaguilera/GEMCAD1401.

Sensitivity Analyses

The length of the grace period in the CCW analysis is arbitrary, and research questions corresponding to different lengths may be relevant for different decision makers or health care systems. We emulated a target trial using a 4‐week grace period to start first line mAb. The estimated 4‐year survival difference (second—first line mAb) was 4.3% (−3.2%; 15.1%) and the 4‐year risk ratio was 0.94 (0.82; 1.04).

In the CCW analysis, the first line mAb strategy, which specifies that mAb must be initiated within 8 weeks of starting first line chemotherapy, can have multiple versions, corresponding to all the possible patterns of initiation of mAb during the grace period [12]. The main analysis evaluates a pattern that is the one observed in the data (where most of the patients initiate mAb early in the grace period, Figure S1) and creates a pseudo‐population where patients initiate mAb at the end of the grace period if they have not done so by then. We explored a different pattern, consisting of a uniform mAb initiation during the grace period (Supporting Methods). The estimated 4‐year survival difference (second—first line mAb) was 8.6% (0.6%; 15.6%) and the 4‐year risk ratio was 0.90 (0.82; 0.99). Risks of grade 3–4 toxicity and of hospitalization were materially the same in the sequential emulation analysis as in the CCW analysis.

In the sequential trial emulation analysis, we classified individuals into treatment strategies based on information from the corresponding baseline week. If the risk of the outcome is not negligible in the weekly time interval, this could introduce immortal time because only patients who live through the baseline week could initiate mAB any day of that week. To evaluate whether our choice of 7‐day intervals for the sequential emulation was sensitive to this, we repeated the analysis decreasing the interval to 1 day, effectively emulating a sequence of 56 trials. The estimated 4‐year survival difference was −2.3% (−5.9%; 2.5%) and the 4‐year risk ratio was 1.03 (0.97; 1.09).

Results of the main analysis (both CCW and sequential trial emulation) were robust to different truncations of extreme weights (Table S3).

Analyses Introducing Immortal Time

Figure 3 shows the natural course of the 1014 eligible patients, together with the survival (from the study baseline) of patients who initiated mAB concomitantly with second‐line chemotherapy (n = 123). The latter represents a selection of the eligible patients based on post‐baseline information and thus biasedly granted with immortal time. Additionally, Figure 3 depicts the survival of patients who initiated mAb within the grace period (n = 610). If individuals who died before the end of the grace period are not considered compliant with the “mAb as first line” strategy, then these patients are misclassified and the “mAb as first line” group would also be granted with immortal time (the time from initiation of first‐line chemotherapy to initiation of mAb). In both cases, the introduction of immortal time translates into an apparently early improvement in survival relative to the natural course of the cohort, compatible with bias rather than with a true causal effect of therapy. Adjustment for baseline variables does not eliminate the bias: the 6‐month baseline‐adjusted RR was 0.13 (0.04; 0.27) (Figure S4 and Table S5).

Kaplan–Meier estimation of the natural survival course of eligible patients, among patients who initiated mAb within the grace period, and among a selection of patients who initiated second‐line chemotherapy, GEMCAD 1401 (2014–2018).

Discussion

Using the GEMCAD 1401 registry data we emulated a target trial comparing the addition of mAb at earlier vs. later lines of treatment. Our estimates suggest that the addition of mAb to first line chemotherapy treatment compared with deferring their addition to second line did not have a meaningful effect on the 4‐year overall survival of patients. We showcase the importance of aligning eligibility and treatment assignment with time zero [14, 15] and propose design solutions to successfully emulate a target trial and avoid introducing immortal time due to selection and due to treatment strategy misclassification [7].

Immortal time due to selection can arise in both RCTs and observational studies when evaluating the effect of different sequences of treatment under complete adherence if patients are assigned to the deferred treatment strategy based on post‐baseline conditions (e.g., tumor progression and fitness to initiate a second line of treatment). Specifying the treatment strategy of the target trial and aligning the application of eligibility criteria with time zero avoids immortal time due to selection.

Immortal time due to treatment strategy misclassification, instead, is a problem that is more likely to happen in observational studies than in RCTs because in RCTs information on treatment assignment exists, as opposed to observational studies, where usually only information on prescription, dispensation or administration is available. In research questions, like the current one, involving a grace period to initiate treatment, individuals' baseline data may be consistent with more than one treatment strategy. Scenarios with early events (as can be the case in advanced cancer treatment studies) or early losses to follow‐up open the door for the misclassification of patients into treatment strategies. CCW and the reformulation of treatment strategies so that they are distinguishable at baseline are two approaches that avoid the problem of immortal time due to treatment strategy misclassification [7, 15]. The preference of one approach vs. another should be based on whether changing the research question is clinically relevant, and on the strength of the identifying assumptions of each approach, which may differ and will be specific to each application. In our case, the CCW approach assumed exchangeability over the grace period conditional on baseline and time‐varying variables, and the sequential emulation approach assumed baseline exchangeability in each of the 8 cohorts, conditional on baseline variables. To illustrate the impact of immortal time due to selection and misclassification, we conducted a biased analysis. This analysis introduced immortal time by imposing post‐baseline selection (requiring patients to start second line chemotherapy) and by misclassifying patients into treatment strategies (excluding from the first line mAb strategy those who die in the grace period without starting mAb), yielding implausible early survival benefits.

Another difference between causal analyses conducted in RCTs vs. in observational data is that in RCTs, the positivity assumption holds by design, whereas in RWD, such assumption will only hold if the treatment strategies are represented across all patient subgroups defined by combinations of covariate levels [16]. In the centers contributing data to GEMCAD1401 there was heterogeneity in mAb administration because reimbursement policies for mAb varied by center, which benefited the current analysis by providing different patterns of use in the study population.

In both cases, an explicit specification of treatment strategies as part of the causal estimand is needed to appropriately estimate the effect under complete adherence [17, 18]. The effect under complete adherence can only be estimated if all the clinical scenarios in which treatment is indicated or contraindicated are known. In our application, the occurrence of contraindications for mAb during follow‐up was an important factor to assess adherence (e.g., a patient who never received mAb during follow‐up is considered compliant with the second line mAb strategy if they develop a contraindication to continue treatment after first line therapy).

Besides its methodological pertinence, this work is clinically relevant because the evaluation of the effects of mAb timing across sequences of treatment is important given the trade‐off between early use of mAb to achieve tumor control or later use to delay potential toxicity as shown in our analysis and associated costs. Anti‐EGFR mAb (cetuximab, panitumumab) can induce skin toxicity like rashes and pruritus [19], and antiangiogenic mAb (bevacizumab, aflibercept) can induce toxicity in the form of hypertension, thromboembolic events, proteinuria, bleeding, and gastrointestinal perforation [20, 21], which merits careful consideration of treatment strategies that minimize toxicity while maintaining clinical efficacy.

While this study's design choices prevent the introduction of immortal time, the potential for unmeasured confounding, as in any observational study, cannot be ruled out. For example, if doctors deferred mAb initiation in frailer patients and we failed to capture and adjust for frailty, the survival of the second line mAb strategy would appear artificially worse. However, it is reasonable to expect that frailty would also affect the risk of hospitalization for any cause, which was very similar across treatment strategies in our analyses, supporting the assumption of no unmeasured confounding.

In conclusion, our results are compatible with no effect on survival by the sequencing of mAb in patients with advanced colorectal cancer who start treatment for metastatic disease. We showcase how aligning time zero and treatment assignment with eligibility avoided introducing immortal time due to selection and using CCW when treatment strategies are not distinguishable at baseline or changing the research question to make them distinguishable at baseline avoided introducing immortal time due to treatment strategy misclassification.
