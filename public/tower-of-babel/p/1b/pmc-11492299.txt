Evaluation of a Diazo‐Based Point‐Of‐Care Bilirubin Assay careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip
Authors: Woojae Kwoun, Jeong Joong Lee, Young‐Ah Youn, Hyojin Chae, Jehoon Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNeonatal jaundice (NNJ) affects a significant proportion of newborns globally, with an increased burden in low‐resource settings. Effective health risk management of NNJ is hindered, particularly in resource‐constrained environments, where early detection and treatment are challenging. The careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip, a point‐of‐care testing (POCT) device based on a diazo‐method, offers a potential solution by enabling onsite bilirubin measurement, thus, addressing the gap in early NNJ detection and management.MethodsThe current study evaluated the analytical performance of the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip for precision, linearity, method comparison, and lot‐to‐lot consistency following CLSI guidelines. For method comparison, 105 residual EDTA whole blood samples were analyzed with the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip and compared with reference measurements from the Roche Cobas c702 analyzer. Additionally, statistical analyses, including Passing‐Bablok regression and Bland–Altman plots, were performed.ResultsThe careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip showed allowable (<10%) within‐laboratory imprecision of 2.5%–3.6% across all levels and demonstrated linearity over the range of 4.16–439.3 μmol/L. Method comparison revealed a constant negative bias with a mean bias −4.19 μmol/L. However, the 95% confidence interval (−7.10 to −1.28 μmol/L) of the bias is covered by the prespecified allowable bias of 8.3%, at medical decision point. Lot‐to‐lot variation ranged from 0.14%–6.49%, and was within the acceptable critical difference of 8.3%.ConclusionThe careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip provided accurate and reliable bilirubin measurements that could contribute to neonatal care in settings lacking central laboratory facilities.

Introduction

Quantitation of serum total bilirubin is performed to confirm the diagnosis of jaundice, also known as icterus. Several methodologies exist for the measurement of total bilirubin concentrations. The coupling reaction of bilirubin with diazotized sulfanilic acid, known as the diazo reaction, is the most widely used [1, 2]. Alternative approaches include the enzymatic assay, based on the oxidation of bilirubin with bilirubin oxidase, the direct photometric measurement, which quantifies bilirubin by measuring its absorbance, and the noninvasive percutaneous measurement of bilirubin levels [3].

Jaundice is a common condition in neonates, occurring in 60%–80% of term neonates globally. Although most cases of neonatal jaundice are self‐limited, severe hyperbilirubinemia, if not promptly recognized and treated, can result in acute bilirubin encephalopathy or kernicterus, leading to long‐term neuromotor impairment and irreversible damage [4, 5, 6]. Notably, the impact of this condition remains significantly greater in low‐ and middle‐income countries compared to affluent countries, highlighting the disparity in healthcare resources [4]. Among other limitations, the lack of total bilirubin assays for accurate quantification of total serum bilirubin levels in neonates in low‐ and middle‐income countries represents an unmet clinical need, leading to an unacceptably high risk of the most preventable form of neonatal brain injury [7, 8, 9].

The CareSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip (WELLS BIO, INC., Seoul, Republic of Korea) is a recently developed point‐of‐care testing (POCT) assay that measures total bilirubin using the diazo method. It consists of the careSTART S1 analyzer, a handheld reflectance photometer based on LEDs and photodiodes, and careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip. Together, they are referred to as the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip. While there are other POCT devices, including BiliDx, Bilistick, and Calmark Neo, the abovementioned devices are based on simple spectrometry whereas the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip is the only POCT based on a diazo method. The diazo method is considered as the clinical reference method for measuring total serum bilirubin concentration, is the most commonly used method on multichannel instruments and unaffected by interference from other bilirubin forms and chromophores, like carotenoids [10]. Hence, this study aimed to evaluate the analytical performance of careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip, a novel POCT device based on a diazo method.

Materials and Methods

Samples

EDTA anticoagulated whole blood samples were used for this study. The samples were selected from residual specimens that have been analyzed within 1 h of collection and then stored at room temperature, without light exposure, for no more than 4 h. The EDTA whole blood was split into two aliquots. One aliquot was used to measure total bilirubin with the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip, while the second aliquot was centrifuged at 2000× g for 10 min to separate plasma, and total bilirubin was then measured with the Roche Cobas c702 analyzer. To minimize differences in results caused by variables such as temperature, artificial light exposure, and storage durations, measurements for each specimen were performed simultaneously. Samples with total bilirubin concentrations outside the analytical measurment range (17.1–513.1 μmol/L for whole blood, 1.7–51.3 μmol/L for serum or plasma) of careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin test were excluded from the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and Ethics Committee of Seoul St. Mary's Hospital (IRB: KC23DISS0442).

Instruments

For careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip, 20 uL of EDTA anticoagulated blood, was loaded onto the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip and inserted in the careSTART S1 Analyzer according to the manufacturer's specifications. The careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip consists of several layers, including a spreading (mesh) layer, a RBC filter layer separating cell from plasma and a reaction layer with embedded reagents based on the modified Malloy–Evelyn method (Figure 1). And the azobilirubin produced by the modified Malloy–Evelyn reaction is measured as the reflectance of a 525 nm light source. Total bilirubin levels were also measured with the laboratory diazo method, standardized against the Doumas reference method [2], using Roche Bilirubin Total Gen.3 assay on a Roche Cobas c702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, US).

Precision

For precision evaluation, three levels of manufacturer‐provided quality control (QC) materials with mean values of 73.03, 186.09, and 298.12 μmol/L, respectively, were used to assess repeatability and within‐laboratory imprecision. Precision was assessed using a single lot of careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip and one careSTART S1 analyzer. Each level of QC material was measured five times daily over 5 days according to the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute guideline (CLSI) EP15‐A3 [11].

Linearity

Linearity was assessed using five levels of the Validate GC4 TBIL Set (LGC Clinical Diagnostics, Inc., US). Each level was measured four times in a single run and a weighted least squares regression was fit through the samples' mean observed results versus their expected concentrations in accordance with CLSI EP06‐Ed2 guideline [12]. The deviation from linearity, that is, the difference between the observed value and the expected value from the linear model, was calculated along with the confidence interval (CI) for each difference. The mean difference and their associated CIs of each sample were compared with the allowable deviation from linearity (ADL), which was established at 12.8%, representing 50% of the allowable total error (TEa) for total bilirubin [11], according to the desirable specifications from the EFLM biological variation database [13].

Method Comparison

For method comparison, 105 residual EDTA anticoagulated whole blood samples were utilized. An anliquot of whole blood was utilized to measure total bilirubin with the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip. Simultaneously, a corresponding plasma aliquot was used to measure total bilirubin using Roche Bilirubin Total Gen.3 assay, a colorimetric diazo method standardized against the Doumas reference method, on a Roche Cobas c702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, US), simultaneously. Pairs of total bilirubin measurements from both methods were analyzed using a Bland–Altman plot and Passing‐Bablok regression, with bias estimated as outlined by CLSI EP09‐A3 guideline [14]. The CI of the measured bias was compared to the allowable bias, which was set at 8.3%, based on the desirable specifications from the EFLM biological variation database [13].

Lot‐To‐Lot Comparison of careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip

In lot‐to‐lot evaluation, three different lots of careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip were assessed following the guideline specified in CLSI EP26‐Ed2 [15], aiming for a statistical power of at least 85% with a predefined critical difference (CD), which was established at an allowable bias of 8.3% [13]. Twelve measurements per lot were conducted, with two consecutive measurements across three different concentrations of QC materials. The equivalence among the lots was then determined.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by Analyse‐it for Microsoft Excel (Analyse‐it Software, Ltd., Leeds, UK). Weighted least square linear regression was used for the assessment of linearity and Passing‐Boblok nonparametric linear regression and Bland–Altman analysis were used for method comparison with two‐tailed p value of 0.05 for statistical significance.

Results

Precision

In precision evaluation, the coefficient of variation (%CV) for repeatability was 2.6% for QC level 1 (73.03 μmol/L), 2.7% for QC level 2 (186.09 μmol/L), and 2.3% for QC level 3 (298.12 μmol/L), which were smaller than the manufacturer's claimed repeatability of 10%; for within‐laboratory imprecision, the %CV was 3.6% for QC level 1, 2.8% for level 2, and 2.5% for level 3, all lower than the within‐laboratory imprecision of 10% stated by the manufacturer (Table 1). The repeatability and the within‐laboratory imprecision were also less than the desirable specifications for imprecision of 10%, based on the EFLM biological variation database [13].

Method Comparison

In method comparison, the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip and the Roche Cobas c702 demonstrated strong correlation with a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.994, with a regression slope of 0.9932 (95% CI, 0.9588 to 1.021) and an intercept of −4.46 μmol/L (95% CI, −7.34 to −1.57 μmol/L) by Passing–Bablok regression analysis. Bland–Altman plots indicated a constant bias with a mean bias of −4.19 μmol/L and 95% CI ranging from −7.10 to −1.28 μmol/L (Figure 3). Based on these analyses, there was no proportional bias (95% CI includes zero), but there was a statistically significant constant bias. However, the CI for constant bias included the prespecified allowable bias of 8.3%, according to biological variation [13], at the medical decision point of 256.55 μmol/L (15 mg/dL) [16].

Discussion

Total bilirubin measurement in neonates, traditionally conducted by automated instruments in central laboratories, can be logistically challenging due to the required time and blood volume required per test. Consequently, POCT within the neonatal unit presents a viable alternative, particularly in resource‐constrained settings where central laboratory facilities may not be available.

Three primary methodologies exist for neonatal total bilirubin assessment, namely optical skin‐based indirect measurement (i.e., transcutaneous bilirubinometer), reagent‐free optical blood analysis (i.e., direct spectrophotometry based bilirubinometer), and reagent‐based blood analysis [17]. Among them, the direct spectrophotometry bilirubinometer is less suited for POCT due to the centrifugation requirement in its pre‐analytical process. Hence, optical skin‐based indirect measurement using a transcutanous bilirubinometer and reagent‐based blood analysis are suitable for POCT. Transcutaneous bilirubinometers does not directly measure bilirubin, but measures the yellowness of the reflected light transmitted from the skin for an indirect prediction of bilirubin concentrations. Hence, while advantageous in its non‐invasiveness, it can be affected by multiple factors, including the site of meaurement, gestational age, degree of skin pigmentation as well as covering of skin during phototherapy [18, 19].

The novel POCT device, careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip, is a reagent‐based blood analysis, consists of disposable bilirubin strips, supports whole‐blood testing without need of a pre‐analytical centrifugation step using cell‐plasma separation layer embedded in the strip. While there are previous studies of other POCT devices, including BiliDx, Bilistick, and Calmark Neo, that have reported no significant difference in test performance between POCT and central laboratory methods [20], these POCT methods are based on a spectrometry. Our study is the first study to have evaluated the analytical performance of a diazo‐based POCT device for total bilirubin meausrement. While a direct comparison with other POCT devices was not the scope of this study, the analytical performance of careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip was validated in terms of precision, linearity, method comparsion, and lot‐to‐lot comparability. The careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip demonstrated strong correlation with the reference Cobas c702 device. Passing–Bablok regression analysis confirmed a robust correlation, without significant proportional or constant bias. Precision evaluation across three QC material levels revealed exceptional accuracy, with imprecision rates below 3% for levels 2 and 3, and slightly above at level 1. Both careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip and the reference method employ the diazo principle, underscoring a longstanding, reliable, and accurate approach to bilirubin measurement in POCT settings.

This study has several limitations. First, the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip was compared in adult patient samples. Evaluating its performance further with neonatal specimens would enhance its utility as a POCT device. Second, factors that could affect POCT measurment of total bilirubin, including interference of hemolysis, the effect of hematocrit, were not specifically sought. Diazo method's accuracy in total bilirubin testing can be influenced by various factors, including hemolysis, which may introduce spectral interference. Additionally, the consistency of plasma volume in the test sample could vary due to hematocrit differences, potentially leading to bilirubin underestimation in high‐hematocrit patients or neonates, necessitating adjustments for this predictable bias.

Nonetheless, POCT devices for total bilirubin measurement that process whole blood samples can significantly enhance the diagnostic capabilities of healthcare facilities lacking central laboratory. The versatility of such devices is remarkably increased if they are designed to accommodate a variety of test analytes, enabling a comprehensive diagnostic analysis beyond the mere determination of bilirubin levels. This adaptability makes these devices an invaluable asset in expanding the diagnostic capacity within resource‐constrained environments, especially for newborn screening.

In conclusion, the careSTART S1 Total Bilirubin Strip shows promising scalability and effectiveness in measuring total bilirubin. Exploring its performance further with neonatal specimens or those with high hematocrit, would enhance its utility as a POCT device. Further studies in this regard could solidify its role as a valuable tool in managing neonatal health.
