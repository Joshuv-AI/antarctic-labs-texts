Multimodal imaging and advanced quantitative techniques for HER-2 status prediction in breast cancer
Authors: Qingxue Wang, Ning Zheng, Qingqing Yu, Shuo Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHER-2-positive breast cancer is a biologically distinct subtype, accurate early assessment of HER-2 status is therefore critical for guiding personalized treatment. Currently mainly detected through immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) on biopsy or surgical specimens. However, these methods are invasive, susceptible to sampling errors, and lack the capability for real-time, noninvasive monitoring of tumor heterogeneity or treatment response. Therefore, the development of noninvasive imaging-based predictive methods has gained significant research interest. Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) can quantify tumor perfusion parameters (Ktrans-vascular permeability, Ve- extracellular space) through dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI), measure the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) using diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and obtain metabolic information via positron emission tomography-MRI (PET-MRI), which are closely associated with HER-2 expression status. Concurrently, radiomics and deep learning (DL) systematically extract multidimensional features of breast tumors from multimodal imaging data, including morphological parameters (sphericity, surface area), first-order statistical metrics (kurtosis, K; skewness), and textural features (gray-level co-occurrence matrix GLCM, quantisation texture space distribution; gray-level run-length matrix GLRLM, evaluation of homogeneous region size), thereby constructing high-dimensional quantitative analysis datasets. Based on the resolution of the heterogeneity of the feature spatial distribution, the DL algorithms can autonomously mine the potential imaging patterns closely related to the expression of HER-2 molecules and establish a non-invasive prediction model. Although traditional single-parameter models, such as the ADC derived from DWI, can provide valuable information about the tumor microenvironment, their predictive efficacy is often constrained by parameter inconsistencies and a lack of standardization. As a narrative review, this article argues that multimodal imaging, radiomics, and deep learning are better equipped to capture the complex HER-2-related tumor heterogeneity, thereby providing a stronger theoretical foundation for guiding personalized treatment strategies and prognostic evaluation.Keywords: Breast cancer, Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, MRI, Radiomics, Deep learning

Introduction

Breast cancer, with the second-highest global incidence rate among all human malignancies, stands as the most prevalent cancer type in women worldwide and ranks first in mortality rates among female malignancies [1]. Treatment options for breast cancer vary by molecular typing in most cases [2]. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2) is a critical prognostic factor in breast cancer biology and a key target for therapy [3, 4]. The intensity of HER-2 expression influences the five-year disease-free survival and overall survival of breast cancer patients. Differences in HER-2 molecular expression status correspond to varying prognoses [5]. Current MRI has high sensitivity but insufficient specificity in breast cancer detection. Conventional sequences mainly reflect anatomical structures and cannot reflect the functional metabolism or molecular characteristics of tumors. Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) can non-invasively provide biological information from breast cancer lesions, including angiogenesis, cellular density, and metabolic status, which are closely associated with the HER-2 expression status. Current magnetic resonance techniques commonly applied in breast imaging include: Conventional functional sequences (single-modality): Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI); Multimodal integration technology: Positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (PET-MRI). Although not yet a routine screening tool due to limitations of radiotracer use and cost, PET-MRI offers unique potential for addressing complex challenges such as HER-2 heterogeneity through its capacity for synchronous acquisition of metabolic and functional parameters [6, 7]. In comparison, traditional multi-parameter MRI remains the clinical preference due to its non-invasive nature and high accessibility. Radiomics and deep learning (DL), emerging research fields focused on high-throughput extraction and quantitative analysis of imaging features, have gained widespread attention in recent years and have been applied in breast cancer research [8, 9]. This review aims to establish a “foundational→advanced→integrated” analytical framework to elucidate the core strengths and clinical value of multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), radiomics, and deep learning in predicting HER-2 status, thereby offering a clear roadmap for future research.

Fundamental imaging techniques

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) model

DWI is an MRI-based imaging technique that detects lesions by revealing tissue microstructural characteristics through water molecule diffusion. The primary parameter of this model is the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) [10]. ADC eliminates confounding T1 and T2 effects visible on DWI images and provides a quantitative estimate of water diffusion processes within tissues [11].

Studies have shown that the ADC values of HER-2-negative breast cancer are lower than those of HER-2-positive breast cancer [12–14]. This phenomenon can be attributed to the combined effects of increased tumor cell density and tissue perfusion on ADC values. Overexpression of HER-2 inhibits normal apoptosis while accelerating cell growth and division, leading to abnormal cellular proliferation [15]. The proliferation of tumor cells reduces the intercellular space and compresses surrounding blood vessels, thereby decreasing vascular perfusion. Meanwhile, HER-2 promotes angiogenesis via vascular endothelial growth factor [16], and newly formed tumor blood vessels tend to have larger diameters and incomplete endothelial structures compared to normal vessels [17]. Consequently, increased tissue perfusion in HER-2-positive tumors outweighs the effect of high cellular density on reducing ADC values. Given that ADC is unable to exclude the influence of microcirculatory perfusion effects [18], it cannot accurately reflect the true motion of water molecules. Therefore, a biexponential model has been introduced to address this limitation.

Advanced imaging techniques

Multi-b-value diffusion models: intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) and stretched exponential model (SEM)

IVIM is an MRI technique that utilizes multi-b-value DWI and biexponential fitting methods to separate and estimate the contributions of tissue perfusion and water molecule diffusion on signal intensity, thereby providing valuable insights into tissue microstructure and physiological status [19, 20]. The core assumption is that DWI signal attenuation is mediated by a combination of fast and slow diffusion components, corresponding to blood perfusion in the capillary network and real diffusion of water molecules between extracellular and intracellular, respectively.

The model is solved by nonlinear fitting for three key parameters: the true diffusion coefficient D, which reflects the real water molecule motion, the perfusion-related diffusion coefficient D∗, which is related to the blood microcirculation and mainly reflects the contribution of perfusion to the signal attenuation of the dispersive image, and the perfusion fraction f, reflects microcirculation volume percentage [21].

Model data collection requires a multiple b-value strategy (typically containing 0–10 sets of low b-values and high b-values ≥ 200 s/mm²), and parameter stability can be improved by a segmented fitting algorithm: the low b-value intervals (typically b < 200 s/mm²) are mainly fitted to f and D∗. While the high b-value data are used to determine D. That is, when under conditions of low b-values, the signal primarily reflects the microvascular perfusion status. In contrast, at higher b-values, the contribution of microcirculatory perfusion to the signal becomes negligible, and the remaining signal predominantly reveals the restriction of water molecule diffusion [22, 23].

FENG et al. conducted MRI imaging on 72 breast cancer patients and delineated regions of interest (ROI) on IVIM images [24]. They found that the mean D∗value in HER-2 positive breast cancer patient group was significantly higher than that in HER-2 negative breast cancer patient group, with a statistically significant difference. This finding may be attributed to the relationship between D* and tissue perfusion status [25], as HER-2-positive tumors exhibit increased neovascularization and enhanced microcirculatory perfusion, leading to higher D* values. Uslu et al. performed DWI imaging on breast cancer patients using 16 different b-values and applied IVIM biexponential fitting to the acquired data [26]. They observed that both f and D* values were higher in HER-2-overexpressing breast cancer than in other subtypes, which deviated from the findings of Wang et al. [27]. The inconsistency in results may stem from differences in b-value selection: Wang’s study employed a sufficiently high b-value (b = 2000 s/mm²), whereas the maximum b-value used in the aforementioned studywas only 1000 s/mm² [26]. This suggests that b-value selection may influence the final quantitative results. Currently IVIM still has huge challenges in terms of b-value optimization difficulties, motion artifact interference and signal-to-noise ratio limitations. Since there is no standardized criterion for b-value selection in IVIM modeling [28], and the biexponential model artificially separates diffusion within a voxel into fast and slow proton pools while neglecting interactions between them and other diffusion mechanisms, it fails to fully capture the diffusion characteristics within a voxel. To address these limitations, the stretched exponential model has been proposed.

The stretched exponential model (SEM) serves as a complementary approach to IVIM, assuming a continuous distribution of diffusion rates within a voxel. This model was introduced by Bennett et al.in 2003 to describe the heterogeneity of diffusion rates within a voxel [29]. SEM parameters include the diffusion heterogeneity index (Alpha, α) and the distributed diffusion coefficient (DDC) [30]. The α parameter reflects the heterogeneity of diffusion rates within a voxel, ranging from 0 to 1 [31]. A higher α value (closer to 1) indicates lower diffusion heterogeneity, whereas a lower α value (closer to 0) suggests higher diffusion heterogeneity [32]. This heterogeneity is closely related to the microstructural complexity observed at high b-values [33]. DDC, which represents a weighted ADC with multi-exponential decay characteristics, is highly correlated with ADC values [34]. It represents the average diffusion rate within the voxel and is inversely correlated to tissue cell density.

Mao et al. included 158 breast cancer patients and performed MRI imaging, from which two parameters, ɑSEM and DDCSEM, were derived using the SEM model [35]. Their study found that both αSEM and DDCSEM were significantly lower in the HER-2 low expression group compared to the HER-2 overexpression group, among these parameters, DDCSEM demonstrated moderate discriminative performance (AUC = 0.752) in the model differentiating the two groups. This reduction in DDCSEM aligns with the known mechanism associated with restricted water molecule diffusion, which may indicate greater intratumoral heterogeneity and more restricted water diffusion in HER-2 low-expression breast cancer. Similarly, Ji et al. demonstrated that the α parameter in the SEM model could effectively distinguish HER-2-overexpressing subtypes from other molecular subtypes, particularly between the HER-2-overexpressing and Luminal A groups (p = 0.006) [36]. These findings suggest that SEM can predict HER-2 expression status by quantifying diffusion heterogeneity, further highlighting the potential role of diffusion heterogeneity in breast cancer molecular subtyping. Despite its advantages in breast lesion assessment, SEM is not widely applied in breast imaging due to its model complexity, as well as its limited stability and specificity. Currently, research on SEM for predicting HER-2 expression status remains scarce, necessitating further studies to validate its clinical utility.

Diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) model

The DKI model is based on a non-Gaussian diffusion model, which quantifies the deviation of water molecule diffusion from a Gaussian distribution using kurtosis (K). It primarily evaluates tumor structural heterogeneity through mean kurtosis (MK) and mean diffusion (MD) [37]. Compared with conventional DWI, DKI captures the non-Gaussian diffusion of water molecules in biological tissues, potentially characterizing tissue heterogeneity and interactions between water molecules and surrounding structures, thereby providing a more accurate representation of microscopic tissue properties [38, 39].

A higher degree of diffusion restriction results in a greater MK value. Regarding the correlation between the DKI parameter MK and HER-2 status, marked contradictions exist among the findings reported by different studies. You et al. analyzed the correlation between non-Gaussian diffusion parameter MK and HER-2 status, revealing that MK values were significantly higher in the HER-2-positive group than in the HER-2-negative group (p = 0.015). The standalone discriminative performance yielded an AUC of 0.629 (95% CI: 0.546–0.707) [40]. However, studies by Zhang, Meng, Kang, et al. reported the opposite pattern, demonstrating lower MK values in the HER-2-positive group compared to the HER-2-negative group [41–43]. This inconsistency underscores the current limitations in the field. Figure 2 presents examples of DKI parameter maps for HER-2-positive and HER-2-negative breast cancer patients. This discrepancy may stem from the complex structural characteristics of HER-2-positive breast cancer, which is associated with high cellular density and abundant tissue perfusion. These factors exert opposing effects on water molecule movement: high cellular density restricts diffusion, whereas increased perfusion facilitates water mobility, thereby reducing diffusion restriction. The relative contributions of these two mechanisms within the tumor may vary, potentially leading to differences in observed results across studies.

Fractional order calculus (FROC) and continuous-time random walk (CTRW) model

The FROC model applies fractional-order calculus theory to analyze the fractional-order dynamic properties associated with diffusion environment variations. It quantitatively evaluates the degree of water molecule diffusion, diffusion range, and tissue structural homogeneity across different tissue types. Compared to conventional ADC analysis, the FROC model provides a more direct assessment of microenvironmental changes [44]. The FROC model includes several key parameters: the diffusion coefficient (D), spatial fractional order (β), and spatial feature parameter (µ) [45]. D, an anomalous diffusion coefficient, is similar to the ADC and indicates the extent of restricted diffusion of water molecules. β is a spatial fractional order index that reflects tissue heterogeneity within a voxel, and µ is a spatial parameter related to tissue microstructure. The CTRW model evolves from the FROC model [46], with key parameters including time-dependent diffusion heterogeneity (α), spatial diffusion heterogeneity (β), and anomalous diffusion coefficient (Dm) [47]. These parameters directly reflect the structural heterogeneity within a voxel, which is linked to tissue complexity and the local microenvironment. The CTRW model can capture the variability in displacement exhibited by water molecules during migration (jumping process) or the inconsistency in the time required for movement (trapping effects). These phenomena are mapped to the heterogeneity in time and spatial diffusion, with the parameters α and β quantifying and describing these variations [48]. By integrating the anomalous diffusion coefficient Dm, the CTRW model enables a multidimensional evaluation of tissue property variations.

A study by Mao et al. demonstrated that α_CTRW, D_CTRW, β_FROC, D_FROC, and µ_FROC values were lower in HER-2 low-expression breast cancer patients than in those with HER-2 overexpression [35]. The reductions in D_CTRW, D_FROC, and µ_FROC suggest that water molecule diffusion is more restricted in HER-2 low-expression tumors than in HER-2 overexpression tumors. A key finding of this study lies in the ROC analysis results: the α_CTRW parameter demonstrated the highest reported discriminative performance to date (AUC = 0.802) in differentiating between HER-2 low-expression and overexpression groups, and it significantly outperformed the traditional DWI-derived ADC value. This may be attributed to the non-Gaussian distribution characteristics of α_CTRW, which directly reflect tumor structural complexity and spatial heterogeneity, whereas the DWI model is constructed based on a Gaussian distribution. These encouraging results strongly suggest that the CTRW model holds unique potential for characterizing HER-2 heterogeneity in breast cancer. However, similar to the SEM, current research investigating FROC/CTRW models for predicting HER-2 status remains scarce. Crucial aspects such as model complexity, parameter stability, and the reproducibility of the reported superior performance require rigorous validation and in-depth investigation in future independent cohorts and large-sample studies.

Functional perfusion imaging: dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI)

DCE-MRI is an imaging technique based on T1-weighted MRI images, which acquires images through continuous dynamic scanning after the intravenous injection of exogenous contrast agents. It is widely used in clinical practice [49]. By employing pharmacokinetic modeling, DCE-MRI can compute quantitative perfusion parameters, serving as an imaging marker for quantifying in vivo tumor angiogenesis. Currently, it is one of the most sensitive methods for breast cancer detection [50]. The key perfusion parameters include: volume transfer constant (Ktrans), extravascular extracellular space volume (ve), rate constant (kep), and plasma volume fraction (vp). Ktrans reflects the combined characteristics of blood flow perfusion, vascular network density, and vascular permeability, while kep focuses on evaluating vascular permeability. Higher Ktrans and kep values are strongly associated with neovascularization [51], indicating enhanced tumor perfusion. In addition, DCE-MRI can also predict HER-2 expression status through semi-quantitative hemodynamic parameters. The semi-quantitative DCE-MRI parameters, inflow enhancement rate (W-in) and outflow decay rate (W-out), represent the rate of concentration changes of the contrast agent in the intravascular and extravascular extracellular space, respectively. Time-to-peak (TTP) after contrast injection represents the moment when the contrast agent concentration reaches its peak in the blood vessels.

Yang et al. analyzed the Kep distribution between the HER-2 positive and HER-2 negative groups, revealing that both the mean Kep and percentile values were significantly higher in the HER-2 positive group [52]. Apart from extreme values, all other parameters showed statistically significant differences, aligning with findings by Du et al., who reported a statistically significant positive correlation between Kep values and HER-2 expression [53]. Liu et al. conducted a prospective analysis of 67 patients with invasive breast cancer, comparing preoperative DCE-MRI and conventional MRI across different molecular subtypes [54]. Their results demonstrated that HER-2-enriched tumors had significantly higher Ktrans values than other subtypes (p < 0.05). This increase is likely due to HER-2-overexpressing breast cancers exhibiting higher histological grades, more advanced pathological stages, and greater invasiveness, leading to increased tumor angiogenesis and immature vessel formation, ultimately raising Ktrans values. Similarly, in this study, Kep values were also higher in HER-2 positive tumors compared to Luminal A tumors, for the same reasons. Integration and intelligent prediction

Positron emission tomography-MRI (PET-MRI)

PET-MRI is an emerging quantitative hybrid imaging technology that integrates metabolic and functional PET data with anatomical details and functional perfusion information from MRI [56], providing a complementary imaging dataset. Currently, research on its clinical applicability in breast cancer is highly active [57]. Among existing clinical imaging modalities, MRI has the highest sensitivity for breast cancer detection [58], but its specificity is relatively low. MRI can achieve high spatial resolution soft tissue imaging of 0.5–1 mm³ through multiparametric imaging based on hydrogen proton relaxation, while PET can provide metabolic information of molecules in vivo through radiotracer with a spatial resolution of only 4–6 mm³, but it can quantitatively detect glycolytic metabolic parameters such as the standardized uptake value of maximal uptake (SUVmax). The integration of these two modalities can complement each other, ensuring spatial and temporal consistency of functional metabolic and anatomical information and enabling improved diagnosis and evaluation of breast lesions [7].

Incoronato et al. developed a multivariate model incorporating Ktrans from DCE-MRI and the maximum standardized uptake value SUVmax from PET [59]. This model correctly predicted tumor molecular subtypes (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER-2, and triple-negative) in 77.6% of patients (38/49). Jena et al. found that higher mean standardized uptake values (SUVmean), SUVmax values, and total lesion glycolysis were associated with larger tumor size, higher tumor grade, higher Ki67 proliferation index, and triple-negative receptor status [60]. Sasaki et al. reported in their study of 94 women that SUVmax was significantly correlated with HER-2 positivity and a higher Ki-67 proliferation index [61].

In addition, preliminary studies have demonstrated the potential of combining imaging genomics with PET-MRI-based machine learning (ML) for comprehensive assessment of tumor phenotypes and development of predictive models [57, 62], the core mechanism of which is: extracting quantitative image features by integrating complementary information from PET metabolic function and MRI anatomy, using ML or DL to build an association model between image features and HER-2, and finally realize non-invasive prediction.

Overall, PET-MRI combines the strengths of both PET and MRI imaging techniques and has demonstrated potential value in predicting HER-2 expression status.

Radiomics

Radiomics is a method for extracting additional information from medical images through advanced feature analysis. It leverages ML to extract high-dimensional features from images to assist in diagnosis [63]. High-dimensional features include handcrafted features extracted by traditional ML algorithms, such as support vector machines (SVM) and random forest (RF), as well as deep features extracted directly from raw images by deep Learning (DL) techniques like convolutional neural networks (CNN) [64]. Handcrafted features can be divided into semantic and non-semantic features, with non-semantic features further categorized into first-order (histogram features), second-order (texture features), and higher-order (filtering patterns) statistical features [65–67]. The basic workflow of radiomics involves image acquisition, tumor segmentation, feature extraction, and the construction and analysis of radiomic models [68]. Notably, technical harmonization proves essential for ensuring reproducible radiomics results. The field is driving standardization via the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI), which establishes unified definitions for quantitative features-including rigorous mathematical specifications for texture filters [69]. Complementarily, the Radiomics Quality Score (RQS) system serves to evaluate the clinical readiness of radiomics studies, with critical metrics encompassing external validation performance and feature stability under technical variations [70].

In recent years, radiomics has found broad application in molecular subtyping research for breast cancer [8, 71, 72], with DCE-MRI being the most widely used and diagnostically effective modality in MRI-based imaging genomics [73, 74]. Ramtohul et al.used fat-saturated T2-weighted imaging (T2) and dynamic contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (DCE-T1) to predict HER-2 expression status in breast cancer [75]. The AUCs for the training and test sets were 0.79 and 0.80, respectively. Additionally, Yin et al. extracted optimal features from DCE images to construct a radiomics model, combining the best features from the training set with statistically significant clinical MRI features to build a nomogram for predicting HER-2 expression status [76]. The AUCs for the radiomics model in the training and test sets were 0.875 and 0.845, respectively, while the nomogram achieved AUCs of 0.892 and 0.886 in the training and test sets. These findings suggest that multi-parametric radiomics can enhance the prediction of HER-2 expression status. Zhou et al. extracted quantitative radiomic features from DCE-T1, T2, and DWI, and used SVM to predict HER-2 expression status [77]. When the tumor peripheral region was expanded by 4 mm and the data from the three different sequences were combined, the maximum AUC in the test set reached 0.795. Further analysis using Delong’s test revealed that the optimal radiomic features and those derived from dual-sequence imaging exhibited statistically significant differences in AUC in the test set (P < 0.05). This suggests that combining features from different regions and sequences can improve the accuracy of HER-2 status assessment. The study also highlighted the importance of Laws and Gabor features for predicting HER-2 status. LI et al. constructed radiomic features using nine Gabor, six Laws, and four gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM) features from the intra- and peri-tumoral regions of functional parameter maps derived from DCE-T1, showing the best performance in predicting HER-2 status [78]. Xu et al. found that morphological, first-order, GLDM, gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), and gray-level zone-length matrix (GLSZM) texture features were associated with HER-2 expression in breast cancer [79]. These findings collectively suggest that radiomic features are valuable in predicting HER-2 expression status in breast cancer. However, it is important to point out that the translational application of imaging genomics as a clinical biomarker still faces multiple challenges: (1) Standardization barriers: Harmonization of critical parameters during image acquisition particularly b-value selection in DWI and contrast agent dosing/injection timing protocols is imperative. Post-processing requires standardized segmentation algorithms (defining scenarios for manual vs. automated approaches) and feature harmonization techniques (e.g., ComBat for multicenter heterogeneity correction). (2) Sources of heterogeneity: Scanner vendor discrepancies, magnetic field strength variations (1.5T vs. 3T), inter-observer variability in ROI delineation, and inadequate sample sizes collectively compromise feature stability. In addition, the standardisation of hardware and software for data processing still needs to be broken through, and how to achieve efficient management and analysis of high-dimensional data generated based on rigorous imaging genomics processes is also a technical bottleneck that needs to be resolved urgently.

Deep Learning (DL)

ML is a branch of artificial intelligence, encompasses two fundamental concepts: shallow learning (SL) and DL. The paramount application of ML in medicine currently focuses on medical image analysis, with particularly significant advancements achieved in breast cancer research [80]. Unlike traditional ML approaches that rely on manual feature engineering, DL—as an advanced subset of ML [81]—leverages multi-layer neural networks to autonomously recognize complex patterns in images and learn discriminative features [82]. This technology exploits the hierarchical architecture of artificial neural networks to extract sophisticated imaging characteristics from massive datasets. Crucially, DL demonstrates superior robustness to image variations compared to conventional ML algorithms, enabling effective model training on diverse, large-scale medical imaging data [83]. Among the many types of DL networks, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are the most widely used [84, 85]. CNNs can classify pixels into masses or non-masses, assisting radiologists in diagnosis [86]. Ha et al. developed a CNN model based on MRI features that effectively predicts the molecular subtypes of breast cancer based on feature information from breast MRI images [87]. The study analyzed MRI data from 216 breast cancer patients, including 74 Luminal A subtype cases, 106 Luminal B subtype cases, 13 HER-2 overexpression cases, and 23 triple-negative breast cancer cases. The model was able to predict the molecular subtypes of breast cancer with an accuracy of 70%, and the AUC value reached 0.853, demonstrating strong predictive performance. Other DL techniques, such as 3D-CNN, have also been used to assess HER-2 expression status, achieving an accuracy of 0.781, with AUC values of 0.812 and 0.762 for the training and validation datasets, respectively, also showing good predictive power [88]. Additionally, previous studies used DCE-MRI radiomics to predict HER-2 expression status in breast cancer, with AUC values ranging from 0.6 to 0.83 [89, 90]. To further improve predictive performance, Guo et al. developed a deep learning radiomics (DLR) model based on MRI. The results showed AUC values of 0.868 and 0.763 for HER-2 negative and HER-2 overexpression groups in the training and validation datasets, respectively [91]. The AUC values for the HER-2 low-expression group and HER-2 zero-expression group were 0.855 and 0.750, respectively, demonstrating strong differentiation ability between different subgroups. In conclusion, MRI radiomics holds significant potential in predicting HER-2 expression status and will greatly assist in improving diagnostic accuracy for clinicians. Guo et al. showed that the significant decrease in AUC values between the training and validation sets suggests that the performance of the current model is still limited by insufficient sample size, heterogeneity of image acquisition parameters in multi-centres, and the limitation of a single imaging sequence. Further analyses showed that differences in technical aspects, ranging from the standardisation of image acquisition parameters, consistency of post-processing procedures to the accuracy of lesion segmentation, may reduce the reproducibility of imaging biomarkers, which in turn affects their clinical translational efficacy. In addition, deep learning-based imaging genomics models are still ‘black-box’ in nature, and the biological interpretation of feature-phenotype associations is insufficient, which may limit the credibility of their practical application in clinical decision support systems. The choice between radiomics and deep learning for HER-2 prediction hinges on two pivotal axes : (1) Interpretability-accuracy trade-off: Radiomics offers clinically transparent biomarkers (e.g., morphology features correlating with HER-2 spatial distribution), whereas DL’s black-box nature constrains pathological mechanism discovery despite superior nonlinear pattern recognition.(2) Data efficiency constraints: DL’s parameter-heavy frameworks necessitate extensive annotation resources, while radiomics with dimensionality reduction techniques (LASSO/mRMR) achieves reliable outcomes with limited datasets. Thus: Select radiomics for hypothesis-driven studies valuing biological insights; employ DL when maximizing prediction accuracy is paramount and sufficient annotated data is available. Conclusion

This review establishes a hierarchical analytical framework—“foundational single-parameter imaging → advanced modeling → multimodal integration and artificial intelligence”—to systematically examine the evolution and current state of predictive efficacy for HER-2 status in breast cancer using multimodal imaging and advanced quantitative techniques. These techniques non-invasively capture functional, metabolic, and heterogeneity characteristics of tumors, offering a potentially powerful tool for molecular subtyping beyond traditional pathology and guiding personalized treatment decisions. Studies indicate that integrating multimodal imaging information and applying artificial intelligence algorithms, such as radiomics and deep learning, significantly enhances predictive accuracy compared to single-parameter approaches or traditional assessments. This integration aids in establishing a more precise diagnostic and therapeutic framework while reducing subjective bias. However, the field faces several critical challenges:

Lack of standardization in imaging acquisition and post-processing workflows, leading to feature drift across centers. Specifically: (1a) Inconsistent parameter results for advanced DWI models (e.g., IVIM, DKI), primarily stemming from the absence of unified standards for b-value selection (number, range, distribution), resulting in unstable parameter estimation and poor inter-center comparability, thereby diminishing their predictive value; (1b) Unverified incremental value and generalizability of novel diffusion models (e.g., CTRW, FROC), as their advantages and robustness relative to traditional methods (e.g., mono-exponential apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), radiomics) under multi-center or different scanner conditions remain unclear.

Scarcity of well-annotated, diverse datasets. This poses a significant risk of overfitting for advanced algorithms, particularly deep learning, limiting model generalizability and severely hindering the development and validation of robust models.

Absence of standardized multi-center evaluation protocols and rigorous prospective validation studies, casting doubt on the clinical translatability of existing research findings.

Lack of effective strategies for fusing multimodal information (imaging + liquid biopsy), restricting opportunities to capture a more comprehensive picture of tumor biology for further enhancing predictive performance.

Future perspectives

To overcome current challenges and advance the field towards clinical application, future research should focus on the following concrete and actionable directions:

Establish technical standards for advanced DWI models: Systematically investigate and define optimal b-value protocols (number, range, distribution) for models like IVIM and DKI to establish unified standards, resolve parameter inconsistencies, and improve inter-center comparability. Rigorously validate novel diffusion models: Conduct well-designed multi-center studies to directly compare the performance of novel models (e.g., CTRW, FROC) against traditional mono-exponential ADC or radiomics models in predicting HER-2 status, rigorously assessing their incremental value, robustness, and generalizability.

Develop robust feature extraction and analysis methods: Investigate novel feature extraction and quantitative analysis methods that minimize feature drift caused by variations in field strength, sequence parameters, and reconstruction algorithms, enhancing model stability across centers.

Conduct prospective multi-center validation trials: Design and implement large-scale, prospective, multi-center clinical trials involving diverse populations (different ethnicities, disease stages, HER-2 expression levels). Rigorously evaluate the diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, stability, and actual impact on clinical decision-making (e.g., treatment selection) and patient outcomes (e.g., treatment response, survival rates) of mpMRI combined with radiomics/deep learning models for predicting HER-2 status on independent external validation sets.

Deeply integrate Explainable AI (XAI): Prioritize the integration of XAI techniques (e.g., SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)) during model development. This enables clinicians to understand the basis of predictions (e.g., key imaging features/regions), builds trust, and may uncover novel biomarkers.

Explore multimodal information fusion: Investigate novel strategies for effectively integrating mpMRI/PET-MRI-derived radiomic features with liquid biomarkers—such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) or HER-2 extracellular domain (ECD) levels—to improve prediction accuracy, early detection, and treatment response monitoring.

Build standardized infrastructure and collaboration mechanisms: (i) Establish cross-institutional, interoperable data-sharing platforms adhering to FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) to facilitate the creation of high-quality, well-annotated multi-center breast cancer imaging-clinical-molecular datasets; (ii) Develop and disseminate standardized software tools or guidelines for the entire radiomics analysis pipeline (image acquisition, preprocessing, segmentation, feature extraction, model training/validation) to ensure result comparability and reproducibility.

By systematically addressing these key issues—particularly optimizing technical standards, completing rigorous multi-center validation, ensuring model transparency, and advancing multimodal fusion—the non-invasive HER-2 status assessment framework based on multimodal imaging, radiomics and DL holds significant promise for transitioning from research exploration to clinical practice. Ultimately, this approach has the potential to provide robust and reliable support for the precise subtyping, personalized treatment decision-making, and prognostic management of breast cancer patients.
