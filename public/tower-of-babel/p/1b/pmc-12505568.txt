Diagnostic accuracy of generative large language artificial intelligence models for the assessment of dental crowding
Authors: Khaled Wafaie, Mohamed E Basyouni, Tanmoy Bhattacharjee, Sabarinath Prasad, Baraa Daraqel, Hisham Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence (AI) models have shown potential for addressing text-based dental enquiries and answering exam questions. However, their role in diagnosis and treatment planning has not been thoroughly investigated. This study aimed to investigate the reliability of different generative AI models in classifying the severity of dental crowding.MethodsTwo experienced orthodontists categorized the severity of dental crowding in 120 intraoral occlusal images as mild, moderate, or severe (40 images per category). These images were then uploaded to three generative AI models (ChatGPT-4o mini, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet) and prompted to identify the dental arch and to assess the severity of dental crowding. Response times were recorded, and outputs were compared to orthodontists’ assessments. A random image subset was re-analyzed after one week to evaluate model consistency.ResultsClaude 3.5 Sonnet successfully classified the severity of dental crowding in 50% of the images, followed by ChatGPT-4o mini (44%), and Copilot (34%). Visual recognition of the dental arches was higher with Claude and ChatGPT-4o mini (99%) compared to Copilot (72%). Response generation was significantly longer for ChatGPT-4o mini than for Claude and Copilot (p < .0001). However, the response times were comparable for both Claude and Copilot (p = .98). Repeated analyses showed improvement in image classification for both ChatGPT-4o mini and Copilot, while Claude 3.5 Sonnet misclassified a significant portion of the images.ConclusionsThe performance of ChatGPT-4o mini-, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in analyzing the severity of dental crowding often did not match the evaluations made by orthodontists. Further developments in image processing algorithms of commercially available generative AI models are required prior to reliable use for dental crowding classification.Keywords: ChatGPT, Microsoft, Claude, AI, Large language models

Background

Quantifying dental crowding influences the orthodontic treatment planning process [1, 2]. Knowledge of the exact amount of crowding brings consistency to decision-making and is invaluable in borderline cases [1]. While traditional methods of assessing dental crowding are useful, they often rely on subjective evaluations and manual measurements, which require time [3]. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) offer opportunities to overcome these limitations. Although many AI applications have focused on diagnostic dental radiology [4, 5], the potential of AI-driven models to analyze dental datasets can be extended to other areas, including orthodontic diagnosis.

Recent reviews have identified AI as the most extensively researched digital technology in orthodontics [6], reflecting the growing integration of machine learning into clinical workflows. Beyond tooth alignment, AI has been applied to facial esthetics; for example, AI-powered face enhancement technologies have shown potential for improving face-driven orthodontic treatment planning by refining soft-tissue visualization and enhancing patient communication [7]. Such advances highlight the role AI plays in orthodontics, which expands to include holistic, patient-centered applications.

Machine learning techniques, particularly deep learning (DL), when applied to vast amounts of textual data, provide AI models with advanced linguistic capabilities [8]. The integration of DL and natural language processing (NLP) enables large-language models (LLMs) to perform complex tasks such as text generation, translation, and question answering. LLMs learn to understand context, decode user intent, and generate contextually appropriate responses in real-time. In healthcare, LLMs have generated significant interest because of their potential to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of clinical, educational, and research work [7]. Since the introduction of LLMs, a range of healthcare applications have emerged. These include assisting with diagnosis [9], structuring data from health records [10–12], answering questions [13], improving crisis response times [14], summarizing evidence [15, 16], facilitating research [17], and data extraction [18]. Studies on LLMs in healthcare have reported mixed results [19], with some instances of LLMs being superior to clinicians [9].

Recent research has highlighted the promising utility of LLMs in dentistry. Chau et al. [20] demonstrated the performance of generative AI in dental licensing exams, showing encouraging results in knowledge-based evaluations. Similarly, Rokhshad et al. [21] found that chatbots performed comparably to clinicians in answering pediatric dentistry questions, emphasizing both accuracy and consistency. Importantly, these studies focused exclusively on text-based applications, while the potential of LLMs for visual diagnostic tasks (such as assessing dental crowding) remains largely unexplored. LLMs have rapidly moved on from being restricted to merely answering text-based questions and serving as conversational agents. Currently, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are capable of processing different data forms. Presently, ChatGPT 4o mini, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet are among the most popular chatbots [22]. As of December 2024, ChatGPT powered by OpenAI Inc., has surpassed 300 million weekly active users [23]. GPT models are built on transformer architectures and have evolved through multiple versions. Each iteration typically improves the performance and capabilities of the previous one [24]. GPT-4o mini is a cost-efficient, smaller AI model designed to be more affordable and less energy-intensive, offering a balance between performance and resource efficiency [25]. On the other hand, Microsoft Copilot bases its search and language understanding on a proprietary technology called Prometheus, which blends functionality from OpenAI’s latest ChatGPT while utilizing the expertise of search data from Bing [26]. Anthropic’s Claude 3 models have sophisticated vision capabilities and are also based on the transformer architecture of neural network [27]. However, unlike ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot, Claude applies the principles of Constitutional AI to govern its behavior [28].

Visual question answering (VQA) is an image-based task that involves responding to open-ended questions [29]. With VQA, the input is based on an image and question, and the output is shown in natural language. LLMs have demonstrated capabilities in medical-domain VQA tasks, showing their ability to effectively integrate visual and textual information [30]. In principle, LLMs can convert encoded image representations to word embedding for clinical questions, which would facilitate diagnostic report generation [31].

Fully automated LLMs can significantly reduce the processing labor and have useful applications in orthodontic diagnosis. Despite these advances, the application of multimodal LLMs for visual diagnostic tasks in orthodontics, such as crowding assessment from intraoral images, remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the diagnostic accuracy of three different LLMs for the accurate classification of the dental arch and severity of dental crowding based on intra-oral occlusal image analysis.

Materials and methods

This comparative study aimed to compare the classification accuracy in assessing the severity of dental crowding between three generative LLMs: ChatGPT-4o mini (OpenAI; San Francisco, California, United States), Microsoft Copilot (Microsoft; Redmond, Washington, United States), and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic; San Francisco, California, United States). This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee (SREC) of the University of Benghazi (approval number 0228/2024). The workflow of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

An overview of the workflow

This study utilized intraoral occlusal images made available from an orthodontist’s practice (M.B.) after obtaining appropriate consents. Two experienced orthodontists (K.W. and H.M.) independently classified the severity of dental crowding into three categories: mild, moderate, and severe. If a disagreement occurred, the image in question was excluded and replaced with a subsequent one until 120 images representing balanced groups of 40 mild, moderate, and severe dental crowding were reached. This approach ensured equal group sizes for descriptive analysis and data visualization, although it does not reflect the natural prevalence of different crowding severities in the population. To be eligible for inclusion, the photographs had to exhibit high image quality with clear occlusal details of the teeth up to the first molar area, proper lighting, and minimal interference from adjacent structures, such as the cheeks and tongue. All occlusal images were captured with a digital single-lens reflex (DSLR) camera with a 100-mm macro lens and twin flash. Prior to the analysis, a pilot test was performed using spare images and various prompt formulations to optimize the instructions given to the LLMs. During pilot testing, ten additional intraoral images (excluded from the final dataset) were used to identify a prompt that consistently elicited interpretable responses across all three models. After iterative testing and panel consensus, the final standardized prompt was: “Classify the dental crowding as mild, moderate, or severe, and indicate whether the image is a maxillary or mandibular occlusal image.” This prompt was uniformly applied to all models to ensure comparability. Subsequently, the image order was randomized using a random number generator (https://www.random.org) to prevent any potential bias arising from image sequencing, which may have led the AI models to recognize patterns in consecutive images. Randomization ensured that the AI models did not inadvertently classify multiple images into the same category based on their order.

The images were then uploaded to the following AI models: ChatGPT-4o mini, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, between October 11 and 17, 2024. These AI models were selected for this study because they are among the most widely available and frequently used general-purpose LLMs at the time of data collection. Their accessibility and integration into real-world platforms made them particularly relevant for evaluating practical orthodontic applications. ChatGPT uses advanced deep learning to detect objects and recognize patterns in images. It supports a single image upload and may use embedded image-processing methods, including techniques such as segmentation and pre-trained vision-language models such as Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) for image classification (available at https://chatgpt.com). On the other hand, Microsoft Copilot, which is built on the Prometheus model, allows users to upload a single image per request and can integrate with ChatGPT. For image classification tasks, Microsoft’s model may use a combination of external libraries and frameworks, including computer vision libraries, such as OpenCV and TensorFlow (available at https://copilot.microsoft.com). Claude 3 models use pre-trained vision-language models for image classification and can accept up to five images in a single upload request (available at https://www.anthropic.com/claude).

All images were uploaded by a single investigator (M.B.) using the same laptop (HP EliteBook Folio G1 Notebook PC) and a fast-fiber internet connection. Each AI chatbot model was then prompted with the same command described earlier (Fig. 2). A stopwatch was used to record each model’s response time, and all AI model predictions were subsequently recorded on a spreadsheet along with the initial clinical diagnosis (i.e., orthodontists’ crowding assessment and occlusal arch). Thirty randomly chosen images representing all crowding categories were re-uploaded into the same LLMs one week later to check the output consistency, robustness of the initial findings, and AI hallucination effects. The three LLMs offer subscription-free options for using their chatbots; however, with limited daily quotes on the number of prompts and image uploads. Due to the large amount of image inputs in this study, the three AI models were utilized on a subscription basis to avoid daily quote restrictions imposed on image uploads and prompts.

Statistical analysis

Inter-rater agreement on crowding classification between the two orthodontists was calculated using Cohen’s kappa statistic. For confusion matrices, data were fed into MATLAB 2024b (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, United States), and built-in functions were used. A confusion matrix is a table comparing the predicted class to the true class, where the diagonal elements show the correct predictions. The true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative values were subsequently calculated from the confusion matrix. True positives (TP) are cases in which the prediction matches the true class for the group being studied. True negatives (TN) are the same, but for the group not under consideration. False positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) are cases in which the predictions do not match the true class for the groups under study and not under study, respectively. Next, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated. The sensitivity or true positive rate quantifies the extent to which LLMs identify true positives. The formula was [TP/(TP + FN)] × 100. Specificity, or the true negative rate, quantifies how well LLMs identify true negatives, and the formula is [TN/(TN + FP)] × 100. Accuracy is a combined measure of sensitivity and specificity, and is calculated using the formula [TP + TN/(TP + TN + FP + FN)] × 100 [32]. For the repeatability analysis, a sample size of 30 images in total was chosen and images were then randomly selected from each group to reassess the LLM responses after a one-week interval. The intra-model consistency was gauged using Cohen’s kappa statistic. For response time comparisons, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using GraphPad Prism, and a scatter plot was created using Microsoft Excel.

Results

The agreement between the two orthodontists was substantial, with a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of.87, indicating strong inter-rater reliability for crowding classification.

To further understand the performance of the LLMs, confusion matrices of the predictions of ChatGPT-4o mini, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in classifying the severity of dental crowding using the original dataset were generated (Fig. 3). The confusion matrix showed that 27 out of 40 mild crowding images were correctly classified by ChatGPT-4o mini as mild, while 12 were incorrectly identified as moderate and 1 as severe. The ChatGPT-4o mini chatbot model misidentified 19 moderately crowded images as mild and 5 as severe. It correctly identified 16 moderate-crowding images. Regarding severe crowding classification, ChatGPT-4o mini could only identify 10 images correctly, whereas it misidentified 14 as mild and 16 as moderate. Microsoft Copilot accurately classified 9, 26, and 6 images as having mild, moderate, and severe dental crowding, respectively. However, it frequently misidentified mild crowding, labeling it as moderate 24 times and as severe 7 times. In addition, it labeled 28 severely crowded images as moderate, and another 6 as mild. Claude 3.5 Sonnet showed balanced predictions by correctly identifying 22, 18, and 20 images as mild, moderate, and severe, respectively.

Confusion charts showing the predictive values of GPT-4o mini, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in classifying the severity of dental crowding

For mild crowding classification, ChatGPT-4o mini had the highest sensitivity, but lower specificity. Microsoft Copilot had high specificity, but very low sensitivity. Lastly, Claude 3.5 Sonnet had higher specificity compared to ChatGPT-4o mini and higher sensitivity than Copilot. Regarding moderate crowding classification, ChatGPT-4o mini and Claude 3.5 Sonnet had low sensitivity and high specificity, whereas the reverse was true for Microsoft Copilot. For the severe crowding classification, all three LLMs displayed high specificity but low sensitivity values (Table 1).

Both ChatGPT-4o mini and Claude 3.5 Sonnet were successful in the VQA relating to the identification of the maxillary and mandibular arch images, only misidentifying one image with an overall accuracy of 99%. In contrast, Microsoft Copilot achieved an overall accuracy of 72%. The time taken by ChatGPT-4o mini (11.9 ± 4.4 s) for image analysis and response generation was significantly longer than for Microsoft Copilot (4.0 ± 2.5 s) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (3.9 ± 2.3 s) (p <.0001). However, response time generation was comparable for both the Microsoft Copilot and Claude 3.5 Sonnet models (p =.98) (Fig. 4).

Scatter plot for response time generation

LLM repeatability analysis showed that ChatGPT-4o mini had the best consistency (87%, Cohen’s kappa 0.74), followed by Microsoft Copilot (73%, Cohen’s kappa 0.41), and lastly Claude 3.5 Sonnet (43%, Cohen’s kappa 0.1), for testing consistency with dental crowding images. For arch identification, ChatGPT-4o mini and Claude 3.5 Sonnet had high consistency (97%, Cohen’s kappa 0.93), whereas Microsoft Copilot changed a considerable portion of the original image categorization, repeating the same answers in 73% of the selected images (Cohen’s kappa 0.53) (Table 2).

Discussion

The performance of three commercially available LLMs for the VQA tasks of dental arch identification and categorization of dental crowding was evaluated in this study. The intraoral occlusal image-based VQA capabilities of all the three LLMs were suboptimal. Crowding in this study was visually categorized. The goal was to test LLMs’ ability to classify dental crowding using intra oral images and therefore intraoral photographs were the most relevant input. The simplified visual inspection method allowed efficient categorization suitable for real-world scenarios such as teleconsultations, where detailed digital models are often unavailable. In such cases, a basic assessment of dental crowding can help orthodontists provide preliminary advice and determine whether an in-person consultation is needed. This approach also reflects common clinical practice, especially in resource-limited or tele-orthodontic settings. Wallis et al. [2] found that direct visualization was the preferred method of crowding estimation among a group of 62 orthodontists. To ensure study reliability, all images were reviewed by experienced orthodontists. Since the focus was on classifying crowding as mild, moderate, or severe, a visual threshold-based assessment was considered both appropriate and sufficient.

In dentistry, LLMs have been studied for accuracy, clarity, relevance, and up-to-date knowledge [12, 33, 34] and they occasionally produce scientifically inaccurate responses [35]. The dental community adopts a balanced approach by considering the potential benefits [36], while also carefully considering the limitations and potential risks of using LLMs [37–39]. All of the aforementioned LLM-based studies in dentistry are limited to text-only prompts. Few studies have investigated the performance of AI models on images of dental relevance. For instance, excellent pixel-wise precision for the detection and localization of caries and hypomineralized enamel has been recently reported [40]. Successful classification with high accuracy and speed for orthodontic images [41, 42] and superior performance in detecting malocclusion class [43] have also recently been reported. However, LLM-based approaches have not yet been explored for arch identification and the classification of dental crowding from orthodontic occlusal images [44].

In this study, three LLMs were tasked with distinguishing between maxillary and mandibular occlusal images. Both Claude 3.5 Sonnet and ChatGPT-4o mini models achieved high accuracy, each misclassifying only one image and displaying strong repeatability across the thirty re-uploaded images. In contrast, Microsoft’s model originally misclassified 24 images and had a lower repeatability (77%). However, these lower repeatability scores may be explained by Microsoft’s model attempting to correct the original arch identification errors.

For the more complex task of categorizing crowding, the patterns varied across the three LLMs. ChatGPT-4o mini identified many mild crowding images, although this was observed to be at the cost of specificity. On the other hand, Microsoft Copilot had high specificity but its sensitivity for mild crowding images was very low. Microsoft Copilot often classified crowding as moderate, resulting in several false positives. For moderate crowding, ChatGPT-4o mini and Claude 3.5 Sonnet favoured conservative predictions with high specificity and low sensitivity, while Microsoft Copilot demonstrated the opposite, favouring sensitivity, again with more false positives. In severe crowding cases, all LLMs displayed high specificity but low sensitivity, suggesting a cautious approach to classification. The consistency of the LLM responses for dental crowding was also evaluated after a one-week interval using a randomly selected number (N = 30) of images.

Although Claude 3.5 Sonnet initially displayed balanced classification across crowding types, it had the lowest repeatability (< 50%), often misclassifying images on the second attempt. ChatGPT-4o mini (87%) and Microsoft Copilot (73%) demonstrated superior performance in this regard. These findings warrant further refinement of LLMs to improve the categorization of dental crowding. The clinical implications of these findings are notable. The generally low sensitivity for moderate and severe crowding could lead to under-diagnosis, delayed intervention, or suboptimal treatment planning. While LLMs might assist in low-risk scenarios or support early triaging in high-volume or underserved settings, their current diagnostic performance is insufficient to support autonomous decision-making in orthodontics. Until performance improves, LLMs should be regarded as supportive tools—not substitutes for clinical judgment.

Overall, the findings pertinent to the performance of the three LLMs were unanticipated. Tasks that predominantly rely on linguistic input are generally more manageable for LLMs than those that require visual interpretation and can potentially explain the observed underperformance [45–47]. Alterations in the performance of LLMs have been reported when text-only scenarios were supplemented with the presentation of images, demonstrating a certain degree of visual data integration into processing [47]. The integration of NLP and computer vision facilitates simultaneous multimodal processing and interpretation of textual and visual information [48]. leading to image-text multimodal models gaining significant attention [49–51]. This capability enables LLMs equipped with a cross-modal encoder to manage multi-source data and conduct advanced reasoning, thereby facilitating complex diagnostic operations.

To answer image-based diagnostic questions, differences in the accuracy and responsiveness of LLMs have been reported in recent Literature. For instance, the Claude 3 family demonstrated high accuracy, even surpassing average human accuracy, but ChatGPT-4o mini showed selectivity, responding more to easier and longer questions with smaller images [52]. Another study found that despite a high accuracy (90%) for text-only multiple-choice questions, Claude 3.5 Sonnet was less successful (accuracy of only 59%) with additional image input, and a version of ChatGPT-4 (ChatGPT-4 V) also performed poorly in interpreting ultrasound images [53]. However, ChatGPT-4o demonstrated the best overall performance in a study that evaluated the ability of eight LLMs to understand graphs [45]. The performance of Microsoft Copilot in image identification tasks has not been extensively studied. In a study on spatial tasks, which included some visual elements, Copilot performed well on tasks related to spatial literacy but showed weaknesses in spatial reasoning [54].

Another significant operational factor is the processing time. ChatGPT-4o mini required substantially more time to analyze the images compared to Copilot and Claude 3.5 Sonnet. An extended processing duration may indicate a more comprehensive or complex analytical process but could lead to potential limitations in real-time applications where speed is crucial. In this study, extended processing times did not correspond with improved diagnostic accuracy, although ChatGPT-4o mini demonstrated better repeatability than the others. Conversely, Microsoft Copilot and Claude 3.5 Sonnet responded faster but without consistent accuracy advantages. Future LLM development should strive to balance speed and diagnostic performance to meet practical clinical demands.

Although most modern LLMs are built on the transformer architecture, their deep learning performance is not solely dependent on architecture but is also influenced by parameter count, training data (quality and quantity), and the specific task at hand [55]. Depending on how well training aligns with the tasks that the LLMs are tested with, disparities in both processing speed and accuracy of responses are likely. For instance, in this study, it is possible that the model trained on a larger share of dental domain-specific images and text datasets demonstrated better accuracy and shorter response times. However, domain-focused training can lead to specialization at the expense of generalizability [8]. Thus, the quality and relevance of the training data are critical; models trained on broad, general datasets may lack sufficient exposure to dental-specific terminology and images, limiting their diagnostic utility.

Interestingly, periodic updates to LLMs are the norm, during which new features or enhancements are introduced [56]. Updates can include fine-tuning based on recent data, adjustments to model architectures, or addition of new training techniques, all of which can affect the accuracy and response time [57, 58]. The findings from this study emphasize the importance of continued AI model refinement and validation to enhance the accuracy and efficiency for facilitating orthodontic diagnostics, particularly when precise differentiation significantly influences clinical decision-making and outcomes.

Beyond diagnostic capabilities, LLMs show promise for augmenting orthodontic workflows by automating non-critical tasks—such as case triaging, preliminary assessments, drafting treatment summaries, and patient education [59, 60]. These roles can reduce clinician burden and improve operational efficiency, especially in high-volume or resource-limited environments. However, our findings also highlight current limitations in the visual interpretation capacities of LLMs, suggesting they are not yet ready to independently support diagnostic decisions. Enhancing their visual performance remains a necessary step toward broader clinical integration.

LLMs have progressed through four generations in terms of their task-solving capacity [61]. While they generally demonstrate impressive performance in purely text-based tasks, this study reinforces the conclusion that visual interpretation remains a challenging domain, with LLM performance varying across different types of VQA tasks. The performance of LLMs for VQA tasks also relies on image characteristics and the complexity of statistical relationships between words to predict subsequent text, based on given prompts. This dependency is likely to increase the variability in their outputs, potentially leading to inconsistencies in the responses [51]. The optimization of the unseen VQA prompt may result in an accurate and meaningful output. Therefore, future VQA diagnostic studies could also focus on evaluating LLM differences in accuracy and responsiveness based on prompting strategies as well as image size and quality.

In addition to the existing accuracy, efficiency, and AI hallucination challenges noted in this study, LLMs may also show susceptibility to biases [62], vulnerability to targeted misinformation attacks [63], or knowledge recency issues [64]. As the visual processing capabilities of LLMs continue to improve, future research should focus on utilizing intraoral images with assigned labels for crowding classes to train a DL model to address the limitations of the current study and facilitate the development of reliable dental domain-specific LLMs [38]. Additionally, applying bioethical frameworks for responsible development prior to clinical use of LLMs is important [65]. Recent debates in the dental literature have raised concerns about patient privacy, data security, informed consent, and algorithmic fairness. A responsible, transparent, and ethically sound approach to AI deployment will be essential to ensure equitable and effective integration of LLMs into orthodontic care.

This study has several limitations. First, the dataset consisted of 120 intraoral images from a single clinical source, which may limit generalizability. Second, the visual categorization of dental crowding into three balanced groups may not represent the actual spectrum of malocclusion. Third, only three widely available LLMs were evaluated. Other AI models, including domain-specific systems, may demonstrate higher accuracy in dental applications. Finally, the analysis relied on 2D images rather than 3D digital models, restricting clinical applicability in settings where comprehensive records are available. The comparison between AI models and orthodontists should therefore be interpreted with caution, as the limited dataset and simplified methodology may not fully reflect clinical decision-making and could underestimate the true capabilities of both human experts and AI systems.

Future research should address these limitations by employing larger, multicenter datasets and incorporating 3D digital models to enhance clinical validity. Evaluating a broader range of AI models, including dental-specific systems, would provide a more comprehensive assessment. Additionally, exploring hybrid workflows in which orthodontists are assisted by AI may better represent real-world applications and optimize patient care.

Conclusions

The ChatGPT-4o mini, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet LLMs showed limited and inconsistent accuracy in classifying dental crowding from intraoral occlusal images. While the models performed somewhat better in distinguishing maxillary from mandibular arches, their ability to grade the severity of dental crowding (mild, moderate, severe) often misaligned with experienced orthodontists’ judgement. Therefore, although LLMs demonstrated some potential, these direct comparisons with orthodontists’ classifications should be regarded as preliminary and interpreted within the methodological constraints of this study.
