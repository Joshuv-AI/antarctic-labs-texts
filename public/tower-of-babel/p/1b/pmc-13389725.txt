Challenges in the distribution of community pharmacists in depopulated areas in Japan
Authors: Kazuhiro Iguchi, Kazuya Nonomura, Nana Takahashi, Hirofumi Tamaki, Arihiro Osanai, Kenichiro Ishii, Mitsuhiro Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Inadequate provision of medical resources in depopulated areas is a challenge in many countries, and Japan is currently experiencing an aging society with a declining birth rate. In other countries, the shortage of medical resources, including community pharmacists, in depopulated areas has emerged as a salient issue, necessitating the development of effective solutions. With the emphasis on the separation between prescription and dispensation, the number of community pharmacists in Japan has increased over time. This study examined whether the increase in the number of community pharmacists over time has contributed to improving the distribution of pharmacists in depopulated areas.Methods: Data from 2012 to 2022 were analyzed. The distribution of community pharmacists was assessed using Gini coefficients.Results: In 2022, the number of community pharmacists had increased by 37,723, or 124.7%, from the corresponding number in 2012. The difference in the number of community pharmacists per population between depopulated and non-depopulated areas was large (the number in depopulated areas was approximately 60% of that in non-depopulated areas), and this difference remained similar over time. The increase in the number of community pharmacists from 2012 to 2022 resulted in a more equal distribution relative to the population. However, the Gini coefficient in municipalities classified as depopulated areas was significantly higher than that in municipalities classified as non-depopulated areas, and this situation did not change despite the increase in the number of community pharmacists.Conclusion: Although the number of community pharmacists increased, it had only a limited impact on correcting the imbalance between depopulated and non-depopulated areas, leaving the problem unresolved in depopulated areas. We hope that these results will facilitate efforts to optimize medical resources in depopulated areas.

Introduction

Japan has the world’s highest aging rate. As of 2023, 29.1% of the population in Japan was over the age of 65 years, and the birth rate is also in decline1, 2). This phenomenon has had a substantial impact on the healthcare field, with an increasing demand for medical and long-term care services owing to the aging population and a decrease in the working population, potentially resulting in an imbalance between supply and demand for healthcare resources. Several policies have been proposed to address these concerns. These include extending healthy life expectancies, promoting employment among the elderly, improving the service framework for medical and elderly care, and establishing a comprehensive community care system3,4,5).

Securing a regional healthcare infrastructure in depopulated areas is challenging. In Japan, the term “depopulated areas” is defined under the “The Act on Special Measures for the Promotion of Sustainable Development in Depopulated Areas”. These areas are characterized by a substantial decline in population, resulting in low levels of social vitality or production activities in comparison with those in other regions. Municipalities are designated as depopulated areas in accordance with specific criteria such as the rate of population decline and the financial strength index. The depopulated areas are further classified into three categories: “totally depopulated”, “deemed depopulated”, and “partially depopulated”6, 7). The shortage of physicians in rural areas is a prominent concern, and various measures have been implemented to address this issue8). However, government support for addressing the shortage of community pharmacists has been insufficient, resulting in challenges in recruiting and retaining human resources and the stable management of pharmacy businesses in rural areas. Such regional disparities in medical services are of particular concern because Japan has a universal national health insurance system.

This challenge is also mirrored globally. Rural populations worldwide face enduring healthcare inequities stemming from geographic isolation, socioeconomic disadvantages, and weak health systems9, 10). A critical manifestation of this issue is the severe maldistribution of the health workforce. While nearly half of the world’s population resides in rural areas, they are served by less than a quarter of the world’s physicians and nurses. The universal challenge of attracting and retaining healthcare professionals in rural and remote areas affects both high- and low-income countries alike10, 11). Recognizing this, the World Health Organization has published guidelines emphasizing the need for comprehensive national strategies to address this shortage10). In many countries, the lack of medical resources, including community pharmacists, in depopulated areas has emerged as a salient issue, necessitating the development of effective solutions12,13,14).

Since the 1990s, Japan has actively promoted the separation of dispensing and prescribing drugs to control medical costs and improve healthcare quality15). This has led to a rapid increase in the demand for community pharmacists. The number of pharmacies handling prescriptions has steadily increased since the 1990s, and as of 2023, 80.3% of outpatient prescriptions are being dispensed by community pharmacies16). To meet this increasing demand, major reforms in pharmacy education were implemented in 2006. The traditional four-year pharmacy school curriculum was replaced by a six-year program that places a stronger emphasis on clinical training17). This shift highlights the importance of pharmacists not only as dispensers of medication, but also as active participants in patient care as members of the healthcare team. Many new pharmacy schools have also been created in response to the shortage of pharmacists in rural areas and the growing healthcare needs of the aging population15). In community healthcare settings, the pharmacist’s role has expanded beyond dispensing medications. Pharmacists are now expected to contribute to a broader range of activities, such as providing home healthcare services and supporting comprehensive community care systems17, 18).

To better understand these developments, evaluation of regional disparities in healthcare resources is essential19,20,21,22,23,24). The Gini coefficient, which was initially developed to measure income inequality, has also been extensively applied to evaluate the equilibrium of healthcare resource distribution at the regional level25, 26). A previous study utilizing the Gini coefficient as an evaluation indicator demonstrated that an increase in the number of community pharmacists in Japan led to a reduction in the intermunicipal disparity in the number of community pharmacists per population19). A series of studies revealed that the number of community pharmacists in depopulated areas is significantly lower than that in non-depopulated areas and that the disparity in the number of community pharmacists in municipalities classified as depopulated areas is substantial, although this disparity is not as pronounced when evaluated across all municipalities23). This substantial disparity in distribution should be considered when formulating policies for depopulated areas. However, the status and extent of improvement in this disparity over time are not yet fully understood.

This study evaluated the number of community pharmacists from 2012 to 2022, with the aim of identifying the changing number of community pharmacists in depopulated areas. Our findings revealed that while the pharmacist workforce has grown, its impact on correcting regional imbalances remains limited.

Methods

Dataset

The number of community pharmacists and physicians per year in each municipality (city/ward/town/village) was obtained from “Survey of Physicians, Dentists and Pharmacists” (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare). The data for each year were compiled on the basis of the 2022 municipal boundaries, with the number of municipalities fixed at 1,741. Data for each municipality’s population and number of general government employees were obtained from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications website. Data were downloaded from the Portal Site of the Official Statistics of Japan (e-Stat) of the National Statistics Center in December 2024. The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications designated 713 “completely depopulated” municipalities, 14 “deemed depopulated” municipalities, and 158 “partially depopulated” municipalities as of 20227). The term “deemed depopulated” was used for areas that had been less affected by depopulation than those that had been completely depopulated. In this study, “depopulated areas” refers to the 713 municipalities that had been entirely depopulated, while “non-depopulated areas” encompassed the 856 municipalities that have not been designated as any kind of depopulated area. For the analysis involving population density, the 1,741 municipalities were divided into quartiles based on their 2022 population per inhabitable land area: “lowest” (n=435), “low” (n=435), “high” (n=435), and “highest” (n=436).

Gini coefficient

We used the Gini coefficient as an indicator of equity among municipalities, as described previously19, 22, 23). The Gini coefficient reflects the degree of inequality in the distribution across municipalities, allowing us to capture disparities that cannot be identified using mean values alone. The coefficient for the number of community pharmacists was calculated on the basis of the population size. The Gini coefficient for the number of community pharmacists per population was obtained by plotting the Lorenz curve of the cumulative relative frequency of the population versus the cumulative relative frequency of the number of community pharmacists arranged in increasing order of the number of community pharmacists per 100,000 inhabitants. The coefficient varies between zero (complete equality) and one (complete inequality).

Discussion

Over the past decade, the number of community pharmacists per population in Japan increased by approximately 28%. At the national level, the distribution of community pharmacists was relatively balanced by the population. Furthermore, over time, a trend toward greater equalization was observed. However, a comparison between depopulated and non-depopulated areas revealed that the disparity in the number of community pharmacists per population has persisted. Importantly, the apparent increase in the pharmacist-to-population ratios in depopulated areas was mainly due to shrinking populations rather than successful recruitment of pharmacists. Moreover, a substantial disparity was observed in the distribution of community pharmacists among municipalities in depopulated areas. This disparity did not significantly improve over time. The small number of community pharmacists may impede residents’ access to medicines. Although the medical facilities in these areas partially compensated for the lack of community pharmacies by dispensing prescriptions in-house, community pharmacies serve broader functions beyond supplying medication. Considering Japan’s rapidly aging population, home healthcare services are expected to play a critical role in the future. Therefore, the provision of pharmacy services to residents of depopulated areas in Japan must be closely monitored.

In municipalities characterized as depopulated areas, the number of community pharmacists increased by only 8.0% in comparison with that in 2012. While the number of pharmacists per population increased by 27% from 2012, this was attributable to a 15.8% decrease in the population of depopulated areas. Consequently, the increase in the number of community pharmacists per population in depopulated areas was almost entirely attributable to population decline, rather than an actual increase in the recruitment or redistribution of pharmacists. This indicates that the distribution problem has not yet been resolved. The number of community pharmacists per capita in depopulated areas was approximately 0.6 times in non-depopulated areas, and this disparity has remained largely unchanged over the past decade.

The shortage of healthcare professionals in depopulated areas is a challenge observed internationally, and various measures have been implemented to address it27,28,29). In the United States, programs that provide scholarships and student loan repayment assistance to healthcare professionals are offered to address workforce shortages in depopulated areas27). In Australia, a government-funded private service called the Rural Locum Assistance Program (LAP) offers substitute support services to healthcare workers in rural areas during their leave28). In Japan, special provisions for dispensing-fee regulations have been introduced to support pharmacies in areas with limited healthcare resources. For example, pharmacies located in such regions are eligible for the highest reimbursement category for basic dispensing fees, regardless of the number of prescriptions or pharmacy size30). Additionally, telemedicine is regarded as a potential solution to issues such as healthcare workforce shortages and regional disparities in depopulated areas. Recently, Japan introduced an electronic prescription system that has strengthened the telemedicine infrastructure, including remote consultations and medication guidance31, 32). In addition, some pharmacy schools have introduced “regional quota” admission systems that require graduates to work in designated rural areas for a specified period. Although not widespread, this policy is gradually expanding. While its long-term effects remain unclear, this policy represents a unique attempt to mitigate pharmacist maldistribution. These measures could be adapted to address the shortage of healthcare professionals in depopulated areas of other countries.

The Gini coefficient for the number of community pharmacists per population in the municipalities classified as depopulated areas was substantial. This result is noteworthy because it is close to the Gini coefficient for physicians, which is frequently regarded as showing an inequitable distribution33, 34). Moreover, the Gini coefficient for the number of community pharmacists per municipal population showed a marked difference between depopulated and non-depopulated areas over a continuous 10-year period. Importantly, this difference in Gini coefficients between depopulated and non-depopulated areas was not observed when the Gini coefficients were calculated for physicians (Figure 1B), dentists, and general government employees (data not shown). This phenomenon was exclusive to community pharmacists. These differences in the Gini coefficients were also evident when the municipalities were categorized by population density. Notably, the improvement in distribution equality over time among low-density municipalities was much smaller than that observed among high-density municipalities.

The reason for the high Gini coefficient for the number of community pharmacists in depopulated areas remains unclear, but one possible explanation lies in the location of pharmacies and the separation of dispensing and prescribing35). Pharmacies in Japan are often located adjacent to medical facilities. Additionally, the medical facility decides whether to dispense outpatient prescriptions in-hospital or out-of-hospital. In particular, small medical facilities such as clinics occasionally dispense prescriptions in-house. Medical facilities are fewer in depopulated areas, and choosing one facility has a major influence on the relative number of community pharmacies in that area. This may explain the variability in the number of pharmacists per capita across these regions. Furthermore, approximately 10% of all municipalities (149 municipalities as of 2022) had no community pharmacists. Since these municipalities were almost exclusively located within depopulated areas, their absence likely contributed significantly to the high Gini coefficients observed in these regions.

Because the municipalities in this study were classified according to the Japanese definition of “depopulation”, this definition should be considered when extrapolating the results to other countries. In Japan, the designation of depopulated areas is determined by the population requirements and the financial strength index of the municipality. To verify the influence of this classification, we conducted a similar analysis by dividing the municipalities into four groups based on population density, and obtained consistent results. Specifically, the group with the highest population density had more community pharmacists per population than the group with the lowest density. Thus, we believe that the results of this study are robust because they agree with the results obtained when considering overpopulation and underpopulation evaluated in terms of population density.

Limitations

In this study, the Gini coefficient was based on data for the population and number of community pharmacists at the municipal level, making it an evaluation at the individual municipality level. However, residents often have access to medical facilities across municipal boundaries. This is particularly true for patients living near municipal borders, who often utilize medical facilities in neighboring municipalities. Consequently, even if a given municipality has limited medical resources, access to healthcare services in adjacent municipalities may mitigate potential issues. Thus, evaluations using the Gini coefficient may tend to overestimate disparities in comparison with evaluations that account for access to healthcare facilities. The Gini coefficient also does not capture actual travel distance, which may be particularly critical for mountainous, remote, and island areas. Another limitation is that the use of municipality-level Gini coefficients may obscure disparities within municipalities, hiding underserved communities within non-depopulated areas or relatively well-served communities in depopulated areas. Additionally, the data from the “Statistics on Physicians, Dentists, and Pharmacists” used here only provide information regarding the municipalities where community pharmacists are employed. This information cannot be directly linked to the addresses of the pharmacies where they work. To evaluate inequalities in access to healthcare services, alternative approaches such as those based on the addresses of the pharmacies are required. Furthermore, the number of community pharmacists per population does not necessarily reflect the actual fulfillment of medical demands; thus, caution is required when interpreting these results. While this study utilized population-based ratios to evaluate regional disparities, actual demand was influenced by various factors, such as population aging and the expanding roles of pharmacists in home medical care and community-based integrated care systems. Future research incorporating the “Pharmacist Maldistribution Indicator” and “Physician Maldistribution Indicator” published by the Japanese government36, 37) can be expected to provide deeper insights into the supply-demand balance in regional healthcare.

Conclusion

This study highlights the geographic inequality in rural areas. Instead of focusing solely on the overall adequacy of resources, national and local governments should recognize potential variations in healthcare resource availability within depopulated areas and provide tailored support systems to address the specific needs of each region.
