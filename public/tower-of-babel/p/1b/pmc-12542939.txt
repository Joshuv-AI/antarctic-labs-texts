An overhead-reduced, efficient, fully analog neural-network computing hardware
Authors: Jiabao Ye, Wannian Wang, Caiping Shi, Xuecheng Cui, Wanyuan Qu, Peng Lin, Xiao Yu, Ran Cheng, Hanming Wu, Bing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human brain–inspired neural-network computing hardware has the potential to overcome the energy efficiency and resource overhead challenges caused by the von Neumann bottleneck. However, existing neural-network computing hardware still exhibits a considerable gap compared to the human brain, likely due to the absence of complex data compression functions and the fully analog domain operating strategy adopted by the brain. To solve these problems, a fully analog neural-network computing hardware (FANCH) with input data compression is proposed and fabricated at both the chip and system levels in this work. FANCH implements the complete computational process of the neural networks using fully analog circuits. FANCH achieves an accuracy with only a 0.36% reduction from the software baseline in the handwritten digit recognition task. FANCH demonstrates advantages in energy efficiency compared to state-of-the-art artificial intelligence acceleration chips and systems. Our work provides an efficient fully analog hardware solution for edge computing applications with low hardware resource demands.

INTRODUCTION

Artificial intelligence (AI) has witnessed remarkable progress in recent years, demonstrating outstanding performance and playing an irreplaceable role in various domains (1–3). Presently, the development of large-scale AI tasks, such as large language model ChatGPT, has resulted in the neural networks (NNs) serving as computational cores with hundreds of billions of parameters (4, 5). This surge has pushed the global demands for computing power to 1023 floating-point operations per second (FLOPS) as estimated. This requires ~1011 W of power consumption, equivalent to the power generated by 10 of the world’s largest hydroelectric power stations, which is a substantial global energy demand (6–8).

In modern computing systems, the computational power is primarily relied on the central processing units (CPUs) and graphics processing units (GPUs) (9). CPUs mainly offer general-purpose computing capabilities with high clock frequencies and large cache capacities. However, they demonstrate relatively low efficiency when handling large-scale parallel AI computations due to the limitations of fewer cores, cache structures, and memory access patterns, which typically operate at ~1 to 10 giga-FLOPS per watt (GFLOPS/W). In contrast, GPUs perform much better in handling AI tasks through the more efficient parallel computing, achieving ~100 GFLOPS/W in energy efficiency, owing to the finer-grain cores, reduced instruction sets, and faster memory access (10). Unfortunately, both CPUs and GPUs are still based on the von Neumann architecture, hindering further improvement in energy efficiency due to the separation of computation and storage (fig. S1) (11).

For the human-brain neural systems, the estimated computational power is ~1016 analog operations per second (AOPS) while its power consumption is only about 20 W, showing an exceptional energy efficiency of several hundred tera-AOPS per watt (TAOPS/W) (12–14). Hence, to overcome the limitations of the current hardware computing paradigms, researchers have proposed the neural-network computing hardware inspired by the human brain structure and computing mechanisms (15–22). By performing vector-matrix multiplication (VMM) in NNs through synaptic arrays, brain-inspired neural-network computing hardware can achieve remarkable macro-energy efficiency at the level of TFLOPS/W, which is several orders of magnitude higher than GPUs (23, 24). Despite these advancements, the reported neural-network computing hardware still has a large gap in system energy efficiency compared to the human brain.

One possible issue is that, unlike the human brain, which has various data compression functions to reduce computational complexity (fig. S2) (25, 26), most of the existing brain-inspired neural-network computing hardware lacks data compression. Although there are effective methods at the software level to simplify neural-network parameters, such as pruning (27), parameter sharing (28), and local connectivity (29), few have been applied to the hardware. From a system perspective, implementing data compression at the hardware level can substantially reduce the computational complexity of AI tasks and minimize hardware overhead.

Another possible reason is that the human brain operates in the analog domain (fig. S2). For AI tasks, this fully analog computation is more efficient because it allows for continuous signal processing without quantization and discretization, enabling higher-density parallel computing (30–34). However, current brain-inspired neural-network computing hardware is implemented using digital schemes or mixed analog-digital schemes. As shown in Fig. 1A, in digital neural-network computing hardware (35–40), external analog signals are first converted to digital signals by analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), and then, all subsequent signal processing is completed in the digital domain, where the memory array uses digital cells, such as static random-access memory (SRAM) or flash memory, and the digital logic circuits typically use field programmable gate arrays or digital signal processors. For brain-inspired neural-network computing, the standard digital cells used for VMM are large in size and consume high power, resulting in considerable area consumption and low energy efficiency. To address these issues, mixed analog-digital neural-network computing hardware was implemented, which has both analog synaptic arrays and digital logic circuits. As shown in Fig. 1B, in the mixed analog-digital neural-network computing hardware (41–48), external analog signals are directly input into analog synaptic arrays, and after VMM, the results are converted into digital signals by ADCs and then processed and output by the digital logic circuits. This approach leverages the low power consumption and high-speed advantages of analog synapse arrays in neural-network computing. However, for parallel computing, the power consumption of ADCs and digital-to-analog converters (DACs) accounts for 85% of the total power consumption in hardware systems (49). This frequent data conversion and buffering have become the main bottleneck in improving the energy efficiency. Currently, there have been some reports on implementing analog neural-network hardware at the subsystem or submodule level, but there have been few demonstrations at the system and chip levels (50, 51).

Different types of brain-inspired neural-network computing hardware.

(A) Digital neural-network computing hardware, where the analog input signals are first converted into digital signals by ADCs and then fed into digital memory arrays for in-memory computation, and, last, the computed results are output through digital logic circuits. REG, register; MUX, multiplexer. (B) Mixed neural-network computing hardware, where the analog input signals are directly input into analog synaptic arrays for in-memory computation, the results of which are converted into digital signals by ADCs and then processed and output through digital logic circuits. (C) Fully analog neural-network computing hardware with input data compression mechanism; input data first undergo compression preprocessing and then directly enters the analog synapse array for in-memory computation, and the results are directly processed and output through analog processing units including TIAs, DFAs, FAFUs, CMPs, etc.

Therefore, in this work, we developed a fully analog neural-network computing hardware (FANCH) with reduced overhead at the system and chip levels, as shown in Fig. 1C. In our hardware, input signals first undergo compression preprocessing and then directly enter the analog synapse arrays for in-memory computation. The results are handled by analog processing units, including transimpedance amplifiers (TIAs), differential voltage amplifiers (DFAs), fully analog activation function units (FAFUs), and voltage comparators (CMPs), without any digital conversion or buffering. In addition, we proposed a software-hardware collaborative method to substantially enhance the generalization performance of FANCH for intelligent classification tasks. Hardware tests show that FANCH achieves the handwriting recognition task with extremely low power and hardware resource overhead, achieving an accuracy comparable to the software simulations, with only a 0.36% reduction compared to the baseline. Compared to brain-inspired neural-network hardware based on mixed analog-digital and digital domains, FANCH demonstrates remarkable advantages in energy efficiency by eliminating unnecessary ADC/DAC (A/D) data conversion and the corresponding peripheral circuits at intermediate nodes.

RESULTS

Compression of input data for NNs

The scale of the input information determines the hardware size of the entire neural-network computing system. Therefore, we have adopted a solution that directly compresses the input data, substantially reducing the scale of FANCH. This approach enables FANCH to perform efficient computation even under resource-constrained conditions. As shown in Fig. 2A, we compressed the original 28-by-28 images from the standard Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) handwritten digit dataset to seven-by-seven images and reduced the three-layer fully connected neural network (FCNN) from an initial scale of 784 by 100 by 10 to 49 by 10 by 10 (fig. S3). By adopting mature data compression algorithms for preprocessing, we effectively demonstrated the impact of reducing the scale of input data on the synapse count, computational complexity, and performance.

To maintain the highest possible accuracy in FANCH after input data compression, we investigated the recognition accuracy of the three-layer FCNN on the task of handwritten digit recognition after several different image compression methods. Figure 2B illustrates the image effect obtained through the local average filtering method. This method focuses on local areas within the dataset, dividing each 28-by-28 image into four-by-four subimages. The average pixel value of each subimage is used as the new pixel value. This technique achieves the effective image compression, providing good smoothing effects, noise reduction capabilities, and robustness to local anomalies. Figure 2C displays the image effect achieved through the pixel value quantization, where the pixel values in the image are uniformly quantized into 16 intervals, and each mapped to a new value to reduce pixel values. Figure 2D shows the image effect from the Gaussian pyramid method, which uses Gaussian filtering to compress image size while preserving key features. This method is suitable for handling various image scale changes and has good boundary handling capabilities. Figure 2E shows the image effect from the local median filtering method, similar to local average filtering, but uses the median of pixel values instead of their average, effectively suppressing noise while preserving edge information. Figure 2F displays the image effect from the bicubic interpolation method, considering the 16 nearest neighboring pixels and using these points to construct a cubic polynomial. This polynomial calculates new pixel values based on position and pixel values, ultimately through bidirectional interpolation to achieve the target pixel value, effectively maintaining image smoothness and edge information. Figure 2G shows the image effect from the subsampling method, which achieves image compression by interval sampling of the original image, using the interval-sampled values directly as the result of image compression.

Figure 2H displays the recognition accuracy of the three-layer FCNN after applying different image scaling compression methods. To evaluate the inference generalization performance of FANCH, we simulated the recognition accuracy of each compression method 1000 times and then selected the highest achievable recognition accuracy for each method. From the figure, the local average filtering method achieved the highest software benchmark accuracy of 88%. Therefore, in subsequent performance testing of FANCH, we adopted the local average filtering method to create a compressed MNIST dataset (fig. S4).

We additionally compared FANCH in terms of synapse count and computational complexity, as well as its recognition accuracy before and after the image compression. As shown in Fig. 2I, after the image compression, there was a slight decrease in the recognition accuracy of FANCH. However, this compression leads to the substantial reduction in hardware resource overhead and computational complexity, with the synapse count reduced by ~135 times and the computational complexity reduced by about 17 times, thereby improving the resource efficiency of the hardware.

Circuit implantation of FANCH

Implementing neural-network computing with fully analog hardware presents several challenges. First, the precision loss at each stage of the analog circuitry makes it difficult for the hardware system to achieve accuracy comparable to software benchmarks. Second, deviations of analog activation function circuits from ideal activation functions pose substantial obstacles in achieving low hardware resource overhead in the system.

Here, we validated the feasibility of fully analog neural-network computation at the system level using discrete components. We developed a fully integrated system-level physical printed circuit board (PCB), called FANCH_SL (fig. S5). FANCH_SL was used to demonstrate the handwritten digit recognition task (movie S1). Furthermore, we have implemented the computing hardware system at the chip level. To solve the challenges of fully analog computation, we designed the high-precision FAFU and other analog computation modules. We performed comprehensive simulation analysis of device mismatch and variations, noise sensitivity, process, voltage, and temperature to ensure that the precision loss at each computation stage was minimized to the lowest level.

Figure 3B shows the circuit-level schematic of FANCH. FANCH uses four independent crossbar arrays: two (each with 49 columns and 10 rows) for the +/− weight mapping between the input layer and the hidden layer, while the other two (each with 10 columns and 10 rows) for the +/− weight mapping between the hidden layer and the output layer (fig. S6). To leverage the parallel computing advantage, these crossbar arrays are directly accessed. Here, our weight array cells can be replaced with nonvolatile devices, such as memristors and the phase-change memory. During the computation process between the input and the hidden layers, the input voltage vectors perform VMM with the input–hidden weight array. The accumulated current at the end of each row is converted to be the voltage change and amplified by the designed TIA circuit for the subsequent processing. The factor is determined based on the output current of the preceding array and the characteristics of the subsequent circuit modules. Following the TIA circuit, we designed DFAs to subtract the output voltages of the two resistor arrays by configuring the two-stage amplifier in a subtractor feedback configuration. This differential amplifier is crucial for enhancing the robustness of the analog circuit, which is essential for the analog computation. Next, we implement the sigmoid activation function using a differential amplifier circuit. Accurate approximation is achieved through the nonlinear conversion of the differential input voltage to the diode-connected load current, with both input pair transistors biased in the saturation region. The sensitivity is determined by the direct current (dc) bias current and the device characteristics of the input pair. The load current is then replicated using a current mirror, and the output current-to-voltage conversion is performed by two cascaded diode-connected P-channel transistors. Last, the output of the FAFU is transmitted through a buffer to the resistance arrays of the second layer. Based on this circuit implementation, the maximum error between the FAFU and the ideal sigmoid curve is only 0.93% (fig. S7). Process variations may cause the voltage range to change, but the designed FAFU still exhibits superior stability under power voltage fluctuations, with a maximum error of only 2.7 mV within a power voltage fluctuation of 10% (fig. S8A). Moreover, under extreme temperature and process corners, the output curve of the designed FAFU shows slight shift, still effectively performing nonlinear activation (fig. S8B). This design allows FANCH to perform high-precision data processing at intermediate nodes without relying on ADCs or any digital logic circuits. In addition, the FAFU is implemented with only nine components, ensuring low hardware overhead for FANCH in parallel to analog computation. During the computation between the hidden layer and output layer, the results from the input and hidden layers, after being activated by FAFUs, serve as inputs to the hidden-output weight array. The subsequent signal processing path is similar to the previous layer. After the second-layer FAFU activation, we also designed a maximum voltage comparison circuit (MVCMP) to compare the 10 output voltage signals from FANCH (fig. S9). MVCMP outputs the path label with the highest voltage signal, representing the final recognition result of FANCH, completing the handwritten digit recognition task.

Traditional analog computing is sensitive to the noise and device variations. Here, we conduct a simulation-based analysis of the impact of these factors on the performance of FANCH. After adding the Gaussian noise (with a standard deviation of 1%) to FANCH, the accuracy is only decreased by 0.33%. After adding the salt-and-pepper noise (with a noise density of 1%), the accuracy is decreased by 1.47%. In the device-to-device variation simulation, the accuracy of FANCH is only decreased by 0.69% after applying a 10% variation (fig. S10). In the device failure simulation, we investigate the inference accuracy of FANCH under different device failure rates (1 to 5%) and compare it with that of the conventional system using A/D conversion (simulated with 8-bit ADCs). Here, the failure state is defined as a deviation of more than 50% from the target conductance in synaptic cells, to simulate severe variations in certain devices within the resistor array. The results show that FANCH consistently exhibits lower accuracy degradation across all device failure rates (fig. S11). These results demonstrate that FANCH exhibits remarkable robustness under various influences. This is mainly attributed to the high-precision circuit modules designed within the fully analog computation process and the accurate mapping from NN algorithms to hardware implementation. These designs play a crucial role in ensuring the stability of performance and minimizing the impact of external factors.

Figure 3D presents the die photo of FANCH, where the compact layout and the high-density integration of the core circuits result in a chip area of only 1.7 mm2. This supports the application of FANCH in edge-side AI tasks. Figure 3E illustrates the fabricated fully integrated physical PCB for testing. This PCB provides stable power supply to FANCH and enables the communication with external subsystems, which is essential for realizing the comprehensive performance testing (fig. Software-hardware collaborative training method for FANCH

To approximate the actual characteristics of the hardware circuit to the software results of NN inference, reduce the impact of nonideal factors in the circuit, and minimize the precision loss during the software-to-hardware mapping process, we have proposed a software-hardware collaborative training method for FANCH. Figure 4A shows the schematic of the proposed method. First, during the software training process, a compressed MNIST dataset is created from the standard MNIST dataset, and a three-layer FCNN with preliminary generalization performance is trained on the compressed dataset using personal computer software. During the training, the actual voltage-current curves of the designed FAFU are used as the activation function. The voltage-current curve of FAFU is approximated using a third-order Taylor expansion, with the coefficients K1 and K2 available for adjustment (fig. S13A). These coefficients were introduced during the FAFU circuit design stage to provide flexibility in adjusting the nonlinear curve of the circuit-level activation function. Next, the trained neural-network weights are mapped onto the resistor crossbar array cells of FANCH. The 10,000 data entries from the compressed MNIST dataset are passed through the resistor crossbar array cells and then processed sequentially by the TIA, DFA, FAFU, MVCMP, and other modules, ultimately yielding the accuracy of FANCH. Last, the tested accuracy is compared with the target accuracy (88% obtained by software simulation). By adjusting the coefficients K1 and K2 (fig. S13B), the actual characteristic curve of the FAFU is synchronously modified, and the adjustments are fed back into the PC software for iterative training until the accuracy loss of FANCH is reduced to below the preset threshold (0.5%). This collaborative training procedure co-optimizes the NN weights and the analog activation characteristics. The fabricated FANCH adopts the weights obtained through this training process. Here, because the entire system adopts the same activation function, all FAFUs use the same K1 and K2. In each iteration of the training, the updated K1 and K2 values are applied to all modules to ensure consistency and design feasibility.

We compared the recognition accuracy of FANCH on the compressed MNIST test dataset before and after the software-hardware collaborative training. As shown in Fig. 4B, after optimization using the proposed method, the recognition accuracy of FANCH reached 87.64%. Moreover, the system-level implementation FANCH_SL achieved a recognition accuracy of 87.52% (fig. S14). Compared to the software baseline of 88%, there is only a slight decrease of 0.36%. Compared to the results without implementing the software-hardware collaborative training method, the accuracy improved by 7.02% (fig. S15). We also analyzed the recognition accuracy of FANCH for labels 0 to 9 before and after the software-hardware collaborative training, as shown in Fig. 4C. The results indicate that the recognition accuracy for all labels have been improved after the collaborative training, especially for the label of digit 8, ensuring the overall recognition performance and comprehensiveness of the system.

These test data validate the feasibility of our proposed software-hardware collaborative training method for FANCH, enhancing its generalization performance.

DISCUSSION

FANCH achieves NNs through fully analog circuits with limited hardware resources, demonstrating the potential for efficient AI computation on edge devices. To adapt FANCH for larger-scale NNs and more complex datasets, its scalability requires further exploration. We have preliminarily verified the feasibility for further scaling of FANCH through simulations and compared its accuracy with that of the conventional system using A/D conversion (fig. S16). In future large-scale hardware implementations, as the size and complexity of NNs increase, more neurons and layers are involved. This results in an exponential growth in the hardware resource demands, particularly in the number of memory and computation units. Therefore, more efficient data compression methods need to be developed to balance the hardware overhead and performance. In addition, analog storage technologies such as ferroelectric capacitor–based memory cells and high-precision memristors (52–55) should be introduced at intermediate nodes to enable efficient resource reuse. Moreover, the adoption of advanced technology nodes is essential, as it can substantially enhance computational power per unit area, ensuring the capability to handle more complex tasks under limited hardware resources.

On the other hand, to improve the generality of FANCH, allowing the hardware to support NNs with different architectures, online training is necessary. In parallel computing, efficient resource reuse plays a vital role in reducing hardware power consumption during online training. To achieve efficient resource reuse, innovative designs for circuit and hardware architecture are also required to avoid increasing computational delays. Furthermore, novel devices that can achieve precise online weight tuning are required. In fully analog computation, the impact of the parasitic capacitances and resistances becomes more severe during the online training. Since parasitic effects can accumulate errors during training, thereby substantially affecting computational accuracy, further optimization in circuit design and chip layout is necessary.

In summary, we developed FANCH, a fully analog neural-network computing hardware with low hardware resource overhead, and demonstrated its application for handwritten digit recognition in this work. FANCH performs the entire computational process of a multilayer NN using fully analog circuits, eliminating the frequent data conversion through A/Ds required in traditional neural-network hardware. Inspired by the visual system of the human brain, we also introduce the data compression methods to FANCH, reducing the synapse count by 135 times and the computational complexity by 17 times. To address the precision loss issues, we designed high-precision FAFU and analog processing circuit modules. In addition, we proposed a software-hardware collaborative training method that further improves the generalization performance, achieving a 7.02% increase in accuracy.

Hardware test results show that the power consumption of FANCH is only 27.3 mW. Without the data conversion, FANCH performs extremely fast recognition, with an execution time as fast as 0.6 μs (table S1). Compared to the state-of-the-art works of mixed analog-digital and fully digital architectures (44, 46, 56, 57), FANCH demonstrates a 1.54 to 2.81 times improvement in the energy efficiency under 8-bit input precision (table S2). Compared to the mainstream commercial system-level AI acceleration hardware (58–61), FANCH_SL demonstrates a potential improvement in energy efficiency of 2.1 to 15.3 times (table S3). Our work can drive the energy and resource efficiency of AI acceleration hardware closer to that of the human brain, laying a solid foundation for the development of next-generation ultra-efficient fully analog AI accelerators for edge applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication of chip-level hardware FANCH and system-level hardware FANCH_SL

FANCH features fully integrated on-chip crossbar weight arrays and is fabricated using standard 180-nm complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor technology, with a die area of 1.7 mm2. The single chip is packaged in a 64-pin quad flat ceramic package (QFP-64) and operates at a voltage range of 1.8 to 2.5 V.

The crossbar weight array of FANCH_SL is manufactured using high-precision discrete components with a resistance accuracy error of less than 1%. Each circuit module in the entire signal path uses operational amplifiers with rail-to-rail and ultralow offset voltage characteristics (Texas Instruments, OPA4317 and OPA4197). The fabricated PCB is an eight-layer board.

Data input methods

The input data can be generated in two ways. The first method involves using an external 4.3-inch (10.9-cm) liquid-crystal display (LCD) capacitive touchscreen (ALIENTEK 4.3 TFT LCD), which is divided into a seven-by-seven capture area both vertically and horizontally. When a finger presses a section leaving a black line, the capacitance value of the touch screen changes. The connected MCU captures this change and records as data 1 for that area, while other areas that do not detect pressure are recorded as data 0. After handwriting a number on the screen, the MCU converts the captured 49 screen capacitance signals into voltage signals and outputs them in parallel through the input/output port (data 1 is converted to a 1-V signal, data 0 to a 0-V signal) and then transmits these voltage signals to hardware through the pins integrated on the PCB (fig. S17). The second method involves storing the compressed MNIST handwritten digit dataset on an external SD card (SanDisk, 32 GB), from which the MCU reads each data entry via the SD card connector integrated on the PCB and outputs analog voltage signals through the DAC (ADI, AD5328; reference voltage of 1 V) integrated on the PCB, finally delivering these signals to hardware through the pins on the PCB.

Electrical measurements and performance testing

FANCH and FANCH_SL are both powered by a power adapter converting 220 to 12 V. The test PCB integrates a 12-V dc power interface corresponding to the power adapter, and the board supplies other voltages on the PCB through DC–DC converter and low dropout regulator power chips (TPS5430 and AMS1117). An oscilloscope (Siglent, SDS2102X) is used to test the electrical characteristics through the connectors on the PCB. In the performance testing, we have written test programs and key control programs (fig. S18). The MNIST dataset is stored on an SD card. Data retrieval and processing from the SD card are managed by an MCU (STMicroelectronics, STM32F103ZET6). This MCU, along with the self-designed peripheral control circuit, generates voltage values representing the handwritten digit images and inputs them into FANCH. By lightly pressing the key switch, the MCU inputs the 10,000 test data from the SD card into hardware for testing at fixed intervals of 500 ms. The outputs are collected through the integrated 10-channel ADC in the MCU, and the data are transmitted to the PC-side serial port transfer assistant by the MCU. The ADC collection transmission rate, the data reception rate of the serial port transfer assistant, and the transmission frequency of test data by the MCU are all synchronized at 500 ms. The 10,000 test results saved by the serial port transfer assistant are processed by a Python program on the PC, determining the accuracy.
