Evaluation of stresses created by forces from different directions on paediatric crowns using finite element analysis
Authors: Cagla Simsek, Mine Koruyucu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFinite element analysis (FEA) is a widely used computational method for assessing stress distribution in dental structures. Understanding the mechanical behaviour of paediatric crowns under functional loads is essential for optimising restorative material selection.AimThis study aims to evaluate the stress distribution in maxillary primary second molars restored with stainless steel, zirconia, and strip crowns under occlusal and lateral forces using FEA.DesignThree-dimensional models of primary second molars with full coronal restorations were analysed. Vertical, horizontal and oblique force vectors were applied, and minimum and maximum principal stresses were calculated using finite element analysis software (Algor Fempro).ResultsUnder vertical (axial) loading, the maximum principal stress values recorded on the root surface were 10.4 MPa (control), 7.8 MPa (stainless steel crown), 17 MPa (zirconia crown) and 10.3 MPa (strip crown). Stresses increased markedly under oblique and, in particular, horizontal forces in every model; the strip crown produced the highest root stresses (up to 505.7 MPa under horizontal loading), whereas the zirconia crown produced the highest stresses within the crown itself.ConclusionAll of the crown materials examined produced relatively low stresses under vertical (axial) loading, but their mechanical performance varied considerably with the direction of loading, with the highest stresses occurring under horizontal forces. Zirconia crowns concentrated the highest stresses within the crown, stainless steel crowns produced the most uniform stress distribution and the lowest root stresses, and strip crowns developed markedly elevated root stresses under non-axial loading. On the basis of stress distribution, the stainless steel and zirconia crowns performed more favourably than the strip crown under non-axial loading. These findings can inform material selection for full coronal restorations in paediatric dentistry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08903-5.

Introduction

Globally, dental caries is a significant public health issue affecting both children and adults, leading to widespread health problems [1]. Early Childhood Caries (ECC), particularly observed in preschool-aged children, is a progressive disease of primary teeth. This condition has the potential to significantly impact children's overall health, nutritional habits, speech development, and, consequently, their quality of life [2]. Several factors contribute to the development of ECC, including genetics, oral hygiene practices, socioeconomic conditions, and diet. This complex interaction necessitates a multidisciplinary approach in developing treatment and prevention strategies [3].

Treatment methods for dental caries vary depending on the stage of caries progression and the type of tooth affected. While preventive treatments such as fluoride applications and remineralization techniques are emphasised in the early stages of caries, advanced caries require restorative treatments [4]. The choice of restorative material is influenced by factors such as aesthetic expectations, the mechanical properties of the material, the functional requirements of the treated tooth, and cost. In paediatric dentistry, the specific needs of young patients and the necessity of maintaining oral health play a significant role in the selection of restorative materials. For cases involving severe tissue loss or abnormalities, prosthetic applications such as stainless steel crowns (SSC), zirconia crowns, and strip crowns are employed [5]. The restorative selection is crucial, as the choice of crown material significantly influences the stress distribution within the tooth structure and the longevity of the restoration [6].

SSCs have long been the gold standard for full coronal restorations in primary teeth due to their durability and ease of use [7]. However, their metallic appearance is often considered aesthetically undesirable by both patients and parents [8]. In contrast, zirconia crowns have gained popularity for their superior aesthetics and biocompatibility, although concerns about their fracture resistance and stress distribution under occlusal loads remain [9–11]. Composite strip crowns, while offering a balance between aesthetics and ease of application, present challenges in terms of durability and stress management [12].

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) is a powerful tool increasingly used in dentistry to simulate and understand the mechanical behaviour of dental structures and restorative materials [13]. This method allows for a detailed modelling of the performance of various materials under stress, strain, and deformation, providing valuable insights for both clinical practice and material science research. FEA has applications in many branches of dentistry, including oral and maxillofacial surgery, restorative dentistry, endodontics, periodontics, and orthodontics [14]. It is particularly useful for making critical decisions regarding dental implant design and placement, selecting restorative materials, analysing the behaviour of bone and teeth under load, evaluating the effectiveness of root canal filling materials, and designing orthodontic appliances. The comprehensive analyses provided by FEA have the potential to improve treatment outcomes and enhance patient satisfaction. Through this method, theoretical simulations of scenarios that cannot be replicated in real life can be conducted, yielding detailed insights into the performance of treatment methods and materials [14].

This study aims to utilise FEA to compare the stress distribution in primary second molars restored with zirconia, SSCs, and composite strip crowns under different loading conditions. By analysing the stresses imposed on dental tissues and adhesive cements, this research seeks to provide evidence-based guidance for the selection of appropriate crown materials in paediatric dentistry, particularly in the context of posterior regions of the mouth. The research focuses on the mechanical behaviour of these materials under occlusal and lateral forces, aiming to simulate clinical conditions as closely as possible.

Materials and methods

The research methodology consists of four main stages: the creation of three-dimensional geometric models of the teeth, the development of three-dimensional solid models for the coronal restoration and adhesive materials, the input of material properties into the program and the formation of mathematical models, and finally, the application of forces and the acquisition of results.

Three-dimensional geometric modelling of the teeth

To transfer the experiment into a simulated setup, it is first necessary to create three-dimensional geometric models. This modelling process involves the detailed reconstruction of the actual tooth in a computerised environment. For this purpose, a computer equipped with an Intel Xeon ® R CPU 3.30 GHz processor, 500 GB hard disk, 14 GB RAM, and Windows 7 Ultimate Version Service Pack 1 operating system, along with Rhinoceros 4.0 (3670 Woodland Park Ave N, Seattle, WA 98103 USA) 3D modelling software, VRMesh Studio (VirtualGrid Inc, Bellevue City, WA, USA), and Algor Fempro (ALGOR, Inc. 150 Beta Drive Pittsburgh, PA 15238–2932 USA) analysis programs were utilised.

In order to include the imaging in the study, data from 4- to 7-year-old patients with structurally sound and caries-free primary teeth were assessed. However, no caries-free primary teeth were found in the tomographic images reviewed. Despite having ethical committee approval, due to the need for modelling in the analysis program, pre-prepared primary tooth models from the database were used. In the formation of the experimental groups, molars were separated for distinct analysis. For the investigation of the maxillary primary second molar, four distinct groups were designed. Geometric models were categorised as the control group, a stainless steel crown (SSC) applied model, a zirconia crown applied model, and a strip crown applied model.

The basic structure of the geometric models was based on the control group. Geometric modelling of enamel, dentin, pulp, cementum and the periodontal ligament (PDL), along with the cortical and spongy bone, was performed for the maxillary primary second molar. As the analysis was performed on a tooth basis, the bone structures were represented as cubic blocks twenty millimetres in width, length and thickness to simulate realistic conditions.

Three-dimensional geometric modelling of restorations

In the models other than the control group, an identical amount of tooth structure was removed for every restoration type — 1.5 mm from the occlusal surface and 1 mm from the axial (peripheral) walls — so that the preparation was standardised across the groups. The thickness assigned to each restoration corresponds to the characteristic wall thickness of that material, as reported by the manufacturer and in the literature, rather than to the depth of the preparation: stainless steel crowns are thin-walled (0.13 mm), prefabricated zirconia crowns are thicker (0.73 mm), and the strip crown is a directly placed composite restoration that fills the prepared space (1.5 mm). For the prefabricated crowns, the space remaining between the prepared tooth surface and the inner surface of the crown was occupied by the luting cement, and the outer contour of each crown was restored to the original anatomical form of the tooth. Accordingly, the stainless steel crown was modelled at 0.13 mm with a 100 μm glass ionomer cement layer; the zirconia crown at 0.73 mm with a 100 μm glass ionomer cement layer; and the strip (composite) crown at 1.5 mm with a 30 μm adhesive layer [16].

Each model was positioned accurately in three-dimensional space using Rhinoceros software to ensure proper alignment for material property loading and analysis.

The geometric models were designed using "brick" (cube) and "tetrahedral" (tetrahedron) elements (Fig. 1). In these modelling systems, the Fempro modelling tool employs 8-noded elements to the maximum extent possible. In regions closer to the center of the structures within the models, elements with fewer nodes were used when necessary to complete the structure. This modelling technique aimed to create the highest quality mesh structure with the maximum number of nodal points to simplify the calculations and ensure accurate results.Fig. 1Brick and tetrahedral element examples

After the geometric models were converted into mathematical models using VRMesh software, they were transferred in.stl format to Algor Fempro (ALGOR, Inc. 150 Beta Drive Pittsburgh, PA 15238–2932 USA) software to prepare them for analysis.

The application of forces

Each model was divided into three separate subgroups considering the forces to be analysed. Accordingly, a total of twelve analyses were planned to separately examine the forces applied in vertical, horizontal, and oblique directions on the four models.

The amounts of force selected to simulate chewing and lateral forces. Assuming a healthy occlusion in our study, the maximum biting forces were determined to be 197 Newton for the posterior region [18, 19].

The trials comprised four groups: the control group, the model with a stainless steel crown (SSC) applied, the model with a zirconia crown applied, and the model with a strip crown applied. Each group was tested with forces applied in three directions: vertical, horizontal and oblique. The vertical force was directed parallel to the long axis of the tooth, the horizontal force perpendicular to the long axis, and the oblique force was applied at an angle of 45° to the long axis. This produced a total of twelve experimental configurations. Forces were applied at the palatal incline of the mesiobuccal cusp, the palatal incline of the distobuccal cusp, the buccal incline of the mesiolingual cusp and the buccal incline of the distolingual cusp. The net force in all three directions was set at 197 N. The vertical and oblique forces were divided into four equal parts applied to the cusp inclines, while the horizontal force, simulating lateral load, was divided into two equal parts applied to the palatal inclines of the buccal cusps.

The stresses generated under the analysed forces were calculated as minimum and maximum principal stresses. Visual stress assessment was conducted based on the colour changes in the colour scale showing stress values located in the upper left corner of each image. According to this assessment, blue areas indicate the regions with the lowest principal stresses, while red areas indicate the regions with the highest principal stresses.

The values obtained from finite element analyses cannot be subjected to statistical analyses since they result from mathematical calculations without variance. Therefore, it is essential to accurately evaluate and interpret the sectional images and the stress amounts and distributions at the nodes [20]. For this reason, the stress values obtained have been assessed both qualitatively, quantitatively, and visually.

Discussion

The results of this study highlight significant differences in stress distribution among the restorative materials applied to maxillary primary second molars under different loading conditions. Finite element analysis (FEA) provided detailed insight into how these materials behave under vertical, horizontal and oblique forces, which can inform conclusions about their suitability in paediatric dentistry.

Zirconia crowns developed the highest stress values within the crown of all the materials tested, particularly under horizontal loading. This behaviour is consistent with the high elastic modulus of zirconia, which was the stiffest restorative material modelled (Table 3): the crown acts as a rigid shell that concentrates stress within itself rather than dissipating it into the surrounding tooth structure. This did not, however, reduce the stress reaching the underlying tooth: the zirconia root stresses were comparable to or higher than those of the stainless steel crown, which produced the lowest root stresses of the restored models. This contrasts with Gezgin et al., who reported that zirconia crowns reduced the stress on dental tissues relative to stainless steel crowns [16]; this difference probably reflects the different stress measure (Von Mises versus principal stress), cementation and applied loading used in the two studies. The elevated stress concentrations observed near the crown margins nonetheless suggest a theoretical risk of marginal chipping or micro-cracking under repeated high non-axial loads, which is of particular relevance in paediatric patients, for whom the long-term durability of restorations is important [9].

Stainless steel crowns showed a more even stress distribution than zirconia crowns, with lower peak stresses transmitted across the tooth structure. This indicates that stainless steel crowns dissipate occlusal forces more uniformly and reduce localised stress concentrations, thereby lowering the likelihood of stress-related failure. This mechanical behaviour is consistent with the long-standing clinical preference for stainless steel crowns in paediatric dentistry, where their durability, clinical longevity and ease of placement are well documented and decisive, supporting their continued use where long-term strength is the primary requirement [7, 8].

The composite strip crown produced a moderate stress distribution within the crown but transmitted markedly higher stresses to the root under oblique and, especially, horizontal forces, where the root stresses greatly exceeded those of the other models. This reflects the comparatively low elastic modulus of composite, which was the least stiff restorative material modelled (Table 3) and permits greater deformation and transmission of force to the underlying tooth under non-axial loading. Clinically, composite strip crowns are used predominantly for anterior restorations, where they provide good aesthetics, but they are technique-sensitive and prone to fracture and marginal breakdown [10, 12]; the present findings reinforce that they are best reserved for situations with limited occlusal load and used with caution where high or laterally directed bite forces are expected.

From a clinical perspective, these findings reinforce that material selection in paediatric dentistry should account not only for the mechanical properties of the restoration but also for the specific clinical situation, including the expected magnitude and direction of occlusal forces, the patient's age and aesthetic requirements. Zirconia crowns are well suited to cases in which aesthetics is a priority and occlusal loads are moderate, although their tendency to concentrate stress warrants caution in patients with heavy or parafunctional bite forces. In this model, stainless steel crowns showed favourable stress behaviour and are a suitable option where robust restorations capable of withstanding high occlusal forces are required. Composite strip crowns are best avoided where high lateral loads are anticipated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to finite element analysis. All structures were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, which does not fully reproduce the anisotropic and viscoelastic behaviour of dental tissues. A single static load of 197 N was applied, so the results do not capture cyclic (fatigue) loading, thermal changes or the moisture of the oral environment. The interfaces between the crown, cement and tooth were assumed to be perfectly bonded, and standardised tooth geometries derived from the literature were used because no suitable caries-free teeth were available on the reviewed CBCT images; consequently, anatomical variation between patients was not represented. Because finite element outputs are deterministic, they cannot be subjected to statistical analysis, and the absolute stress values should be interpreted as relative comparisons between models rather than precise predictions of clinical failure. Future studies incorporating dynamic loading, fatigue simulation and validation against in vitro or clinical data would strengthen these findings.

Conclusion

Within these limitations, all of the crown materials examined produced relatively low stresses under axial loading, but their mechanical performance differed considerably with the direction of loading, and stresses were consistently highest under horizontal forces. Zirconia crowns concentrated the greatest stresses within the crown, stainless steel crowns produced the most uniform stress distribution and the lowest root stresses, and composite strip crowns transmitted the highest stresses to the root under non-axial loading. On the basis of stress distribution alone, the stainless steel and zirconia crowns performed more favourably than the composite strip crown under non-axial loading. As with any restoration, the final choice of crown should also take into account cost and aesthetic considerations.
