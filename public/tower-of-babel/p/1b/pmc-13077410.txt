Defining the role of PET/CT in posttreatment surveillance for non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Francesco Gabriele Tatangelo Fazzari, Anand Swaminath
Published: 2026

Introduction

There is interest in evaluating novel post-treatment surveillance strategies which may detect potentially salvageable recurrences after curative treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The recent SUPE_R phase III randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted by Guldbrandsen et al. to evaluate the utility of fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) vs. conventional imaging (contrast-enhanced CT) for post-treatment surveillance of stage IA–IIIC NSCLC (1). The overall conclusion from the trial was that PET/CT surveillance did not meet its primary endpoint of increasing the proportion of curatively treated recurrences. In both arms 48% of recurrences were treated with curative intent. Additionally, time to recurrence (TTR) and overall survival (OS) were not improved with PET/CT compared to contrast-enhanced CT. Thus, PET/CT should not be implemented into routine post-treatment surveillance. How will these results impact clinical practice?

This trial was designed to provide level 1 evidence regarding the impact of PET/CT surveillance on patient outcomes given previous retrospective studies that supported its use (2,3). Despite initial excitement, the negative results of the trial are unlikely to change current practice guidelines for surveillance following curative treatment of NSCLC (4).

Strengths

This study has many strengths. First, the trial was a large RCT, randomizing 750 patients 1:1 to the control and experimental arms, and included patients with stage IA–IIIC NSCLC treated with curative intent via surgical resection, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), or concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Second, their primary outcome, defined as the proportion of recurrences that could be treated with curative intent was pragmatic and clinically relevant, as early detection could provide an increased opportunity for salvage curative intent therapy, with potential downstream impacts on oncological outcomes. Third, the secondary outcomes of TTR, OS, avoidance of invasive procedures, and adverse events provided further insight on the impact of the surveillance regimens studied. Finally, mandating multidisciplinary assessment of treatment intent determination reflected real-world practice, and allowed for consensus discussions and not tissue biopsy alone, to be a part of the diagnostic algorithm. This made the workflow applicable to patient-care decision making in non-trial settings, where resources, time, and more importantly patient factors often make confirmatory tissue diagnosis prohibitive.

How generalizable is the study?

This study provided evidence against the implementation of PET/CT in NSCLC post-treatment surveillance. However, there are several limitations which limit the generalizability of the results. These limitations raise several outstanding questions.

First, is there value in PET/CT for detection of low volume metastatic disease? The authors did not stratify for the extent of non-curative recurrence detected, or by the treatment options available if non-curative recurrence was identified. Not all non-curative recurrences are equal. For example, some patients with locoregional recurrence may not have curative options available but may survive longer than patients with widespread metastatic recurrence. On the other hand, current American Joint Committee on Cancer 9th edition tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging for NSCLC subdivides metastatic disease to four categories given differential survival amongst these patients, including whether there is limited or more extensive extrathoracic disease (5). As such, although some treatments may not be curative intent there may still be the potential for greater survival with earlier detection. For example, oligometastatic disease may be amenable to radical intent treatment using metastasis-directed therapies such as surgery or SBRT. Although survival data was captured, longer follow-up may be required to detect a difference between the groups.

Second, while pragmatic, there is an element of bias in the primary outcome of proportion of recurrences treated with curative intent. PET/CT may detect more extrathoracic recurrences which may not be amenable to curative-intent therapy. As such although the proportion of patients with an intrathoracic recurrence appears similar (13.3% vs. 12.9%, P=0.87), there is a trend towards a higher proportion of recurrences that are intrathoracic (65% vs. 55%, P=0.20) in the CT arm. This provides an element of bias against the utility of PET/CT to detect a larger proportion of curative recurrences since there is a numerically larger number of non-curative recurrences identified. As such, selection of an endpoint such as disease-free survival following curative treatment may have been more informative as it may better capture outcomes with more intensive re-staging using PET/CT versus not.

Third, are these results valid for patients with stage II–III NSCLC, or those treated with SBRT or chemoradiotherapy? This study may not be generalizable across all stages and treatment modalities for NSCLC, as only approximately 15% of patients had stage II disease, and another 15% with stage III disease. Given a population skewed towards stage I disease managed with surgery, the authors observed a lower than expected recurrence rate of 22% in the entire cohort, which affected the power of their study (with the original assumption of a 45% relapse rate). In addition, only 21% of patients received SBRT or chemoradiotherapy so these patients were also not as well represented in the study population. The inclusion of patients who received SBRT may also influence the results as it is known that early PET/CT following SBRT is challenging to interpret and may lead to false positives due to persistent standardized uptake value (SUV) caused by post-radiation inflammatory reaction (6). As such these patients may have required invasive investigations due to false positive scans that may not have been required.

Finally, does PET/CT improve outcomes when compared to less intensive surveillance schedules? The standard of care arm in this trial consisted of the intensive surveillance schedule in Denmark (the country the study was conducted) involving contrast enhanced CT every 3 months for 2 years, but was not consistent with more widely adopted guidelines such as from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) (4). ASCO recommends surveillance for recurrence using CT every 6 months for 2 years (4). The ASCO guideline has been adopted by other Health Authorities such as Cancer Care Ontario which also recommends that surveillance for recurrence should occur every 6 months for 2 years (7), There is a preference for non-contrast low-dose CT chest including the adrenals, however contrast is recommended if indicated (7). While the surveillance timeline of 2 years is consistent with these other schedules, it is also possible that curative recurrences are identified beyond this time point. It remains to be seen that PET/CT may perform better than a 6-month surveillance schedule with traditional imaging.

Future directions

If PET/CT cannot improve current surveillance recommendations while leading to additional invasive diagnostic procedures new avenues for the improvement of post-treatment surveillance are required. One potential avenue is implementation of circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) in response-adaptive surveillance schedules. Many studies across cancer types have stratified patients into recurrence risk categories based on ctDNA (8). This has also been the case in NSCLC treated with curative intent therapy (9-11). Ongoing trials such as the ADAPT-C trial (NCT04585490) are focusing on response-adaptive therapies using ctDNA as personalized treatment intensification. As part of the SUPE_R trial the authors collected ctDNA from patients to assess the clinical validity of a tumour-agnostic approach for minimal residual disease (MRD) detection and recurrence prediction for patients with NSCLC undergoing curative intent treatment (12). They found that ctDNA detection was a reliable biomarker for predicting cancer recurrence following treatment, and the timing for blood sampling depended on the type of treatment a patient received. Given these results it is possible that a new surveillance schedule could be created and tested in a manner similar to the SUPE_R trial. This schedule could also be tailored depending on the curative treatment received and response adapted depending on presence of MRD by ctDNA analysis. For example, in patients who receive surgery for stage I NSCLC there could be sampling of ctDNA at first and second follow-up. If MRD is identified and the patient is at high risk of recurrence, then imaging with PET/CT every 6 months may be indicated. If they are negative for MRD then low-dose CT of the chest and adrenals every 6 months for 2 years may be sufficient (see Figure 1). Certainly, designing trials to evaluate all potential scenarios may prove to be exceedingly difficult. Our hope is that future research will aim to identify the optimal strategy to maximize the chance of early detection so that patients can benefit from salvage therapy and potentially improve long-term survival.

Conclusions

The SUPE_R trial provides high quality evidence against the routine implementation of PET/CT in post-treatment surveillance of patients with NSCLC treated with curative intent. The authors performed a well-conducted, robust RCT to support their research question. Their pragmatic endpoint allows for application of their data to real-world decision making. Although routine PET/CT may not be the current solution, future novel strategies are required to enhance surveillance schedules. Research may utilize hybrid risk adaptive strategies based on other biomarkers or novel imaging modalities.
