Are primary healthcare facilities in Ethiopia complying with accessibility standards for disability-inclusion? an accessibility audit in Bahir Dar City
Authors: Desta Debalkie Atnafu, Femke Bannink Mbazzi, Abdmagidu Menya, Mezgebu Yitayal, Hannah Kuper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccessible health facilities are a crucial component of disability-inclusive healthcare. Yet audits are rarely undertaken, particularly in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs). This study evaluated the accessibility of Ethiopia’s primary healthcare facilities to determine its readiness for delivering disability-inclusive services. Furthermore, it proposes actionable solutions to guide national efforts in strengthening health system inclusivity.MethodsA cross-sectional accessibility audit was conducted across all 10 public primary healthcare facilities in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia. We collected data through a structured Disability Awareness Checklist, using face-to-face, participatory and action-oriented interviews with medical directors. This was complemented by direct observations of facility premises, and physical measurements (e.g. ramp slopes) across four dimensions: universal design, reasonable accommodation, staff capacity, and disability service linkages. We computed median percentage accessibility scores and compared between urban and rural facilities using Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Additionally, Spearman’s correlation was used to examine relationships between accessibility, patient flow, and staffing levels. Finally, facility staff co-proposed tailored action plans to address identified gaps.ResultsThe median overall accessibility score was 35% (range: 14–60%; maximum possible 100%), with urban facilities (42%) scoring higher than rural (18%). By dimension, the highest scores were for reasonable accommodation (47%), followed by staff capacity (42%) and universal design (31%). The lowest performance was recorded in linkages to disability-related services (8%). Differences in median accessibility scores were observed across facilities (χ² = 22.8, p ≤ 0.01) and core dimensions (χ² = 8.9, p < 0.05). Accessibility was strongly and positively correlated with both annual patient volume (ρ = 0.84, p < 0.01) and staff size (ρ = 0.83, p < 0.01). A median of 30 recommendations were proposed, with 45% requiring “high” funding, while 22% were identified as cost-free.ConclusionAccessibility standards for disability inclusion remain largely unmet across primary healthcare facilities in Ethiopia, with marked urban-rural disparities. This undermines the goals of equity and universal health coverage targeted for 2030. Closing these health equity gaps requires strengthening the health system through improved infrastructure, upgraded staff capacity for compassionate care, and stronger referral linkages. Furthermore, these measures are ultimately essential for guaranteeing inclusive, accessible, and rights-based primary care services, particularly in rural contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14838-y.

Introduction

Globally, approximately 1.3 billion people, about one in six, live with disabilities [1]. An estimated 80% of persons with disabilities reside in LMICs [1]. In Ethiopia, the focus of this study, the prevalence is 17.6% [2, 3]. Growing evidence shows that people with disabilities experience substantial health inequities [1, 4], due to exclusion and poverty, including shorter life expectancy [5]. People with disabilities also frequently experience a higher burden of health conditions such as HIV, diabetes, and hypertension, and have more complex healthcare needs, including general, rehabilitation, and secondary health services [1, 4].

Despite these needs, people with disabilities often face multiple barriers to accessing appropriate, affordable, and quality healthcare services [1, 2, 6], due to systemic exclusion from health systems [1, 7]. These barriers including inaccessibility of facilities, nonadjustable medical equipment, and information, absence of reasonable adjustments, lack of disability-informed health workforce, weak referral and follow-up systems, and transportation difficulties [8, 9]. Such barriers may be particularly critical for people with disabilities in LMICs, where health infrastructure is weak and health system coverage is limited [1]. As a result, people with disabilities often underutilize or forgo essential healthcare, which can impair their functioning and exacerbate preventable complications, ultimately contributing to poorer health outcomes, including premature death [1, 4–6].

Accessibility within primary healthcare (PHC) facilities is a foundational prerequisite for ensuring equitable healthcare access [10]. Yet, studies across LMICs consistently show that PHC facilities frequently lack essential accessibility features, such as wide doorways, adjustable examination tables for wheelchair users, and signage in braille or simplified formats [8–13]. In Ethiopia, the limited available evidence shows that these challenges are evident across PHC facilities, where basic physical accessibility features such as ramps, accessible toilets, and inclusive signage are often lacking [12–14]. Despite commitments under the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD), such as Article 9 on accessibility, most LMICs, including Ethiopia, lack systems to monitor and enforce compliance [15, 16]. Accessibility audits, where they exist, typically focus narrowly on physical infrastructure, with limited attention to informational access, staff awareness, or inclusive service readiness and availability. Furthermore, there are few initiatives or policy frameworks by healthcare facilities to support routine audits or improvements [16].

The Disability Awareness Checklist (DAC) has been developed as a practical tool to assess and strengthen disability inclusion in PHC [8]. Unlike traditional audit tools, DAC takes a holistic approach, evaluating physical accessibility, inclusive service provision, communication accessibility, staff awareness, and feasible solution options for change in resource-limited settings [8, 16]. In 2022 and 2024, DAC was piloted in South Africa [16] and Uganda [8], respectively, showing potential to build disability awareness, identify facility-level gaps, and promote practical improvements. However, Ethiopia lacks a standardized, comprehensive system for assessing PHC accessibility. National strategies acknowledge disability inclusion (e.g. Health sector transformation plan II), yet implementation and monitoring remain weak, often falling short of UNCRPD obligations [15, 17].

Therefore, this study aims to assess the accessibility of PHC facilities in Bahir Dar City in Ethiopia using the DAC, and to propose context-appropriate solutions to inform national efforts toward a more inclusive, accessible, and equitable PHC system.

Methods and materials

Study settings and design

The study was conducted in Bahir Dar City, the capital of Amhara Regional State, located approximately 565 km northwest of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Situated at 11°29′N and 37°29′E along the southern shore of Lake Tana, the city spans approximately 213.43 km² and had an estimated population of 420,000 as of 2023 (Central Statistical Services of Ethiopia). Bahir Dar City has 10 public PHC facilities, along with four government, six private hospitals and several private clinics. This audit was carried out across all 10 functional public PHC facilities, four of which are located in rural villages. These facilities deliver both preventive and curative services, supported by community-based promotion activities led by urban health extension workers. A cross-sectional study design was employed for the audits.

Sampling and sample size determination

We conducted an accessibility audit across all currently functioning public PHC facilities (n = 10) between May 15 to 27, 2025. In each facility, one key respondent (typically the medical director) was selected for a face-to-face interview (Table 1) and to propose potential solutions for identified accessibility gaps. All PHC facilities were included provided they were publicly owned, currently operational and situated within the lower tier of the health system. However, the hospitals and private facilities were excluded. The key respondents were purposefully chosen based on their conceptual skill and adequate knowledge of the facility and their ability to propose and take ownership of improvement measures.

Data collection tool and procedure

This study employed the DAC to audit public PHC facilities in Bahir Dar City. The DAC is a structured, rights-based tool developed, validated, and piloted in South Africa [16] and Uganda [8]. It is designed to assess disability-related accessibility and identify gaps in service delivery in low-resource settings. The tool follows a two-stage process: auditing to identify accessibility and service gaps, and action planning to guide facility-level improvements. It was selected for its practicality in generating inclusive solutions with minimal resource requirements. Data were collected through on-site facility visits by the research team, primarily the lead author, an experienced DAC implementer from Uganda, and a trained local field worker. The data collectors received training on the DAC technique and on ethical engagement with participants (PHC facility or representative).

The audit component of DAC comprises 79-item indicators that covers four key dimensions and 12 sub-dimensions (Table 2). The four domains include: universal design and accessibility (39 items), reasonable accommodation (14 items), staff capacity development (13 items), and linkages to disability and rehabilitation services (13 items). Permission letter to conduct the study was obtained from the local city health department authorities. Next, the research team contacted each facility head, primarily the medical directors, to obtain approval to conduct the facility audit and to invite them to participate in the interviews. Written informed consent was then obtained from each medical director prior to data collection. Then, the team conducted face-to-face, participatory and action-oriented interview with an individual representing each facility (e.g., medical director), supplemented by direct observation, and physical measurement. Ramp gradients and structural dimensions (e.g., doorways, corridors) were measured using a calibrated angle measuring app (e.g., Angle Meter Pro/Inclinometer) and a measuring tape, respectively. This information was used to rate each indicator as ‘Yes,’ ‘No,’ or ‘Not Sure,’ reflecting the presence or absence of inclusive services. The facility representative who participated in the interview and data collectors jointly determined responses. The second component of the DAC tool is a feedback form used to document identified gaps and proposed recommendations. The study team completed the form across four dimensions, after which interviews with facility representative facilitated reflection on areas for improvement. Facilities were then supported to identify feasible changes to implement within a defined timeline. Hence, a summary action plan was co-developed by data collectors and healthcare facility representative. Finally, a structured feedback report was prepared, and shared for future reference, outlining the facility’s overall accessibility status and proposed changes. Each facility was encouraged to conduct regular self-assessments using the DAC tool.

Study variables

Outcome measure

The outcome variable, healthcare facility accessibility, was assessed using four proxy dimensions comprising a 79-item checklist with “Yes,” “No,” or “Not Sure” response options. A score of 1 was assigned to “Yes,” and 0 to “No” or “Not Sure.” Scores were summed and transformed to a 0–100 scale to generate both subscale and overall accessibility scores. Accessibility levels were calculated using the median percentage of the maximum possible score per facility [8]. Overall and dimensional scores were categorized as either high or low accessibility based on cut-off values derived from a demarcation threshold formula [18]. We used Microsoft Excel and R (with the ggplot2, tidyr, and dplyr packages) to generate graph or chart. Each of the four dimensions was equally weighted, regardless of the number of indicators [8, 9]. Rural and urban settings further stratified these analyses. Data distribution was assessed for normality and found to be skewed due to the small sample size; thus, non-parametric tests were used. The median percentage scores were calculated per facility and per indicator, along with ranges, following methods piloted in Uganda [8]. Bivariable associations between accessibility scores and patient flow/ staff size were analysed using Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ). The Mann–Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test was also applied to compare accessibility across facilities, taking into account for non-homogeneous variances. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Proposed solutions were categorized into one of eleven thematic areas and assigned indicative levels of investment using content analysis informed by disability-inclusive healthcare financing, following the method used by Mactaggart et al. [8]. Additionally, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework was applied to identify and prioritize strategies (Table 3).

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was secured from the Ethics Review Boards of the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (LSHTM Ethics Ref: 30698) and Bahir Dar University (BDU Ethics Ref: 1069/2024), Ethiopia. Additional permission and support letter were obtained from the Bahir Dar city health department and facility management. Written informed consent was taken from all participants representing each facility prior to the commencement of the audit. Then, the study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Characteristics of healthcare facilities

Ten government-run primary healthcare facilities (six urban, four rural) were audited. The workforce was predominantly female (72%), and the majority of patients were women (60%) across both settings. Few staff reported having disabilities. Urban facilities had more personnel trained in disability inclusion than rural facilities (83% vs. 50%) and took longer median ± ranges time to audit (275 (112–200) vs. 113 (105–143) minutes), likely reflecting larger facility size and more service departments to evaluate. While all facilities had adjacent drop-off points for motorcycle (auto rickshaw) access, rural health centres offered closer proximity to bus/taxi drop-offs (Table 1).

Accessibility of healthcare facilities and disaggregated by location

The median percentage accessibility score across all facilities was 35% (range: 14–60, highest possible score 100%) (Fig. 1). Only 50% of the facilities were classified as having high accessibility (Supplementary Table 1). Urban healthcare centres demonstrated a higher median accessibility score of 42% (range: 33–60) than rural facilities 18% (range:14–23) (Supplementary Fig. 1). A statistically significant difference was also observed in the median accessibility scores across the core dimensions of accessibility (χ² = 8.894, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2). The highest accessibility score was for reasonable accommodation (47%; 14–64), followed by staff capacity (42%; 0–69). However, key dimensions scored poorly, with 60% of the facilities having low accessibility in universal design and physical accessibility, and 80% for linkage to disability and rehabilitation services (Supplementary Table 1). Two notable gaps, in both rural and urban facilities, were the lack of accessibility of toilets and linkages to disability and rehabilitation services (Table 4).

Individual indicators also revealed gaps in accessibility (Supplementary Tables 3–6), including the absence of designated parking or fully accessible compounds, lack of ramps with proper gradients and handrails, emergency exit systems, and doorways or corridors that often failed to meet accessibility standards. Signage was rarely available in accessible formats. Most facilities lacked trained reception or support staff, height-adjustable triage desks, or patient examination beds. Service window counters in the laboratory or pharmacy were frequently too high, and accessible toilets were virtually non-existent. Staff were generally untrained in disability screening, with no standard disability registration tools available. Only one facility had a referral and follow-up tool, and none had formal disability linkages or partnerships in place.

Comparison of accessibility between primary healthcare facilities

A Kruskal-Wallis test (Supplementary Table 7) revealed significant variation in median accessibility scores across primary health centres (χ² = 22.8, p ≤ 0.01). While urban (χ² = 9.4, p = 0.09) and rural (χ² = 2.5, p = 0.47) facilities showed no significant internal differences, urban facilities overall scored significantly higher than rural ones (Z = -2.56, p = 0.01), with accessibility scores more than double those of rural counterparts.

Comparison of facility-level accessibility across each sub-dimension

Figure 2 (lollipop graph), supplemented by radar plots (Supplementary Fig. 3), illustrated the difference in facility-level accessibility across sub-dimensions. Accessibility scores differed significantly across the four main dimensions (χ² = 8.89, p ≤ 0.05) and their sub-dimensions (χ² = 29.77, p ≤ 0.01) (Supplementary Tables 2 and 8). Facility 02 achieved the highest overall score, driven by strong performance across all domains, most notably in sub-dimension referral processes (100%), disability training (80%), healthcare worker training (75%), entrances (72.5%), and assistance and support (71.4%). Facility 01 also scored highly, with strengths in staff capacity (61.5%), driven by a sub-dimension disability awareness and accessibility training (100%), as well as toilet accessibility (83.3%) and reasonable accommodation (57.1%). In contrast, the weakest performance across multiple facilities was observed in toilet accessibility, disability data capture, referral processes, and disability-related service linkages (Supplementary Table 9).

A strong positive correlation was found between patient volume (ρ = 0.84), staff size (ρ = 0.83) and accessibility (Supplementary Table 10).

Proposed implementation strategies to address accessibility gaps

A median of 30 proposed solutions (range: 19–39) were co-developed and submitted for each facility to address identified accessibility gaps (Table 3). The most frequently proposed solutions included major structural upgrades (20%), such as ramps and service window counter modifications, followed by development of disability-specific guidelines and screening tools (e.g. routine disability data capture) (13%), staff training (12%), accessible information formats (10%), signage and directional cues (e.g. absence of disability parking across all facilities) (10%), and minor construction adjustments like ditch filling and entrance door adjustments (9%). The cost of the proposed actions was summarised as no, low, medium or high. Overall, 45% of proposed actions required high levels of funding, 14% were low-cost and 22% required no cost.

Discussion

The audit revealed that only half of the primary healthcare facilities in Bahir Dar met the threshold for high disability-inclusive accessibility, with a low median overall score of 35%. Urban facilities performed notably better accessibility across all core dimensions. In contrast, rural facilities remain chronically under-resourced, lacking both structural accessibility (e.g., ramps, signage, toilets) and institutional capacity (e.g., trained staff, referral protocols). Even among higher-performing facilities, critical domains remained inadequate such as accessible toilets, ramps with handrails, staff training, disability-related service linkage and emergency evacuation systems. A strong positive correlation was observed between staff size/patient volume and accessibility scores. To address these gaps in accessibility, facilities proposed a range of context-specific solutions, from high-cost structural upgrades and training to low- or no-cost actions, such as signage, protocols, and disability-focused data. These proposed actions highlight both the depth of systemic challenges and opportunities for immediate, scalable improvements.

The disparities in the overall median accessibility score reflect global patterns observed in other LMICs [8, 9, 11, 19–21] and high income countries [10, 22], where accessibility gaps are particularly seen in under-resourced areas. For instance, our findings are in line with those in Brazil, where critical accessibility components, such as designated parking space, inclusive signage, assistive equipment, and trained staff, are typically absent or inadequate given certain physical features like ramps or wide entrances are partially in place [9]. In India, only one-third of audited PHC centres had accessible internal doors, and none had accessible toilets or adjustable examination beds [11], consistent with the current study. In Botswana, nearly half of the people with spinal cord injuries similarly reported that they could not independently access healthcare facilities, demanding third person assistant [23]. This specific gap in toilet accessibility noted in our audit, is consistent with findings from Brazil [9], Uganda [8], Malawi [19], and Bangladesh [20], highlighting a shared pattern of structural neglect in sanitation design within LMIC health systems. Accessibility issues have been documented in high-income countries as well. For instance, in the United States, Chile and Saudi Arabia, compliance with physical building codes did not guarantee functional accessibility, with substantial shortages of height-adjustable medical equipment and accessible diagnostic tools still observed [10, 22, 24, 25]. These global patterns suggest that, although accessibility standards may exist, they are often inadequately integrated into broader systems of service design, staff capacity building, or functional referral mechanisms. In addition to these gaps, several audits also reported limited staff capacity and insufficient disability-related training [9, 13, 14, 20, 22, 23, 26]. These human resource development constraints suggest that, even when facilities meet basic physical accessibility standards, the quality of care and responsiveness to the needs of people with disabilities may still remain inadequate.

Like any other LMIC [8], disability inclusive health care delivery in Ethiopia is negatively affected by the absence of disability-specific referral systems, low levels of disability-related training among rural staff, and inadequate data capture on disability status [24]. These observations align with findings from most studies, which have described accessibility challenges are not only infrastructural, but also institutional and procedural [6, 10, 21, 22, 27, 28]. Globally, the lack of accountability mechanisms and routine accessibility audits further impedes progress toward UHC, particularly in achieving disability-inclusion at the lower levels of the healthcare system [28].

As argued by scholars, achieving UHC without explicitly addressing disability inclusion is not feasible [7, 21, 29]. Thus, there is a pressing need for integrated, multisectoral strategies that implement disability inclusion in the full continuum of healthcare delivery. Yet, in many cases, physical accessibility remains a low priority in facility planning and budgeting. Globally, a clear and consistent pattern emerges: where modest infrastructural improvements exist, they are often not integrated within broader systems of inclusive service design, staff capacity, or referral system, despite the high attendance of health facilities by people with disabilities [1, 2, 7, 9]. Hence, without targeted interventions to address these gaps, the promise of UHC by 2030 will remain out of reach for billion of people with disabilities.

Strength and limitations

The facility accessibility audit in Ethiopia using the DAC was the first of its kind, and offers a structured and context-relevant approach to evaluating the readiness of primary healthcare facilities to serve people with disabilities. The DAC provides a standardized, multidimensional tool that enables a comprehensive assessment across key domains, including physical access, staff capacity, communication and support, and service linkages, highlighting systemic gaps often overlooked in standard evaluations. The DAC uses a rights-based structure that captures not only physical barriers but also programmatic and attitudinal shortcomings. It generates actionable, facility-level data to guide targeted improvements and inform inclusive health planning. The tool is low-cost, scalable, and aligns with Ethiopia’s health policy goals and international commitments such as the UNCRPD. Moreover, it fosters awareness and accountability, promoting rights-based care through participatory, evidence-driven action. While the audit was conducted by experts with prior experience piloting the DAC in Uganda, it is not without limitations. The assessment did not interview service users with disabilities. The small number of facilities audited and their restricted geographic range limits the generalizability of the findings within Ethiopia. There is some overlap among its dimensions, particularly between universal design and reasonable accommodation. Additionally, the weighting of dimensions, each with differing numbers of indicators, was complex, and the assumption of equal weight may have led to an overemphasis on physical accessibility (universal design) over other critical domains.

Implications of the findings

These findings highlight an urgent need to scale up the application of accessibility auditing approaches, such as DAC in PHC systems across LMICs. The persistent lack of accessibility of PHC facilities e.g. toilets, examination equipment, limited availability of trained personnel etc. underlines a systemic neglect of the healthcare needs of people with disabilities. This situation directly contradicts the commitments outlined in the UNCRPD and the SDGs, particularly the goal of achieving UHC and ensuring no one is left behind. The study demonstrated that required or suggested improvements are often low- or medium-cost changes that can be made even in a resource-limited context, such as adding accessible signage, filling of ditches, and implementing data management for disability. Future research should also expand geographically, adopt participatory and mixed-method approaches, and include the perspectives of both client and service provider with disabilities to inform system-wide reform more effectively.

Conclusion

Accessibility standards for disability inclusion remain inadequately met across primary healthcare facilities in Ethiopia, with profound urban-rural disparities. These deficiencies in compliance with accessibility standards collectively limit the ability of people with disabilities to obtain equitable and quality healthcare service. Closing these accessibility gaps requires comprehensive health system strengthening efforts, including improving accessible infrastructure, enhancing staff capacity through disability-inclusive training, and establishing effective referral support linkages. These measures are ultimately essential for advancing accessible, and rights-based primary healthcare services for all, particularly rural settings.
