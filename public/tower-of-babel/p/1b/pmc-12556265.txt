Are marketing bans on alcoholic beverages a quick buy?
Authors: Jakob Manthey, Carolin Kilian, Jürgen Rehm
Published: 2025

Gauden Galea and colleagues have identified a set of 25 evidence-based interventions that are expected to have measurable public health effects in a short period of time. These so-called “quick buys” are expected to tackle noncommunicable diseases by reducing key risk factors.1 However, classifying alcohol marketing bans as ‘best-buys’ or ‘quick-buys’ appears premature, given insufficient empirical evidence that such policies yield immediate reductions in alcohol per capita consumption (APC).

While marketing exposure is clearly, and likely causally, linked to alcohol use, best demonstrated among adolescents,2 a ban on marketing has not been consistently linked to immediate population-level effects on empirical grounds. Galea et al. have cited a 2014 Cochrane review that failed to find a consistent and statistically significant population effect based on three Canadian studies from the 1970s and 80s.3 In an update ten years later, we reviewed a total of eleven studies evaluating marketing bans, of which three studies reported a reduction of drinking after a marketing ban, while two studies found the opposite (most found no impact4). A recent expert review focussed on those four studies evaluating the effectiveness of ‘strong’ marketing bans.5 That review found sufficient causal evidence in favour of marketing restrictions, contradicting the conclusions drawn in two of the reviewed studies.6,7

Notwithstanding this controversial debate on the conflicting evidence at the population level, we see a multitude of good reasons to restrict marketing, e.g., to protect youth and maintain low levels of drinking in the long-term.8

Until stronger evidence emerges, marketing restrictions should not be designated as ‘best-buys’ or ‘quick-buys.’ This classification does not reflect the available evidence on the effectiveness or immediacy of APC reduction and may mislead decision makers.
