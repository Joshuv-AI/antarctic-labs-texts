Background

Chorea is a hyperkinetic disorder characterized by involuntary, irregular, and wriggling movements in the face, neck, trunk, or limbs [1]. The presence of chorea with a positive family history often suggests neurodegenerative diseases, including spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs). Progressive ataxia is typically the main feature of SCA; however, on rare occasions, the disease also causes chorea, frequently accompanied by other neurological abnormalities [2]. In such cases, the diagnosis can be difficult in the absence of a suggestive family history.

SCA type 8 (SCA8) is a rare SCA subtype that usually presents in adulthood with slowly progressive cerebellar dysfunction, such as dysarthria, limb ataxia, and gait disturbance [3]. Patients with SCA8 can also develop pyramidal signs, extrapyramidal signs, lower motor neuron signs, cognitive decline, and involuntary movements during the disease course; therefore, SCA8 was reported to mimic other neurodegenerative diseases [4, 5]. The disease is caused by expansion of CAG/CTG repeats in the overlapping ATXN8 and ATXN8OS genes on chromosome 13q21. The diagnosis is based on the neurological findings and molecular genetic test result indicating abnormal CTA/CTG repeat expansion in the ATXN8OS gene [6]. Although SCA8 is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, it may appear to be a sporadic disease because of reduced penetrance [6].

The dopamine transporter (DAT) is a presynaptic transmembrane protein that reuptakes the neurotransmitter dopamine and regulates dopamine level in the synaptic cleft [7]. The number of DATs located at the terminal of the nigrostriatal pathway is inversely related with neuronal loss in the substantia nigra. Hence, DAT-single photon emission computed tomography (DAT-SPECT) is commonly used to detect nigrostriatal degeneration in patients with parkinsonism. It is also used to find subtle nigrostriatal degeneration in certain neurological diseases whose main feature is not parkinsonism; for example, several types of SCA were reported to have DAT-SPECT abnormalities [7]. Herein, we describe the case of a patient with SCA8 who demonstrated hemichorea as the sole clinical manifestation and had no family history of chorea or ataxic disorders. Moreover, DAT-SPECT revealed reduced striatal accumulation in the bilateral striata.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old Japanese woman visited our hospital because of involuntary movements in her right limbs that she intermittently experienced for two weeks. Her paternal grandmother was diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease, and her medical and medication history was unremarkable. On physical examination, distal dominant, involuntary, and twisting movements in the right extremities were observed (i.e., right-sided hemichorea; video can be found in Supplementary Material 1). She had no nystagmus with normal saccades and pursuit eye movements. Her articulation was accurate; moreover, her muscle power and tone, coordination, and deep tendon reflexes were preserved in the limbs. The patient had no difficulty in finger tapping and tandem gait without sensory disturbance. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) she initially underwent on suspicion of stroke revealed no acute lesions. Oral haloperidol 1.5 mg/day was initiated as symptomatic treatment, and it largely resolved the hemichorea. The involuntary movements became unapparent on physical examination; however, she reported rare occurrences of small involuntary movements in her right lower limb.

On follow-up visits to our clinic, more investigations were performed for her hemichorea. Repeated head MRI revealed mild cerebellar atrophy on T1-weighted imaging (arrows, Figs. 1a and 1b) and no T2 abnormality in the basal ganglia (Fig. 1c). Moreover, DAT-SPECT with 123I-N-ωfluoropropyl-2β-carbomethoxy-3β-(4-iodophenyl)nortropane (123I-FP-CIT) displayed decreased tracer uptake in the bilateral striata (Fig. 1d). The specific binding ratio was 3.95 for the right striatum and 4.78 for the left (normal range: 5.2–10.7) with an asymmetry index of 19% (< 10%). Her mini-mental examination score was 30/30. Electroencephalogram indicated no abnormality. Her blood laboratory test results such as glucose, hemoglobin A1c, ammonia, lupus anticoagulant, antinuclear antibody, thyroid stimulating hormone, free triiodothyronine, free thyroxine, ceruloplasmin, copper, and anti-cardiolipin antibody levels were normal. No acanthocytes were found in the blood smear. The cell count, total protein level, and glucose level of the cerebrospinal fluid were unremarkable. Chest-abdomen-pelvis computed tomography detected no tumors. Additionally, because of the mild cerebellar atrophy found on MRI, a set of genetic molecular tests for SCA was performed with multiplex polymerase chain reaction fragment analysis. It detected CTA/CTG repeat expansion in the ATXN8OS gene (18/125 repeats; normal range: 15–50, high penetrance range: 54–250) [6]. The results for SCAs 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 12, 17, and 31 and dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy were normal. Her genetic test result for Huntington’s disease (HD) also proved normal. Finally, she was diagnosed with SCA8, which was considered the etiology of her hemichorea. The patient did not reveal any involuntary movements, parkinsonism, or cerebellar signs when she visited our hospital for 18 months after the diagnosis.

Discussion and conclusions

In the presented case, hemichorea was the sole clinical manifestation of SCA8 in a patient with no family history of chorea or ataxic diseases. Moreover, although she had no clinically apparent parkinsonism, DAT-SPECT revealed decreased tracer uptake in the bilateral striata.

Patients with SCA8 basically develop slowly progressive cerebellar dysfunction in adulthood [3, 8] and often exhibit nystagmus, dysarthira, gait ataxia, and limb incordination [3, 8, 9]. Moreover, SCA8 is known to mimic other neurodegenerative diseases since it causes a wide spectrum of neurological abnormalities that are not restricted to cerebellar sings. For instance, SCA8 can involve cerebellar and pyramidal signs with bladder dysfunction; hence, SCA8 reportedly mimicked the cerebellar form of multiple system atrophy [10, 11]. A small number of SCA8 patients have parkinsonism as the main feature and may demonstrate clinical characteristics resembling those of progressive supranuclear palsy [12], corticobasal degeneration [5], or Parkinson’s disease [4, 13]. In addition to the pyramidal sign, lower motor neuron sign is a rare manifestation in this disorder. The disease reportedly mimicked amyotrophic lateral sclerosis when the pyramidal and lower motor neuron signs were present in a patient [4]. Other neurological manifestations include cognitive decline, psychiatric symptoms, and sensory disturbance [9]. As for involuntary movements, patients with SCA8 can have tremor (resting, kinetic, or postural tremor) [5, 12], myoclonus [14], or dystonia [5, 12]. The presence of chorea in the extremities and face was reported in a 58-year-old woman with SCA8 who also demonstrated extrapyramidal features and cognitive decline and had a younger sister developing chorea [15]. However, hemichorea without other physical abnormalities has not been reported as the clinical manifestation of SCA8.

Hemichorea is typically caused by a lesion in the subthalamic nucleus (STN); however, its neural basis has not been completely elucidated. The basal ganglia consist of the striatum, globus pallidus interna and externa (GPi and GPe), STN, and substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) and compacta. In a major hypothesis, three neural pathways are considered essential when processing inputs from the cerebral cortex to the basal ganglia [16] (Fig. 2a), i.e., the hyperdirect pathway (constituents, cortex–STN–GPi/SNr), direct pathway (cortex–striatum–GPi/SNr), and indirect pathway (cortex–striatum–GPe–STN–GPi/SNr). When a voluntary movement is performed, neural inputs from the cortex initially suppress the thalamus extensively through the hyperdirect pathway, subsequently evoking focal excitation of the thalamus via the direct pathway, and finally suppressing the thalamus extensively again via the indirect pathway. The focal excitation of the thalamus, which excites the cortex, is associated with the voluntary movement, whereas the extensive suppression of the thalamus is regarded a requirement for restraining unnecessary movements. Therefore, dysfunction of the hyperdirect and/or indirect pathways can lead to hyperkinesias (i.e., hemichorea; Fig. 2b).

Basic neural circuitry between the cerebral cortex and basal ganglia. The normal and hemichoreic conditions are presented in the panels a and b, respectively. To make a voluntary movement, neural inputs arising from the cerebral cortex initially suppress the thalamus extensively via the hyperdirect pathway (constituents, cortex–STN–GPi/SNr; a). Subsequently, using the direct pathway (cortex–striatum–GPi/SNr), the inputs focally disinhibit the thalamus, which is otherwise strongly restrained by GABAergic projections from the GPi/SNr. Finally, the inputs from the cortex suppress the thalamus extensively again through the indirect pathway (cortex–striatum–GPe–STN–GPi/SNr). Given that glutamatergic neurons in the thalamus project excitatory fibers to the cortex, the focal disinhibition of the thalamus evokes excitatory effects on the cortex and is associated with the voluntary movement; meanwhile, the extensive suppression of the thalamus serves to restrain unnecessary movements. Therefore, disruption of the hyperdirect and/or indirect pathways may lead to unnecessary movements (i.e., hemichorea; panel b indicates the hemichoreic condition due to STN damage, which is one of the most probable conditions in the presented case). The lesion is expressed with an X mark. The bold and broken lines indicate increased and decreased neural activity, respectively. In neuropathological studies examining patients with SCA8, neuronal loss was consistently observed in the Purkinje cell layer of the cerebellar cortex and substantia nigra [17]. It was also found in the inferior olivary nucleus, globus pallidus, putamen, and STN. Considering neural damage in the hyperdirect and/or indirect pathways can cause hemichorea [16, 18, 19], the neuronal loss in the globus pallidus, putamen, STN, or combination of these structures may have induced the hemichorea in our patient.

Another characteristic of this case is that the patient, in whom parkinsonism was not observed, exhibited decreased tracer uptake of the bilateral striata on DAT-SPECT. In the literature, most of the SCA subtypes are classified as multisystem disorders involving noncerebellar clinical and imaging findings [7]. Especially, nigrostriatal hypofunction was reportedly detected on DAT-SPECT in patients with SCA type 2 or SCA type 3 who had no parkinsonian signs [20, 21]. Nigrostriatal hypofunction was also described in patients with SCA8 who had DAT-SPECT abnormalities when they manifested parkinsonism [10, 12]. As neuronal loss in the substantia nigra is commonly observed in patients with SCA8 [17], it is reasonable that they demonstrate nigrostriatal hypofunction on DAT-SPECT.

The presented patient had mild cerebellar atrophy found on MRI in the absence of cerebellar signs. The typical MRI finding of SCA8 is cerebellar atrophy in patients with cerebellar symptoms [8]; in addition, the finding is also found in patients with atypical presentations, e.g., in those who had clinical features of progressive supranuclear atrophy [12], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [4], or Parkinson’s disease [4]. In our patient, the asymptomatic cerebellar atrophy provided a clue for suspecting the presence of SCA and led to the genetic testing. Genetic molecular testing for SCA8 may be beneficial when diagnosing a patient with chorea and asymptomatic cerebellar atrophy.

As described in previous articles, clinical significance of CTA/CTG repeat expansion in the ATXN8OS gene should be interpreted with caution because an individual with the repeat expansion does not necessarily develop relevant neurological abnormalities due to reduced penetrance [6]. Furthermore, the penetrance may reveal interfamilial differences for the same repeat length [6]. In our patient, one of the alleles had prolonged CTA/CTG expansion of 125 repeats, which was in the high penetrance range [6] and compatible with the repeat length found in the great majority of symptomatic SCA8 patients (i.e., 91–200 repeats) [3]. Although the mild cerebellar atrophy and nigrostriatal hypofunction found on MRI or DAT-SPECT did not cause clinically apparent neurological dysfunction, these abnormalities were in accord with findings previously reported in patients with symptomatic SCA8 and probably indicated subclinical abnormalities of this disorder. Therefore, it is considered that this patient developed hemichorea as the initial manifestation of SCA8.

In the presented case, the diagnostic process was more focused on SCAs than on HD and its phenocopies. The process was perhaps influenced by the frequency of neurodegenerative diseases causing chorea. In Japan, patients with dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy are often encountered as an SCA subtype that involves chorea, whereas HD is relatively rare. This local situation is considered to have affected the diagnostic process; therefore, in countries where HD is frequent, SCA8 should be remembered as one of HD phenocopies. As for the treatment in this case, haloperidol was administered to treat her hemichorea; however, atypical neuroleptics should have been used because typical neuroleptics have a higher risk of developing drug-induced parkinsonism than atypical neuroleptics. Another limitation of this report is the follow-up period was not sufficient in light of the slowly progressive nature of this disorder. The current patient would reveal gradual aggravation of the nigrostriatal hypofunction on DAT-SPECT and possibly develop parkinsonism in the long run.

This case report will provide a new piece of clinical information that is helpful when diagnosing patients with hemichorea and augment the notion that SCA8 causes a wide spectrum of neurological abnormalities. In clinical practice, we should remember that SCA8 can have hemichorea as the sole clinical manifestation in a patient with an unremarkable family history. Moreover, DAT-SPECT may display nigrostriatal dopamine hypofunction in patients with this disorder even if they do not have clinically apparent parkinsonism.
