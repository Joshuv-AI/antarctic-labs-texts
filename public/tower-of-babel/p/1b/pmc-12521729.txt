Recent advances in immunotherapy for breast cancer
Authors: Yi Guo, Gang Zheng, Yu Fan, Liangchang Li, Yang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer immunotherapy is now defined as enhancing the immune function of patients through active or passive methods, such as biological therapy, to achieve the goal of killing tumor cells. With increasing morbidity and mortality rates annually, breast cancer (BC) is the most diagnosed cancer in the world in 2023, surpassing lung cancer, colon cancer and stomach cancer. Immunotherapy plays an important role in treating BC and provides patients with other new options. Numerous studies have recognized the feasibility of immunotherapy in BC, and many innovative treatment methods have been proposed. In this article, we review the advances in and types of treatments for BC, especially the challenges and opportunities in integrating these therapies, a topic that has not been systematically explored in previous studies, with the aim of providing a reference for clinical treatment.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Immunotherapy, Breast cancer, Checkpoint, Monoclonal antibody, Targeted drugs

Introduction

According to 2023 U.S. cancer statistics, colorectal cancer, lung cancer and BC account for 52% of all new diagnoses in women, with BC having the highest incidence at 31% [1]. In addition, the incidence has increased by approximately 0.5% annually since the mid-2000s [2]. Although the mortality rate of BC has decreased by 41% since 1989, it is still the leading cause of cancer-related death in women [3–5]. Generally, early-stage breast cancer is removed by surgery, which is often combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy in later stages. However, the long-term prognosis of patients is still not very optimistic because of multiorgan metastasis, complex heterogeneity and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [6, 7]. There have been numerous case reports documenting lung, bone, and brain metastases after treatment [8–10]. Therefore, it is crucial for researchers to find more effective methods for treating BC and to further explore the clinicopathology of this disease.

Immunology has long been the source of many significant medical breakthroughs, from vaccines for infection to therapeutics for cancer. The use of immunotherapy in treating cancer has a long history (Fig. 1). As our understanding of the immune system has increased, researchers have discovered various methods of immunotherapy for tumors, generally including active immunotherapy and passive immunotherapy. Passive immunotherapy focuses on restoring the immune system’s ability to respond to cancer, whereas active immunotherapy aims to induce sustained tumor antigen-specific immune responses for prevention or treatment. In addition, the use of nonspecific stimuli such as adjuvants or biological response modulators, including cytokines, can further amplify the immune response against a tumor. Recently, targeting anticancer immunity in various tumors has become an effective method of anticancer therapy because of its low toxicity and high specificity [11–13]. By stimulating the immune system and restoring the immunosuppressive effect of immune cells on cancer cells in the TME, immunotherapy can transform “cold tumors” into “hot tumors”, promote the infiltration of immune cells, inhibit the growth of tumors, and generate specific immune memory. Breast cancer is considered a “cold tumor”. However, owing to the low response of breast cancer to immunotherapy, as well as the heterogeneous characteristics and complex pathogenesis of breast cancer, only a single immunotherapy has shown limited therapeutic effects in clinical treatment. For example, the immunogenicity of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is low; thus, the efficacy of immunotherapy alone or traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy is not very high. However, if pembrolizumab (a PD-1 inhibitor) combined with chemotherapy is administered, the overall survival rate of TNBC patients can be improved significantly [14]. In contrast, HER2+ BC, such BS with luminal B and HER2 overexpression, have good immune targets due to the presence of HER2. Therefore, some vaccines tend to utilize this foundation to improve their accuracy and reduce toxicity. In summary, the process of immunotherapy provides patients with a foundation for self-therapy and offers valuable insights into the process of treating cancer.

A brief history of tumor immunotherapy. From the first case of tumor immunotherapy in the 1890 s to recent advances in this area

In this review, we comprehensively discuss the recent advances in BC immunotherapy, providing an in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms of various immune cells, factors and immune checkpoints in BC. Specifically, we also discuss the effects of immunotherapy on the biological behavior of BC. Furthermore, we critically examine the profound impact of immunotherapy on the biological behavior, such as tumor invasion, metastasis and immune evasion of BC. Finally, we synthesize emerging trends and highlight unresolved challenges, offering forward-looking perspectives to guide the next generation of research and clinical translation in this rapidly evolving field.

Active immunotherapy in breast cancer

Active immunotherapy for cancer often refers to a tumor vaccine that utilizes tumor cell or tumor antigen substances to immunize BC patients and promote the body to actively resist and destroy tumor cells, thereby preventing the occurrence and development of tumors. Tumor vaccines include tumor cell-based vaccines, peptide/protein-based vaccines, and dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines (Fig. 2). In some types of BC, tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), such as HER2, are overexpressed, which provides favorable targets for active immunotherapy [15]. Owing to their accuracy and low cytotoxicity, tumor vaccines (often combined with passive immunotherapy) are considered a feasible way to induce tumor regression.

Methods of active immunotherapy

Tumor cell-based vaccines

Tumor cell-based vaccines have been shown to stimulate effective immune responses by providing the immune system with various TAAs of BC. Recent studies have focused mainly on the effects of vaccines on the tumor microenvironment (TME) [16, 17].

Dranoff et al. reported that modifying tumor cells through gene editing or other methods to secrete immune-stimulating cytokines and human granulocyte‒macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) into the TME can prevent tumor cells from further dividing and promote T-cell-mediated immune responses [18]. Additionally, combining a tumor cell vaccine with cyclophosphamide and adriamycin can induce a HER2-specific delayed hypersensitivity response (DHR) in BC patients [19, 20]. The vaccine presents good safety without dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) [17]. Although cyclophosphamide can prompt the immune system to produce antibodies targeting the vaccine, thus reducing the effectiveness of the vaccine, high doses of adriamycin inhibit the response described above [21]. In regard to clinical trials, Peethambaram et al. reported that lapeucel-T (APC8024) had satisfactory safety in a phase I trial of patients with metastatic refractory BC. Lapeucel-T is a type of cell-based active immunotherapy accompanied by GM-CSF, which can stimulate an immune response that targets HER2. Compared to other cancer management strategies, patients show good tolerance of this therapy. This trial is based on the level of HER2 expression among all groups in which a proliferative immune response was observed, which suggests that this therapy may be suitable for patients with expression of HER2 in whom passive immunotherapeutic approaches are not effective [22].

While HER2+ BC benefits from tumor cell-based vaccine strategies, TNBC remains a formidable challenge because of its immunologically ‘cold’ phenotype and lack of driver oncoproteins. Recent efforts have focused on exploiting TNBC-specific neoantigens derived from genomic instability or the overexpression of immunogenic proteins. For example, the sPD-1-expressing senescent tumor cell vaccine developed for TNBC attracts more mature dendritic cells and fewer depleted PD-1 T cells in vivo, indicating that it effectively induces anticancer and antitumor immune responses and can be used as a tool for the prevention and treatment of TNBC [23]. Moreover, tumor cell vaccines based on emerging technologies such as engineered exosomes have shown good antitumor activity in TNBC animal models and are expected to enter clinical research in the next step [24].

DC-based vaccines

DCs are considered the most effective antigen-presenting cells (APCs), expressing high levels of HLA I and II molecules, costimulating proteins, and producing cytokines necessary for T-cell activation, thereby stimulating immature T cells and memory T cells [25]. DCs can express BC antigens and modify them by fusing with BC cells, pulsing with peptides or translation.

Studies have shown that Th17-induced DC-based vaccines can stimulate effective CD4 T-cell-dependent antitumor immunity, thereby overcoming resistance to immune checkpoint blockade agents such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Specifically, IL-15 costimulation and p38-MAPK blockade can enable DC vaccines to quickly stimulate the body to produce antigen-specific Th17 cells, whereas Th17-DC vaccines can induce a unique T-cell-mediated cytokine network, including CCL6, CXCL1, and GM-CSF, and eliminate tumor cells, thereby extending the lifespan of mice. In addition, after the DC vaccine was administered to BC mice, the growth rate of the tumors decreased from eight to two times. However, the programmed cell death 1/programmed cell death legend 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) immune regulatory axis limits the activity of the vaccine, and studies have shown that blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway selectively enhances bone marrow immunity in the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that DC vaccines have favorable prospects in treating BC [26, 27]. Cauwels et al. restricted tumor progression by modifying type I interferons that target Clec9A + DCs, which increased their efficacy against target cells 1000-fold. This therapy has shown strong antitumor activity in a mouse model of breast cancer, with no side effects [28]. Currently, DC vaccines, such as NCT04348747, are still being evaluated in clinical trials. Huang et al. loaded the immunogenic cell death (ICD) inducer human neutrophil elastase (ELANE) and Hiltonol (a TLR3 agonist) into α-lactalbumin (α-LA) - engineered breast cancer - derived exosomes to form an in situ dendritic cell (DC) vaccine (HELA-Exos). The HELA-Exos were characterized using transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle flow cytometry, and Western blot analysis. The targeting ability, cytotoxicity, and immune activation of HELA-Exos were evaluated in vitro. The antitumor and immune - activating effects of HELA-Exos were explored in immunocompetent mice and patient - derived organoids. The results showed that HELA-Exos have a strong ability to specifically induce ICD in breast cancer cells. After ICD induction in cancer cells by HELA-Exos, exposure to tumor antigens and Hiltonol can activate type 1 conventional dendritic cells (cDC1) in situ and cross - prime tumor - reactive CD8 + T - cell responses, resulting in potent antitumor effects in tumor organoids derived from patients with poorly immunogenic triple - negative breast cancer (TNBC) [29]. These trials are designed mainly on the basis of DC vaccines targeting HER2 and HER3 combined with pembrolizumab, rintatolimod, celecoxib and IFN-α2b, which target HER2+ BC and brain metastasis of TNBC [30].

In conclusion, the treatment of DC-based vaccines for breast cancer is still focused mainly on their combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockers to increase their efficacy. From the perspective of breast cancer type, DC-based vaccines are similar to tumor cell-based vaccines, both of which are effective mainly for HER2+ BC, but further combined treatment is still needed for TNBC.

Peptide/protein-based vaccines

Peptide/protein-based vaccines use the short amino acid sequence of TAA to trigger targeted immune responses against malignant cells. Compared with whole-protein or attenuated live vaccines, these vaccines have multiple advantages, including ease of synthesis, specificity, good safety, and a reduced likelihood of triggering an autoimmune response. However, these vaccines also have limitations, such as poor immunogenicity, rapid degradation in the body, and immune evasion-related immunosuppressive problems.

At present, a relatively mature peptide vaccine for BC is nelipepimut-S, also known as NeuVax, which targets BC expressing HER2 and is suitable for patients with early HER2 1+ and 2+ BC, for whom the standard HER2 treatment is not satisfactory. The vaccine combines the E75 peptide in HER2/neu with GM-CSF as an adjuvant to enhance the immune response [31–33]. Mittendorf et al. administered 1000 mg of E75 and 250 mg of GM-CSF to patients monthly for 6 months. After 5 years of follow-up, the survival rate of vaccinated patients was 89.7%, whereas the survival rate of control patients was 80.2% (P = 0.08) [34–36]. According to recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating vaccines in phase I and phase II trials, there were significant differences in the number of CD8 + T cells and in the DHR between vaccinated patients and control patients. Although the incidence of adverse events in the vaccination group was low, E75 did not improve the overall survival rate [37, 38]. However, the latest phase III trial results revealed that at a median follow-up of 16.8 months, the incidence of adverse events was similar between the vaccine group and the placebo group, but no difference was observed in disease-free survival, indicating satisfactory tolerance of E75 but a lack of clinical benefits [33]. GP2 is another peptide-based vaccine that targets HER2+ BC and was subjected to a phase 2 adjuvant trial by Mittendorf et al. They found that although the survival rate in the GP2 vaccine group was not significantly different from that in the control group, the recurrence rate among HER2 + BC patients could be reduced to 0%, providing a new therapeutic perspective [34, 39–43]. In addition, another peptide/protein-based vaccine, AE37, was also designed for the intracellular domain of HER2. It includes a portion of the MHC-II-related invariant chain (Ii-Key) to facilitate molecular loading into the MHC-II channel and GM-CSF as an immune adjuvant [44, 45]. A clinical trial also revealed that it can induce an immune response effectively and has good safety in specific patients with BC [39, 40].

NeuVax, GP2, and AE37 represent peptide-based vaccines targeting HER2/neu in BC, yet they exhibit distinct clinical profiles. NeuVax, derived from the extracellular domain of HER2, combined with GM-CSF as an adjuvant, demonstrates promising 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rates in patients with HER2-low BC [46]. However, in its phase III trial, it failed to show significant improvement in DFS overall [47], highlighting limitations in treating HER2-overexpressing tumors due to immune tolerance. GP2, which targets the HER2 transmembrane domain, showed exceptional efficacy in HER2-high patients, resulting in 100% DFS at 5 years in a phase IIb trial [48]. However, its benefits were negligible in HER2-low cohorts, and its HLA-A2/A3 restriction limits broader applicability [49]. AE37, which incorporates an Ii-Key moiety to enhance CD4+ T-cell responses, exhibited modest clinical benefits in TNBC and HER2-low subgroups [50], although phase II trials revealed no significant improvement in overall survival [49]. Key limitations for all three include HLA-restricted eligibility, variable efficacy across HER2 expression levels, and tumor immune evasion mechanisms. NeuVax and GP2 face challenges in overcoming preexisting tolerance in HER2-high tumors, whereas the reliance of AE37 on CD4+ T-cell activation may require complementary strategies to bolster cytotoxic responses. Future research should focus on multiepitope vaccines to mitigate antigen escape, combination therapies with immune checkpoint inhibitors to reverse immunosuppression, and personalized approaches leveraging neoantigens or multitarget antigens. Enhancing vaccine platforms with novel adjuvants or delivery systems could improve immunogenicity. Additionally, expanding HLA compatibility and integrating biomarkers may refine patient stratification. These strategies aim to transform peptide vaccines from niche therapies into broadly effective tools in the evolving landscape of BC immunotherapy.

There has been relatively little research on peptide/protein-based vaccines, but on the basis of existing research, they are also applicable mainly to HER2+ BC.

DNA-based vaccines

A DNA-based vaccine, also known as a gene vaccine, represents a technology that integrates genes encoding tumor antigens into an expression vector (recombinant virus or plasmid DNA) using genetic engineering that is then directly injected as the vaccine into the body. With the help of the vector itself and gene expression in the body, the system expresses tumor antigens, thereby inducing a true cellular immune response to tumor antigens. Currently, the most commonly used viral vectors are the lentivirus (LV) vector, adenovirus (AdV) vector, and adeno-associated virus vector, the latter two of which are mostly used in the field of cancer treatment [51].

Notably, a viral vaccine recently discovered by Crosby et al. is currently undergoing phase II clinical trials (NCT03632941) and has made a significant contribution to the treatment of HER2+ BC. In this study, researchers reported that the use of the HER2Δ16 virus vaccine alone may produce antitumor immunity and stimulate the production of CD8+ T cells, but this effect is not long-lasting and has good therapeutic effects only on early-stage tumors. If the vaccine is used in combination with αPD-1, it can sustainably induce the functional gene expression characteristics of CD8+ T cells, thereby producing a sustained antitumor response. These findings also suggest that tumor vaccines should be combined with other therapies to improve their efficacy [52]. Shukla et al. evaluated the efficacy of plant virus nanoparticle (VNP) platform technology in the treatment and prevention of HER2+ BC and reported that once it enters the body, it produces an immune response against the HER2 protein. In preclinical studies, mice were implanted with HER2+ tumor cells and treated with a VRP-HER2 vaccine. Researchers have reported that vaccine administration can induce the production of HER2-specific T cells and antibodies while inhibiting tumor growth, ultimately leading to tumor regression in mice. In a subsequent phase I clinical trial, 22 patients with advanced HER2+ breast cancer were recruited for the VRP-HER2 trial. Researchers have reported that the VRP-HER2 vaccine can increase the number of HER2-specific memory CD8 T cells and has antitumor effects in preclinical and clinical studies [51, 53, 54].

Passive immunotherapy in breast cancer

Passive immunotherapy for tumors, also known as adoptive immunotherapy, typically involves passively delivering immune agents or cells with antitumor activity to patients with tumors to achieve the goal of treating tumors. At present, research on this topic can be divided into two parts: the treatment of tumors with monoclonal antibodies and adoptive cell therapy. Notably, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell technology has recently become the most popular therapeutic approach in this field. In addition, in recent years, basic and clinical research on other directions of passive immunotherapy for breast cancer has made rapid progress (Fig. 3).

Monoclonal antibody therapy for breast cancer

In monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapy for tumors, mAbs are used as carriers due to their high specificity to target local tumor lesions with cytotoxic substances, thus enabling specific killing of cancer cells. There are several types of medicines related to monoclonal antibodies.

Given that intratumoral drug delivery is a promising alternative to systemic pathways, several nanoscale immunotherapeutic agents have been developed for local administration. A9B5-Fc is a novel nanobody-based mAb with high affinity and nonoverlapping epitope recognition that targets domain I of HER2-ECD. This antibody can be used for patients with trastuzumab-resistant BC. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that after 4 h, A9B5-Fc was able to induce significantly higher internalization rates of HER2 than trastuzumab or pertuzumab. Concentration-dependent inhibition of cell viability was also observed after the internalization of A9B5-Fc. In addition, the combination of A9B5-Fc and trastuzumab has greater efficacy than does the combination of trastuzumab and pertuzumab [55, 56]. Chua et al. promoted the intratumoral delivery of two immunostimulatory monoclonal antibodies, OX40 and CD40, using nonfluid drug-plugging seeds (NDESs). The results showed that in a 4T1 in situ mouse cancer model (representing the TNBC class), the formulation was able to release drugs continuously into the TME, leading to an effective immune response [57, 58]. 2Mab-77-mG2a-f is another afucosylated anti-HER2 mAb. ELISA and flow cytometry showed a comparable dissociation constant (KD) to HER2, which means that it can effectively recognize HER2 with high affinity. Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) can also be activated by H2Mab-77-mG2a-f against HER2-positive cells, and the mAb can also inhibit the proliferation rate of cancer cells in mice, which means that it has considerable antitumor activity [59]. Suppan et al. reviewed the treatment methods for HER2+ BC and proposed the use of margetuximab to treat HER2+ BC. Compared with trastuzumab, this drug is an anti-Her2 antibody with a modified Fc domain that has a stronger binding affinity for activated CD16A receptors on immune cells. This drug is an anti-Her2 antibody with a modified Fc domain with a stronger binding affinity to activated CD16A receptors on immune cells compared to trastuzumab. However, its binding affinity to inhibitory Fc receptors on antigen-presenting cells is lower, indicating that it can lead to better immune responses. The SOPHIA trial revealed that the median progression-free survival of HER2+ BC patients under margetuximab treatment was 5.8 months, whereas the median progression-free survival under trastuzumab treatment was 4.9 months, indicating the superiority of this treatment [60].

In a phase 2 clinical MIMOSA trial, Geurts et al. attempted to treat metastatic HER2 + BC by using a combination of monalizumab and trastuzumab to release NK cells and CD8+ T cells. They treated HER2+ BC patients with trastuzumab and 750 mg of monalizumab every two weeks. However, the results revealed good treatment tolerance, no dose-limiting toxicity, and no objective response, indicating that the MIMOSA trial has not yet reached its primary endpoint [61]. The lack of efficacy in the MIMOSA trial may reflect the immunologically “cold” microenvironment of heavily pretreated HER2+ tumors and tumor-specific resistance to NKG2A blockade. While monalizumab enhances ADCC, its synergy with trastuzumab may require preexisting NK/CD8+ T-cell activity or complementary immune modulation. Future studies should integrate biomarkers and explore combinatorial strategies to overcome immune evasion. In addition, in the phase III clinical IMpassion050 trial, Huober et al. divided patients into atezolizumab/placebo groups and combined them with dense doses of doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide, followed by paclitaxel and PH. They reported that in the intensive-to-treat (ITT) population, the placebo group and atezolizumab group had pathological complete response (pCR) rates of 62.7% and 62.4%, respectively (P = 0.9551). Among PD-L1-positive BC patients, the pCR rates in the placebo group and atezolizumab group were 72.5% and 64.2%, respectively (P = 0.1846). In terms of safety, a total of 5 grade 5 adverse events occurred, and overall, its safety is consistent with that of atezolizumab reported in other joint studies. These results show that, compared with placebo, atezolizumab combined with the new adjuvant, dose-dense doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide paclitaxel and PH did not increase the pCR rate in ITT- or PD-L1-positive individuals with high-risk, HER2-positive early breast cancer [62]. The absence of a significant pCR benefit with atezolizumab in the IMpassion050 trial may stem from the unique interplay between HER2-positive tumor biology and immunotherapy dynamics. While a pCR is a validated surrogate for response to chemotherapy, its utility in assessing immune checkpoint inhibitors remains uncertain, as their benefits—such as immune memory or delayed antitumor activity—may emerge over longer follow-up periods [63]. Compared with triple-negative subtypes, HER2-positive tumors often exhibit lower baseline immune infiltration [64], potentially limiting PD-L1 inhibitor efficacy. Additionally, the dose-dense chemotherapy backbone, although effective for tumor debulking, may suppress T-cell proliferation, counteracting the immune activation of atezolizumab [65]. PD-L1 expression heterogeneity and the inherent immunomodulatory effects of HER2-targeted therapies could further obscure treatment differences [66]. Future studies should prioritize composite biomarkers and explore optimized chemoimmunotherapy sequencing to enhance synergy in this population. In addition, another study showed that the combination of trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and docetaxel had similar results in the treatment of HER2+ BC in Korea [67].

Adoptive cell therapy

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) involves isolating autologous or allogeneic immune effector cells, activating them in vitro and infusing them back, directly killing tumors or stimulating the antitumor immune response. This method can not only correct the state of cellular immune dysfunction and promote antitumor immune function but can also directly exert antitumor effects. Currently, research on treatment involves T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cells. This review focuses mainly on CAR-engineered T (CAR-T) cells, CAR-engineered NK (CAR-NK) cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and T-cell receptor (TCR)-engineered cells, which are classified by their different mechanisms [68].

CAR-T cells in breast cancer

CAR-T-cell therapy uses gene transfer technology to transduce the constructed CAR into T cells. The CAR consists of a costimulatory domain, a transmembrane domain, an antigen recognition domain and a signal domain. After these T cells are cultured in vitro to a certain number and returned to the body of patients, they can recognize efficiently disguised tumor cells [43, 69].

At present, CAR-T-cell therapy has developed to the fourth generation, and the fifth generation is still actively being developed, with the hope of solving the bottlenecks of the previous generations. In fourth-generation CAR-T-cell therapy, a nuclear factor of the activated T-cell (NFAT)-responsive cassette was added to the CAR construct, which enables it to activate the expression of some cytokines, such as IL-2, IL-5, and IL-12, in the TME. These cytokines can not only activate innate immune cells to kill cancer cells but also further increase T-cell proliferation and activate memory cells, which greatly reduces systemic toxicity and increases safety for BC patients [70]. Xia et al. reported that CAR-T cells can activate at least three signaling pathways in TNBC (Fig. 4): the granzyme-perforin-poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP), Fas-FADD-caspase, and IFN-γ signal transduction pathways. In BC cells, perforin can form a transmembrane pathway on the cell membrane, allowing granzymes to enter the cytoplasm. Human granzyme B can directly cleave key substrates, such as BH3-interacting domain death agonist (BID), PARP, and cysteine, to activate mitochondrial and DNA damage pathways. Western blot experiments also revealed that treatment of the BC cell line MDA-MB-231 with CAR-T cells could activate the Fas/FasL signaling pathway, leading to the activation of caspases and the cleavage of substrates such as PARP, resulting in tumor cell death. Similarly, CAR-T cells can express high levels of IFN, and IFNγ can induce Janus kinase 1 phosphorylation (pJAK1), signal transducer and transcriptional activator 1 phosphorylation (pSTAT1), and increased protein expression of interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1), leading to dephosphorylation of retinoblastoma protein (Rb) and inhibition of malignant progression of BC [71]. In addition, the STING agonists DMXAA and cGAMP can increase the efficacy of Th/Tc17 CAR-T cells, and the combined use of anti-PD-1 and anti-GR-1 mAbs can damage the DNA of BC cells, thereby achieving sustained tumor regression [72, 73]. Moreover, this therapy can effectively eliminate tumor immune suppression and improve the efficacy of traditional therapies such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy [74]. Adapter CAR-T cells (AdCAR-T cells) differentiate target antigen recognition from CAR-T cells by introducing adapter molecules and adapter CAR constructs. This therapy is different from traditional CAR-T-cell therapy, as it can reduce cell toxicity and improve flexibility and selectivity against heterogeneous tumors [75]. AdCAR-T cells have a significant therapeutic effect against TNBC. Currently, the first AdCAR-T cell therapy has entered clinical trials (NCT04230265), and TnMUC1-CAR-T-cell therapy for TNBC is also undergoing phase I clinical trials (NCT04025216).

By 2023, 8 CAR-T-cell products have been approved for marketing, indicating that their industry chain is approaching maturity. In addition, this therapy continuously optimizes production processes, shifting attention to automated production processes with less pollution and lower costs. The success of CAR-T-cell therapy in clinical practice also provides new strategies for treating patients with BC [76, 77].

CAR-NK cells in breast cancer

The construction of CAR-NK cells extends the approach of CAR-T-cell development. NK cells are derived mainly from peripheral blood, umbilical cord blood, and stem cells. The model is obtained by using viral or nonviral vectors to transduce the CAR construct into NK cells [78]. The main advantage of this therapy is that NK cells possess innate antitumor immunity without the need for prior sensitization and/or antigen presentation. In addition, allogeneic NK cell infusion does not induce graft-versus-host disease (GvHD), which is typically associated with allogeneic T-cell transplantation [79, 80].

Hu et al. designed and expressed tissue factor (TF)-targeting CAR monomers and dimers in NK cell lines. TF-CAR-NK cells can increase the expression of CAR1 and CD16, thereby killing TF-positive TNBC cells. In addition, they can also exhibit stronger cytotoxicity toward TNBC cells through the action of L-ICON ADCC [81]. Jan et al. utilized low-dose chemotherapy drugs to downregulate the expression of DNA methyltransferase 1, stimulating demethylation of the promoter of the antigen processing-related transporter 1 gene and thereby promoting the expression of human leukocyte antigen G (HLA-G) on the surface of tumor cells. Afterward, HLA-G-targeting CAR-NK cells could convert inhibitory signals into activation signals, triggering tumor cell death by downregulating the expression of phosphate-SHP-1 and increasing the expression of phosphate-Syk/Zap70 [82]. Kim et al. discovered an interesting phenomenon when combining NK cells with radiation therapy in which compared with radiation therapy, NK-cell therapy did not significantly reduce the volume of the primary tumor. However, in terms of systemic metastasis of the primary tumor, on the 71 st day after tumor injection, the xenograft tumor intensity in the group receiving NK cells combined with radiation therapy was 5 times lower than that in the group receiving radiation therapy alone [57, 83]. This phenomenon may indicate to an interesting mechanism that can be further discussed in the future. Additionally, Tao et al. used a combination of chimeric antigen CAR-NK cell-derived exosomes (ExoCAR) and a nanobomb to increase the effectiveness of antitumor treatment by disrupting ferroptosis-based defense mechanisms. Owing to the modification of transferrin receptor-binding peptide (T7) and CAR expression on the exosome surface, the nanoplatform can traverse the blood‒brain barrier and selectively target HER2 + breast cancer cells, thereby preventing the metastasis of BC to the brain [84].

At present, clinical trials involving anti-PD-L1 CAR-NK cells are also ongoing (NCT04847466, NCT02844335 and NCT02843126). These clinical trails were projected to contribute to the progress free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) of BC patients with HER2. Checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 have shown astonishing clinical efficacy against solid tumor cells, and they can also predict side effect characteristics on the basis of PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade [85–87].

CAR-T- and CAR-NK-cell therapies exhibit distinct characteristics in breast cancer immunotherapy. CAR-T cells, derived from autologous T cells, have potent tumor-cell-killing efficacy and long-term persistence. However, their application in treating solid tumors is significantly limited by severe toxicity, immunosuppression within the TME, and off-target risks due to antigen expression in healthy tissues [88]. In contrast, CAR-NK cells leverage innate cytotoxicity to avoid CAR-T-cell-associated toxicity, enabling allogeneic applications [89]. The ability of these cells to recognize tumor heterogeneity through NK receptors and adapt metabolically to hypoxic or adenosine-rich TMEs enhances their therapeutic potential. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of CAR-NK cells is hindered by their short in vivo persistence, low genetic engineering efficiency, and poor infiltration into dense tumor stroma. Both therapies face shared challenges in solid tumors, such as antigen heterogeneity, TME immunosuppressive factors, and physical barriers [90–92]. Current research emphasizes combinatorial strategies, including dual-target CAR designs and TME modulation, to overcome efficacy bottlenecks. Future advancements require optimizing cell durability and specificity to advance the practical application of these immunotherapies in BC.

TCR-T cells in breast cancer

TCR-T-cell technology uses lentiviruses as vectors to transduce specific TCRs, which are expressed on the surface of T cells. TCR-T cells and CAR-T cells are referred to as TCR redirection techniques for T cells, but their antigen recognition mechanisms differ significantly. TCR-T cells are absolutely dependent on the major histocompatibility complex (MHC). CAR-T cells can directly bind to tumor surface antigens, while TCR-T cells must be expressed through MHC α-β presentation and recognition of heterodimers. However, this characteristic means that TCR-T cells are not limited in their selection of tumor cell antigens, making them more suitable for the treatment of solid tumors [93, 94].

Compared with CAR-T cells, TCR-T cells have a lower affinity for TAAs; thus, research on TCR-T cells has focused mainly on improving their affinity for antigens. However, blindly pursuing high affinity can also cause many harmful side effects in the process of antitumor treatment [95]. The affinity between the TCR and peptide MHC is low, and their binding cannot induce a strong immune response. Zhao et al. reported that the construction of TCR catch bonds via engineering can prolong the duration of interaction between T cells and MHCs, improve T-cell responsiveness to ligand signals, and ensure moderate affinity levels, which suggests that it can be used to target Her2 and treat patients with Her2+ BC [96]. In a clinical trial, Wada et al. found that eribulin can increase the expression of HLA class I molecules in breast cancer cells and enhance the binding of TCR-T cells and MHC, thereby enhancing the cytotoxic T-cell response. Compared with eribulin or TCR-T cells alone, eribulin in combination with TCR-T cells significantly reduced the number of live target cells. These results indicate that the combination of azebrin and immunotherapy has synergistic effects and may be a promising treatment method for breast cancer patients [97].

In this context, clinical trials related to TNBC, such as ACT (NCT05483491) targeting TCR-T cells of KK-LC-1 and MAGE-A1 combined with atezolizumab (NCT04639245), are all in the enrollment stage [68, 98].

TILs in breast cancer

Unlike CAR-engineered TILs, TILs mainly originate from tumor tissue. TILs that can secrete IFN-γ from tissues can be extracted, cultured, and finally injected into the body of patients [99]. Compared with CAR-T cells and TCR-T cells, TILs are more suitable for the treatment of solid tumors. T cells that can identify surface antigens of cancer cells are crucial for the use of TILs to attack tumors.

Because of the low immunogenicity of some metastatic breast cancers (especially TNBC), the effects of traditional treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, are poor. Therefore, some studies have focused on TILs. In a clinical study, Zacharakis et al. selected 42 breast cancer patients to isolate TILs from their tumor fragments, and pembrolizumab was used to adopt and transfer the selected new antigen-specific TILs. After 66 months of treatment, some patients experienced complete resolution of liver, lymph node, and mediastinal metastases, and subsequently, only surgery was needed to control the condition [100]. In addition, TILs can also predict the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) in breast cancer patients according to their molecular subtype. Western blot experiments have shown that high TIL counts can improve the tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy and are associated with increased long-term survival in HER2-overexpressing patients and TNBC patients. Moreover, there was a statistically significant correlation (p < 0.05) between interstitial TILs and tumor grade, tumor size, the number of distant metastases, and molecular subtype, whereas intratumoral TILs were significantly correlated with tumor grade, tumor size, distant metastasis, molecular subtype, staging, and lymph node metastasis (p < 0.05) [101, 102].Recently, Loi et al. the relationship between the quantity and location of TIL and the clinical feature of the BC. The study contains 2009 lymph node-positive breast cancer samples from the BIG 02–98 adjuvant therapy phase III trial. The outcomes show that in the global or estrogen receptor (ER) positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) negative population, there is no significant prognostic correlation. However, for every 10% increase in TILs and intratumoral infiltration, the risk of recurrence decreases by 17% and 15%, respectively, while the risk of death in ER negative/HER2 negative breast cancer decreases by 27% and 17%. This suggests that increased TIL infiltration is associated with a favorable prognosis in lymph node-positive, ER negative/HER2 negative breast cancer. Nevertheless, further validation of the clinical utility of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in this context is still necessary [103].

At present, clinical trials of the combination of ACT and TIL therapy for TNBC are in the recruitment stage for volunteers (NCT04842812 and NCT04111510), indicating the broad prospects of this therapy [100, 104].

Therefore, adoptive cell therapy is applicable mainly to refractory types of breast cancer, such as TNBC, because it can increase the expression of specific cytokines, thereby improving the ability of immune cells to kill TNBC cells. TILs are especially suitable for treating this type of breast cancer. However, this therapy is currently in the clinical trial stage and still requires a combination of multiple methods for treatment. We believe that in the near future, this therapy will have wider and more effective clinical applications.

Nonspecific Immunomodulatory therapy in breast cancer

There are two main antitumor mechanisms of nonspecific immune modulators: one involves stimulating effector cells, such as cytokines, to exert their effects; the other is to act by inhibiting immune-negative regulatory cells or molecules, such as CTLA-4 blockers and PD-1/PD-L1 blockers [105, 106]. The former, as mentioned earlier, often combines with tumor vaccines and ACTs, such as GM-CSF in tumor cell-based vaccines and IL-2, IL-5 and IFN-γ in CAR-T-cell therapies. In this section, we turn our attention to negative immune regulatory inhibitors, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA-4 inhibitors.

PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in breast cancer

The interaction between programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) can suppress immune responses, thereby enabling tumors to evade immune surveillance. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors can regulate immunosuppressive cells and enhance the immune response. At present, the FDA has approved multiple such drugs, including pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab, and ipilimumab. As mentioned earlier, pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy has a significant therapeutic effect on TNBC [107]. In fact, currently, this type of drug is used mainly for TNBC [108].

The KEYNOTE-012 clinical study investigated 111 patients with TNBC, and the results showed that if PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, especially pembrolizumab, were administered at a dosage of 10 mg/kg every two weeks, the overall response rate (ORR) was 18.5%. If the administration frequency was reduced to 10 mg/kg every three weeks, the ORR decreased by 5.7% [109, 110]. In a phase 1b clinical trial, Dirix et al. used a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor called avelumab to treat TNBC and reported an ORR of 22.2% in PD-L1-positive TNBC patients. Importantly, the safety of avelumab can be adjusted. Currently, many clinical studies are being conducted to explore the combination of avelumab and novel treatment strategies to improve favorable outcomes associated with monotherapy [111].

Although PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have significant therapeutic effects in patients with TNBC, their monotherapeutic effect is still very limited. Crosby et al. studied the combined effects of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and other immune therapies in HER2+ BC. By performing single-cell RNA sequencing on T cells isolated from the TME of HER2+ BC patients, researchers demonstrated that the addition of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors can eliminate an effective exhaustion gene present in TILs after vaccination alone [52]. Du et al. used BALB/c mice as a TNBC model and found that the tumor weight of mice receiving double-blocking therapy with LAG-3 and PD-1/PD-L1 was significantly lower than that of mice receiving either therapy alone [112]. Clinical studies have also revealed that LAG-3 inhibitors can significantly overcome resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [113]. In addition to use with dual immune checkpoint inhibitors, pembrolizumab can be used to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy. Bardia et al. divided TNBC patients into two groups: those receiving pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy and those receiving a placebo combined with chemotherapy. The pathological complete response rates were 64.8% and 51.2%, respectively (P < 0.05) [114].

Angiogenesis often hinders the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, leading to immune suppression. Therefore, a new strategy focused on PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors is to inhibit angiogenesis within the TME. Wu et al. reported that the objective response rate of 48 patients with advanced TNBC was 81.3% and that the median progression-free survival was 13.6 months when the angiogenic inhibitor famitinib combined with the anti-PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor carelizumab was used [115, 116].

CTLA-4 inhibitors in breast cancer

CTLA-4 is a key inhibitory receptor on T cells. After T-cell activation, CTLA-4 signaling negatively regulates the immune response, leading to immune suppression.

After many studies, ipilimumab was the first CTLA-4 inhibitor approved by the FDA for tumor treatment. At present, clinical trials evaluating CTLA-4 inhibitors for HER2+ breast cancer are ongoing [51, 109, 117]. In basic studies, Gao et al. proposed the use of a combination of a recombinant oncolytic virus and an anti-CTLA-4 antibody for the targeted treatment of HER2+ BC. This method mainly belongs to the category of viral DNA vaccines, and compared with virus therapy alone, which can prolong survival, this therapy can cure most HER2+ BC patients [118]. A similar study conducted by Liu et al. combined a CTLA-4 inhibitor with mRNA vaccines to increase the effectiveness of treatment for BC patients. In vivo studies have shown that NP-based mRNA vaccines targeting the mannose receptor on DCs can successfully express tumor antigens in the DCs of the lymph nodes. NP vaccines can induce a robust antigen-specific cytotoxic T lymphocyte response against TNBC 4T1 cells. Compared with the use of a vaccine or monoclonal antibodies alone, combination immunotherapy with a CTLA-4 inhibitor significantly enhances the antitumor immune response. These data support the use of NPs as a delivery vector for mRNA vaccines, as well as the potential combined immunotherapy of NP-type mRNA vaccines with CTLA-4 inhibitors for TNBC [119]. In addition, Vonderheide et al. tested the combination of the CTLA-4 blocking antibody tremelimumab and exemestane in 26 ER+ BC patients but reported only limited signs of clinical activity. The best overall response was that 42% of patients had stable disease for at least 12 weeks. Interestingly, in most patients, the therapeutic effect was usually associated with an increase in the number of peripheral T cells expressing ICOS, resulting in a significant increase in the ratio of ICOS+ T cells to FoxP3+ Tregs, which may be secondary to the immune activation of CTLA-4 [120]. In a recent preliminary study on preoperative breast cancer patients, the combination of single dose of iprimma (anti-CTLA-4) with cryoablation showed good intratumoral and systemic immune effects, providing a new direction for the treatment of breast cancer [121, 122].

At present, few studies on CTLA-4 inhibitors in breast cancer exist, and most of them have focused on HER2+ BC, which also provides new perspectives on treating breast cancer.

Conclusion

The development of tumor immunotherapy provides new insights into treating cancer. From active immunity to passive immunity and then to nonspecific immune modulators, each type of therapy has its own advantages for treating different types of BC. However, combination therapy still has the best effect, whether it is combination therapy within immunotherapies or immunotherapy in combination with traditional or new therapies. Although vaccine monotherapy has shown unique potential in some clinical trials, its efficacy is still limited by the influence of tumor heterogeneity and the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Therefore, combined treatment strategies have become important for overcoming current bottlenecks. Several studies have shown that the combination of vaccines and ICIs can enhance antitumor effects through synergistic mechanisms. Moreover, in situ vaccine technology, combined with systemic immune modulation, can reshape the TME and induce systemic antitumor responses [123, 124]. Future studies are needed to further optimize the timing and dose of combination therapy. Moreover, the development of personalized vaccines based on neoantigens is expected to accurately cover the heterogeneous antigens generated by tumor clonal evolution, whereas universal vaccines may provide convenient immunoprophylaxis for a wide range of people. In summary, through the integration of multimodal treatment strategies and individualized vaccine design, breast cancer immunotherapy is expected to achieve comprehensive improvements from local control to systemic immune memory.

BC cells can evade immune system surveillance through various mechanisms, which is the main mechanism by which most patients develop cancer. Therefore, immunotherapy is very important in its treatment process. If active immunotherapy intervention is carried out in the early stages of the disease and combined with other traditional therapies, it can significantly improve the prognosis of patients. Active immunotherapy for tumors, namely, tumor vaccines, is mainly suitable for HER2+ BC patients because of their high immunogenicity, whereas TNBC requires combination therapy because of its lack of corresponding antigens, and the effect is often poor. Monoclonal antibodies are mainly combined with multiple therapies. In terms of adoptive cell therapy, clinical trials of therapies based on CAR-T cells and TILs targeting TNBC are currently in the recruitment stage, and it is believed that these approaches will have a wide range of clinical applications in the future. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are mainly used to target TNBC and require combination with traditional chemotherapy to achieve better prognostic outcomes.

However, immunotherapy for BC also faces many challenges. In active immunotherapy, the targeting and antigen expression of tumor vaccines are sometimes low, and the immunogenicity cannot be fully utilized. In passive therapy, although CAR-T cells have good affinity for tumor cells, their therapeutic effect against solid tumors is limited. Although TCR-T cells are relatively effective against solid tumors, their affinity is low. There is currently an urgent need to improve the affinity of TCR-T cells appropriately and curb their side effects. Currently, more monoclonal antibody-related drugs are becoming available, as we have mentioned. We believe that with such rapid development, immunotherapy for tumors will improve patient survival and bring more hope to BC patients.
