The effect of exogenous mitochondria in enhancing the survival and volume retention of transplanted fat tissue in a nude mice model
Authors: Haoran Li, Zhengyao Li, Xiaoyu Zhang, Yan Lin, Tongtong Zhang, Leijuan Gan, Dali Mu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the pivotal role of fat grafting in plastic, reconstructive, and aesthetic surgery, inconsistent survival rates of transplanted adipose tissue, primarily due to early ischemic and hypoxic insults, remain a significant challenge. The infusion of healthy mitochondria has emerged as a promising intervention to support tissue recovery from ischemic, hypoxic, and other types of damages across various organ systems.ObjectivesThis study aims to evaluate the impact of supplementing human adipose tissue grafts with healthy exogenous mitochondria on their volume and mass retention rates when transplanted into the subcutaneous layers of nude mice. This approach seeks to improve and optimize fat grafting techniques.MethodsHuman adipose tissues were preconditioned with exogenous mitochondria (10 µg/mL), a combination of exogenous mitochondria and the inhibitor Dyngo-4a, Dyngo-4a alone, or PBS, and then transplanted into the subcutaneous tissue of 24 nude mice. Samples were harvested at 1 and 3 months post-transplantation for analysis of mass and volume retention. The structural morphology and integrity of the adipose tissues were assessed using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining.ResultsMitochondrial preconditioning significantly enhanced the retention of mass and volume in fat grafts, demonstrating superior structural morphology and integrity compared to the control group.ConclusionsThis study highlights the potential of exogenous mitochondrial augmentation in fat transplantation to significantly improve fat graft survival, thereby optimizing the success of fat grafting procedures.Keywords: Exogenous mitochondria, Fat grafting, Hypoxic injury, Mitochondria transplantation

Background

Autologous fat grafting has become an essential technique in plastic and reconstructive surgery, offering numerous advantages such as excellent tissue compatibility, the potential for long-term results, simplicity of procedure, low cost, minimal invasiveness, and a low rate of complications. Additionally, the harvesting of subcutaneous adipose tissue for repair and reconstruction can simultaneously improve body contour [1–3]. Initially, autologous fat grafting was primarily utilized to fill soft tissue deficits [4]. However, its applications have expanded to include breast augmentation, facial rejuvenation, scar treatment, wound healing enhancement, and body contouring [5–9]. Despite its wide adoption, the process of fat graft absorption remains inevitable, with retention rates varying significantly from 25-70% [10]. This unpredictable volume retention rate has limited its broader application. The low oxygen and nutrient environment during ex vivo purification and the early ischemic and hypoxic conditions post-transplantation contribute to significant adipocyte death and fat tissue necrosis. Studies have shown that mature adipocytes are particularly sensitive to ischemic and hypoxic damage, with cell death occurring as early as the first day of ischemia [11]. Therefore, improving the survival rate of mature adipocytes post-transplantation is crucial for enhancing the efficacy of this technique.

As the most complex and crucial organelles in eukaryotic cells, mitochondria provide the essential energy required for cellular activities [12]. The essence of ischemic, hypoxic injury, and energy depletion in organ tissues is fundamentally due to mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to cellular energy deficit [13]. Therefore, a sufficient number of high-quality, healthy mitochondria are necessary to maintain tissue homeostasis and cell survival. Recent studies have confirmed the role of intercellular mitochondrial transfer in sustaining homeostasis and cell function. The phenomenon of organelle transfer between mammalian cells through tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) was first documented in 2004 [14]. Following this, the process of mitochondrial transfer from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to mammalian cells was elucidated in 2006 [15].The transfer of healthy mitochondria has shown significant potential in rescuing tissues from ischemic and hypoxic injuries across various organ systems. For instance, healthy mitochondria from astrocytes can be transferred to damaged neurons, rescuing them from hypoxic injury and promoting neural recovery in a mouse stroke model [16]. The transplantation of healthy mitochondria from autologous pectoral muscles to ischemic cardiac tissue has demonstrated significant cardiovascular protective effects, notably reducing the area of myocardial infarction in rabbit models of heart attack [17]. Mitochondria can be delivered to target cells either through stem cells or by directly using isolated exogenous mitochondria [18]. The latter method, known globally as Artificial Mitochondria Transfer (AMT), bypasses the ethical concerns associated with live cells transplantation and allows for precise control over dosage, transfer rates, and treatment standardization, offering significant advantages over cell-assisted transplantation [19]. Building on these principles, this study aims to explore the application of exogenous mitochondria in fat grafting, with the goal of enhancing graft survival and improving overall surgical outcomes.

Methods

Patients

The fat used for the study was sourced from patients who underwent liposuction at our facility between December 2022 and May 2023.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they were aged 18 or older and had undergone liposuction.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had a history of tumor diseases, systemic diseases, or drug or alcohol abuse.

Fat harvest

Patients underwent routine anesthesia and disinfection procedures. 3 mm incision were made on each side of inguinal area. Tumescent fluid (0.04% lidocaine with 1:1,000,000 adrenaline) was used for abdominal liposuction area tissue swelling through a water-assisted liposuciton system. Fat tissue was evenly extracted from both deep and superfical sucutaneous layers. Then, the extracted fat tissue was purified by standing 15 min. The purified fat tissue was then prepared for further experiments.

Assessment method

The harvested fat was evaluated for purity and viability prior to transplantation. The assessment involved centrifuging the fat to separate viable adipocytes from other components such as blood, oil, and debris. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Isolation and identification of adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs)

The fat particles obtained from liposuction were aliquoted into 50 ml centrifuge tubes, each containing 10-20 ml. They were washed with PBS solution, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and washed three times to thoroughly remove impurities such as oil, blood, and tumescent fluid. An equal volume of 0.2% type I collagenase was added to the thoroughly washed adipose tissue and digested in a constant temperature shaker set at 37 °C and 120 bpm for 45 min. After digestion, an equal volume of low-glucose DMEM complete medium was added to fully neutralize the collagenase and stop the digestion. The adipose tissue was filtered through a 200 μm mesh screen, then centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The cell pellet at the bottom of the centrifuge tube, containing adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), was retained. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in medium. The cell suspension was then passed through a 100 μm mesh to remove oil and fibrous strands, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Finally, the cells were resuspended in a complete culture medium specifically designed for ADSCs. After counting with a cell counter, approximately 1 × 106 cells were seeded into a 10 cm diameter culture dish, constituting the primary cells (P0). These were then incubated in a cell culture incubator [20, 21].

ADSCs growth and morphology were meticulously assessed on a daily basis. Upon reaching approximately 70–90% confluency, the cells were deemed ready for passaging. After the cells reached the 3rd passage (P3), a comprehensive trilineage differentiation protocol was employed, encompassing adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic pathways [22]. This differentiation process is crucial for validating the multipotency of ADSCs, indicating their capability to differentiate into multiple cell types, which is a defining characteristic of stem cells.

Isolation of exogenous mitochondria

To isolate mitochondria from passaged human ADSCs (P3), the cells were first washed three times with PBS, followed by enzymatic digestion using trypsin. Subsequently, the cells are centrifuged at 800 g for 5 min to facilitate collection and enumeration. For each isolation, a cell count of 2–5 × 107 is required. Cells were then resuspended in 1 ml of ice-cold Lysis Buffer. This suspension is transferred to a small-volume glass homogenizer and subjected to grinding on ice for 30–40 passes to ensure thorough lysis. The homogenized cell lysate was then transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 4℃ and 1,000 g for 5 min to separate the lysate from debris, with the supernatant being carefully transferred to a new centrifuge tube. This process was repeated under the same conditions to further clarify the lysate. Subsequently, the clarified lysate was centrifuged at 4℃ and 12,000 g for 10 min to pellet the mitochondria. The supernatant was discarded, and the mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in 0.5 ml Wash Buffer. This suspension was then centrifuged at 4℃ and 1,000 g for 5 min, with the supernatant again being discarded. A final centrifugation at 4℃ and 12,000 g for 10 min yielded a high-purity mitochondrial pellet at the bottom of the tube [23, 24].

The mitochondrial pellet was then resuspended in 50–100µL of Store Buffer or PBS, depending on the requirements of the subsequent experiments. Following mitochondria isolation, it is critical to assess the concentration of mitochondrial proteins. The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay is commonly employed for this purpose due to its sensitivity and reliability in detecting protein concentrations. Additionally, the presence and integrity of mitochondria within the suspension can be confirmed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Experimental animals and grouping conditions

Twenty-four female BALB/c nude mice, aged between 6 and 8 weeks, were used in this study (Beijing Weitonglihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China). All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with ad libitum access to food and water. The animal study protocols were reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee of our institution, ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines for animal research. The animals were allocated into four groups based on different intervention conditions: Group A, the mitochondria group (10ug/ml); Group B, mitochondria + Dyngo-4a; Group C, Dyngo-4a; and Group D, PBS.

Anesthesia for the nude mice was administered using of 1% Pentobarbital Sodium, with a dosage of 0.2 ml per mouse via intraperitoneal injection. The liposuctioned human fat was thoroughly mixed with the interventions specific to each group before being transplanted subcutaneously into the nude mice. Each mouse received injections at four different sites, each representing one of the four groups, with 0.3 ml of the mixed adipose tissue injected at each site. To minimize individual variability and eliminate differences due to the injection site, the injection sites on the dorsum of the mice were standardized in a clockwise direction, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Twelve mice were euthanized at 1 month and another twelve at 3 months post-transplantation to measure the mass and volume of each sample, enabling a comparative analysis of the effects of different interventions on the retention and integration of transplanted fat tissue. The nude mice were euthanized using CO2 anesthesia to minimize animal suffering. During the allocation and experiment stages, only the researchers and experimenters were aware of the group assignments. During the outcome assessment and data analysis stages, each participant was informed of their experimental group assignment.

Volume and mass retention rate

The volume and mass of the transplanted fat were measured using the displacement method and a balance scale, respectively [25].

Histopathologic evaluation

After obtaining the fat samples, they are fixed in a fixative solution specially designed for adipose tissue for 24 h. Following fixation, the samples were dehydrated in ethanol and then embedded in paraffin. Each paraffin-embedded specimen was stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) to facilitate microscopic examination of the tissue architecture. Images of the H&E-stained slides were captured using an automated slide scanning system and could be observed and analyzed with CaseViewer digital slide reading software (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). This process allowed for the assessment of the integrity of adipocyte structures within the fat grafts.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). For comparisons across multiple groups, one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Results

Identification of ADSCs

To ensure the purity of ADSCs, the extracted ADSCs were progagated to the third passage (P3) before proceeding with subsequent experiments. At P3, ADSCs demonstrated the ability to form cell colonies, some of which displayed ring-like or vortex patterns (Fig. 2a). Differentiation assays were conducted using P3 ADSCs to evaluate their potential for adipogenesis, osteogenesis, and chondrogenesis. After approximately 3–4 weeks of adipogenic induction, Oil Red O staining revealed clustered red droplets, indicating successful differentiation into mature adipocytes (Fig. 2b). For osteogenic induction, which spanned 2–4 weeks, Alizarin Red staining showed sheet-like red osteogenic nodules, confirming that the ADSCs had differentiated into osteoblasts with calcium ion deposits (Fig. 2c). Following 3–4 weeks of chondrogenic induction, chondrocyte aggregates were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with Alcian Blue, which displayed light blue cell clusters, demonstrating successful differentiation into chondrocytes capable of secreting acidic polysaccharides (Fig. 2d).

3rd Passage ADSCs and the Trilineage Induction. a: 3rd Passage ADSCs; b: Adipogenic induction; c: Osteogenic induction; d: Chondrogenic induction

Identification of exogenous mitochondria

Mitochondria extracted from ADSCs were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C overnight, followed by ultrathin sectioning and observation under a transmission electron microscope (TEM) to confirm the presence of mitochondria. TEM analysis revealed that the mitochondrial suspension contained a high quantity of exogenous mitochondria with high purity (Fig. 3). Some morphological alterations in the mitochondria were observed, likely due to the extraction and embedding processes for sectioning.

Exogenous mitochondria observed through transmission electron microscopy

Volume and mass retention rate

Samples were collected 1 and 3 months post-transplantation. After euthanizing the nude mice under CO2 anesthesia, a dorsal midline incision was made to harvest the transplanted fat (Fig. 4). The volume and mass of the transplanted fat in each group were measured, and the retention rates of volume and mass were calculated.

Different letters (A, B, C, D) correspond to different groups as follows: A: Exogenous mitochondria + fat group; B: Exogenous mitochondria + Dyngo 4a + fat group; C: Dyngo 4a + fat group; D: PBS + fat group

The results indicated that the volume and mass retention rates of the transplanted fat in the mitochondria group were significantly higher than those in the other groups at both 1 month and 3 months, with statistically significant differences. No significant differences were observed among the other three groups (Figs. 5 and 6). Specifically, in the mitochondria group, the volume retention rate at 1 month was 82.22%, and the mass retention rate was 77.22%. At 3 months, the volume retention rate was 63.33%, and the mass retention rate was 62.50%. Detailed measurements of volume and mass for each group are presented in Table 1.

H&E staining

H&E staining was performed on fat grafts at 1 and 3 months to assess and compare the structural integrity of adipocytes across different groups. The results demonstrated that, compared to the other groups, the mitochondria group exhibited superior adipocyte integrity, with a notable reduction in cyst and vacuole formation, as well as significantly lower levels of inflammation and tissue fibrosis, differences that were statistically significant. No significant statistical differences were observed among the other groups (Figs. 7, 8 and 9; Table 2).

Discussion

Fat grafting has revolutionized the correction of both congenital and acquired soft tissue deficits, simplifying treatments and establishing itself as a key procedure in plastic surgery. However, the high rate of fat absorption post-operation remains a significant challenge.To maximize the volume retention rate of transplanted fat, Coleman et al. [26] first introduced a standardized fat handling procedure in the 1980s, which includes fat harvesting, purification, processing of the lipoaspirate, and fat injection. This laid the foundation for substantial methodological advancements aimed at enhancing graft viability, such as the incorporation of adrenaline in tumescent fluids, refinement of fat harvesting and purification techniques, and the adoption of micrografting practices. Despite these technical enhancements, achieving a consistent and satisfactory survival rate for fat grafts remains elusive.

Fat absorption following transplantation is primarily due to ischemic and hypoxic injury, which leads to cellular damage and subsequent cell death [27]. Mitochondria, the most complex and essential organelles in eukaryotic cells, provide the energy necessary for cellular activities. The root cause of ischemic and hypoxic tissue damage, as well as energy depletion, lies in mitochondrial dysfunction, which triggers cellular energy deficits [28]. Therefore, maintaining an adequate number of proper functioning mitochondria is crucial for tissue homeostasis and cell survival.

Recent studies have highlighted the role of mitochondrial transfer between cells in preserving homeostasis and cellular functions. The protective effects of mitochondrial transfer against cell damage have been demonstrated in various systems, including neurological, cardiovascular, and respiratory system [15, 29–31]. This transfer can occur through stem cells or directly via isolated exogenous mitochondria [18]. However, introducing exogenous cells directly into damaged tissues raises ethical concerns, along with challenges related to uncontrollable survival rates, transfer efficiency, and potential immune rejection. In contrast, transplanting isolated exogenous mitochondria circumvents the ethical issues associated with live cell transplantation and offers controlled therapeutic dosage and transfer rates, presenting significant advantages over cell-based transplantation [19]. Therefore, we pioneered the use of exogenous mitochondrial transplantation in the field of plastic surgery to investigate its impact on fat grafting, representing a significant step forward in enhancing graft viability and addressing the challenge of postoperative fat absorption.

Research suggests that the primary mechanism by which exogenous mitochondria enter target cells is likely through endocytosis [18, 32]. Dyngo-4a, a structural analog of Dynasore, is an improved, low cytotoxicity, and universal dynamin inhibitor that can suppress cellular endocytosis [33, 34]. To explore the mode of mitochondrial transfer, we established a mitochondria + Dyngo-4a control group. Our study compared the volume and mass retention rates of transplanted fat across all groups and conducted H&E staining. The findings revealed that the mitochondria group achieved the best transplantation outcomes at both 1 month and 3 months post-transplantation. The H&E staining results indicated that mitochondria reduced cyst and vacuole formation in the transplanted fat, thereby enhancing the integrity of adipocytes. In the groups treated with mitochondria + the inhibitor Dyngo-4a, as well as those treated solely with the inhibitor Dyngo-4a or PBS, no significant differences were observed in the volume and mass retention rates of the transplanted fat. Furthermore, the integrity of adipocytes showed no notable differences across these groups. Therefore, the inhibitor Dyngo-4a effectively reduces the entry of exogenous mitochondria into adipocytes without significantly impacting the survival of the transplanted fat.

Recent advancements in regenerative medicine, including stromal vascular fraction (SVF), conditioned media, exosomes, and others, have shown considerable promise in enhancing tissue repair and regeneration [35–40]. These medicine also hold significant potential for fat grafting applications [41, 42]. While these regenerative strategies improve the extracellular environment and provide additional cellular support, targeting a potential fundamental cause of graft failure—mitochondrial dysfunction—could offer a more focused and effective solution. Our findings suggest that mitochondrial transplantation has the potential to enhance the survival of transplanted fat by addressing ischemic and hypoxic stress at the cellular level, thereby reducing absorption. Unlike traditional regenerative medicine that mainly focus on modifying the extracellular environment, the infusion of exogenous, healthy mitochondria directly into adipocytes offers a noel cellular-level intervention. This approach may replace compromised mitochondria within cells, thereby restoring energy metabolism and potentially increasing resistance to ischemic and hypoxic stress.

While our study provides promising evidence for the benefits of mitochondrial transplantation, several limitations remain. Future research should explore the potential dose-response relationship of mitochondrial concentration on fat graft survival and work to identify the optimal conditions for maximum efficacy. Additionally, the precise pathway for mitochondrial transfer remains unclear; while endocytosis is a likely method, further verification is necessary. Most importantly, while we have preliminarily validated the beneficial effects of mitochondria on transplanted fat in vivo, the involved signaling pathways and specific mechanisms are still not fully understood. For instance, it remains uncertain whether the positive effects are mediated through enhanced angiogenesis. This question forms a key focus of our ongoing research, and we hope to update our findings promptly.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that exogenous mitochondrial transplantation significantly improves the outcomes of fat grafting, though the precise mechanisms underlying these effects require further exploration. This pioneering application of mitochondrial transplantation in plastic surgery opens new avenues for enhancing the efficacy of fat grafting procedures and provides promising therapeutic strategies for addressing a wide range of challenges in the field.
