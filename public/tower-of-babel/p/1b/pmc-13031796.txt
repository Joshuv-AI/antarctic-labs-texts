Synergistic dye removal through curcumin functionalized MCM-22 zeolite as a photocatalyst nanocomposite via the simultaneous photocatalysis and adsorption method
Authors: Elaheh Shadi, Mehdi Amirinejad, Ali Ashraf Derakhshan, Hooman Rezaie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the efficiency of simultaneous adsorption/photocatalyst system for dye removal. A novel composite material consisting of curcumin functionalized MCM-22 zeolite, titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and cerium nanoparticles was utilized. TiO₂ nanoparticles were layered by reacting zeolite with a titanium butoxide solution, followed by cerium incorporation using cerium nitrate. FESEM, TEM, BET, FT-IR, XRD, DRS, PL, Zeta potential, and TGA analyses were performed. The performance of the composite material was evaluated in a slurry photocatalyst reactor under visible light. Operating parameters, including cerium concentration, photocatalyst loading, initial contaminant concentration, and pH were varied to determine the reaction rate and total contaminant removal. The performance of the simultaneous adsorption and photocatalytic reaction for methylene blue dye removal was evaluated and compared with individual processes. The composite material’s reusability was tested over five cycles. Dye removal efficiencies of 11%, 32%, and 96% were achieved for the photocatalytic process, adsorption process, and the simultaneous adsorption/photocatalyst system, respectively. The novel MCM-Cur/TiO2/Ce composite demonstrates high photocatalytic efficiency, stability, and reusability, making it a promising and sustainable solution for wastewater treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40712-2.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the rapid increase in population, excessive exploitation of limited water resources, and widespread climate change have led to a dual challenge: a decline in available water resources and the proliferation of toxic, stable, and non-degradable pollutants. These issues have significantly impacted water quality. Various technologies such as precipitation, membrane filtration, coagulation, ion exchange, and biological degradation have been employed to address the removal of organic dyes from wastewater1–7.

Heterogeneous photocatalysis, a process categorized under advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), has emerged as a superior method for treating complex organic dye pollutants in wastewater. This environmentally friendly and renewable technique offers higher efficiency compared to other physical, chemical, and biological processes. Titania (TiO2) is one of the most widely studied photocatalysts due to its exceptional photocatalytic performance and maximum quantum efficiency8–15. However, the primary drawback of TiO2 is its wide bandgap (3.2–3.7 eV), which limits its light absorption to the UV region. Extensive research has been focused on reducing the bandgap to overcome this limitation. Additionally, the high recombination rate of electron-hole pairs in TiO2, along with the small size of TiO2 particles (10–15 nm), can hinder its efficiency. These small particles tend to escape from the reaction zone along with the output product. As a result, significant efforts have been dedicated to developing modified TiO2 with a broader absorption spectrum and enhanced quantum performance16–20.

Modifying the surface of the photocatalyst by incorporating metals and stabilizing it on high-quality substrates with suitable acidity can enhance the adsorption of organic pollutants near the active sites. This, in turn, reduces electron-hole recombination and activates the photocatalyst under visible light21–35. Adsorption, with its high efficiency in dilute solutions, ease of operation, cost-effectiveness, lower required surface area (half or a quarter of the biological process), environmental compatibility, potential for adsorbent reduction and reuse, and high potential in removing fine molecular materials (such as dyes and organic materials), has established itself as one of the best materials for dye removal from effluent when compared to other options. Among various substrate materials, porous structures like zeolite, carbon, and silica have shown promise due to their high surface area, adsorption capacity, and stability. Zeolite, in particular, stands out for its adjustable and uniform porosity, ion exchange capacity, and mechanical strength. However, activated carbon adsorption methods suffer from drawbacks such as high costs, expensive regeneration, and potential adsorbent loss21,22,24,32. Semiconductor and porous zeolites have attracted significant research interest because of their unique properties, which include a distinct structure, adjustable and perfectly uniform porosity, pore widths within the molecular dimensions range, high surface area and adsorption capacity, ion exchange capacity, thermal and mechanical stability, and adjustable acidity. These characteristics make them suitable for various applications, such as simultaneous systems, catalysts, adsorbents, molecular sieves, ion exchangers, and substrates.

Tongon et al.33 created visible-light-activated nanocomposite films by incorporating Ag into TiO2 and synthesizing them on the surface of MCM-41. The photocatalytic effectiveness of these composite films was tested by decolorizing methylene blue under both ultraviolet and visible light. Arellano et al.36 examined the degradation of water-soluble Congo red using catalysts with a shell-core structure. These catalysts were doped with metal oxides (Fe, V, and Cu) and immobilized on Ti-MCM-41 porous zeolite. Degradation experiments were carried out under ultraviolet light with various loadings36. In another study, Reddy et al. used Ce/Al-MCM-41, TiO2/Al-MCM-41, and TiO2/Ce-Al-MCM-41 with different weight percentages of Ce and TiO2 immobilized on Al-MCM-41 to investigate the photocatalytic degradation of phenol under ultraviolet radiation. They found that samples with low Ce content in the Ce3+ state performed better than those with high Ce content or pure ceria37.

Another approach to enhance TiO2 levels and, consequently, its catalytic activity involves the use of suitable pore-forming agents such as surfactants or organic compounds38. Zanjanchi et al. investigated the improved performance of stabilized TPA-TiO2 composite on MCM-41 for enhanced synergy, stability, and effectiveness. Their experimental results demonstrated that materials containing TPA were primarily responsible for the adsorption of methylene blue (MB) from dye solutions. The combined utilization of adsorption and photocatalytic processes significantly increased the dye removal rate using the MCM-41-TPA-TiO2 composite39. In another study Zhou et al.40 reported the synthesis of ZSM5 titanium dioxide and zeolite composites for azo dye degradation, demonstrating a significant reduction in time and energy consumption. Nagarjuna et al.41 prepared TiO2 stabilized on zeolite-4 A for the degradation of methylene blue, a widely used dye in the industry. Surface area measurements revealed a 2.5-fold increase in surface area for 20% TiO2/zeolite-4 A compared to the physical mixture of 20% TiO2 and zeolite-4 A In another related study, Hieu et al.42 reported the hydrothermal synthesis of MgFe2O4–TiO2 (MFO-TiO2) nanocomposites using chitosan as a stabilizer. Calcination optimization revealed 800 °C for 2 h as the optimal condition.

This study explores an innovative approach to dye removal by integrating photocatalytic and adsorption processes using cerium-modified TiO₂ nanoparticles and the natural phenolic compound curcumin. Curcumin, with its unique bidentate diketone functional group, offers excellent solubility in alcohol and the ability to form stable complexes with metal salts, enhancing the photocatalytic activity. The TiO₂ nanoparticles were effectively stabilized on MCM-22, a high-performance zeolite adsorbent, creating a synergistic system for simultaneous photocatalysis and adsorption. The operational parameters were systematically optimized to identify the most efficient conditions. Notably, a thorough review of the published literature revealed no prior studies employing this combined methodology, underscoring the novelty and significance of this research.

Experimental

Chemicals and materials

In this study, titanium butoxide (C₁₆H₃₆O₄Ti) from Merck was used as the source of TiO₂ synthesis. Commercial TiO₂ in the anatase phase from Merck was employed for comparison. Additionally, turmeric was utilized as the source of curcumin, along with 98% ethanol and toluene from Dr. Mojalli (Iran), cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO₃)₃·6 H₂O) from Sigma-Aldrich, 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) from Merck, SiO₂ (Aerosil 200, Degussa), hexamethyleneimine (HMI, 99%, Aldrich) as the organic template, NaAlO₂ (56% Al₂O₃, 37% Na₂O, Carlo Erba), and distilled water as additional components.

Preparation of photocatalyst

Preparation of MCM-Cur

The MCM-22 zeolite, a member of the MWW family, was used as the base material for the titanium oxide photocatalyst. The MCM-22 zeolite was synthesized hydrothermally with a Si/Al molar ratio of 30, using hexamethyleneimine, ethanol, SiO₂, NaAlO₂, and distilled water as precursors. The synthesis took place in a 500 mL Teflon-lined rotary autoclave.

In the next step, curcumin was extracted from turmeric powder using ethanol, as shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, 20.3 g of turmeric powder was mixed with 150 mL of ethanol, and the solution was filtered through filter paper to obtain a clarified curcumin extract.

Fig. 1Extraction of curcumin from turmeric and ethanol solution.

Extraction of curcumin from turmeric and ethanol solution.

As depicted in Fig. 2, the surface of the MCM-22 zeolite was activated by attaching curcumin molecules. Initially, the surface hydroxyl (-OH) groups of the zeolite were silanized with the silane-Si(OEt)₃ groups from APTES reagents, forming strong siloxane bonds. This was followed by a reflux reaction between curcumin and the zeolite’s amine groups, resulting in the creation of a C = N imine bond between the amine and carbonyl (C = O) group of the curcumin molecule. The modified curcumin zeolite then acted as a substrate for the complexation of Ce³⁺ metal ions.

Fig. 2Reaction scheme of MCM-Cur synthesis.

Reaction scheme of MCM-Cur synthesis.

The photocatalytic stabilization of the zeolite was achieved in two steps. Firstly, 1 g of zeolite powder was mixed with 2.5 mL of ethoxysilane solution and a 4% (v/v) toluene solution. The mixture was then refluxed at 110 °C for 3 h, resulting in a white product. In the second step, the zeolite powder and APTES were combined with the extracted curcumin solution and refluxed at boiling temperature for 3 h.

Preparation of MCM-Cur /TiO2 /Ce

The final step in immobilizing titanium oxide on the zeolite involved adding 0.57 g of titanium butoxide solution to 40 mL of hexane, followed by the addition of 1 g of MCM -Cur powder. The mixture was placed in an ultrasonic bath to improve dispersion. The solution was then stirred at medium speed at room temperature for one hour. Next, cerium salt was dissolved in ethanol before being added to the previous solution. Specifically, 0.363 g of cerium trinitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in 30 mL of ethanol, mixed with aforementioned solution, and further combined with 5 mL of water. The resulting mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for 20 min.

Characterization

The unique properties of the prepared photocatalyst (TiO₂ stabilized on a combination of zeolite and curcumin), were examined using high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) from TSCAN, in the Czech Republic. EDX images and diagrams of the structure ranging from 500 nm to 20 μm were also analyzed. The structure of the zeolite nanoparticles, zeolite-curcumin composite, and stabilized photocatalyst were further evaluated using various techniques, including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) with an ALPHA model spectrometer, X-ray diffraction (XRD) with an Intel X-ray device, Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (DRS), Photoluminescence (PL), thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA), and zeta potential measurements. TEM analysis (Zeiss, EM10C, 80 kV) was used to characterize the photocatalyst composite sample. BET analysis was performed to calculate the specific adsorption surface area and adsorption isotherm using a Belsorp mini II analyzer at 77 K.

Photocatalytic experiments

In this research, methylene blue dye (C₁₆H₁₈N₃SCl) at a concentration of 10 ppm was used. To measure the concentration of the dye, a 20 mL solution of methylene blue at 10 ppm was prepared, with distilled water used as a control to calibrate the device. The procedure is as follows: First, based on the measuring range of the device (0.1 to 4 mg per liter of dye), the original sample is diluted with water to fall within this range. Next, 20 mL of the prepared sample is placed into a clean, dry cylinder. At regular intervals (every 30 min), the solution is kept in the dark while being exposed to radiation. A 3 mL sample is taken from the reaction mixture using a syringe, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The photocatalyst material is separated from the reaction medium, and the resulting solution is transferred into a clean spectrophotometer cuvette (50 µL). The absorbance of light is measured using the spectrophotometer, relative to the control solution. The control solution is prepared in the same way as the original solution, but instead of 20 mL of the prepared sample, 20 mL of distilled water is used.

Results and discussion

Morphology analysis

Figure 3 displays the FESEM images of the purchased TiO₂ nanoparticles. The figure reveals a spherical structure with a particle size ranging from approximately 50–80 nm. Moreover, the images depict particle agglomeration, forming clumps ranging from 100 nm to several microns in size. The use of titanium oxide nanoparticles alone as a photocatalyst is problematic due to their tendency to agglomerate, which can diminish the photocatalyst’s efficiency in pollutant removal. However, if titanium oxide nanoparticles can be synthesized in situ within a zeolite substrate, their agglomeration can be prevented. Figure 4 showcases the FESEM images of MCM-Cur at various magnifications. The morphological structure of the zeolite closely resembles flake-like plates, which are occasionally stacked on top of each other30.

Fig. 3FESEM images of TiO₂ nanoparticles at various magnifications.

FESEM images of TiO₂ nanoparticles at various magnifications.

Fig. 4FESEM image of the MCM-Cur composite at different magnifications.

FESEM image of the MCM-Cur composite at different magnifications.

The thickness of each zeolite layer is approximately 50 nm, and the diameter of the zeolite flakes is around 1.8 μm. The adhesion of multiple zeolite layers together creates multilayer structures of MCM-22, with a thickness of about 200 nm.

In principle, functionalizing metal oxide nanoparticles or nanosheets with organic molecules can reduce the severity of their agglomeration. In this article, the curcumin molecule is used to functionalize the zeolite surface to prevent severe agglomeration of the sheets.

The presence of ribbon-like structures in the zeolite structures is related to the agglomerated curcumin molecules. Curcumin extracted from turmeric is typically observed as ribbon-like structures when it is agglomerated.

Figure 5 illustrates the FESEM images of the optimal MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce photocatalyst sample at various magnifications. Comparing these images to the base images from the previous figure, we can observe that the presence of Cerium nanoparticles on the surface of the zeolite has resulted in a smoother surface. This smooth surface of the zeolite plates suggests that they are now coated with extremely fine nanometer particles.

More detailed information on the presence of cerium and titanium oxide nanoparticles can be seen in the TEM analysis, as shown in Fig. 6. The images reveal spherical titanium oxide nanoparticles measuring approximately 100–500 nm, formed within the zeolite plate substrate with a uniform distribution. Additionally, ribbon-like and rod-like structures are present, possibly related to curcumin agglomerates.

Fig. 6TEM images of the MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce composite at different magnification levels.

TEM images of the MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce composite at different magnification levels.

The plate-like structures of MCM-22 zeolite are clearly visible in the TEM images. This zeolite plates consist of 4–5 layers stacked on top of each other. Upon closer inspection of the zeolite surface at higher magnifications, a uniform distribution of very fine, spherical nanoparticles with a diameter of 5–10 nm can be observed, which are associated with cerium nanoparticles.

TEM images clearly confirm that the synthesized photocatalyst is a nanocomposite consisting of titanium oxide and cerium nanoparticles on an MCM-22 zeolite substrate, and particle agglomeration did not occur in this photocatalyst. The photocatalyst synthesis was designed so that cerium cations (Ce3+) could easily complex with curcumin molecules arranged on the surface of MCM-22 zeolite, ultimately forming cerium nanoparticles. The use of ethanol solvent to selectively dissolve cerium nitrate helps cerium cations easily complex with curcumin because curcumin is soluble in ethanol. Hexane and ethanol solvents are not well miscible, so we initially dissolved the titanium butoxide in hexane solvent before adding it to the reaction mixture. In these conditions, cerium nitrate in ethanol readily complexes with curcumin molecules and does not enter the hexane phase. However, over time, titanium butoxide can slowly migrate from the hexane phase to the ethanol phase and undergo a hydrolysis reaction with the small amount of water present in ethanol, converting it to titanium oxide. Using titanium butoxide in a hexane solvent helps to create titanium oxide nanoparticles in a state where cerium ions cannot enter their crystal lattice structure. The difference in polarity between hexane and ethanol solvents, along with the complexation of cerium with curcumin, enabled the synthesis of cerium nanoparticles and titanium oxide nanoparticles in the MCM-22 zeolite simultaneously, without cerium doping into the crystal lattice of titanium oxide.

This could have led to the formation of a nanocomposite structure, (see Fig. 7). Figure 8 displays the X-ray mapping and EDX analysis of the optimal MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce sample. Colored dots clearly indicate the presence of key elements Ti, Ce, N, Al, Si, C, and O on the photocatalyst surface. The map images show Si, Al, and O elements, indicating the aluminum silicate structure of zeolite MCM-22. The uniform distribution of carbon and nitrogen elements confirms the presence of curcumin molecules in the zeolite substrate. Additionally, the distribution of cerium and titanium elements on the photocatalyst surface is very uniform, covering the zeolite surface. Peaks in the EDX spectrum further confirm the successful synthesis of the photocatalyst.

Optical band-gap determination and discussion

Diffuse reflectance UV–Vis’s spectra (DRS) were converted to pseudo-absorbance using the Kubeka–Munk function, F(R∞) =(1 − R)2/ (2R) and Tauc plots were generated as [F(R∞) hʋ] 1/2 vs. hʋ, assuming an indirect allowed transition suitable for anatase TiO₂. The band gap (Eg)​ was determined from the intersection of the linear edge with the energy axis. This method adheres to recommended practices for extracting band gaps from reflectance data, while recognizing the assumptions and limitations of the Kubelka-Munk/Tauc approach for highly scattering powders.

As shown in Fig. 13, Pristine TiO₂ exhibited Eg ≈ 3.2 eV, which is consistent with reported values for anatase. When combined with MCM/Cur and Ce, the apparent band gap decreased to 2.79 eV (Ce = 4.5%), reached a minimum of 2.72 eV at 9.5% Ce, and slightly widened to 2.84 eV at 17% Ce (Refer to Fig. 13). The non-monotonic trend in Eg corresponds to the interaction of (i) dye-sensitized charge transfer from curcumin, (ii) Ce-induced electronic states, and (iii) microstructural/scattering changes on the MCM support: (i) Curcumin sensitization. Curcumin strongly absorbs in the visible spectrum and can inject electrons into TiO₂ under visible excitation, effectively shifting the absorption onset of the composite to longer wavelengths without altering the intrinsic TiO₂ band edges.

Fig. 13Tauc plots of TiO₂ and MCM-Cur/TiO2/Ce composite samples showing band-gap shifts with Ce content: Ce(wt%) Sample1: (4.6), sample 2: (9.1), sample 3: (16.96), and TiO₂.

Tauc plots of TiO₂ and MCM-Cur/TiO2/Ce composite samples showing band-gap shifts with Ce content: Ce(wt%) Sample1: (4.6), sample 2: (9.1), sample 3: (16.96), and TiO₂.

Its contribution is maximized at appropriate surface coverages but can diminish if quenched by excessive defect density or by phase segregation at higher Ce. (ii) Additionally, cerium electronic play a role. Moderate Ce incorporation introduces Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ 4f-related donor states and oxygen-vacancy levels lying below the TiO₂ conduction band. This produces tailing into the gap (Urbach edge) and an apparent narrowing in Tauc analysis. This explains the red-shift and the minimum Eg ​ at 9.5% Ce. At higher Ce (17%), two effects can counteract the narrowing: (a) formation of CeO₂-rich domains that do not contribute to visible-light charge transfer to TiO₂ and/or screen the dye, and (b) an apparent blue shift due to filling of conduction-band states or increased carrier concentration (Burstein–Moss-like effect) together with enhanced recombination centers, which steepen the absorption edge used in the Tauc fit. The net result is a partial recovery of Eg​ to 2.84 eV. Similar “optimum-doping” behavior and roles of mixed Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ and oxygen vacancies have been widely reported for Ce-modified TiO.

The electronic configurations of 4f15d0/4f05d0 could result in different optical properties and improve photoelectrons acceptance by retarding e−/h+ recombination, thereby improving photostability and quantum yield47.

(iii) Role of the MCM support and scattering. Dispersing TiO₂ on mesoporous MCM-22 improves particle separation and can alter multiple scattering. Because Tauc fits on F(R∞​) assume a linear relation between F(R) and the true absorption coefficient, changes in scattering with composition or texture (e.g., at high Ce loading) can subtly bias the extracted Eg​. The observed slight re-widening at 17% Ce is therefore consistent with both electronic (ii) and optical-scattering contributions. Overall, moving from 4.5% → 9.5% Ce increases visible-light absorption (smaller apparent Eg​), attributable to cooperative effects of curcumin sensitization and Ce-induced donor/defect states; further increasing to 17% Ce likely crosses the optimal regime, where defect-assisted recombination, CeO₂ segregation, and scattering effects diminish the red-shift and yield a slightly larger extracted Eg​. Methodological robustness. Because debates persist over indiscriminate use of Tauc exponents and the KM transform in particulate systems, we verified that linear fitting in the indirect-allowed representation gave the highest R2 and the most stable intercepts around the absorption edge. As a robustness check recommended in the literature, evaluating both indirect (n = 1/2) and direct (n = 2) forms and reporting the model that best linearizes the edge is advisable; reporting fit windows and goodness-of-fit metrics improves reproducibility. Context with literature values. The baseline Eg​ of anatase near 3.2 eV is in line with recent reports, while Ce-modified TiO₂ commonly exhibits optimum visible-light response at intermediate Ce contents—beyond which band-tail states turn into recombination centers and phase segregation diminishes sensitization benefits—matching the 2.72 → 2.84 eV turnaround observed here.

Zeta potential

According to Fig. 15, the photocatalyst surface becomes positively charged in acidic conditions due to the presence of H⁺ ions, which rapidly adsorb to the negatively charged composite surface (due to the curcumin OH⁻ ions), while methylene blue molecules remain positively charged. This results in electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged dye molecules and the photocatalyst surface, leading to a reduction in dye removal efficiency in acidic environments. However, as the pH increases and the environment becomes more alkaline, the photocatalyst surface acquires a negative charge due to deprotonation or the presence of hydroxyl ions (OH⁻). This creates electrostatic attraction with the positively charged dye molecules, enhancing the removal of the dye. Therefore, the composite synthesized in an alkaline environment exhibits higher efficiency49.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Figure 16 presents the TGA thermogram of the synthesized MCM-Cur (a) and MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce (b) samples. In Fig. 16 (a), the thermogram shows several stages of weight loss as the sample is heated from 30 °C to 800 °C, with a temperature increase of 10 °C per minute. In the first stage, up to about 90 °C, a weight loss of 3.1% occurs, attributed to the removal of volatile solvents and water in the sample. The second and third stages of weight loss, up to 340 °C, are due to the breakdown and decomposition of some chemical bonds and functional groups in the sample structure. Combustion of organic compounds takes place at 241 °C and 340 °C, continuing up to 565 °C. It can be concluded that approximately 15% of the sample’s weight is composed of APTES-Curcumin organic groups that have been stabilized at the zeolite surface. In the final stage, from 565 °C to 800 °C, a significant weight loss of 8.75% is observed, related to the transition of the zeolite phase and the loss of hydroxyl groups from the structure.

The TGA thermogram of the synthesized MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce sample is shown in Fig. 16b. The various stages of weight loss in this sample are similar to those shown in Fig. 16a. The organic component of the sample undergoes a weight loss of about 34% by 450 °C, which is higher than the sample in Fig. 16. This increased weight loss is attributed to the synthesis of titanium oxide, where titanium butoxide salt is used, leading to the production of butanol. The butanol is absorbed into the zeolite structure, contributing to the additional weight loss observed.

In the MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce sample, the organic structure is mainly composed of curcumin molecules, which have aromatic and conjugated structures and begin to combus when heated to 240–340 °C. Due to its aromaticity, curcumin can lead to carbonization. At a higher temperature of 565 °C, the carbonized materials also undergo a oxidation process and are ultimately destroyed.

In the MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce composite sample, in addition to curcumin molecules, butanol molecules are adsorbed in the zeolite structure. The presence of cerium and titanium oxide, acting as catalysts, aids in the combustion and oxidation process at temperatures lower than 550 °C. As a result, the majority of the organic structure of the material undergoes combustion between 220 and 310 °C with a sharp slope. Only a small amount of carbonized material is produced, which can be oxidized at a temperature of around 450 °C.

In fact, the reason for the lower weight loss in this sample at 550 °C is due to the complete combustion process at a lower temperature and less carbonization of curcumin molecules. Butanol as a supplementary fuel and titanium oxides act as co-catalysts in the combustion and oxidation process leading to reduced carbonization of curcumin molecules.

Photocatalytic activity

The photocatalytic reactor (Fig. S1, Supplementary Data) features two 300-watt xenon lamps that emit light in the visible wavelength range, peaking at 420 nm. These lamps are situated on opposite walls of the chamber. Additionally, the reactor includes two air transfer fans that facilitate air circulation within the chamber for cooling purposes. Within the reactor, the methylene blue contaminant solution is mixed with the synthesized photocatalyst using a magnetic stirrer, while the solution’s temperature is continuously monitored with a digital thermometer. Air can also be introduced into the solution via a tube. The contaminant concentration is determined using a two-beam UV-Vis-Unico4800 spectrophotometer, capable of measuring within the range of 0–10 ppm.

The photocatalytic reaction mechanism for the removal of methylene blue from solution is illustrated in Fig. S2 (Supplementary Data). Zeolite MCM-22 serves as a porous mineral substrate that holds TiO2 and cerium nanoparticles together. The porous layered structures of MCM-22 provide a substrate that aids in distributing TiO2 and cerium nanoparticles, preventing them from agglomerating. This can ultimately enhance the performance of the photocatalyst.

Curcumin molecules functionalize the zeolite surface, creating active sites for complexing cerium cations. Curcumin is a strong ligand for complexing with metal cations, helping to stabilize cerium on the zeolite surface. This ultimately leads to the formation of cerium nanoparticles on the zeolite substrate.

On the other hand, curcumin molecules can bind to the surface of TiO2 nanoparticles on the zeolite surface and stabilize them. Curcumin, acting as a ligand with conjugated bonds, facilitates electron transfers between cerium and TiO2 particles, enhancing the photocatalyst’s performance.

Additionally, curcumin on the surface of the photocatalyst aids in absorbing pollutant molecules, thereby improving removal efficiency. Curcumin achieves this by adsorbing pollutant molecules through hydrogen bonding and pi-pi interactions.

Given that the photocatalyst synthesis conditions were carried out in the presence of organic curcumin molecules, there is a possibility of forming cerium metal nanoparticles. On the other hand, with the presence of some water in the synthesis conditions, there is also a possibility of forming cerium oxide nanoparticles. Therefore, there is a possibility of forming both metal and oxide nanoparticles for cerium. Accordingly, the photocatalytic mechanism is proposed for this synthesized photocatalyst.

The proposed mechanism for the photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue can be used for visible or ultraviolet light irradiation. When exposed to the UV light, TiO2 nanoparticles become excited, producing electrons that move from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB). These electrons then transfer to cerium (Ce) nanoparticles in the CB band, facilitating efficient charge separation for the photocatalytic reaction. The electrons generated can convert oxygen into superoxide radicals (O2.−), which subsequently react with water to form hydroxyl radicals (.OH)50.

It is possible that some of the cerium ions in the composite are converted into cerium nanoparticles, which were not detectable in the XRD patterns, and this is due to the low concentration of these nanoparticles, which are not detectable due to overlap with the zeolite peaks. Therefore, if cerium oxide nanoparticles are situated adjacent to titanium oxide nanoparticles, the band gap of cerium oxide is reduced in comparison to titanium oxide. This results in the photocatalyst absorbing light within the visible spectrum and stimulating electrons to move to the conduction band of cerium oxide. These electrons have the ability to transform oxygen into superoxide radicals (O2.−), which then interact with water to generate hydroxyl radicals (.OH)51.

Kinetic analysis of photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue

The photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB) over the MCM-Cur/TiO₂/Ce composite was investigated under visible irradiation, and the reaction kinetics were modeled using four different approaches: zero-order, first-order, parabolic diffusion, and modified Freundlich models.

As shown in Fig. 22, among the models tested, the pseudo-first-order kinetic model provided the best fit to the experimental data, with a high correlation coefficient (R² = 0.9900). This suggests that the degradation rate is directly proportional to the concentration of MB remaining in solution. The calculated rate constant (k = 0.0245 min⁻¹) reflects the efficiency of the photocatalyst in generating reactive species, such as hydroxyl radicals, which attack the dye molecules. This behavior is typical for photocatalytic systems where the reaction occurs primarily on the surface.

Photocatalyst reusability

To evaluate the reusability of the photocatalyst (Fig. 23), MCM-Cur/TiO2/Ce catalysts with a cerium weight% of 9.1 g/L and pH of 6.8 were used for treating 10 ppm methylene blue. A specific amount of the catalyst, prepared according to the described method, was added to the reaction vessel. After 150 min of reaction, with 30 min in the dark followed by 120 min under irradiation, the catalyst was separated from the solution using centrifugation and washed with distilled water.

For the second cycle, the reaction was repeated without the 30-minute dark phase, only under 120 min of irradiation. After five recycling cycles, the photocatalytic activity for degrading methylene blue decreased by approximately 8% for the MCM-Cur/TiO2/Ce composite, indicating relatively stable activity. The decline in photocatalyst efficiency may be due to the deactivation of active sites from coverage by dye molecules.

Conclusions

A simple and cost-effective method was utilized to modify TiO2 nanoparticles with cerium in order to adjust the band gap energy and anchor them onto the surface of an adsorbent. The surface modification of titania with cerium and its stabilization on MCM-22 zeolite functionalized with curcumin exhibited enhanced photocatalytic activity for methylene blue degradation compared to unmodified titania. FESEM analysis images suggest that the formation of TiO2 nanoparticles on the zeolite surface results in a smoother surface, indicating that the method employed for stabilizing TiO2 on the support is effective for this purpose. FT-IR, PL, XRD, DRS, and zeta potential analyses validate the bonding between cerium and the zeolite-curcumin structure, demonstrating that the presence of cerium ions in the structure enables the photocatalyst to efficiently absorb light in the visible region and decrease recombination rates.

Operational parameter analysis revealed that under alkaline conditions (pH = 11), with a weight% of 9.1% cerium loading on TiO2, the best results were achieved after 120 min of reaction with a 300 W xenon lamp, resulting in 96% pollutant removal. The photocatalytic performance of the synthesized composite after five reuse cycles demonstrated the multi-use capability of the system, with an increased percentage of pollutant removal or degradation achieved through the simultaneous system. The superior photocatalytic performance of the composite compared to adsorption and photocatalytic processes indicates a photocatalyst/adsorption synergy effect. The findings from this research suggest that the MCM-Cur/TiO2/Ce composite could be an effective solution for wastewater treatment, as it not only offers high photocatalytic efficiency but also demonstrates stability and reusability over multiple cycles, making it a potential candidate for sustainable and cost-effective wastewater treatment applications.
