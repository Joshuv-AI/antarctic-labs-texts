Where are orthopedic surgery graduates practising? A critical look at the last 30 years in Ontario
Authors: Silvio Ndoja, Holly S Howe, Steven R Papp, Emil H Schemitsch, Brent A Lanting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:There has been considerable discussion regarding the demand for orthopedic surgery care and simultaneously the lack of employment opportunities for new graduates. We sought to understand the practice patterns and locations of orthopedic surgeons trained in Ontario.Methods:We invited 618 surgeons trained between 1992 and 2020 to participate in a survey investigating their current practice location, practice patterns, available resources, and reasons for emigration if practising outside of Canada.Results:A total of 253 surgeons responded (40.9%), with 242 full responses. Emigration out of Canada and number of fellowships did not change with time; however, those who graduated more recently were more likely to leave out of necessity rather than interest. Graduates of the University of Ottawa were more likely to work in the United States and more likely to settle farthest from their school if they stayed in Ontario. Graduates of the University of Toronto were more likely to work within 50 km of their training program than graduates of other schools. Most surgeons described themselves as specialists (community or academic), with very few self-described generalists (12%).Conclusion:We found an effect of school on the probability of leaving Canada, with surgeons who graduated more recently leaving out of necessity. This suggests that more recent graduates believe there are not enough opportunities, with graduates of the University of Ottawa being the hardest hit. We also found that almost no one viewed themselves as a generalist, which suggests a re-evaluation of the goals of residency education.

There is a substantial shortage of orthopedic care in Ontario1,2 and across North America.3,4 The literature on the orthopedic care shortage focuses primarily on arthroplasty,5 with some studies showing that there may be an oversupply of orthopedic trauma surgeons.6 Studies have shown that, in the United States, 90% of orthopedic surgery graduates do a fellowship, with the most common being sports (30%), spine (16%), and hand fellowships (14%).7 Data are lacking on fellowship choices in a Canadian context. Our group previously published on Ontario graduates’ readiness for independent practice.8 The current study data were captured in the same population.

We sought to gain a better understanding of what an orthopedic surgeon’s practice looks like, specifically, what fellowships they did and how much operating room and clinic time they received. We also sought to assess where their practice is (Canada v. not Canada) and if this was a function of their training location. We hypothesized that surgeons who graduated more recently would be more likely to be employed outside of Ontario and Canada.

Methods

The methods are discussed in detail in a previous publication.8 We surveyed orthopedic surgery graduates from 1992 to 2020 from Ontario residency programs (n = 618). We investigated surgeons’ current practice location, practice patterns, available resources, and reasons for emigration if practising outside of Canada. The survey is reproduced in Appendix 1 (available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.011525/tab-related-content). Residents with foreign funding were excluded, as they are attached to a return-of-service agreement, and motivations for fellowship choices are different in this group than in those without a return-of-service agreement. Where possible, the survey was distributed electronically, with a reminder 1 week later, and a second reminder 1 month after distribution. A paper copy of the survey was sent through physical mail, with return postage, to the surgeon’s office address. We stored electronic data in university secure servers behind 2-factor authentication. Data were automatically uploaded anonymously to the servers. We collected data from February 2023 to July 2023.

Survey and data analysis

We collected demographic data from publicly available sources. These included the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario (CPSO) for surgeons practising in Ontario and the relevant regulatory body where each surgeon was practising. We collected gender, school of graduation, year of graduation, and practice address.

We surveyed surgeons’ self-description of their practice: academic generalist/subspecialist, community generalist/subspecialist. We asked them to report how many operating room and clinic days on average they had a week. We asked surgeons who were practising in the US 5 questions regarding their motivations for emigration. These were graded on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The first 2 questions asked about lack of opportunities in the type of practice (community v. academic), or location type (urban v. rural). The third question asked about lack of opportunities in a respondent’s subspecialty. The fourth question pertained to better income opportunities than in Canada. We performed factor analysis on these items. The fifth question asked about the practice set-up (Appendix 1).

In our analysis of motivations, factor 1 had the items “lack of opportunities in the type of practice (academic v. community),” “lack of opportunities in the type of location (urban v. rural),” and “lack of opportunities in the subspecialty I wanted.” These fit the theme of lack of employment opportunities (necessity). Factor 2 had items “better income opportunities than in Canada” and “better practice type opportunities.” These fit the theme of extrinsic motivators.

We performed a 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) comparing school of graduation and emigration out of necessity. In our examination of practice type, we performed a binary regression analysis. We analyzed data using SPSS.9 We considered year of graduation and Likert item responses continuous data. We considered school of graduation and number of fellowships categorical. We considered country of practice (Canada, not Canada) binary categorical.

We performed the χ2 test when evaluating 2 categorical variables. We used binary regression analysis to compare binary categorical data with continuous data. We used 1-way ANOVA for categorical data with multiple categories when compared with a continuous data set. We used linear regression to compare 2 continuous data sets.

We obtained postal codes of surgeons’ official address from the CPSO. These were converted to longitude and latitude coordinates, which we obtained from a public database of a security company.10 We manually checked a sample of 10 with Google Maps for cross validation and deemed them accurate.

We calculated distance using the following formula: Distance = ACOS(COS(RADIANS(90 – Lat1))) × COS(RADIANS(90 – Lat2)) + SIN(RADIANS(90 – Lat1)) × SIN(RADIANS(90 – Lat2)) × COS(RADIANS(Long1 – Long2)) × 6371, where Lat1 and Lat 2 are latitude of practice and school, respectively, and Long1 and Long2 are the longitude of the practice and school, respectively.

The Western University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board reviewed and approved this study.

Analysis of motivations for emigration

The factor analysis showed 2 factors with Eigen values greater than 1.

There was a significant relationship between year of graduation and emigration from Canada to the US out of necessity (F1,47 = 9.243, p = 0.004), with surgeons who graduated more recently being more likely to emigrate out of necessity (B = 0.08, SE = 0.03, β = 0.41). However, this was a weak correlation (R2 = 0.16), with the model predicting only 16% of the variability. Year of graduation did not predict extrinsic motivations for emigration from Canada to the US (B = −0.01, SE = 0.03, β = −0.07; p = 0.6).

In a 1-way ANOVA comparing school of graduation and emigration out of necessity, there was a main effect (F4,44 = 3.20; p = 0.02). However, with a post hoc analysis after Bonferroni correction, the difference between the University of Toronto and Western University approached, but did not meet, conventional levels of significance (p = 0.1) (Figure 2).

There was no effect of school of graduation on emigration based on extrinsic factors (F4,44 = 0.55; p = 0.7).

Discussion

Although most of our respondents stayed in Canada (76%), graduates from the University of Ottawa were most likely to be practising outside of Canada. The number of surgeons practising in the US did not change with time. However, more surgeons who graduated more recently reported emigrating from Canada to the US out of necessity. There was an effect of school; however, our post hoc analysis did not show a statistical difference. We found that graduates of the University of Toronto reported a greater need to emigrate out of necessity than graduates of Western University, but this finding was not significant (p = 0.06). Those practising in the US reported more operating room time (2.14 d v. 1.65 d) and clinic time (2.43 d v. 1.86 d) than those practising in Canada. The top 3 states to which graduates moved were California, New York, and Texas.

Most respondents described themselves as subspecialists. This result did not change with time or by school.

Most graduates stayed in Ontario (82%). However, graduates from the University of Ottawa were more likely to practise in Alberta than graduates of other schools. It would not be appropriate to make conclusions from these findings because, although the results were significant, there were fewer than 5 events, which is fewer than the minimum for a valid χ2 test.

We found an effect of school of graduation on location of practice for surgeons practising in Ontario. Graduates of the University of Toronto had the lowest distance from school of graduation, with a mean distance of 60 km. The University of Toronto also had more graduates than expected working less than 50 km from their school of graduation. This may be a function of the density of opportunities in and around Toronto.

Graduates of Queen’s University were equally spread out, and University of Ottawa graduates either stayed very close to their location of training or clustered around 400 km away.

There are 2 main takeaways from our data. First, while the rate of emigration from Canada does not appear to have changed over the years, the reasons behind it have changed — more recent graduates appear to be emigrating out of necessity. This supports the notion that more recent graduates have had to leave Canada because of a lack of opportunities. We may not have been able to capture an overall difference of emigration owing to a lack of power. Our results show that graduates from the University of Ottawa are more likely to go to the US. If these graduates stayed in Ontario, they either stayed very close to their school, or they clustered around 400 km away. This is an interesting finding, and the underlying etiology of this is outside the scope of this study. One hypothesis is that this a founder-effect phenomenon. By chance, they may have had a larger-than-expected number of graduates emigrating, and graduates then may have formed connections based on alumni experiences. Ottawa is also the capital of Canada, and there may be more international exposure by virtue of living in the city, which may have lead to an implicit influence.

The second takeaway is that there was a paucity of self-described community generalists. As we discussed in our previous publication, most graduates are doing 1 to 2 fellowships.8 As the field is becoming more complex, even community surgeons have become subspecialists to best care for their patient population. This further shows that a critical look at the goal of residency training is required. While the supposed goal is to train surgeons ready to enter general practice, it is evident that residents do not feel adequately trained as generalists.

Limitations

This study has the same limitations as our previous work.8 The limitation is in capturing the data set. Those who did not register as an independent practice on CPSO may not have been captured. The residency offices of Western University, the University of Ottawa, and the University of Toronto were able to share their alumni list with us; we consider our database for those schools to be comprehensive. McMaster University stated that they had recently started to build their list and shared what they had. Queen’s University and NOSM University did not share their lists. As such, there is a chance that we did not capture graduates from those 3 schools.

Another limitation is that we did not capture where graduates originally completed their education before residency (high school to medical school). This may be a factor when graduates decide where to practice.

Conclusion

We found that surgeons who graduated more recently are emigrating from Canada to the US out of necessity, further supporting the notion of graduates not having adequate opportunities in Canada. Graduates from the University of Ottawa appear to be the most affected, as they had a higher propensity to leave Canada, were more likely to go to Alberta, and had the farthest distance from their school if they stayed in Ontario. Our findings have important implications for health human resource planning. Further investigation is needed to optimize health human resource planning in Ontario to ensure a synchrony between those trained and retained.
