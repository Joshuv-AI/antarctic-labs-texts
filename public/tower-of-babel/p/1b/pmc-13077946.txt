Validation of the Arabic version of the short 10/66 dementia diagnostic schedule for the Lebanese population
Authors: Khalil El Asmar, Alexandra Abi Nassif, Zeina Karam, Aya El Sammak, Maëlenn Guerchet, Monique Chaaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs dementia becomes a major health concern, developing a quick and accurate diagnostic tool is crucial for early detection and intervention in resource-limited settings. This paper aims to validate the short 10/66 algorithm against the clinical diagnosis of dementia in a sample of older adults in Lebanon.MethodsThis study is a secondary analysis of data from the Lebanese validation of the standard 10/66 dementia assessment, evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of the Arabic short 10/66 algorithm against a clinical reference standard. 231 participants aged 65 + were recruited from institutions and their dwellings, including geriatric clinics and community-based primary care centers. Clinical dementia diagnosis followed DSM-IV criteria. The final sample included 88 cases (diagnosed with mild to moderate dementia) and 143 controls. The short algorithm used is derived from the Community Screening Instrument for Dementia, the CERAD 10-word list recall and the Euro-D. The psychometric parameters of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), false positive rate (FPR), and area under the curve (AUC) were estimated for the short 10/66 algorithm and its subcomponents.ResultsCompared to clinical diagnosis, the short 10/66 showed high specificity (95.1%), good sensitivity (79.5%), excellent PPV (90.9%), high NPV (88.3%), low FPR (4.9%), and substantial agreement between the two (AUC = 0.97, kappa = 0.765).ConclusionThe short 10/66 is accurate and feasible for the diagnosis of dementia in the older Lebanese population, offering a practical tool with a lower time requirement that is adaptable to different literacy levels. Interpretation of predictive values should consider the enriched sampling design, and performance should be further assessed in lower prevalence community settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07286-0.

Introduction

Dementia is a neurodegenerative syndrome of late adulthood that affects individuals’ cognition, memory, behavior, and independent functioning [1]. It is one of the major causes of disability and dependence among older adults [1]. Dementia poses a substantial medical, economic, and social burden on nations, as large amounts of resources are needed to care for people with this condition [1, 2]. As dementia becomes a major health concern straining healthcare systems worldwide, developing cost effective and efficient diagnostic tools becomes, in turn, crucial for early detection and intervention [2, 3].

The standard 10/66 dementia diagnostic assessment was developed by the 10/66 Dementia Research Group (DRG) [3] to provide a culturally and educationally sensitive tool that avoids the literacy biases common in traditional screenings [4, 5]. It relies on interviewer administered, fully verbal tasks, avoids written or reading based items, and uses standardized interviewer instructions designed to minimize the influence of literacy on performance. While highly validated [4], the standard version is hindered by the inclusion of the Geriatric Mental State (GMS-AGECAT) instrument, which requires 25–40 min to administer and specialized 2 to 3-day training, extending the total assessment time to approximately one hour [4, 6]. This duration often leads to participant fatigue, emotional distress, and higher attrition rates, particularly in time-constrained research settings. To address these limitations, a short version of the 10/66 was developed. By replacing the GMS-AGECAT with the Euro-D scale, a 12-item depression tool requiring only 3–5 min and minimal interviewer training, the assessment significantly reduces the burden on respondents [6–8]. This adaptation maintains diagnostic integrity while enhancing efficiency and feasibility in populations where extensive training and lengthy interview sessions are impractical [3, 6–8].

Three studies in Italy [7], Singapore [3], and Pakistan [8] have validated the short 10/66 against the standard version or the clinical diagnosis of dementia in older adults aged 60 and above. Compared with the clinical diagnosis, the short 10/66 revealed good [7, 8] to excellent [3] sensitivity, and fair [7] to high [3, 8] specificity. It also performed well compared to the standard 10/66, demonstrating nearly perfect agreement and only slight differences in prevalence rates [3]. In summary, the short 10/66 diagnostic schedule and its algorithm provides comparable results to the standard version and the clinical diagnosis, however, with the added advantages of greater feasibility and efficiency.

This paper focuses on Lebanon, a Middle Eastern country with a rapidly aging population. According to the United Nations’ 2019 World Population Prospects, the fertility rate in Lebanon, which was 2.20 in 2000, is expected to decline to 1.77 by 2050 [9]. In parallel, a 2022 United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) report stated that the number of people aged 60 years and older in Lebanon is expected to more than double between 2020 and 2050, rising from approximately 765,000 (11.2% of the total population) to 1.7 million (27.1%) [10]. This demographic shift will likely lead to a higher prevalence of dementia, creating a significant medical and economic burden on the Lebanese population in the future. At the same time, illiteracy remains common among older cohorts, and access to specialized geriatric and dementia care is limited [11, 12]. Long-term care infrastructure is less developed than in high income countries, and primary care services face substantial resource constraints [13].

Given this, the need for an appropriate, literacy-independent, diagnostic tool for dementia in Lebanon is imminent. To date, one study has validated the standard 10/66 schedule in Lebanon by comparing its results and those of its individual components against clinical diagnoses of dementia [5]. This validation was conducted in a sample of Lebanese older adults, a substantial proportion of whom had limited or no formal education (50.4%) [5]. It concluded in 2013, during a period when Lebanon was already progressing through demographic aging, and projections indicate further increases in the older population in subsequent decades. The validation findings showed that the standard 10/66 DRG algorithm outperformed its subcomponents in diagnostic accuracy, with a sensitivity of 92.0% (95% CI: 85.0–95.9) and a specificity of 95.1% (95% CI: 90.3–97.6). However, no studies have validated the short 10/66 in Lebanon. Thus, the aim of this study is to validate the short 10/66 against the clinical diagnosis of dementia. Given the rising need for accessible and efficient diagnostic tools in resource-limited settings, this study is fundamental as it addresses the gaps in validating a more feasible version of the 10/66 for the Lebanese population, and contributes to global efforts in enhancing early dementia detection, care, and management in similar contexts.

Methods

Design and source of data

This is not a case control study. Rather, this is a secondary analysis of an existing diagnostic accuracy dataset collected for the validation of the standard 10/66 DRG diagnostic assessment and algorithm in Lebanon [5]. That parent study recruited 244 participants aged 65 and above from social organizations, geriatric clinics, and community-based primary care centers between March 2012 and February 2013. These clinics and centers identified and referred potential cases and controls. All participants not based in their dwellings were categorized as residing in “Institutions”. This recruitment ensures sufficient representation of individuals with and without clinically diagnosed dementia for validation against an independent clinical reference standard. Thus, dementia prevalence in this analytic sample is enriched and does not represent population prevalence.

Only 231 participants had sufficient data to run the short 10/66 DRG algorithm. As a result, we applied the algorithm to the data from these 231 participants, and we evaluated the outcomes against the clinical diagnosis of dementia. To be included, participants had to be aged 65 years or older, have mild to moderate dementia (for the cases) or have normal cognition (for the controls), and have an informant capable of providing an independent assessment of their cognitive function [5]. Exclusion criteria included (1) having severe physical or mental illness that could interfere with the ability to diagnose dementia without extensive medical and psychological evaluation, and (2) having mild cognitive impairment (MCI) diagnosed according to the core clinical criteria for MCI by the National Institute on Aging - Alzheimer’s Association Workgroups. We used the GMS output of the standard 10/66 assessment to diagnose depression, which creates a potential overlap when interpreting results stratified by depression status.

Reference standard (clinical diagnosis)

Geriatricians and neurologists determined clinical dementia status in the Lebanese standard 10/66 validation study (parent study) according to DSM-IV criteria, as described in that publication [5]. The reference standard diagnosis was established independently of the 10/66 algorithm outputs. In addition, trained interviewers administering the 10/66 assessment were blind to participants’ cognitive status. We summarize these procedures here for transparency, while the full diagnostic workflow is reported in the parent validation study. In the present manuscript, the term “clinical diagnosis” refers to this DSM-IV based clinician diagnosis from the parent study.

Instruments

This secondary data analysis study used the short 10/66 DRG diagnostic algorithm adopted from a 2016 study [6], which only classifies mild and moderate dementia. It is a subset of the standard 10/66 that retains both the Community Screening Instrument for Dementia (CSI-D) and the Consortium to Establish a Registry of Alzheimer’s Disease 10-word recall (CERAD-10) elements but substitutes the full GMS for the Euro-D, which is derived from the GMS dataset [6]. We used the same Arabic versions of the 10/66 instruments as those implemented in the parent Lebanese validation study, which included formal translation, back translation, and cultural adaptation procedures as previously reported [5]. We refer to the short 10/66 as a “diagnostic tool” throughout the manuscript, but it’s important to note that it does not regard a clinical diagnosis, but rather a case ascertainment in the context of population-based epidemiological studies.

Subcomponents

The CSI-D is a 32-item cognitive test that requires no reading, writing, or arithmetic skills, and that covers multiple cognitive domains for the participant [5, 6]. It also includes a 26-item questionnaire for the caregiver about the participant’s cognitive function [5, 6]. Two scores are generated: a global cognitive score for the participant (COGSCORE), and an informant score (RELSCORE) [5, 7].

The CERAD modified 10-word list learning recall task consists of two phases: first, an immediate learning phase where participants listen to a list of 10 words read aloud and are asked to immediately recall as many as possible. This procedure is repeated three times, resulting in a total learning score out of 30. After a delay of five minutes, participants complete the delayed recall phase, where they attempt to recall the same 10 words; the resulting delayed recall score (out of 10) is then incorporated into the algorithm [5].

The Euro-D is a depression screening scale developed by a European consortium [14]. It is a short scale used to identify existing symptoms of depression among older adults, and comprises of 12 items, including depression, suicidal ideations, guilt, sleep, irritability and others [14]. It was originally developed for international research and has been extensively used and validated [6]. It can be derived from individual GMS items, and thus, it may be extracted from studies that have used the GMS or administered on its own as a brief 5-minute assessment [6].

Ethics

The Institutional Review Board of the American University of Beirut, Lebanon approved the parent study (Protocol # FHS.MC.19). All participants provided written informed consent, or in cases of impaired decision-making capacity, consent was obtained from relatives.

Statistical analysis

We assessed diagnostic accuracy using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Cohen’s kappa, with 95% confidence intervals, comparing the short 10/66 algorithm to the clinician dementia diagnosis. We stratified the calculations by participants’ sex, age, education level, depression status, and type of residence. It was necessary to stratify by residence type because half of the participants lived in long-term care institutions with nurse or physiotherapist informants, while the other half lived in the community with non-medical informants [5]. Reporting follows STARD recommendations for diagnostic accuracy studies.

Further, we estimated sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, false positive rate (FPR) among participants with depression, FPR for participants with formal education (i.e., those with primary education or higher), and FPR for participants without formal education (i.e., those who were illiterate or with very limited literacy) for the subcomponents of the short 10/66 DRG diagnostic assessment.

We assessed the overall predictive accuracy of the short 10/66 DRG and its subcomponents by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC: sensitivity plotted against 1-specificity) [5]. We calculated the AUC based on the predicted probability from logistic regression models.

We adopted the same logistic regression coefficients and single global cut off as the 2016 study [6] for the short 10/66. We used the previously derived cut-points for CERAD (cut-off ≤ 3) [5], COGSCORE (cut-off ≤ 29.5) [15, 16], and Euro-D (cut-off ≤ 3 used in algorithm) [6]. We did not perform any additional calibration or re-scoring specific to this sample beyond applying the Arabic language instruments used in the standard 10/66 validation study.

We did all analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25 and Stata SE 18.0.

Results

The sample used for our secondary data analysis consisted of the 231 participants who had complete data to run the short 10/66 DRG algorithm. The sample was predominantly female (65.8%) and living in the community (64.5% vs. 35.5% in institutions). The majority (40.7%) of the participants were aged between 75 and 84 y.o., one-third were between 65 and 74 y.o., and 26.0% were between 85 and 100 y.o. Overall, 143 controls (61.9%) and 88 cases (38.1%) were enrolled.

The short 10/66 algorithm showed high specificity (95.1%; FPR = 4.9%) when compared to clinical diagnosis, with substantial agreement between the two (AUC = 0.97, kappa = 0.765). It also showed good sensitivity (79.5%) and NPV (88.3%), and high PPV (90.9%; Table 2). The short version identified fewer cases of dementia when compared to the clinical diagnosis (33.3% vs. 38.1%). The short 10/66 performed similarly to the standard 10/66, with the same specificity and AUC, and comparable PPV and NPV. However, the standard 10/66 exhibited higher sensitivity than the short version (Table 1).

Psychometrics by participant characteristics

Finally, we looked at the psychometric properties of the three different components of the short 10/66. The Euro-D performed poorly in comparison to the dementia clinical diagnosis. It demonstrated low sensitivity (36.4%) and PPV (35.6%), and unsatisfactory specificity (59.4%) and NPV (60.3%). It also showed high FPR (40.6%), with the highest among people with depression (62.0%) and the lowest among those with formal education (28.4%; Table 3).

The CERAD 10-word list delayed recall had high sensitivity (91.0%) and NPV (91.5%), but unsatisfactory PPV (65.9%) and specificity (67.4%). The FPR was similar among those with depression, those with and those without formal education (31.7%, 31.0%, and 33.9%, respectively; Table 3).

The CSI-D COGSCORE performed similarly to the CERAD, in terms of high sensitivity (98.0%) and NPV (97.3%), but low PPV (57.3%) and specificity (49.3%). It performed very poorly among people with no formal education with a FPR of 67.7% (Table 3).

Discussion

Main findings and implications

This study validated the short 10/66 DRG diagnostic assessment and its algorithm against the clinical diagnosis of dementia in a sample of Lebanese older adults. This analysis is a secondary evaluation of diagnostic accuracy rather than a new validation cohort or prevalence study, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Overall, our findings suggest that the short 10/66 maintains strong psychometric performance, with high specificity (95.1%) and excellent agreement with the clinical diagnosis (AUC = 0.97, kappa = 0.765). In prior validations of the short 10/66, reported specificity, AUC, and kappa values ranged from 61% to 90.7%, from 0.74 to 0.87, and from 0.399 to 0.712, respectively [3, 7, 8]. Our results are largely consistent with these ranges, reinforcing the potential of the short 10/66 for broader application in multiple settings.

Replacing the GMS-AGECAT with the Euro-D scale significantly reduces the administration time and training requirements. The shortened administration time also mitigates fatigue related inaccuracies in older participants, especially those with mild cognitive impairments, and thereby improves feasibility in real world clinical or community-based contexts [3, 7, 8]. The short 10/66 showed consistently high specificity across all demographic subgroups, indicating a low false positive rate. However, sensitivity varied somewhat, with older adults (85 + years) and those with no formal education exhibiting higher sensitivity. This is possibly due to the instrument’s design, which largely avoids reading, writing, or arithmetic tasks [5, 6]. In contrast, participants aged 65–74 had lower sensitivity (64.3%). This suggests a need for future studies to investigate whether age-specific cut-points or additional informant questions can improve detection in younger-old adults.

Despite its strong overall performance, sensitivity was low among participants with depression (69.0%), though specificity remained high (97.2%). These findings imply that mood symptoms may interfere with the detection of dementia among depressed individuals [17]. Nonetheless, the false positive rate was low even among depressed participants (2.8%), suggesting that the short 10/66 effectively discriminates between depression and dementia in most cases, but with the tradeoff of potential under detection of dementia in depressed older adults.

When analyzing each component of the short 10/66 separately, the Euro-D showed low standalone performance, with low sensitivity (36.4%) and PPV (35.6%). This highlights that depression screening instruments alone are insufficient for identifying dementia, as overlapping symptoms can confound accurate detection [18]. The CERAD 10-word list delayed recall showed high sensitivity (91.0%), although its moderate specificity resulted in an elevated false positive rate, which could unnecessarily label cognitively intact individuals as potential dementia cases. Similarly, the CSI-D COGSCORE maintained high sensitivity (98.0%) but low specificity (49.3%), resulting in a high proportion of false positives, especially among those with no formal education. These findings highlight the importance of using the short 10/66 assessment and algorithm, rather than relying on a single component.

Lebanon’s rapidly aging population mirrors trends observed in high income countries, with an anticipated rise in dementia prevalence [9]. However, unlike these settings, access to specialized diagnostic services and long-term care remains limited [13]. This, in addition to the relatively high illiteracy rates among older adults in Lebanon [5, 11], make the short 10/66’s brief and culturally adapted approach particularly appealing. This is especially true when compared to other widely used cognitive screening tools of short duration that incorporate literacy-dependent tasks. Two such tools are the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), which is unreliable for participants with low education [19], and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), which was validated on participants with high education [20]. Although adapted versions of these tools exist and remain commonly used with education-adjusted cut-offs, they retain literacy-dependent tasks that can still affect performance among older adults with very limited or no formal education. By minimizing literacy-dependent tasks and reducing the interview duration, the short 10/66 tool can be administered in community centers, long-term care institutions, or clinics that lack specialized geriatric services. Such efficiency in detecting dementia at early stages could facilitate timely clinical interventions, reduce the burden on the healthcare system, and inform national policy for dementia care planning. Potential implementation scenarios include use as a structured case ascertainment tool in community-based ageing studies, integration into long term care assessments, and use by trained primary care teams to identify individuals who warrant further clinical evaluation.

Strengths

A principal strength of this research is that it serves as the first validation of the short 10/66 DRG diagnostic tool in Lebanon and Arabic speaking countries, a context characterized by high illiteracy among older adults and limited geriatric resources. By building on a previously validated standard 10/66 study in the same population, the work draws on robust foundational data and clinical diagnoses, ensuring a reliable comparison. Additionally, the sample size for this study is among the largest in the short 10/66 DRG literature. Moreover, using the Euro-D extracted from the GMS is comparable to using the Euro-D as a standalone tool [21], allowing for reliable comparisons across studies that use different versions of the 10/66 algorithm. Finally, the study uses comprehensive psychometric metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, receiver operating characteristic analysis) to thoroughly assess diagnostic performance.

Limitations

As a secondary analysis of an enriched diagnostic accuracy dataset, the dementia prevalence in this sample is higher than typical community or primary care settings. Consequently, predictive values, especially PPV and NPV, should be interpreted as context and prevalence dependent. In contrast, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC are less sensitive to prevalence and remain the main indicators of diagnostic discrimination in this design.

While this enriched sampling can resemble a case control distribution, the aim here is diagnostic accuracy evaluation, not estimation of dementia prevalence. The sampling excludes individuals with severe physical or mental illnesses, and focuses on older adults with mild to moderate dementia without severe comorbidities. Therefore, the performance of the short 10/66 may differ in other clinical contexts, limiting generalizability to more complex clinical cases [5].

Additionally, it’s important to note that by using the Euro-D, the short 10/66 only captures depressive symptoms, whereas the GMS of the standard 10/66 captures a diverse collection of mental health disorders (dementia, schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety neurosis) which may play a big role in the cognitive function and cognitive impairment diagnosis of older adults.

Because the short 10/66 uses components derived from the same underlying instruments used in the standard 10/66 assessment, and both are applied within the same diagnostic context, findings may reflect the case mix and diagnostic culture of the parent validation setting. This does not invalidate the current results, but it means external validity should be further evaluated in independent samples, ideally population based and primary care cohorts reflecting current service patterns in Lebanon.

Finally, subgroup analyses by age, sex, education, depression status, and residence were descriptives and were not powered for formal subgroup claims, and thus do not account for potential confounding between sociodemographic characteristics.

Conclusion

In summary, the short 10/66 DRG diagnostic tool demonstrated strong accuracy and feasibility in a Lebanese older population, replicating validation outcomes from other international contexts. Its reduced time requirement and adaptability for varying literacy levels make it especially suitable for resource-constrained environments facing the imminent rise in dementia prevalence. By balancing diagnostic accuracy with practical efficiency, the short 10/66 can serve as a promising component in broader dementia screening efforts. It warrants further evaluation in a more diverse population, in the hopes of ultimately supporting earlier detection and improved management of dementia in Lebanon and similar settings worldwide.
