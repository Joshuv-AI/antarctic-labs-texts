Dielectric characterisation of human adrenal glands and adrenal tumours for the development of microwave ablation technologies for hypertension treatment
Authors: Bilal Amin, Adnan Elahi, Grazia Cappiello, Niko Ištuk, Eoghan Dunne, Finn Krewer, Anne Marie Quinn, Aoife Lowery, Punit Prakash, Michael Conall Dennedy, Martin O’Halloran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Adrenal gland-induced hypertension results from underlying adrenal gland disorders including Conn’s syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma. These adrenal disorders are a risk for cardiovascular and renal morbidity and mortality. Clinically, treatment for adrenal gland-induced hypertension involves a pharmaceutical or surgical approach. The former presents very significant side effects whereas the latter can be ineffective in cases where the adrenal disorder reoccurs in the remaining contralateral adrenal gland. Due to the limitations of existing treatment methods, minimally invasive treatment options like microwave ablation (MWA) have received significant attention for treating adrenal gland disorders. A precise comprehension of the dielectric properties of human adrenal glands will help to tailor energy delivery for MWA therapy, thus offering the potential to optimise treatments and minimise damage to surrounding tissues. This study reports the ex vivo dielectric properties of human adrenal glands, including the cortex, medulla, capsule, and tumours, based on the data obtained from four patients (diagnosed with Conn’s syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma) who underwent unilateral adrenalectomy at the University Hospital Galway, Ireland. An open-ended coaxial probe measurement technique was used to measure the dielectric properties for a frequency range of 0.5–8.5 GHz. The dielectric properties were fitted using a two-pole Debye model, and a weighted least squares method was employed to optimise the model parameters. Moreover, the dielectric properties of adrenal tissues and tumours were compared across frequencies commonly used in MWA, including 915 MHz, 2.45 GHz, and 5.8 GHz. The study found that the dielectric properties of adrenal tumours were influenced by the presence of lipid-rich adenomas, and the dielectric properties of Cushing's syndrome tumour were lowest in comparison to the tumours in patients diagnosed with Conn's syndrome and Pheochromocytoma. Furthermore, a notable difference was observed in the dielectric properties of the medulla and cortex among patients diagnosed with Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma. These findings have significant implications for the diagnosis and treatment of adrenal tumours, including the optimisation of MWA therapy for precise ablation of adrenal masses.

Introduction

Hypertension is a highly prevalent health condition worldwide1. According to the World Health Organization, in 2021, approximately 1.28 billion people aged 30 to 79 years have hypertension globally, with a prevalence rate of around 22%2,3. Moreover, hypertension is responsible for approximately 10 million deaths every year globally, and it is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and kidney failure2. The economic burden of hypertension is also significant, with estimates suggesting that it costs around $370 billion globally each year in healthcare expenses and lost productivity4. This burden is expected to increase in line with both the ageing population and the rising costs of diagnostic and therapeutic technologies4.

Adrenal gland-induced hypertension, also known as secondary hypertension, is one of the leading curable causes of hypertension resulting from abnormalities in the adrenal glands5. Adrenal endocrinopathies collectively represent the most common identifiable secondary cause, often caused by primary aldosteronism (PA), and rarely Cushing syndrome and Pheochromocytoma5. Within this cluster of diseases, PA and Cushing disease arise from the adrenal cortex, whereas phaeochromocytoma arises from the inner medulla6. PA accounts for 6–12% of all hypertension, and as high as 22% of so-called resistant hypertension5. Cushing syndrome is a multimorbid systemic disease, which carries an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, osteoporosis, and infection7. Phaeochromocytoma is typically associated with severe treatment-resistant hypertension and carries a higher cardiovascular risk when compared to primary hypertension8. PA is typically characterised by hypertension which is resistant to standard pharmacotherapy and is also associated with a higher risk of atrial fibrillation, stroke, and myocardial infarction compared with blood pressure-matched hypertension5. Adrenal lesions underlying Cushing Syndrome and Phaeochromocytoma are typically unilateral, large, and easily identifiable and are treated surgically with unilateral adrenalectomy9. There are selected case studies, which have also used radiofrequency (RFA) to treat both Cushing’s and Phaeochromocytoma5. Although in patients with Phaeochromocytoma, this approach carries the risk of hypertensive crisis and consequently should not be routinely undertaken, should never be undertaken in large lesions, and must be done under appropriate anti-adrenergic pre-treatment.

PA is caused by unilateral aldosterone-producing adenomas (APA) in 30–40% of all cases. In the remaining cases, it is caused bilaterally by aldosterone-producing nodules, micronodules, or hyperplasia. In this regard, APAs are typically treated with unilateral adrenalectomy and bilateral disease is treated with anti-hypertensive pharmacotherapy, primarily with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA)5. Bilateral adrenalectomy is rarely undertaken for bilateral causes of PA because of the unacceptable post-procedural complication of adrenocortical insufficiency. Hence more than 60% of all patients with PA remain on life-long pharmacotherapy with no option for definitive management of this endocrinopathy10. Advances in the localisation of aldosterone-producing adrenocortical lesions using molecular imaging offer the potential of directing more selective intervention to definitively manage both unilateral and bilateral PA. These interventions include selective adrenalectomy and minimally invasive techniques such as thermal ablation10. Where bilateral intervention is undertaken, the pathologic tissue must be appropriately ablated or removed. However, it is equally important that the normally functioning adrenocortical tissue is preserved to avoid post-procedural adrenocortical insufficiency.

Microwave ablation (MWA) offers the potential for treating APA and aldosterone-producing nodules with a high degree of accuracy and precision. The pattern of ablation exhibited by MWA applicator probes offers more uniform and controlled heating of the target tissue and directionality when compared to RFA, thus offering a more precise ablation zone confined to the diseased part of the gland/nodule, leading to better preservation of surrounding healthy tissues11–13. Moreover, MWA has a higher thermal efficiency and can achieve higher temperatures more rapidly compared to RFA or cryoablation12. This results in shorter procedure times and more effective tissue destruction. Additionally, MWA is less susceptible to the “heat sink” effect caused by nearby blood vessels, making it more effective in highly vascularised tissues13. In terms of safety, MWA has been associated with lower complication rates and less post-procedural pain compared to other ablation techniques. Limited clinical case series of MWA have shown promising results as a minimally invasive treatment option for adrenal tumours and other adrenal diseases, offering several benefits over traditional adrenalectomy or RFA, such as precise targeting, efficient ablation, and reduced risk of complications13,14. In a MWA procedure, the ablation antenna, usually enclosed in a needle or stiff catheter, is inserted into the target area under the guidance of ultrasound or computed tomography (CT) imaging15,16. The microwave energy emitted by the antenna raises the temperature of surrounding tissues to 50–60 °C or above, which leads to cell death by the mechanism of coagulation necrosis14. Numerous clinical studies have documented the use of MWA to treat tumours in the liver, lung, kidney, and breast17. Currently, available MWA systems in clinical use operate at frequencies of 915 MHz, 2.45 GHz, or 5.8 GHz18,19. The latter frequency provides faster heating and shorter antenna lengths. In previous studies, the authors have demonstrated that the use of selective MWA within the adrenal gland can be used to target areas of 1 cm3 or below (the typical size of an APA or aldosterone-producing nodule), in both adrenal glands, while preserving the adjacent adrenal cortex and without inducing post-procedural adrenocortical insufficiency20. It has been demonstrated that sublethal hyperthermia at temperatures expected within the transitional zone between ablated and non-ablated tissue induces only transient inhibition of steroidogenesis20. The development of precise MWA devices which selectively target pathological tissue types requires a comprehensive knowledge of the dielectric properties of both normal and pathological tissue.

Dielectric properties play an important role in determining the efficiency and effectiveness of the MWA treatment of all tissues including adenomas of the adrenal gland15,18,21. Overall, dielectric tissue properties determine how they interact with the microwave energy emitted by a thermal ablation antenna, affecting the amount of energy absorbed and the resulting heating22. Adrenal tumours typically have low to intermediate dielectric values, which can result in more efficient absorption of microwave energy and better compared to heating13,15,18. However, the surrounding tissues, such as blood vessels and muscle, have higher dielectric values and can act as barriers to microwave energy transmission, reducing the amount of energy that reaches the tumour13. This reduction in delivered energy can result in a less- effective ablation and in the need for higher energy levels and longer treatment times to be used. Therefore, understanding the dielectric properties of adrenal tumours and surrounding tissues is essential for the effective planning and execution of MWA procedures. Accurate characterisation of these properties can therefore inform optimal energy levels and antenna placement for efficient and effective ablation, to improve treatment outcomes for patients15,23,24.

Significant experimental work has been performed on characterising the dielectric properties of various biological tissues including breast, tumour, lung, kidney, skin, bone, and heart among others24–27. Although a large number of experimental studies were conducted acquiring the dielectric properties of various biological tissues, to our knowledge only three studies have reported the dielectric properties of adrenal glands with only one study reporting properties of human adrenal glands18,21,28. Peyman et al.28 reported the in vivo dielectric properties of the outer layer of the porcine adrenal gland (N = 12), referred to as the adrenal cortex, for a frequency range of 50 MHz–20 GHz. However, it is important to note that this study did not report the dielectric properties of the adrenal medulla, which limits its applicability for developing comprehensive anatomically and electrically accurate computer models of adrenal glands. Shahzad et al.21 reported the ex vivo dielectric properties of bovine adrenal glands (N = 12), for a frequency range of 500 MHz–20 GHz. This study reported properties of both the adrenal cortex and the medulla. However, it is important to note that this work was conducted on animal tissues, which may limit the direct applicability of the findings to human tissues. The differences in tissue composition and structure between bovine and human adrenal glands could influence the dielectric properties, and thus, further studies on human tissues are necessary to validate and extend these results. Bottiglieri et al.18 reported the ex vivo dielectric properties of ovine (N = 8) and human (N = 6) adrenal glands for a frequency range of 500 MHz–8.5 GHz. In this study, the authors were unable to examine the inner portions of the human adrenal glands, specifically the medulla, and their findings only pertain to the normal adrenal cortex of human glands. Moreover, the ex vivo studies may not perfectly represent in vivo conditions particularly when the time from excision to measurement is long. Further, the literature suggested that there is a noticeable lack of dielectric properties data on adrenal tumours. Therefore, it is also necessary to characterise the dielectric properties of adrenal tumours to develop and optimise MWA-based selective treatment of shallow APAs and aldosterone-secreting nodules.

This study presents new data which outlines the detailed human dielectric properties of the normal adrenal gland which includes the capsule, cortex, and medulla. We also describe the dielectric properties of APA and compare these with non-aldosterone-producing adrenocortical adenomas (NAPACA) causing Cushing Syndrome and Phaeochromocytoma. The data presented herein will help inform treatment planning and future applicator probe design for improved selective, precision ablation of APA. To this end, a total of four adrenal gland samples were acquired from four different patients who have undergone adrenalectomy at the University Hospital Galway, Ireland. Two of the patients included in the study were diagnosed with Cushing's syndrome, while the other two were diagnosed with PA and Pheochromocytoma. The samples were sectioned by the pathologist to access the medulla, cortex, and capsule. The pathologist highlighted the tumour regions within each adrenal gland. Once the adrenal gland samples were sectioned, the ex vivo dielectric properties were measured using an open-ended coaxial probe across the microwave frequency range of 0.5–8.5 GHz. To the best of the knowledge of the authors, this study is the first to document the dielectric properties of the human adrenal medulla, capsule, and adrenal tumour from patients diagnosed with Cushing syndrome in the literature. Moreover, the dielectric properties of tumours from patients diagnosed with Conn’s syndrome and Pheochromocytoma measured in this study are compared with the dielectric properties of human adrenal glands reported by Bottiglieri et al.18. The knowledge of dielectric properties acquired from patients diagnosed with different adrenal disorders will help in the development and optimisation of MWA-based technologies for hypertension treatment. By understanding the dielectric properties of tissues affected by hypertension, medical professionals can better predict how those tissues will respond to electromagnetic (EM) energy used in MWA-based treatments. This knowledge can help to optimise treatment parameters and improve treatment efficacy and safety18. Furthermore, comparing the dielectric properties of healthy and diseased tissues could facilitate the development of more targeted and personalised MWA-based treatments for hypertension.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: section "Materials and methods" discusses the source of human adrenal gland samples, the methodology used to measure the dielectric properties of adrenal glands, the measurement protocol and system uncertainty analysis of the dielectric properties measurement equipment and the fitting model used on dielectric properties data. Section "Results and discussion" presents the results and discussion on the dielectric properties measurement of ex vivo human adrenal glands including the medulla, cortex, capsule, and tumours. Finally, conclusions are drawn in section "Conclusions".

Materials and methods

Source of tissues

The human adrenal glands were acquired from a total of four patients (N = 4) with informed consent. The ethical approval for the experiments was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee, Galway University Hospitals, Galway, Ireland. The patients were diagnosed with adrenal disorders including Conn’s syndrome (N = 1), Cushing’s syndrome (N = 2), and Pheochromocytoma (N = 1). The mean age of patients was 48.75 ± 14.14 years. Two of the four patients were female. The unilateral adrenalectomy of each patient was performed at the University Hospital Galway, Ireland using a retroperitoneoscopic approach. During the unilateral adrenalectomy of each of the four patients, the entire adrenal gland was excised including the adrenal cortex, medulla, and tumour. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Two of the patients had left unilateral retroperitoneoscopic adrenalectomy whereas the other two patients had right unilateral adrenalectomy. The pre-operative radiological dimensions of the tumour varied among the patients with different diagnoses. For the patient diagnosed with Conn's syndrome, the tumour size measured 9 × 7 × 4 mm. Meanwhile, the two patients diagnosed with Cushing's syndrome presented with tumour sizes of 55 × 45 × 15 mm and 34 × 15 × 21 mm, respectively. As for the patient diagnosed with Pheochromocytoma, the tumour size measured 52 × 41 × 46 mm. After each unilateral adrenalectomy in the surgical room, the samples were enclosed in a closed plastic container and transported to the pathology room of the University Hospital Galway, Ireland. The approximate time elapsed between the surgery and the dielectric properties measurement was less than 10 min to ensure ex vivo measurements closely represent in vivo measurements. The samples were not preserved in any preservative liquid during the time between surgery and dielectric properties measurements. Dielectric properties measurement setup.

The ex vivo dielectric properties of human adrenal glands were measured in the pathology laboratory of the hospital. The measurement location of the medulla, cortex, capsule, and tumour was univocally identified by the pathologist before taking the dielectric properties measurements. At each measurement location of the sample, a total of three measurements were performed. Additionally, histological confirmation was performed to ensure the accuracy of the tissue identification and to validate the integrity of the measured properties. Accordingly, for the four patients, a total of 192 dielectric measurements were recorded and analysed. The probe was cleaned with an alcohol wipe before each measurement. The probe was placed at maximum locations of a given tissue type, depending on the size of the sample. Measurement protocol and system uncertainty analysis

Calibration of the equipment was performed with three standard loads (open (air), short (shorting block), and load (deionized water))29,30. The temperature of the deionized water during each measurement session was 19.55 ± 1 °C. The calibration was verified with 6 validation measurements of the dielectric properties of 0.1 M NaCl (saline) solution at 21.6 °C29,31. An estimation of the uncertainty of the measurement accuracy was performed for each frequency point and averaged over the frequency range. The Cole–Cole model is computationally more complex than the Debye model and cannot be easily expressed in the time domain for tissue dielectric simulations25. The Debye model can be easily expressed in both time and frequency domains and is less computationally expensive25. The Debye model parameters are widely used in EM numerical simulations33. Results and discussion

This section presents the ex vivo dielectric properties of human adrenal glands. The dielectric properties of the medulla, cortex, and tumour are separately presented, to understand the variations in dielectric properties within each adrenal tissue. This individual analysis will provide an intra-tissue comparison of the dielectric properties of each tissue obtained from different adrenal diagnoses. Consequently, the measured dielectric properties of the cortex and tumours in the current study are compared with the existing literature. Finally, the two-pole Debye model parameters for the mean dielectric properties of the medulla, cortex, capsule, and tumour for different adrenal disorders are presented.

The relative permittivity and conductivity of the medulla are shown in Fig. 3a,b respectively for patients diagnosed with Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma. The solid curves in Fig. 3 show the mean dielectric properties across different measurement locations of the medulla for each patient, while the error bars on each curve indicate the range of variation in dielectric properties across different measurement locations. Dielectric properties of cortex

The relative permittivity and conductivity of the cortex are shown in Fig. 4a, b for patients diagnosed with Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma. It can be observed from Fig. 4a that the patient with Pheochromocytoma exhibits the highest relative permittivity and conductivity of the cortex, as compared to those with Conn's syndrome and Cushing's syndrome. Further, a comparative analysis of the dielectric properties of the human adrenal cortex from the literature is presented in Fig. 4. Since the dielectric properties are highly species-dependent37, the results are compared only with measurements of Bottiglieri et al.18 who reported the ex vivo dielectric properties of the human adrenal cortex in the frequency range of 0.5–8 GHz. It can be observed from Fig. 4a that the relative permittivity of the human adrenal cortex reported by Bottiglieri et al.18 agrees with the relative permittivity of the cortex of the Pheochromocytoma patient reported in this study, however, the conductivity values showed a significant difference. Dielectric properties (a) Relative Permittivity (b) Conductivity for the cortex of ex vivo human adrenal glands.

The variation in the dielectric properties of the cortex between Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma could be attributed to their different underlying causes. Conn's syndrome results from an excess of aldosterone, which regulates salt and water balance, in the adrenal glands38. The overproduction of aldosterone can lead to changes in the composition of the water content and salts in the adrenal gland, including an increase in the number and size of cells in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex39,40. On the other hand, Cushing's syndrome results from an overproduction of cortisol, which has several functions in the body, by either the adrenal or pituitary gland39. The overproduction of cortisol can lead to changes in the structure and composition of the adrenal gland, including an increase in the size and number of cells in the zona fasciculate and zona reticularis of the adrenal cortex40. Pheochromocytoma is a rare condition caused by tumours in the adrenal gland or the sympathetic nervous system that produce excessive amounts of catecholamines, hormones that regulate blood pressure and heart rate38,39. Both Conn’s syndrome and Cushing's syndrome primarily affect the adrenal cortex38, whereas Pheochromocytoma primarily affects the adrenal medulla. Overall, the underlying changes in the tissue composition and structure in these medical conditions can lead to variations in the dielectric properties of the adrenal cortex. Dielectric properties of adrenal tumours

The relative permittivity and conductivity of the tumours are shown in Fig. 5a,b for patients diagnosed with Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma, respectively. It can be observed from Fig. 5a that the Pheochromocytoma tumour exhibits the highest relative permittivity and conductivity of the tumour, as compared to tumours of Conn's syndrome and Cushing's syndrome. Further, a comparative analysis of the dielectric properties of ex vivo human aldosterone-producing adenoma and Pheochromocytoma from the literature is presented in Fig. 5. Bottiglieri et al.18 have reported the dielectric properties of human aldosterone-producing adenoma and Pheochromocytoma in the frequency range of 0.5–8 GHz. The dielectric properties of the human aldosterone-producing adenoma reported by Bottiglieri et al.18 are consistent with the results of this study, with an average percentage difference of 10% for mean relative permittivity and 9% for conductivity. However, there is a notable difference in the dielectric properties of Pheochromocytoma reported by Bottiglieri et al.18 compared to the results of this study, with an average percentage difference of 17% for mean relative permittivity and 29% for conductivity. The study by Bottiglieri et al.18 did not specify whether the reported dielectric properties were for the Pheochromocytoma tumour or the cortex of Pheochromocytoma samples. In contrast, the current study reported the dielectric properties specifically for the Pheochromocytoma tumour. ALRAs are characterised by their high lipid content41, which likely contributes to the significantly lower dielectric properties of Cushing syndrome tumours compared to Pheochromocytoma and Conn’s syndrome tumours. Dielectric properties of capsule

The relative permittivity and conductivity of the capsule are shown in Fig. 6a,b for the patient diagnosed with Conn's syndrome. The patients diagnosed with Cushing's syndrome and Pheochromocytoma did not exhibit the presence of the capsule. The dielectric properties of the capsule were found to be higher when compared to the dielectric properties of the medulla in Conn’s syndrome. The differences in physiological functions between the adrenal medulla and capsule can lead to differences in their cellular and molecular composition42, which in turn can affect their dielectric properties. For example, the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla contain large amounts of catecholamines and other neurotransmitters42, which can affect the dielectric properties of the tissue. In contrast, the adrenal capsule is primarily composed of collagen and other connective tissue components43, which likely have different dielectric properties compared to the cells of the adrenal medulla. Therefore, the differences in dielectric properties between the adrenal capsule and medulla in Conn's syndrome are likely due to both the pathological changes occurring in the gland and the inherent differences in their physiological functions and cellular composition.Fig. 6Dielectric properties (a) Relative Permittivity (b) Conductivity for the capsule of ex vivo human adrenal gland.

The Debye model is a mathematical model that describes the behaviour of dielectric properties of a material as a function of frequency. The model is commonly used to model biological tissues due to their complex dielectric behaviour, which is influenced by the presence of various molecular and cellular structures25,44. Conclusions

This study has reported the ex vivo dielectric properties of human adrenal glands obtained from four different patients who underwent unilateral adrenalectomy at the University Hospital Galway, Ireland. Two of the patients included in this study were diagnosed with Cushing’s syndrome, while the remaining two patients were diagnosed with Conn’s syndrome and Pheochromocytoma. The dielectric properties were characterised for a frequency range of 0.5–8.5 GHz by using an OECL probe. To the best of the knowledge of the authors, this study has documented first time the dielectric properties of the human adrenal medulla, capsule and different tumour types associated with adrenal glands from patients diagnosed with Conn’s syndrome, Cushing’s syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma. A two-pole Debye model was fitted on the dielectric properties of the investigated adrenal tissues from all three different diagnosed adrenal disorders. The dielectric properties of each of these tissues from all four patients were compared at three different frequency points including 915 MHz, 2.45 GHz, and 5.8 GHz. These frequencies are commonly used for designing an interstitial antenna for the application of MWA.

The results of this study indicate that there exists a significant difference in the dielectric properties of tumours from patients with Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and Pheochromocytoma, with Cushing's syndrome tumour exhibiting the lowest dielectric properties due to its lipid-rich composition compared to Conn's syndrome and Pheochromocytoma tumours. The dielectric properties of the adrenal medulla and cortex also show a notable difference among patients diagnosed with these three syndromes, attributed to the distinct nature of diseases as Conn's and Cushing's syndrome affect the adrenal cortex while Pheochromocytoma affects the adrenal medulla. Moreover, patients with Cushing’s syndrome typically experience more severe hypokalemia and hypernatremia than those with Conn’s syndrome. Consequently, the variation in dielectric properties between patients with these two syndromes is likely due to the electrolyte imbalances caused by these conditions. Pheochromocytoma, on the other hand, arises from tumours in the adrenal medulla that secrete excess amounts of catecholamines, which can affect the dielectric properties of the adrenal medulla.

The dielectric properties of human adrenal glands presented in this paper can be utilised to create precise anatomical models to assess new medical imaging and therapeutic devices for addressing adrenal gland-induced hypertension. Moreover, these properties will be useful in developing and optimising MWA therapy for adrenal tumours. While this paper reported ex vivo dielectric properties of human adrenal glands, it would be more insightful to perform the in vivo measurements to better understand how the dielectric properties of the glands change under physiological conditions. Additionally, further comprehensive studies are necessary to confirm these preliminary findings and to better understand the influence of patient-specific variables on the dielectric properties of human adrenal glands. Future research with an expanded patient cohort will allow for a more robust analysis, potentially leading to more definitive conclusions regarding the impact of age, sex, and other factors on tissue dielectric properties. The accurate characterisation of these properties will help in designing an optimal treatment protocol that delivers the appropriate amount of energy to the tumour while minimising damage to surrounding healthy tissue.
