Cognitive functioning predicts vulnerability to financial exploitation in APOE e4 carriers
Authors: Gali H. Weissberger, Hussein N. Yassine, Jenna Axelrod, Laura Fenton, Daisy T. Noriega-Makarskyy, Cassidy Molinare, Jordan T. Williams, Emma Oyen, Paige Kim, Mark H.C. Lai, S. Duke Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An emerging body of work suggests that financial exploitation vulnerability (FEV) may be an early marker of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). We examined how APOE e4 genotype and cognition at baseline interact to predict subsequent FEV one year later. Participants were 95 older adults without dementia aged 50 or older (M age = 69.09, SD = 7.51; 72.63 % female; 72.63 % White non-Hispanic). Participants completed two annual assessments that included comprehensive neuropsychological testing and a measure of FEV. Saliva samples for APOE genotyping were collected. Linear regression models regressed Year 2 FEV on APOE, global cognition, and their interaction. Age, sex, education, and Year 1 FEV scores were covaried. Models were re-run considering cognitive domains separately (memory, language, and attention/working memory and executive functioning). Main effects of APOE and cognition were not found. However, cognition and APOE status interacted to predict Year 2 FEV. Specifically, worse cognitive functioning predicted higher FEV only for e4 carriers. Cognitive domain analyses revealed more nuanced relationships. Findings suggest that cognition is a relevant risk factor of FEV for e4 carriers, and support the emerging idea that FEV may be an early marker of AD-related neuropathological changes.

Introduction

A recent body of work suggests that financial exploitation vulnerability (FEV) may be an early marker of AD [1]. The goal of the present study was to examine whether cognitive functioning predicts one-year change in FEV, and whether presence of an Apolipoprotein-E epsilon 4 allele (APOE e4), a known risk factor of AD, modifies this effect.

Declining cognition has been demonstrated to be a relevant correlate of FEV due to its effects on financial decision making and related skills [1]. Additionally, evidence points to FEV as a possible early marker of AD [1]; however, not all individuals with FEV go on to develop AD [1]. It may be the case that certain individuals evidence a stronger connection between cognitive functioning and FEV. The APOE e4 allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, with carriers of at least one e4 allele showing greater risk of developing AD than non-carriers [2]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated poorer cognitive functioning and more pronounced cognitive decline in older adults with the e4 allele relative to non-e4 carriers (e.g., [3]). Thus, FEV may be particularly high for those who evidence poor cognitive performance and who have a greater genetic risk of developing AD.

This study examined whether baseline cognitive functioning predicts FEV at one-year follow-up, and whether presence of the e4 allele strengthens the cognition-FEV link in older adults without dementia. We hypothesized that lower global cognition scores would predict FEV at follow-up. Second, we hypothesized that e4 carriers would exhibit higher FEV. Third, we hypothesized that the predictive relationship of cognition to FEV would be strongest amongst e4 carriers, given a link between AD risk and FEV [1]. In a set of additional analyses, relationships were examined separately across cognitive domains.

Methods

Participants and procedure

A total of 108 participants aged 50 or older were recruited into an ongoing longitudinal study of finances, cognition and health (see Supplemental File 1 for recruitment details). Study measures were completed in a laboratory setting. All study procedures were approved by the institutional review board of the University of Southern California (IRB approval number: HS-20–01024; Study: Finance, Cognition, and Default Network in Aging). All study participants provided their informed consent to participate in the study.

Study measures

Study measures included the Perceived Financial Vulnerability Scale (PFVS; [4]) which assesses financial awareness and psychological vulnerability related to personal finances, a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment in which tests were grouped into language, memory, and attention/working memory/executive functioning domains, and APOE genotyping. Covariates of age, sex, and education were also assessed. Supplemental File 1 includes detailed descriptions of study measures.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in R Statistical Software 4.4.2. Bivariate analyses between Year 2 PFVS scores and all other modeled variables were first performed. Then a hierarchical linear regression model was conducted to examine the effects of global cognitive functioning at Year 1, APOE genotype, and their interaction, on Year 2 PFVS scores. Step 1 of the model included covariates and Year 1 PFVS scores. Step 2 of the model added the main effects of global cognition at Year 1 and APOE genotype. Finally, Step 3 added the cognition by APOE interaction. Interactions were further probed using the PROCESS script for R. Assumption checks and power analyses are described in Supplemental File 1.

Results

A final sample of 95 participants (M age = 69.09, SD = 7.51; 72.63 % female; 72.63 % White non-Hispanic) of the initial 108 recruited were included in the present study following exclusion of participants who did not have data on APOE (n = 10) or who carried the e2/e4 allele combination (n = 3). Sample characteristics and correlations between study variables are summarized in Supplemental Table 1.

With regards to the hierarchical linear regression model, which regressed PFVS on global cognitive functioning and APOE status, main effects of global cognition or APOE status were not found (Step 2, Table 1; both ps ≥ 0.394). However, the interaction effect between cognitive functioning and APOE status was significant (Step 3, Table 1; B = 1.751, SE = 0.59, β = 0.453, p = 0.004), accounting for 5.6 % additional variance (ΔR2) in PFVS Year 2 scores, compared to the model without the interaction. Importantly, results remained the same using robust regression (rlm() function of the MASS package in R).

The significant interaction was further probed using PROCESS for R by examining the effects of cognitive composite scores at Year 1 on PFVS at Year 2 separately for e4 and non-e4 carriers. This revealed that higher Year 1 global cognition scores were significantly associated with lower PFVS Year 2 scores for e4 carriers (B = −1.12, SE = 0.49, β = −0.61, p = 0.025) but not for non-e4 carriers (B = 0.63, SE = 0.36, β = 0.34, p = 0.082; see Fig. 1). Results of regression models separately by cognitive domains are summarized in Supplemental File 2, Supplemental Table 2, and Supplemental Figures 1–2.

Discussion

In this study, we examined whether cognitive functioning predicts change in FEV, and whether AD genetic risk moderates this association. Contrary to our first hypothesis, cognitive function at the first annual assessment did not predict FEV at the second annual assessment. Additionally, participants with the e4 allele did not exhibit heightened FEV. However, our third hypothesis was confirmed. Specifically, higher global cognition at Year 1 was associated with lower PFVS score at Year 2 for e4 carriers.

Findings from this study suggest that cognitive functioning plays a significant role in predicting FEV specifically when someone is an APOE e4 carrier. Of note, the interaction effect between APOE and global cognition explained an additional 5.6 % of variance in PFVS scores at Year 2, suggesting a small to medium effect size (f2 = 0.10; [60]). This modest effect has both theoretical and practical significance in that it suggests that the effect of cognition on FEV may be particularly impactful for genetically at-risk individuals. This finding aligns with a recent study which demonstrated that older age and lower working memory predicted heightened susceptibility to phishing only in e4 carriers [5]. There are several speculative possibilities for the observed interaction in this study. First, e4 carriers may be more susceptible to deception or social influence due to compromised neural integrity associated with the e4 allele in regions specifically vulnerable to deception detection (e.g., the amygdala, see [6]; also see [7] for review). Thus, reduced structural and functional integrity of specific brain regions in e4 carriers may compromise abilities critical for mitigating FEV such as deception detection, and this may synergistically interact with other cognitive changes to increase FEV. A second possibility may be that e4 carriers have less awareness of cognitive difficulties compared to non-e4 carriers, and thus carry out less compensatory measures to mitigate FEV when faced with these difficulties (e.g., financial consultation; seeking help from social supports). Consistent with this, a body of work has demonstrated that participants at higher risk of developing AD are less aware of their cognitive deficits (e.g., [8]). Of course, future research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms behind the cognition by e4 status interaction.

Examining domains separately revealed a more nuanced picture and suggested that the global cognition by e4 interaction may have been driven mainly by the language domain (comprised of confrontation naming, animal fluency, and phonemic fluency). A handful of previous studies have implicated these cognitive functions in FEV ([1] for review) and related financial constructs (e.g., financial literacy, [9]). Importantly, semantic memory is known to be affected early by AD pathology [10]. In the context of FEV, impaired semantic memory may impact one’s ability to retrieve stored financial concepts important for making key financial decisions (a notion discussed by [9] in regards to financial literacy), though further work is needed to explore this possibility.

This study has limitations. First, participants were mostly of non-Hispanic White origin and highly educated which reduces the generalizability of the study. A second limitation relates to the small number of e4 carriers. Future research with larger samples of e4 carriers will be useful for corroborating study findings. Finally, the data were collected across just two assessment periods. Examining these relationships with more assessment time points will help elucidate how FEV and cognition evolve together over time, and how AD risk factors may strengthen these relationships. In this regard, incorporating other AD biomarkers will further elucidate the relationship between AD-related neuropathological changes, cognition, and FEV.

The findings of this study are novel and are consistent with the emerging idea that FEV may be an early marker of AD-related neuropathological changes. Findings suggest that cognitive functioning may be particularly relevant in predicting FEV for those at greater risk of AD, such as APOE e4 carriers. Findings also suggest that certain cognitive functions may hold greater relevance than others for predicting FEV, and that these relationships may differ depending on other key risk factors. More broadly, the results underscore the importance of accounting for the multidimensional nature of established risk factors and their differential predictive utility with respect to FEV.
