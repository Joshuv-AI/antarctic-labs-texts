Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, type 2 diabetes, and liver-related outcomes: Secondary data analysis of a prospective recruited cohort
Authors: Yu Wang, Lintao Dan, Tian Fu, Yuhao Sun, Jie Chen, Ren Mao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The association of vitamin D deficiency, which is prevalent in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with liver disease and related mortality has not been quantified. Our study aimed to (1) investigate whether there is a synergistic association of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM with liver-related outcomes and (2) explore whether high 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations are associated with a lower risk of liver-related outcomes in T2DM.Method:Leveraging the data from UK Biobank, we conducted 2 studies: study I assessed the joint associations of vitamin D deficiency [25(OH)D <50 nmol/L] and T2DM with liver-related outcomes among 439,276 participants, and study II explored the associations of vitamin D status with liver-related outcomes among 21,519 individuals with T2DM. Baseline T2DM was identified through medication, laboratory test, and electronic health-related records. Serum 25(OH)D was measured by direct competitive chemiluminescent immunoassay. Liver-related outcomes included 6 liver disease end points and mortality by overall liver disease, chronic liver disease, and severe liver disease.Results:During an average follow-up duration of 11.6 years, we observed a significant positive additive interaction effect (all synergy index>1.0) of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency on the risk of liver-related outcomes. Compared with participants without either T2DM or vitamin D deficiency, the multivariable-adjusted HRs of overall liver diseases were 1.29 for participants without T2DM but with vitamin D deficiency, 1.73 for participants with T2DM but without vitamin D deficiency, and 2.19 for participants with both T2DM and vitamin D deficiency. In individuals with T2DM, we observed that participants without vitamin D deficiency were inversely associated with incident liver disease and related mortality (multivariable-adjusted HRs 0.41–0.81) when compared with individuals with vitamin D deficiency.Conclusions:There are positive synergistic associations of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM with liver-related outcomes. Inverse associations between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and liver-related outcomes were observed in individuals with T2DM.

INTRODUCTION

Individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are comorbid, with a heavy burden of liver disease. The global prevalence of NAFLD in patients with T2DM is 55.5%,1 while other studies have shown a prevalence varying from 17% to 70% depending on the types and severity of liver disease.2,3 Since liver disease can exacerbate the course of T2DM and is an important cause of death in T2DM,4,5 there is an urgency to identify potential modifiable risk factors for liver disease in T2DM.

Vitamin D deficiency is a risk factor for liver disease in general population.6,7 As the most common indicator of serum vitamin D status, 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] was widely used to define vitamin D deficiency [25(OH)D <50 nmol/L] in epidemiological studies.8 Studies in the general population have reported positive associations between vitamin D deficiency and risk of NAFLD, cirrhosis, and HCC.6,9 These associations were also supported by experimental work.10 Vitamin D deficiency is common in individuals with T2DM,11,12 but the impact of vitamin D deficiency on liver-related outcomes in T2DM has not been quantified. Previous studies showed that both vitamin D deficiency and T2DM can disturb adipocyte regulation and glucose metabolism.13 These pathways are important in the pathology of chronic liver disease.14 This raised the question whether there is a positive joint association of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM with the risk of adverse liver-related outcomes. The second question is, if there is a positive synergistic association of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM, whether higher 25(OH)D level is still inversely associated with risk of liver-related outcomes in individuals with T2DM.

Therefore, the study first investigated whether there was a joint effect of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM on the risk of liver diseases and related mortality (study I), and then explored the association of serum 25(OH)D with these liver-related outcomes in patients with T2DM (study II).

METHODS

Study population

The study leveraged data from a large-scale prospective cohort, the UK Biobank, which is an ongoing program that enrolled 500,000 participants at 22 assessment centers across the UK in 2006–2010.15 Each participant would sign a consent form and receive a set of self-reported electronic questionnaires, face-to-face interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. The health-related outcomes of participants can be followed with external links to national databases including inpatient data, cancer registry, primary care data, and death registry. The UK Biobank study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Electronic written consent were obtained at recruitment. This work was conducted under application number 79612. The North West–Haydock Research Ethics Committee granted ethical approval to use the UK Biobank database (REC reference: 21/NW/0157).

Individuals with T2DM were identified based on recognized criteria16,17: (1) diagnoses recorded in inpatient data [International Classification of Diseases (ICD), ICD-10 codes E11], primary care data (mapped read codes to ICD codes), or self-reported data; (2) random blood glucose level ≥11.1 mmol/L or fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L; (3) blood glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥48 mmol/mol; and (4) used antidiabetes drugs collected by a baseline touchscreen questionnaire and verified in the face-to-face interview. Details of the definition are presented in Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605.

As shown in Figure 1, we excluded (1) participants with incomplete information for identification of T2DM (n=1951); (2) participants with baseline T1DM (n=2644); (3) participants with unavailable information for serum 25(OH)D concentration (n=51,916); (4) participants with liver disease at baseline (n=3219); and (5) participants with serum 25(OH)D level outside the normal distribution (n = 3455) based on Rosner generalized extreme studentized deviate test.18 Finally, we included 417,757 participants without T2DM and 21,519 individuals with T2DM in study I and 21,519 individuals with T2DM in study II.

Measurement of serum 25(OH)D

At baseline, participants underwent blood sample collection; the samples would be stored and analyzed under rigorous quality control systems. Serum 25(OH)D concentration was measured using a direct competitive chemiluminescent immunoassay method (DiaSorin Liaison XL platform, detection range: 10–375 nmol/L) and the coefficients of variation for internal quality control samples ranged from 5.04% to 6.14%. The assay performance was also evaluated by a third-party quality control.19 More details about the procedure have been published online.19 Vitamin D deficiency was defined as serum 25(OH)D concentration less than 50 nmol/L, which is widely accepted as a threshold for deficiency that is associated with unfavorable health outcomes.8

Ascertainment of liver disease and related mortality

The outcomes of interest included overall liver disease, NAFLD, liver cirrhosis, HCC, chronic liver disease (CLD), severe liver disease (SLD), liver disease–specific mortality, CLD-specific mortality, and SLD-specific mortality. These outcomes were considered based on clinical significance and the prevalence in the study population. Overall liver disease was the composite outcome of all studied liver diseases. NAFLD was the most common liver comorbidity in patients with T2DM.5 Cirrhosis and HCC were important life-threatening liver end points.5 Two common composite end points, CLD (fibrosis, cirrhosis, alcohol-associated liver diseases, and chronic hepatitis) and SLD (cirrhosis, decompensated liver disease, HCC, and liver transplantation), were included.20,21 The ranges of these 2 composite end points overlap somewhat, but CLD emphasizes the protracted course of the disease, while SLD emphasizes the severity of the disease, both of which are composite life-threatening end points widely used in research.20,21

These outcomes were identified through inpatient data, primary care data, cancer registry, and death registry. ICD codes (ICD-10) used for these outcomes are presented in Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605. Self-reported information was also considered to exclude prevalent events. Cause-specific mortality was identified using the ICD-10 code recorded in the contributory or underlying cause of death. The Audit Commission review of 2009-2010 showed that the overall accuracy of ICD-10 diagnostic codes was 89%.22.

Assessment of covariates

In this study, we used predefined covariates including demographic factors [age, sex (female, male), race (White, non-White), Townsend deprivation index, education (college, below college)], lifestyle factors [body mass index (BMI), diet (healthy, unhealthy), alcohol consumption (none to moderate, heavy), smoking status (never, ever), physical activity (adequate, inadequate)], laboratory measurement (systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, HbA1c, triglycerides, total cholesterol, apolipoprotein a), and serum 25(OH)D-related factors (sampling season, and use of vitamin D, multivitamin and mineral supplement). Townsend deprivation index was a measurement of socioeconomic deprivation status. Heavy consumption of alcohol was defined as more than 14 g/d for women and 28 g/d for men according to US dietary guidelines.23 A healthy diet was defined according to the frequency of seven major food groups collected by valid food frequency questionnaires.24–26 We also calculated Charlson comorbidity index, which was constructed based on 17 comorbidities with assigned weights associated with ICD codes from inpatient data.27 More details are presented in Supplemental Table S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605. For the missing values of covariates, the median values and the most populated category were imputed for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

Statistical analysis

According to vitamin D deficiency status (<50 nmol/L) and diagnosis of T2DM, we summarized the baseline characteristics of participants. The continuous and categorical variables were described as means (SDs) and numbers (percentages, %), respectively.

Two Cox proportional hazards models were used in study I and study II: The minimally adjusted model was adjusted for age, sex, and race; the fully adjusted model was further adjusted for Townsend deprivation index, education, diet quality, body mass index, alcohol consumption, smoking status, Charlson comorbidity index, sampling season, and use of vitamin D, multivitamin and mineral supplement, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, HbA1c, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and apolipoprotein a. Based on the Cox models, we evaluated the HRs and 95% CIs and calculated person-years from the baseline date to the date of diagnosis, death, loss, or the end of follow-up (September 2021 for participants in England, July 2021 for participants in Scottish, and February 2018 for participants in Wales), whichever came first.28 The proportional hazard assumption was confirmed with the Schoenfeld residuals method.29

In study I, we evaluated the joint associations of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM with incident liver diseases and related mortality among 439,276 participants (417,757 participants without T2DM and 21,519 individuals with T2DM). The exposure was the combined status of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM, and we set participants without both T2DM and vitamin D deficiency as the reference group. The additive interaction of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM was assessed by 3 indicators: (1) the relative excess risk due to interaction; (2) attributable proportion due to interaction; and (3) the synergy index.30 A relative excess risk due to interaction or attributable proportion more than zero and an synergy index more than 1 represent positive interaction or more than additivity.

In study II, we explored vitamin D status and serum 25(OH)D concentration in quartiles and the risk of liver diseases and related mortality among 21,519 individuals with T2DM. Trends for the associations were estimated using the median value of each category. Restricted curve splines were used to visualize and evaluate the potential dose-response association with 3 knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. The overall significance of the spline curve was tested using the likelihood ratio test.31 Subgroup analyses were conducted among participants stratified by age (<60 or ≥60 y), sex (female or male), BMI (<25, 25–<30, or ≥30 kg/m2), and assessment centers (Southern or Northern UK). The multiplicative interaction was calculated using likelihood ratio tests. Given that Black participants with T2DM frequently have lower vitamin D levels but have similar levels of bioavailable vitamin D,32 we tried stratification analysis between White participants (n=18,708) and Black participants (n=811). Due to the limited sample size and a varied threshold for vitamin D deficiency in Black participants, we only explored the associations between 25(OH)D concentrations and overall liver diseases using the restricted curve spline method. To verify the robustness of the results, we performed several sensitivity analyses based on the fully adjusted model. First, we excluded outcomes of interest that occurred in the first one (n=104) and 2 years (n=234) of follow-up to reduce the potential reverse causation bias. To further address the seasonal variation in serum 25(OH)D concentration, we applied May-standardized serum 25(OH)D concentration.33–35 This method generated an approximate 25(OH)D concentrations in May by regressing 25(OH)D on the month of sampling and adjusting for age and sex. The mean 25(OH)D concentration in May (46.1 nmol/L) was comparable to the annual mean 25(OH)D concentration for all participants (45.6 nmol/L).33 Finally, we explored the association of serum 25(OH)D with the risk of liver disease and death in a narrower interval by grouping serum 25(OH)D concentrations in 7 tertiles or into 4 categories (<25, 25–<50, 50–<75, ≥75 nmol/L).

All statistical analyses were performed with R 4.1.1. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and because of potentially inflated type I errors due to multiple tests, the p-value was corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate, with a false-discovery rate of 0.05. In this study, we used the Q value to represent the corrected p-value.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study population according to vitamin D deficiency and baseline T2DM are shown in Table 1. Among 439,276 participants, the mean (SD) concentration of 25(OH)D was 48.8 nmol/L, with 54.5% of participants without T2DM and 67.5% of participants with T2DM having vitamin D deficiency status, respectively. Of the 21,519 included participants with T2DM, 37.3% were female with a mean (SD) age of 59.7 (7.1) years. The mean (SD) concentration of 25(OH)D was 42.3 (20.1) nmol/L, with 14,527 (67.5%) participants having vitamin D deficiency status. Participants with higher serum 25(OH)D concentrations were more likely to be nonsmokers, supplement users, with a higher educational level, lower BMI, more physical activity, and lower HbA1c level (All p<0.05).

Study I, joint association of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM

In study I, we documented 13,445 incident liver disease and 911 deaths resulting from overall liver disease during the follow-up. As shown in Table 2, we observed incrementally increased HRs of overall liver disease by the combined status of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency. In the fully adjusted model, compared with participants without either T2DM or vitamin D deficiency, increased risk of liver disease was observed in participants without T2DM and with vitamin D deficiency (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.24–1.34), participants with T2DM and without vitamin D deficiency (HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.56–1.91), and participants with both T2DM and vitamin D deficiency (HR 2.19, 95% CI 2.04–2.35). Trends of change in the estimate of the combined status of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency were consistent when evaluating other liver-related outcomes. Further analysis revealed significant positive synergistic associations of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency with all of the studied liver outcomes (Table 3, all synergy index >1.0 with Q value <0.05).

Study II, serum 25(OH)D and liver outcomes among individuals with T2DM

In study II, during an average follow-up duration of 11.6 years, we documented 1616 incident liver disease, 1052 NAFLD, 505 cirrhosis, 74 HCC, 430 CLD, and 518 SLD events. During the follow-up, 179, 109, and 132 deaths resulting from overall liver disease, CLD, and SLD were observed. As shown in Table 4, based on the fully adjusted model, compared with those in deficiency status, participants with 25(OH)D concentrations higher than 50 nmol/L were associated with a 19%–52% lower risk of incident liver disease and a 35%–59% lower risk of cause-specific mortality. Study II, subgroup and sensitivity analysis

In subgroup analysis, the association of serum 25(OH)D status with liver disease and related mortality did not differ after stratification by sex, age, BMI, and assessment centers (all Q values for interaction >0.05, Supplemental Table S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605). For the subgroup analysis by race, we documented 31 incident liver diseases and 2 liver-related deaths in 811 Black participants with T2DM. Therefore, we only analyzed overall liver diseases as the outcome. As shown in Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605, serum 25(OH)D concentrations were inversely associated with overall liver diseases in White patients with T2DM, while they were positively associated with overall liver diseases in Black patients with T2DM (P-interaction=0.008, uncorrected by the FDR method).

The main results remained similar in the sensitivity analysis when excluding outcomes of interest that occurred in the first 1 and 2 years of follow-up (Supplemental Table S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605). When re-evaluating vitamin D deficiency status using May-standardized 25(OH)D concentration, consistent associations were observed and the estimate just slightly varied (Supplemental Table S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605). As shown in Supplemental Table S6, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A605, we observed that compared with the corresponding reference groups, all categories with a 25(OH)D concentration of >50 nmol/L were inversely associated with risk of liver diseases and liver-related mortality.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we first found that there is a positive joint association of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM with the risk of adverse liver-related outcomes. T2DM patients with vitamin D deficiency have a higher risk of adverse liver outcomes (multivariable-adjusted HR ranges from 2.19 to 5.97) compared to participants without T2DM and vitamin D deficiency. Then, we found that among individuals with T2DM, participants with 25(OH)D concentration higher than 50 nmol/L were associated with a lower risk of incident liver disease and related mortality (multivariable-adjusted HR ranges from 0.35 to 0.81) when compared with individuals with vitamin D deficiency. Dose-response associations reveal a significant inverse linear association of serum 25(OH)D concentrations with liver disease and related mortality in patients with T2DM. The results were similar in most subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Our study also observed this association in the opposite direction in White versus Black participants.

In study I, we found the additive interaction between T2DM and vitamin D deficiency and risk of incident liver diseases and liver-related mortality. We provided novel evidence for the synergistic associations of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency with adverse liver-related outcomes. Although the association of serum 25(OH)D and vitamin D deficiency with T2DM is well supported by laboratory evidence,5,11 the joint associations of the 2 on health-related outcomes have rarely been reported. Two cross-sectional studies found that maternal 25(OH)D deficiency and gestational diabetes were jointly associated with an increased risk of excessive fetal growth and large for gestational age.36,37 This indirect evidence supported the hypothesis that vitamin D deficiency and T2DM might lead to an increased risk of adverse liver outcomes with shared similar pathways, that is, modulating adipocyte and glucose metabolism.13 Our finding added evidence to understanding the interaction between vitamin D status and T2DM.

Study II incrementally demonstrated the inverse associations between 25(OH)D concentration and liver-related outcomes in individuals with T2DM based on the findings from study I. Vitamin D has been reported to be inversely associated with adverse liver-related outcomes. A systematic review indicated that higher serum 25(OH)D was associated with a lower risk of HCC.38 A prospective cohort study using the data from Finnish population-based health examination surveys FINRISK 1997 and Health 2000 (n=13,807) found that a 1 nmol/L increase in 25(OH)D concentration was associated with a lower risk of advanced liver disease (HR=0.972).7 For mortality, most studies have focused on the association between serum vitamin D and all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and cancer-specific mortality, whereas the evidence for liver-related mortality was less reported. One Danish prospective cohort study found that higher 25(OH)D concentrations were associated with a substantially lower risk of death due to diseases of the digestive system (HR highest vs. lowest quartiles 0.28) among 9146 individuals,39 which may partly support our findings.

The inverse associations between vitamin D and liver-related outcomes in patients with diabetes are biologically reasonable. Vitamin D supplementation may improve hepatic steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis by modulating lipid metabolism, immune responses, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in liver cells.6,40 In addition, 25(OH)D may also enhance insulin secretion and sensitivity by affecting calcium signaling, free fatty acid flux, and epigenetic modifications in pancreatic beta-cells in animal models.11 However, relevant evidence from observational studies was limited. One single-center, cross-sectional study covering 434 patients with T2DM reported that patients with vitamin D deficiency status [defined as 25(OH)D < 50 nmol/L] had a 2.05 times higher risk of developing NAFLD than those without vitamin D deficiency.41 Another cross-sectional study (n=339) in China found that higher 25(OH)D concentrations were associated with a lower risk of NAFLD and progressive liver fibrosis.42 Our study was in line with these studies and provided novel evidence for association between 25(OH)D and other liver diseases and related mortality.

Patients with T2DM are facing a heavy burden of liver disease. Multiple pieces of evidence show that patients with diabetes mellitus have an increased risk of liver disease. Liver disease will aggravate the glycemic control of the disease4,5,43 and is an important cause of death in T2DM. It was reported that cirrhosis is the fourth leading cause of death in patients with T2DM in the population-based Verona Diabetes Study.44 Although the incidence rates of liver disease and related mortality were very low in our study, the associations between serum vitamin D and these liver-related outcomes among individuals with T2DM were enlightening for future studies.

Before forming suggestions on the use of vitamin D supplements, future randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm the inverse association between serum 25(OH)D and liver-related outcome that was found in observational studies. The effect of vitamin D supplementation on liver disease has been inconclusive in randomized clinical trials. A qualitative systematic review of six clinical trials for the effects of vitamin D supplementation in adults with NAFLD found that only 2 trials reported significant changes in insulin resistance and biomarkers of inflammation and oxidative stress after the intervention.45 The last review of vitamin D supplementation for CLDs in adults by the Cochrane Hepato-Biliary Group concluded that the certainty of the evidence for all outcomes is very low.40 This systematic review included 27 randomized clinical trials with 1979 adult participants, with a mean follow‐up of 7 months (range 1–18 mo). The review only finds 1 trial of 18 participants with very uncertain evidence of vitamin D supplementation for liver-related mortality (relative risk 1.62, 95% CI 0.08–34.66).40 The larger sample size and a longer follow-up period of our observational study compared to these randomized clinical trials overcome these biases to some extent. Combined with the synergistic association of T2DM and vitamin D deficiency in our study, our findings added confidence to the implementation of randomized trials on vitamin D supplementation in patients with diabetes.

An additional finding of our study was that the association of serum 25(OH)D with adverse liver outcomes was different between Black and White participants. Previous studies have found that serum 25(OH)D levels and thresholds of clinical significance differ between races.32 Data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study in 8120 White and 2102 Black participants reported that high 25(OH) D levels (per 1-SD) were only associated with reduced risk of diabetes in Whites (HR 0.95) but not Blacks (HR 1.06).46 The differences in the association among races in our study appear to be similar to previous literature. However, the proportion of Black participants in our study was small, and these findings need to be validated in other multiracial cohorts.

To our knowledge, our study is the first cohort study reporting a positive joint association of vitamin D and T2DM with liver disease and related mortality, and further demonstrating the dose-response association of serum 25(OH)D concentration with adverse liver-related outcomes in individuals with T2DM. This study took advantage of a large sample size, long follow-up, and linkages to external national data sets with reliable records of outcomes.

However, our study also has several limitations. First, the baseline serum 25(OH)D concentrations may not reflect the actual 25(OH)D status during a long follow-up. However, population-based studies have demonstrated a high correlation coefficient among measurements, suggesting that a single 25(OH)D measurement is a reliable tool in epidemiologic studies.33,47–49 In addition, in our study, 25(OH)D measurements were measured under strict quality control,19 and we performed additional sensitivity analyses to account for seasonal fluctuations in concentrations, which ensured the reliability of our conclusions. Third, residual confounders could still exist though we considered multiple confounders. Of these, physical activity was not adequately considered as an important confounder. Although physical activity was collected in the study using a validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire, accelerometer activity may be a more accurate indicator.50 The participants in this study were almost exclusively White and aged 40–69 years. Therefore, the generalization of the results to other populations should be interpreted with caution. Finally, due to the observational nature of this study, causality could not be determined, and therefore, validation through clinical trials is necessary.

In conclusion, our study revealed positive joint associations of vitamin D deficiency and T2DM with liver disease and related mortality and described the dose-response association between serum 25(OH)D and these adverse liver-related outcomes among individuals with T2DM. These results are enlightening for the implementation of clinical trials to further explore the impact of vitamin D supplementation in individuals with T2DM.
