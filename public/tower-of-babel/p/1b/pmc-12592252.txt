Two-year follow-up assessment of a randomized controlled trial evaluating the durability of Tai Chi on cognitive function in older adults with type 2 diabetes and mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Xia Li, Jia Huang, Haiyin Deng, Zhizhen Liu, Yize Ruan, Zhenming Huang, Yannan Chen, Liyuan Tao, Lidian Chen, Jing Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsBuilding upon established short-term benefits, this study aimed to assess Tai Chi’s durability for preserving global and domain-specific cognition in older adults (≥ 60 years) with type 2 diabetes and mild cognitive impairment (T2D-MCI), compared to fitness walking and health education.MethodsParticipants (n = 328) were randomized 1:1:1 to Tai Chi, fitness walking, or health education groups, completing 24-week interventions. Cognitive outcomes—global cognition (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA), executive function, attention, visuospatial ability, and language fluency—were evaluated at baseline, 24 weeks(post-intervention), 36 weeks, and 2 years. Linear mixed models analyzed group-by-time interactions, while time-dependent Cox regression quantified minimal clinically important difference (MCID) attainment (MoCA improvement ≥ 4 points).ResultsAt 2-year follow-up, linear mixed models revealed significant group-by-time interactions for MoCA (p < 0.001). The Tai Chi group showed greater MoCA improvement versus health education (mean difference: 1.8, 95% CI 0.3–3.3, p = 0.016). Crucially, 49.8% of Tai Chi practitioners achieved MCID—nearly double the probability of health education (HR 2.0, 95% CI 1.4–2.8, p < 0.001) and exceeding fitness walking (HR 1.7, 95% CI 1.2–2.4; p = 0.005). Tai Chi selectively enhanced executive function (shorter Stroop reaction times vs. health education, p < 0.05; fewer color-word errors vs. fitness walking, p = 0.049) and visuospatial ability (higher Rey-Osterrieth copy scores vs. health education, p = 0.034).ConclusionsTai Chi provides superior long-term preservation of global cognition and executive/visuospatial function in T2D-MCI older adults, with nearly 50% achieving clinically meaningful cognitive improvement. As a scalable mind-body intervention, it warrants integration into geriatric preventive care to mitigate dementia risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03218-x.Keywords: Tai chi, Type 2 diabetes, Mild cognitive impairment, Cognitive preservation, Minimal clinically important difference

Introduction

Older adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D) face significantly elevated risks of cognitive dysfunction, with approximately 45% concurrently diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) [1]. This population frequently exhibits deficits in global cognition and specific domains—including executive function, attention, and visuospatial abilities [2, 3]—exacerbated by T2D’s role in accelerating progression from MCI to dementia [4, 5]. Critically, adults with type 2 diabetes and mild cognitive impairment (T2D-MCI) confers a 1.5–3-fold increased dementia risk compared to non-diabetic peers [6], imposing profound burdens on daily functioning and societal systems [7]. Against rising global T2D prevalence, international consensus now prioritizes T2D-MCI as a pivotal window for dementia prevention, emphasizing non-pharmacological interventions within geriatric care paradigms focused on dementia prevention [8, 9].

Exercise is a cornerstone of T2D management [10] and recommended by the American Academy of Neurology for MCI [9]. While the American College of Sports Medicine notes exercise may benefit cognition in T2D [11], optimal modalities for long-term cognitive preservation remain unclear.

Tai Chi, a culturally adapted mind-body therapy, has demonstrated dual benefits for glycemic control [12, 13] and short-term cognitive improvement [14–16] in T2D-MCI patients. Our prior randomized controlled trial (RCT) established that 24 weeks of Tai Chi significantly enhanced global cognition and specific cognitive domains relative to controls [17]. The present study is a 2-year follow-up assessment of the same cohort from this previous RCT [17], aiming to evaluate the long-term durability of the intervention. Critically, however, two fundamental gaps persist: (i) Whether these cognitive benefits can be sustained across the pivotal 2-year window for dementia prevention, and (ii) Whether Tai Chi’s effects translate to clinically meaningful outcomes—as measured by minimal clinically important difference (MCID)—in this high-risk population. The MCID is a critical metric for evaluating intervention efficacy, representing the smallest change in outcome that patients or clinicians identify as meaningful [18]. The unestablished long-term efficacy of mind-body therapies and clinically relevant efficacy—especially versus passive or active comparators—limits their adoption in preventive geriatric care for high-risk populations.

Addressing these dual gaps, this 2-year follow-up study is the first to:

(i)Evaluate Tai Chi’s durability on global and domain-specific cognition versus control interventions, (ii) Quantify its efficacy in achieving MCID thresholds on the global cognition (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA)—a critical metric for clinical decision-making in preventive geriatrics and a prerequisite for health policy recommendations in dementia prevention.

Evaluate Tai Chi’s durability on global and domain-specific cognition versus control interventions, (ii) Quantify its efficacy in achieving MCID thresholds on the global cognition (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA)—a critical metric for clinical decision-making in preventive geriatrics and a prerequisite for health policy recommendations in dementia prevention.

Methods

Study design

The full trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are provided in Supplementary 1. This study is a 2-year follow-up assessment of a previously conducted RCT (Clinical Trials.gov NCT04416841). The participants for this follow-up study were the cohort originally enrolled in the parent RCT. Briefly, the parent RCT was conducted between June 1, 2020, and February 28, 2022. Participants for the original RCT were recruited as volunteers from the communities of Fuzhou (Fujian Province), Harbin (Heilongjiang Province), Beijing Municipality, and Shenzhen (Guangdong Province). Recruitment was conducted through a combination of community advertisements and enrollment via local clinics. Individuals who expressed interest were contacted by the research team, provided with a detailed explanation of the study, and invited to participate voluntarily. The participation rate for the original RCT was 36.2% (328 out of 906 eligible individuals who were approached agreed to participate and were subsequently enrolled). A total of 328 eligible participants with T2D-MCI were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to the Tai Chi group, fitness walking group, and health education (control) group using block randomization (Fig. 1). All participants provided informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital of Rehabilitation (2020KY-004-002).

Study flowchart

Participants

Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Clinically confirmed diagnosis of T2D, (2) Mild cognitive impairment meeting Petersen criteria [19], excluding dementia, (3) Aged ≥ 60 years, (4) Sedentary lifestyle, defined as no structured exercise regimens (>30 min/session, ≥ 3 sessions/week) in the preceding 3 months, (4) Provision of written informed consent and willingness to adhere to the study protocol. Individuals were excluded for: (1) Secondary cognitive impairment due to neurological, psychiatric, or substance-related etiologies, (2) Significant cardiopulmonary, musculoskeletal, or neurological comorbidities contraindicating physical activity, (3) Concurrent enrollment in clinical trials involving cognitive or metabolic interventions.

Interventions

The health education group regularly attended health lectures given by clinical endocrinology experts, covering topics such as proper diet, blood glucose monitoring, and prevention of complications. The health education sessions were held once every four weeks, each lasting half an hour, for a total duration of 24 weeks. The Tai Chi group, in addition to the health education, underwent group 24-form simplified Tai Chi training (officially promulgated by China’s General Administration of Sport in 1956), guided by certified coaches of Tai Chi with over five years of teaching experience. The training was conducted three times a week, each session lasting one hour, and continued for 24 weeks. The fitness walking group, in addition to the health education, engaged in group fitness walking sessions, guided by physical education instructors. The sessions were held three times a week, each lasting one hour, with an exercise intensity of 50% to 70% of the maximum heart rate, and continued for 24 weeks. Adherence to the assigned 24-week interventions was high. The overall intervention attendance rate across the 24 weeks was 95 (88.8%) in the Tai Chi group (attended ≥ 54 sessions, or > 75.0% of the planned total intervention class sessions) and 99 (90.0%) in the fitness walking group (attended ≥ 54 sessions, or > 75.0% of the planned total intervention class sessions). In the health education group, 92 participants (82.9%) attended 5 or more lectures (> 75.0% of the planned health education sessions). Cognitive outcomes—global cognition, executive function, attention, visuospatial ability, and language fluency—were evaluated at baseline, post-intervention (24 weeks), and follow-ups (36 weeks and 2 years). Assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Data collection and study outcomes

The use of diabetes medications, level of physical activity, fasting blood glucose, and glycated hemoglobin at baseline, post-intervention (24 weeks), 36 weeks, and 2 years were collected. The level of physical activity was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [20]. The IPAQ-SF data were processed to calculate a continuous measure of total physical activity volume. Time (minutes) spent in walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity activities in the last week were multiplied by standard MET values (3.3, 4.0 and 8.0 METs, respectively) and summed to yield a total physical activity score in MET-minutes per week [20].

Global cognition was assessed using the MoCA scale [21], an increase of ≥ 4 points on the MoCA score compared to before treatment is considered to have reached the MCID [18]. Cognitive subdomains such as memory, attention, language fluency, visuospatial ability, and executive function were assessed using the Wechsler Memory Scale, the Digit Symbol Substitution Test, the Boston Naming Test, the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test, the Trail Making Test Part B, and the Stroop Color Word Test.

Statistical methods

The intention-to-treat (ITT) population included all randomized participants, with analyses of global cognitive function (assessed via MoCA) conducted in this full cohort. The per-protocol (PP) population was defined as participants who completed the original 24-week intervention (≥ 75% attendance), attended 2-year follow-up including endpoint MoCA assessment, avoided major protocol deviations (prohibited medications/cognitive training/cross-over), and provided baseline/2-year MoCA data. Missing values of MoCA at baseline, post intervention, 36-week and 2-year follow-up were handled using multiple imputation (50 imputations) incorporating demographic characteristics in the model, while other outcomes were analyzed using available-case without imputation. Prespecified sensitivity analyses comprise: (a) complete-case analysis in the ITT population, and (b) unadjusted analysis in the PP population without imputation.

SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corporation) was used for statistical analyses. A bilateral test with α level of 0.05 was adopted, A p-value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference. Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (inter-quartile range). Categorical variables are presented as number n (%). Differences of demographic characteristics of 3 groups at baseline and 2-year follow-up were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis H tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Linear mixed models (LMMs) were employed to analyze the changes in global cognition (MoCA score) and domain-specific cognitive function scores. The models examined the fixed effects of intervention group (Tai Chi, fitness walking, health education), time (baseline, 24 weeks, 36 weeks, 2 years), and their interaction. To control for the potential confounding influence of overall physical activity levels unrelated to the study intervention, self-reported total physical activity volume (in MET-min/week)—measured at all four assessment timepoints—was included as a time-varying covariate in all LMM models. Comparisons among the three groups at the same time point were conducted using ANOVA, and pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using the Bonferroni method.

A time dependent Cox regression model was applied to explore the influence of different intervention on achieving the MCID of global cognitive function. Sex, age, body mass index, duration of diabetes, years of education, medical history, smoking history, physical activity level, fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin, fasting insulin, etc., were included as covariates. The dependent variable was whether the MoCA score achieved the MCID compared to the baseline score.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 328 individuals completed the baseline assessment and included in the ITT analysis, with 289 (88.1%) completing the 24-week assessment, 282 (86.0%) completing the 36-week assessment, and 222 (67.7%) completing the 2-year follow-up assessment (Fig. 1). Among the 222 participants (all ≥ 75% attendance of 24-week Tai Chi intervention), the Tai Chi group, fitness walking group, and health education group consisted of 78, 72, and 72 individuals, respectively. They were included in the PP analysis (Fig. 1).

When comparing the baseline characteristics between the 2-year follow-up population and the 2-year dropout population, no statistically significant differences were observed (see Supplementary Table 1).

Comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics among 3 groups of participants at baseline and at 2-year follow-up are displayed in Table 1. At 2-year follow-up, there were differences in overall physical activity levels between 3 groups (p = 0.04). Specifically, significant differences were observed between 3 groups in terms of moderate-intensity physical activity levels (p = 0.002) (Table 1).

Global cognitive function among the three groups

After adjusting for the levels of self- reported physical activity at four time points, in the ITT analysis, the global cognitive function MoCA scores exhibited significant time effects (p < 0.001), group effects (p = 0.02), and group by time interaction effects (p < 0.001). In the PP analysis, similar results were observed, with a time effect of p < 0.001, a group effect of p = 0.123, and a group by time interaction effect of p = 0.019.

At 2-year follow-up, in the ITT analysis, compared with the health education group, the Tai Chi group demonstrated a greater improvement in the mean MoCA score, 25.5 (3.2) vs. 24.0 (3.9), respectively. The between group difference in mean change in MoCA level was 1.8 (95% CI, 0.3 to 3.3; p = 0.016) (Table 2). The PP analysis showed similar results. Between group difference was 1.8 (95% CI, 0.1 to 3.4; p = 0.029) (Table 2).

Subjects achieved the minimal clinically important difference of global cognitive function among the three groups

The number of cases that did not reach the MCID was 113 (50.2%) in the Tai Chi group, 127 (58.8%) in the fitness walking group, and 164 (77.0%) in the health education group, respectively. As revealed in the time dependent Cox regression, Tai Chi group was associated with the highest probability of achieving the MCID of global cognitive function. Compared to the health education group, the hazard ratios were 2.0 (95% CI, 1.4 to 2.8; p < 0.001) for Tai Chi group and 1.7 (95% CI, 1.2 to 2.4; p = 0.005) for fitness walking group.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence that Tai Chi, a culturally rooted mind-body therapy, induces sustained cognitive preservation in older adults with T2D-MCI over a 2-year follow-up period. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale investigation to demonstrate the durability of Tai Chi’s benefits on global cognition, executive function, and visuospatial abilities in this high-risk population—addressing a critical gap in preventive geriatrics. Notably, the Tai Chi group achieved MCID in global cognition, outperforming both fitness walking and health education groups, and these effects persisted without continued formal intervention. This underscores Tai Chi’s potential as a self-sustained neuroprotective intervention, aligning with the urgent need for scalable strategies to mitigate dementia risk in aging populations with metabolic comorbidities.

The observed benefits may stem from Tai Chi’s distinctive integration of physical activity, cognitive engagement, and stress reduction, which was not observed to the same extent in the control groups. Unlike conventional exercise, Tai Chi requires practitioners to memorize sequences, coordinate movements with breath control, and maintain mental focus [22]—processes that activate neural networks associated with executive control and procedural memory. Prior studies in MCI populations have supported this hypothesis, demonstrating Tai Chi’s association with enhanced cognitive flexibility and delayed recall. For instance, Lam et al. [23] in a large 1-year RCT (n = 389; Clinical Dementia Rating 0.5 or amnestic-MCI) reported a significantly reduced risk of dementia progression in the Tai Chi group compared to stretching/toning controls (2.2% vs. 10.8%; OR for dementia progression = 5.34, 95% CI 1.6 to 18.3, favoring the control group for progression risk, implying a protective effect of Tai Chi). Yu et al. [24] in a pilot RCT (n = 34; aged ≥ 50 with MCI) demonstrated superior global cognitive function (MoCA-HongKong version) at 12 weeks with Tai Chi compared to conventional fitness training (mean difference 2.2, 95% CI 1.0 to 3.5). The studies by Sungkarat et al. [25] (6-month RCT, n = 66; amnestic-MCI) and Wang et al. [26] (3-month quasi-experimental study, n = 108; MCI aged 65–85) further demonstrated Tai Chi’s benefits on memory (Logical Memory delayed recall: mean difference 0.4, 95%CI 0.2 to 0.7; Auditory Verbal Learning Test: z =−2.7, p = 0.006) and executive function (Trail Making Test B minus A: mean difference − 0.4, 95% CI −0.6 to −0.2; Trail Making Test -A: z=−2.1, p = 0.034) compared to health education control group. Our findings extend these results by confirming the long-term sustainability of benefits, a key factor for clinical adoption in preventive geriatrics.

Notably, Tai Chi demonstrated sustained benefits in executive function at the 2-year follow-up, with significantly faster Stroop test reaction times compared to health education group and fewer color-word errors compared to fitness walking. These findings align with Li et al.’s trial, which reported enduring executive function improvements in older adults with MCI after 24 weeks of Tai Chi (vs. stretching controls), persisting through a 48-week follow-up (Trail Making Test-B difference: −8.4 [95% CI, −14.6 to −2.1] at 24 weeks; −5.5 [98.75% CI, −11.4 to −0.4] at 48 weeks) [27]. While Li et al. focused on cognitive-enhanced Tai Chi, their observations in the traditional Tai Chi subgroup corroborate our results [27].

Critically, neuroimaging evidence further elucidates the neural substrates of these behavioral gains. Ruan et al. demonstrated that a 24-week Tai Chi intervention increased the betweenness centrality of the middle temporal gyrus (MTG) in T2D-MCI patients compared to health education, with left MTG centrality changes positively correlating with MoCA score improvements [28]. Betweenness centrality—a measure of a brain region’s role as a network hub—suggests that Tai Chi may improve executive function by enhancing functional connectivity between MTG and other brain regions, thereby promoting integration and processing of information across neural networks. This neuroplastic adaptation may underpin the sustained cognitive resilience observed in our cohort.

Beyond functional and structural neural adaptations, the cognitive benefits of Tai Chi are likely further supported by its positive impact on a range of systemic metabolic and neurotrophic pathways. Tai Chi improves glycemic control and insulin sensitivity in T2D, attenuating glucotoxicity and chronic inflammation that contribute to neuronal injury [17]. Enhanced glycemic control reduces accelerated glycation and formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [29], and favorably modulates the AGE/soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products (sRAGE) ratio [17]. A lower AGE/sRAGE ratio attenuates neurotoxicity mediated via the AGE–RAGE axis—where ligand-receptor binding instigates oxidative stress and inflammation—including reduced hippocampal RAGE binding, attenuated Aβ and Tau pathology, and suppressed neuronal apoptosis [30, 31], promoting a healthier cerebral microenvironment [32]. These systemic effects support neuroplasticity, likely facilitated by upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a critical mediator of neuronal survival and synaptic function. Tai Chi training increases plasma BDNF levels in older adults with mild cognitive impairment, correlating with improved memory and executive function [25]. Moreover, BDNF may enhance insulin sensitivity, suggesting a positive feedback loop between metabolic and neurotrophic pathways [33]. Thus, Tai Chi exerts cognitive protection through a multi-system mechanism wherein metabolic improvements converge with enhanced BDNF signaling to preserve cognitive function.

The higher MCID attainment in global cognition among Tai Chi practitioners—with 49.8% achieving clinically meaningful improvement versus only 23% in health education and a near doubling of probability (HR 2.0, 95% CI 1.4 to 2.8)—demonstrates Tai Chi’s capacity to modify natural cognitive decline trajectories in high-risk T2D-MCI older adults. Given the critical value of safeguarding global cognition for sustaining independent community living and preventing dementia conversion [34], these findings carry significant clinical relevance. This aligns with dementia prevention frameworks prioritizing clinically meaningful outcomes over statistical differences alone [35]. Future studies should investigate whether sustained MCID achievement predicts reduced dementia incidence, extending these benefits to hard clinical endpoints.

The generalizability of our findings should be considered in the context of our study design and participant selection. Participants were volunteers recruited from specific urban communities in China, which may introduce self-selection bias and limit direct extrapolation to the broader general population of older adults with T2D-MCI. Individuals who volunteer for long-term trials may be more health-conscious and motivated than non-participants, potentially influencing their engagement with the intervention and the outcomes. However, the demographic and clinical characteristics of our cohort are representative of a typical urban-dwelling older adult population in China with T2D-MCI. Therefore, our results are likely generalizable to this large and clinically significant subgroup, particularly those who are amenable to and capable of initiating mind-body exercise programs. Future implementation studies in broader, more diverse community and clinical settings are warranted to confirm the effectiveness and scalability of Tai Chi for cognitive preservation.

Despite these strengths, limitations warrant consideration. First, attrition during the extended follow-up period may have introduced selection bias, though efforts like WeChat groups-based engagement were implemented to minimize loss. Second, while this study represents the longest follow-up of supervised Tai Chi in T2D-MCI, additional timepoints (e.g., annual assessments) could better delineate dynamic cognitive trajectories. Third, the mechanisms underlying Tai Chi’s sustained effects remain unclear. Future research should incorporate neuroimaging (e.g., fMRI) or biomarkers (e.g., BDNF, inflammatory markers) to elucidate pathways linking mind-body practices to neuroprotection.

Conclusions

This 2-year post-RCT follow-up study in elderly patients with T2D-MCI demonstrates that Tai Chi provides sustained cognitive benefits, particularly in global cognition, executive function, and visuospatial abilities. Notably, Tai Chi achieved the MCID threshold in global cognition, underscoring its clinical relevance. These findings advocate for integrating Tai Chi—a culturally adaptable and scalable mind-body therapy—into multidisciplinary geriatric care protocols. Such integration aligns with preventive geriatric objectives by offering a practical, evidence-based approach to combat the growing burden of diabetes-related dementia.
