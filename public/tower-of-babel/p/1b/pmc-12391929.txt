Advanced Multi-Parameter Prediction of Coal Quality Using LIBS and Ensemble Machine Learning Techniques
Authors: Qingsong Wang, Donglian Zhang, Youquan Dou, Qingzhao Wang, Yiyi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate assessment of coal quality is essential for optimizing combustion efficiency and reducing pollutant emissions in coal-fired power plants. In this study, we developed a laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS)-based framework, combined with advanced machine learning techniques to predict key coal quality parameters, including elemental carbon, ash content, volatile matter, total sulfur, and calorific value. After applying spectral preprocessing methods. such as outlier removal and baseline correction, predictive models were established using algorithms including the least squares support vector machine (LS-SVM), which achieved the highest accuracy with an R2 of 0.9940 for elemental carbon. The results indicate that the proposed method provides a reliable and efficient alternative for rapid coal quality analysis, with potential for future application in intelligent monitoring and control frameworks.

Introduction

Coal remains the dominant source of global electricity, accounting for over 36% of power generation worldwide in 2023, with China relying on coal for more than 55% of its electricity production. In coal-fired power plants, fuel quality critically affects combustion stability, thermal efficiency, and pollutant emissions. Key parameters such as ash content (A ad), volatile matter (V ad), fixed carbon, total sulfur (S t,ad) and calorific value directly influence boiler performance and environmental outcomes. Poor-quality coalcharacterized by high ash and sulfur content and low heating valuecan lead to reduced boiler efficiency, increased slagging, and elevated emissions of SO2 and particulate matter. Therefore, an accurate and timely assessment of coal quality parameters is essential for optimizing combustion processes, improving energy utilization, and supporting environmental compliance in modern power generation systems.

Traditional coal quality analysis methods employed in China predominantly rely on offline laboratory testing, which is characterized by low efficiency, delayed feedback, and high labor costs. Although various online analysis techniques, including X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy, prompt gamma neutron activation analysis (PGNAA), near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, and microwave analysis have been developed, they suffer from notable limitations. These methods often require extensive sample preparation, are time-consuming, and may lack sufficient representativeness due to a limited sampling mass. Consequently, they fail to meet the growing demand for high-throughput coal quality monitoring in modern power plants.

Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) has emerged as a promising alternative due to its advantages of minimal sample preparation, multielement detection capability, high spatial resolution, and rapid analysis speed. LIBS has demonstrated potential for on-site coal quality assessment, offering the possibility of real-time optimization of combustion processes. However, LIBS-based quantitative analysis remains challenging due to issues such as spectral complexity, matrix effects, and relatively low measurement precision. To overcome these challenges, researchers have explored the integration of LIBS with advanced mathematical and statistical modeling techniques, aiming to extract reliable quantitative information from complex spectral data sets.

Previous studies have attempted to improve the performance of LIBS through hybrid approaches, such as combining LIBS with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to enhance the prediction of volatile content or applying traditional chemometric methods like partial least squares regression (PLSR) and principal component regression (PCR). Nonetheless, these efforts often focus on limited coal quality parameters, and the robustness and generalization capabilities of the models under complex industrial conditions remain insufficiently addressed.

In this research, the present study proposes a novel multiparameter coal quality prediction framework by integrating LIBS with four advanced machine learning algorithms: support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), kernel extreme learning machine (K-ELM), and least squares support vector machine (LS-SVM). A comprehensive data preprocessing pipeline, including outlier removal based on Mahalanobis distance, baseline correction using the AirPLS algorithm, and spectral noise reduction via wavelet transform, was employed to enhance the data quality and model robustness. The predictive models target five critical coal quality indicators: elemental carbon content (C ad), A ad, (V ad, total sulfur (S t,ad), and gross calorific value (Q gr,ad). By systematically comparing different machine learning strategies, this study aims to identify the most effective modeling approach for LIBS-based coal quality assessment. In doing so, it enhances the quantitative application of LIBS technology and lays a solid foundation for future development of semiautomated or near real-time intelligent monitoring systems in coal-fired power plants. The findings provide practical insights into improving analytical efficiency, supporting environmental compliance, and contributing to carbon emission management in the energy sector.

Materials and Methods

Manual Assay

The 49 experimental coal samples (bituminous coal) were all routine testing samples from the National Energy Nanjing Coal Quality Supervision and Inspection Co., Ltd. The coal sample was prepared into a general analysis test sample with a particle size of 0.2 mm by using the GB/T 474 method. Generally, the Q gr,ad, A ad, V ad, S t,ad content, and C ad of the analytical test samples are measured using the methods outlined in GB/T 212, GB/T 213, GB/T 214, and GB/T 30733. The selected coal samples covered a wide range of quality indices, specifically: Q gr,ad: 15.07–24.78 MJ/kg; C ad: 37.48–62.73%; A ad: 8.10–44.31%; V ad: 7.02–35.96%; and S t,ad: 0.14–3.64%. To address the limited number of coal samples (n = 49), we adopted a strategy of high-frequency repeated measurements. Each coal sample was ground, homogenized, and pressed into a single pellet, on which LIBS measurements were performed at 200 different spatial positions across the pellet surface. These measurements captured the inherent microstructural and compositional heterogeneity of the coal, especially variations related to A ad, resulting in a rich and diverse spectral data set. While the spectra originate from the same sample, they reflect meaningful local differences and within-sample variability. Treating these spectra as individual data points effectively enhances the representativeness of the training set and enables the machine learning models to learn both average composition trends and intrasample variance. LIBS Coal Quality Analysis Measurement

The LIBS measurement system primarily consisted of an Nd:YAG laser (Dwa-200, Beijing LeiBao Photoelectric Technology Co., Ltd., China), a multichannel spectrometer (AvaSpec-ULS2048, Avantes, Netherlands), an optical path and mechanical support system, and a computer control module. The laser energy was set to 75 mJ with a pulse width of 10 ns and a repetition rate of 5 Hz. The spectrometer delay time was set to 1.3 μs, and the integration time was 1.05 ms (Figure ). Approximately 3 g of the general analytical test carbon sample was weighed and placed in an aluminum mold with a diameter of 30 mm. An electric hydraulic press was then used to apply a constant pressure of 200 MPa to the sample for 1 min. The coal cake was removed, and a LIBS rapid coal quality analyzer was used for detection. The analysis points covered the square area around the center of the briquette, with a total of 256 points. After the detection is completed, the original spectral data ar output. The raw spectral data are then used for data preprocessing and modeling.

Outlier Removal, Baseline Correction, and Spectral Noise Reduction

In this research, the Mahalanobis distance methodology is applied to eliminate anomalous data in coal sample spectral data sets, ensuring the retention of reliable data and enhancing the stability of spectral line intensity. Rooted in the theory of multivariate normal distribution, Mahalanobis distance effectively identifies data points deviating from the normal distribution, facilitating the detection of anomalies. The computation of the Mahalanobis distance involves determining the distance between the single excitation spectrum of each sample and the mean spectrum of the sample. By assessing the magnitude of the Mahalanobis distance, data points significantly deviating from the overall pattern are identified. To prevent signal distortion due to excessive removal, a threshold is established, excluding the last hundred data points while retaining 70% of the data set.

For the baseline correction, the adaptive iteratively reweighted penalized least squares (AirPLS) method is employed. This technique, grounded in penalized least squares, dynamically adjusts weights during the iteration process for fitting the baseline and the sum of squared residuals of the original signal. It efficiently identifies and subtracts irregular variations in the baseline without requiring user intervention or prior information, such as peak detection.

Furthermore, the wavelet transform method is utilized to mitigate noise interference arising from background noise and interactions between characteristic spectral lines. Wavelet transform technology proves adept at handling diverse backgrounds and spectra with low signal-to-background ratios, eliminating the need for prior knowledge of sample composition. It circumvents the necessity of selecting suitable background correction points or making mathematical assumptions about the background distribution. The denoised spectra were then used as inputs for model training.

Spectral Coal Quality Nonlinear Quantitative Models

Four algorithms, including SVM, RF, K-ELM, and LS-SVM, were used to establish quantitative models to predict the coal quality indicators. The SVM is a supervised learning algorithm based on statistical learning theory proposed by Cortes and Vapnik in 1995. It has unique advantages in handling small samples, nonlinear data, and high-dimensional pattern recognition. The RF model is a learning algorithm based on ensemble trees and is considered one of the most powerful learning algorithms. Random decision forests adapt to the nonlinearities present in the data and are often more effective than linear regression. Detailed methods can be found in ref . K-ELM is an algorithm for training single hidden layer feedforward neural networks that enables fast network training with minimal human supervision The core idea of ELM is that the parameters of the hidden layers in the network do not need to be learned; they can be randomly assigned, and subsequent analysis calculates the output parameters of the network. The specific method can be found in ref . Recent studies have shown that ELM networks outperform other advanced classifiers such as SVM. LS-SVM is a variant of traditional support vector machines that replaces the inequality constraints on the slack variables in SVM with equality constraints. Model Evaluation Metrics

During the data preprocessing phase, we randomly divide the data set into a training set and a test set, with 70% of the data used for training the regression model and 30% used for evaluating the model’s performance. This allows for a comprehensive assessment of the model’s effectiveness. In addition, to mitigate the risk of overfittingespecially considering the limited sample size10-fold cross-validation was employed during model development. This method partitions the data set into 10 subsets, using 9 subsets for training and the remaining one for validation in each iteration. The final model performance is then averaged across all folds, which helps ensure the model’s generalizability and robustness. This study uses the following metrics to evaluate the performance of each model: (1) coefficient of determination (R 2), (2) root-mean-square error (RMSE), (3) root-mean-square error of prediction (RMSEP), (4) mean absolute error (MAE), and (5) mean relative error (MRE). R 2 represents the linearity between the reference value and the model prediction. The closer R 2 is to 1, the stronger the correlation between the spectral signal and the indicator content, indicating a more significant regression effect. R 2 > 0.7 indicates a trustworthy data representation, while R 2 > 0.9 indicates a good fit. RMSE and RMSEP measure the deviation between the reference value and the model prediction. MAE and MRE represent the average absolute error and average relative error between the model prediction and the reference value. The smaller the value of these indicators, the better the performance of the prediction model. Discussion

In this study, we developed a multiindex coal quality prediction framework by integrating LIBS with four advanced machine learning algorithms: SVM, RF, K-ELM, and LS-SVM. Among these, the LS-SVM model consistently outperformed the others in predicting elemental carbon, A ad, V ad, S t,ad, and Q gr,ad. The superior performance of LS-SVM, particularly with R 2 reaching 0.9940 and RMSEP as low as 0.64% for carbon content, highlights its strong generalization ability and robustness in handling high-dimensional LIBS data.

In the prediction of S t,ad content, the model yielded a relatively higher mean relative error (MRE) compared to other coal quality indicators. This can be primarily attributed to two factors. First, spectral interference may occur due to the proximity of sulfur emission lines to those of other elements (such as carbon, oxygen, or metal impurities), leading to overlapping peaks and reduced signal specificity. This interference complicates the accurate extraction of sulfur-related spectral features. Second, nonuniform sulfur distribution within coal samples may contribute to variability in the LIBS measurements. Sulfur exists in various chemical forms (e.g., organic, pyritic, and sulfate sulfur), and its spatial heterogeneity across the pellet surface can affect local plasma characteristics during ablation. Although each sample was measured at 200 distinct positions to enhance spectral representativeness, such heterogeneity may still introduce noise and affect the model’s predictive accuracy. These factors collectively explain the higher prediction error for sulfur and highlight the need for further refinement in both spectral preprocessing and sampling uniformity to improve the model’s robustness.

The superior performance of the LS-SVM model can be attributed to both its algorithmic structure and its suitability for handling the complex characteristics of LIBS spectral data. Unlike conventional SVM, LS-SVM replaces inequality constraints with equality constraints and employs a least-squares cost function, significantly simplifying the optimization process. This modification reduces computational complexity and enhances numerical stability, which is particularly important for data sets with high dimensionality and noise-prone featuresboth common in LIBS spectra. Moreover, LS-SVM is highly effective in managing nonlinear relationships between spectral variables and coal quality indicators owing to its use of kernel functions. In our study, the radial basis function (RBF) kernel allowed LS-SVM to construct flexible, nonlinear decision boundaries that better capture the intricate dependencies in the spectral data. In comparison to other models, such as random forest, which may struggle with overfitting in small data sets, and K-ELM, which depends on the random initialization of hidden layer parameters, LS-SVM achieves a better balance between bias and variance, leading to superior generalization performance. These advantages make LS-SVM particularly well-suited for applications involving small sample sizes, complex variable interactions, and high-dimensional inputsprecisely the challenges encountered in LIBS-based coal quality prediction. Recent studies have further demonstrated the efficacy of the LS-SVM in similar contexts. For example, Li et al. developed a high-precision coal classification model using LIBS coupled with a kernel-based extreme learning machine (KELM), achieving classification accuracies exceeding 99%. Similarly, Zheng et al. employed LIBS combined with machine learning techniques for rapid classification and quantification of coal, highlighting the potential of such approaches in handling complex spectral data. These findings underscore the robustness and versatility of LS-SVM in dealing with the challenges posed by LIBS spectral data, reinforcing its suitability for coal quality prediction.

Previous studies employed LIBS in combination with chemometric methods for the quantitative analysis of coal quality. For instance, Zhang et al. used four modeling methods, including PLSR, SVR, ANN, and PCR, to model and compare parameters such as A ad, V ad, and calorific value. While their work provided a valuable foundation, our study advances the field in several important ways. First, we extended the quantitative analysis to cover five key coal quality parameters: carbon content, A ad, V ad, S t,ad, and calorific value, thereby enhancing the practical applicability of the model. Second, we introduced a set of advanced machine learning algorithmsSVM, RF, K-ELM, and LS-SVMalong with a systematically optimized spectral preprocessing pipeline comprising outlier removal, baseline correction, and noise reduction. These enhancements significantly improved the model’s robustness and prediction accuracy under complex coal composition conditions. Notably, the LS-SVM model achieved an R 2 of 0.993–0.994, clearly outperforming the ANN model reported by Zhang et al. Third, our data set, consisting of 49 raw coal samples from diverse sources, is more representative of real-world operational conditions, which supports the practical applicability of the model for rapid, on-site quality assessment in coal-fired power plants.

Although traditional coal quality analysis methods, from sampling and sample preparation to testing, typically take several hours, which makes them unsuitable for providing real-time data support for boiler operation optimization, the LIBS technique employed in this study offers advantages such as fast analysis, multielement simultaneous detection, and compact equipment. In recent years, LIBS has become a promising method for rapid coal quality detection. Compared to traditional online analysis techniques like near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and prompt gamma neutron activation analysis (PGNAA), NIRS has limited sensitivity for detecting inorganic components like ash and sulfur, while PGNAA, although capable of nondestructive analysis, suffers from large equipment size, high costs, and radiation safety concerns, limiting its widespread application in industrial settings. In contrast, LIBS can cover multiple key coal quality parameters, including carbon, ash, V ad, S t,ad, and calorific value, while also offering good integration and flexibility for deployment, making it particularly suitable for complex industrial environments such as coal-fired power plants. In this study, we combined LIBS spectral data with advanced machine learning models, including support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), kernel extreme learning machine (K-ELM), and least-squares support vector machine (LS-SVM), to establish multiparameter quantitative models for fast coal quality assessment. The LS-SVM model demonstrated excellent performance with R 2 values ranging from 0.993 to 0.994, significantly outperforming traditional models. Preprocessing steps such as outlier removal, baseline correction, and noise reduction further enhanced the model’s ability to handle complex coal samples. Although sample preparation is still required, ongoing advancements in automated sampling devices, portable LIBS systems, and real-time spectral interpretation algorithms make it feasible for LIBS to transition toward quasi-real-time or even real-time applications in coal-fired power plants.

One limitation of this study is the relatively small number of coal samples (49), which may increase the risk of model overfitting. To mitigate this, we employed high-frequency repeated measurements, collecting spectral data from 200 different positions on each coal pellet to capture within-sample variability and subtle spectral differences, thereby enhancing the representativeness of the training set. Coupled with cross-validation, regularization, and model simplification techniques, this strategy effectively reduced overfitting and improved model robustness. Nevertheless, the model’s generalization to coal samples from different sources or under varying environmental conditions requires further validation. Variations in elemental composition, mineral structures, and matrix effects across different coal types may influence LIBS spectral characteristics, while environmental disturbances such as temperature fluctuations, dust contamination, and humidity changes may introduce additional variability. Therefore, future studies should expand the sample diversity and conduct field validations under realistic industrial conditions. To further enhance robustness and adaptability, advanced modeling strategies such as multimodel fusion or deep learning could be explored. For instance, convolutional neural networks may extract spatial-spectral features from LIBS spectral images for a more nuanced interpretation of complex patterns. Additionally, challenges intrinsic to LIBS technology, including spectral interference and sample heterogeneity, must be addressed to ensure the input stability. Importantly, the proposed LS-SVM-based framework was designed with real-time deployment in mind, offering a fast inference speed and low computational overhead suitable for embedded or edge computing platforms. Its integration into existing LIBS-based online coal quality monitoring systems could support timely feedback for combustion optimization and emission control. Future work will focus on systematically investigating spectral interference mechanisms, optimizing data preprocessing, and refining model structures to improve robustness, reduce latency, and enhance real-time industrial applicability.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of combining LIBS with advanced machine learning algorithms for rapid, multiparameter coal quality prediction. Among the models evaluated, the LS-SVM model achieved the highest predictive performance across all key indicators, particularly for C ad (R 2 = 0.9940, RMSEP = 0.64%, and MRE = 1.17%). Through rigorous spectral preprocessing and model optimization, our approach significantly enhances the quantitative capabilities of LIBS technology. While full real-time monitoring of raw coal remains challenging due to the need for sample preparation and signal stability, our method provides a practical solution for replacing manual laboratory testing after standard processing steps (e.g., drying and grinding). This enables faster feedback of key coal quality indicators and supports semiautomated inspection workflows. These findings highlight the potential of LIBS-based intelligent detection systems to improve the efficiency of combustion optimization, pollutant control, and carbon emission accounting in coal-fired power plants. Future work should focus on expanding the data set, validating the models under real industrial conditions, and exploring multimodal fusion and deep learning techniques to further enhance system robustness and application readiness.
