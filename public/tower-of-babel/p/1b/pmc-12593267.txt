Multi‐Objective Optimization of Dental Implant Designs With Multi‐Recessed Holes: Insights From Static and Dynamic Finite Element Analysis
Authors: I‐Chiang Chou, Shih‐Hao Huang, Cheng‐Kang Lee, Mayur Jiyalal Prajapati, Jian‐Cheng Huang, Yung‐Chang Cheng, Cho‐Pei Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study presents a novel dental implant design featuring multi‐recessed holes, developed through an integrated multi‐objective optimization framework. The impact of the implant geometry on longevity, strength, and success rate is explored under both static and dynamic loading conditions. The novel design features multi‐recessed holes, and five key implant parameters are selected as control factors for optimization. Experimental simulations are conducted using the uniform design (UD) method. Finite element analysis (FEA) is used to simulate the implant system to evaluate the fatigue safety factor and equivalent stress at the primary, middle, and final stages of osseointegration. Additionally, the dynamic FEA is employed to calculate the maximum micromotion under dynamic chewing loads. To maximize the fatigue safety factor and minimize equivalent stress and micromotion, a multi‐objective optimization approach is applied, integrating Kriging interpolation (KGI), entropy weighting analysis (EWA), the technique for order preference by similarity to ideal solution (TOPSIS), and genetic algorithms (GA). The optimal design achieves a fatigue safety factor of 3.022 and an equivalent stress of 459.49 MPa, with improvements of 28.1% and 3.2%, respectively, compared to the original design. Furthermore, the optimized design results in a micromotion of 32.96 μm, reflecting a 37.1% improvement. Overall, the multi‐objective optimization process enhances the implant's safety factor and strength under various loading conditions.Keywords: fatigue safety factor, genetic algorithm, Kriging interpolation, micromotion, multi‐recessed holes implant system, TOPSIS, uniform design

Introduction

Dental implants have long been used as a treatment for tooth decay. One of the factors that influences the success of dental implant placement is the reliability of the bond between the implant and the surrounding bone. Stable implant and bone bonds will greatly affect osseointegration. Since dental implants are inferior in strength when compared to the natural tooth, implant design becomes a key factor. Therefore, a good implant design is important and can affect the primary stability, structural robustness, and osseointegration.

An efficient dental implant design has been a focus of several studies. Many of them have examined the fatigue safety during the design and manufacturing process. Calculating fatigue safety is a critical factor in predicting dental implant strength. Abdoli et al. investigated the fatigue life prediction of the nine dental implant systems using FE‐Safe software using the ISO 14801 standard [1]. Applying the titanium abutments, cast abutments with nickel‐chromium alloy, and zirconia abutments, the effect of cyclic fatigue behavior of the implant‐abutment system was evaluated via the cyclic loads of 133 N at a frequency of 19.1 Hz for 200,000 cycles. Zieliński et al. investigated the fatigue crack initiation and equivalent stress of the dental system built on the INTEGRA OPTIMA software by the finite element analysis (FEA) method using the EN 14801 standard [2]. The nominal normal stress of the three‐part implant system made of Ti6Al4V material was evaluated from the FEA results by Armentia et al. using the EN 14801 standard [3]. Ziaie et al. used the FEA method and the Goodman and Soderberg theory to calculate the fatigue life of the implant systems under the 700 N force with 3 × 105 cycles [4]. Hernandez et al. used the Gerber, Goodman, and Soderberg fatigue criteria for the fatigue analysis of the single‐unit dental implant model with two screws, which was conducted from the FEA [5].

Besides the static FEA of the implant system, the dynamic behavior and response of dental implant systems under the dynamic chewing loads are also important. Micromotion (dynamic deformation) and stress distributions are key to evaluating the dynamic properties of a dental implant. Micromotion is the relative movement that occurs when a load is applied to an implant surface and bone. Demirbas et al. performed dynamic analysis using ABAQUS to analyze the stress distribution in the cancellous and cortical bones [6]. Considering the implant, food, and jaw systems, Razaghi et al. investigated the equivalent stress for the cancellous and cortical bones in the ITI implant system by using the nonlinear explicit FE code LS‐DYNA [7]. Applying the static loading with a frequency of 1 Hz on the implant model, Gökay et al., evaluated the stress distribution of the implant, abutment, and screw models by the FEA software ALGOR [8]. Cheng et al. investigated the micromotion and equivalent stress of the zirconia implant system using dynamic FEA software while applying the dynamic chewing load in the axial direction [9]. Jiang et al. applied the dynamic chewing loads with three directions and ANSYS/LS‐DYNA software to evaluate the micromotion of the 3‐piece Semados dental implant system [10]. Furthermore, the maximal micromotion in the modified C‐Tech implant system was illustrated and investigated by Yudistiro and Cheng [11].

In recent years, the innovative and optimal design of the dental implant model has become a rather special research topic. Numerous optimal design techniques are used to decrease the equilibrium stress and increase the safety. In order to achieve the minimum von Mises and shear stress, Srivastava et al. obtained the optimal shape of the implant model using the response surface method (RSM) and design of experiment (DOE) techniques [12]. For the purpose of reducing the risk of late implant failure, Poovarodom et al. introduced an optimization process for designing a two‐piece zirconia dental implant model [13]. Based on the FEA method and Statistical Analysis System (SAS) software, the stresses and strains in dental implants and surrounding bone were efficiently evaluated and reduced by Chang et al. [14]. Elleuch et al. utilized the FEM and response surface methods in order to optimize the equivalent stresses in the implant model [15]. Kim et al. investigated the structural stability of the dental implant systems by reducing the maximum equilibrium stress value and the minimum principal strain calculated via ABAQUS software [16]. Applying the uniform design of experiment, Kriging interpolation, and genetic algorithm, the optimal design in reducing the equivalent stress and micromotion of the implant model was performed by Cheng et al. and Yudistiro et al. [9, 11]. Other studies have also involved innovative design strategies for dental implant profiles in order to improve the success rates of the implant [11, 17, 18]. From the literature it can be concluded that the previous studies have primarily focused on improving dental implant performance using three main approaches: fatigue life prediction using FEA, analysis of micromotion and dynamic loading effects and optimizing implant design using design of experiments. These studies have addressed either fatigue or micromotion, without integrating all the relevant performance indicators into a unified optimization framework. Thus, it becomes important to combine the implant geometry with a comprehensive multi‐objective optimization strategy that incorporates static and dynamic performances.

The novelty of the current study lies in two key aspects. Firstly, the implant design introduces multi‐recessed holes between the threads which is a unique strategy to enhance osseointegration and improve structural performance. Secondly, unlike previous studies which focus on single‐objective or limited optimization strategies, this study integrates a comprehensive multi‐objective optimization approach combining Uniform Design (UD), Kriging Interpolation (KGI), Entropy weighting analysis (EWA), Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) decision‐making, and genetic Algorithms (GA). This framework enables the simultaneous enhancement of fatigue safety, equivalent stress, and micromotion under static and dynamic loads, validated by ISO‐standard simulations. Such integration of advanced modeling and optimization techniques to a novel implant design has not been reported in the literature to date.

Torsional Analysis

Torsion testing is a crucial component of standard testing for dental implants. The process typically begins with the attachment of a dental implant body to the jawbone, followed by the attachment of other parts to create a dental prosthetic. The components must be securely attached to the dental implant body and able to withstand masticatory loads, including torsional loads.

In order to evaluate the torsional yield strength and maximum torque on a dental implant body or connecting endosseous dental implants, ISO 13498 was used [24]. The implant body and connecting components are secured in a testing apparatus in accordance with this standard to determine the torsional yield strength and maximum torque. Data Analysis Methods

Kriging Interpolation Method

Due to the geometric complexity of the implant system, it is difficult to evaluate the stress and deformation based on the theoretical analysis. Therefore, the FEA software is used to obtain the analysis results under the static and dynamic loads. After the design of the experiment process, the input and output data are obtained by the FEA software. However, it is difficult to use explicit functions to define input–output relationships. A statistical surrogate model was used to construct formulas connecting input and output data. A surrogate model is a simplified approximation used to predict complex simulation outcomes, allowing optimization with significantly reduced computational cost. Kriging is one such method that builds predictive models based on limited simulation data. To construct the surrogate model, Kriging interpolation is used to define the objective function through statistical regression. Kriging interpolation has been applied to create the surrogate models and response surface in many engineering cases [11, 25, 26, 27]. Each objective function is represented by a Kriging surrogate (KGS) model, created using experimental points from the UD results.

TOPSIS Method

Technique for order preference by similarity to ideal solution (TOPSIS) is one of the most widely used multi‐criteria decision analysis methods. It was proposed by Hwang et al. and extended by Yoon et al. [28, 29]. The TOPSIS algorithm has been used in many applications [30, 31, 32, 33]. It chooses the alternative with the shortest Euclidean distance from the ideal solution and the greatest distance from the negative ideal solution. TOPSIS is based on the fundamental premise that the best solution is closest to the positive ideal solution (PIS) and farthest from the negative ideal solution (NIS). Optimal Design Analysis

This study uses an innovative methodology to develop a multi‐objective optimization design for the implant model, incorporating the UD table, KGI, EWA, TOPSIS, and GA. Figure 16 illustrates the optimization design strategy to identify the optimal design. The justification and numerical results are presented in detail below.

Multiple objective optimal design process.

This study adopts a surrogate model optimization strategy based on UD. Initially, UD is used to generate the initial set of sample points in the initial DOE strategy. Then, the Kriging surrogate model for each objective function is constructed. The EWA method is applied to determine the optimal weighting for three objective functions: SF, ST, and MD. Applying the EWA method and TOPSIS technology, the three objective functions are integrated into one objective function, Closeness. Using KGI technology, the Kriging surrogate model for Closeness is created. Finally, the optimal value and solution have been evaluated via the GA method.

The optimal solution obtained is then validated by comparing the real analyzed value with the surrogate model's predicted value. If the prediction error exceeds 3%, the corresponding point is added to the sample set, the surrogate model is reconstructed, and the optimization is repeated. If the error is less than 3%, the sampling procedure is terminated, and the solution is accepted as the final optimal result.

Sensitivity Analysis

To examine the contribution of each input variable to the model outputs, this study employed global sensitivity analysis using UQLab. The Sobol method was applied to calculate the total‐order sensitivity indices. UQLab, developed by Marelli and Sudret [38], is a MATLAB toolbox specifically designed for uncertainty quantification in engineering and scientific fields. It offers flexible modules, including sensitivity analysis, reliability analysis, surrogate modeling, and uncertainty propagation. With its user‐friendly interface and seamless integration with custom models, UQLab serves as an essential tool for robust design and decision‐making analysis.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and optimized the novel dental implant design featuring multi‐recessed holes through a comprehensive multi‐objective optimization framework integrating uniform design, Kriging interpolation, entropy weighting analysis, TOPSIS, and genetic algorithms.

The optimized implant design achieved significant quantitative improvements compared to the original design. A 28.1% increase in fatigue safety factor (from 2.36 to 3.022), a 3.2% reduction in equivalent stress according to the von‐Mises criterion (from 474.51 to 459.49 MPa), and a 37.1% reduction in micromotion (from 52.43 μm to 32.96 μm) with predicted error less than 3% between surrogate and actual FEA results was reported in this study. These improvements were validated through finite element analysis following ISO 14801 and ISO 13498 standards, with prediction error less than 3% between surrogate models and actual FEA results.

The multi‐objective optimization approach successfully balanced competing design objectives through entropy weighting analysis, demonstrating computational efficiency by reducing optimization time from approximately 280 h of direct FEA to 2 h using surrogate models while maintaining high accuracy. The results demonstrate that the multi‐recessed hole design with optimized geometric parameters effectively enhances implant mechanical performance across all three metrics: fatigue safety, stress distribution, and micromotion control.

Limitations and Clinical Implications

While the proposed implant design demonstrated promising biomechanical performance through simulation, there are several challenges that need to be addressed in the future works. First, all the mechanical property evaluations conducted using FEA are with homogeneous material properties with simplified boundary conditions. Endogenous factors such as specific bone quality, anatomical variability, and biological responses were not accounted for in this study. Second, the effects of the surgical procedures, long‐term fatigue under variable masticatory loads, and osseointegration remain unvalidated. Despite these challenges, the optimized design featuring multi‐recessed holes and improved safety factor, stress distribution, and micromotion control hold significant clinical relevance. The reduction in micromotion suggests better stability, which may lead to improves osseointegration, shorten healing time and increase long‐term success rates. Future work will include in vitro mechanical testing, biocompatibility assessments, and patient‐specific clinical trials to validate the practical application of the proposed implant system.
