The effect of human genital warts virus on the quality of women’s sexual life: A look at the effective factors
Authors: Hajar Habibpour, Shabnam Omidvar, Afsaneh Bakhtiari, Karimollah Hajian-Tilaki, Hossein Ghorbani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The global prevalence of HPV is rising. Despite numerous studies on its effects on psychological dimensions, quality of life, and sexual performance, its impact on women’s sexual quality of life (QoL) remains unclear. This study assesses the sexual QoL among women with genital HPV and the influencing factors.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted on 395 women undergoing HPV testing. The data collection instrument comprised a questionnaire covering demographic characteristics, HPV-related traits, and a survey on sexual quality of life (SQOL-F). Univariate and multiple linear regression models were employed to forecast factors influencing the sexual QoL.RESULTS:The mean age of the participants was 30.58 ± 5.84 years. A statistically significant association was found between HPV infection in women (β = −4.39, CI = −7.94, −0.84) and their husbands (β = −4.89, CI = −8.46, −1.31) and lower sexual QoL in the participants. This association persisted even after adjusting for variables in the multiple linear regression models. Among the individual characteristics of the participants, only female occupation demonstrated a significant relationship with sexual QoL. Employed women scored lower on total sexual QoL compared to housewives (β = −3.94, CI = −7.77, −0.02).CONCLUSION:This study highlights HPV’s significant effect on women’s sexual QoL. Healthcare systems should offer support in primary care, including education and counseling.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a widespread sexually transmitted infection with over 100 types, posing a risk to a significant portion of the sexually active population. It is a leading cause of cervical cancer and a risk factor for various other cancers.[12] Apart from the direct health consequences, HPV, particularly in women, can lead to emotional distress, stigma, concerns about sexual health, and feelings of embarrassment and isolation. Visible genital warts can impact body image, causing negative self-perception and relationship issues, even leading to divorce. The impact of HPV on sexual relationship quality extends beyond physical symptoms, affecting mental and emotional well-being as well as relationships.[34]

The quality of sexual life (sexual QoL) holds paramount importance in the realms of sexual health and fertility. Contemporary perspectives affirm that the sexual QoL shares a symbiotic relationship with overall satisfaction and life quality. It serves as an indicator of individuals’ health status and the general well-being of society. Tailored to the context of each society, the impact of sexual QoL is evident in bolstering overall satisfaction, enhancing interpersonal relationships, particularly among women, and ultimately contributing to the health and cohesion of families and societies.[56] This concept encompasses psychological and sexual feelings, reflecting women’s experiences related to sexual satisfaction, the frequency and quality of sexual relationships, and their overall sentiments. It encapsulates positive emotions tied to self-perception and interpersonal connections, as well as negative feelings like diminished self-confidence, guilt, sexual repression, loss of pleasure, embarrassment, and avoidance of sexual activity.[7]

Insufficient attention has been directed toward a comprehensive understanding of the sexual QoL of women with HPV and the influencing factors, despite numerous studies exploring the QoL among individuals with HPV[8910] and their sexual performance.[111213] Lee et al.’s,[14] research revealed that genital warts had no discernible impact on men’s QoL but significantly affected women, leading to a reduction in their mental health. A qualitative study underscored the negative psychological and social consequences of this disease on both men and women, impacting their sexual relationships.[15] A study in Egypt focusing on women with genital warts reported that 98% of participants faced sexual problems, highlighting the correlation between women’s sexual dysfunction and their QoL.[16] However, Alay et al.’s[17] study did not observe any decline in the women’s sexual well-being after the diagnosis of HPV.

While the physical health consequences of HPV, such as its role in cancer, are well-documented, the psychological and emotional impacts, particularly on sexual QoL, have received less attention. Understanding how HPV affects sexual well-being is crucial, as it influences women’s mental health and relationships. Genital warts can damage self-esteem, body image, and emotional health, while the stigma surrounding HPV can lead to long-term effects on sexual satisfaction and relationships. Addressing these factors will enhance clinical care and help reduce stigma, encouraging women to seek both medical and psychological support.

Given the rising prevalence of HPV worldwide and its complex impact on sexual health, this study is crucial for filling a significant gap in the literature. By examining the various factors that influence sexual QoL in women with genital warts, the study will provide critical insights that can guide both healthcare providers and policymakers in designing comprehensive support strategies. This research holds the potential to enhance the quality of care for women affected by HPV, offering a more holistic approach that integrates physical, psychological, and relational dimensions of care, and ultimately improving their overall well-being and life satisfaction. The study aims to assess the sexual QoL among women with genital human HPV and explore the factors influencing it.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022 on women who visited the Pasteur referral laboratory/pathology center in Babol City, Mazandaran province, Iran, for HPV genital testing. The Pasteur Laboratory in Babol is a reputable and well-established private laboratory that offers a wide range of services with high accuracy. It is widely trusted and used by doctors and patients as a reliable reference in the region.

Study participants and sampling

Given the study’s focus on investigating the participants’ sexual QoL, all eligible women were included in the study by having undergone an HPV test up to one year before the study commenced. Inclusion criteria comprised female gender, marriage, having undergone an HPV virology test, literacy, Iranian citizenship, access to virtual space, and willingness to participate. Individuals were excluded if they completed the questionnaire incompletely or were engaged in polygamous relationships. The sample size was calculated with the assumption that the prevalence of a decrease in the sexual QoL in individuals without HPV is 20%. Using a confidence level of 0.95 and a test difference power P1–P2 = 0.08, a sample size of 395 was determined to evaluate the risk of a decrease in sexual QoL.

Data collection tools and technique

Initially, electronic files from the pathology department were reviewed to extract information such as age, sampling date, Pap smear result, HPV infection status (and, if positive, the type: high-risk or low-risk), and colposcopy results (if performed). Following this, initial contact was made with the participants via telephone. During these calls, the researcher introduced themselves, explained the purpose and methodology of the study, and emphasized the confidentiality of the information. Oral consent was obtained from the participants, after which a personal characteristics questionnaire was completed. Subsequently, a link to the sexual QoL questionnaire, prepared online through pressline, was sent to each participant with a unique code via WhatsApp. At the beginning of the link, the study’s purpose and the participants’ consent to complete the questionnaire were reiterated. If the participants consented, they completed the questionnaire. After submission, the researcher verified that the questionnaire was fully completed. Participants were then categorized into two groups based on HPV status: positive and negative. For those with a positive HPV status, the virus type (high-risk or low-risk) was determined.

Measurements

1. Demographic characteristics include age, length of marriage, number of children, financial sufficiency, residence place, education, and employment status. HPV-related traits encompass the duration of disease diagnosis, Pap smear, and colposcopy results (if applicable), physical and mental health conditions, presence of external genital warts, treatment history for genital warts, history of HPV vaccination, HPV status, and, if positive, the type of HPV in both the women and their husbands.

2. Regarding the HPV detection method in this laboratory, it should be noted that the sampling was performed by a physician in the office, medical centers, or by an experienced technician in the laboratory. A sampling was taken from the cervix, and if there were wart lesions on the skin, a sampling of these lesions was also taken. After transferring the sample to the pathology unit, the sample was examined using the AB Aronika kit for HPV detection. To identify the HPV type, the real-time PCR method was used.

3. Sexual quality of life-female (SQOL-F) is a self-reported questionnaire designed to assess individuals’ attitudes toward various aspects of sexual QoL. Distinguishing itself from existing tools in Iran that primarily concentrate on sexual function dimensions, this four-factor 18-question questionnaire was developed by Symonds et al. in 2005.[18] It specifically considers the quality of sexual relations. The subscales include “Psychosexual Feelings,” “Sexual Satisfaction and Relationship,” “Self-Worthlessness,” and “Sexual Suppression. Each question is rated on a six-point scale, spanning from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” There is no designated cut-off point, and a higher total score indicates a better sexual QoL. The data for this variable were analyzed quantitatively. The psychometric properties of this questionnaire were validated in Iran in 2013.[19]

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into the SPSS version 26 software. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For analytical analysis, independent t-tests and Chi-square tests were employed. Single and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to predict the sexual QoL score. Variables with a significance level below 0.1 in the simple analysis were included in the multiple model. A significance level of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

This research received ethical approval with the code IR.MUBABOL.REC.1400.194 from the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all of the participants in the study.

Results

The mean age of the participants was 30.6 ± 5.8 years (minimum 20 and maximum 50 years), and their husbands were 34.5 ± 5.7 years. Regarding educational background, 51.5% of women had a university education, and 46% were housewives [Table 1]. Table 2 presents the individual characteristics related to HPV for the women and their husbands. The results of the relationship between the sexual QoL and the HPV status revealed a higher score of the total sexual QoL (47.5 ± 17.4 vs. 43.1 ± 14.1; P ≤ 0.009) and two of its subscales such as self-worthlessness (7.5 ± 3.5 vs. 6.1 ± 2.4; P ≤ 0.017) and sexual suppression (7.9 ± 3.7 vs. 7.0 ± 3.1; P ≤ 0.011) in women with negative HPV, in contrast to women with HPV positive [Table 3]. Interestingly, the HPV type had no discernible effect on the sexual QoL and its subscales. Binary analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between the individual characteristics of women and their husbands with the sexual QoL according to women’s HPV status. In particular, the sexual QoL was significantly higher among HPV-negative women who were housewives with a family income considered adequate and without the presence of physical diseases (P < 0.05). Other characteristics did not show significant association (Data not shown).

Discussion

The study found a significant decrease in the sexual QoL among HPV-positive women. Specifically, scores on two subscales, such as self-worthlessness and sexual suppression, were significantly lower in HPV-positive individuals compared to HPV-negative individuals. Women are particularly vulnerable to the health and sexual effects of HPV infections, experiencing nearly double the rate of genital warts compared to men and facing increased susceptibility to oncogenic HPV, particularly in the cervical and vulvar areas. Despite this, women often underestimate the health and sexual risks associated with HPV infections. Research on the specific impact of HPV, including factors like HPV type, genital warts, and abnormal cervical cytology, on women’s sexual QoL is limited. Existing studies tend to focus on overall QoL,[91116] psychological consequences such as depression and anxiety,[8142021] or women’s sexual performance.[11113]

In a study from Portugal, researchers investigated the psychosocial effects of HPV on 194 women using the HPV impact profile questionnaire. They found that women with more psychological complications and emotional suppression due to HPV experienced lower sexual satisfaction.[22] Similarly, Ciccarese et al.[13] demonstrated, in Italy, the impact of sexually transmitted diseases on women’s QoL, mood, and sexual performance. A study from Brazil also highlighted the negative impact of HPV infection on women’s overall QoL, including their sexual, emotional, physical, and daily habits.[23] Caruso et al.[24] further supported these findings by showing a reduction in all aspects of women’s sexual QoL, following a positive HPV result.

Research on sexual dysfunction in HPV patients has consistently found that many individuals experience difficulties, particularly in sexual desire and frequency of intercourse.[111316] Numerous studies have documented a significant decrease in sexual activity among participants following an HPV diagnosis.[23] For instance, Taberna et al.[25] found that only 38% of HPV patients reported no impact on their sexual lives or their partners, with the majority experiencing changes in sexual function after diagnosis.

However, some findings present contradictory results, likely influenced by variations in study design, participant demographics, and diagnostic methods used.[2627] For instance, a UK randomized controlled trial examining the psychosexual impact of HPV testing found no differences in psychosexual functioning between individuals with HPV-positive or negative results.[28] Similarly, a study by Alay et al.[17] assessing the impact of HPV on the Sexual Function Index and Beck Anxiety Inventory reported no significant differences between HPV-positive and negative groups. Nahidi et al.[29] also found no disparities in sexual satisfaction, intercourse frequency, or arousal among women with and without HPV. Keles et al.’s[30] research indicated that while HPV diagnosis did not significantly affect anxiety levels or sexual performance, it did elevate depression scores. Participant characteristics, such as educational level, may contribute to these discrepancies, with higher education potentially providing more access to information and effective coping strategies for dealing with HPV. Cultural and social differences among participants, as well as variations in measurement time points, may also influence attitudes toward HPV. Prospective studies involving more diverse samples can further explore these hypotheses.

Upon learning of their HPV infection, women often encounter a range of challenges that disrupt their daily lives, leading to physical, emotional, psychological, sexual, and social changes. Genital alterations, a prominent feature in women with HPV, not only cause discomfort and bleeding during intercourse but also evoke feelings of shame and embarrassment, potentially triggering marital difficulties.[23] Concerns about distrust and the risk of infidelity, as noted by Jeng et al.,[31] can strain relationships and disturb women’s peace of mind, with suspicions of extramarital affairs serving as a source of anxiety about infection. Qualitative studies delving into the experiences and perceptions of women with HPV reveal a spectrum of emotions, including fear, anxiety, worry, anger, confusion, doubt, struggle, sadness, embarrassment, tension, regret, hopelessness, and depression following diagnosis. While these feelings often subside within a year for most women, they persist long term for a third of women.[3233]

Regarding the demographic factors affecting the sexual QoL in women with HPV, our study revealed that HPV-negative women who were housewives had a stable family income and were free from physical ailments enjoyed a higher quality of sexual life. In a longitudinal study, Hsu et al.[8] observed that working women had better HPV-related QoL. Similarly, Nahidi and colleagues found a higher prevalence of sexual dysfunction among unemployed individuals and housewives compared to self-employed and government employees.[29] However, none of these studies specifically investigated women’s sexual life quality, which differs from overall QoL and sexual performance. Various cognitive factors can influence women’s sexual experiences. For instance, employed women often face heightened stress due to work demands, while irregular schedules and long hours may lead to fatigue and reduced energy, thereby limiting sexual opportunities. This disparity is notably less pronounced among housewives.

One unexpected findings from this study were the absence of a connection between HPV type and Pap smear results with women’s sexual QoL. Research has indicated that patients testing positive for HPV 16 and 18 often report heightened anxiety, especially with abnormal cytological findings.[2627] It is hypothesized that the uncertainty surrounding the prognosis of high-risk HPV infection and abnormal cytology, coupled with delays in diagnostic procedures, may exacerbate impairment in both QoL and sexual function. Interestingly, compared to individuals with other sexually transmitted viral diseases, those with genital HPV tend to report better health-related QoL outcomes.[34] Several factors may contribute to this phenomenon.[3536] Unlike some STIs, HPV infections often resolve spontaneously, reducing symptom burden and long-term complications. Advances in screening and vaccination have bolstered awareness and prevention efforts, potentially mitigating psychological distress post-diagnosis. Additionally, the intermittent nature of HPV infection, characterized by periods of latency and shedding, may lessen the chronicity of symptoms and their impact on daily life. However, it is important to note that health-related QoL outcomes can vary widely based on factors such as HPV-related complications, coping strategies, support systems, and healthcare access. Moreover, the psychosocial ramifications of HPV diagnosis may differ significantly among diverse populations, underscoring the need for further research to comprehensively explore these complexities.

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting our study’s results. Firstly, the cross-sectional design prohibits establishing causality between research variables. Additionally, reliance on self-reporting tools may influence the findings. Given the scarcity of studies examining HPV-related aspects of Iranian women’s sexual lives, integrating our findings into an evidence-based argument is challenging. While our study population was relatively homogeneous, our samples may not fully represent all women undergoing HPV testing in Babol City. However, a notable strength of our study is its novelty; it is the first to assess changes in women’s sexual QoL-based on HPV genotype, Pap smear cytological status, vaccination status, and husband’s HPV status simultaneously, providing insights through a reliable methodology.

Given the limitations of this study, future research should consider using a longitudinal design to better examine causal relationships between variables. To reduce bias from self-report instruments, it would be beneficial to combine various data collection methods, including clinical interviews. Expanding the sample to include women from different regions could also improve the generalizability of the findings. As this study is the first to simultaneously assess the effects of HPV genotype, Pap smear status, and partner HPV status on women’s sexual QoL, future studies could explore the socio-economic and cultural factors that influence these outcomes.

Conclusion

The study revealed that HPV infection can significantly impact women’s sexual QoL. An important and unexpected finding was the absence of correlation between Pap smear results, HPV type, and participants’ sexual QoL. This may reflect a lack of awareness among women regarding the significance of HPV for their health. Hence, supportive measures are needed in primary healthcare settings to address this issue, including informing women about various aspects of the disease and offering counseling sessions.
