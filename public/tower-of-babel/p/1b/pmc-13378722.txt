Association of CBC‑derived inflammation indices with prevalent CKD: a cross‑sectional analysis of a Chinese data set and NHANES
Authors: Ruichao Feng, Yang Shao, Yuxin Zhao, Xiangfei Cui, Congcong Jiao, Hua Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the established association between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and systemic inflammation, this study examined the relationships between complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory markers and prevalent CKD.MethodsTwo data sets (Chinese clinical data, n = 4,518; NHANES 2017–2020, n = 7,724) are analyzed. Seven CBC‑derived inflammatory indices were calculated: SII, SIRI, NLR, dNLR, NMLR, MLR, and PLR. Each index was categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4). Weighted logistic regression (for NHANES) and unweighted logistic regression (for the Chinese clinical data) were applied to estimate associations with CKD, with sequential adjustment for demographics, body mass index, and comorbidities. To control for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Furthermore, subgroup analysis, restricted cubic spline analysis and sensitivity analysis were also performed in this study.ResultsIn the two fully adjusted models, the highest quartile (Q4) of SIRI, NLR, and NMLR was significantly associated with higher odds of prevalent CKD compared with Q1. After FDR correction, these associations remained significant in both cohorts: in the Chinese cohort, all padj < 0.001; in NHANES, padj = 0.028 for SIRI, 0.040 for NLR, and 0.041 for NMLR. Sensitivity analyses consistently supported the primary findings: excluding outliers, log2 transformation, and the alternative CKD definition yielded similar effect directions and significance levels. Heterogeneity was observed across subgroups, and restricted cubic splines revealed nonlinear dose‑response relationships (p for nonlinearity <0.05).ConclusionsElevated SIRI, NLR, and NMLR are associated with prevalent CKD, suggesting systemic inflammation may play a role, yet prospective studies are required to establish temporality and causality.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, complete blood count, inflammation, cross-sectional study, NHANES

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is clinically defined as sustained (>3 months) kidney damage or reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) [1]. This condition has become a major global health issue, with 1.2 million deaths worldwide in 2017 attributed to CKD [2]. Notably, cardiovascular disease (CVD) emerges as the predominant cause of mortality in CKD populations, with inflammation serving as the central pathophysiological link connecting CKD and CVD [3]. At the molecular level, endogenous ligand-induced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome constitutes a key driver of inflammatory responses in both CKD and CVD [4]. Accumulating evidence indicates that inflammation plays a pivotal role in both the development and progression of CKD [5]. Particular attention has been directed toward the relationship between complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory markers and CKD in recent research.

Current literature has documented preliminary investigations into associations between individual inflammatory markers, such as leukocyte count, monocyte count, platelet count, and mean platelet volume, with CKD [6–9]. Recent research focus has shifted toward composite inflammatory indices, including the C-reactive protein-to-lymphocyte ratio (CLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) in relation to CKD epidemiology [10–15].

Although these markers demonstrate potential associations with CKD, most previous studies have focused on one or a few inflammatory indices within a single population. A systematic comparison of multiple CBC‑derived inflammatory indices across distinct ethnic groups has not yet been conducted. This study, therefore, compares seven CBC‑derived inflammatory markers using a Chinese hospital data set and NHANES 2017–2020, assesses their dose–response relationships and provides cross‑population replication.

Materials and methods

Sample size calculation

According to the Sixth China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance, the prevalence of CKD in China was approximately 8.2% [16]. Data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) indicate that approximately 14% adults in the United States have CKD (https://www.cdc.gov/kidney-disease/php/data-research/index.html). The minimum required sample sizes were calculated to be 2,892 for the Chinese population and at least 4,626 for the U.S. population.

Research population

The Chinese clinical data set consisted of patients admitted to the Department of Nephrology at Shengjing Hospital between January 1 and December 31, 2023. Including demographic, laboratory, and diagnostic information from nephrology patients between January 1 and December 31, 2023 (n = 4,536). After handling missing values, 4,518 valid samples were retained.

To further validate the findings, we incorporated data from the NHANES. The NHANES study period used in this analysis covers 2017-March 2020. Publicly available data were downloaded (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes; December 27, 2024), and participants were selected based on the availability of key variables relevant to our study objectives. Standardized NHANES protocols were followed for data collection, including structured interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. From an initial pool of 15,560 eligible participants, we applied consistent data filtering and missing value handling procedures, resulting in 7,724 valid samples for external replication.

This study strictly adhered to ethical standards and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants were required to sign an informed consent form before participation. The sample size exceeded the calculated requirement for sufficient statistical power. The flowchart of this study is shown in Figure 1.

Variable definitions

The exposure factors in this study were inflammatory markers related to CBC (Table 1), including SII, SIRI, NLR, dNLR, NMLR, MLR, and PLR. This study defined CKD as the primary outcome measure. CKD cases in Shengjing Hospital’s clinical data were diagnosed according to the KDIGO 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline [17]. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated from serum creatinine (Scr) levels using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI 2009) equation [18]. This equation has been adopted in large‑scale epidemiological studies on CKD. In the NHANES database, CKD was defined as a single eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 without albuminuria or a 3‑month confirmation [19].

Covariates in this study included demographic information and other health conditions. Shengjing Hospital’s clinical data contained age and gender as demographic variables, while comorbid conditions, including body mass index (BMI), hypertension (defined by High Blood Pressure variable), hypercholesterolemia (defined by High Cholesterol Level variable), diabetes, hepatitis B, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, and thyroid disease, were obtained from medical records and discharge diagnoses. The NHANES database comprised demographic parameters, including age, gender, and race. Health conditions were ascertained through participant self-reports and proxy interviews. Among them, BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable, with categorization into quartiles based on the study of population distribution during statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi‑square test. All tests were two‑sided, and a significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted. Each inflammatory index was categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on its distribution, with the lowest quartile (Q1) as reference. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed in three steps. In Model 1, only the impact of exposure factors on the outcome was considered. In Model 2, adjustments were made for demographic factors such as age and gender. In Model 3, building upon Model 2, additional covariates were incorporated, including BMI, high blood pressure, high cholesterol level, diabetes, hepatitis B, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, and thyroid disease. For NHANES data, all regression models accounted for the complex survey design using the survey package (svyglm) with weights (WTMECPRP), strata (SDMVSTRA), and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU). The Chinese clinical data set was analyzed using unweighted logistic regression (glm). To control the false discovery rate (FDR) for testing seven inflammatory indices, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to the p values of Q4 vs Q1 (fully adjusted model, Model 3). An FDR‑adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subgroup analyses were performed to estimate the association of each inflammatory index with CKD across strata of key covariates. Effect heterogeneity was formally tested using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without exposure‑by‑subgroup interaction terms. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to examine potential nonlinear dose‑response relationships, and the overall and nonlinear p values are reported. NHANES data analyses (logistic regression, subgroup, RCS) were weighted.

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, three sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, observations with values beyond Q1–3 × IQR or Q3 + 3 × IQR for exposure, BMI, and age were removed, and the logistic models (Models 1–3) were re‑run. Second, the exposure was log2‑transformed and entered into the models (Models 1–3) as a continuous variable. Third, diagnostic criteria for CKD differed between the Chinese clinical and NHANES data. To assess the effect of this diagnostic heterogeneity and maintain consistency across the two data sets, we performed a supplementary sensitivity analysis, in which CKD was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Results

The Chinese clinical data set comprised 4,518 participants, including 1,709 diagnosed with CKD (Table 2). Participants had a median age of 55 [41,65] years, with 2,636 (58.3%) males and 1,882 (41.7%) females. Compared to non-CKD participants, CKD patients demonstrated significantly elevated levels of systemic inflammatory indices (SII, SIRI, NLR, dNLR, NMLR, MLR, PLR; p < 0.001). Significant differences between the two groups were observed in age, BMI, gender, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, congestive heart failure, and coronary heart disease between the CKD and non-CKD groups (p < 0.05).

Logistic regression model

Multi-model logistic regression analyses were employed to assess associations between inflammatory exposures and CKD. In the Chinese data set (Table 4, Supplementary Table 1-3), higher quartiles of inflammatory markers (SII, SIRI, NLR, dNLR, NMLR, MLR, PLR) were associated with significantly higher odds of prevalent CKD compared to the lowest quartile (Q1) across all logistic regression models (Models 1–3). In NHANES (Table 5, Supplementary Table 4-6), Q3/Q4 levels of SIRI, NLR, dNLR, NMLR, and MLR showed higher odds of CKD compared to Q1 levels in Model 1 (p < 0.05). After adjusting for age, gender, and race (Model 2), Q4 levels of SIRI, NLR, dNLR, NMLR, and MLR maintained significant associations with prevalent CKD (p < 0.05). Following additional adjustment for clinical covariates (Model 3), Q4 quartiles of SIRI (OR = 1.893, 95% CI: 1.225–2.924, p = 0.004), NLR (OR = 1.717, 95% CI: 1.130–2.609, p = 0.011), and NMLR (OR = 1.669, 95% CI: 1.094–2.546, p = 0.017) persistently exhibited significant associations. To minimize false positives, FDR correction was applied (Supplementary Table 7). The adjusted p values for Q4 vs Q1 in the Chinese clinical data and NHANES data revealed that SIRI, NLR, and NMLR remained significantly associated with prevalent CKD (Chinese clinical: all FDR < 0.001; NHANES: SIRI FDR = 0.028, NLR FDR = 0.040, NMLR FDR = 0.041).

Subgroup analysis results

Subgroup analyses revealed heterogeneity in associations between inflammatory markers (SIRI, NLR, NMLR) and CKD. Within the Chinese clinical data set, the significant association between SIRI and CKD remained robust regardless of sex, age, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and thyroid disease status (Figure 2A). Such a significant association between SIRI and prevalent CKD was also consistently observed in subgroups without hyperlipidemia, hepatitis B, congestive heart failure, or coronary heart disease. NLR (Figure 3A) and NMLR (Figure 4A) maintained significant CKD associations across all subgroups stratified by gender, age, BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and thyroid disease status. These associations persisted even after excluding participants with hepatitis B, congestive heart failure, or coronary heart disease (Figures 3A and 4A). Notably, BMI, congestive heart failure, and thyroid disease status exhibited significant interaction effects with all three markers (SIRI, NLR, NMLR) on CKD (p < 0.05).

In the NHANES dataset, SIRI was significantly associated with higher odds of prevalent CKD across all subgroups stratified by gender, age, BMI, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and thyroid diseases (p < 0.05; Figure 2B). This association was also statistically significant among racial groups, including Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, and Non-Hispanic White participants. Furthermore, the association was significant only in patients without hepatitis B or congestive heart failure, and a significant interaction with age was observed (p for interaction = 0.044). Similarly, NLR exhibited significant associations with prevalent CKD in subgroups of BMI, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and thyroid diseases (Figure 3B), and was significant in all racial groups except the ‘Other Hispanic’ subgroup. The NLR-CKD association remained significant in individuals who were ≤65 years, male, non-diabetic, free of hepatitis B, congestive heart failure, and coronary heart disease. Likewise, NMLR was significantly associated with prevalent CKD in most subgroups, including gender, BMI, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and thyroid disease (Figure 4B), but not in the age > 65, hepatitis B, congestive heart failure, and ‘Other Hispanic’ racial group. Importantly, significant interaction effects with BMI were identified for the associations of both NLR and NMLR with CKD (p for interaction <0.05).

Results of RCS analyses

On the RCS curves, the points corresponding to OR = 1 and the 5th and 95th percentiles of the exposure distribution were marked as references. In the Chinese clinical data set, the odds of prevalent CKD increased progressively with rising levels of SIRI (Figure 5A), NLR (Figure 5B), and NMLR (Figure 5C). The nodal points (OR > 1) were identified at SIRI > 1.415, NLR > 2.688, and NMLR > 3.186. For example, for SIRI, the exposure increased from 0.429 (5th percentile) to 6.600 (95th percentile), while the corresponding OR rose from 0.898 to 1.129. In the NHANES dataset, similar nonlinear patterns were observed for SIRI (Figure 5D), NLR (Figure 5E), and NMLR (Figure 5F), with points at SIRI > 1.046, NLR > 1.898, and NMLR > 2.120 (OR > 1). In contrast, the increase in OR from the 5th to the 95th percentile was more gradual in NHANES than in the Chinese clinical data set. The consistent nonlinear associations across two distinct populations support the robustness of the dose‑response relationships.

Sensitivity analysis results

Three sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, after extreme outliers were excluded (Supplementary Table 8), SIRI, NLR, and NMLR remained significantly associated with CKD in both data sets. In the Chinese clinical data (Supplementary Table 9), the ORs for Q4 vs Q1 were 1.634 (95% CI: 1.344–1.987, p < 0.001) for SIRI, 3.113 (95% CI: 2.532–3.826, p < 0.001) for NLR, and 3.134 (95% CI: 2.550–3.852, p < 0.001) for NMLR. In NHANES (Supplementary Table 10), the corresponding ORs were 1.833 (95% CI: 1.171–2.867, p = 0.008) for SIRI, 1.664 (95% CI: 1.091–2.538, p = 0.018) for NLR, and 1.699 (95% CI: 1.071–2.694, p = 0.024) for NMLR. These results were consistent with the primary analysis, indicating that extreme outliers did not drive the observed associations. Second, when the exposure was log2‑transformed and analyzed as a continuous variable, each doubling of SIRI, NLR, and NMLR was significantly associated with an increased risk of CKD in the Chinese data set (Supplementary Table 11). In NHANES (Supplementary Table 12), the corresponding ORs were 1.223 (95% CI: 1.039–1.440, p = 0.016) for SIRI, 1.254 (95% CI: 0.990–1.588, p = 0.060) for NLR, and 1.273 (95% CI: 1.000–1.620, p = 0.050) for NMLR. Although the p values of NLR and NMLR in the NHANES log2-conversion analysis are at the significant boundary value. The direction and magnitude of the ORs were consistent with the primary quartile‑based findings. Overall, the sensitivity analysis supports the robustness of SIRI as the most consistent marker, while the associations for NLR and NMLR require further investigation in larger prospective studies. Third, to harmonize CKD definitions across the two data sets, we redefined CKD in the Chinese clinical data as a single eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Under this definition, the associations of SIRI, NLR, and NMLR with CKD remained statistically significant (all p < 0.001 for Q4 vs Q1 in Model 3) (Supplementary Table 13). This suggests that the observed associations were not materially affected by the heterogeneity in CKD diagnostic criteria. In summary, the three sensitivity analyses consistently supported the primary findings.

Discussion

Biological significance of CBC-derived inflammatory indices

This study systematically evaluated the associations between CBC-derived inflammatory markers and CKD. Our findings indicated that elevated levels of SIRI, NLR, and NMLR were significantly and positively associated with higher odds of prevalent CKD (p < 0.05), demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship. Multiple studies have proposed SIRI as a potential biomarker associated with CKD. Elevated SIRI is significantly associated with higher odds of CKD and is linked to increased CKD prevalence and all-cause mortality [14,15]. In hypertensive populations, elevated SIRI is linked to a higher prevalence of CKD among hypertensive individuals, presenting a positive correlation between them [20]. Furthermore, heightened SIRI is expected to become an independent factor associated with CKD progression [21,22]. Elevated NMLR levels are positively associated with higher odds of adverse cardiovascular events and CKD progression in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD. Routine assessment of NMLR, especially when exceeding 1.6, may facilitate risk stratification and early intervention [23]. Evidence suggested that an elevated NLR correlated with more advanced CKD stages, serving as a potential biomarker for predicting disease progression [24]. Additionally, NLR had value as a supplementary indicator for the prediction of uremic symptoms and hemodialysis requirement in patients with stage 5 CKD [25]. Moreover, NLR has been shown to predict mortality risk during intensive care unit hospitalization in patients with CAD or CKD, providing a useful tool for risk assessment and management in high-risk populations [26]. In summary, higher levels of SIRI, NLR, and NMLR are associated with increased odds of renal impairment and warrant clinical attention.

Population heterogeneity in inflammatory markers

This study integrated data from NHANES and electronic medical records from Chinese clinical settings, demonstrating cross‑population consistency of systemic inflammation indices (SIRI, NLR, NMLR) in association with prevalent CKD. The RCS model was employed to quantify population heterogeneity in inflection point. The points where the OR exceeded 1 were higher in the Chinese data set (SIRI > 1.415; NLR > 2.688; NMLR > 3.186) than in the NHANES population (SIRI > 1.046; NLR > 1.898; NMLR > 2.120), suggesting that the level at which these markers begin to show elevated odds of CKD may vary by genetic background, comorbidity profiles, or environmental exposures. In both data sets, inflammatory indices showed stronger associations in the high‑BMI subgroup, with significant interaction effects, indicating that obesity may amplify inflammation‑related kidney damage. Mechanistically, adipose tissue secretes pro‑inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNF‑α, IL‑6, IL‑1β, MCP‑1), promoting chronic low‑grade inflammation and immune cell infiltration, which stress the renal microenvironment [27,28]. Additionally, obesity‑related insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and altered renal hemodynamics (e.g. intraglomerular hypertension) directly injure the kidney and interact with inflammatory pathways, creating a vicious cycle [29–32]. Therefore, enhanced inflammatory monitoring and early intervention are essential in obese populations. Future research should explore the impact of population characteristics such as race and fat distribution patterns on the results.

CKD-induced systemic inflammation

Although this study focused on assessing the association between inflammatory markers and prevalent CKD, accumulating evidence indicates that CKD itself can drive systemic inflammation through different mechanisms. In CKD, dysfunctional mitochondria release damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These DAMPs may activate immune receptors, upregulate chemokines, and recruit inflammatory cells [33]. Mendelian randomization studies have revealed bidirectional causal relationships between systemic inflammatory regulators and CKD or kidney function Reverse analysis showed that CKD is positively associated with levels of granulocyte colony‑stimulating factor (G‑CSF) and stem cell factor (SCF) [34]. However, due to the cross‑sectional design of this study, we cannot determine whether inflammation is a cause or a consequence of CKD. Future prospective data set studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between inflammation and CKD, thereby elucidating the exact role of inflammatory responses in the onset and progression of CKD.

Considerations for individualized assessment

The clinical interpretation of systemic inflammatory markers requires careful consideration of inter-individual heterogeneity. Key determinants, such as age, genetic background, concurrent pharmacotherapy, and coexisting inflammatory disorders, may significantly influence the prognostic reliability of these indices. For instance, elderly individuals often exhibit elevated baseline levels of inflammatory markers due to immunosenescence, a process linked to an enhanced pro-inflammatory state mediated by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype [35]. This may confound renal risk stratification. Furthermore, common comorbidities such as hypertension [36], hyperlipidemia [37], diabetes [38], coronary heart disease [39], heart failure [40], hepatitis B [41], and thyroid disorders [42] can independently influence inflammatory indices, thereby attenuating their specificity for kidney-related pathology. Multivariable adjustment cannot fully eliminate unmeasured confounders. Acute illness may rapidly raise inflammatory markers (e.g. NLR) [43], potentially overestimating CKD associations if acute illness is more prevalent among cases. NSAIDs, commonly used in CKD patients, are linked to acute kidney injury [44]; immunosuppressants can alter kidney injury marker concentrations, introducing residual confounding [45]. Lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diet, ect) also influence systemic inflammation [46] and CKD risk [47]. Moreover, we did not exclude key populations that substantially alter CBC parameters (e.g. dialysis, transplantation, active infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, immunosuppressive therapy). In dialysis patients, NLR levels are elevated and correlate with inflammatory biomarkers [48]. This inclusive approach enhances real‑world generalizability, but there will be offsets. Nonetheless, consistent findings across the Chinese clinical data set and NHANES suggest robustness against these biases. Future studies should systematically collect data on acute illness, medication use, and lifestyle factors to further validate these results.

Implications for public health and health system integration

Our cross‑sectional findings may inform future prospective studies. If confirmed in longitudinal data sets, closer monitoring of inflammatory markers in high‑risk populations could be considered for early detection of renal injury signals. Similarly, lifestyle modifications [49,50] and optimized metabolic management (e.g., glycemic and blood pressure control) [51,52] might be explored as part of preventive strategies. Given the low cost and wide availability of CBC testing. Digital health platforms, such as electronic health records systems, could assist in monitoring these parameters and assessing their impact on outcomes and healthcare efficiency [53]. Further cost‑effectiveness evaluations would be valuable to determine whether such screening might reduce long‑term CKD complications and costs.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, CKD in NHANES was defined using a single eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 without three‑month confirmation, which may introduce misclassification. However, a sensitivity analysis using the same definition in the Chinese data set yielded consistent results, supporting robustness. Second, the Chinese data set was hospital‑based, limiting generalizability to community‑dwelling populations. Third, CBC‑derived indices can be affected by acute conditions (e.g. infections, trauma) and medications (e.g. steroids, immunosuppressants), which were not fully accounted for. Fourth, the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference. Fifth, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g. diet, physical activity) and the inclusion of individuals with acute infections, malignancies, or autoimmune diseases may bias associations, though this inclusive approach improves real‑world representativeness. Finally, the lack of interventional or longitudinal data limits clinical translation. Whether modulating these inflammatory indices improves renal outcomes remains unknown. Future prospective studies with repeated measurements and community‑based controls are needed to validate these findings and explore causal pathways.

Conclusion

By integrating multicenter evidence from clinical data in China and the U.S. NHANES database, this study systematically demonstrated a significant association between CBC-derived inflammatory markers (SIRI, NLR, and NMLR) and CKD. Future prospective studies are warranted to clarify the temporal sequence and causal relationship, evaluate the dynamic predictive value of these inflammatory markers, and lay an evidence foundation for targeted anti-inflammatory intervention strategies.
