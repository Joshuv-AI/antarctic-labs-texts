Association of the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality: evidence from two nationwide prospective cohort studies

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is closely linked to cardiometabolic disorders, and cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death among affected individuals. Identifying simple biomarkers that capture metabolic–inflammatory burden and predict long-term mortality in MASLD remains a clinical priority. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) integrates inflammation, dyslipidaemia, and glycaemic status, but its relevance to MASLD and long-term mortality has not been fully elucidated.MethodsWe conducted a multi-stage investigation using two nationally representative cohorts from the United States and China. Cross-sectional associations between CTI and MASLD were assessed in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Prospective associations of CTI with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality among participants with MASLD were examined utilising multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic splines, threshold analyses, and competing risk models. Causal mediation analyses were performed to measure the mediating functions of diabetes, hypertension, and body mass index. Extensive sensitivity analyses using alternative MASLD definitions and analytic strategies were conducted to assess robustness. Results from NHANES were externally validated in CHARLS.ResultsHigher CTI levels were strongly and nonlinearly associated with the presence of MASLD in both cohorts. Among individuals with MASLD, elevated CTI was associated with significantly increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Each unit increase in CTI in NHANES was linked to a 57% increased risk of cardiovascular death (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.24–1.99) and a 47% increased risk of all-cause death (hazard ratio [HR] 1.47, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.28–1.69) in fully adjusted models. A pronounced threshold effect was observed, with mortality risk rising sharply once CTI exceeded approximately 5.6. Consistent associations with all-cause mortality were observed in CHARLS. Mediation analyses indicated that diabetes accounted for a substantial proportion of the association between CTI and mortality, whereas body mass index played a minimal mediating role.ConclusionsCTI is a robust metabolic–inflammatory marker associated with MASLD and long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality across diverse populations. The strong mediating role of diabetes underscores the central importance of glycaemic dysfunction in cardiometabolic risk. Introduction

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, has become the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide [1]. Its global burden continues to increase in parallel with the rising prevalence of obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [2]. Although MASLD is primarily a hepatic condition, accumulating evidence indicates that cardiovascular disease (CVD)—rather than liver-related complications—is the leading cause of death among individuals with MASLD [3]. The identification of reliable, easily obtainable biomarkers that capture the metabolic and inflammatory disturbances underlying MASLD and predict long-term mortality, therefore, represents a major clinical priority.

Metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation metabolic dysregulation play central roles in both the progress of MASLD and its associated cardiometabolic complications [4]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides, and fasting glucose are well-established markers linked to insulin resistance, subclinical inflammation, atherogenesis, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [5]. The C-reactive Protein–Triglyceride–Glucose index (CTI) combines these three components into a single composite marker, reflecting the combined metabolic–inflammatory burden [6]. Recent studies have shown that CTI is associated with metabolic syndrome, cardiometabolic risk, and all-cause mortality in general populations [7]. However, the relevance of CTI to MASLD and its long-term prognosis has not been fully elucidated.

Two major knowledge gaps remain. First, it is unclear whether CTI is associated with the presence of MASLD at the population level. Second, and more importantly, no study has comprehensively evaluated whether CTI predicts long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with MASLD, nor assessed the generalizability of such associations across diverse populations. Addressing these gaps would provide valuable insights into the metabolic–inflammatory pathways linking MASLD and cardiometabolic mortality.

To fill these evidence gaps, we conducted a multi-stage investigation using two nationally representative, long-term prospective cohort studies from the United States and China. We first evaluated the cross-sectional association between CTI and the prevalence of MASLD in the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. We then examined the associations of CTI with long-term cardiovascular and all-cause mortality among individuals with MASLD in both the NHANES and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohorts. Together, these analyses aimed to determine whether CTI is a clinically meaningful biomarker for identifying MASLD and predicting its adverse long-term results.

Methods

Study design

This study integrated cross-sectional analyses and two prospective cohort analyses using two nationally representative datasets from the United States and China. The overall analytic framework consisted of three components: (1) cross-sectional assessment of the association between CTI and MASLD in NHANES and the baseline wave of CHARLS (2011); (2) prospective analysis of CTI and long-term fatality among participants with MASLD in NHANES; and (3) external validation of mortality associations in the CHARLS cohort. Data sources and study populations

To reflect the civilian population in the United States, NHANES uses a multistage probability sampling design [8]. Participants aged ≥ 20 years with available data on CRP, triglycerides, fasting glucose, MASLD indicators, mortality linkage, and covariates were included. Exclusion criteria included: excessive alcohol intake (≥ 14 drinks/week for men, ≥ 7 drinks/week for women), viral hepatitis (HBV/HCV positivity), pregnancy, implausible laboratory values, and missing covariates [9]. NHANES analytic procedures incorporated sampling weights, clusters, and strata per NCHS guidelines [10].

CHARLS is an ongoing nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years selected via multistage, stratified household sampling. Two components were utilised: the CHARLS 2011 baseline for cross-sectional analysis, and the CHARLS 2011–2018 follow-up for prospective mortality analysis [11]. Participants with available CRP, triglycerides, fasting glucose, anthropometric measures, covariates, and mortality follow-up were included. Individuals with viral hepatitis, excessive alcohol consumption, or missing data were excluded.

Exposure: C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI)

Although CTI shares certain metabolic components with the indices used to define MASLD, it was conceptualised as a composite marker of systemic metabolic–inflammatory burden rather than a liver-specific steatosis indicator.

CTI was defined as:

CRP (mg/L), triglycerides (mg/dL), and fasting glucose (mg/dL) were measured using standardised laboratory procedures in both cohorts [12]. CTI was modelled continuously and categorically (quartiles). Normal distribution assessments are shown in Fig. S1. CTI was not designed to replace existing TyG-based indices, but rather to extend the TyG framework by incorporating systemic inflammation, thereby capturing an integrated metabolic–inflammatory burden.

In NHANES, serum C-reactive protein was measured using standardised high-sensitivity assays under the supervision of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, with rigorous internal and external quality-control procedures. In CHARLS, CRP measurements were conducted in centralised laboratories following standardised protocols, including batch calibration and internal quality-control checks. Although laboratory platforms differed between cohorts, both studies employed validated assays and strict quality-control procedures to ensure measurement reliability.

Outcomes (MASLD)

In the CHARLS cohort, direct assessments of hepatic steatosis, such as liver imaging or histological data, were not available. Therefore, MASLD could not be defined using gold-standard diagnostic modalities. In line with previous large-scale epidemiological studies conducted in similar population settings, we operationalised MASLD using a validated non-invasive surrogate index [13–17].

MASLD was operationally defined using the TyG-WC index, a metabolic surrogate that integrates fasting triglycerides, fasting glucose, and waist circumference, which are closely linked to hepatic fat accumulation and metabolic dysfunction [13]. We acknowledge that TyG-WC is not a liver-specific marker; however, in cohort studies where direct assessment of liver condition is unfeasible, this index has been employed in several pioneering articles as a surrogate indicator for fatty liver disease [18]. MASLD was defined using validated non-invasive indices: (1) NHANES: U.S. Fatty Liver Index (USFLI), excluding participants with viral hepatitis or alcohol overuse [19]. (2) CHARLS: TyG-WC index, excluding those with hepatitis or excess alcohol intake [17, 20].

MASLD was identified in individuals with hepatic steatosis (FLI ≥ 60) and at least one of five cardiometabolic risk factors, as defined by expert panel consensus: overweight or obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia and/or low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels [21].

The TyG-WC index was defined as:

TyG = ln (2Fasting Triglycerides (mg/dL) ×Fasting Glucose (mg/dL)​).

TyG-WC = TyG×Waist Circumference (cm).

According to previous validation studies in Chinese adults, MASLD was defined as: TyG-WC ≥ 750–800 in men, and TyG-WC ≥ 540–600 in women [16].

To harmonise classification across sex, we applied sex-specific cutoffs and coded MASLD as a binary variable (yes/no). Participants with viral hepatitis or excessive alcohol consumption were excluded to avoid misclassification.

Prospective mortality outcomes

Mortality status in NHANES was obtained through linkage with the National Death Index (NDI) and was available through December 31, 2019. Both all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality were identified. All-cause mortality: death from any cause. Cardiovascular mortality: deaths attributed to cardiovascular disease, defined using ICD-10 codes I00–I99 [22]. Time-to-event was calculated from the baseline examination date to the date of death or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Participants who remained alive at the final NDI linkage were censored at the corresponding date.

In CHARLS, mortality information was collected during 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 follow-up waves through structured household interviews and verification by family members or local mortality registries [23]. Only all-cause mortality could be reliably ascertained in CHARLS, as cause-specific mortality information was not consistently available and therefore was not used in the main analysis. All-cause mortality: defined as death from any cause reported in follow-up waves. Survival time was calculated from the 2011 baseline to the final available follow-up in 2020 or the date of death. Participants without a death report were censored at their last contact date.

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by both clinical knowledge and a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Fig. S2), which identified variables with potential confounding influence on the relationship between CTI and mortality. The DAG indicated that demographic and socioeconomic factors—including age, sex, race/ethnicity (NHANES), educational attainment, and household income—could simultaneously affect CTI levels and mortality risk, and were therefore treated as key confounders [24–28]. Lifestyle behaviours (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity) were also contained because of their established affiliations with metabolic health and long-term outcomes [29–31].

Several cardiometabolic conditions, including BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, were modelled as intermediate pathways or confounding factors depending on the specific analytic framework (e.g., mediation vs. multivariable adjustment), consistent with their positions in the DAG. Clinical laboratory markers such as ALT, AST, HDL-C, LDL-C, and HbA1c were incorporated to capture underlying metabolic and hepatic dysfunction. Additional comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular disease, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertension) were included as confounders based on their direct links to mortality in the DAG. Together, these covariates represent all minimally sufficient adjustment sets derived from the causal structure.

Statistical analysis

Cross-sectional analyses (NHANES & CHARLS)

Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for MASLD across CTI levels. Three models were fitted: Model 1: A crude model with no covariates adjusted. Model 2: Model 1 + age, gender, race. Model 3: Model 2 + education, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, Hypercholesterolaemia, smoking, alcohol, and physical activity.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) examined non-linear CTI–MASLD relationships. Subgroup analyses tested effect modification by age, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, and gender. Threshold effect analysis used two-piecewise logistic models.

Prospective analysis

In the prospective analysis, the relationships between CTI and mortality outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. For NHANES, both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality were evaluated, whereas for CHARLS, only all-cause mortality was available. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed, incorporating demographic, lifestyle, and metabolic covariates. Survey-weighted Cox models were used in NHANES analyses to take the intricate sample design into consideration.

For cardiovascular mortality in NHANES, competing risk analyses were additionally conducted using Fine–Grey subdistribution hazard models, treating non-CVD death as a competing event and assessing potential competing-suppression effects [32].

Subgroup analyses examined potential effect modification by age, sex, BMI, diabetes, and hypertension using multiplicative interaction terms. Finally, threshold effect analyses were performed using Cox regression piecewise to identify possible non-linear inflexion points in the relationship between CTI and mortality outcomes.

Mediation analyses

Causal mediation analyses were performed exclusively within the MASLD population of the NHANES and CHARLS prospective cohorts. These analyses quantified the extent to which BMI, hypertension, and diabetes mediated the associations between CTI and mortality, specifically all-cause mortality in both cohorts and cardiovascular mortality in NHANES only. For each mediator, indirect, direct, and total effects were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap iterations. Mediation proportions were calculated, and suppression effects were reported when observed. The mediation analyses were conducted under standard causal assumptions, including no unmeasured confounding of the exposure–mediator, mediator–outcome, and exposure–outcome relationships, as well as no time-varying confounding affected by prior exposure. Given the observational design and baseline assessment of variables, these assumptions cannot be formally tested and should be interpreted with caution.

Sensitivity analyses

To address potential concerns regarding the biological specificity of MASLD definitions based on surrogate indices, we conducted extensive sensitivity analyses using alternative steatosis-related definitions. Sensitivity analyses also incorporated alternative MASLD definitions, using HSI in NHANES and applying lower TyG-WC cutoffs as well as VAI-based criteria in CHARLS [9, 33, 34]. In addition, sensitivity studies relevant to each cohort were carried out to evaluate the consistency of results between the two datasets.

HSI was defined as:

HSI = 8 × (alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase ratio) + BMI (+ 2 if diabetes; +2 if female).

An HSI value > 36 was considered indicative of hepatic steatosis, whereas < 30 ruled out steatosis.

Software

Stata (version 18) and R (version 4.5.1) were used for all statistical analyses, with primary R packages including survey, survival, rms, cmprsk, segmented, and mediation. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Study population

Participant screening and selection procedures for NHANES and CHARLS are displayed in Fig. 1. A total of 8918 NHANES participants and 9419 CHARLS participants were included in the cross-sectional analyses, while 5130 NHANES and 5369 CHARLS participants were included in the prospective mortality analyses.

Cross-sectional analyses

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics for the cross-sectional analyses are presented in Table S1 (NHANES) and Table S2 (CHARLS), stratified by MASLD status. In both cohorts, participants with MASLD exhibited a substantially more adverse metabolic profile than those without MASLD, including higher BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, fasting glucose, and a greater prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. CTI values were consistently higher among participants with MASLD, supporting the hypothesis that inflammatory–metabolic dysregulation is associated with steatotic liver disease.

Association between CTI and MASLD

In NHANES, CTI showed a strong positive association with MASLD in unadjusted and adjusted models (Tables S3 and S4). In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest CTI quartile had significantly greater odds of MASLD compared with the lowest quartile (OR = 2.33, 95% 1.85–2.92).

Smooth curve fitting using generalised additive models (GAM) revealed a clear non-linear increase in MASLD probability with rising CTI levels (Fig. S3). The fitted curve showed a modest slope at lower CTI values, followed by a progressively steeper rise at higher CTI levels, indicating that MASLD risk accelerated as the metabolic–inflammatory burden increased.

CTI and MASLD showed a strong non-linear association, according to restricted cubic spline analysis (Fig. 2, A: NHANES curve). MASLD prevalence rose steadily with increasing CTI. Subgroup analyses (Fig. S4) showed consistent results across subgroups defined by age, sex, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes, with no significant interactions (Table S5). ROC analysis (Fig. S5, Table S2) confirmed that CTI improved discriminatory ability for identifying MASLD. Compared with models incorporating TyG alone, CTI demonstrated modest but consistent improvements in discrimination for MASLD.

In CHARLS, similar associations were observed using TyG-WC–defined MASLD (Tables S6 and S7). Higher CTI levels were associated with significantly increased MASLD odds, and spline curves (Fig. 2, B: CHARLS curve) showed a comparable non-linear pattern. Subgroup results were consistent (Fig. S6).

Threshold-effect analysis further demonstrated a significant non-linear pattern (Table S8). A clear inflexion point was identified at CTI = 10.52. Below this threshold, the association was extremely strong (OR = 8.31, 95% CI 6.88–10.04), whereas above the threshold the association remained significant but weaker (OR = 1.59, 95% CI 1.47–1.72). The likelihood ratio test was significant (P < 0.001), indicating that the two-piecewise model provided a superior fit compared with a single-line model.

Together, these results demonstrate a reproducible cross-sectional relationship between CTI and MASLD across the U.S. and Chinese populations.

As shown in Table 1, participants with higher CTI levels were more likely to be older and female and showed significant racial/ethnic variation across quartiles. There were also notable differences in socioeconomic factors between CTI groups, such as marital status, education level, and poverty-income ratio.

Clear cardiometabolic gradients were observed: higher CTI quartiles showed markedly increased frequency of obesity (BMI ≥ 30), hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia. Unhealthy lifestyle factors, including smoking and lower levels of vigorous physical activity, were more common in higher CTI groups. Together, these patterns indicate that elevated CTI corresponds to an accumulation of adverse metabolic and lifestyle risk factors at baseline.

Baseline characteristics for the CHARLS cohort are summarised in Table 2. Similar to NHANES, higher CTI quartiles were associated with a more unfavourable cardiometabolic profile. The prevalence of hypertension and diabetes increased sharply across quartiles, particularly diabetes, which rose from 4.47% in Q1 to 15.7% in Q4.

Higher CTI was also significantly associated with greater BMI categories, with the proportion of obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²) increasing from 21.9% in Q1 to over 51% in Q4. Total cholesterol levels showed a comparable upward trend. Demographic and lifestyle factors—such as age, smoking, alcohol consumption, education, and household income—showed modest but non-significant or weak gradients across CTI quartiles.

Overall, both NHANES and CHARLS cohorts demonstrated a consistent pattern: higher CTI quartiles were strongly linked to older age, poorer metabolic health, and a higher burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities, supporting CTI as a robust marker of systemic metabolic–inflammatory risk at baseline.

Threshold effect analysis

For NHANES and CHARLS, threshold effect studies employing two-piecewise Cox models showed different patterns (Tables 6 and 7). In NHANES, clear inflexion points were detected at CTI = 5.56 for all-cause mortality and CTI = 5.60 for CVD mortality. Below these thresholds, CTI showed no significant association with mortality (HR = 1.08, 95% CI 0.83–1.41, P = 0.560 for all-cause death; HR = 1.11, 95% CI 0.71–1.72, P = 0.643 for CVD mortality). However, once CTI exceeded the thresholds, the risk increased sharply, with hazard ratios rising to 2.31 (95% CI 1.66–3.22) for all-cause mortality and 2.76 (95% CI 1.55–4.91) for CVD mortality. The likelihood ratio test supported a significant threshold effect for all-cause mortality (P = 0.014), with a borderline result for CVD mortality (P = 0.070).

Mediation analysis

Mediation analyses (Fig. 7) showed that part of the association between CTI and mortality was transmitted through hypertension, diabetes, and BMI, although the magnitude varied across mediators and outcomes. For all-cause mortality in NHANES, hypertension accounted for 5.58% of the total effect (indirect effect = − 0.43, 95% CI − 0.95 to − 0.04, P = 0.040), while diabetes contributed a substantially larger proportion, mediating 25.92% of the association (indirect effect = − 2.41, 95% CI − 3.38 to − 1.61, P < 0.001). BMI did not demonstrate a significant mediating contribution (indirect effect = 0.12, P = 0.872), with the proportion mediated (–1.73%) indicating no meaningful indirect pathway. For cardiovascular mortality, mediation patterns were directionally similar: hypertension mediated 5.32% of the effect (indirect effect = − 1.36, P = 0.040), and diabetes again exerted a more prominent influence, explaining 13.64% of the association (indirect effect = − 3.73, P = 0.004). BMI accounted for 13.33% of the total effect, but its indirect path did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.212). Taken together, these findings suggest that diabetes is the dominant mediator linking CTI with excess mortality risk, hypertension provides a smaller yet significant contribution, and BMI does not play a meaningful mediating role in this context.

Sensitivity analyses

A sequence of comprehensive sensitivity analyses was performed on the NHANES and CHARLS datasets (Supplementary Tables S9–S19 and Supplementary Fig. S10–S24) to assess the robustness of the primary findings. In NHANES, results remained stable after excluding deaths occurring within the first two years of follow-up, removing cancer- or diabetes-related deaths, further adjusting for metabolic biomarkers, and employing alternative MASLD definitions (HSI and USFLI). In the CHARLS data, consistent results were observed in both cross-sectional and prospective analyses when using a lower TyG-WC threshold, applying VAI ≥ 2 (often indicative of fatty liver and insulin resistance), adjusting for expanded metabolic covariates, and stratifying by major metabolic comorbidities. Across all sensitivity analyses in both cohorts, the strength and direction of the association between CTI and outcomes showed minimal variation, strongly supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussion

In this large bi-national study integrating cross-sectional and prospective evidence from NHANES and CHARLS, we demonstrated that the composite triglyceride–inflammation index (CTI), integrating C-reactive protein, triglycerides, and fasting glucose, is robustly associated with both the presence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. These associations were consistent across two independent populations with distinct ethnic, socioeconomic, and healthcare backgrounds and remain robust after extensive adjustment and sensitivity analyses. Notably, diabetes accounted for a substantial proportion of the association between elevated CTI and mortality risk in the mediation analyses, suggesting that glycaemic dysfunction may represent an important contributing pathway in cardiometabolic outcomes.

CTI should be viewed as a complementary composite marker that integrates metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation, rather than as a replacement for established TyG-based indices. Its potential value lies in capturing inflammatory information that is not reflected by TyG alone. Higher CTI levels were strongly and nonlinearly associated with MASLD prevalence in both cohorts, supporting CTI as a marker of adverse metabolic–inflammatory status relevant to hepatic steatosis. CTI may serve as a potential risk marker reflecting integrated metabolic–inflammatory burden, which could be useful for population-level risk characterisation rather than for direct clinical decision-making. Among individuals with MASLD, elevated CTI was associated with substantially increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with clear dose–response relationships observed in both populations. Threshold analyses further revealed that mortality risk increased sharply beyond a critical CTI level in NHANES, whereas a more linear risk gradient was observed in CHARLS, suggesting population-specific patterns of metabolic risk accumulation. The observed differences in CTI threshold values between NHANES and CHARLS likely reflect population-specific distributions of CTI and its components, as well as differences in metabolic background, rather than methodological inconsistency. Therefore, CTI thresholds should be interpreted as cohort-specific inflexion points rather than universal cut-off values.

From a biological perspective, CTI reflects the convergence of several interrelated upstream drivers of cardiometabolic injury that act synergistically rather than independently. Chronic low-grade inflammation, reflected by elevated C-reactive protein, promotes endothelial dysfunction, vascular stiffness, and plaque instability through sustained activation of pro-inflammatory signalling pathways [35–37]. Hypertriglyceridaemia contributes to lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and ectopic lipid deposition, further impairing insulin signalling and vascular homeostasis [38–42]. Persistent hyperglycaemia amplifies these processes by enhancing oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and advanced glycation end-product formation, thereby accelerating both microvascular and macrovascular injury [43–47]. Through the integration of these components, CTI may capture a state of cardiometabolic convergence in which compensatory mechanisms are progressively overwhelmed.

Importantly, these metabolic and inflammatory pathways are tightly interconnected [48]. Inflammatory signalling exacerbates insulin resistance, while dysglycaemia and lipid abnormalities further fuel systemic inflammation, creating a self-reinforcing metabolic–inflammatory loop [49, 50]. CTI may therefore identify individuals in whom metabolic resilience has been lost, rendering them particularly vulnerable to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. This biological framework provides a plausible explanation for the strong and consistent associations observed between CTI and mortality across populations.

One notable observation from the mediation analyses was that diabetes accounted for a substantial proportion of the association between CTI and mortality. Diabetes may reflect a clinically manifest state associated with sustained metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation [51]. Chronic hyperglycaemia has been associated with enhanced inflammatory signalling, increased oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, which may contribute to both microvascular and macrovascular damage [52–54]. In addition, insulin resistance and glycaemic variability contribute to adverse lipid remodelling and pro-atherogenic states, providing a biologically plausible explanation for the strong associations observed between CTI and cardiovascular mortality [55, 56]. In contrast, hypertension mediated a smaller proportion of the association, while body mass index showed minimal indirect effects, reinforcing the concept that cardiometabolic risk is driven more by metabolic dysfunction and inflammation than by adiposity alone [57].

Although MASLD is defined by hepatic steatosis, it is increasingly recognised as a manifestation of systemic cardiometabolic dysfunction characterised by profound insulin resistance and chronic inflammation [58]. Our findings suggest that MASLD represents a high-risk cardiometabolic phenotype in which elevated CTI identifies individuals with particularly adverse metabolic–inflammatory profiles and heightened vulnerability to premature cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. In this context, MASLD may be viewed as a hepatic manifestation of a broader metabolic–inflammatory milieu rather than an isolated liver condition.

The identification of a threshold effect in NHANES suggests a transition from compensated metabolic dysfunction to overt metabolic decompensation once CTI exceeds a critical level. Such a threshold may reflect the loss of metabolic resilience, beyond which escalating inflammation and worsening glycaemic dysregulation rapidly translate into accelerated vascular injury and excess mortality risk. The more gradual association observed in CHARLS may reflect differences in baseline metabolic profiles, lifestyle factors, or healthcare access; nevertheless, both cohorts demonstrated a consistent overall relationship between higher CTI and increased mortality risk, underscoring the generalisability of CTI as a cardiometabolic risk marker.

From a clinical perspective, CTI is derived from routinely available laboratory parameters and does not require specialised testing or imaging, making it a pragmatic tool for cardiometabolic risk assessment. Elevated CTI may help identify individuals, particularly those with MASLD, who could benefit from earlier and more intensive interventions targeting glycaemic control, lipid management, and systemic inflammation, with the aim of mitigating long-term cardiovascular risk.

This study has several strengths, including the use of two large, nationally representative cohorts, long-term prospective follow-up, and comprehensive analytic strategies incorporating non-linear, threshold, and mediation analyses. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to the lack of direct hepatic imaging or histopathological measurements, MASLD in the CHARLS cohort was defined using noninvasive surrogate indices rather than gold-standard methods. Although this approach has been widely adopted in large population-based studies and supported by prior validation research, a certain degree of misclassification is inevitable. Nevertheless, the consistency of results obtained across different cohorts and multiple sensitivity analyses using alternative MASLD definitions suggests that the main findings of this study are robust and not driven by a single operational definition. Second, the observational nature of the study precludes strict causal inference. In addition, CTI was assessed only at baseline and therefore does not capture its potential dynamic changes over time. CTI was assessed only at baseline and therefore does not capture longitudinal changes in metabolic and inflammatory status during follow-up. This may introduce regression dilution bias, which would be expected to attenuate associations toward the null. Consequently, the observed associations may represent conservative estimates of the true long-term effects. Moreover, the CHARLS database lacks cause-specific mortality information, which limits direct comparisons of cardiovascular mortality outcomes across different cohorts.

Another consideration is the partial overlap between the components of CTI and the variables included in the operational definition of MASLD. This overlap reflects shared metabolic pathways linking systemic metabolic–inflammatory burden and hepatic steatosis, rather than a circular reasoning inherent to the study design. Notably, CTI includes C-reactive protein, an inflammatory marker not incorporated into the MASLD definition, and the primary mortality analyses were conducted within the MASLD population, with mortality outcomes themselves being independent of MASLD classification. Furthermore, the consistency observed across multiple sensitivity analyses further indicates that the findings are not solely driven by component overlap. Finally, although CTI demonstrated some incremental discriminative ability over TyG-based indices in risk stratification, the magnitude of improvement was modest. Therefore, CTI should be interpreted as an integrative marker of metabolic–inflammatory burden rather than as a standalone predictive tool that is substantially superior to existing indices. Future studies incorporating repeated CTI measurements are warranted to examine whether longitudinal trajectories or cumulative exposure provide additional prognostic information beyond a single baseline assessment.

Conclusions

In this bi-national prospective study, the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) was strongly associated with long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality across diverse populations. These correlations were especially strong in people with steatotic liver disease, a high-risk cardiometabolic phenotype linked to metabolic dysfunction. Diabetes emerged as the primary mediator linking elevated CTI to excess mortality risk, highlighting the central role of glycaemic dysfunction. As a readily obtainable marker, CTI may aid in cardiometabolic risk stratification and support earlier identification of individuals at increased cardiovascular risk. CTI represents a readily available composite marker of metabolic–inflammatory burden that is associated with MASLD and long-term mortality risk. These findings support its potential value as an epidemiological risk marker, while further validation is required before clinical application.
