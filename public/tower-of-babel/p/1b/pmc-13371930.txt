Efficient chemical reprogramming of human T cells into functional megakaryocytes and platelets
Authors: Jinhua Qin, Zheng Hua, Xingxing Zhao, Jianan Jiang, Jisheng Li, Sihan Wang, Yingwei Pan, Bowen Zhang, Yunqiao Li, Qianqian Zhou, Yiming Zhang, Yanzhou Chen, Yiming Liu, Caiping Xu, Tao Fan, Yang Lv, Xinlong Yan, Wen Yue, Xuetao Pei, Yanhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The generation of megakaryocytes (MKs) from human somatic cells through chemical reprogramming represents a promising strategy for developing alternative platelet sources. Building on our prior chemical reprogramming protocol for converting erythroblasts to MKs, we established a robust method that successfully generated induced MKs (iMKs) from human cord blood–derived CD3+ T cells, which is a more abundant source. This method used a five–small molecule cocktail containing a reprogramming booster, AZD4205, to promote erasure of T cell identity and facilitate fate transition toward MKs. T cell–derived iMKs exhibited characteristic MK cellular and molecular signatures, demonstrating the capacity to produce proplatelets and release functional platelets. Single-cell RNA sequencing further revealed that iMKs were heterogeneous with distinct functional profiles, including cycling, immune, and thrombopoiesis-biased MKs. Our findings highlight an optimized chemical reprogramming strategy that enables efficient conversion of T cells to MKs, providing a practical and convenient approach to generating clinically relevant MKs and platelets.

INTRODUCTION

Platelet transfusion is a crucial, life-saving treatment for individuals with severe thrombocytopenia. Currently, platelet preparations largely rely on whole blood donations from voluntary donors. The limited availability of healthy donors, the short shelf life of platelets, and their susceptibility to contamination collectively pose substantial constraints on platelet supply. Moreover, the need for repeated platelet transfusions in many patients further intensifies the existing supply-demand imbalance (1, 2). In recent years, the rapid advancements in stem cell research and regenerative medicine have introduced innovative approaches to addressing the clinical shortage of platelets (3–5). Specifically, the utilization of stem cell–induced differentiation and cell reprogramming technologies has enabled the in vitro generation of megakaryocytes (MKs) and platelets, offering a promising solution to meet the increasing demand for platelet products (6, 7).

Direct lineage reprogramming techniques can facilitate the generation of desired cell types suitable for cell therapy applications (8, 9). Multiple studies have demonstrated that the overexpression of specific transcription factors (TFs) can reprogram murine or human somatic cells into hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) (10–15), erythroid progenitors (16), and MKs (17, 18). However, TF-based reprogramming approaches requires genetic manipulation, which raises safety concerns and hampers their clinical application. Alternatively, chemical compounds with compelling advantages can completely replace TFs to convert somatic cells into desirable cells, including induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (19–21), HSPCs (22), endodermal progenitors (23), neurons (24–26), and cardiomyocytes (27). We previously reported that a specific combination of four small molecules [4M; Bix01294, RG108, PD0325901, and valproic acid (VPA)] drove the reprogramming of cord blood erythroblasts into MKs that produce platelets (28). This finding prompted us to explore whether T cells, which are more abundant and clinically accessible, could be chemically reprogrammed to acquire the MK cell fate. T cells represent an ideal starting material for the in vitro generation of MKs, constituting 40 to 70% of cord blood or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (29). Conversion of T cells to MKs by chemical reprogramming offers a promising approach to generating a practical source of seed cells for the safe and efficient production of platelets.

In this study, we observed that the initial 4M chemical cocktail, which successfully convert erythroblasts to MK-like cells, induced the chemical reprograming of CD3+ T cells into induced MKs (iMKs) with relatively low efficiency. We optimized the reprogramming protocol by high-throughput small-molecule screening and found that a Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) inhibitor AZD4205 could enhance the chemical reprogramming of T cells to iMKs. T cell–derived iMKs displayed typical megakaryocytic characteristics and functions. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) revealed heterogeneity of iMKs, identifying distinct iMK subpopulations, including cycling, immune, and thrombopoiesis-biased MKs. Pseudo-time analysis further delineated divergent developmental routes underlying this heterogeneity. Functionally, iMKs generated proplatelet structures in vitro that released platelet-like particles. In vivo transplantation experiment further confirmed that iMKs transfused to thrombocytopenic mice released functional human platelets into peripheral blood.

RESULTS

AZD4205 enhances chemical reprogramming of human T cells to iMKs

To investigate whether the 4M chemical reprogramming strategy could drive the conversion of human T cells into MKs, we enriched cord blood–derived CD3+ T cells and induced them with the established two-stage reprogramming protocol used for the generation of MKs from erythroblasts (28) (fig. S1A). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that by the end of stage 1 on day 7, the percentages of CD41a+ cells and CD41a+CD42b+ cells were 8.29% ± 2.74% and 0.81% ± 0.12%, respectively. By the end of stage 2 on day 14, these proportions rose significantly to 39.95% ± 5.79% for CD41a+ cells and 9.68% ± 1.77% for CD41a+CD42b+ cells (fig. S1B). In addition, the mRNA expression levels of key MK TFs and markers, including GATA1, GATA2, HOXC6, MEIS1, TAL1, ITGB3, and ITGA2B, were significantly up-regulated in iMKs on days 7 and 14 compared to the initial T cells (fig. S1C). These results indicated that the 4M reprogramming system induced the conversion of CD3+ T cells into iMKs with relatively low efficiency.

To enhance the efficiency of T cell–to–MK conversion, we used the high-content imaging screening platform to screen small-molecule compound libraries. Cord blood–derived CD3+ T cells were initially cultured in medium containing 4M for 7 days and then treated with chemicals from a signaling pathway regulatory small-molecule library, which contains 320 small-molecule compounds (table S1). After 48 hours of treatment, cells were analyzed for CD41a+CD42b+ expression using a high-content imaging analysis system (fig. S2A). T cells derived from cord blood of two donors were used for small-molecule screening. Among the top 20 small molecules identified from each donor based on their fold increase in CD41a+CD42b+ cell percentage, LY3214996, Y-33075, and Crenigacestat consistently demonstrated a promoting effect in both batches (fig. S2B). Next, a total of 21 candidate small molecules were selected for further evaluation via flow cytometry. These candidates were either among the top 10 compounds identified in fig. S2B or demonstrated an enhanced CD41a+CD42b+ cell percentage in both screening batches. The results indicated that AZD4205, ERK-IN-2, and Crenigacestat were the top three compounds in terms of increasing the percentage of CD41a+CD42b+ cells (fig. S2C). Specifically, AZD4205 notably increased both the percentage and the number of CD41a+CD42b+ cells. Treatment with ERK-IN-2, Crenigacestat, or their combinations led to an increase in the percentage of CD41a+CD42b+ cells but a decrease in the total cell number (fig. S2, D and E).

We then optimized the protocol for T cell–to–MK conversion by adding AZD4205 to the basic 4M cocktail and compared the iMKs generated with or without AZD4205 (4M + AZD or 4M) (Fig. 1A). Morphologically, iMKs induced by either 4M + AZD or 4M exhibited obviously larger cell diameters compared to the initial T cells. Although the cell size did not differ significantly between the two iMK groups, the number of proplatelets was substantially higher in the 4M + AZD group than that in the 4M group (Fig. 1, B to D). Flow cytometry data showed that the percentage and absolute number of CD41a+ cells, as well as CD41a+CD42b+ cells, were significantly increased in the 4M + AZD group compared to the 4M group (Fig. 1, E and F). By day 14, the 4M + AZD group exhibited 68.13% ± 0.75% CD41a+ cells and 42.6% ± 1.08% CD41a+CD42b+ cells (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, we compared AZD4205 with two other JAK1 inhibitors (PF-04965842 and JAK1-IN-13); each was combined with the 4M cocktail. The results demonstrated that PF-04965842 enhanced both CD41a+ and CD41a+CD42b+ cell populations, similar to AZD4205, whereas JAK1-IN-13 only increased the percentage of CD41a+ cells (fig. S3), suggesting that AZD4205 is a more effective candidate for the reprogramming system. Furthermore, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis demonstrated that AZD4205 significantly up-regulated the expression of MK-related TF genes (e.g., RUNX1, MEIS1, FLI1, GATA1, and GATA2) and MK-specific marker genes (e.g., ITGA2B and ITGB3), it down-regulated the expression of T cell–specific genes, including CD3E, IL7R, RUNX3, BCL11B, and GATA3 (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these findings indicate that AZD4205 enhances the conversion of T cells to MKs.

AZD4205 drives T cell–to–MK conversion by suppressing JAK/signal transducers and activators of transcription and activating WNT signaling

AZD4205, a highly selective JAK1 inhibitor, was reported to suppress the proliferation and survival of T cell lymphoma by blocking JAK/signal transducers and activators of transcription (STAT) signaling pathway (30). Following treatment with AZD4205, we observed a significant decrease in phosphorylated STAT3 levels in cord blood T cells (fig. S4), confirming effective suppression of JAK/STAT signaling. To further elucidate the role of AZD4205 in the conversion of T cells to iMKs, we performed a transcriptomic analysis of T cells treated with or without AZD4205 for 7 days. Bulk RNA-seq results revealed that in the AZD4205-treated groups, several T cell–related TF genes (e.g., ETV7 and BATF3), cytokine genes (e.g., IL13, IL21, IL22, and CSF2), and chemokine genes (e.g., CXCL8, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11) related to T cell development and activation were considerably down-regulated (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that the down-regulated genes in AZD4205-treated cells were enriched in biological processes such as immune system process, immune response, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB)–inducing kinase (NIK)/NF-κB signaling pathway, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)–mediated signaling pathway, and JAK/STAT cascade (Fig. 2B). Genes up-regulated in AZD4205-treated cells were enriched in biological processes related to WNT signaling pathway and β-catenin–T cell factor (TCF) complex assembly (Fig. 2B). A substantial number of genes associated with WNT signaling pathway were up-regulated in response to AZD4205 treatment (Fig. 2, C and D). It has been well documented that the activation of the WNT signaling pathway is crucial for cell fate decisions that promote MK development (31). Moreover, we found that the WNT/β-catenin signaling inhibitors XAV939 and IWR-1 effectively counteracted the enhancement of MK reprogramming efficiency mediated by AZD4205 (Fig. 2E). Treatment with either XAX-939 or IWR-1 led to notable down-regulation of key MK development–related transcriptional factor genes (RUNX1, GATA2, MEIS1, and FLI1) and surface marker genes (ITGA2B and ITGB3) (Fig. 2F). These results suggested that AZD4205 promoted T cell–to–MK conversion by inhibiting T cell proliferation and survival through suppression of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, while simultaneously facilitating the fate transition toward MKs via the WNT signaling pathway.

T cell–derived iMKs display typical MK characteristics

We subsequently assessed the characteristics of iMKs derived from T cells following two-phase induction (Fig. 3A). Giemsa staining of iMKs on day 14 showed the classic morphology of MKs, characterized by large, polylobulated nuclei and abundant cytoplasm with granular accumulation (Fig. 3B). Transmission electron microscopy analysis of iMKs unveiled the characteristic ultrastructure of MKs, including lobulated nuclei, a developing demarcation membrane system, cytoplasmic multivesicular bodies, and mature granules (Fig. 3C). Flow cytometry analysis of the nuclear content in iMKs revealed that ∼14% of CD41a+CD42b+ iMKs exhibited a 4-N or >4-N DNA content (Fig. 3D). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that iMKs robustly expressed the MK-specific surface markers CD41 and CD42b, as well as the characteristic intracellular proteins platelet factor 4 (PF4) and thrombospondin 1 (THBS1) (Fig. 3E).

Transcriptome analysis was performed on T cells at day 0 and reprogrammed cells at days 7 and 14 to evaluate global gene expression changes during the reprogramming process. Notably, TF genes essential for megakaryopoiesis, including GATA1, GATA2, LYL1, TAL1, NFE2, and PLEK, were up-regulated in cells on days 7 and 14. In contrast, TF genes associated with T cell development, such as GATA3, BACH2, RORA, BCL11B, and TCF7, were down-regulated. Consistent trends were also observed in the expression of surface markers characteristic of MKs or T cells, respectively (Fig. 3, F to H). The expression of genes associated with platelet function, including DMTN, PF4, CLEC1B, and THBS1, was also elevated in cells on days 7 and 14 (Fig. 3I). GO analysis showed that the up-regulated genes in iMKs on day 14 were enriched in biological processes such as platelet degranulation, while down-regulated genes in iMKs were predominantly associated with T cell activation (fig. S5, A and B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated enrichment of the “platelet degranulation” pathway in T cell derived–iMKs, while the “T cell selection” pathway was enriched in T cells (Fig. 3J and fig. S5C). Furthermore, the iMKs up-regulated CD62P in response to thrombin, indicating their activation and subsequent release of α-granules (Fig. 3K). Together, iMKs derived from T cells had typical MK characteristics.

scRNA-seq analyses reveals MK fate commitment dynamics and transcriptional heterogeneity within iMKs

To further understand the chemical reprogramming process of T cells to iMKs, we performed scRNA-seq analysis of the initial CD3+ T cells (day 0) and reprogrammed cells on days 7 and 14 using the 10x Genomics platform. We captured a total of 35,328 individual cells and identified four transcriptionally distinct clusters labeled as C1 to C4 based on unsupervised clustering by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis (Fig. 4A and table S2). The initially isolated CD3+ T cells from day 0 could be divided into two clusters, C1 and C2 (Fig. 4B). C1 was defined by markers including TF genes (e.g., TCF7, BCL11B, LEF1, and BACH2) and genes involved in T cell identity and homeostasis (e.g., CAMK4, LTB, TRBC2, TRAC, CD247, and IL7R). In contrast, C2 showed high expression of inflammatory mediators, including IL1B, CCL3, CCL4, CCL3L1, CXCL8, CXCL5, CXCL3, and CXCL1 (Fig. 4, C and D). Scoring results using the TCellSI (32) tool also showed that C1 mainly comprised quiescent, proliferating, regulating, and progenitor-exhausted T cells, while C2 primarily contained cytotoxic and terminally exhausted T cells (fig. S6A). C3 was characterized by high expression of ELANE, PRTN3, CEBPA, and MPO, which were highly expressed in myeloid cells. In addition, C3 exhibited high expression of TF genes critical for erythroid and megakaryocytic development, such as MYB and FLI1(Fig. 4, B to D). Most cells in C3 were in the G2M or S phase, with elevated expression of proliferation-associated genes including PCNA, MKI67, and CDK4, indicating an actively proliferating state (Fig. 4, E and F, and fig. S6B). Genes up-regulated in C3 were predominantly involved in myeloid development and functions, such as “myeloid leukocyte mediated immunity” and “myeloid cell activation involved in immune response” (Fig. 4, G to I, and fig. S6C). These findings suggested that C3 represented an intermediate myeloid progenitor state, thus defined as induced common myeloid progenitors (iCMPs). C4 was a cell cluster that emerged on day 7 and expanded on day 14, which displayed robust expression of various canonical MK-featured genes, including MEIS1, GATA2, ITGA2B, GP1BA, PF4, THBS1, TUBB1, GP9, and F2R, hereafter defined as iMKs (Fig. 4, C and D, and fig. S6D). Genes up-regulated in iMKs were significantly enriched in pathways related to MK development and platelet function, such as “MK differentiation,” “blood coagulation,” “platelet activation,” and “hemostasis” (Fig. 4, G to I, and fig. S6C).

As demonstrated in the UMAP projection of C4, pronounced heterogeneity existed within the iMK population (Fig. 4, A and D). To further dissect this heterogeneity, we therefore performed unsupervised reclustering of 7515 iMKs isolated from C4, identifying four transcriptionally distinct subpopulations designated iMK1 to iMK4 (Fig. 5A and table S3). Cells in iMK1 were designated as cycling MKs based on their predominance in G2M-S phase and elevated expression of genes associated with cell proliferation and mitosis, such as PCNA, MKI67, CDK1, and CCNB1 (Fig. 5, B to E). GSEA further revealed enrichment of the “DNA replication” in iMK1 (Fig. 5F). 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine incorporation assay confirmed a proliferative subpopulation within iMKs (fig. S7). Consistent with these observations, GO analysis demonstrated that iMK1 were enriched for gene sets associated with “chromosome segregation,” “nuclear division,” and “organelle fission” (Fig. 5G). iMK1 constituted the majority of the MK population on day 7 (fig. S8A). Notably, HPGD and HPGDS, encoding enzyme genes critical for prostaglandin production and implicated in regulating inflammatory and immune responses, were among the top 10 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) enriched in either iMK2 or iMK3 (Fig. 5B and fig. S8B). Additional immune-associated genes, including ANXA1, IL1RL1, IL18R1, and IFITM2, also exhibited higher expression in iMK2 and iMK3. The cell surface markers CD44 and CD53, which were involved in leukocyte adhesion and immune cell activation, were also enriched in iMK2 and iMK3 (Fig. 5H and fig. S8B). In particular, CD53 was previously identified as a marker for inflammatory response–associated MKs (33). In addition, iMK2 and iMK3 both showed enrichment of GO terms related to regulation of immune response (Fig. 5G). GSEA also revealed enrichment of genes associated with “inflammatory response” in iMK2 and enrichment of genes associated with “regulation of immune response” in iMK3 (Fig. 5I). On the basis of these molecular signatures, iMK2 and iMK3 were classified as immune MKs. Despite their similarities, 385 DEGs were identified between iMK2 and iMK3, among which genes up-regulated in MK2 were enriched in regulation of B cell proliferation (fig. S8D and table S4).

Distinguished from the other iMK subpopulations, the top 10 DEGs characteristic of iMK4 included key thrombopoiesis-associated genes, such as PPBP, PF4, THBS1, CLEC1B, TUBB1, CD9, and GP9 (Fig. 5, B and H, and fig. S8C). The enriched GO terms in iMK4 included “wound healing,” blood coagulation, and “platelet alpha granule,” while GSEA further highlighted enrichment of genes associated with platelet activation. Collectively, these molecular signatures confirm that iMK4 exhibited a strong bias toward thrombopoiesis, prompting its designation as thrombopoiesis-biased MKs. To validate this conclusion, we compared our data to published scRNA-seq data derived from natural MKs, which identified the MK heterogeneity (34). Correlation analysis revealed that iMK1 closely resembles cycling MKs, while iMK4 showed strong concordance with thrombopoiesis-biased MKs, further confirming our findings (fig. S8E).

In summary, scRNA-seq analysis revealed the dynamic transcriptional trajectory from human T cells to iMKs, with unexpected emergence of iCMPs during the reprogramming process. We further uncovered heterogeneity within the iMK population, and subclustering identified distinct iMK subpopulations, including cycling, immune, and thrombopoiesis-biased MKs. These subpopulations exhibited distinct molecular signatures and functional enrichments, aligning with the heterogeneity observed in natural MKs and collectively defining the specialized cellular identities arising during T cell reprogramming to iMKs.

Pseudo-time trajectory analysis reveals distinct transcriptional dynamics in iMK reprogramming

To understand the basis for iMK heterogeneity, we analyzed the reprogramming trajectory of reprogramming from T cells to iMKs in a pseudo-time manner. Trajectory analysis of initial T cells, iCMPs, and four iMK subpopulations revealed a major bifurcation in developmental pathways. Specifically, the iMK4 subpopulation was predominantly localized along the “direction 1” branch, whereas iCMPs, iMK2, and iMK3 were mainly concentrated along the “direction 2” branch (Fig. 6A). These findings suggest that the subpopulations of iMKs derived from T cells may arise through divergent developmental routes.

We next characterized gene expression patterns and potential functions along the two developmental routes. Using Monocle 2–based branched expression analysis modeling (BEAM) and hierarchical clustering, we identified three distinct gene expression modules (modules 1 to 3; Fig. 6B and table S5). Module 1 was enriched for GO terms associated with platelet activation, blood coagulation, and “megakaryocyte development,” including thrombopoiesis-related genes such as GP1BA, SELP, F2R, and PF4, whose expression considerably increased along direction 1. Pan-MK marker ITGA2B and TF MEIS1 also exhibited consistent up-regulation along both trajectories (Fig. 6, B to D). In contrast, module 2 genes, represented by genes critical for T cell development and function, such as BACH2, BCL11A, FOXP1, LEF1, RUNX3, and ZFP36L2, showed progressive down-regulation along both directions (Fig. 6, B to D), reflecting the loss of T cell identity during reprogramming. Module 3 genes were enriched for GO terms including nuclear division, “myeloid cell differentiation,” and “myeloid leukocyte cytokine production.” Notably, myeloid-related genes ELANE, MPO, and PRTN3 with higher expression in iCMPs exhibited transient up-regulation, followed by down-regulation along direction2 (Fig. 6, B to D), indicating the existence of a special intermediate state of the reprogrammed cells. Moreover, proliferation-related gene PCNA increased along direction 2 but decreased along direction 1, with higher expression in iMK1 subpopulation, while immune-related genes HPGDS and IL18R1 showed notably up-regulation along direction 2, enriched in iMK2 and iMK3 (Fig. 6D). These findings highlight the distinct transcriptional dynamics shaping functional heterogeneity of reprogrammed MKs.

The TF networks further elucidated the potentially distinct functional associations of the three modules (Fig. 6E and table S6). Module 1 exhibited expression of TFs essential for thrombopoiesis, such as FLI1, MEIS1, PLEK, GATA2, and NFE2. By contrast, TFs associated with T cell development, including BACH2, LEF1, and FOXP1, were enriched in module 2. Cell cycle–associated factors DACH1 (35), CENPA (36), and WDHD (37), myeloid-related TFs NFIA (38) and HMGB2 (39), and hematopoiesis-regulating TFs ERG and MYB were enriched in module 3. Together, these results dissected the sequential molecular progression that led to the successful reprogramming of T cells to MKs and revealed an intermediate state of iCMPs during the reprogramming pathway.

T cell–derived iMKs produced proplatelets and platelets in vitro

Next, we examined the capacity of iMKs to produce proplatelets and platelets in vitro. On day 14, the iMKs were cultured in a MK maturation medium for an additional period of 7 days. Our observations indicated that a subset of iMKs displayed a morphology characteristic of proplatelets, which included multiple protrusions and elongated, branching extensions that resembled a string of beads interconnected by slender cytoplasmic bridges (Fig. 7, A and B). The differentiated cells were found to express markers such as CD41, CD42b, and von Willebrand factor (vWF), thereby confirming their maturation into proplatelets. Furthermore, phalloidin staining demonstrated the presence of actin filament networks within the differentiated proplatelets, which are essential for the development and branching of proplatelet extensions (Fig. 7B).

To further determine whether these proplatelets could produce platelets, we collected the supernatants from the cell cultures and subjected them to staining with surface markers specific for platelet identification. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that iMK-derived platelets (iMK-PLTs) expressed high levels of platelet-specific proteins, including CD41, CD42b, THBS1, PF4, vWF, and α-tubulin (Fig. 7C). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated the presence of viable platelets, characterized by calcein-positive staining, which expressed the markers CD41a and CD42b (Fig. 7D). One iMK can produce ∼17.56 ± 1.92 platelet-like particles (fig. S9A). Electron microscopic analysis revealed that iMK-PLTs exhibited the characteristic morphology of platelets, including an open canalicular system, and alpha granules (Fig. 7E). To investigate the functionality of iMK-PLTs, we measured the expression levels of the platelet activation marker CD62P following stimulation with platelet agonists. Upon stimulation with thrombin or adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP)/PAR-1 agonist peptide (TRAP), iMK-PLTs exhibited increases in CD62P expression intensity (Fig. 7F). iMK-PLTs exhibited the capacity to form platelet plugs and facilitate the formation and retraction of fibrin clots (Fig. 7G), thereby indicating that platelets produced from iMKs in vitro had potential coagulation function. These findings imply that iMKs derived from CD3+ T cells through a two-phase induction protocol are capable of differentiating into proplatelets and producing functional platelets in vitro.

In vivo platelet production of iMKs in thrombocytopenic mice

To evaluate platelet release capacity and tissue distribution of iMKs in vivo, we transfused 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine, 4-chlorobenzenesulfonate salt (DiD)-labeled iMKs into 2.5-Gy irradiated immunodeficient mice (Fig. 8A). The tissue distribution of infused iMKs in mice was evaluated using live fluorescent microscopy. Before transplantation, cells were labeled with the DiD fluorescent dye. Initially, most of infused cells were trapped in the murine lung, resulting in strong fluorescent signals. After 3 hours posttransplantation, some cells began to migrate to other organs (Fig. 8B). Twenty-four hours later, iMKs were detected in multiple organs, including the lung, spleen, liver, and bone marrow (Fig. 8C). No fluorescence signals could be detected in the control group [administered with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)]. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed the presence of transfused iMKs in the lung, bone marrow, and spleen, with the highest chimerism observed in the lung (Fig. 8D).

Human platelets released by infused iMKs were detectable in mouse peripheral blood within 24 hours posttransfusion. In the initial hour, human CD41a+ platelets appeared at a low level of 0.48 ± 0.04%. Subsequently, the level of human platelets increased rapidly, reaching a peak at 3 hours (2.32 ± 0.38%). The percentage of human platelets then gradually declined to below 1% after 9 hours (Fig. 8E). Similar trends were observed in the number of human platelets in mouse peripheral blood (Fig. 8F). The responsiveness of released human platelets to agonists was examined to evaluate their functional activity. Following stimulation with ADP, platelet activation was assessed through the expression of the CD62P (P-selectin) surface antigen. Before stimulation, these platelets exhibited a relatively quiescent state, characterized by low mean fluorescence intensity of hCD62P expression. Upon incubation with ADP, the mean fluorescence intensity of hCD62P increased significantly (Fig. 8G and fig. S9B). These findings suggest that human platelets released by iMKs in a murine model retain the capacity for activation.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we established a robust chemical reprogramming strategy that efficiently converted human cord blood–derived CD3+ T cells into functional MKs and platelets. T cell–derived iMKs exhibited typical megakaryocytic features, including characteristic morphology, ultrastructure, MK-specific marker expression, and transcriptional profiles. scRNA-seq revealed a reprogramming trajectory involving an unexpected iCMP intermediate and uncovered heterogeneous iMK subpopulations such as cycling, immune, and thrombopoiesis-biased MKs. Functionally, iMKs were capable of producing proplatelets in vitro, which released platelet-like particles. Critically, in vivo transplantation experiments confirmed that iMKs administered to thrombocytopenic mice released functional human platelets into peripheral circulation.

The selection of the initial cell type is critical for establishing efficient direct reprogramming strategies to produce MKs. Different starting cells can profoundly influence the efficiency, fidelity, and scalability of the reprogramming process. For instance, some studies have shown that certain cell types, such as preadipocytes or adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (40), are amenable to convert into MKs. In our previous work, we developed a direct reprogramming method for converting erythroblasts into MKs, capitalizing on the fact that erythroblasts and MKs are developmentally related and share a common progenitor cell (28). T cells, however, represent a more attractive starting material for direct reprogramming due to their abundance and accessibility, prompting us to explore a reprogramming protocol to convert T cells into iMKs that can produce platelets. On the basis of the 4M chemical reprogramming protocol used for erythroblast-to-MK conversion, we performed high-content screening and identified the JAK1 inhibitor AZD4205 as a potent enhancer for T cell–to–iMK reprogramming. When combined with the 4M cocktail, AZD4205 significantly increased the yield of iMKs, up-regulated MK-specific TFs and marker genes, and simultaneously silenced T cell identity genes, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency and specificity of the reprogramming process. The reprogramming toward the MK lineage is a stable and irreversible process (fig. S10).

AZD4205 is a selective inhibitor of JAK1, and it has been reported that JAK1 inhibition impaired T cells function and suppressed the proliferation and survival of T cell tumors (30, 41). Our findings indicate that AZD4205 down-regulated multiple TF genes related to T cell development and activation (Fig. 1G). Biological processes such as immune system process, immune response, NIK/NF-κB signaling pathway, and TNF-mediated signaling pathway were significantly enriched in genes that were down-regulated in AZD4205-treated cells (Fig. 2, B and C), indicating that AZD4205 promoted the erasure of T cell identity. This pharmacological silencing aligns with established reprogramming strategies that utilize transforming growth factor–β inhibitors, such as RepSox (616452), A83-01, and SB431542, to facilitate lineage conversion by eroding the identity of fibroblasts during the generation of iPSCs (19), neuronal cells (26), or cardiomyocytes (42). A recent study developed a method to reprogram human erythroid progenitor cells (EPCs) into iPSCs using small molecules that efficiently erase EPC identity (43). On the other hand, the WNT signaling pathway was highly enriched in up-regulated genes in response to AZD4205 treatment. Critically, the WNT inhibitors XAV939 and IWR-1 abolished AZD4205-mediated enhancement of MK reprogramming efficiency (Fig. 2, E and F). This is consistent with extensive evidence that highlights the critical role of WNT signaling pathway in MK development (31, 44, 45). Collectively, these findings suggested that AZD4205 enhanced the chemical reprogramming of T cells into MKs by suppressing T cell identity through the inhibition of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, while simultaneously promoting the acquisition of a MK fate via activation of the WNT signaling pathway. Notably, we found that expanded T cells reprogrammed into iMKs with lower efficiency (fig. S11), likely because the expansion process decreased stem-like characteristics and cellular plasticity (46, 47). In future work, we will optimize the expansion conditions to preserve naïve/stem-like T cell phenotypes and thereby improve reprogramming efficiency.

Advances in MK research have elucidated that natural MKs can be transcriptionally categorized into platelet-generating, niche-supporting, immune, and cycling MKs, which are distinguished by their specific gene expression patterns and cellular markers (33, 34, 48, 49). In this study, we performed scRNA-seq analysis and observed heterogeneity within the T cell–derived iMKs. On the basis of distinct molecular signatures and functional enrichments, these iMKs were classified into three subsets: cycling (iMK1), immune (iMK2/iMK3), and thrombopoiesis-biased (iMK4) MKs. Cycling MK (iMK1) constitutes the majority of the MK population on day 7, but its proportion decreased by day 14 (fig. S8A). Conversely, thrombopoiesis-biased MKs (iMK4) increased remarkably at day 14 compared to day 7 (fig. S8A), indicating that our reprogramming system is beneficial for generating thrombopoiesis-biased MKs.

Furthermore, an unexpected iCMP population emerged during the T cell–to–MK reprogramming process, which exhibited high expression of TFs critical for myeloid cell differentiation, including ELANE, PRTN3, CEBPA, and MPO (Fig. 4, C and D). Moreover, the iCMPs also showed elevated expression of MYB and FLI1 (Fig. 4C), which are key regulators of erythroid and megakaryocytic development. This coexpression pattern suggests a transitional multipotent state capable of differentiation along multiple hematopoietic lineages. This phenomenon parallels our earlier observation of induced precursors for erythrocytes and MKs during erythroblast-to-MK reprogramming (28). Both observations demonstrate that reprogramming cascades can recapitulate native hematopoietic processes, generating intermediate progenitor states mirroring physiological developmental hierarchies.

In conclusion, we have established an efficient chemical reprogramming protocol to generate human MKs and platelets from cord blood T cells. T cell–derived iMKs exhibited hallmark cellular and genetic characteristics of authentic MKs, including the heterogeneity among iMKs, with distinct functional profiles such as cycling, immune-responsive, and thrombopoiesis-biased MKs. Notably, iMKs demonstrated the capacity to form proplatelets and release functional platelets both in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, chemical reprogramming of T cells to iMKs offers a promising strategy for the on-demand production of functional MKs and platelets, with the potential to alleviate transfusion dependence, minimize hemorrhagic complications, and improve outcomes for patients with refractory thrombocytopenia. Nevertheless, a limitation of the current study is that, although our chemical reprogramming approach achieves a high efficiency of cell fate conversion (∼1 iMK per input T cell), the subsequent efficiency of platelet release (∼17.56 ± 1.92 platelets per iMK) remains relatively low. This represents a recognized technical challenge in the in vitro manufacturing of platelets. Therefore, further optimization of platelet generation conditions and development of advanced bioreactor systems are urgently required to enhance platelet yield and facilitate future translational applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

NVSG [NOD-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm201(−/−)/V] mice were purchased from Beijing Viewsolid Biotech Co. Ltd. All mice were kept under specific pathogen–free conditions, and all procedures were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-DWZX-2023-P704).

Human individuals

Human cord blood samples were obtained from Beijing Cord Blood Bank. Women with healthy, full-term pregnancies who met the Beijing Cord Blood Bank criteria for inclusion and gave informed consent were eligible to donate cord blood. Sample IDs and the corresponding experiments performed were listed in table S7. All procedures involving human individuals in this study were approved by Ethics Committee of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (approval no. AF/SC-08/02.177).

Purification of human cord blood–derived T cells

Human cord blood mononuclear cells were separated by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation (1.077 g/liter; TBD Science, Tianjin, China). CD3+ T cells were isolated using the EasySep Human CD3 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). Either freshly isolated or cryopreserved T cells were used for reprogramming.

High-throughput screening

CD3+ T cells were cultured in StemSpan SFEM II medium supplemented with 0.5 μM Bix01294 (Selleck, Houston, TX, USA), 0.04 μM RG108 (Selleck), 0.5 μM PD0325901 (Selleck), 0.2 mM VPA (Selleck), stem cell factor (SCF; 50 ng/ml; Peprotech), thrombopoietin (TPO; 50 ng/ml; Peprotech), interleukin-3 (IL-3; 20 ng/ml; Peprotech), and IL-6 (50 ng/ml; Peprotech) and aliquoted into 96-well plates (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The compounds were added immediately after plating. T cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 72 hours and analyzed using Opera Phenix High-Content Screening System and Harmony High-Content Imaging and Analysis software (Revvity, Waltham, MA, USA). The compound library composed of 320 chemical compounds was purchased from Center of Pharmaceutical Technology at Tsinghua University (table S1).

Conversion of T cells to iMKs

T cells were cultured in StemSpan SFEM II medium supplemented with 0.5 μM Bix01294 (Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA), 0.04 μM RG108 (Selleckchem), 0.5 μM PD0325901 (Selleckchem), 0.2 mM VPA (Targetmol), 1 μM AZD4205 (Selleck), SCF (50 ng/ml), TPO (50 ng/ml; Peprotech), IL-3 (20 ng/ml; Peprotech), and IL-6 (50 ng/ml; Peprotech). After 7 days, the cells were transferred to StemSpan SFEM II medium supplemented with SCF (50 ng/ml), TPO (50 ng/ml), IL-3 (20 ng/ml), and IL-6 (50 ng/ml) for 7 days. The medium was refreshed every 2 to 3 days.

Proplatelet formation and platelet production

The iMKs were cultivated on human fibrinogen (50 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich)–coated plates in StemSpan SFEM II medium supplemented with SCF (20 ng/ml) and TPO (50 ng/ml), the plates were placed on a Digital Mini Rotator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 95 rpm (28).

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were stained with cell surface antigens in PBS at 4°C for 40 min. The stained cells were analyzed using BD FACSAria (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) or Guava easyCyte 12HT flow cytometer (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software (TreeStar, Ashland, OR, USA) (28).

Morphologic analysis

Cells were rinsed in PBS, cytocentrifuged onto glass slides, stained with Wright-Giemsa solution (Baso), and then observed by light microscopy (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). NIS-Elements F software (Nikon) was used to capture the images (28).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cultured cells were cytocentrifuged onto glass slides, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, rinsed with PBS, blocked with 10% donkey serum in PBS for 1 hour, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Labeled isotype–specific secondary antibodies were added when necessary and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. The cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for visualization of cell nuclei and observed using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and Zeiss ZEN microscope software (Zeiss) (28). The antibodies are listed in table S8.

Quantitative real-time PCR analyses

Total RNA was isolated using an RNeasy extraction kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). RNA was reverse transcribed using high-efficient reverse transcriptase ReverTra Ace (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green real-time PCR master mix (TOYOBO) on a Bio-Rad iQ5 Real-Time PCR detection system (Hercules, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method (28). The primers are listed in table S9.

Transmission electron microscopy

The cells or platelets were rinsed with PBS and fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde/0.1 M sodium cacodylate (pH 7.4). Following three rinses with sodium cacodylate buffer, the samples were postfixed for 1 hour in 1% osmium tetroxide/0.1 sodium cacodylate buffer. After rinsing in deionized water, the samples were dehydrated and embedded in Polybed 812 epoxy resin (Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA, USA). The samples were sectioned perpendicularly to the substrate at a thickness of 70 nm using a diamond knife. Ultrathin sections were collected on 200-mesh copper grids and stained with 4% aqueous uranyl acetate for 15 min, followed by staining with Reynolds’ lead citrate for 7 min (28). The samples and stained sections were observed using an HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 80 kV and photographed using Hitachi TEM system control (Hitachi).

Ploidy analysis

Cells were immunostained for determining CD41a and CD42b expression and then incubated with Hoechst 33342 (10 μg/ml; BD Biosciences) for 30 min before flow cytometry (28).

Preparation of human peripheral blood–PLTs and iMK-PLTs

Human platelet-rich plasma was prepared by centrifugation of sodium citrate–treated human peripheral blood at 120g for 20 min. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 400g for 20 min in the presence of 1 μM Prostaglandin E1 (Selleckchem) and Apyrase (1 U/ml; Sigma-Aldrich). The pellet was washed and resuspended in Hepes–Tyrode’s buffer containing 1 μM PGE1 and Apyrase (1 U/ml). For iMK-PLTs, the culture media containing iMK-PLTs were gently collected with 1 μM PGE1 and Apyrase (1 U/ml) to prevent platelet activation. The iMK-PLTs were enriched and washed, as described above (28).

Flow cytometry analysis of iMK and platelet activation

iMKs were stained with CD41a-BV421, CD42b–phycoerythrin (PE), and CD62P–allophycocyanin (APC) antibodies, stimulated with thrombin (2 U/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at 37°C, and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry. iMK-PLTs were stained with CD41a-BV421, CD42b-APC, and CD62P-PE antibodies, stimulated with thrombin (2 U/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) or 100 μM TRAP6 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 μM ADP (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at 37°C, and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry (28).

Clot formation and retraction

Human blood PLTs or iMK-PLTs (∼1.5 × 107/ml) were resuspended in 200 μl of platelet-depleted plasma in siliconized glass tubes. Thrombin (2 U/ml) was added to induce clot formation and retraction. The clots were allowed to retract at 37°C for 1 hour and then photographed (28).

In vivo MK transfusion experiments

On day −7, 8- to 12-week-old male NVSG mice (Viewsolid Biotech Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) were irradiated (2.5 Gy) to induce thrombocytopenia. On day −1, the mice were depleted of macrophages by treatment with 0.04 mg of clodronate liposomes (Liposoma Technology, Amsterdam, Netherlands) per gram of mouse weight. iMKs were stained with 5 μM DiD (Thermo Fisher Scientific, V22887) for 20 min at 37°C before washing. PBS (400 μl) containing 1 × 107 cells was injected into mice through the tail vein. Human platelets were monitored by flow cytometry from peripheral blood samples at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 24 hours after transfusion using antibodies specific for human CD41a. The absolute platelet count was determined using a Mindray Bc-2800 Vet Blood Cell Counter (Mindray, Shenzhen, China). The mice were euthanized 24 hours after transfusion, and samples from the spleen, bone marrow, and lungs were collected. All mice were kept under specific pathogen–free conditions, and all procedures were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Institute of Radiation Medicine.

Bulk RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN), and quality was assessed using NanoDrop and Bioanalyzer to ensure high integrity (RNA integrity number > 7). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit, using oligo(dT) beads for mRNA enrichment. Single-end sequencing (SE50) was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform. Bioinformatics analysis included quality control with FastQC, read alignment using HISAT2 against the GRCh38 reference genome, and gene expression quantification. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2, applying thresholds of |log2 fold change| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Cryopreserved cord blood CD3+ T cells (day 0) and viable cells from the iMK induction culture on days 7 and 14 were collected and analyzed using the Chromium system (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for an expected capture rate of ∼10,000 single cells per sample. scRNA-seq and data analysis were performed by Annoroad Gene Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China.

Primary process and quality control of scRNA-seq data

FASTQ files of three samples were processed by using Cell Ranger (v7.1.0) count pipeline coupled with human genome reference (Homo sapiens.GRCh38.100.chr) to generate feature-barcode matrices, respectively. The raw digital gene expression matrix data were further filtered by Seurat package (v4.4.0) (50) with R software (v4.3.1) as follows: Genes expressed in at least three cells and cells detected gene number ranged from 200 to 10,000 were kept for further analysis; low-quality cells were also removed if ≥20% unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) derived from the mitochondrial genome; and double cells were removed.

Visualization and clustering

Data integration, cell clustering, and dimension reduction were performed by Seurat (v4.4.0) and harmony (v1.2.0). The raw counts were normalized using 10,000 as the scale factor per cell and log(count + 1) transformed. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified for integration. Data scale and principal components (PCs) analysis were performed with default settings. Then, the significant PCs were selected on the basis of the elbow of the standard deviations of the PCs and used for neighbor detection, followed by Louvain clustering (51) and UMAP. The same analysis pipeline was performed for subcluster detection.

Cell cycle regression

The difference in cell cycle stages between cells can contribute to heterogeneity in the scRNA-seq datasets. The CellCycleScoring function implemented in Seurat was used to evaluate the cell cycle status based on the expression of previously reported G1-S and G2M phase–specific genes (52, 53).

GO enrichment analysis

GO and pathway enrichment (using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database) analyses were performed using DAVID (v6.8, https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov).

T cell state assessment

T cell state was assessed using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, using gene sets derived from the R package of TCellSI (32) (https://github.com/GuoBioinfoLab/TCellSI).

Cell type correlation analysis

scRNA-seq datasets from natural MK from yolk sac and fetal liver, corresponding to six MK subpopulations, were obtained from a previous study (34). To measure the similarity among iMK subclusters and different natural MK cell types, we plotted a heatmap illustrating the expression of marker genes for the six natural MK subpopulations across iMK subclusters.

Gene sets enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA (www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) (54) was performed to identify pathways enriched in specific cell clusters. Gene sets were downloaded from the MSigDB database (www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/genesets.jsp). The normalized gene expression matrix was input to GSEA, with significance thresholds set at FDR < 0.05 and P <0.05. Enrichment plots were generated using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0).

Trajectory analysis

To investigate the dynamic changes during chemical reprogramming, pseudo-time trajectories were reconstructed using the single-cell trajectory analysis module in Monocle 2 (v2.28.0) (55). The 2000 highly variable features were selected as ordering genes (table S5), followed by dimensionality reduction using the Discriminative Dimensionality Reduction with Trees method and visualization with the plot_cell_trajectory function. To compare molecular features between the two directions, BEAM was used to identify the genes differentially expressed along the two paths (q < 0.05). The expression of genes in branched modules was smoothened and visualized with the plot_genes_branched_heatmap function.

TF network construction

To construct a TF correlation network during the conversion of T cells to MKs, Spearman coefficients were calculated from normalized expression of significant TFs to generate the correlation matrix. Visualization was performed with Cytoscape (v3.10.3) (56).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical data are presented as means ± SEM. Sample sizes are indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v8, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For most comparisons, statistical probability (P values) was calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
