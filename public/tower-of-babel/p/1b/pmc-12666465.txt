Citrullination in Tumor Metastasis, Inside and Outside the Cells
Authors: Takeshi Tomita, Priyanka Sharma, Sachie Hiratsuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTumor metastasis remains a poor prognosis because it occurs in all tissues and is difficult to diagnose or prevent before metastatic tumor nodules form. Metastasis is a multi‐step process involving tumor cells, bone marrow‐derived cells, and tissue resident cells, making it a biological three‐body problem. It has been shown that a pre‐metastatic soil/niche (PMN) is formed in metastatic tissue before tumor cells physically appear at the metastatic site, and suppressing this PMN is key to preventing metastasis. Recent studies highlighted the importance of protein citrullination, an irreversible post‐translational modification of proteins, in tumor metastasis. Peptidyl arginine deiminase (PADI) catalyzes the modification of arginine to citrulline. In this enzymatic reaction, the amino acid residue's net charge changes, inducing a structural change in the protein. This review discusses the role of protein citrullination in cancer metastasis. Intracellular citrullination regulates gene expression and genome structure by citrullinating RNA polymerase and histones, while extracellular citrullination provides a pro‐metastatic environment. These factors play an important role in PMN formation. Additionally, we discuss PADI inhibitors and anti‐metastatic immune cells from the viewpoint of metastasis prevention.

Introduction

In recent decades, significant advances in surgery, radiology, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy have improved cancer treatment outcomes. However, metastasis remains a major challenge, as it significantly reduces the survival rate of cancer patients [1]. A metastatic tumor shows a poor prognosis because it can occur anywhere in the tissue and is difficult to diagnose at an early stage or prevent before the formation of metastatic tumor nodules. Research investigating the cellular and molecular mechanisms of tumor metastasis has revealed that metastasis occurs through multiple processes. The first step is the spread of tumor cells from the primary tumor site to a metastatic site in a distant organ [2]. Tumor cells, host tissues, and bone marrow‐derived cells all play critical roles in these processes. Specifically, in the metastatic organ, these factors establish a pre‐metastatic soil/niche (PMN), which resembles a chronic inflammatory site where pro‐tumor factors are enriched and anti‐tumor properties are suppressed. This facilitates tumor metastasis before tumor cells physically appear at the metastatic site [3]. Analyses of mouse models and clinical samples have identified essential cells and molecules for PMN formation. Importantly, comprehensive studies have focused on the role of post‐translational modifications (PTMs) in tumor metastasis due to their ability to regulate cellular functions. For example, intracellular signaling is regulated by protein phosphorylation to activate or deactivate the signal through kinases and phosphatases. Aberrant phosphorylation signaling cascades are found in tumor cells [4]. Protein glycosylation controls cell‐to‐cell interactions, and increased protein glycosylation is found in cancer patients [5]. Recently, molecular studies have shed light on protein citrullination [6]. This PTM is the enzymatic conversion of an arginine residue to citrulline and has long been recognized in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Large amounts of citrullinated proteins are observed in the synovial fluid of RA patients [7]. These patients also tend to have antibodies that recognize citrullinated peptides, and this phenomenon is used as a diagnostic tool [8]. This implies that, although citrullination occurs in the body, it is not normally exposed to citrullinated peptides, which may be immunogenic compounds. In the context of cancer, a proteomic study suggested that protein citrullination could serve as a source of cancer neoantigens, as determined through the analysis of breast cancer cell lines and clinical samples from patients with breast cancer [9]. Although the relationship between protein citrullination and tumor metastasis has not been extensively studied, recent publications suggest that citrullination plays a significant role in tumor metastasis and may be a target for new therapies to prevent metastasis [10, 11]. In addition, the citrullinome study, a comprehensive analysis of protein citrullination in cells, revealed that over 4000 proteins can be citrullinated in neutrophils. This study provides valuable information for PMN research [6]. In this review, we discuss protein citrullination in tumor metastasis.

Formation of PMN

At the beginning of tumor metastasis, it is believed that some tumor cells gain the ability to spread from the primary tumor. Tumor cells that undergo morphological changes, such as epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), can invade surrounding tissues. Some of these cells enter blood vessels (intravasation) and become circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [12]. When such CTCs reach a distant organ, they have the opportunity to invade the new location (extravasation). Then, they undergo another morphological change (mesenchymal‐to‐epithelial transition, MET). Finally, tumor cells form a new nodule at the metastatic site [12].

This process explains what happens to metastatic tumor cells. Because CTCs are more unstable and fragile than they were in the primary tumor, the vast majority are excluded from host tissues. In fact, a study demonstrated that 99% of intravenously injected tumor cells did not survive in the mouse [2]. Only a small fraction of CTCs succeed in forming a metastatic nodule. Studies have shown that the formation of a PMN is crucial for this rare event. The PMN is formed by host tissue and cells recruited to the metastatic site [13], where they create a tumor‐supportive microenvironment that facilitates metastasis [13, 14]. The primary tumor releases molecular signals that precede the circulation of tumor cells. The molecular profile of these signals depends largely on the malignancy and tissue specificity of the tumor cells, so they are directly related to the organotropism of metastasis [12]. Some molecular signals influence immune cells at the primary tumor site. Primary tumor growth is accompanied by tissue destruction, which leads to the recruitment of innate immune cells, such as macrophages [15]. Initially, tumor‐associated immune cells are anti‐tumor; however, upon receiving molecular signals, they become pro‐tumor. Some transcription factors, such as C/EBP, were found to be involved in this process by regulating gene expression [16]. Similarly, CD11b+ immune cells are recruited to the metastatic organ and are reprogrammed into pro‐tumorigenic phenotype [17]. These cells assist in guiding CTCs to metastatic sites and shield them from cytotoxic immune attack. In the metastatic organ, molecular signals released by the primary tumor are sensed by stromal cells, contributing to the formation of a PMN [13]. Within PMN, vascular permeability increases as endothelial cell‐to‐cell junctions loosen, and coagulation proteins such as fibrinogen accumulate in the hyperpermeable region [18]. The Evans blue dye leakage assay has been successfully used to visualize pulmonary PMN in the mouse model. Importantly, these pre‐metastatic sites exhibit non‐uniform, patchy patterns of dye leakage [18]. Regions with high Evans blue accumulation indicate areas of hyperpermeability, a signature of a pre‐metastatic site, whereas other regions show dye retention levels comparable to those in control lungs, suggesting that much of the lung tissue remains unaffected. In hyperpermeable regions, the disruption of endothelial junctions facilitates the extravasation of CTCs into the tissue [19].

Protein Citrullination

Citrullination is an irreversible protein modification catalyzed by peptidyl arginine deiminase (PADIs) [20], and two isoforms (PADI2 and PADI4) are potential inducers of cancer progression and/or metastasis [9, 21, 22]. PADIs convert arginine to citrulline in a Ca2+‐dependent manner. PADI2 has six Ca2+‐binding sites, two of which have high binding affinity, and the others have low binding affinity [23]. One low‐affinity Ca2+‐binding site is in the catalytic domain, and the other three sites are apart from it. The former controls enzyme activity, while the latter is involved in structural changes upon Ca2+ binding [23]. The enzyme reaction removes a positively charged group (NH) and adds a potentially negatively charged group (=O). Although there is no strict sequence specificity in the enzyme reaction, a proteomic study of in vitro PADI2 and PADI4 citrullination revealed weak sequence specificities of the flanking amino acids. Analysis of citrullination in PADI2‐ or PADI4‐overexpressed COS‐1 or HEK293 cell lysates revealed that PADI2 and PADI4 tend to generate CitGXY and (F/Y)G(M/S)(D/G/R/S)Cit(D/G/M)G(G/Y) peptides, respectively [24]. The citrullinome study indicated that structural factors are more important than adjacent amino acid sequences. The study found that citrullination occurs in the intrinsically disordered domain rather than the secondary structural domain [6]. Because citrullination leads to a loss of positive charge in the conversion of arginine to citrulline residues, it is expected to cause structural changes that result in different protein–protein interactions. For example, citrullination in a secondary structure such as an alpha helix may disrupt the structure. In the case of citrullination in an intrinsically disordered domain, a disorder‐to‐order structural change is possible [25]. PADI2 and PADI4 can citrullinate themselves [26], as well as other substrates; this process is known as autocitrullination. Analysis of human PADI4 revealed that autocitrullination does not affect enzyme activity but does impact protein–protein interactions [27]. Co‐immunoprecipitation analysis reported that HDAC1, PRMT1, and citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) bind to PADI4, but not to citrullinated PADI4 [27]. An in vitro citrullination study showed that prolonged incubation or higher Ca2+ concentration resulted in the production of inactive citrullinated enzymes, in which the substrate‐binding site was citrullinated [28]. This finding was confirmed by a unique technique: the site‐specific incorporation of citrulline into recombinant PADI4 [29]. These findings suggest that the folding of the overall structure of PADI4 does not change under mild reaction conditions, allowing it to maintain catalytic activity. Trypsin recognizes a basic amino acid residue (lysine or arginine) to cleave the peptide bonds at the C‐terminus. However, because the character of the amino acid residue changes, trypsin cannot recognize the citrulline residue as a substrate. A protein engineering study showed that modified trypsin can digest at the citrulline residue [30]. In this mutant enzyme, the substrate recognition site was modified to recognize the citrulline instead of the arginine. Interestingly, this study also reported that the mutant enzyme cannot digest an arginine residue [30]. Thus, citrullination can modulate protein function by irreversibly altering the interface of target proteins involved in the enzymatic reactions or protein–protein interactions.

Importance of Citrullination in Cancer Metastasis

The Dual Roles of PADI in Cancer and Intracellular Citrullination

Many studies have found that PADI plays an important role in cancer. Surprisingly, the role of PADI in cancer is complicated: some research revealed that PADI is a tumor suppressor, while others reported that the enzyme enhances cancer progression. Matsuda and co‐workers found that the citrullination of the 3rd arginine residue in histone H4 is negatively correlated with tumor size in non‐small cell lung cancer tissues [31]. A recent publication supports the idea that PADI4 is a tumor suppressor [32]. The paper showed that PADI4 overexpression negatively regulated various cancer cells, including M93‐047 and YUMM1.7 melanoma cells, in a cell proliferation and a xenograft model [32]. It was reported that PADI2 is expressed in normal colon tissue, but not in colon cancer tissue [33]. This paper describes that PADI2 overexpression in HCT116 cells reduced cell proliferation [33]. PADI4 expression in normal breast epithelium was also reported [34]. This paper showed that PADI4 knockdown in MCF7 cells induced EMT and TGFβ signaling. PADI4 citrullinates GSK3β, leading to nuclear accumulation, and the MCF7 xenograft model showed that PADI4 knockdown in tumor cells induced tumor cell invasion [34].

On the other hand, it was reported that PADI2 is upregulated in castration‐resistant prostate cancer, and the knockdown of PADI2 in LNCaP cells was shown to reduce cell viability, while PADI2 overexpression was shown to increase tumor volume in the xenograft model [35]. The authors of the paper concluded that PADI2 stabilizes the androgen receptor and facilitates its nuclear translocation [35]. In a study of gastric cancer, PADI4 upregulation in cancerous tissue was reported [36]. It was also demonstrated that PADI4 knockdown in SGC‐7901 cells reduced cell proliferation and invasion and interfered with the cell cycle [36]. One study showed that the cancer stem cell‐like phenotypes and cisplatin resistance of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) were sustained by PADI4 [37]. Further analysis revealed that PADI4, which is upregulated in OSCC, citrullinates protein arginine methyltransferase 2 (PRMT2) to maintain PRMT2 stability. This leads to the regulation of transcription factors designated as inhibitors of DNA binding (IDs) [37]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, PADI4 was shown to citrullinate HIF1a [38]. This study demonstrated that dihydroergotamine mesylate (DHE), an inhibitor of the PADI4 enzyme, reduced tumor growth in the Hep3B xenograft model [38]. A study on colorectal cancer revealed that PADI4 was upregulated in cancer tissue, and that PADI4 overexpression in HCT116 and SW480 cells increased cell proliferation and migration [39]. The study also demonstrated that PADI4 citrullinated GSK3β, which led to its nuclear accumulation and increased EdU incorporation [39]. Further analysis revealed that PADI4‐dependent citrullination of GSK3β promoted CDKN1A degradation [39]. The correlation between the citrullination of RNA polymerase II (RNApol II) and tumor malignancy was clearly demonstrated. The large subunit of RNApol II possesses a long C‐terminal tail (CTD), which plays an important role in regulating transcription. The CTD contains many heptapeptide repeats, and the PTM on these repeats controls the transcription process [40]. The CTD also contains two arginine residues, and the citrullination of R1810 activates the transcriptional machinery [41, 42]. The R1810 citrullination was observed in cancer cells, and it is interpreted to increase the malignancy [21]. Additionally, Tiwari and co‐workers defined metastatic signatures based on single‐cell transcriptome analyses of cancer patient samples, identifying 286 metastatic signatures [43]. We searched the data in the citrullinome study and found that 23 of the proteins are potentially citrullinated (Figure 1). NETosis

NETosis is a neutrophil self‐defense mechanism [44]. In response to cellular stress, such as pathogen‐associated molecular patterns, intracellular Ca2+ levels rise, promoting PADI enzymatic activity in the nucleus [45, 46]. The Ca2+‐activated PADs hypercitrullinate histones, causing chromatin decondensation [47]. NETosis also promotes metastasis [48] by enhancing tumor cell invasion and increasing vascular permeability within pre‐metastatic niches [49, 50]. NETosis both physically captures CTCs and shields them from immune attack [51]. Mouse studies highlight the importance of NET DNA's large, flexible structure in facilitating metastasis [52], and administration of DNase to degrade NETs can reverse these effects. Moreover, extracellular Ca2+ can activate released PADI enzymes, leading to citrullination of extracellular matrix proteins. Both macrophages and cancer cells can undergo NETosis‐like apoptotic cell death, further contributing to tumor progression and metastasis [53, 54].

Extracellular Citrullination in Relation to Cancer Metastasis

It is possible that PADI is expressed on the surface of cancer cells. PADI4 was detected on the surface of MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF7 cells [55]. The addition of a PADI4 antibody to the medium reduced cell proliferation, viability, and migration, and induced apoptosis and glycoATP production [55]. Additionally, they demonstrated that high level expression of PADI in the tumor tissue and reducing the citrullination of fibronectin are key mechanisms underlying the efficacy of the antibody therapy [56]. Similarly, citrullinated proteins (GPR78, vimentin, and enolase) were detected on the cell surface of various cancer cells, including B16 and MCF7. These citrullinated proteins can stimulate CD4+T cells [57, 58, 59]. Therefore, the presence of PADI enzymes and citrullinated proteins in the extracellular environment is not surprising. In a study of colorectal cancer liver metastasis, citrullination of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, including collagen I, was observed specifically in the metastatic liver tissue [11]. Importantly, the same study showed that liver metastasis in mice was significantly reduced by treatment with the PADI inhibitor or by knockdown of PADI4 in cancer [11]. These findings highlight the role of PADI4‐mediated extracellular citrullination in both metastatic niche formation and tumor progression.

In the pulmonary vasculature, fibrinogen undergoes citrullination in the PMN and endothelial cells support this process [10]. In vitro analysis revealed multiple citrullination sites in fibrinogen, predominantly located in the alpha chain [60, 61]. The citrullination ratio (defined as the proportion of citrulline relative to arginine at a specific residue) varies depending on the reaction conditions (e.g., enzyme activity, Ca2+ concentration, and incubation time). The crystal structure of fibrinogen shows that citrullination sites are found in both ordered and disordered regions [62]. Fibrinogen is processed by thrombin to form fibrin, leading to the formation of a fibrin clot. In vitro studies showed that citrullination blocks the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin [63]. However, another study reported that citrullinated fibrinogen reacts with thrombin to form an aggregation that differs from the canonical fibrin clot in fiber diameter, morphological structure, and mechano‐structural properties [64]. This discrepancy occurs because enzymatically produced citrullinated fibrinogens contain a mixture of citrullinated and non‐citrullinated residues. Fibrinogen deposits are formed in the hyperpermeable regions and can be citrullinated. Citrullinated fibrinogen facilitates the extravasation of CTCs because CTCs can attach to it (Figure 2) [10]. The addition of citrullinated peptide disrupted this attachment, and tumor metastasis was suppressed by peptide administration in the mouse model [10].

A few studies have investigated the correlation between blood levels of citrullinated proteins and cancer progression. Thålin et al. developed a detection method for nucleosomal CitH3 and discovered elevated serum CitH3 levels in cancer patients [65]. This biomarker can distinguish cancer from non‐cancerous conditions and autoimmune diseases, but not infectious diseases. Arfman et al. reported an increased level of citrullinated fibrinogen in the blood of cancer patients and identified it as a risk factor for venous thromboembolism [66]. In this paper, the authors increased the sensitivity and specificity of the assay by quantifying a chemical reactant generated by the coupling reaction of alpha‐ketoaldehyde with the ureido group of citrulline.

Preventing Citrullination‐Dependent Cancer Metastasis

PADI Inhibitors

There are two types of inhibitors: reversible and irreversible. Irreversible inhibitors typically contain a haloacetamidine group at their core, which forms a covalent bond with the ‐SH group of the cysteine residue in an enzyme's active site. Since the ‐SH group attacks the substrate to form an intermediate in the catalytic reaction, the inhibitors effectively block the reaction. In contrast, reversible inhibitors do not form a covalent bond with the enzyme; rather, they slip into the substrate binding pocket to inhibit the reaction [67]. A representative irreversible inhibitor, Cl‐amidine, modulated extracellular vesicle release in glioblastoma multiforme cells, decreasing pro‐oncogenic microRNA21 and increasing anti‐oncogenic microRNA126 [68]. In tamoxifen‐resistant MCF7 breast cancer cells, which overexpress PADI2 compared to their tamoxifen‐sensitive counterparts, Cl‐amidine restored tamoxifen sensitivity [69]. The alpha‐carboline derivative and pan‐PADI inhibitor YW3‐56 analogue acts as an irreversible PADI4‐selective inhibitor. It inhibited the growth of solid tumors and pulmonary metastatic nodules in a triple‐negative breast cancer xenograft mouse model [70]. PADI2‐IN‐1 (also known as AFM32a), an irreversible, selective inhibitor of PADI2, blocked the formation of macrophage extracellular traps and colon cancer liver metastasis in a mouse model [54]. JBI‐589 is a reversible, selective inhibitor of PADI4. In a mouse model study, administering JBI‐589 reduced primary tumor growth and lung metastasis. Detailed analyses revealed that JBI‐589 reduced CXCR2 expression and blocked neutrophil recruitment [71]. Some studies use drug delivery systems to increase the efficacy of these inhibitors. For instance, a YW3‐56 analogue coupled with a chitosan nanomedicine containing an RGD peptide prevented more Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) lung metastasis in C57BL/6N mice than the uncoupled compound did [72]. To enhance the therapeutic effects of PADI inhibitors, nanomedicine‐based drug delivery systems have been explored. For example, YW3‐56 analogue, YW403, inhibited LLC lung metastasis in C57BL/6N mice compared to the free drug. The antimetastatic effect was enhanced when the compound was loaded onto Fe3O4‐oxidized carboxymethyl chitosan magnetic nanoparticles, and a magnetic field was built up after drug administration [73].

Anti‐Metastatic Immune Cells

In the mouse liver, a small subset of natural killer (NK) cells characterized as B220+CD11c+NK1.1+ has been identified [16, 74]. These cells, referred to as hepato‐entrained leukocytes, undergo phenotypic changes in response to signals from the primary tumor and subsequently migrate to the lungs [74]. Because they express thrombospondin‐1 (Tsp‐1), which interacts with fibrinogen to tether immune cells to fibrinogen deposits in areas of vascular hyperpermeability, it is unclear whether Tsp‐1 binds to citrullinated fibrinogen. However, given that fibrinogen deposits at the pre‐metastatic site contain a mixture of citrullinated and uncitrullinated proteins, it is not surprising to observe that these cells in the pre‐metastatic site clear the area of hyperpermeability and prevent CTCs from settling. In humans, certain subsets of human CD3−CD56+ NK cells are believed to be the human counterpart of these hepato‐entrained pulmonary leukocytes and can become anti‐metastatic immune cells. These cells are activated by non‐vesicular extracellular RNA in a nucleotide sequence‐dependent manner [75]. Unlike vesicle‐encapsulated RNA, non‐vesicular exRNA is not enclosed by a lipid bilayer, enabling it to directly interact with cell surface receptors [76]. One such receptor, the ZC3H12D protein, is a cell surface receptor found in the hepato‐entrained pulmonary leukocytes. This ZC3H12D‐RNA interaction enhances the tumoricidal activity of the cells. Notably, injection of a chemically modified RNA analogue engineered to resist RNase degradation blocked tumor metastasis in a mouse model, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy [77].

Perspectives

Immunohistochemical analysis can detect metastases by identifying circulating tumor cells that invade metastatic tissue. At this stage, the tumor cells are less robust than they were within the primary tumor. Thus, the most effective strategy to treat metastasis may be to intervene early—at future metastatic sites. However, a major clinical challenge remains to be solved: accurately predicting the location of these PMNs. Citrullinated extracellular proteins can guide us to the pre‐metastatic point. Unlike other well‐studied PTMs, such as phosphorylation and acetylation, citrullination is irreversible. This makes citrullinated protein deposits stable markers of the pre‐metastatic microenvironment. Additionally, NETosis‐derived extracellular DNA plays a significant role in shaping this environment. Therefore, visualizing both citrullinated proteins and cell‐free DNA may provide a powerful method for identifying and targeting pre‐metastatic sites. Therapeutically, removing citrullinated proteins and cell‐free DNA from tissues is key to preventing metastasis. As described in this manuscript, PADI inhibitors are very powerful tools to suppress metastasis. However, since many tissues express PADIs and they play a protective role against infection and inflammation [78], side effects may not be inevitable, especially when PADI inhibitors are administered long‐term. A promising alternative is to harness anti‐metastatic immune cells, such as hepato‐entrained pulmonary leukocytes, which can both degrade citrullinated ECM and eliminate CTCs at pre‐metastatic sites (Figure 3). Future therapeutic strategies may include activating these immune cells using novel agents. One exciting direction involves combining extracellular RNA analogues, which selectively activate anti‐metastatic cells. This approach opens a new frontier in immune‐based metastasis prevention.
