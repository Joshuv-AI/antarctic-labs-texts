Clinical practices in general anaesthesia for caesarean section

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAnaesthesia is central to obstetric care, yet contemporary data on clinical practices, particularly concerning general anaesthesia (GA) for caesarean section (CS), remain scarce. Given evolving guidelines and technologies, an updated understanding of real-world anaesthetic management is needed.OBJECTIVEOur aim was to evaluate current anaesthetic practices in obstetric care in Germany, focusing on the use of GA for CS, perioperative management, and adherence to guideline recommendations.DESIGNNationwide, anonymised, cross-sectional questionnaire survey.SETTINGThe questionnaire targeted both experienced obstetric anaesthesiologists and other anaesthesiologists regularly working in obstetric anaesthesia in German hospitals.PARTICIPANTSWe analysed 567 completed questionnaires from anaesthesiologists actively involved in obstetric care, representing 171 geographic locations and institutions covering approximately half of all annual births in Germany.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURESContraindications for neuraxial procedures, estimated GA rates, perioperative management strategies, and conformity with guidelines.RESULTSThe survey yielded 29 984 individual responses. Neuraxial anaesthesia was strongly preferred over GA (Likert score 4.8/5), with a median GA rate of 5 [5 to 10]%. Postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis was provided by 89.4% of respondents. Platelet thresholds for neuraxial anaesthesia were more liberal than in previous surveys, with reliance on individual risk-benefit assessment instead of fixed numerical thresholds. For GA induction, propofol has largely replaced thiopental, but suxamethonium remains the favoured neuromuscular blocking agent (71.7%). Balanced anaesthesia with sevoflurane predominated (84%), while nitrous oxide use has declined sharply (2.1%). Gaps in guideline adherence were noted regarding the timing of antibiotic prophylaxis, the use of active warming and intraoperative red blood cell salvage. Compared with international practice, postoperative analgesia was mainly opioid-based, with regional techniques underused (23%).CONCLUSIONSDespite progress towards guideline adherence, substantial gaps persist between recommendations and clinical practice in German obstetric anaesthesia. Improvement opportunities include better implementation of evidence-based perioperative strategies and greater use of regional analgesic techniques postoperatively.

Introduction

Anaesthesia is a cornerstone of modern obstetric care, essential for ensuring the safety and comfort of women undergoing caesarean section (CS). In high-resource healthcare systems such as in the United Kingdom, anaesthetic techniques are involved in approximately 65% of all births, highlighting their central role in contemporary maternity care.1 While overall birth rates have remained relatively stable, the global rate of CS has steadily increased. In Germany, CS accounted for over 30% of all births, with nearly 237 000 procedures performed per year.2

CS can be performed under either neuraxial anaesthesia or general anaesthesia (GA), with current guidelines clearly recommending neuraxial techniques as the preferred approach. Although evidence does not show significant differences in maternal mortality between the two techniques, neuraxial anaesthesia is associated with several clinical advantages, such as reduced risk of severe complications and aspiration, improved neonatal outcomes, and the facilitation of immediate maternal–infant bonding.3,4

Despite broad consensus on best practices, anaesthetic care for CS varies substantially across countries and institutions.5,6,7 For example, the frequency of GA use differs internationally, reflecting a range of structural, cultural, and organisational influences. These variations illustrate the need for up-to-date insight into real-world clinical practice.

Decision-making in obstetric anaesthesia is often complex, time-sensitive, and based on individual risk–benefit considerations, which makes it challenging to apply standardised, evidence-based recommendations in every situation. Robust evidence is lacking for several key areas, including contraindication thresholds for neuraxial anaesthesia, the management of emergency scenarios, and other aspects of best practice. Generating high-quality evidence is particularly challenging due to the rarity of certain events and ethical limitations in conducting controlled studies during acute obstetric scenarios. As a result, documenting current practice becomes an essential foundation for identifying modifiable factors for further improvement.1,8

Although several countries have conducted national surveys to evaluate obstetric anaesthesia practices,1,7,9 the last comprehensive survey in Germany was carried out in 2011.10 Since then, clinical guidelines, technology, and workforce structures have evolved significantly, but it remains unclear how these changes have influenced daily clinical routines in Germany.

To address this gap, we conducted a nationwide, individual-level, cross-sectional survey among practising anaesthesiologists working in obstetrics in Germany. Our objectives were to assess the use of general versus neuraxial anaesthesia in both elective and emergency CS; explore key clinical and decision-making factors influencing the choice of anaesthetic technique; and evaluate intra- and postoperative management practices, including the availability of relevant equipment. By mapping the current landscape of obstetric anaesthesia in Germany, this study aims to identify areas of interest for future standardisation and quality improvement.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a nationwide cross-sectional survey in Germany to investigate the anaesthetic management of CS. The target population consisted of clinically active anaesthetists involved in obstetric care, both specialist and non-specialist obstetric anaesthetist. No specific exclusion criteria were defined for participation. Ethical approval by the local committee of Würzburg was obtained before survey distribution (ref. no. 2025-39-ka). All participants were asked to provide informed consent by ticking a checkbox for the anonymised processing and analysis of their responses.

Instrument development

The survey was designed according to the CHERRIES checklist and is reported according to the STROBE Statement. Rather than being tailored to hospital institutions, the survey was directed at individual anaesthesiologists to capture more authentic and reliable responses while reducing potential bias from “desired answers”.11 The questionnaire was developed in German. Thereby, a consensus-based approach by a core team of the authors was employed for the development of the questionnaire. Draft questions underwent five iterative rounds of review and refinement by the core study group members before receiving final approval from all team members. An internal pilot phase was then conducted. The online survey was designed, employing an established platform, hosted at the University hospital Würzburg “evasys” (evasys GmbH, Lueneburg, Germany), ensuring compliance with data protection regulations. To maintain data integrity and prevent duplicate submissions, no incentives were offered to participants.

The final questionnaire consisted of 56 questions across eight categories: “General Information”, “Indications”, “Contraindications”, “Emergency CS Management”, “Elective CS Management”, “Intraoperative Management”, “Equipment”, and “Postoperative Management”. Question formats included 43 single-choice, 8 multiple-choice, 4 numerical-entry, and one matrix question. Single-choice questions were either standard single-choice items with the selection of values, frequencies, or categories, or structured according to the Likert methodology. It was not mandatory to complete all questions. Respondents were able to modify their answers at any time before submission. A conditional questioning approach was implemented to optimise survey length by displaying specific questions based on previous responses.

Instrument distribution

The questionnaire was distributed as an open survey via a web-link and a QR code. It was disseminated through the E-Mail distribution list of the German Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine (DGAI), as well as via personal correspondence from the authors (i.e. direct E-Mail invitations to professional contacts). To maintain a targeted respondent group, no public advertisement was conducted. The survey started on 18 February 2025 and was closed on 4 April 2025 for data extraction.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the R statistical environment (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Only questionnaires that were fully submitted (i.e. the “Submit” button on the final page was clicked) were included. Descriptive statistics are presented as numbers with percentages for categorical variables and median [IQR] for continuous variables. Before analysis, invalid responses, e.g. incomplete postal codes or percentage values ≥100% were removed. Rather than calculating a sample size in advance, the survey was designed to maximise response yield. For subgroup comparisons, the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test was applied to continuous variables and Fisher's exact test to categorical variables. Shifts in the favourability of specific anaesthetic drugs across clinical scenarios were visualised using alluvial plots, and bar plots were employed to illustrate additional practice patterns. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a P value <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Overview of the cohort

We analysed 567 submitted questionnaires, comprising 29 984 item responses (item completion rate 94.4%) (Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content, Table 1). The participants were specialist obstetric anaesthetists (25.6%), other senior consultants (30.9%) and physicians holding leadership positions (24.0%) and residents (15.0%). Most respondents were employed at hospitals providing maximum or specialised care (39.8%), followed by basic or standard care hospitals (30.4%), and university hospitals (28.6%). Most respondents were experienced obstetric anaesthesiologists, with 92.4% having managed over 50 CS, 78.8% attending >100, and 60.1% doing so several times per week. The median duration of professional experience in obstetric anaesthesia was 15 [6 to 24] years with a maximum of 45 years. By extrapolating from current German birth statistics, using the postal codes of the participants’ institutions to identify the corresponding regional birth numbers, we estimated that the participating hospitals collectively cared for approximately 323 863 births per year, corresponding to about 50% of all births nationwide.

Half of the respondents (49%) indicated that 30% to 40% of women undergoing vaginal delivery at their institutions receive neuraxial analgesia. A strong preference for neuraxial anaesthesia over GA for CS evident, reflected by a mean Likert-scale score of 4.8/5 (SD 0.7). Additionally, the frequency of GA for CS as reported by respondents for their institution ranged from around 5% to 10%.

Contraindications for neuraxial anaesthesia

Almost one-quarter (23%) of respondents indicated that obtaining current laboratory coagulation tests, including prothrombin time (Quick), PTT, INR and platelet count, was obligatory before performing neuraxial anaesthesia. Regarding platelet count thresholds, 35.5% of respondents emphasised that clinical trends were more relevant than fixed numerical cut-offs. Specific laboratory thresholds identified as contraindications for neuraxial anaesthesia varied among participants and are presented in detail in Fig. 2. A majority (92.8%) of participants preferred GA to neuraxial techniques in cases of local infection at the puncture site, while 62.4% identified symptomatic aortic stenosis as a contraindication. Only a minority considered tattoos at the puncture site or ongoing aspirin medication at a dose of 150 mg per day as contraindications (14.5% and 15.3%, respectively). Notably, 3.4% of respondents stated they would perform neuraxial anaesthesia in all clinical scenarios outlined in Figure 2.

Induction of anaesthesia

The three most frequently used agents for induction of general anaesthesia in CS were propofol, thiopental, and ketamine, whereas benzodiazepines and etomidate played negligible roles and were not routinely administered. Most respondents (73.9%) indicated that they do not administer opioids before umbilical cord clamping. Suxamethonium was the preferred muscle relaxant among most participants (71.7%), while rocuronium was used by 27.4%. For elective CS, >90% of respondents considered monitoring blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), blood pressure, electrocardiogram (ECG), and capnometry as mandatory. In emergency CS, 99% considered SpO2 monitoring mandatory, while capnometry (81%), blood pressure (74%), and ECG (66%) were each deemed essential by fewer respondents (Figure 2 Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content). Endotracheal intubation was universally recognised as the gold standard for airway management during GA for CS, employed by nearly all respondents (99.8%), with only a minority (0.2%) using a laryngeal mask. Rapid sequence induction (RSI) was performed by 86.6% of respondents. Conventional laryngoscopy constituted standard practice for 59% of respondents, while 40.9% preferred videolaryngoscopy.

Perioperative drugs and anaesthesia management

Most respondents (61.1%) routinely administered antacids before induction, while 18.6% used antacids only in selected cases, and 20.3% reported never using antacids. For intraoperative analgesia, sufentanil was the most commonly administered opioid (72.5%), followed by fentanyl (29.5%), piritramide, and remifentanil. Only a small minority (0.2%) reported performing opioid-free anaesthesia. Regarding maintenance of anaesthesia, inhalational anaesthetics were preferred by most participants (86.0%), with sevoflurane clearly favoured (83.7%) compared with isoflurane and desflurane (1.1% and 1.2%, respectively). Total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) was used by 9.9%, and a combination of continuous propofol infusion with inhalational anaesthesia by 4.1%. Nitrous oxide has become significantly less relevant and was used by only 2.1% of respondents.

For primary management of intraoperative hypotension, the majority reported choosing cafedrine/theodrenaline (Akrinor) as their first-line agent (78.1%), followed by phenylephrine (8.8%), ephedrine (6.9%), and noradrenaline (6.0%). Routine antibiotic prophylaxis was administered by 95.4% of participants, predominantly before skin incision (71.3%) rather than after umbilical cord clamping (24.1%) or exclusively in the presence of specific risk factors (2.1%). Regarding prophylactic antiemetic administration for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), 89.4% of respondents indicated giving prophylaxis, with the majority always administering it (57.1%), while 9.5% did so only during balanced anaesthesia, and 22.8% restricted its use to patients with known risk factors. Ondansetron (55.7%) and dexamethasone at 4 mg (53.6%) or 8 mg (40.5%), were the most commonly used antiemetic drugs (Figure 1 Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content).

Equipment

Intraoperative salvage of red cells (IOS) was employed by 44.0% of respondents only in cases with anticipated high blood loss, whereas routine use was reported by just 0.7%. Among the 55.3% who did not use IOS, two-thirds (66.1%) cited unavailability as the primary reason. Active warming therapy was routinely implemented by 47.5% of participants, while 36.6% either did not use active warming or reserved it exclusively for cases of documented hypothermia. Blood pressure monitoring during CS under GA predominantly involved a conventional blood pressure cuff (98.6%), with only a minority (1.4%) adopting continuous non-invasive methods, such as the volume-clamp technique. Monitoring for depth-of-anaesthesia was routinely employed by 12.4% of participants, while 39.2% limited its use to patients with specific risk factors, such as a history of intraoperative awareness. In total, 83.9% of respondents confirmed that depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring systems were available in their institutions. Regarding nasogastric tubes, the majority (54.1%) indicated no routine perioperative use, 18.3% restricted their use to high-risk patients, and 27.6% placed them routinely in every case of GA for CS.

Postoperative management

For postoperative analgesia, most respondents administered opioids (83.6%), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (68.3%), and paracetamol (65.8%) (Fig. 5). Regional anaesthesia techniques were routinely employed by 23.1%, while routine local infiltration techniques were reported by 13.8%. It was reported by 98.4% of respondents that postoperative monitoring took place in a recovery or delivery room. Postoperative care was delivered by nursing staff in 51.1% of cases, by midwives in 45.9%, and by physicians in 2.8% of cases.

Comparing university with non-university hospitals

In a subgroup analysis comparing responses from university hospitals (n = 162) and non-university hospitals (n = 405), several significant differences in clinical practice were identified. IOS was used significantly more often in university hospitals, particularly in cases with risk factors (63.6% vs. 36.0%, P < 0.001) and IOS was more frequently unavailable in non-university hospitals (23.6% vs. 6.8%). Nasogastric tubes were routinely used more often in university settings (42.6% vs. 21.3%, P < 0.001). Depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring was applied more frequently in university hospitals (17.3% vs. 10.4%, P = 0.029): non-university hospitals reported a twofold higher rate of unavailability of such monitoring systems. Routine use of active warming therapy was also more common in university hospitals (57.8% vs. 43.6%, P = 0.034).

Discussion

We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional survey among anaesthesiologists active in obstetrics in Germany to describe current clinical practices in the anaesthetic management of CS. The intended target group of obstetric anaesthesia specialists and other anaesthesiologists regularly working in obstetrics was effectively reached, with 93% of respondents reporting experience of more than 50 CS and a median professional experience of 15 years in obstetric anaesthesia. Our findings demonstrate that GA for CS is rare in Germany (5%) compared with international studies reporting varying rates ranging from 4–45%.6,7,12–15 While some international data report higher GA use in peripheral hospitals (35.4%) compared with major urban centres (22.8%),7 our results did not reflect such regional disparities.

Contraindications for neuraxial anaesthesia

A key challenge in obstetric anaesthesia is the use of neuraxial techniques in patients with impaired coagulation, as evidence-based recommendations and guideline recommendations are sparse. In previous surveys from Germany (2007) and Europe (2012), 39% of anaesthesiologists indicated a platelet count <80 000 mm−3 as a threshold contraindicating neuraxial anaesthesia and more than half of respondents cited 65 000 mm−3.16,17 Two international surveys reported similar variation in platelet count thresholds for neuraxial anaesthesia, with 21% and 42% of respondents considering counts below 100 000 mm−3 as a contraindication, while 30–39% assumed a threshold of 80 000 mm−3, and 22% accepted 75 000 mm−3. Only 4–29% allowed neuraxial anaesthesia at platelet counts below 50 000 mm−3.9,16 Additionally, around 11% prioritised a significant drop from baseline over the absolute value. In contrast, our current findings show a clear shift away from strict numerical thresholds toward greater emphasis on clinical dynamics. Notably, our respondents demonstrated a more liberal approach. However, explicit guideline recommendations addressing these thresholds remain sparse, highlighting an urgent need for consensus statements to guide clinical decision-making.

Although current German guidelines do not recommend routine laboratory coagulation testing before neuraxial procedures in patients without a bleeding history or known coagulation disorders, almost one quarter of respondents considered such testing mandatory.18 Furthermore, 15% of respondents regarded chronic medication with 150 mg aspirin per day as a contraindication to neuraxial anaesthesia, despite this being contrary to current guideline recommendations. These findings indicate a potential for improvement in the nationwide implementation of up-to-date guidelines.

Induction of anaesthesia

Our findings show a clear shift toward propofol as the preferred induction drug for GA in CS, regardless of urgency. In 2011, 81.5% of German anaesthesiologists reported using thiopental,10 while earlier studies on emergency CS showed equal use of propofol and thiopental (46.7% each).19 Comparing these data with a European survey investigating the management of rapid sequence induction (RSI) outside obstetric anaesthesia in 2020, 90.6% preferred propofol, compared with 11.2% for thiopental and 3.8% ketamine.20 The increasing use of propofol over time may be explained by growing evidence indicating that it is not inferior to thiopental with respect to neonatal outcomes, particularly Apgar scores.18 In addition, propofol is associated with a more favourable pharmacokinetic profile and is widely used in non-obstetric settings.18 Thiopental, by contrast, is associated with rapid placental transfer and a risk of foetal accumulation. Furthermore it is rarely used outside obstetrics nowadays, and many younger anaesthesiologists may have limited practical experience with this agent.18 The use of specific muscle relaxants has remained relatively stable over the past decade. With its use by 72–82% in our study, suxamethonium continues to be the most frequently used drug, depending on the urgency of the procedure; this is in line with earlier reports ranging from 66–90%.9,10,19

Almost all respondents in our study (99.8%) performed tracheal intubation as airway management, which remains consistent with current guidelines and previous data, reporting a 97% intubation rate.10 Nevertheless, 13.4% in our study reported not performing an RSI, despite its strong recommendation in national and international guidelines.18

Perioperative drugs and anaesthesia management

Our findings confirm the ongoing preference for balanced general anaesthesia for CS (86%), alongside notable shifts in drug selection. Sevoflurane is now used by a majority of respondents (84%), up from 60% in a 2011 German survey, largely replacing isoflurane, desflurane, and nitrous oxide.10 The use of nitrous oxide has almost ceased, being reported by only 2.1% of respondents, compared with 20% previously.10 Internationally, this contrast is even more striking with a 2021 UK survey reporting nitrous oxide use in 84% of cases.21

Nearly all respondents (95.4%) reported administering antibiotic prophylaxis, most commonly before skin incision, but 24.1% administered it after umbilical cord clamping. These findings indicate a clear trend in Germany toward aligning with international guidelines recommending administration before skin incision. In contrast, international data from 2005 to 2019 reported adherence rates ranging from 47% to 78%.11,19

Akrinor was the most frequently used vasopressor for managing hypotension in our survey (78.1%), although its use has declined compared with 2011 (86.2%).10 This suggests a gradual shift toward international practices with phenylephrine as the standard vasopressor (93%) in the UK.1,16 Recent studies suggest comparable efficacy between Akrinor and phenylephrine but phenylephrine has a lower risk of foetal acidosis.22 Routine prophylaxis for PONV was reported by 57.1% of respondents in our survey, with an additional 32.3% using antiemetics after individual risk-benefit consideration. This is significantly lower than the 77.3% routine use reported in international data.9

Equipment

Women undergoing CS under GA face a disproportionately high risk of unintended intraoperative awareness, underscoring the clinical relevance of depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring.18,23 In our survey, 83.9% of respondents confirmed institutional availability of depth-of-anaesthesia monitors, yet only 12.4% used them as standard, with a further 39.2% employing them in high-risk patients. Although use has increased compared with studies from 2013 (1.8%) and 2021 (9.1%),24,25 the persistent gap between availability and routine use suggests that demonstrated benefits have not yet translated into widespread clinical use. A similar phenomenon was observed in use of active warming systems (e.g., forced-air warming blankets), with only 47.5% of respondents reporting routine use, compared with 90.9% in international data.9 Although there is broad awareness of the risk of perioperative hypothermia under both neuraxial and general anaesthesia, the findings indicate potential for more consistent implementation in clinical practice.

Postpartum haemorrhage remains one of the most serious complications in obstetric care, with an incidence of 4.8%. IOS represents a key strategy in reducing allogeneic blood transfusion during CS. Compared with registry data from 2020 to 2021 showing IOS use at only 0.31%,26 our findings indicate a clear trend toward broader implementation, with 44% of respondents reporting the use of IOS in situations with anticipated high blood loss. Despite growing awareness of the benefits of IOS, its routine use is still very rare (0.7%), even though postpartum haemorrhage often occurs without risk factors. Although international guidelines recommend IOS for anticipated high blood loss, one third of respondents either lacked access or did not use it despite availability. This reflects a notable discrepancy between guideline recommendations and real-world clinical practice, despite an encouraging trend toward increased use.

Postoperative management

In terms of postoperative pain, CS ranks among the ten most predisposing surgical procedures with mean resting pain-scores exceeding NRS 6/10 on the first postoperative day.27 In our survey, 84%of clinicians relied on systemic opioids, markedly more than the 65.2%reported in an international study of CS under general anaesthesia.9 Use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs was less frequent in our survey (68%) compared with 77.3%abroad, whereas paracetamol was more widely adopted here at 66%versus 48.9%.7,9 Dexamethasone has been shown to reduce both PONV and pain intensity on day 1,28 yet while 54% of anaesthesiologists administer it for PONV prophylaxis, only 34% use it specifically for its analgesic benefit. This difference indicates a potential for further training of healthcare professionals.

The use of regional anaesthetic techniques in our cohort was modest. Local anaesthesia was performed by only 14% of respondents, despite international reports indicating rates between 3% and 65%.9,19 Transversus abdominis plane blocks were used slightly more frequently, but with a reported use of 23%, it remains <22–44% rates reported in other studies.9,19 These findings highlight the potential to improve postoperative analgesia through wider integration of regional and local techniques in addition to systemic therapies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its study design. First, a true response rate cannot be calculated, as the total number of individuals who received or accessed the survey is unknown. While approximately 26 600 anaesthetists were estimated to be practising in Germany in 2022, the proportion actively involved in obstetric anaesthesia remains unclear. As a result, the generalisation of the results cannot be guaranteed, and a nonresponse bias cannot be ruled out. A meaningful completion rate cannot be determined, as only questionnaires that were fully submitted by clicking “submit” on the final page were included in the analysis. As a result, the ratio of users who started but did not complete the survey remains unknown. The participation rate, defined as the ratio of unique visitors who agreed to participate, could not be evaluated, as no visitor counter was implemented on the survey website. Reported response rates in anaesthesiology surveys vary considerably. A German survey on CS reported a 50% return rate.10 International surveys among individual anaesthesiologists show lower rates with 10.2–20.1%, whereas institution-based surveys report higher rates of 35.6–99%.1,29,30,31

Possible self-selection bias and possible multiple participation bias must also be considered. It is likely that anaesthesiologists with a specific interest or expertise in obstetric anaesthesia were more inclined to participate, which may have influenced the findings. Furthermore, response bias cannot be excluded, where respondents may have reported perceived best practices rather than their actual routine behaviour. To mitigate this, we opted for an individual- rather than institution-based survey design. However, external validation of the responses was not possible, and it remains uncertain to what extent the reported data reflect real-world clinical practice. Furthermore, as a cross-sectional questionnaire, our work cannot analyse and explain the complex contextual and individual factors underlying deviations from guideline recommendations or fully capture the nuances of clinical decision-making. To tackle these limitations, a nationwide prospective observational study would be desirable to systematically capture real-world conditions and treatment strategies.

Geographical distribution showed a balanced national spread, but certain regions, particularly parts of central and eastern Germany outside major urban centres, were underrepresented. Additionally, responses from highly engaged centres such as Würzburg and Heidelberg may have led to a clustering effect, with a disproportionate number of responses from a few institutions. Comparisons with other studies should be made cautiously, as clinical anaesthesia practices evolve rapidly. Moreover, data collected via self-reported surveys differ substantially from findings obtained through objective registry data or prospective observational studies. Nonetheless the resulting dataset offers a contemporary, nationwide overview of anaesthetic practices for caesarean sections in Germany. It provides valuable insights to guide quality improvement initiatives, highlights areas requiring attention, and establishes a foundation for hypothesis generation and the design of future studies.

Conclusion

Despite progress toward guideline adherence, substantial gaps persist between recommendations and clinical practice in German obstetric anaesthesia. Improvement opportunities include better implementation of evidence-based perioperative strategies and greater use of regional analgesic techniques postoperatively.
