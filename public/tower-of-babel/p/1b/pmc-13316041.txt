Early identification of persistent progressive myocardial injury in Duchenne muscular dystrophy: a prospective, single-center cohort study
Authors: Dan Zhou, Yang Li, Qin Hu, Yulian Chen, Min Huang, Hui Zhou, Huayan Xu, Chuan Wang, Li Yu, Xiaomei Sun, Na Li, Xiaotang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to clarify the different developmental processes of myocardial injury and their significance in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), and to optimize the long-term management of DMD-associated cardiomyopathy. A prospective, single-center cohort study of children with DMD was conducted between June 2023 and April 2025 at West China Second University Hospital. Clinical symptoms and heart function-related indicators were monitored in children aged > 7 years. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, and chi-squared test were applied according to the data type and normality, and post hoc Bonferroni correction was performed among the intragroup factors. A total of 174 children with DMD were enrolled, 25.29% of whom had high cardiac troponin I (cTnI) levels (cTnI-H group), among whom 75% had cTnI elevations < 10-fold (cTnI-H1 subgroup), 15.91% had cTnI elevations > 10-fold but < 30-fold (cTnI-H2 subgroup), and 9.09% had cTnI elevations > 30-fold (cTnI-H3 subgroup). During follow-up, 15.38% of the children in the normal cTnI level group (cTnI-N group) had newly elevated cTnI levels, whereas the elevated cTnI levels in the cTnI-H group had decreased to normal in 9.09% of the children, exhibited repeated elevations in 33.33% of the children, had continuous elevations in 57.58% of the children. 100% of the children in the cTnI-H3 subgroup had newer or expanded late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and perfusion defects on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). The longest duration of elevated cTnI was 15 months, in which 100% had newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR. There might be three different states of myocardial injury in DMD according to the degree of cTnI elevation, and the outcomes of elevated cTnI levels and CMR: transient injury, fluctuating injury, and persistent progressive injury. These three states might alternate and fluctuate with each other. The significant increase and long duration of elevated cTnI levels might be associated with persistent progressive myocardial damage. Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked inherited myopathy that causes progressive skeletal and cardiac muscle disease1,2. The cardiac pathology of DMD is characterized by chronic inflammation, edema, degeneration, and fibrosis resulting from repeated myocyte injury3,4. This chronic process leads to progressive loss of cardiac function5,6. Previous studies reported that patients with DMD present with episodes of acute chest pain, transient elevations in cardiac troponin I (cTnI), acute heart failure, and even sudden death7–11. Cardiac failure is currently the leading cause of mortality in patients with DMD1,3,12. However, in clinical practice, the development process of silent myocardial injury before acute heart failure or sudden death in patients with DMD is difficult to identify in the early stage; moreover, the process of its occurrence and development has not been fully elucidated. Indeed, to date, there have been no clinical studies or relevant literature reports in this area.

In accordance with the international DMD management standards12,13, our center routinely monitors cardiac function using biomarkers such as cardiac troponin I (cTnI), together with electrocardiogram (ECG), echocardiography, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) every 6 months. cTnI serves as a sensitive marker of myocardial necrosis14–16 and might help identify early cardiac involvement before overt symptoms appear. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 779 patients with DMD who were > 7 years old from May 2022 to May 2023. During this period, 139 (17.8%) patients had elevated cTnI levels at some point. However, the final outcomes of these elevated cTnI levels were unclear because these patients with elevated cTnI levels were only followed up every 6 months and monitored once or twice. Therefore, we conducted a prospective, single-center cohort study, using cTnI as a sensitive marker of myocardial necrosis to identify the different developmental processes of myocardial injury and their significance in patients with DMD, with the aim to optimize the long-term management of DMD-associated cardiomyopathy.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a prospective, single-center cohort study in which patients with DMD were enrolled between June 2023 and April 2024, and followed up until April 2025 at the West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of West China Second University Hospital (IRB#2023 − 303). The research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents and caregivers of the children with DMD prior to their participation in the study.

Diagnosis of DMD was confirmed by genetic testing and/or skeletal muscle pathology. The inclusion criteria were: (1) male patients with a diagnosis of DMD; (2) aged > 7 but < 18 years; and (3) regular follow-up. The exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of, or concern for structural or functional cardiovascular disease (congenital heart disease, cardiomyopathy); (2) other diseases that may affect the cardiovascular system (any secondary cardiovascular disease); and (3) unscheduled follow-up.

Data collection

This study was divided into two steps. First, we enrolled children with DMD who were regularly followed up (usually every 6 months) at our hospital between June 2023 and April 2024. We regularly monitored heart function-related indicators, including cTnI, ECG, echocardiography, and CMR images, in children aged > 7 years, as well as other relevant clinical data that were routinely followed, including age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), blood biochemical indicators, North Star Ambulatory Assessment score, steroid administration, cardioprotective drug usage, respiratory infection, respiratory function, and vaccination. Next, we conducted a dynamic follow-up of cTnI levels, ECG, cardiac ultrasound, and CMR until April 2025 (Fig. 1).

Follow-up

In the cTnI-N group, ECG, echocardiography, and CMR were routinely monitored every 6 months, whereas cTnI levels were monitored every 3 months. In the cTnI-H group, echocardiography and CMR were also routinely monitored every 6 months, while cTnI was monitored every month, the ECG was conducted immediately if the patient experienced any symptoms such as palpitations or chest tightness related to the heart, if the patients were hospitalized for treatment, they routinely undergo continuous monitoring of ECG, blood pressure and oxygen saturation; when the cTnI level returned to normal, the cTnI levels were monitored every 3 months. The follow-up period for elevated cTnI levels was at least 6 months, the median follow-up period was 12 months, and the frequency of monitoring for elevated cTnI levels was at least 3; otherwise, patients were excluded (Fig. 1).

Grouping and its definition

The normal cTnI level was 0.0–0.058 µg/L according to our hospital’s testing method, if the cTnI level was > 0.058 µg/L, it was a high cTnI level. Children were allocated into high and normal cTnI-level groups (cTnI-H and cTnI-N, respectively). The cTnI-H group was further subdivided into cTnI-H1 (cTnI elevation < 10-fold), cTnI-H2 (10-fold < cTnI elevation < 30-fold), and cTnI-H3 (cTnI elevation > 30-fold). Based on the dynamic changes in cTnI-H during follow-up, the children were further assigned into a cTnI-RTN group (returning to normal cTnI), a cTnI-RE group (repeated elevation of cTnI), and a cTnI-CE group (continuous elevation of cTnI). The “returning to normal, repeated elevation and continuous elevation” of elevated of cTnI levels were defined as “dropped to the normal range and remained consistently within the normal range, dropped to the normal range but then exceeded the normal range, and remained consistently above the normal range, respectively” during the follow-up period (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS/22.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The mean ± standard deviation (SD) present normally distributed data, while the median and interquartile ranges (IQRs) of 25%–75% present data that were not normally distributed. Categorical variables are described as relative frequencies (%). Comparisons between the groups of continuous variables were assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or Mann‒Whitney U test for abnormally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared between groups using chi-square test; a post hoc Bonferroni correction was performed for intragroup factors. Correlation analysis was used to for measuring the degree of correlation between two variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Population and clinical characteristics

A total of 174 children with DMD were enrolled and evaluated between June 2023 and April 2024. The average age, weight, height, and BMI was 10.34 ± 2.68 years, 31.50 kg, 131.36 ± 14.70 cm, and 19.45 ± 3.71 kg/m2, respectively. The cTnI-H and cTnI-N groups comprised 44 and 130 children, respectively; the positive rate for detecting elevated cTnI was 25.29%. In the cTnI-H group, 33 (75%), 7 (15.91%), and 4 (9.09%) children were in the cTnI-H1, cTnI-H2, and cTnI-H3 subgroups, respectively (Fig. 1). Regarding age distribution, 29.55% of the children ranged from 7 to 10 years, 52.27% ranged from 11 to 13 years, and 18.18% ranged from 14 to 16 years. With respect to motor function distribution, 22.73% of children lost their ability to independently walk. Regarding the hospitalization distribution, 25% of the children were hospitalized due to significantly elevated cTnI levels and received intravenous infusions of creatine phosphate sodium and vitamin C (VC).

In the cTnI-H group, only four (9.09%) children, all of whom were in the cTnI-H1, experienced chest tightness or accelerated heartbeats; the symptoms were mild. Compared with the cTnI-N group, the participants in the cTnI-H group were older (P < 0.001), heavier (P = 0.011), taller (P = 0.002), more likely to have clinical symptoms (P = 0.02), cardiac dysfunction at baseline (P = 0.002), abnormal ECGs (P = 0.003), abnormal CMRs (P < 0.001), more cardioprotective drug usage (P < 0.001), and a lower vaccination rate (P = 0.025). The other clinical characteristics were not significantly different between the two groups. A summary of the characteristics of the study population is presented in Table 1.

Compared with the cTnI-N group, the cTnI-H2 subgroup showed a greater proportion of atrial premature beats and T wave changes in ECG (P < 0.01), the cTnI-H1, cTnI-H2, and cTnI-H3 subgroups had a greater proportion of right axis deviations in the ECG (P < 0.01) (Table 2). The other ECG indicators were not significantly different between the two groups (Supplementary Materials).

Compared with the cTnI-N group, the cTnI-H1 group had a lower proportion of normal CMR (P < 0.001) and a greater proportion of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on CMR (P < 0.001), the cTnI-H1, cTnI-H2, and cTnI-H3 groups had a greater proportion, newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR (P < 0.001) (Table 2). LGE was more frequently observed in the free wall of the left ventricle and interventricular septum, and showed transmural changes in seven children: In six children were in the cTnI-H1 group and one in the cTnI-H3 group. The perfusion defects meant that the first-pass perfusion imaging showed microcirculation disorders in the subendocardial areas of the left ventricular free wall and the interventricular septum. Other indicators of CMR did not differ significantly between the two groups (Supplementary Materials).

Dynamic trends of different degrees of elevated cTnI levels

We conducted a dynamic follow-up of the cTnI-N and cTnI-H groups (cTnI-H1, cTnI-H2, and cTnI-H3 subgroups). 20 (15.38%) of the children in the cTnI-N group had newly elevated cTnI levels. In the cTnI-H1 subgroup, nine children were excluded according to the exclusion criteria, 17 (70.83%) children received VC, creatine phosphate treatment, or a combination of both; in one (5.88%) child, the cTnI level returned to normal; six (35.30%) children had repeated elevation of cTnI, and ten (58.82%) children had continuous elevation of cTnI; for the other seven (29.17%) children who did not receive treatment, in two (28.57%) children, the cTnI levels returned to normal, while two (28.57%) experienced repeated elevation of cTnI, and three (42.86%) experienced continuous elevation of cTnI levels. In the cTnI-H2 subgroup, two children were excluded according to the exclusion criteria, among five (100%) children who received treatment, one (20%) child showed a repeated elevation in cTnI level and four (80%) showed continuous elevation of cTnI. In the cTnI-H3 subgroup, all were included according to the inclusion criteria, among four (100%) children who received treatment, two (50%) experienced repeated elevation of cTnI, and two (50%) experienced continuous elevation of cTnI (Fig. 1).

Different degrees of elevated cTnI and progression of CMR

Compared with the cTnI-N group, the cTnI-H1, cTnI-H2, and cTnI-H3 subgroups showed a greater incidence of cardiac damage (P < 0.001). Among the children in the cTnI-H1, cTnI-H2, and cTnI-H3 subgroups, 63.64%, 57.13%, and 100% of the children exhibited newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR, respectively (Table 2).

Duration of elevated cTnI and progression of CMR

Of the children in the cTnI-H group, 19 (57.58%) exhibited persistently elevated cTnI levels throughout the follow-up period, irrespective of the treatment received. Six children (31.58%) lasted 7–10 months, 66.67% of whom had newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR; nine children (47.37%) lasted 11–14 months, 77.78% of whom had newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR; and four children (21.05%) lasted for 15 months, 100% of whom had newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Early identification of the different developmental processes of myocardial injury, particularly persistent progressive myocardial injury in patients with DMD, is crucial for delaying DMD-associated cardiomyopathy progression and improving overall outcomes. cTnI is generally recognized as a sensitive indicator of myocardial necrosis14–16. In our study, 25.29% of the children with DMD had elevated cTnI levels, among whom 75% had cTnI elevations < 10-fold, 15.91% had cTnI elevations > 10-fold but < 30-fold, and 9.09% had cTnI elevations > 30-fold, suggesting that there might be three different developmental processes of myocardial injury in patients with DMD according to the degree of the cTnI elevation.

During the follow-up period, 15.38% of the children in the cTnI-N group exhibited newly elevated cTnI levels. In the cTnI-H group, the elevated cTnI levels had decreased to normal in 9.09% of the children, exhibited repeated elevations in 33.33% of the children, had continuous elevations in 57.58%, and the longest duration of continuous elevations of cTnI levels was 15 months. These results suggest that the three different developmental processes of myocardial injury in patients with DMD might include transient, fluctuating and persistent injuries, which can alternate and fluctuate with each other. If the elevation in cTnI levels lasts for more than 1 month, myocardial cells may enter into the stage of chronic damage17. In our study, 57.58% of the children with DMD showed continuous elevations of cTnI levels for more than 1 month, suggesting that these children might have entered a state of persistent chronic damage after a stage of acute damage, aggravating the progression of DMD-associated cardiomyopathy.

The progression of LGE of CMR have been associated with myocarditis and worse prognosis in patients with myocardial injuries18–20. In our study, the cTnI-H group had a greater proportion of LGE and perfusion defects, and newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR, indicating a persistent progressive stage of DMD-associated cardiomyopathy and a worse prognosis. In addition, 100% of the children in the cTnI-H3 group exhibited newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR; the higher elevated cTnI levels were, the newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR were detected. When the elevation of cTnI levels lasted as long as 15 months, all had newer or expanded LGE and perfusion defects on CMR, indicating that the longer duration of elevated cTnI levels were, the more severe and persistent the progression of myocardial injury in children with DMD were found.

In our study, only 9.09% of the children in our study experienced chest tightness or accelerated heartbeat; the symptoms were mild in all cases; moreover, all children were in the cTnI-H1 subgroup. The vast majority of children with DMD with elevated cTnI levels do not present with any clinical symptoms (e.g., fever, chest pain, chest tightness, palpitations, or dyspnea)21. This inconsistency might be attributed to the fact that children with DMD have relatively low daily activity levels22, which reduce cardiac load and alleviate cardiac symptoms, complicating the early identification of silent myocardial injury in DMD-associated cardiomyopathy. Therefore, regular monitoring of cTnI levels can help identify different developmental processes, specifically persistent progressive myocardial injury, in DMD-associated cardiomyopathy.

Previous studies have revealed that recurrent episodic insults to dystrophin-deficient cardiac myocytes may strongly contribute to the progression of primary cardiomyopathy23,24. In our study, the participants in the cTnI-H group were older and heavier, indicating that cardiac myocytes with dystrophin deficiency may accumulate recurrent episodic insults with increasing age and weight, which were similar to the results of previous studies25. Therefore, the frequency of cTnI monitoring should be adjusted accordingly. In addition, our study revealed that the cTnI-H group had lower vaccination rates. Combined with the monitoring of cTnI, our study was carried out mainly in winter and spring, during seasons that may involve a high incidence of respiratory infection. This resulted in inflammatory immune response that might have triggered an increase in cTnI levels, probably explaining the higher positive rate of elevated cTnI levels in our study. Therefore, inquiring about symptoms related to respiratory infections, monitoring details on markers of infection including white blood cell (WBC) levels, respiratory viral panel results, Chest X-ray (CXR) are crucial for managing children with DMD children with elevated cTnI levels when there are any signs of respiratory infection, vaccinating children with DMD is also crucial.

The current DMD management guidelines recommend initiating cardioprotective therapy upon detection of cardiac abnormalities at any age12,26. DMD-associated cardiomyopathy may be delayed and improved with steroids and cardioprotective therapies (e.g., ACE inhibitors, β-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists)27–29. In our study, 62.15% of children with suspected DMD-associated cardiomyopathy routinely received cardioprotective therapy. In addition, the cTnI-H group had a greater proportion of cardioprotective therapy than did the cTnI-N group. Patients with significantly elevated cTnI levels received VC, creatine phosphate, or a combination. Nevertheless, the cTnI levels returned to normal in only a few children, whereas the vast majority showed a trend toward repeated or continuous elevation of cTnI levels. Therefore, optimizing the long-term management of DMD-associated cardiomyopathy, initiating or adjusting treatment in a timely manner, delaying cardiomyopathy progression, and improving overall outcomes.

In conclusion, based on the degree of cTnI elevation and outcomes of cTnI levels and CMR, there might be three different states of myocardial injury in DMD: transient injury, fluctuating injury, and persistent progressive injury. These states alternated and fluctuated with each other. The significant increase and long duration of elevated cTnI levels might be associated with persistent progressive myocardial damage. Regular monitoring (once a month for elevated cTnI levels) of cTnI levels might help identify persistent progressive myocardial damage at an early stage and optimize the long-term management of DMD-associated cardiomyopathy.

Our study has some limitations. First, although we evaluated a relatively large number of patients with DMD compared with previous studies, the number of participants might be insufficient to generalize our results to a larger cohort. However, the rarity of this muscle disease makes conducting research using larger samples difficult. Second, although this was a large prospectively enrolled DMD cohort to be followed up longitudinally, the participants were enrolled with varying degrees of cardiac dysfunction at baseline. We suspect that there might have been episodes of cardiomyocyte destruction and cTnI leakage that were not detected between visits due to the relatively short half-life of cTnI in circulation. Third, our study failed to reveal whether drugs such as steroids or cardiac medications could inhibit the release of cTnI or prevent the deterioration in cardiac function in children with DMD, as few children’s elevated cTnI levels returned to normal, with the vast majority children’s elevated cTnI levels showing a trend toward repeated or sustained elevation of cTnI. Therefore, further studies with more patients with DMD, unified standards for the follow-up time of cTnI, and appropriate treatment measures are needed.
