Adalimumab in refractory pediatric tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis syndrome: a case report of sustained renal-ocular remission and literature review
Authors: Danni Yang, Weibo Xi, Weiqin Liu, Wai Wilson Cheung, Jianjiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU) syndrome is a rare disease characterized by idiopathic acute tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis. To date, there are no standard therapeutic protocols. The renal damage may be clinically severe and persistent, and the uveitis tends to recur or progress to a chronic state, posing significant treatment challenges. We present a pediatric case of refractory TINU syndrome successfully treated with adalimumab, which provide a reference for its management.Case presentationA 7-year-old boy initially presented with mild proteinuria and acute kidney injury, accompanied by a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 57.5 mL/min/1.73 m². A kidney biopsy revealed acute tubulointerstitial nephritis. He was treated with oral prednisolone, with partial improvement of kidney function. Three months after the first symptoms, bilateral anterior uveitis developed, establishing the diagnosis of TINU syndrome. Treatment initially involved systemic glucocorticoids combined with mycophenolate mofetil, but the uveitis persisted with recurrent episodes refractory to treatment. The patient received a 10-month course of adalimumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). There was a significant resolution of ocular inflammation and normalization of kidney function, with a restored eGFR of 101.05 mL/min/1.73 m². Throughout the 1.25-year follow-up, no adverse events (including infections) occurred, and no recurrence was observed.ConclusionsAdalimumab can effectively control inflammatory responses and reduce recurrence in pediatric TINU syndrome, with a favorable safety profile, thereby providing a novel and promising biologic alternative for steroid-resistant or refractory cases. It is recommended to focus on ocular lesions in patients with tubulointerstitial injuries to avoid missing the diagnosis of TINU syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04535-z.Keywords: Tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis syndrome, TINU syndrome, Treatment, Adalimumab, Case report

Introduction

Tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU) syndrome is a rare inflammatory condition that mostly affects children and adolescents with a median age at onset of 15 years. The definite pathogenesis remains unknown. It is thought to be an immune-mediated process that can have multiple triggers, most notably infections or drugs. A shared antigen in kidney and ocular tissues may underlie the pathogenesis [1]. Owing to its low prevalence, there are no standard therapeutic protocols currently. Conventional therapies for TINU syndrome rely on systemic corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, which aim to control inflammation but are limited by variable efficacy, high relapse rates, and significant side effects such as growth suppression and metabolic disturbances. Tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitors (TNFi) have been widely used in the management of autoimmune disorders. Among them, adalimumab and infliximab have been used for years in clinical practice in treating non-infectious uveitis and achieved satisfactory effects and safety. Here, we describe a pediatric case of refractory TINU syndrome achieving sustained remission through combined adalimumab and immunosuppressants, alongside a literature review highlighting the therapeutic implications of TNFi in this population.

Case presentation

A 7-year-old boy presented with isolated mild proteinuria in the absence of purpura, edema, hematuria, or hypertension. He was previously fit and had no relatives with renal disease. There was no history of antibiotic or NSAID use preceding the onset, nor history of infection or other diseases.​ The physical examination findings were normal. Laboratory work up revealed 1 + proteinuria on routine urinalysis and a serum creatinine level of 0.96 mg/dL, corresponding to a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 57.5 mL/min/1.73 m², calculated using the Schwartz formula (eGFR = 0.413 × Height [cm] / Serum Creatinine[mg/dL]). The urine output was 874 ml/d, and the urine protein volume was 0.25 g/d.​​ The patient met the criteria for acute kidney injury stage 1, ​​as defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines.​ Indices of kidney tubular dysfunction were profoundly elevated, including urinary β1-microglobulin [64 mg/L (normal, 0–10 mg/L)] and β2-microglobulin [3.15 mg/L (normal, 0-0.3 mg/L)]. Serological testing for infections, immunological parameters, and autoantibody screening were unremarkable. Initial ophthalmologic exam was normal. The kidney biopsy revealed diffuse interstitial infiltrates composed of lymphocytes, monocytes, and scattered eosinophils, along with features of tubular injury such as vacuolar degeneration of epithelium, focal dilation, and brush border loss. These findings were diagnostic of acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (ATIN) (Fig. 1). The patient received oral prednisone therapy at an initial dose of 45 mg/day (1 mg/kg per day) for 8 weeks, then tapered by 5 mg every 1–2 weeks.

Three months after the first symptoms, the patient developed eye redness, pain, photophobia, and decreased visual acuity. Ophthalmic examination revealed conjunctival injection, keratic precipitates, anterior chamber cells (2+), and posterior synechiae. B-mode ultrasonography of both eyes revealed granular vitreous opacities. Fundus fluorescein angiography showed diffuse vascular leakage bilaterally, along with blurred optic disc margins. Bilateral anterior uveitis was identified. Additionally, the urinary β2-microglobulin levels persisted within 3.15–3.21 mg/L, suggesting ongoing tubular injury. TINU syndrome was diagnosed based on characteristic clinical and histopathological findings, following exclusion of other systemic inflammatory diseases. At this stage, the dosage of prednisone was tapered to 20 mg/day, and mycophenolate mofetil (500 mg, twice daily) was introduced as a combined therapy. Five months later, the tubular dysfunction exhibited modest improvement, evidenced by declining levels of urinary β1-microglobulin (14 mg/L) and β2-microglobulin (2.4 mg/L). However, the uveitis remained persistent with recurrent episodes. Upon ophthalmologic recommendation, subcutaneous adalimumab was initiated with a loading dose of 80 mg, followed by 40 mg every 2 weeks beginning the second week. Active infections, including hepatitis B and tuberculosis, were screened for and ruled out prior to the initiation of therapy. Following 6 months of treatment, the patient’s ocular inflammation had completely subsided and his kidney function had returned to normal with a restored eGFR of 101.05 mL/min/1.73 m² (Fig. 2). The urinary β2-microglobulin had decreased to 0.27 mg/L. The treatment was generally well tolerated and there were no severe side effects such as infections or anaphylaxis. Adalimumab was discontinued after 10 months of therapy due to the achievement of clinical remission, which was defined as the resolution of uveitis, normalization of urinary parameters and renal function. The patient remained recurrence-free throughout the 1.25-year follow-up.

Patient perspective

The patient described his experience with the treatment as follows: “I am very pleased to report that my vision has improved significantly following the therapy and regular medical check-ups. Previously blurry objects, such as the classroom whiteboard and text in my books, are now clear and sharp. I did not experience any adverse effects from the medication, such as stomach discomfort or other side effects. My mother told me that all of my urinalysis results were normal, which also makes me feel good. I am grateful that the treatment was effective and that I feel completely well. Overall, I feel very positive knowing that my eyesight has improved and that the doctors are satisfied with the results of my tests.”

Discussion

TINU syndrome is an uncommon and probably underrecognized condition. ATIN, which often precedes uveitis, typically presents with glomerular hypofiltration and proximal-distal tubular dysfunction, including renal glycosuria and elevated urinary β2-microglobulin levels. Kidney biopsy is the gold standard for ATIN, which is characterized by diffuse interstitial inflammatory infiltrates. Kidney and ocular diseases are asynchronous and may exhibit asymptomatic or atypical presentations, leading to underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis in clinical settings. TINU syndrome can be easily confused with other systemic diseases affecting the eyes and kidneys, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, dry syndrome, sarcoidosis, or juvenile idiopathic arthritis. The diagnosis of TINU syndrome, based on the diagnostic criteria established by Mandeville et al. [2], requires both ATIN and uveitis after excluding other systemic diseases known to be associated with ATIN or uveitis. Differential diagnosis should also include infection-induced acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and drug-induced interstitial nephritis (DIN). ATN manifests as oliguric acute renal failure, with milder interstitial injuries compared to ATIN. DIN is characterized by a history of medication exposure and systemic manifestations (e.g., fever, rash), with prominent interstitial eosinophilic infiltration.

To date, there are no standard therapeutic protocols. Corticosteroids remain the mainstay of treatment of TINU syndrome. Immunosuppressive agents are indicated for cases exhibiting frequent relapses, chronic uveitis, steroid resistance, or glucocorticoid intolerance (e.g., obesity, ocular hypertension). Mycophenolate mofetil and methotrexate are the most commonly used drugs. There is no reported significant difference in terms of therapeutic effectiveness of the different immunosuppressants. However, these therapies are often associated with significant side effects and uncertain effectiveness. Adalimumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting TNF-α, is mainly used in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, and noninfectious uveitis. In contrast, their use are seldom reported in the management of TINU syndrome. The patient described in this report, although presented with marked tubulointerstitial injury and refractory uveitis, had complete recovery of kidney function and resolution of ocular inflammation at the final follow­up. Adalimumab was administered for 10 months and was well tolerated with no evidence of infection.

We searched the following databases from their inception to April 31, 2025: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science、The Cochrane Library、China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI)、Wanfang Data、SinoMed and VIP database. The search strategy combined both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, using the following search string: ((“Tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis syndrome”) OR (“TINU syndrome”)) AND ((“TNF-α inhibition”) OR (“Adalimumab”) OR (“Infliximab”) OR (“Etanercept”) OR (“Golimumab”)) AND ((“child”) OR (“adolescent”) OR (“pediatrics”)). Review of the literature reveals several case reports that describe patients who treated with adalimumab or infliximab (Table 1). It was found that patients at a young age, male, and with higher grades of uveitis at presentation were more likely to require additional biologic therapy. There was a high rate of clinical remission following TNFi initiation in patients refractory to conventional immunosuppressive therapy. Tirelli et al. [3] analyzed four adolescents with refractory TINU syndrome treated with TNFi. All rapidly achieved inflammatory quiescence and maintained prolonged inactivity after treatment. Studies have shown a slightly greater response to adalimumab than infliximab (87% vs. 72%; p = 0.08) in children with uveitis, though the difference was not statistically significant [4]. In patients who failed to respond to adalimumab, switching to infliximab or interleukin-6 receptor inhibitors (e.g., tocilizumab) may be attempted [5]. Among patients we retrieved, no severe adverse events were reported except for a boy who stopped adalimumab due to complications of skin abscess, suggesting a favorable safety profile [6].

The ATIN in TINU syndrome was often described as self-limiting and with a good prognosis, with normal serum creatinine levels restored within 4–8 weeks. ​​However, recent studies have shown that renal function does not always fully recover, particularly in patients with treatment delays secondary to late diagnosis.​ Data from adult cohorts showed that at 1-year follow-up, 37% of patients progressed to CKD stage 2, 23% to stage 3, and 9% to stage 4 [10]. A cohort study of 46 children with TINU syndrome indicated that only 43% achieved complete recovery of kidney function, defined by an eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m² without proteinuria and high blood pressure, at last follow-up (median 2.8 years) [11]. Renal prognosis seemed to be correlated with the severity of kidney involvement at diagnosis and was independent of uveitis. Notably, patients presenting with severe low-molecular-weight proteinuria at diagnosis experienced longer durations of kidney function normalization [12].​​ Persistent surveillance of renal functional is warranted in patients with severe tubular injuries at the onset.​ In this case, the final eGFR of 101.05 mL/min/1.73 m² exhibited marked improvement from baseline, yet sustained monitoring remains imperative to assess long-term nephritis outcomes.

In conclusion, we report a boy with refractory TINU syndrome successfully treated with adalimumab, thereby providing an effective and promising biologic alternative for steroid-resistant or refractory cases. Nevertheless, the single-case, retrospective design inherently limits generalizability of these findings to broader TINU syndrome populations, and the relatively short follow-up duration precludes assessment of long-term outcomes such as late adverse effects or disease relapse. Future prospective studies with large-scale cohorts and extended follow-up are needed to further establish the therapeutic utility of TNFi in this disorder. Given its atypical presentations and underestimated prevalence, enhanced recognition of TINU syndrome remains a clinical priority; we recommend routine screening for ocular lesions in patients with tubulointerstitial injuries to avoid diagnostic delays.​.
