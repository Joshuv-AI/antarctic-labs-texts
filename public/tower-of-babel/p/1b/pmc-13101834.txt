Effects of HO-1 on the Integrity of Retinal Pigment Epithelial Cells in Regulating Axial Elongation in Experimental Myopia
Authors: He-Yan Li, Yu-Hang Yang, Li Dong, Rui-Heng Zhang, Wen-Da Zhou, Hao-Tian Wu, Jiao-Yue Dong, Chu-Yao Yu, Shan-Shan Wang, Wen-Bin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe study aimed to explore the potential involvement of the heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) protein in myopic axial elongation. Tight junctions (TJs) form the structural basis of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier functions. Although oxidative stress contributes to myopia, it remains unclear how HO-1 influences TJ integrity in RPE cells.MethodsGuinea pigs aged 2 to 3 weeks underwent bilateral lens-induced myopization (LIM) and received intraperitoneal injections (i.p.) of the HO-1 activators hemin 50 mg/kg, with or without inhibitor ZnPP 50 mg/kg. Ocular biometry, optical coherence tomography, and fundus photography were performed. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and confocal microscopy were used to assess TJ changes in myopic guinea pigs. ARPE19 cell cultures were treated with NaIO3 or ZnPP that regulated HO-1 expression. TJ integrity was examined by anti–zonula occludens 1 (ZO-1) and antioccludin staining of cultured cells, flatmount RPE tissues, and average length from the TEM pictures. Western blot analysis was used to evaluate protein expression levels.ResultsEyes subjected to LIM and LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) treatment exhibited significantly greater axial elongation than those in the control group (0.71 ± 0.03 mm and 0.68 ± 0.04 mm vs. 0.49 ± 0.06 mm, respectively), accompanied by increased HO-1 protein expression during the study period. No significant differences in axial length or refractive error were observed between the LIM and LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) groups. In contrast, eyes in the LIM + hemin (i.p.) group showed the greatest axial elongation (0.81 ± 0.04 mm), whereas eyes treated with LIM + ZnPP (i.p.) demonstrated inhibition of HO-1 overexpression and a marked attenuation of axial elongation (0.54 ± 0.05 mm). ZnPP treatment also preserved TJ integrity and mitigated retinal and choroidal thinning. Mechanistically, TJ structures were compromised in association with elevated HO-1 expression but were rescued by the HO-1 inhibitor ZnPP. Immunofluorescence analysis identified RPE cells as the primary site of HO-1 activation. Moreover, HO-1 overexpression resulted in downregulation of the TJ proteins ZO-1 and occludin, a finding that was further confirmed by Western blot analysis.ConclusionsHigher expression of HO-1 caused axial elongation and the development of refraction errors in guinea pigs. TJs were destroyed with myopia development and higher HO-1 expression. ZnPP, as an HO-1 inhibitor, may alleviate myopia development and TJ destruction in guinea pigs and RPE cells.

The prevalence of high myopia is projected to surge to approximately 938 million cases by 2050, establishing it as one of the leading causes of vision loss worldwide.1,2 High myopia is associated with a substantially increased risk of ocular complications that can progress to pathologic myopia, and nearly half of highly myopic adults are expected to develop this sight-threatening condition.3–5 Without effective intervention, pathologic myopia may result in irreversible visual impairment or even blindness.4 Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying axial elongation is crucial for developing strategies to halt or slow the progression of myopia.

The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), a monolayer of cuboidal cells, is essential for maintaining photoreceptor structure and function. Disruption of RPE junctional integrity has been implicated in the development of choroidal neovascularization and macular degeneration observed in both high myopia and age-related macular degeneration (AMD).6,7 Tight junctions (TJs), located at the apical region of the junctional complex, form the structural foundation of the outer blood–retinal barrier.8 Key TJ-associated proteins include the cytoplasmic scaffold proteins zonula occludens (ZO)−1, −2, and −3, as well as the transmembrane protein occludin.9 ZO-1 links transmembrane components to the cytoskeleton and thereby regulates paracellular permeability and maintains stability of TJs in RPE cells. Through its intracellular interactions with ZO-1 and ZO-2 and extracellular homophilic binding between adjacent cells, occludin contributes to the formation of a selective permeability barrier.10 However, the mechanisms by which TJs contribute to RPE degeneration remain incompletely understood.

Oxidative stress is a major driver of RPE dysfunction. An imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defense systems can result in cellular injury and degeneration.11 Heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), encoded by HMOX1, catalyzes the degradation of heme into biliverdin, carbon monoxide, and free iron.12,13 Interestingly, while HO-1 induction has been reported to promote ferroptosis, other studies indicate that HO-1 overexpression can mitigate ferroptotic cell death.7,14 The complicated role of HO-1 in diseases has aroused interest in studying its mechanisms in different models. In guinea pig and rat models, the HO-1 activator hemin and its inhibitor ZnPP have been extensively employed and administered via intraperitoneal injection at doses ranging from 10 to 100 mg/kg to study the underlying mechanisms of various ocular diseases, including AMD, diabetic retinopathy, and uveal melanoma.15–17 However, the specific role of HO-1 in regulating TJ integrity within retinal pigment epithelial cells, as well as its impact on axial elongation in guinea pigs with high myopia, remains unclear.

This study aims to elucidate the relationship between TJ integrity and redox homeostasis in myopia. Using a lens-induced myopia guinea pig model and RPE cell experiments, we investigated the potential protective effects of ZnPP on RPE TJs and its ability to mitigate myopia progression in guinea pigs with high myopia.

Methods

Animals

Guinea pigs aged 2 to 3 weeks were housed in the Laboratory Animal Department of Beijing Tongren Hospital. Each animal was identified with an ear tag prior to the start of the experiment, and its body weight ranged between 150 and 200g. Guinea pigs were housed in standard polycarbonate cages (length × width × height: approximately 60 × 40 × 30 cm), with solid bottoms covered by autoclaved paper-based bedding. The cages were maintained in a specific pathogen–free animal facility under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 20°C–24°C; relative humidity: 40%–60%; 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle; noise under 60 dB). Animals were housed in groups of two to three per cage to allow social interaction. Environmental enrichment was provided in the form of shelters, gnawing blocks, and hay to encourage natural behaviors. All animals had easy access to standard guinea pig chow supplemented with vitamin C and fresh water. Cage cleaning and bedding replacement were performed two to three times per week. All animal procedures adhered to the guidelines of CALAS (AFLAS member, ARVO-recognized) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital (TREC2023-KY076).

Lens-Induced Myopia Model and Grouping

Following the approach reported by Noordzij et al., the sample size was determined using preexperimental data, where the standard deviation (σ) was 0.05. With a type I error (α) of 5% and τ = 10 representing the number of planned comparisons, the required sample size was calculated as n = 10.18 Based on this, animals were allocated into experimental and control groups.

Guinea pigs received HO-1 activator hemin and inhibitor ZnPP through intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections. Lens-induced myopization (LIM) was induced by fitting −10.0 diopter (D) goggles on both eyes, targeting a myopic shift of approximately −6.0 to −7.0 D. Goggles were secured to the orbital rims, allowing the animals to open and blink freely, and were checked daily. If needed, they were removed, cleaned, and reattached. During periodic examinations, the goggles were temporarily taken off and then reapplied. The study included two experimental protocols: 1)To examine the pharmacokinetics of an intraperitoneal injection of hemin and ZnPP, the concentrations of the agents were examined in the ocular tissues and plasma of LIM guinea pigs. The dose injection of the two drugs included 10, 50, and 100 mg/kg for the two agents, respectively. The pharmacokinetic analysis assessed the concentrations in plasma, whole ocular tissue, and RPE–choroid complex (n = 3 for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours after injections) (supplementary methods).2)To assess the effects of hemin (i.p.) and ZnPP (i.p.) in LIM guinea pigs, the agents were injected intraperitoneally every day based on the pharmacokinetic profiles and the estimated elimination half-life. The guinea pigs were randomly assigned to five groups: control + PBS (i.p.), LIM + PBS (i.p.), LIM + hemin 50 mg/kg + ZnPP 50 mg/kg (i.p.), LIM + hemin 50 mg/kg (i.p.), and LIM + ZnPP 50 mg/kg (i.p.). In the LIM + hemin + ZnPP group, ZnPP was injected before hemin. Body weight was measured weekly. The control and LIM + PBS groups were given PBS (i.p.) weekly according to body weight. The experiment lasted 4 weeks, and animals were euthanized on day 29.

Refractive State and Ocular Biometrics

Eye examinations and axial length assessments were carried out at baseline and on days 8, 15, 22, and 29. Baseline data were collected immediately prior to the initial injection on day 1. Each week at 2:00 PM, axial length was determined using ocular ultrasound biometry (A/B-mode, oscillator frequency 11 MHz; Quantel Co., Les Ulis, France) under topical anesthesia with oxybuprocaine hydrochloride eye drops (Santen Co., Osaka, Japan). For every guinea pig within each group, 10 consecutive readings were obtained. When the standard deviation of these readings was below 0.10 mm, the average value was taken forward for statistical analysis. All eye examinations were conducted by two board-certified ophthalmologists specializing in retinal diseases, each with more than 3 years of clinical experience at Beijing Tongren Hospital (H.Y.L; Y.H.Y; W.D.Zh).19,20

Images Collection and Analysis

At both the start and conclusion of the experiment, guinea pigs from each group were randomly chosen for optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging at 2 PM without anesthesia. Bilateral swept-source OCT (VG200D; SVision Imaging, Henan, China; laser wavelength 1050 nm) was performed using a star-scan protocol centered on the optic disc. Retinal and choroidal thicknesses were recorded 1000 µm temporal to the optic disc in the horizontal section (from 3 to 9 o'clock) and 1000 µm superior and inferior to the optic disc in the vertical section (from 6 to 12 o'clock). Fundus images were acquired using a wide-field mode fundus camera (ZEISS CLARUS 500; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany).

Tissue Collection

After the animals were euthanized, eyeballs from each group were removed, and the cornea, iris, and lens were excised. The remaining eyecup was gently cleaned and inspected under a microscope before being dissected into the sclera, neurosensory retina, and RPE–choroid complex. Only the RPE–choroid complex was used for Western blot detection. Samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at −80°C, and analyzed within 1 week. For histologic assessment, intact eyeballs were immersed in 10 mL FAS Eyeball Fixative Solution (Wuhan Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China) for 24 hours, then embedded in paraffin.

Liquid Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry

Well-prepared plasma and ocular tissue samples (supplementary methods) were processed using protein precipitation with cold acetonitrile containing an internal standard. After vortexing and centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 minutes at 4°C, the supernatants were subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a reverse-phase C18 column using a gradient elution with water and acetonitrile, both containing 0.1% formic acid. Detection was conducted in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring. Quantification was achieved using matrix-matched calibration curves, and the method demonstrated acceptable linearity and reproducibility within the concentration ranges relevant to in vivo exposure.

Histologic and Toxicologic Analysis

Paraffin-embedded eyes were sectioned at an 8-µm thickness and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE). Retinal thickness in different regions was measured using a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) in combination with Slideviewer software (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). Apoptotic cells were identified by TUNEL staining, carried out with a Cell Death Detection Kit (Kaiji Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). For each group, three retinal sections were imaged, and TUNEL-positive cells in the ganglion cell layer, inner nuclear layer, and outer nuclear layer at the posterior pole were counted. In addition, guinea pigs were periodically examined by ophthalmoscopy during the study to detect any signs of intraocular inflammation or other abnormalities. A total of three sections per eye were evaluated, and the mean value from the sections was used for statistical evaluation. Histopathologic assessment was conducted by a board-certified ocular pathologist from the Pathology Department at Beijing Tongren Hospital in a masked fashion (W.B.W).

Cell Culture and Oxidative Stress Induction

ARPE19 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (CRL-2303; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. The culture medium was Gibco Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM)/F-12 medium (11330057; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Gibco Antibiotic-Antimycotic (100×) (15240096; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Approximately 4 hours before the addition of experimental chemicals, culture media were replaced with FBS-free DMEM/F-12. Chemical stocks were made in PBS, and final concentrations in the DMEM/F-12 media were as follows: 0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mM sodium iodate (NaIO3) and 5 µM ZnPP. The duration of chemical treatments was 16 hours unless otherwise specified. The dosing regimen and treatment duration were determined based on prior literature and preliminary experiments to balance efficacy and cell viability11 (supplementary methods).

Immunofluorescence

After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, RPE tissue samples and cultured ARPE-19 cells were washed three times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes at room temperature. The sections or cell monolayers were then blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 minutes and incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody against HO-1 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA; 1:200 dilution). After three PBS washes, samples were incubated with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (AiFang Biology, Hebei, China; 1:1000 dilution) for 1 hour at 37°C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1 µg/mL) for 5 minutes at room temperature. The sections were examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS-CKX53; Olympus). The samples were mounted using an antifade mounting medium and observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG).

Transmission Electron Microscope

The eyeballs of the animals were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative (Wuhan Servicebio Technology) for 10 minutes. The globes were pierced with a syringe through the equator, and the anterior segment of the eye and the lens were removed. The eye wall of the posterior part was cut into small pieces of approximately 1 mm2 in size, which were placed in fresh fixing solution in the dark and at room temperature. After approximately 2 hours at room temperature, the samples were stored at 4°C. The tissues were coated with epoxy resin, and we cut ultrathin sections 60 nm thick using the Leica EM UC7 (Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL, USA). The sections were examined using a Hitachi transmission electron microscope system (Hitachi, Japan), and images were taken.

Western Blot Analysis

RPE–choroid tissues and ARPE-19 cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Amresco, Solon City, OH, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 minutes and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 minutes at 4°C to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of total protein (20–40 µg) were separated on 8% to 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 to 2 hours at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Millipore) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Detailed information on the antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Unless otherwise specified, continuous data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed Student's t-test, while comparisons among more than two groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Pharmacokinetics of Intraperitoneal Injection of Hemin and ZnPP in LIM Guinea Pigs

To investigate the ocular effects of the intraperitoneal administration of hemin and ZnPP in LIM guinea pigs, we evaluated the pharmacokinetics of both agents at doses of 10, 50, and 100 mg/kg (Fig. 1). The maximum concentration (Cmax) of hemin in the RPE–choroid complex reached 0.28 ± 0.06, 0.65 ± 0.02, and 0.69 ± 0.02 µg/mL following intraperitoneal injection at 10, 50, and 100 mg/kg, respectively. Correspondingly, the Cmax values of ZnPP were 0.23 ± 0.01, 0.57 ± 0.02, and 0.61 ± 0.01 µg/mL at the same dose levels. At 24 hours after intraperitoneal administration, detectable concentrations of both agents persisted in the RPE–choroid complex, indicating tissue accumulation of hemin and ZnPP. Considering both efficacy and safety, a dose of 50 mg/kg (i.p.) was selected for subsequent experiments, as its Cmax and AUC values did not differ significantly from those observed at 100 mg/kg.

Pharmacokinetics of 10, 50, and 100 mg/kg hemin and ZnPP in guinea pigs after intraperitoneal injections. The agents’ concentrations were recorded in plasma (A, D), whole eye tissue (B, E), and RPE–choroid complex tissues (C, F) for hemin and ZnPP, respectively.

The main pharmacokinetic parameters of hemin and ZnPP in plasma and whole ocular tissues following intraperitoneal injection are summarized in Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Table S4. Notably, the Cmax and AUC values in the whole ocular tissue and RPE–choroid complex demonstrated preferential distribution to the RPE–choroid complex, indicating that this tissue represents the primary ocular target of hemin and ZnPP after intraperitoneal administration. Based on the plasma elimination half-lives of hemin (23.30 hours) and ZnPP (39.56 hours), a once-daily dosing regimen was selected to ensure sustained systemic and ocular exposure without excessive accumulation.

HO-1 Overexpression in LIM Guinea Pigs

After 4 weeks of intraperitoneal injection of hemin 50 mg/kg (i.p.) or ZnPP 50 mg/kg (i.p.), guinea pigs exhibited distinct effects on axial elongation and refractive error during the study period (Table). Axial elongation was significantly attenuated in the ZnPP-treated group, whereas it was exacerbated in the hemin-treated group. Specifically, axial length increased from baseline to the end of the study as follows: from 8.00 ± 0.06 mm to 8.49 ± 0.04 mm in the control + PBS (i.p.) group, from 7.98 ± 0.05 mm to 8.69 ± 0.03 mm in the LIM + PBS (i.p.) group, from 8.00 ± 0.02 mm to 8.68 ± 0.03 mm in the LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) group, from 8.00 ± 0.04 mm to 8.81 ± 0.02 mm in the LIM + hemin (i.p.) group, and from 7.99 ± 0.04 mm to 8.53 ± 0.04 mm in the LIM + ZnPP (i.p.) group (Table and Supplementary Table S1). Correspondingly, axial elongation was 0.49 ± 0.06 mm, 0.71 ± 0.03 mm, 0.68 ± 0.04 mm, 0.81 ± 0.04 mm, and 0.54 ± 0.05 mm in the control + PBS (i.p.), LIM + PBS (i.p.), LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.), LIM + hemin (i.p.), and LIM + ZnPP (i.p.) groups, respectively (all P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). Changes in refractive status from baseline to study end were as follows: from 3.4 ± 0.70 D to −0.4 ± 0.52 D in the control + PBS (i.p.) group, from 3.4 ± 0.70 D to −6.4 ± 1.0 D in the LIM + PBS (i.p.) group, from 3.8 ± 0.63 D to −6.3 ± 0.95 D in the LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) group, from 3.6 ± 0.52 D to −7.8 ± 0.92 D in the LIM + hemin (i.p.) group, and from 3.5 ± 0.71 D to −4.2 ± 0.92 D in the LIM + ZnPP (i.p.) group (Table and Fig. 2B). Body weight remained stable across all groups throughout the study (P > 0.05; Fig. 2C). Histologic analysis using HE staining revealed no significant differences in posterior retinal thickness among groups, except for the LIM + hemin (i.p.) group, in which the retina was significantly thinner than in all other groups (P < 0.05; Figs. 2D–E). TUNEL staining showed no significant differences in apoptotic cell density among the groups (all P > 0.05; Figs. 2F–H).

Retina and Choroid Atrophy in LIM Guinea Pigs

OCT images revealed thinner posterior retina (1000 µm temporal to the optic disc) with the development of myopia from baseline to study end as follows: 118.63 ± 3.58 µm to 132.08 ± 1.69 µm in the control + PBS (i.p.) group, 117.85 ± 3.48 µm to 111.68 ± 2.96 µm in the LIM + PBS (i.p.) group, 119.75 ± 3.69 µm to 111.83 ± 2.69 µm in the LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) group, 120.50 ± 3.59 µm to 107.83 ± 1.87 µm in the LIM + hemin (i.p.) group, and 119.00 ± 2.63 µm to 122.25 ± 2.38 µm in the LIM + ZnPP (i.p.) group (Figs. 3A–C). Thickness of the posterior choroid (1000 µm temporal to the optic disc) also decreased with the development of myopia from baseline to study end as follows: 100.73 ± 0.95 µm to 127.70 ± 1.25 µm in the control + PBS (i.p.) group, 101.40 ± 2.43 µm to 104.67 ± 3.80 µm in the LIM + PBS (i.p.) group, 100.30 ± 0.95 µm to 100.40 ± 1.67 µm in the LIM + hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) group, 99.40 ± 1.85 µm to 96.30 ± 1.37 µm in the LIM + hemin (i.p.) group, and 98.67 ± 2.91 µm to 110.10 ± 4.57 µm in the LIM + ZnPP (i.p.) group (Figs. 3A, 3B, 3D). Fundus photography showed that myopic guinea pigs had atrophy of the retina and choroid and RPE pigment changes. Guinea pigs in the LIM + hemin group showed characteristic tessellated retina and lacquer cracks, which corresponded to refraction errors of −7.8 D (Figs. 3E–I). Compared to the control group, well-defined choroidal vessels at the posterior pole were observed in guinea pig eyes with refraction errors of −6.0 to −7.0 D. These well-defined choroidal vessels shared similar characteristics of fundus tessellation in myopic human eyes.

Effects of HO-1 Upregulation on ZO-1 and Occludin Expression

Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the elevated expression of HO-1 protein in the RPE cells of myopic guinea pigs, together with the downregulation of ZO-1 and occludin proteins in LIM + PBS (i.p.) and LIM + hemin (i.p.) groups compared to the control group (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, Western blot analysis in the RPE-choroid tissue also showed that HO-1 protein expression was upregulated with axial elongation, while the expression level of ZO-1 and occludin was downregulated correspondingly in LIM + PBS (i.p.) and LIM + hemin (i.p.) groups compared to the control group (Figs. 4B–E). Four-week treatment with hemin + ZnPP (i.p.) or ZnPP (i.p.) attenuated the upregulation of HO-1 and the downregulation of ZO-1 and occludin, as well as alleviated myopia development and axial elongation at different levels (Figs. 4A–E). These results indicate that RPE cells were the primary target of HO-1 protein regulation in myopia development, which influenced the integrity of TJs in the cell.

Tight Junction Destruction in RPE Cells in LIM Guinea Pigs

RPE flatmount analysis revealed the differences in en face cell morphology among different groups. Inhibition of HO-1 Overexpression Protects TJs in ARPE19 Cells

To investigate whether inhibition of HO-1 overexpression by ZnPP exerts protective effects on TJs in RPE cells, sodium iodate (NaIO₃, 2 mM) was used to induce HO-1 expression in ARPE-19 cells. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed to evaluate the expression and distribution of the TJ proteins ZO-1 and occludin in ARPE-19 cells among the control, NaIO₃-treated, and NaIO₃ + ZnPP treatment groups (Figs. 7A, 7B). In addition, the protein expression levels of HO-1, ZO-1, and occludin were assessed by Western blot analysis (Figs. 7C–F). The results showed that NaIO₃ treatment significantly upregulated HO-1 expression, which was accompanied by a marked downregulation of ZO-1 and occludin. In contrast, ZnPP pretreatment effectively attenuated NaIO₃-induced HO-1 overexpression and preserved ZO-1 and occludin expression. Consistent with these findings, no significant reduction in ZO-1– and occludin-positive cell junctions was observed in the NaIO₃ + ZnPP group compared with the control group, as evidenced by the preserved junctional localization of these proteins (Figs. 7A–F).

Discussion

The present study employed guinea pigs to investigate the mechanisms underlying axial elongation in the development of high myopia. By establishing an LIM model using −10.0 D lenses, we successfully simulated axial elongation and refractive changes that closely resemble the pathologic features observed in human high myopia.20 After 4 weeks of lens induction, the guinea pigs developed significant myopia, with an average refractive error of approximately −6.4 D and an axial length of 8.69 mm. Previous studies have demonstrated that axial elongation during the development of high myopia involves retinal and choroidal atrophy, RPE damage, and scleral thinning and remodeling, which together form a vicious cycle that drives progressive axial elongation.19 The general understanding has proven that the RPE function could be damaged by oxidative stress, but there is little knowledge on how the integrity of TJs is affected by oxidative stress, especially in the high myopia guinea pig model. In the present study, we successfully established an LIM guinea pig model treated with hemin (i.p.) or ZnPP (i.p.) at different doses. The pharmacokinetic analysis proved the efficiency of the two agents targeting the RPE tissues. Also, the medium, safest dose of 50 mg/kg was used to investigate the mechanisms of the effects of HO-1 on axial elongation in LIM guinea pigs.

Structurally, TJs are formed through high-affinity interactions between the extracellular domains of occludins/claudins in neighboring cells and are stabilized by zona occludens, such as ZO-1, which link the intracellular domains of occludins/claudins to cytoskeletal fiber actin filaments.21 Occludin, a key transmembrane TJ protein, directly contributes to paracellular sealing and regulates selective permeability across the RPE monolayer.22 Functionally, TJs serve as a critical barrier that prevents harmful molecules, pathogens, and circulating cells in the choroidal vasculature from entering retinal tissues.23,24 Disruption or downregulation of either ZO-1 or occludin compromises RPE barrier integrity, leading to increased paracellular permeability and dysregulation of ion and fluid homeostasis. In several cultured cell models, including bovine aortic endothelial cells, colonic epithelial cells, and renal tubular epithelial cells, exposure to ROS-generating agents such as NaIO₃ has been shown to induce TJ disassembly.23,25,26 Emerging evidence suggests that such RPE dysfunction may be relevant to the pathophysiology of myopia.27 Thus, disruption of TJ integrity may indirectly promote axial elongation by altering RPE-dependent fluid dynamics and scleral extracellular matrix homeostasis.28,29 Therefore, alterations in ZO-1 and occludin expression may not only reflect RPE stress but also actively contribute to the microenvironmental changes associated with myopic progression.

Excessive activation of HO-1 in the RPE appears to play an important role in impairing epithelial barrier integrity and may contribute to the pathologic processes underlying myopic axial elongation.17 Although HO-1 is classically considered a cytoprotective enzyme that mitigates oxidative stress, sustained or dysregulated overexpression can shift cellular homeostasis toward excessive production of ROS and iron overload, ultimately triggering oxidative injury in RPE cells.30,31 Such oxidative disturbance compromises the stability of TJ proteins, particularly ZO-1 and occludin, which are essential for maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the outer blood–retinal barrier. When HO-1 overexpression drives junctional disassembly or mislocalization of ZO-1 and occludin, the RPE barrier becomes leaky, leading to disturbed ion balance, impaired fluid regulation, and altered signaling between the retina, RPE, and choroid.32 Standard cultures of the ARPE-19 cell line readily exhibit many of the characteristics of well-differentiated RPE cells, including a cobblestone-like appearance, apical-basal polarity, and expression of ZO-1 and occludin.33 The ARPE-19 cell line exhibits stable TJs and a cobblestone-like appearance compared with the induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)–derived RPE cells, making it widely used in ROS and TJ studies. However, the iPSC RPE cells show greater variability depending on donor tissue source and characteristics, and the differentiation process is costly and time-consuming.34

These barrier defects may have direct consequences for ocular growth. The RPE plays a central role in controlling choroidal fluid flux and extracellular matrix turnover in the sclera—two processes known to influence axial elongation during myopic development.35 A compromised RPE barrier may enhance pro-elongation signals, promote remodeling of the scleral extracellular matrix, and facilitate the biomechanical weakening that characterizes progressive myopia.36,37 Therefore, HO-1–induced TJ disruption could represent a mechanistic link between oxidative stress and abnormal ocular growth regulation. Myopia has been shown to disrupt endothelial barrier properties and decrease the expression level of ZO-1 and occludin. Facilitation of such factors across the blood–retinal barrier would also encourage neovascularization, which occurs in high myopia.38,39 These observations are in agreement with the electron microscopic findings obtained in the present study, suggesting that the disruption of RPE cells may locally enlarge Bruch's membrane and trigger axial elongation eventually.28 Further investigation is needed to determine whether reinforcing RPE TJs could provide a therapeutic strategy for slowing or preventing myopic progression.

However, our study has some limitations. First, the process of axial elongation may be influenced by the type of goggles used to induce myopization. The −10 D goggles applied in our study may differ in their effect from −6.0 D goggles or diffuse lenses. Second, although ARPE-19 cells have been widely used in eye research and general epithelial cell studies, the limitations of ARPE-19 cells compared with primary RPE cells or iPSC-derived RPE cells should be noted, including incomplete differentiation and reduced pigmentation. However, primary human RPE cells and iPSC RPE cells exhibit significant heterogeneity among cultures from different donors or sources. A significant obstacle to using primary human RPE cultures is the scarcity of donor tissue. We should have more investigations on epithelial mechanisms in different in vitro models. Third, the integrity of the TJs may not represent the function of the TJs, and further studies of cell permeability to investigate the barrier function should be conducted.40

In conclusion, ZnPP application may alleviate axial elongation in guinea pigs with high myopia. The underlying mechanisms include the downregulation of HO-1 expression in RPE cells, which may reduce ROS production and oxidative effects in RPE degeneration, a potential therapeutic target for axial elongation. In addition, oxidative reaction caused by the overexpression of the HO-1 protein may destroy the integrity of the TJs in the RPE cells, leading to a vicious cycle of RPE degeneration and axial elongation.
