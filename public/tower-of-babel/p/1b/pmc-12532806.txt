Morphometric similarity network-based graph convolutional networks for schizophrenia classification
Authors: Hye Won Park, Won Hee Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Schizophrenia is a complex neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by significant heterogeneity, posing a challenge for accurate classification using neuroimaging data. Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have emerged as a promising approach for leveraging the inherent graph structure of brain connectivity to discriminate between patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. However, existing GCN-based methods often struggle to capture the subtle neuroimaging differences associated with the disorder. To address these limitations, we propose a novel GCN framework (MSN-GCN) that integrates morphometric similarity networks (MSN) derived from structural MRI scans. Our method involves constructing individual brain graphs based on multiple morphometric features, including cortical thickness, surface area, gray matter volume, mean curvature, and Gaussian curvature. These individual graphs are then combined into a population-level graph that incorporates both topological and phenotypic information. By employing a variational edge learning approach, our model adaptively optimizes the edge weights to capture the complex relationships between brain structure and schizophrenia. We evaluated our proposed method on a large, multi-site dataset comprising 377 schizophrenia patients and 590 healthy controls. Experimental results demonstrate superior classification performance compared to state-of-the-art methods, achieving a mean accuracy of 80.85%. Notably, the superior temporal gyrus emerged as a key region contributing to classification. Significant differences in the clustering coefficient of the superior temporal gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and lateral occipital cortex between patients and healthy controls, and their correlations with negative symptoms were detected in post-hoc analyses. These findings demonstrate the potential of MSN-GCN for accurate schizophrenia detection and provide valuable insights into the neural correlates of the disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19894-8.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a debilitating mental disorder characterized by hallucinations, delusions, and impaired cognitive function, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life1,2. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for effective treatment and improved outcomes3. However, the complex and heterogeneous nature of schizophrenia symptoms poses a significant challenge for early detection, as traditional diagnostic methods primarily rely on clinical assessments, which can be subjective and often lead to delayed or inaccurate diagnoses4. Therefore, there is an urgent need to establish an objective approach for an accurate diagnosis of schizophrenia.

To address these limitations, there has been a growing interest in developing objective and data-driven methods for schizophrenia classification using neuroimaging data. While traditional machine learning methods have been explored, their reliance on handcrafted features extracted from neuroimaging data often limits their generalizability across different datasets and rely on expert knowledge to define relevant features5,6. Recent advancements in deep learning have shown promise in extracting complex features directly from neuroimaging data for automatic brain disease diagnosis7,8. However, conventional deep learning methods typically treat subjects independently, neglecting potential relationships between individuals.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) offer a potential solution by modeling data as interconnected graphs, enabling the capture of complex relationships and interactions among subjects9–14. Among GNN variants, graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have gained prominence due to their ability to preserve graph structure while learning discriminative features9. GCNs have shown success in various medical image classification tasks, including schizophrenia10, Alzheimer’s disease11,12, and autism spectrum disorder13,14.

While previous studies have explored the use of functional brain networks for schizophrenia classification10,15,16, there is growing interest in utilizing morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) derived from structural MRI data. MSNs have shown potential in capturing individual differences in brain structure and predicting cognitive variation17,18. Furthermore, MSNs have been used to identify patterns of abnormal brain morphology associated with psychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder, Alzheimer’s disease, and mild cognitive impairment19–21. However, the clinical utility of MSNs for schizophrenia classification remains largely unexplored.

This study aims to investigate the effectiveness of GCNs in classifying schizophrenia patients using morphometric similarity networks derived from structural MRI data. By leveraging the power of GCNs to model complex relationships between subjects and incorporating rich information from MSNs, we aim to develop a robust and accurate classification model for schizophrenia. Related work

This section provides an overview of relevant research, including morphometric similarity networks (MSNs), deep learning-based methods for schizophrenia diagnosis, and graph neural networks (GNNs).

Morphometric similarity networks

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) as a valuable tool for understanding brain structure and function. By capturing the similarity of cortical regions based on morphometric features, MSNs have shown promise in predicting individual differences in cognitive abilities17. Notably, aberrant MSNs have been linked to various neurological and psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia22, autism spectrum disorder21, and Alzheimer’s disease19. While these studies have provided valuable insights, they often focus on specific brain regions or employ limited feature sets, hindering their generalizability. Moreover, the full potential of MSNs for schizophrenia classification remains largely unexplored.

Deep learning for schizophrenia diagnosis

The advent of deep learning has revolutionized medical image analysis, including schizophrenia diagnosis. Studies have employed deep neural networks (DNNs) to classify schizophrenia patients and healthy controls, uncovering abnormalities in functional connectivity23,24. Furthermore, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have excelled in extracting discriminative features from neuroimaging data, leading to improved classification performance25–28.

However, deep learning approaches for schizophrenia diagnosis face several challenges. Data scarcity and heterogeneity can limit model performance and generalizability29,30. Additionally, the black-box nature of deep neural networks often hinders interpretability, making it difficult to understand the underlying mechanisms of classification31. While CNNs have excelled in capturing spatial patterns in neuroimaging data, they may not fully exploit the complex relationships between brain regions.

While deep learning methods have shown promise in schizophrenia diagnosis8,32, they primarily rely on neuroimaging data, neglecting potentially valuable non-imaging information. Incorporating clinical assessments or demographic factors could provide additional insights into disease progression and treatment response33. Furthermore, deep learning models typically focus on individual-level analysis, overlooking potential inter-subject relationships that could be exploited for improved classification. By addressing these limitations, future research can develop more comprehensive and clinically relevant models for schizophrenia diagnosis.

Graph neural networks for disease classification

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing complex relationships in medical image data9. In the context of brain disorders, GNN-based approaches can be broadly categorized into regional brain graph and population graph models.

Regional brain graph models focus on analyzing the connectivity patterns within individual brains. For example, Lei et al.10 demonstrated the efficacy of such models in distinguishing patients with schizophrenia from healthy controls. Similarly, Qin et al. proposed a graph convolution network (GCN) model to classify patients with major depressive disorder, while Gao et al.34 developed a graph model based on functional connectivity to predict the brain age of Alzheimer’s patients. While these models have achieved success in identifying disease-related brain regions, they often overlook inter-subject variability and the potential benefits of population-level analysis.

On the other hand, population graph models consider relationships between subjects, enabling the identification of shared disease patterns. Recent studies have explored population graph construction using affinity scores or correlation distances between subjects for autism14,35 and Alzheimer’s disease14,35. However, these methods often rely on hand-crafted similarity measures and may not fully capture the complex interactions between subjects. To address these limitations, some studies have proposed adaptive graph learning approaches36,37. For instance, Huang et al.36 proposed an edge variational graph model, which adaptively updates the graph’s edges during training. This model successfully classified autism based on functional connectivity and ocular disease using CNN-based feature extraction. Zhang et al.37 introduced a local-to-global graph convolutional network for brain disease classification.

Despite these advancements, existing GNN-based methods primarily focus on functional connectivity and have limited exploration of morphometric similarity networks. Additionally, there is a need for more effective integration of non-imaging information to improve classification accuracy. Our proposed method aims to address these challenges by leveraging MSNs to construct a population graph and incorporating both structural and phenotypic information. By combining the strengths of GNNs and MSNs, we aim to develop a more robust and informative framework for schizophrenia classification.

Methods

Figure 1 provides an overview of our proposed classification framework. For each subject, we construct individual morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) by calculating the inter-regional similarity of various morphometric features extracted from T1-weighted MRI scans. These MSNs are used to formulate a population graph model, where nodes represent graph-theoretical measures of the MSNs, and edges denote the similarity between the topological features of subjects, incorporating phenotypic information. A threshold is applied to the population graph to eliminate spurious connections. The edge weights are adaptively refined using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)-based encoder that leverages non-imaging data. Learning is achieved through spectral graph convolutions, followed by MLP-based classification for schizophrenia detection. We employ a semi-supervised approach where all node features are input into the graph convolutional network (GCN). During training, only a subset of nodes is labeled and used in the optimization process.Fig. 1Overview of our proposed classification framework.

Datasets

In this study, structural T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were obtained from six public databases (Supplementary Table S1), including DecNef (n = 326, 138 females, age range 18–78 years)38, COBRE (n = 172, 41 females, age range 18–65 years)39, CANDI (n = 49, 21 females, age range 5–16 years)40, MCICShare (n = 203, 56 females, age range 18–60 years)41, UCLA CNP (n = 163, 70 females, age range 21–50 years)42, and CCNMD (n = 43, 14 females, age range 13–30 years)43. After quality control, a total of 967 subjects were included in the analysis, comprising 377 patients with schizophrenia and 590 healthy controls. Ethical approvals and informed consents were obtained locally for each study, covering both participation and subsequent data sharing. Detailed sample and demographic information are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Morphometric similarity network construction

Structural T1-weighted MRI scans underwent standardized preprocessing using the recon-all pipeline in FreeSurfer (version 7.2.0)44. The preprocessing pipeline included removal of non-brain tissue, automated Talairach transformation, intensity normalization, skull stripping, segmentation of subcortical white matter and deep gray matter structures, tessellation of the gray-white matter boundary, and automated topology correction. Surface-based registration was performed to align each subject’s reconstructed cortical surface to the fsaverage template. We implemented strict quality control measures following the ENIGMA protocol (https://enigma.ini.usc.edu/protocols/imaging-protocols/), with visual inspection performed at each processing stage to assess segmentation and surface reconstruction outputs (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Preprocessing pipeline for constructing morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) from T1-weighted MRI. structural MRI images are processed using FreeSurfer, followed by cortical parcellation, feature extraction, and correlation-based network construction.

Preprocessing pipeline for constructing morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) from T1-weighted MRI. structural MRI images are processed using FreeSurfer, followed by cortical parcellation, feature extraction, and correlation-based network construction.

Cortical parcellation was performed using the Desikan–Killiany atlas, dividing the cortex into 308 regions22,45. For each cortical region, we extracted five morphometric features (gray matter volume, surface area, cortical thickness, Gaussian curvature, and mean curvature). This resulted in 1540 features per subject (308 regions × 5 measures). These morphometric features were standardized to zero mean and unit standard deviation. Individual MSNs were then constructed as 308 × 308 correlation matrices, where each matrix element represents the Pearson correlation coefficient between the standardized morphometric features of two regions, calculated across all five morphometric measures17,22 (Fig. 2).

Node feature extraction and selection

To effectively characterize individual subjects within the population graph, we developed a systematic approach for feature extraction from MSN. The original MSN data presents a significant dimensionality challenge, with 47,278 features in the upper triangle of each network matrix. To address this, we implemented a robust dimensionality reduction strategy focused on preserving essential network properties.

To capture the essential topological properties of these complex networks, we computed a suite of graph-theoretical measures using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (BCT)46. Specifically, we quantified node strength, betweenness centrality (BC), eigenvector centrality (EC), and clustering coefficient (CC) for each brain region. Strength represents the sum of the weights of edges connected to a given node, reflecting the node’s influence within the network. BC is defined as the fraction of all shortest paths in the network that pass through a given node, revealing its role in network information flow within the network46. EC measures a node’s importance by considering both the quantity and quality of its connections, accounting for the influence of neighboring nodes. Finally, CC assesses network segregation by quantifying the fraction of triangles around a node, indicating the presence of cliques or clusters47. These measures collectively provide a comprehensive representation of node influence, network centrality, and community structure within the MSN. By aggregating these graph-theoretical measures for all cortical regions, we constructed a 308-dimensional feature vector for each subject. This transformation effectively reduced data dimensionality while maintaining the critical topological information necessary for subsequent analyses.

To further optimize the feature set and enhance model predictive power, we implemented two complementary feature selection techniques: recursive feature elimination (RFE)48 and k-best selection with analysis of variance (ANOVA)49. RFE is an iterative process that ranks features based on their contribution to predictive performance. By progressively removing the least informative features, it identifies a subset that maximizes model accuracy and interpretability. In our study, we used a ridge classifier to assess feature importance and guide feature elimination. Alternatively, k-best selection with ANOVA offers a univariate approach to feature ranking. By evaluating the statistical significance of differences between groups for each feature, ANOVA identifies those features that exhibit the strongest associations with the target variable. The k-best selection method selects the top-ranking features based on their F-values, providing a computationally efficient means of dimensionality reduction. Through the combined application of these feature extraction and selection strategies, we aimed to construct a robust and informative representation of subjects that would serve as the foundation for subsequent graph-based analysis and classification tasks.

Graph edges

A critical aspect of our population graph construction was the definition of an edge weighting scheme that effectively captures both feature-based similarities and phenotypic relationships between subjects. Our approach combined initial static similarity metrics with dynamic weight adaptation to optimize the graph’s neighborhood structure for subsequent graph convolutional operations.

We implemented our edge weight calculation using a two-step approach (Fig. 3). Initially, we constructed a baseline graph by computing edge weights from subject feature vector similarities, establishing fundamental relationships in the population structure. Building on this foundation, we incorporated a learnable function that dynamically adjusts edge weights during model training36. This adaptive mechanism enables the model to discover and refine complex subject relationships that may not be apparent from feature similarities alone. The integration of phenotypic information into our edge weight calculations, combined with dynamic weight adjustment, allows our graph convolutional network to capture subtle but meaningful associations between subjects. This approach significantly improves upon static graph models, leading to enhanced classification performance in schizophrenia detection36. All trainable parameters in these edge encoders are initialized using He initialization54 and jointly optimized with the graph convolutional network in an end-to-end training process.

Spectral graph convolutions

To extract spatial features from each graph node, we employed spectral graph convolution, which is a technique commonly used in previous studies focused on brain disease diagnosis14,35,36. In spectral graph analysis, the graph Laplacian is represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L=D-W$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D$$\end{document} is a diagonal degree matrix with elements defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\left(i,i\right)={\sum }_{j}W\left(i,j\right)$$\end{document}. The Laplacian can be diagonalized using the Fourier basis U, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L=U\Lambda {U}^{T}$$\end{document}, where U is a matrix of eigenvectors of the normalized graph Laplacian, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda$$\end{document} is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues56. The spectral convolution is defined as the multiplication of the graph signal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document} with a filter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${g}_{\theta }=diag\left(\theta \right)$$\end{document}, parametrized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{\theta } *x = Ug_{\theta } \left( \Lambda \right)U^{T} x$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$*$$\end{document} denotes the convolution operator. However, performing eigenvector decomposition is computationally expensive. To address this, we used Chebyshev polynomials to reduce computational complexity. The Chebyshev polynomials are computed recursively as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{k}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)=2\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}{T}_{k-1}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)-{T}_{k-2}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)=1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{1}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}$$\end{document}.

The trainable parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Theta }_{k}^{l}$$\end{document} serve as node feature transformers.

Our proposed MSN-GCN model consists of a graph convolutional network with L hidden layers. Each hidden layer is followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce non-linearity. To prevent overfitting, we applied dropout to the hidden layers. The output layer is fully connected and includes two convolutional layers with 256 and 2 channels, respectively.

We trained our semi-supervised GCN model using the entire population graph, a methodological choice that offers significant advantages for neuroimaging-based schizophrenia classification. The semi-supervised approach is particularly valuable in this context due to several factors: First, the acquisition of labeled neuroimaging data in psychiatric populations is often constrained by clinical recruitment challenges, resource limitations, and ethical considerations, resulting in relatively modest sample sizes despite our substantial multi-site dataset of 377 schizophrenia patients and 590 healthy controls. Second, schizophrenia is characterized by considerable neuroanatomical heterogeneity, making it essential to learn from diverse brain morphometric patterns beyond just the labeled examples.

In our implementation, while the loss function was computed based solely on the labeled nodes and backpropagation was employed to update the parameters, the unlabeled test nodes were actively incorporated during the training phase. This allowed the unlabeled data to meaningfully influence the convolutional representations learned from the labeled data, creating a more comprehensive model of brain morphometric patterns associated with schizophrenia. By leveraging both labeled and unlabeled data, our approach mitigates overfitting risks inherent to high-dimensional neuroimaging features and enhances the model’s ability to generalize across the neuroanatomical variability present in schizophrenia. The performance of the model was evaluated using these test nodes that had been incorporated into the learning process but not directly used for loss calculation. Cross-entropy was used as the loss function for optimization.

Identifying the most salient regions contributing to classification

To gain insights into the specific brain regions contributing to schizophrenia classification, we employed GNNExplainer57, a model-agnostic method for interpreting GNN-based predictions. GNNExplainer operates by optimizing a mask over input node features to maximize the mutual information between the masked graph and the GNN’s output. This process identifies a subset of node features that are most critical for the model’s decision-making. By applying GNNExplainer to our model, we generated explanations for individual node predictions, highlighting the most influential features for each subject. To quantify the overall importance of each brain region, we calculated the average feature importance across all subjects. The top 10 brain regions with the highest average feature importance were considered to be the most relevant for schizophrenia classification. This analysis provided valuable information about the brain regions that drive the model’s predictions, enhancing our understanding of the underlying neural mechanisms associated with schizophrenia.

Statistical analysis

To assess the statistical significance of differences in graph-theoretical measures between healthy controls and schizophrenia patients, we conducted permutation tests using the SciPy library58. For each of the top 10 salient regions identified through the GCN model, we iteratively (10,000 times) computed the difference in graph-theoretical measures between randomly permuted groups of patients and controls. This process generated a null distribution of differences under the assumption of no group effect. By comparing the observed difference to the null distribution, we calculated p-values and corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure59. Graph-theoretical measures with an FDR-corrected p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Correlation analysis

To investigate the association between clinical symptom severity and graph-theoretical measures of the identified salient regions, we conducted a general linear model analysis. As the multi-site dataset employed various clinical rating scales, including the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptom (SAPS)60, the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS)61, and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)4, we standardized symptom severity measures by converting SAPS and SANS scores into equivalent PANSS positive and negative subscale scores using a validated conversion equation62. This allowed for a consistent assessment of symptom severity across datasets. Clinical symptom severity was measured by the converted PANSS scores in the MCICShare, UCLA CNP, and CCNMD datasets, and by the original PANSS scores in the COBRE and DecNef samples. Due to the absence of clinical score information in the CANDI dataset, this dataset was excluded from the clinical correlation analysis.

We formulated a general linear model to assess the association between the PANSS scores and various graph-theoretical measures within the sample population (n = 351). Specifically, the clinical symptom severity for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} participant was modeled as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PANSS_{i} = B_{0} + G_{i} B_{1} + age_{i} {\text{B}}_{2} + age_{i} G_{i} {\text{B}}_{3} + sex_{i} B_{4} + sex_{i} G_{i} {\text{B}}_{5} + Site_{i} {\text{B}}_{6} + e_{i}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PANS{S}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${G}_{i}$$\end{document} represent the PANSS scores and the graph-theoretical measures of the i-th participant, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document} denotes the fitted regression coefficients, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e}_{i}$$\end{document} is the error term. The model was independently applied to each graph-theoretical measure for salient brain regions. Separate models were fitted to evaluate the relationship between the PANSS positive and negative subscales and the graph measures. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the FDR correction across the independent tests59. This analysis allowed us to identify significant associations between clinical symptom severity and specific graph-theoretical measures within the salient brain regions.

Experiments

Experimental settings

To evaluate the proposed MSN-GCN model, we employed a five-fold pooled stratified cross-validation strategy. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five equal-sized folds, ensuring balanced representation of both schizophrenia and healthy controls within each fold. In each iteration, one-fold served as the holdout test set, while the remaining four-folds were combined to form the training set. This process was repeated five times, with each fold evaluated as a test set once. Model evaluation was conducted across five independent iterations, with the results averaged to obtain a robust performance estimate. A comprehensive set of metrics was utilized to evaluate classification performance: balanced accuracy (BAC), precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). BAC, calculated as the average of sensitivity and specificity, addresses the potential imbalance between classes, providing a more reliable performance indicator. Precision, recall, and F1-score offer complementary insights into the model’s ability to correctly classify positive and negative instances. AUC provides an overall assessment of the model’s discriminative power.

In our experiments, we implemented the proposed MSN-GCN with carefully tuned hyperparameters. The architecture consisted of three hidden layers with channel dimensions [128, 64, 32], complemented by a Chebyshev polynomial of order K = 3 and a quantile level of Q = 0.6. For optimization, we employed the Adam optimizer with a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler that dynamically adjusted between 0.0001 and 0.01. To mitigate overfitting, we incorporated regularization techniques including a weight decay of 5 × 10–5 and dropout rate of 0.2. The model was trained for 300 epochs using PyTorch Geometric framework on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU.

Competing methods

To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed MSN-GCN model, a comparative analysis was conducted against a suite of established classification algorithms. Traditional machine learning methods, including support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN), served as benchmarks for comparison. These models were implemented using the scikit-learn library49 and optimized through a nested cross-validation process to identify optimal hyperparameters. Additionally, the MSN-GCN model was compared to state-of-the-art GNN-based approaches, namely, population GCN14, edge variational GCN (EVGCN)36, local-to-global GNN (LGGNN)37, and MMP-GCN63. To ensure a fair comparison, feature extraction and selection methods were applied consistently across all graph-based models. A deep neural network (DNN) was also included as a non-graph-based baseline for comparison64. All models were evaluated using a five-fold cross-validation strategy to assess generalization performance. Model performance was quantified using balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC.

To investigate the neural underpinnings of schizophrenia, we compared graph-theoretical measures between patients and healthy controls within the identified salient brain regions. Association between clinical symptoms and graph-theoretical measures

To explore the relationship between clinical symptoms and graph-theoretical measures, we conducted general linear model (GLM) analyses. Our results showed a significant association between PANSS positive symptom scores and clustering coefficients in several key brain regions, including the lateral occipital cortex (p = 0.024), cuneus cortex (p = 0.029), superior temporal gyrus (p = 0.015), and pericalcarine cortex (p = 0.015). Additionally, a significant correlation was found between PANSS positive symptom scores and eigenvector centrality in the lateral occipital cortex (p = 0.049). These findings suggest that alterations in network topology within these regions may be associated with the severity of positive symptoms in schizophrenia.

We conducted additional GLM analyses focusing on the PANSS negative symptom subscale. Our results showed significant associations between negative symptom severity and clustering coefficients in multiple brain regions, including the superior temporal gyrus (p = 0.003), postcentral gyrus (p = 0.028), lateral occipital cortex (p = 0.004), cuneus cortex (p = 0.004), and pericalcarine cortex (p = 0.003). Furthermore, eigenvector centrality in the postcentral gyrus (p = 0.009) and strength in the superior frontal gyrus (p = 0.009) and postcentral gyrus (p = 0.016) were significantly associated with negative symptom severity. These findings suggest that alterations in network topology within these regions may be associated with the severity of negative symptoms in schizophrenia. To evaluate the determinants of our model’s classification performance, we conducted a series of analyses targeting key methodological components. Our investigation systematically examined the impact of multiple critical elements, including MSN density, feature selection strategies, edge encoder architecture, and the influence of non-imaging phenotypic variables. Through methodical manipulation and assessment of these components, we sought to elucidate their individual and collective contributions to the model’s predictive accuracy.

Influence of MSN connection density on model performance

To assess the influence of connection density on MSN-based classification, we systematically varied the density of the MSNs from 10 to 100% in 10% increments. For each density level, we computed standard graph-theoretical measures (strength, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, and clustering coefficient) and evaluated their impact on classification performance. The results, visualized in Fig. 5, show the average balanced accuracy across five-fold cross-validation for each combination of density and graph-theoretical measure, providing insights into the optimal network topology for schizophrenia classification. Clustering coefficient demonstrated the most robust performance across varying densities, with an optimal accuracy of 81.19% achieved at 40% connection density. In contrast, strength exhibited significant sensitivity to density, peaking at 81.03% accuracy at 80% density but deteriorating to 71.08% at maximum density. Eigenvector centrality and betweenness centrality showed moderate performance variations, with highest accuracies of 80.6% and 80.24%, respectively. Eigenvector centrality demonstrated a moderate sensitivity to connection density, with optimal performance at 90% density (80.6% accuracy) and a decline to 75.17% at 50% density. Betweenness centrality exhibited a similar trend, peaking at 80.24% accuracy at 30% density and decreasing to 72.82% at 50% density. Overall, both eigenvector centrality and betweenness centrality showed less consistent performance compared to clustering coefficient and strength, suggesting that these measures might be less robust to variations in connection density. Discussion

In this study, we proposed a novel GCN framework that effectively leverages the topological properties of morphometric similarity networks derived from structural MRI data and non-imaging phenotypic data for schizophrenia classification. By integrating graph-theoretical measures into the GCN architecture, we aimed to improve the classification accuracy and provide deeper insights into the neural underpinnings of the disorder.

Model performance

Our study demonstrated the superior performance of the proposed MSN-GCN model in distinguishing schizophrenia patients from healthy controls, surpassing both traditional machine learning methods and existing GCN-based approaches. The incorporation of the clustering coefficient as a node feature proved particularly effective, highlighting the critical importance of carefully selected graph-theoretical measures in optimizing classification performance. These findings suggest the potential of GCNs to uncover complex neural patterns associated with schizophrenia, offering valuable insights into the disorder’s underlying brain network alterations.

The robustness of our results is supported by our carefully considered methodological framework. Our optimization strategies were deliberately tailored to address the distinct characteristics and computational demands of each model architecture. For deep learning models like our GCN, which operate in high-dimensional parameter spaces, we employed manual search as a theoretically sound and computationally efficient approach that balances thorough exploration with practical constraints. While conventional classifiers benefit from exhaustive techniques like grid search with nested cross-validation due to their lower-dimensional parameter spaces, applying such methods to deep learning architectures would be computationally prohibitive and could potentially compromise model performance. This differentiated optimization strategy reflects established best practices in the field and ensures a fair comparison that appropriately considers the inherent properties of each model class. To validate the robustness of our findings, we conducted extensive experiments demonstrating that the MSN-GCN’s superior performance remains consistent across multiple random initializations and validation splits. This consistency confirms that our results are not artifacts of the optimization strategy but rather reflect genuine improvements in the model’s ability to capture and classify complex brain network patterns associated with schizophrenia.

Ablation studies and model optimization

To assess the impact of key model components on classification performance, we conducted a series of ablation studies. First, we investigated the influence of MSN density on model accuracy. Our results indicate that clustering coefficient exhibited the most stable performance across different connection densities, with optimal performance at 40% connection density. In contrast, strength demonstrated significant sensitivity to density changes. Second, we compared the effectiveness of different feature selection strategies. Graph-theoretical measures consistently outperformed RFE and k-best selection, highlighting the importance of carefully selecting informative features for improved classification. Finally, we evaluated the impact of edge encoders on classification performance36,53. The PAE encoder emerged as the top-performing option, particularly when combined with clustering coefficient. However, the choice of edge encoder was found to interact with the specific graph-theoretical measure employed, underscoring the need for careful consideration of these factors in model development. These findings collectively emphasize the importance of careful model design, including feature selection and edge encoding, for optimizing the performance of GCN-based models in schizophrenia classification.

Impact of data harmonization on classification performance

The harmonization of multi-site neuroimaging data presents a critical challenge in maintaining data quality while preserving biological variations of interest. To address potential site-specific effects in our analysis, we implemented ComBat harmonization using the neuroCombat package, which employs an empirical Bayes framework to adjust for site-specific technical variations while preserving biological variability66. We selected ComBat over alternative approaches based on several key considerations: its established reliability across multi-site schizophrenia studies67, its effective preservation of disease-related signals critical for classification68,69, its transparent statistical framework facilitating interpretation of morphometric features70, and its computational efficiency that complemented our GCN architecture. These properties made ComBat particularly suitable for harmonizing the morphometric features prior to constructing our similarity networks while maintaining the discriminative information needed for accurate patient-control classification.

Our comparative analysis revealed that classification performance remained largely stable before and after harmonization. For clustering coefficient features, we observed minimal changes in the key performance metrics: balanced accuracy showed a slight decrease (80.85 to 80.64%) after harmonization, while the AUC exhibited a marginal improvement (91.01 to 91.19). Similar trends were observed across other network metrics, with minimal variations (detailed results in Supplementary Table S3). The modest impact of harmonization on classification performance suggests that our initial methodology effectively controlled for site-related confounds. These findings indicate the robustness of our approach across different preprocessing pipelines. Future work could explore more sophisticated harmonization approaches, such as GAMLSS71, deep learning-based methods66, or multi-site attention graph techniques63, particularly when dealing with larger, more heterogeneous datasets or when combining data across different scanning protocols. Such methodological comparisons could further optimize the performance and generalizability of GCN-based classification models across diverse acquisition protocols.

Salient brain regions and clinical correlations

GNNExplainer analysis identified the superior temporal gyrus (STG) as a key region contributing to schizophrenia classification, revealing its crucial role in disease-specific network alterations. This finding aligns with and extends previous neuroimaging research documenting structural abnormalities in the STG among patients with schizophrenia72,73. Specifically, prior studies have reported reduced gray matter volume in the left STG, with these reductions showing significant negative correlations with the severity of hallucinations and thought disorders74,75. The involvement of the STG is particularly noteworthy, as it plays a crucial role in auditory processing, language comprehension, and social cognition—core functions that are often disrupted in schizophrenia76,77. Moreover, studies of early-onset schizophrenia have demonstrated significant volumetric deficits in the STG, particularly in the right hemisphere, suggesting that these alterations may reflect underlying neurodevelopmental disruptions72. Our network-based findings provide a novel perspective on STG involvement in schizophrenia by characterizing its altered connectivity patterns within the broader context of brain network organization.

Our graph-theoretical analysis showed significant alterations in brain network organization, particularly within the STG and its associated regions. The analysis of the clustering coefficient revealed marked disruptions in local network architecture. This metric, which quantifies how tightly interconnected neighboring nodes are within the network78, showed substantial reductions in the STG region. These findings align with and extend previous graph-theoretical investigations in schizophrenia, which have reported decreased clustering coefficients in the STG – a region critical for auditory processing and higher-order cognitive functions76,79. The observed reduction in clustering coefficient within the STG network indicates compromised local information processing efficiency, potentially contributing to the characteristic auditory processing abnormalities and cognitive deficits in schizophrenia76,80.

The observed correlations between graph-theoretical measures, such as clustering coefficient, and clinical symptom severity, particularly the association with PANSS symptom scores, align with emerging evidence supporting the utility of these measures as biomarkers for schizophrenia progression and treatment response. Studies have shown that alterations in graph-theoretical metrics are linked to symptom severity. For example, reduced clustering coefficients in key brain regions, such as the STG, are associated with impaired information processing and correlate with positive symptoms such as delusions and hallucinations as measured by PANSS79,81 Reduced structural covariance within networks involving the STG and other temporal regions has been directly associated with persistent auditory hallucinations. The temporal pole of the left STG often serves as a critical hub in these networks79. Additionally, the STG has been highlighted as a potential biomarker for schizophrenia due to its structural and functional abnormalities, including reduced gray matter volume and hypoconnectivity in both early and chronic stages of the illness82. Lower clustering coefficients and reduced small-worldness in the STG are correlated with PANSS positive scale scores, suggesting that these disruptions may reflect impaired local network efficiency and dysregulated information processing in schizophrenia79,83,84. These findings suggest the potential utility of graph-theoretical measures as biomarkers for disease progression. They could aid in identifying subgroups of patients with distinct neurobiological profiles, facilitating personalized treatment strategies and better prediction of clinical outcomes.

Comparison with existing methods

For node features, our model employed graph-theoretical measures, including strength, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, and clustering coefficient, to construct node representations that capture critical topological properties of the brain connectivity graph. In contrast, population GCN85, EVGCN36, and MMP-GCN63 relied on recursive feature elimination, which iteratively eliminates less informative features but may overlook important topological properties. LGGNN took a different approach by using regional brain embeddings from MRI data, focusing primarily on local connectivity patterns within specific brain regions37.

Our approach constructed the initial population graph by computing subject-wise similarities using both imaging and non-imaging features. These complementary similarity measures were integrated into a unified affinity matrix that forms the foundation for graph construction. We implemented an adaptive quantile-based thresholding method that retains the top 60% most significant connections, ensuring robust graph structure across different data distributions. This approach contrasts with population GCN, which used predefined graph structures without explicit thresholding, and EVGCN/LGGNN, which employed fixed threshold values. Our adaptive thresholding strategy provides better generalization across diverse datasets and maintains optimal graph connectivity by automatically adjusting to varying data characteristics.

We developed our model using variational edge learning with adaptive edge weights, enabling dynamic optimization of edge importance during training. The edge encoder utilizes a 64-dimensional hidden layer configuration, achieving an optimal balance between computational efficiency and model expressiveness. This architecture differs from population GCN, which relied on static similarity metrics with fixed edge weights, limiting its ability to capture evolving relationships. While EVGCN and LGGNN also employed variational edge learning, they used larger 128-dimensional encoders, which increase computational complexity without proportional gains in performance. Our more compact architecture maintains modeling capacity while improving computational efficiency.

The core architecture of our model comprised three GCN layers with progressively reducing hidden dimensions [128, 64, 32], enabling efficient hierarchical feature learning through systematic dimension reduction. We implemented Chebyshev graph convolution (ChebGCN) to capture higher-order graph dependencies effectively. This architecture contrasts with population GCN’s single-layer design (16 dimensions), which limits hierarchical feature extraction, and EVGCN/LGGNN’s four-layer architectures16,16,16,64. While EVGCN enhanced ChebGCN with a fusion block and LGGNN incorporated adaptive weight aggregation for multi-scale feature fusion, our progressive dimension reduction strategy achieves comparable feature representation while maintaining computational efficiency.

For optimization, we implemented the Adam optimizer with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule ranging from 0.0001 to 0.01. This adaptive learning strategy enables better convergence and helps avoid local optima during training. In contrast, population GCN (0.005), EVGCN (0.01), LGGNN (0.01), and MMP-GCN (0.005) utilized Adam with fixed learning rates, which may limit their ability to adapt to varying optimization landscapes and potentially impact model convergence.

Limitations, methodological considerations, and future directions

While this study demonstrates the potential of MSN-GCN for schizophrenia classification, several limitations warrant consideration. The generalizability of our findings may be constrained by the sample size and demographic characteristics of the included population. Although our dataset is relatively large compared to prior studies in the field86,87, the potential for overfitting remains a concern due to the high dimensionality of the features. Additionally, our reliance solely on T1-weighted images represents a limitation, as other MRI modalities could provide valuable complementary information for classification. Various imaging sequences capture distinct aspects of brain pathology, each offering unique insights into neural alterations associated with schizophrenia86,87. Furthermore, our current approach does not fully address the neuroanatomical heterogeneity of schizophrenia, including recently identified subtypes characterized by distinct patterns of gray matter alterations88–90. The focus on cortical regions, as defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas, may limit the capture of subcortical contributions to disease pathology. Moreover, the influence of medication status and comorbidities on brain structure and symptom expression was not explicitly accounted for in this study. Future research should address these limitations through several approaches. First, findings should be validated in larger, more diverse cohorts to enhance generalizability. Despite our cross-validation approach, we acknowledge that external validation would provide stronger evidence of generalizability. The high-dimensional nature of our feature space relative to sample size necessitates cautious interpretation of our findings. While our regularization strategies help mitigate overfitting risks, future studies should prioritize validation using independent, demographically diverse cohorts to further establish the broad applicability of our model. Second, the MSN framework could be extended to incorporate features from multiple imaging modalities while developing advanced fusion techniques. Third, integrating subcortical measures and investigating alternative parcellation schemes could provide additional insights into disease mechanisms. Fourth, longitudinal studies will be essential to track brain network alterations over time and their association with clinical outcomes. Finally, medication status and comorbidities should be accounted for through comprehensive statistical modeling. These extensions of our work could enhance both model performance and clinical utility while deepening our understanding of schizophrenia’s neurobiological basis. Furthermore, studies with larger samples and comprehensive morphometric measurements will be crucial for validating neuroanatomical subtypes and understanding their clinical implications, potentially advancing the field toward more personalized diagnostic approaches.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel graph convolutional network (GCN) framework, leveraging morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) to discriminate between schizophrenia patients and healthy controls. By integrating graph-theoretical measures and employing an optimal edge encoder, our model achieved a balanced accuracy of 80.85%, surpassing traditional machine learning algorithms and other GCN-based approaches. The findings underscore the importance of clustering coefficient as a key feature for capturing discriminative information within brain networks. Moreover, the identification of salient brain regions, including the superior temporal gyrus, provides valuable insights into the neural correlates of schizophrenia. The proposed MSN-GCN framework holds promise as a valuable tool for aiding in the diagnosis and prognosis of schizophrenia. By integrating multimodal data and refining model architectures, future research can further enhance the diagnostic capabilities of these models. Ultimately, this work contributes to the development of advanced computational tools for early detection and personalized treatment of schizophrenia.
