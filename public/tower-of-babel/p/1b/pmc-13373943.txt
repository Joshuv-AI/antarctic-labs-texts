Psychiatric effects of cannabidiol, cannabinol, and tetrahydrocannabinol: A narrative review of clinical evidence and risk profiles
Authors: Yasuhiro Kishi, Ichiro Takumi, Kakusho Chigusa Nakajima‐Ohyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCannabinoid products are increasingly used worldwide despite limited psychiatric evidence. This narrative review synthesizes preclinical and clinical evidence on cannabidiol (CBD), cannabinol (CBN), and tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) to clarify their distinct psychiatric profiles and discuss how current evidence may inform ongoing clinical and regulatory discussions. A targeted search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science through March 2026 was conducted, prioritizing randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and meta‐analyses. While preclinical studies consistently suggest anxiolytic and antidepressant‐like effects of CBD, clinical evidence remains inconsistent and fails to establish robust therapeutic efficacy. Recent meta‐analyses indicate no consistent clinically meaningful benefit of cannabinoids across major psychiatric disorders, and evidence for CBN remains minimal and insufficient to support any clinical recommendation. In sharp contrast, a robust body of evidence indicates that THC is strongly and dose‐dependently associated with adverse psychiatric outcomes—including psychosis, cognitive impairment, mood instability, and suicidality—particularly with early‐onset or high‐potency use. Overall, current evidence does not support the routine clinical use of cannabinoids for psychiatric indications. Although CBD is an investigational agent with a favorable safety profile, its clinical utility remains unproven, whereas THC exposure carries well‐documented risks of psychiatric adverse effects. These findings underscore the importance of cautious, compound‐specific, and evidence‐based approaches to clinical practice and regulation, particularly within the precautionary frameworks observed in several Asian countries.

INTRODUCTION

Mental, neurological, and substance use disorders remain leading contributors to global disability. 1 Despite advances in pharmacotherapy, a significant therapeutic gap persists for major psychiatric conditions, driving interest in novel neurobiological targets. 2 In this expanding landscape of novel targets, which includes rapid‐acting glutamatergic modulators and classic psychedelics, exogenous cannabinoids have attracted attention due to their interactions with the endogenous cannabinoid system, a network regulating stress, affect, and cognition. Specifically, these plant‐derived compounds, termed phytocannabinoids (such as THC and CBD), are increasingly scrutinized for their clinical viability. However, clinical evidence remains inconclusive despite increasing use.

The endocannabinoid system, comprising the canonical cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2) and endogenous ligands, plays a central role in regulating stress, affect, and cognition. 3 , 4 Importantly, the broader endocannabinoid network also involves noncanonical targets, such as G‐protein coupled receptor 55 (GPR55), whose physiological and pharmacological functions are increasingly being elucidated, rendering a uniform class‐effect assumption oversimplified. 5 , 6 Phytocannabinoids interact with this system in distinct ways that may underlie their differential psychiatric effects. Δ9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) acts as a partial agonist at CB1 and CB2 receptors, which is associated with its psychoactive and potentially psychotogenic properties. 7 In contrast, cannabidiol (CBD) has low affinity for cannabinoid receptors and instead modulates the endocannabinoid system indirectly, including through interactions with noncannabinoid targets such as serotonin 5‐HT1A receptors. 8 Cannabinol (CBN), an oxidative metabolite of THC, exhibits weak partial agonist activity at CB1 receptors, although its pharmacological profile remains insufficiently characterized. 8 These pharmacological differences may contribute to the heterogeneity of clinical outcomes observed across cannabinoid‐based interventions.

Major phytocannabinoids—THC, CBD, and CBN—differ substantially in pharmacology and psychoactivity, suggesting divergent psychiatric profiles rather than a uniform class effect. 8 While cannabis policies have been liberalized globally 9 —encompassing both the decriminalization of recreational use in certain jurisdictions and the regulatory expansion of medical cannabinoid programs—the current review focuses specifically on the clinical evidence base relevant to medical and psychiatric applications, distinct from recreational contexts.

In contrast to global trends toward liberalization, several Asian countries, including Japan, South Korea, and Singapore, maintain strict regulatory frameworks, particularly regarding THC. 10 From a public health perspective, such policies may be interpreted as reflecting a precautionary regulatory approach. However, global trends toward harm reduction are often conflated with medical safety, which may contribute to differing interpretations of the biological risks associated with THC.

To contextualize the current state of evidence, it is worth noting that the clinical utilization of certain phytocannabinoids has achieved robust empirical support in nonpsychiatric medical fields. For example, high‐quality multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have established the efficacy of highly purified CBD in reducing treatment‐resistant seizures in Lennox‐Gastaut and Dravet syndromes, leading to regulatory approvals by major health authorities. 11 Similarly, standardized THC:CBD formulations have demonstrated clear therapeutic benefits for spasticity in multiple sclerosis. 12 This established medical success in neurology throws the striking lack of consistent, high‐level clinical trial evidence for primary psychiatric indications into sharp relief.

This narrative review synthesizes preclinical and clinical evidence on the psychiatric effects of CBD, CBN, and THC. By distinguishing their risk profiles, we aim to inform evidence‐based practice and evaluate how current evidence regarding psychiatric benefits and risks may inform ongoing regulatory and clinical discussions, including those occurring in Asia.

METHODS

We conducted a targeted literature search across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for studies published through March 2026. The search strategy employed keywords related to cannabinoids (e.g., “CBD,” “CBN,” “THC”) in combination with psychiatric conditions (e.g., “anxiety,” “depression,” “psychosis,” “sleep disorders,” “PTSD”).

Studies were selected based on relevance to psychiatric outcomes, prioritizing RCTs, systematic reviews, and meta‐analyses. Preclinical studies were included to contextualize mechanistic findings. In areas where clinical evidence is sparse or conflicting—such as the therapeutic efficacy of CBD in anxiety or the clinical utilization of CBN—potentially conflicting findings were handled by presenting the data from both supportive and nonsupportive high‐quality studies side‐by‐side. Disagreements regarding study inclusion and interpretation were resolved through consensus among all authors. Given substantial heterogeneity in study design, dosing, and outcome measures, a quantitative synthesis was not appropriate; therefore, a narrative approach was adopted. The conclusions drawn are bounded by the inherent limitations of a narrative synthesis and are intended to highlight trends and data gaps rather than provide a definitive meta‐analytic pooling.

RESULTS

Pharmacology and endocannabinoid interactions

Preclinical evidence

To clarify how molecular‐level differences contribute to distinct psychiatric and behavioral outcomes, it is crucial to examine the specific cannabinoid receptor binding profiles and baseline pharmacological efficacies of each compound. 5 , 8 THC acts as a high‐affinity partial agonist at canonical CB1 and CB2 receptors, directly modulating neurotransmitter release and driving its psychoactive and psychotogenic effects. 3 , 4 , 7 In contrast, CBD possesses low binding affinity for both canonical receptors, functioning as a low‐affinity negative allosteric modulator or weak antagonist at CB1 and an inverse agonist at CB2, while it exerts its putative anxiolytic‐like effects via noncanonical targets such as serotonin 5‐HT1A receptors. 5 , 8 CBN exhibits a distinctive profile, acting as a weak partial agonist with an estimated 10% of the affinity of THC for CB1 receptors, but showing a relatively higher preference for CB2 receptors. 5 , 8 , 13 These divergent actions at specific receptor subtypes directly underlie the heterogeneous preclinical phenotypes observed across these phytocannabinoids.

CBD

Preclinical studies consistently indicate that CBD reduces anxiety‐like behaviors in rodent models via modulation of serotonergic (5‐HT1A) and endocannabinoid signaling. 14 CBD also reverses behavioral deficits in schizophrenia models through mechanisms distinct from D2 receptor antagonism. 15 These findings suggest anxiolytic, antidepressant‐like, and neuroprotective potential, although translational validity remains to be established.

CBN

CBN is nonpsychoactive and exhibits markedly weaker CNS effects than THC. 13 While animal models suggest potential analgesic effects, particularly in combination with CBD, 16 data regarding its psychiatric effects are scarce. In vitro studies have reported potential cytotoxicity at high concentrations, 17 and evidence supporting its use for mental health indications is virtually absent. 18

THC

THC exerts complex effects on mood and cognition. Preclinical data show that THC induces striatal dopamine release, contributing to reward and dependence. 19 Crucially, adolescent exposure in rodent models leads to persistent cognitive deficits and alterations in mesolimbic dopamine signaling, increasing vulnerability to psychiatric symptoms in adulthood. 20 , 21

Clinical evidence

CBD

Clinical studies have explored the potential anxiolytic effects of CBD across several conditions; however, findings remain inconsistent across study designs and populations. In young adults with treatment‐resistant social anxiety disorder, daily doses of 200–800 mg were associated with symptomatic improvement, accompanied by reduced activation in anxiety‐related neural circuits on functional neuroimaging. 51 , 52 , 53 A randomized trial in adolescents with social anxiety disorder similarly demonstrated significant symptom reduction with 300 mg/day of CBD compared with placebo. 54 Acute administration of CBD has also been shown to attenuate anxiety and cognitive impairment during simulated public speaking tasks. 55

Preliminary evidence has explored the potential effects of CBD in post‐traumatic stress disorder; however, findings remain limited and primarily observational. Case series and observational studies have reported improvements in sleep disturbance, anxiety, and overall symptom burden, 56 , 57 possibly related to facilitation of fear extinction and modulation of rapid eye movement sleep architecture. 58 , 59

In schizophrenia, adjunctive treatment with high‐dose CBD (up to 1000 mg/day) has been associated with reductions in positive symptoms and improvements in global functioning, with favorable tolerability profiles. 22 Comparative trials have suggested similar efficacy to amisulpride, accompanied by fewer extrapyramidal and metabolic adverse effects and increases in circulating anandamide levels. 23 , 24 In contrast, other randomized controlled studies have failed to demonstrate superiority over placebo, 25 and systematic reviews have emphasized substantial heterogeneity in dosing regimens, patient populations, and outcome measures. 23 , 26

Importantly, studies in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis and in patients with established psychotic disorders indicate that CBD does not exacerbate psychotic symptoms and may normalize dysfunction in limbic–striatal circuits, consistent with the absence of psychotogenic effects observed in human studies. 30 , 31

Emerging evidence also suggests a potential role for CBD in substance use disorders, with experimental and clinical studies demonstrating attenuation of cue‐induced craving and modulation of the reinforcing effects of THC and other substances. 44 , 60 , 61 However, clinical data remain preliminary.

With respect to mood disorders, RCTs specifically targeting depressive symptoms are lacking. Observational and registry‐based studies suggest that CBD is frequently used for self‐management of depressive symptoms and have reported symptom improvement, although confounding and selection bias limit interpretation. 62 , 63

CBD‐based interventions have additionally been explored in neurodevelopmental disorders, particularly autism spectrum disorder. Observational studies and small clinical trials have reported improvements in irritability, aggression, sleep disturbances, and social functioning. 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 A small randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled crossover trial in children with autism spectrum disorder (n = 29) found no significant improvement in the primary outcome of social responsiveness with CBD treatment. 67 However, secondary outcomes suggested modest improvements in social relating, anxiety, and parental stress. In contrast, evidence supporting CBD use in attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder remains sparse and inconsistent. 69 , 70

Regarding sleep disorders, randomized and crossover trials have yielded mixed results. Moderate to high doses of CBD may improve selected sleep parameters in some populations, whereas low‐dose regimens frequently fail to demonstrate superiority over placebo. 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 Systematic reviews highlight substantial variability in study design, dosing strategies, and outcome measures. 27 , 28

These findings are largely derived from small, short‐term studies with heterogeneous designs and should be interpreted with caution. Overall, current evidence does not establish consistent or clinically meaningful efficacy of CBD across psychiatric disorders, despite multiple small‐scale studies reporting symptom‐level improvements.

CBN

Preliminary trial protocols have investigated the effects of 30–300 mg CBN on sleep parameters, 32 though published results are not yet available. No completed randomized trials evaluating psychiatric outcomes of CBN have yet been published. However, based on preliminary data indicating improved sleep initiation and reduced nocturnal awakenings in subjective assessments and polysomnography, these rigorous RCTs are currently underway to confirm such effects. 32

However, evidence supporting the efficacy of CBN in psychiatric disorders beyond sleep and pain‐related conditions remains insufficient. Reviews focusing on minor cannabinoids have emphasized that, despite growing public interest, clinical evidence supporting the psychiatric use of CBN remains extremely limited and insufficient to inform therapeutic recommendations. 18

THC

In terms of clinical dosing parameters, while purified CBD is typically administered at high doses (e.g., 200–1000 mg/day) in psychiatric trials, 22 , 29 comparable clinical data for THC are constrained by its narrow therapeutic window. In jurisdictions where medical THC or synthetic analogues (such as dronabinol) are authorized for select nonpsychiatric indications, clinical dosing typically starts at a low threshold of 2.5–5 mg/day and is strictly titrated up to a maximum of 20–30 mg/day in divided doses to minimize acute cognitive and psychotomimetic adverse effects. 75 , 76

THC use is strongly associated with increased risk of psychosis, 33 particularly among individuals who use high‐potency THC‐containing products or initiate use at a young age. 34 , 35 , 36 A dose–response relationship has been demonstrated in meta‐analytic studies, indicating progressively higher psychosis risk with consistent findings across meta‐analytic and multicenter epidemiological studies. 37 , 38 The risk is especially elevated in individuals with a family history of psychosis, and THC‐induced dopamine release has been implicated in this association. 77 Psychotogenic effects are dose‐dependent and may be attenuated by CBD. 39 , 45 Although reverse causation cannot be excluded, longitudinal evidence strongly supports an association between THC exposure and the development of psychotic disorders, suggesting a potential contributory role.

THC acutely impairs working memory, attention, and decision‐making, with particularly pronounced effects during adolescence. 46 , 47 Longitudinal cohort studies have further demonstrated that persistent THC‐containing products use is associated with neuropsychological decline from childhood to midlife, suggesting potential long‐term cognitive consequences. 48 Clinical and neurobiological evidence summarized in high‐impact reviews indicates that THC adversely affects cognition, reward processing, and behavioral control, with particular vulnerability observed during adolescence. 21

THC‐containing products use has also been associated with increased risk of mania, suicidal behavior, and affective instability, especially among individuals with pre‐existing mood disorders. 45 A large systematic review and meta‐analysis further demonstrated that adolescent THC‐containing products use is associated with increased risk of depression, anxiety, and suicidality in young adulthood. 49 Moreover, THC‐containing products use disorder has been associated with increased mortality and adverse psychiatric outcomes, underscoring the broader clinical implications of long‐term THC exposure. 50

Two studies from Japan examined THC‐containing products‐related psychiatric outcomes using observational and clinical approaches. 78 , 79 Although these studies provided descriptive insights into clinical characteristics and risk factors, neither directly evaluated THC exposure, product potency, or dose–response relationships. Therefore, while providing local context, the absence of THC‐specific analyses should not be interpreted as evidence supporting the safety of THC‐containing products.

THC demonstrates limited symptom‐modulating effects alongside substantial psychiatric risks, particularly with respect to psychosis, cognitive impairment, and dependence.

A limited body of clinical evidence suggests that THC‐containing formulations may exert symptom‐modulating effects in selected conditions. RCTs in Tourette syndrome have reported mixed findings, with earlier small studies suggesting improvement in tic severity, whereas more recent crossover trials failed to demonstrate significant effects on primary outcomes and showed only modest improvements in secondary measures. 40 , 41 , 42 THC‐containing formulations have also been investigated in sleep‐related conditions, with some studies reporting improvements in sleep initiation and subjective sleep quality; however, systematic reviews highlight substantial heterogeneity and inconsistent findings across studies. 27 , 28 In addition, cannabinoid‐based therapies, particularly THC/CBD combinations, have shown potential utility in the management of cannabis withdrawal symptoms. 44 Overall, recent large‐scale meta‐analytic evidence indicates that the efficacy of THC in psychiatric disorders is limited and varies across conditions, with no consistent benefit observed for most disorders, 29 consistent with earlier large‐scale meta‐analytic findings. 43

Evidence from RCTs

Evidence from RCTs remains limited and heterogeneous. A recent large‐scale systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs reported limited and disorder‐specific efficacy of cannabinoids, 29 consistent with earlier meta‐analytic findings. 43 Complementary systematic reviews further indicate that THC may produce transient symptom‐modulating effects but is also associated with increased psychiatric adverse effects, including psychotic and affective symptoms. 39 Individual randomized trials illustrate this heterogeneity. For example, adjunctive CBD has demonstrated some efficacy in schizophrenia in a multicenter RCT, 22 whereas more recent trials of THC‐containing formulations in Tourette syndrome have failed to show significant improvement in primary outcomes, with only modest effects observed in secondary measures. 42

Translational gap between preclinical promise and clinical evidence

A central finding of the present review is the persistent translational gap between robust preclinical observations and limited clinical confirmation, particularly with respect to CBD. Experimental studies consistently demonstrate anxiolytic, antidepressant‐like, and anti‐inflammatory effects of CBD in animal models; however, these findings have not translated into consistent or reproducible clinical efficacy across psychiatric disorders. 23 , 25 , 26 Human studies remain heterogeneous in design, sample size, dosing strategies, and outcome measures, which substantially limits their interpretability and generalizability. 27 , 28

This discrepancy raises important concerns regarding the extrapolation of mechanistic or behavioral findings from animal models to clinical practice. While preclinical data provide valuable insights into biological plausibility, they do not substitute for rigorous clinical validation, particularly in psychiatry where symptom heterogeneity and placebo effects are prominent.

Differential psychiatric profiles of major phytocannabinoids

Another key implication of this review is that phytocannabinoids should not be regarded as a homogeneous pharmacological or clinical category. CBD, CBN, and THC differ markedly in receptor interactions, psychoactivity, and psychiatric risk profiles, and their effects cannot be inferred from one another. 5 , 8

Among these compounds, evidence supporting the psychiatric efficacy of CBN remains particularly limited. Although early pharmacological studies suggested weak central nervous system activity relative to THC, 13 , 80 contemporary clinical data supporting CBN use in psychiatric disorders are sparse. Reviews focusing on minor cannabinoids emphasize that, despite growing public and commercial interest, clinical evidence for the psychiatric application of CBN remains insufficient to inform therapeutic recommendations. 18 These distinctions highlight the importance of compound‐specific evaluation rather than generalized discussion of “cannabinoids” in psychiatric research and practice.

Clinical and public health implications of THC exposure

The findings from RCTs highlight a fundamental limitation in the current evidence base: although cannabinoids, particularly THC‐containing formulations, may produce short‐term symptom‐modulating effects in selected conditions, these effects are generally modest, inconsistent, and highly context‐dependent. Importantly, the observed improvements in domains such as sleep or subjective distress may reflect nonspecific sedative or psychoactive effects rather than true disease‐modifying or therapeutic benefits. Moreover, the lack of consistent efficacy across major psychiatric disorders, as demonstrated in large‐scale meta‐analytic evidence, underscores the limited clinical utility of THC in psychiatry.

This distinction between transient symptomatic relief and clinically meaningful therapeutic outcomes is particularly critical in psychiatric populations, where long‐term functional recovery, cognitive preservation, and safety are central treatment goals. In this context, the psychiatric risks associated with THC—including psychosis, cognitive impairment, and affective instability—are supported by a more consistent and robust body of evidence than its therapeutic effects. The current evidence does not support the routine clinical use of THC for psychiatric indications and instead favors a cautious, risk‐aware approach that clearly differentiates THC from nonpsychotogenic cannabinoids such as CBD. These findings are consistent with current clinical guidelines, which generally do not recommend routine use of cannabinoid‐based products for psychiatric disorders. 81

High‐impact reviews and experimental studies indicate that THC adversely affects cognition, reward processing, and behavioral control. 21 While dysregulation of downstream dopaminergic neurotransmission is heavily implicated in its psychotomimetic and rewarding properties, 21 , 77 , 82 a growing body of evidence suggests that THC‐induced cognitive impairment cannot be attributed solely to dopamine cascades. Rather, alterations in glutamatergic signaling and direct disruption of endocannabinoid‐mediated synaptic plasticity play pivotal roles. Specifically, chronic THC exposure has been shown to alter astrocytic ensembles at specific pathways, such as the ventral hippocampal‐nucleus accumbens synapses, leading to persistent deficits in cognitive and execution functions. 83 , 84

Early longitudinal studies suggested that genetic variation in catechol‐O‐methyltransferase (COMT) may moderate the association between adolescent THC‐containing products exposure and later psychotic symptoms, supporting a gene–environment interaction model. 85 Moreover, THC‐containing products use disorder has been linked to increased all‐cause mortality and unfavorable long‐term psychiatric outcomes, underscoring the broader clinical and public health implications of sustained THC exposure. 50 The cumulative evidence suggests that THC‐containing products cannot be assumed to be benign or therapeutically neutral, particularly in vulnerable populations.

Implications of regulatory approaches in Asia

Amid increasing global liberalization of THC‐containing products, several Asian countries represent a distinctive regulatory context defined by its continued strict control of THC. 10 Although available observational studies have identified associations between cannabis‐related behaviors and psychiatric outcomes, many reports lack detailed assessments of THC potency or cumulative exposure. 78 , 79 Consequently, while causality remains unproven, precautionary regulatory strategies may be considered consistent with preventive mental health principles in the absence of compelling evidence for THC's psychiatric safety or therapeutic value.

Moving forward, policy discussions on the medical use of cannabinoids in Asian contexts should incorporate comprehensive pharmacovigilance and component analysis, including quantification of THC/CBD ratios and product composition. Such frameworks would enable evidence‐based oversight and facilitate future clinical evaluation within controlled regulatory environments.

Clinical implications beyond psychiatry

Beyond psychiatry, evolving cannabinoid policies in several Asian countries have important implications for neurological practice, where psychiatric vulnerability and neurodevelopmental risk often overlap. Policy development in this field increasingly benefits from collaboration between psychiatric and neurological experts, underscoring the value of interdisciplinary input in shaping balanced and evidence‐informed regulatory frameworks.

While psychiatric safety remains the central concern, clinical discussions in several Asian countries and other regions have been most active in epilepsy, where pharmaceutical‐grade CBD has demonstrated antiseizure efficacy and is generally considered nonpsychotogenic. 11 , 23 , 86 Preliminary interest in movement disorders and certain neurodevelopmental conditions has also emerged, though these populations require particularly cautious, compound‐specific evaluation due to heightened psychiatric vulnerability. 64 , 65 , 87

From a neurological standpoint, maintaining a clear distinction between CBD and THC is essential. CBD supports potential therapeutic use under regulated frameworks, whereas THC carries established psychiatric and cognitive risks, warranting continued caution in both clinical and legislative contexts.

Limitations and future directions

This review is limited by its narrative design and the heterogeneity of the included studies. Differences in study design, dosing, outcome measures, and patient characteristics constrain direct comparison across trials. Moreover, long‐term safety data for CBD and CBN remain limited. Furthermore, given the rapid commercialization of CBN as a sleep aid despite the paucity of clinical data, there is an urgent need for rigorous, double‐blind RCTs to systematically evaluate its effects on sleep architecture and psychiatric outcomes.

Although the so‐called “entourage effect” has been proposed to suggest synergistic interactions among cannabinoids and other cannabis constituents, 88 supporting evidence remains largely preclinical. Importantly, the possibility of such interactions does not obviate the need for compound‐specific evaluation, particularly with respect to psychiatric and neurodevelopmental safety.

Beyond the major phytocannabinoids discussed in this review, emerging compounds such as tetrahydrocannabivarin (THCV), cannabigerol (CBG), and cannabichromene (CBC) warrant increasing scientific attention. Preclinical studies suggest that THCV may act as a CB1 receptor antagonist at low doses and partial agonist at higher doses, potentially conferring antipsychotic and appetite‐suppressant properties distinct from THC. 8 Similarly, CBG and CBC have demonstrated preliminary anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective effects in translational models, but clinical data remain exceedingly sparse. Systematic evaluation of these minor cannabinoids is therefore essential to clarify their psychiatric safety profiles, therapeutic potential, and regulatory relevance within evolving cannabinoid research frameworks.

Future research should prioritize well‐designed, adequately powered RCTs with standardized dosing and clinically meaningful endpoints. In parallel, mechanistic studies linking neurobiological effects to clinical outcomes will be essential to bridge the translational gap identified in this review.

CONCLUSION

Cannabinoids have been investigated as potential therapeutic agents, but current evidence does not support their routine clinical use in psychiatry. While CBD is nonpsychoactive and appears to have a favorable safety profile, its clinical efficacy remains unproven and insufficient to support standardized use. Large‐scale, well‐designed clinical trials are required to establish long‐term efficacy, safety, and appropriate therapeutic positioning. In contrast, available evidence indicates that THC‐containing products are associated with important psychiatric risks, whereas evidence supporting therapeutic benefit in psychiatric disorders remains limited and inconsistent. Evidence for CBN remains insufficient to justify its clinical use.
