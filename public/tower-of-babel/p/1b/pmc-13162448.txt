Knowledge of dementia and factors associated with dementia knowledge among 35- to 65-year-old adults in Bandaragama Divisional Secretariat area in Sri Lanka- a lower- middle-income country
Authors: Y. Chathurangani Nilushika, Mansoor Muthahhara, H. Jayakody, P. K. Buddhika Mahesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSri Lanka is undergoing rapid population ageing due to increased life expectancy and declining fertility, resulting in a rising burden of dementia. Scarce local evidence on knowledge of community on dementia in Sri Lanka underscores the need for context-specific research to guide effective interventions.ObjectivesTo describe the level of knowledge of dementia and to identify factors associated with knowledge among adults aged 35 to 65 years residing in the Divisional Secretariat area of Bandaragama.MethodA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 640 adults aged 35–65 years using probability-proportionate-to-size cluster sampling. Grama Niladhari divisions were used as clusters, with 30 participants per cluster across 22 clusters. Data was collected through a pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire. A dementia knowledge score and expert-defined cut-off for satisfactory knowledge were established. Associations between dichotomized independent variables and knowledge levels were examined using the chi-square test and Fisher’s Exact test at 5% significance level. Variables with P < 0.25 were entered into binary logistic regression. Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) analysis was followed.ResultsResponse rate was 97.5% with 238 males and 386 females. Median age of participants was 47 years (IQR 40–55). Out of the total 624 participants, 327(52.4%) had satisfactory knowledge of dementia while 297(47.6%) had unsatisfactory knowledge. In bivariable analysis, ethnicity (P < 0.001), educational level (p = 0.039), being currently employed (P < 0.001), having heard the word called “dementia” (P < 0.001), and contact with a person with dementia (P < 0.001) were significantly associated with satisfactory knowledge. Binary logistic regression showed individuals who had contact with a person with dementia (OR = 2.56; 95%CI = 1.64–4.01), and Sinhalese-ethnicity (OR = 1.15; 95%CI = 1.80–5.59), being male (OR = 0.59, 95%CI = 0.41–0.85) currently-employed (OR = 2.32; 95%CI = 1.56–3.44) had a higher probability of having satisfactory knowledge on dementia. Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) analysis elicited similar associations.ConclusionsApproximately half of the participants demonstrated satisfactory knowledge of dementia. Sinhalese ethnicity, currently being employed, prior contact with a person with dementia, and female gender were associated with a higher knowledge irrespective of educational attainment and income. These findings suggest a need for enhanced community-based educational initiatives and consideration of incorporating dementia-related components into existing health services to promote awareness and support healthy aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27140-7.

Introduction

Dementia refers to a clinical syndrome characterized by progressive cognitive decline that interferes with the ability to function independently [10]. The symptoms of a person with dementia may progress gradually but continuously. People who have dementia experience changes in behavior, cognition, and function (Chan et al., 2022). Currently, there are more than 55 million people living with dementia [28]. It is estimated that there will be 135.46 million people with dementia by 2050 [25]. With approximately 10 million new cases getting added every year, and sixty percent of dementia patients are living in low and middle-income countries [28]. Sri Lanka has the fastest-growing elderly population in the Southeast Asian region. According to the weekly epidemiological report of Sri Lanka, the annual cost for dementia is US$ 818 billion in Sri Lanka, which is more than 1% of the global gross domestic product [9].

As dementia is a growing global health concern, exploring the understanding of the public about this condition is critically important in designing interventions to mitigate its impact. Studies conducted worldwide have highlighted that poor knowledge often hinders early screening, diagnostic tests, and treatment interventions [27]. Families frequently delay seeking care due to misconceptions about dementia [15]. Improving public knowledge of dementia can notably enhance health seeking behaviors. Awareness campaigns and educational interventions have been shown to promote an increase in early detection, timely diagnosis, and treatment [24]. Additionally, raising public awareness about dementia can contribute to enhancing the quality of care and slowing disease progression [20]. Having adequate public knowledge also plays a vital role in reducing stigma, enabling individuals with dementia and their caregivers to lead dignified lives. Moreover, greater awareness of dementia symptoms is positively correlated with proactive care-seeking behaviors and effective management of the condition [24].

Since there is no cure, identifying and reducing risk factors is the best way to prevent it. There were twelve modifiable risk factors for dementia that were identified by the 2017 and 2020 Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, intervention, and care. These factors include: lower levels of education, hypertension, obesity, depression, physical inactivity, traumatic brain injury, social isolation, exposure to air pollution, and diabetes. But there have been new risk factors being introduced in 2024. These are untreated vision loss and high LDL cholesterol levels [17].

Sri Lanka is undergoing a rapid demographic transition with a growing elderly population. Western Province of Sri Lanka records the highest proportion of elderly people (31%), and about 77% of them reside in rural areas [23]. Within this province, the Bandaragama Divisional Secretariat in the Kalutara District represents semi-urban population distribution, diverse socioeconomic characteristics, and established primary healthcare infrastructure. The elderly population is around 13.8% in Bandaragama Divisional Secretary area, according to the population census in year 2024 (Census of Population and Housing—2012, n.d.)

The study focuses on adults aged 35–65 years. By the age of 35 years, individuals typically assume greater family responsibilities and become physically, economically, and socially capable of managing the demands of caring for older adults. Therefore, assessing practices from an early mid-adult stage, such as 35 years, can provide valuable policy-relevant information to support timely preventive strategies for dementia [16]. Meanwhile, individuals over 65 years often experience multiple comorbidities and age-related health conditions that may confound dementia-specific assessments, making the 35–65 age range more suitable for exploring protective behaviors.

The present study aimed to assess dementia related knowledge and its associated factors among adults aged 35 to 65 years in the Divisional Secretary area Bandaragama.

Methods

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among adults aged 35–65 years (age calculated in completed years at the time of data collection) residing in the Bandaragama Divisional Secretariat area. Individuals who had been permanent residents of the area for at least six months prior to the commencement of data collection were considered eligible for inclusion. Those who were unavailable at the time of data collection despite repeated visits and individuals who were unable to communicate effectively were excluded from the study.

The sample size was determined as 640, with a degree of precision 5%, 50% as the expected proportion, with a satisfactory knowledge level determined following literature review and expert inputs. As cluster sampling was employed, a design effect of 1.5 was applied to account for the potential intra-cluster correlation. The adequacy of this design effect was considered with previous methodological literature [14], particularly in the absence of prior ICC estimates for similar settings. Accordingly, the adjusted sample size at the data collection stage was 377 × 1.5 = 566. A 90% anticipated response rate was then incorporated to determine the final required sample size.

The Probability-proportionate-to-size cluster sampling method was used. There were 59 Grama Niladari areas in Bandaragama Divisional Secretariat area. One Grama Niladhari (GN) division was considered a cluster. The cluster size was determined to be 30 participants, taking into consideration the number of households that could feasibly be covered within a single day of data collection. Accordingly, the average cluster size was fixed at 30, and the total number of clusters required to achieve the calculated sample size was 22. Only fully completed questionnaires were considered when calculating the response rate. Participants who withdrew during the completion of the questionnaire were categorized as non-respondents. Within each selected Grama Niladhari (GN) division (cluster), main junctions leading to residential areas were first listed with the assistance of the Divisional Secretary. One junction was selected using simple random sampling. From the selected junction, one lane was randomly chosen. Households within that lane were then selected using systematic sampling, with the sampling interval calculated by dividing the total number of households in the lane by the required cluster size (30 households). If the required number of households was not achieved within the selected lane, the next lane in a clockwise direction was approached until the cluster size was completed. When more than one eligible individual was present in a household, one participant was selected randomly. If a household was closed at the time of visit, the immediately adjacent household was approached.

The questionnaire was developed using the best evidence available in the literature as well as with expert inputs. Variables were selected based on a conceptual framework and existing literature identifying socio-demographic and health-related characteristics as determinants of knolwdge on dementia. It was initially prepared in English and then translated into Sinhala and Tamil using forward and back-translation to ensure accuracy and resolve any discrepancies. The face validity, content validity, and consensual validity were ensured through expert review. A pilot study was conducted among 10 participants to check the clarity and feasibility of the tool and, test- retest reliability was assessed to ensure consistency of responses over time [1]. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used due to variations in participants’ education levels, in order to minimize response variability and information bias. Data collectors received comprehensive training from the principal investigator on standardized administration of the questionnaire, including consistent wording, neutral tone, and careful prompting to reduce social desirability bias. The questionnaire was pre-tested prior to data collection to ensure clarity and appropriateness. Interviews were conducted in the participants’ preferred language (Sinhala or Tamil) using a structured interviewer guide. Supervision and random checks were performed during data collection to ensure adherence to standard procedures and accuracy of responses. Participants were selected based on predetermined eligibility criteria from a well-defined sampling frame to reduce selection bias. Before each interview, a brief introduction and information sheet were provided, and clarifications were offered to address any questions. Confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the process.

Knowledge was assessed using 20 questions. Each correct answer was marked with 5 marks, and each incorrect and did not know 0 marks. The expert panel considered the measures of location as well as the content of the questionnaire and recommended the median split method described in literature [6]. With expert inputs and with the analysis of the score distribution, those with scores of more than 61 and above considered as with “satisfactory knowledge” on dementia. The association factors were dichotomized. The categorization of the age, educational level, income status and employment status based on epidemiological relevance and compatibility with previous literature, such as participants aged 35–65 years was categorized into two groups (35–49 and 50–65 years) based on evidence that dementia risk and midlife exposures increase substantially after age 50. The associations between associative factors with knowledge on dementia were assessed using the Chi-square test and Fisher Exact test with 5% significance level. Variables with a P-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were entered into a binary logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with the level of dementia knowledge. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Since the cluster sampling technique was employed, Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) analysis was done to further confirm the findings of the regression analysis, adjusting for the clustering effects.

Results

A total of 624 adults completed the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 97.5% (624/640). The remaining 16 eligible participants, who declined to participate at the outset, were classified as non-respondents. Their characteristics are shown in Table 1. Nearly half of the participants were between 35 to 45 years (46%). Male to female ratio was 1:1.62, and the majority had completed secondary education or engaged in higher studies (n = 524, 83.9%). Employment status was classified as currently employed, unemployed, or never employed. “Never employed” referred to participants who had never engaged in paid employment at any point in their lifetime. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) analysis was performed to identify factors associated with the outcome while accounting for clustering effects. Gender (β = −0.523, 95% CI: −2.16 to −0.13,P = 0.09), Ethnicity-Sinhalese (β = 1.154, 95% CI: 0.61 to 1.69, P < 0.05), employment status (β = 0.840, 95% CI: 0.49 to1.18, P < 0.05), having previous contact with a person living with dementia (β = 0.942, 95% CI: 0.44 to 1.44, P < 0.05) showed significant associations with the level of knowledge on dementia. In contrast, education level (β = 0.111, 95% CI: −0.30 to 0.52, P = 0.59) and monthly income (β = −0.115, 95% CI: − 0.39 to 0.16, P = 0.41) did not significantly associate with the outcome after adjusting for other variables in the GEE model.

Discussion

This is the first documented study conducted to describe the level of knowledge on dementia and its associated factors among adults aged 35–65 years covering an entire Divisional Secretariat area within a district in Sri Lanka. The findings revealed that just over half of the participants demonstrated satisfactory knowledge regarding dementia, indicating important gaps in community awareness. The study further identified several associated factors through multivariable analytical approaches. After adjusting for potential confounding and clustering effects using generalized estimating equation analysis, several socio-demographic and experiential characteristics remained significantly associated with satisfactory dementia knowledge. Gender showed a significant positive association, suggesting variation in exposure to health information and caregiving roles within the community. Ethnicity demonstrated a strong association, highlighting possible cultural and language-related disparities in access to dementia-related information. Employment status was also significantly related to knowledge level, with economically inactive individuals showing a lower likelihood of satisfactory knowledge, possibly due to reduced social engagement and limited exposure to health promotion activities. In addition, previous contact with a person living with dementia was significantly associated with knowledge level in the adjusted model, indicating that informal exposure alone may not ensure accurate understanding of the condition. In contrast, educational attainment and household income were not significantly associated with dementia knowledge after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that general socioeconomic advantage may not necessarily translate into better disease awareness. These findings underscore the importance of targeted and context-specific public health education strategies to improve dementia literacy at the community level.

More than half of the participants (52.4%, n = 327) were found to be have “satisfactory knowledge”. In a study conducted in Cape Town, Metropole, it was found that 53.4% of participants were having average level of knowledge [13]. This study was conducted among 100 participants of the community and was done in an urban setting. In this study, 87% of participants answered correctly for the question that “dementia occurs due to change in the brain”. In the present study, around 63.7% of participants answered correctly to the question of dementia occurring with brain changes. In contrast to the present study, the sampling technique was “more convenient in type” in that study, which may have imposed some selection bias [13].

A study conducted in the United Kingdom assessed and compare knowledge on dementia among South Asian older people and Caucasian older people. 191 Indians and 55 Caucasians participated. Knowledge on dementia was generally poor in both groups, but significantly low among Indian older adults (Purandare et al., 2007).

During the knowledge assessment study done in Manchester, UK, Indian participants demonstrated significantly low understanding of basic aspects and epidemiology of dementia. (P < 0.001). while both groups performed similarly poorly on questions regarding aetiology and symptomatology. Indians were also less aware that personality, reasoning, and speech are affected in dementia (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, P = 0.04) (Purandare et al., 2007).

Furthermore, a cross-sectional survey was done in two Italian speaking sites south Switzerland (Ticino) and northern Italy (Piedmont). The knowledge on dementia was measured using the Dementia Knowledge Assessment Survey (DKAS). Here, general knowledge regarding dementia is low, and misconceptions about dementia were common among respondents. Lack of knowledge has been identified in dementia causes, characteristics, risk factors, and health promotion [21].

A cross-sectional study among Egyptian adults assessed knowledge of dementia using a structured questionnaire. Results showed variable knowledge levels, with nearly one-third of participants having poor overall knowledge. Participants performed best in the domains of manifestations and prevention, but poorly in treatment and life impact. Factors associated with lower dementia knowledge included not working in the medical field, lack of contact with dementia patients, and no prior knowledge about dementia [3]

In a study done in Singapore to assess the knowledge of the public regarding dementia, it was found that 64.2% were having “good knowledge” [11]. A total of 1,500 participants were recruited for that study with the snowball sampling method. This was an online self-completed questionnaire. The level of literacy in Singapore was 98% in a year [26]. The internet usage in Singapore is 99% among the residents, which is higher than in Sri Lanka (Singapore digital society report, 2023). With that higher accessibility to the internet, people may acquire better information.

In the present study, most of the participants were aware of the risk factors and protective factors which may lead to dementia. Particularly, they were much more aware of lifestyle factors and medical factors. A Chinese study has been done among Webchat users in China on knowledge over risk factors and protective factors of dementia [29]. In that study, it was found that the community knowledge on the risk factors and the protective factors was high. However, in contrast to the present study, the percentage of participants with overall satisfactory knowledge was lower. The reason for that could be the well-developed preventive sector infrastructure in Sri Lanka as well as the differences of the content of the questions used.

In the present study, factors associated with the level of knowledge were evaluated. Ethnicity (P = 0.005), educational level (P = 0. 0.553), employment status (P < 0.001), and Contact with a person with dementia (P < 0.001) were found to be significantly associated with the level of knowledge. Findings of a study done in China yielded similar associated factors for the level of dementia knowledge. Two such factors were educational level and being in contact with a person with dementia [29].

In the present study, factors associated with the level of knowledge were evaluated. Gender (P = 0.005), Ethnicity(P < 0.001), Employment status (P < 0.001) and contact with a person with dementia (P < 0.001) were statistically significant.

Furthermore, in a study done in Singapore, it was found that ethnicity (P = 0.02), and the educational level (P = 0.018) were significantly associated with the satisfactory knowledge on dementia, which is also compatible with the present study [11]. In that study, it was shown that age (P < 0.001) was significantly associated with the knowledge of dementia. Yet in the present study, age was not a significantly associated factor. In Singapore, there are several targeted campaigns for older adults, which may lead to this difference. Specially, there are shops which are dementia-friendly, which may increase the awareness among the people in Singapore (Catering to Those with Dementia, n.d.). Limited dementia specific education in Sri Lanka could have led to this discrepancy.

A study was done in Turkey among 1106 people who were “18 years and above” via online contacts, through Facebook, and WhatsApp. Prior education about dementia (P = 0.001), prior contact with a person with dementia (P = 0.001), previous contact with person with dementia (P = 0.001), and having a family history of dementia (P = 0.001) were found to be significantly associated with ta higher level of knowledge on dementia. [2]. These findings are closely aligned with the present study. In the present study, significantly associated having a previous contact with a person with dementia (P < 0.001) and Employment status (P < 0.001).

Also, in the cross-sectional survey was done in two Italian speaking sites south Switzerland (Ticino) and northern Italy (Piedmont). The overall DKAS score was significantly and positively associated with female sex (β = 0.21; P < 0.001), educational level (β = 0.15; P < 0.001) and contact with a person living with dementia (β = 0.17; P < 0.001), but not with age (β = −0.01; P = 0.57) [21].

This study suggests enhancing dementia knowledge through public awareness campaigns integrated into existing health programs, such as Healthy Lifestyle Centers and Well Woman Clinics. Life-course educational interventions, exposure to the term “dementia,” and contact with affected individuals can improve knowledge and attitudes, guiding culturally appropriate public health strategies and policy planning.

This study highlights critical gaps in dementia knowledge, emphasizing the need for focused education programs. Integrating dementia care into routine training and community awareness initiatives could improve care quality and support for older adults. Future research should assess the impact of such interventions and explore barriers to effective training.

There were several limitations of the study. The study was conducted within a single Divisional Secretary division; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions of Sri Lanka with differing sociodemographic, cultural, and health service contexts. This study used a self-developed dementia knowledge questionnaire, and although content validity was assessed through expert review and pilot testing, formal reliability testing was not fully performed. The absence of standardized, validated measurement tools and reliability testing, such as Cronbach’s alpha may limit the generalizability and internal consistency of the findings. In addition, certain exposure categories contained small numbers, which may have resulted in unstable regression estimates and reduced precision of odds ratio estimates. Therefore, the magnitude of some associations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Just over half of the study participants demonstrated satisfactory knowledge of dementia, with the lowest understanding observed in areas related to disease progression, management, and prognosis. While general awareness of dementia risk was relatively high, knowledge regarding the influence of family history appeared limited. Factors such as female gender, Sinhalese ethnicity, and being currently-employed, personal contact with individuals living with dementia showed associations with higher knowledge in this sample. Age, education level and income level were significantly associated with knowledge. These findings suggest that there may be gaps in dementia knowledge within this Divisional Secretariat area and indicate potential areas for targeted education or awareness efforts. Given the cross-sectional design, small sample sizes in some categories, and statistical limitations, these results should be interpreted cautiously in generalizing. Nevertheless, the study highlights the need for further research to explore dementia knowledge and inform context-specific interventions to support early recognition and care practices.
