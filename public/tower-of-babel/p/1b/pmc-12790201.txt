Palpitations Associated With Frequent Premature Ventricular Contractions as an Important Manifestation of Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease
Authors: Dae-Won Sohn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere has been growing interest in the association between gastriesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and cardiac arrhythmias.Case SummaryA 48-year-old man presented with palpitations predominantly occurring after alcohol intake or meals. Wearable electrocardiogram patch monitoring revealed that his frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) were exclusively observed after alcohol intake or during postprandial periods. The PVCs could effectively be controlled with therapy for GERD. Another 40-year-old woman whose symptomatic PVCs were initially controlled with flecainide reported that her palpitations were aggravated when lying down. Flecainide was switched to a potassium-competitive acid blocker (PCAB), and the PVCs were better controlled with PCAB than with flecainide (0.01% vs 2.8% on flecainide).DiscussionPVCs may be triggered by GERD, and they can be effectively controlled by the appropriate treatment of the underlying GERD.Take-Home MessageWhen performing continuous electrocardiographic monitoring for frequent PVCs, it is important not only to assess their frequency but also to carefully analyze the timing of their occurrence.

Patient 1

History of presentation

A 48-year-old normotensive man presented with palpitations that usually occurred after alcohol intake or meals. The palpitations usually lasted 10 to 20 minutes but could last up to 1 hour after alcohol intake. Physical examination showed regular heart beats without murmur. Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of palpitation should include 3 conditions: first, fast heart rate either associated with sinus rhythm or due to tachyarrhythmia; second, irregular heartbeat; and third, increased contractility when increased stroke volume is needed.

Investigations

The patient's complete blood count showed a hemoglobin of 16.2 g/dL. The thyroid function test was within normal limits. Chest x-ray and electrocardiogram (ECG) were normal. Echocardiogram demonstrated normal left ventricular size and function and did not show mitral valve prolapse. Wearable ECG patch monitoring (patch monitoring) was performed. In the initial patch monitoring, frequent PVCs were noted, not infrequently in salvos, after alcohol intake in the evening and after breakfast the next morning (Figure 1). The patient was advised to avoid alcohol intake, but frequent PVCs still appeared after meals on repeat patch monitoring (Figure 2). Maintaining alcohol abstinence, a potassium-competitive acid blocker was prescribed. A third patch monitoring revealed frequent PVCs occurring exclusively after dinner (Figure 3). The patient reported having overeaten during that meal. Subsequently, the patient was instructed to avoid overeating in addition to abstaining from alcohol while on potassium-competitive acid blocker therapy (PCAB). There was a decrease in the frequency of PVCs compared with the previous examination (Figure 4). Patient 2

History of presentation

A 40-year-old woman presented with palpitations. No significant abnormalities were observed in routine chemistry, chest x-ray, ECG, or echocardiogram. Patch monitoring indicated the PVCs occurred during 12.4% of the total QRS complexes. Initially, a beta-blocker was tried for her symptomatic PVCs, however it was not effective. Medication was switched to flecainide 100 mg twice daily, and the frequency of PVCs decreased to 2.8%. During the follow-up visit, she reported that her palpitations worsened while lying down. Review of previously performed patch monitoring showed that the majority of PVCs occurred during midnight, after the patient went to bed. Drawing from the experience of case 1, flecainide was withdrawn, and treatment for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) was instituted. Discussion

Since the early 20th century, it has been recognized that upper gastrointestinal tract disorders can provoke a spectrum of cardiovascular manifestations. This clinical phenomenon has been referred to as “Roemheld syndrome” or “gastroesophageal syndrome.” However, with the absence of a plausible pathophysiological mechanism to explain this association, combined with the wide range of cardiovascular symptoms—including chest pain, dyspnea, and rhythm disturbances—this diagnostic entity has gradually become obsolete.

Recently, with respect to cardiovascular symptoms, the association between GERD and cardiac arrhythmias has received increasing attention. In particular, several recent studies have explored the relationship between GERD and atrial fibrillation.1, 2, 3

In contrast, the connection between GERD and ventricular arrhythmias remains less clearly defined. However, in the past 2 to 3 years, several reports have observed that high-burden PVCs occur in patients with various structural esophageal abnormalities, such as hiatal hernia, esophageal stenosis, and achalasia, and these PVCs can be controlled when the underlying esophageal conditions are corrected.4,5 Although some of these patients had concomitant GERD associated with hiatal hernia, there has been no prior report documenting frequent PVCs caused by GERD alone.

In the 2 cases reported here however, the patients exhibited no structural abnormalities of the esophagus and had no prior gastrointestinal symptoms suggestive of GERD. Notably, the patients' frequent PVCs were temporally related to factors aggravating GERD. The near-complete resolution of PVC after treatment for GERD in the second patient suggests that GERD alone may serve as a trigger for ventricular arrhythmias.

This observation is clinically significant because GERD may present solely with cardiac symptoms in certain individuals, without typical reflux-related complaints. Therefore, in patients presenting with frequent PVCs, especially when the ectopy is temporarily related to food intake or conditions known to aggravate reflux, clinicians should consider the possibility of PVCs triggered by the GERD. Before initiating antiarrhythmic pharmacotherapy for symptomatic palpitations, it may be reasonable to perform a therapeutic trial with acid-suppressive agents and lifestyle modifications. Such an approach could not only clarify the underlying cause but also potentially obviate the need for long-term antiarrhythmic therapy.

Conclusions

It is not uncommon to encounter patients presenting with frequent PVCs despite the absence of known predisposing factors. It is possible that GERD could be a contributing cause of frequent PVCs in a proportion of these patients.
