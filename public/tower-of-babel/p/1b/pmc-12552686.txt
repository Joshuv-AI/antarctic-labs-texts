Simulation analysis of traffic flow stability for intelligent connected vehicles at mountain tunnel entrances considering nonlinear coupling effects
Authors: Mengmeng Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe sudden changes in light and slope at the entrance of mountain tunnels can easily lead to unstable traffic flow, especially in mixed traffic flow containing ICV. The existing control methods are difficult to simultaneously address three challenges: dynamically changing lighting and slope environments, vehicle data privacy protection requirements, and insufficient adaptability to different tunnel scenarios. This study developed the MF3DQN-TF control framework to address these issues by integrating environmental perception and vehicle control. The framework first establishes a correlation model between light gradient and slope resistance, converting environmental risks into quantifiable control signals; Secondly, design an intelligent weight allocation mechanism to automatically increase the decision-making priority of environmental factors in strong light areas; Finally, a distributed training architecture is adopted to achieve knowledge sharing in multi tunnel scenarios while protecting vehicle data privacy. The verification results show that in typical tunnel testing scenarios, this framework significantly improves performance compared to traditional methods: the amplitude of speed fluctuations is reduced by about 40% compared to conventional control methods, the risk of rear end collisions is reduced to one-third of that of traditional reinforcement learning schemes, and the communication transmission volume is only half of that of typical federated learning methods. The verification results show that in typical tunnel testing scenarios, this framework reduces speed standard deviation by 41.4% (vs. PID control), cuts high-risk TTC < 2 s events by 62.5% (vs. centralized DQN), and nearly halves communication volume (46.2% reduction vs. FedProx). These improvements stem from the collaborative processing mechanism of the framework for environmental risks and vehicle status, providing a new technological path for safety control in complex tunnel environments.Keywords: Mountain tunnel traffic flow, Nonlinear coupling effect, Federated reinforcement learning, Attention mechanism, Dynamic collaborative control, Robust optimizationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

The entrance section of mountain tunnels is a high-risk area for traffic accidents, which is closely related to its special environment and road conditions1,2. When a vehicle rapidly enters a relatively dim tunnel from a bright outdoor environment, the driver’s eyes need time to adapt to the drastic changes in light (known as the ‘black hole effect’). At the same time, tunnel entrances are often accompanied by significant uphill or downhill slopes (longitudinal gradient)3–5. The combined effect of sudden changes in light and slope can significantly interfere with the driver’s judgment and the normal driving state of the vehicle. For mixed traffic flows composed of human driven vehicles (HDVs) and Intelligent Connected Vehicles (ICVs), the problem is even more complex. The response time of HDV drivers to sudden changes in light varies greatly, and the sensors and control algorithms of CAVs may also be affected by the boundaries of light6–8. These factors are intertwined and can easily lead to unstable vehicle speed, abnormal changes in following distance, and even rear end collisions, posing a serious threat to driving safety (as shown in Fig. 1).

Real-time display interface of environmental monitoring parameters at the entrance section of a mountain tunnel.

In order to address the issue of traffic flow stability at tunnel entrances, researchers have attempted various control methods, but all have obvious limitations9. Traditional control methods, such as PID control or Model Predictive Control (MPC), typically use fixed control parameters. However, the combination of light gradient and slope at the entrance of the tunnel is dynamically changing, and fixed parameters are difficult to adapt to this complex and ever-changing environment, resulting in poor control effectiveness10,11. Although centralized intelligent control methods (such as certain reinforcement learning algorithms) can perform global optimization, they require uploading detailed driving data of all vehicles to a central server for processing12–14. This not only brings a huge communication burden, but more seriously violates increasingly strict data privacy protection regulations (such as GDPR), and there is a risk of central server failure causing the entire system to crash. In addition, existing methods often separate light changes, slope effects, and vehicle control, ignoring their close interactions and making it difficult to accurately capture and respond to the comprehensive effects of such coupling effects15.

As the proportion of CAV in traffic flow gradually increases, the control of mixed traffic flow faces new challenges. There are essential differences in the decision-making methods between HDV and CAV: HDV relies on real-time perception and response from the driver, while CAV is controlled based on sensor data and preset algorithms16–18. In an environment with drastic changes in light and slope at the tunnel entrance, the reaction delay of HDV drivers may conflict with the control rhythm of CAVs. This type of conflict amplifies speed fluctuations, forming “speed oscillations” that propagate backwards, reducing the efficiency and safety of the entire traffic flow. Although existing distributed learning methods, such as federated learning, can protect local data privacy, their model update mechanisms often do not fully consider the critical impact of environmental abrupt factors, such as strong light gradients, on control decisions19. Meanwhile, the control model trained in a tunnel scene is often difficult to directly apply to other tunnels with different lighting conditions and slope designs, lacking the necessary adaptability and generalization ability.

In response to the above challenges, this article proposes a new intelligent control framework called MF3DQN-TF, aimed at improving the stability of mixed traffic flow at the entrance of mountainous tunnels. The core idea of this framework is to synergistically consider and integrate environmental changes (Fig. 2) with vehicle control. Specifically, we first establish a mathematical model to quantify the dynamic coupling relationship between the gradient of sudden light changes and slope resistance and integrate these key environmental factors into the vehicle’s state information, enabling the control system to “perceive” environmental risks. Secondly, design an intelligent weight allocation mechanism (dual channel attention) that allows the system to dynamically adjust the level of attention to environmental factors such as lighting and slope, as well as the vehicle’s own state (such as speed and distance) based on the real-time environmental risk during decision-making. Finally, combining federated learning and transfer learning techniques: Federated learning is used for model collaborative training while protecting the privacy of vehicle data; Transfer learning helps to effectively apply learned control knowledge to new and different tunnel scenarios. Through the integration of collaborative perception, intelligent decision-making, and knowledge transfer, the MF3DQN-TF framework strives to achieve more stable and safe mixed traffic flow control in complex and ever-changing tunnel entrance environments.

Modeling of nonlinear coupling effects

Theoretical basis

The safety risk at the entrance of the tunnel arises from the physical interaction between sudden changes in light and changes in slope20. According to the theory of driving visual adaptation, the human eye experiences a brief visual blind spot ("black hole effect") when entering dark areas from bright spots, leading to distorted judgments of distance and speed by the driver. Meanwhile, vehicle dynamics theory suggests that longitudinal slope can significantly alter driving resistance21,22. The coupling effect of these two environmental factors creates a conflict between the driver’s cognitive load and the vehicle’s motion state, which is the fundamental cause of safety hazards.

A Nash equilibrium game framework is adopted to address the decision-making differences between human driven vehicles and connected autonomous vehicles23–25. This theory models the conservative actions of HDV drivers (such as sudden braking to avoid danger) and the system optimization objectives of CAV (such as fleet efficiency) as interest game players. By solving for the optimal strategy, CAV can generate guidance instructions (such as progressive speed regulation), bridge the response time difference between the two types of vehicles, and achieve overall traffic stability26.

To meet data security regulations, a control network is constructed based on federated learning architecture27. Its core is the principle of localized training: vehicles independently learn control strategies on terminal devices and only share encrypted model parameters to the aggregation center. Combined with differential privacy technology, noise interference is added to the transmission parameters to ensure that the original driving trajectory is not traceable28–30. This architecture synchronously adapts to the weak network environment of mountainous tunnels and breaks through the communication bottleneck of traditional centralized transmission.

In the face of spatial heterogeneity in tunnel environments, attention mechanisms are introduced to achieve dynamic allocation of decision-making resources31,32. This theory is based on real-time risk levels (such as light gradient intensity) and automatically adjusts the weight priority of environmental factors in control decisions. In response to the adaptation needs of new scenarios, domain adaptation transfer theory is adopted, and feature space mapping technology is used to transfer the control knowledge of validated scenarios to the new tunnel, significantly reducing the cost of repetitive training33.

Analysis of the impact of attention weights.

Experiments have confirmed the existence of nonlinear correlation in key state responses. Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

The MF3DQN-TF framework proposed in this study achieves a significant breakthrough in controlling traffic flow stability at the entrance of mountain tunnels. As shown in Table 22, the speed standard deviation is reduced to 5.1 km/h, a 41.4% decrease compared to traditional PID control and a 17.7% improvement over centralized DQN. This breakthrough stems from the dynamic weighting of environmental change factors by the dual-channel attention mechanism, effectively addressing the issue of prediction errors as high as 30% under sudden changes in lighting conditions that traditional models face. Crucially, the Lyapunov exponent remains stable at −0.35, confirming the inhibitory effect of the light-slope coupled dynamical model on chain instability. In terms of safety performance, high-risk events with TTC < 2 s were reduced to 3 per 10,000 vehicle kilometers, a 62.5% reduction compared to centralized DQN. This is attributed to the synergistic optimization of slope resistance and illuminance gradients by the federated reward function, which controls the braking temperature peak to 498 °C. Notably, the excellent performance of a lateral offset of 0.18 m validates the aerodynamic model’s ability to compensate for crosswind disturbances, addressing the issue of braking errors exceeding the limit by 32% on sections with a gradient exceeding 6%.
