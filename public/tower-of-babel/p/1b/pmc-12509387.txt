Deep learning-based coronary calcium score derived from non-gated chest CT and major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Yidan Xu, Yarong Yu, Xiaoying Ding, Jiajun Yuan, Lihua Yu, Xu Dai, Runjianya Ling, Yufan Wang, Jiayin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep learning (DL) models can quantify coronary artery calcification using non-gated chest CT scans. However, the prognostic value of a DL-based coronary artery calcium score (DL-CACS) for predicting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) remains unclear.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of DL-CACS derived from non-gated chest CT scans in patients with T2DM and to develop a risk stratification model for predicting MACEs.MethodsPatients with T2DM who underwent non-gated chest CT scans were retrospectively included and followed up for at least 2 years. Patients from Hospital A were randomly assigned to a training cohort and an internal validation cohort in a 3:2 ratio. Two predictive models were developed in the training cohort: Model 1 used the Framingham risk score (FRS), and Model 2 incorporated FRS and DL-CACS. The external validation cohort from Hospital B and the internal validation cohort were used to validate the proposed model.ResultsA total of 2,241 patients with T2DM (median age, 61 years; range, 54–68 years; 1,257 males) were included in this study. MACEs occurred in 10.71% (240/2241) of patients during follow-up. Patients who experienced MACEs exhibited significantly higher DL-CACS values than those without MACEs (p < 0.001). In the training cohort, multivariate Cox regression analysis identified DL-CACS as an independent predictor of MACEs (hazard ratio [HR], 1.07; p < 0.001). Moreover, Model 2 demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to Model 1 across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. In the external validation cohort, the C-index of Model 2 was larger than that of Model 1 (C-Index, 0.70 [0.63–0.77] vs. 0.67 [0.61–0.74]; p = 0.007).ConclusionDL-CACS derived from non-gated chest CT is an independent predictor of MACEs and provides incremental value in risk stratification for patients with T2DM compared with the FRS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02934-y.Keywords: Diabetes, Coronary artery calcium score, Deep learning, Major adverse cardiac events

Introduction

As of 2014, the World Health Organization reported that > 422 million adults globally were living with diabetes mellitus (DM), a number that continues to rise [1]. Patients with DM have a 2–4 times greater risk of developing coronary artery disease (CAD) compared to those without DM [1, 2]. The pathological mechanisms underlying the connection between DM and atherosclerosis involve factors such as dyslipidemia and hyperglycemia. These conditions contribute to the production of advanced glycation end-products, increased oxidative stress, and inflammation [2, 3].

The metabolic disturbances associated with DM play a crucial role in the accelerated progression of atherosclerosis, a condition characterized by plaque accumulation in the arterial walls [4]. Moreover, cardiovascular diseases, particularly CAD, are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among patients with type 2 DM (T2DM) [5]. This highlights the importance of precise risk stratification in patients with DM to guide appropriate treatment strategies and improve prognosis. The Framingham risk score (FRS), an established tool for predicting the risk of developing CAD within the next 10 years, is based on known CAD risk factors [6, 7]. However, FRS does not account for early calcification changes in the coronary arteries, which is an important pathological feature of atherosclerosis.

The coronary artery calcium score (CACS) is a valuable tool for predicting cardiovascular events in asymptomatic individuals. This scoring system directly quantifies the coronary artery calcium burden [8–11]. CACS is not only a marker of atherosclerosis but also a strong and independent predictor of cardiovascular events [12, 13]. Notably, the 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of chronic coronary syndromes[14] explicitly recommend the use of CACS in symptomatic patients with low pre-test probability for CAD, further underscoring its clinical relevance in risk stratification. However, the requirement for tailored imaging protocols may hinder the widespread application of CACS [15].

The advancement of artificial intelligence technology has led to the successful development of deep learning (DL) models for quantifying CACS based on chest computed tomography (CT). This automated assessment has significantly enhanced the efficiency and reproducibility of CACS measurements without the requirement for a dedicated gated scan [16–18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that DL-CACS shows high consistency with gated CACS across multicenter datasets [19, 20]. Considering that patients with T2DM typically exhibit diffuse coronary artery calcification, the integration of DL-CACS with the FRS may provide more accurate cardiovascular risk assessment for patients with DM [21].

This study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of DL-CACS in patients with T2DM and to establish a risk stratification model for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs).

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the participating hospitals, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the data collection. Consecutive patients with T2DM who underwent non-gated chest CT scans during admission at two tertiary hospitals between February 2016 and December 2020 were retrospectively included. Patients were admitted due to suboptimal blood glucose control and/or the presence of microvascular complications. These patients with DM were followed up for at least 2 years to assess the prognostic value of DL-CACS in predicting MACEs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a history of percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting, or cardiac metal implants; (2) those with significantly impaired image quality of chest CT; and (3) those who were lost to clinical follow-up. In total, 1,735 patients from Hospital A were randomly assigned to the training and internal validation cohorts in a 3:2 ratio. The external validation cohort included 506 patients from Hospital B.

Chest CT and cardiac protocol

We included chest CT scans performed using six different CT scanners from five vendors, covering various CT hardware configurations: (1) third-generation dual-source CT (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany); (2) 256-row wide detector CT scanner (Revolution, GE Healthcare, USA); (3)80-row 160-slice spiral CT scanner (uCT 780, United Imaging, China); (4) dual-layer detector spectral CT (IQon, Philips Healthcare, Netherlands); (5) 64-row multidetector CT (uCT 760, United Imaging, China); and (6) 320-row wide detector CT scanner (Aquilion ONE, Canon, Japan). The CT scanning parameters were as follows: helical acquisition mode, 120 kV, application of automated current modulation, slice thickness ranging from 0.625 to 1.0 mm, and reconstructed slice interval from 0.625 to 1.0 mm. The aforementioned images were reconstructed using a standard soft tissue kernel on a 512 × 512 matrix. Additional File 1 presents more details regarding the acquisition parameters.

A third-generation dual-source CT (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) or a 256-slice wide detector CT scanner (Revolution HD, GE Healthcare, USA) was used for gated CACS scanning. The slice thicknesses were 3 mm (SOMATOM Force) and 2.5 mm (Revolution HD), with a tube voltage of 120 kVp and an automatically adjusted tube current. Acquisitions included the entire heart, from the carina to the diaphragm level.

Development of the DL-CACS model

We developed a DL framework (uAl Discover-Nongated Coronary Artery Calcium Score, version R001, Shanghai United Imaging Intelligence, China) to quantify coronary artery calcification from non-gated chest CT scans, as illustrated in Online supplemental Figure E1. Initially, a cardiac segmentation model was trained to crop the cardiac region from chest images, thereby establishing the search range for subsequent segmentation of calcification. Following the definition of calcification, threshold segmentation was then performed to identify candidate calcification voxels, characterized by Hounsfield Units exceeding 130[17]. Subsequently, DL models were trained to further screen for calcifications in the four main coronary arteries (i.e., left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary artery) from the candidate voxels. The candidate voxels exhibited a significant class imbalance, with a predominance of negative samples (e.g., bone and other tissue calcifications) over positive samples, which hindered effective model training. To address this issue, a two-stage cascaded segmentation model framework was implemented. In the first stage, a binary classification model was trained to distinguish between positive and negative voxels. By optimizing the classification threshold, this model effectively eliminated most false-positive voxels while retaining nearly all true-positive voxels, thereby achieving a more balanced class distribution for the subsequent stage. The second-stage model further classified the input voxels into five categories, including calcifications in the four main coronary artery branches and an additional category for others. Additional File 1 presents more details regarding the development and sampling method of the DL model. The architecture of the deep-learning-based segmentation network was shown in the Online supplemental Figure E 2.

Validation of the DL-CACS model

To validate the CACS quantification accuracy of this DL model, patients with suspected CAD who underwent both gated CACS acquisition and non-gated chest CT scans within 1 month at a tertiary hospital between April 2021 and April 2024 were retrospectively included as an additional separate cohort (Additional File 1: Online supplemental Fig. E3). Conventional CACS was quantified using the Agatston scoring method, as previously reported [22].

The severity of the calcium burden was categorized according to CACS: 0 (none), 1–99 (mild), 100–399 (moderate), and ≥ 400 (severe) [23]. To test the classification performance of the DL model across different severities of coronary calcification, patients with the four aforementioned categories were selectively included at an even sample size (100 patients of each category). Patients were excluded from further analysis if the DL model failed to proceed with CACS calculation because of severely degraded image quality of chest CT.

The DL model automatically quantified CACS on chest CT images through an end-to-end pipeline, requiring no manual postprocessing intervention. DL-CACS was categorized using the same criteria as the gated CACS. The accuracy of DL-CACS was tested using the gated CACS results as the reference standard. The correlation and quantification differences between CACS derived from the two methods were evaluated (Additional File 1: Online supplemental Figs. E4 and E5 and Table E1).

Clinical follow-up and risk model development

The patients were followed up for at least 2 years or until the occurrence of MACEs. MACEs were defined as all-cause mortality, cardiac death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, revascularization, and rehospitalization due to heart failure or aggravated angina.

The base model, FRS (Model 1), incorporated the following parameters: age, sex, systolic blood pressure, current smoking status, high-density lipoprotein c , and total cholesterol. This model has served as the basis for numerous subsequent studies on predictive models for CAD prognosis [6, 7]. To investigate the incremental value of DL-CACS in improving MACE prediction among patients with DM, a new model (Model 2) was constructed by incorporating DL-CACS into Model 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as means ± standard deviations. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The Spearman correlation coefficient (R) was used to evaluate the correlation between CACS after DL and gated methods. The reliability of the Agatston scores was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between the DL and gated methods, and their agreement was assessed by examining Bland–Altman plots with 95% limits of agreement. Given that the CACS index is right-skewed, the Log transformation was used when assessing the agreement between the continuity values obtained from gated and non-gated CACS measurements. The consistency analysis of categorical variables was performed using the Cohen κ coefficient. The concordance rate of risk classifications between DL-CACS and the reference standard gated CACS (defined as the proportion of patients with matching risk categories relative to the total patient cohort) was computed.

Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test for two-group comparisons, whereas analysis of variance was performed for multigroup analyses. The frequency distributions of the categorical variables were assessed using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when comparing the subgroups, with the test selection guided by the contingency table dimensions.

MACE incidence curves in the three cohorts were developed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with statistical comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Two-sided p-values of < 0.05 were used to denote statistical significance. In the training set, prognostic factors were identified using both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to assess the relationship between clinical and imaging variables and the occurrence of MACEs and to identify independent predictors. To compare the performance between the two models, Harrell’s C-statistics (C-index), likelihood ratio test, and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were employed. Furthermore, the net reclassification index (NRI) was introduced as a statistical tool to gauge how the inclusion of the new variable enhances risk prediction models compared with the baseline model. The models’ goodness of fit was evaluated using calibration curves based on the Brier score, and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was computed. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.4.1).

Results

Demographic data

In total, 2,515 patients with T2DM who underwent non-gated chest CT examinations were initially included between February 2016 and December 2020. Among them, 274 patients were excluded based on the prespecified criteria (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 2,241 patients (mean age, 61.18 ± 11.29 years; 1,257 males) were included in this study. The patients were subsequently divided into a training cohort (n = 1041), an internal validation cohort (n = 694), and an external validation cohort (n = 506). Table 1 presents more details on demographic characteristics. All patients were followed up for a median of 4.69 years (2.67–5.76 years). MACEs occurred in 10.71% (240/2241) of patients. Of the 240 patients who developed MACEs, 6 patients died of cardiac (n = 2) and noncardiac (n = 4) causes, 33 patients experienced acute myocardial infarction, 45 patients underwent revascularization, 6 patients experienced heart failure, and 150 patients were rehospitalized due to aggravated angina.

Efficacy of the DL model for CACS quantification

A strong correlation was observed between gated CACS and DL-CACS (R = 0.976, p < 0.001). (Additional File 1: Online supplemental Fig. E4). Log (DL-CACS + 1) and Log(Gated CACS + 1) demonstrated strong agreement, exhibiting an ICC of 0.980. Simultaneously, Bland–Altman analysis revealed a mean difference of 23.5 between DL-CACS and gated CACS measurements (Additional File 1: Online supplemental Fig. E5). The classification accuracy analysis indicated that DL-CACS categorized calcification severity in 88% (352/400) of cases, as shown by great inter-method agreement (κ = 0.83) (Additional File 1: Online supplemental Table E1). Representative cases of patients with gated CACS and DL-CACS were shown in Fig. 2.

Comparison of clinical and imaging parameters between patients with and without MACEs

Regarding conventional clinical parameters, older age was observed in the MACE group in the training and external validation cohorts. Table 2 provides more details about the parameter comparison. In both the internal and external validation cohorts, individuals with MACEs had higher DL-CACS values than those without MACEs (Table 2). Furthermore, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with DL-CACS values of ≥ 400 exhibited greater rates of MACEs than those without severe calcification (Fig. 3). The log-rank test revealed significant differences in MACE rates between the DL-CACS:0 and DL-CACS:1–99 groups in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts (p < 0.001, p = 0.015, and p = 0.001, respectively).

Discussion

This study had two major findings. First, DL-CACS was identified as a strong and independent predictor of MACEs in patients with T2DM. Second, combining the FRS with DL-CACS improved cardiovascular risk stratification compared with the conventional FRS model.

Individuals with DM exhibit 2–4 times higher CAD risk than populations without DM [2]. Conventional clinical risk factors exhibited limited predictive accuracy for cardiovascular events in patients with T2DM, particularly considering the high prevalence of subclinical atherosclerosis in this population[6, 7]. A multiethnic cohort study revealed that measurement of coronary artery calcium added incremental value to the prediction of CAD risk over that of clinical risk factors [24]. Conventional calcium quantification depends on the electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated method, necessitating dedicated cardiac protocols, which may restrict their broad clinical utility. However, non-gated CT chest scans are widely used without ECG gating. The findings from a meta-analysis demonstrated that > 50% of patients undergoing routine non-gated chest CT for noncardiac indications exhibit detectable coronary calcification [25], indicating that deriving CACS from easily accessible non-gated chest CT scans offers value for stratifying cardiovascular risk in patients with DM.

Recent advancements in DL algorithms for CACS calculation from chest CT scans have exhibited significant progress [26]. DL methods can automatically extract coronary artery calcification information from chest CT images to calculate CACS without manual postprocessing, achieving reliability comparable to that of traditional approaches [19, 27, 28]. In this study, the non-gated CACS derived from the DL model exhibited an excellent correlation (R = 0.976, p < 0.001) with the conventional gated CACS method, which is similar to the findings from a previous study (R = 0.96, p < 0.001) [25]. Furthermore, the agreement between DL-CACS and gated CACS (κ = 0.83) surpassed the consensus levels reported in earlier studies (κ = 0.68–0.77) [19, 29]. Consequently, DL-CACS may be a clinically feasible alternative imaging biomarker to conventional gated CACS.

Based on the aforementioned findings, DL-CACS was used for cardiovascular risk stratification in patients with DM in this study. Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that DL-CACS was an independent predictor of MACEs. Kaplan–Meier curve analysis indicated that patients with DM with DL-CACS of ≥ 400 exhibited a higher cumulative incidence of MACEs than other groups. These findings were consistent with those of a previous study on gated CACS [30], demonstrating the reliability of DL-CACS as a robust biomarker. Furthermore, the new model incorporating DL-CACS exhibited improved predictive value for long-term MACEs in patients with T2DM compared with the FRS (AUC: 0.67 vs. 0.73, p < 0.001). These results were consistent with those of a previous study on gated CACS in patients with DM (AUC: 0.70 vs. 0.75, p < 0.001) (31), demonstrating that DL-CACS, comparable to traditional gated CACS, outperformed conventional clinical factors in both cardiovascular risk stratification and predicting MACEs. Thus, a fully automated DL pipeline for coronary calcium quantification in our study, which eliminates manual calcium quantification steps through direct image segmentation and analysis, has potential applications in predicting MACEs in patients with DM.

Considering the aforementioned findings, the clinical implications of this study lie in the following aspects. First, the proposed DL model enables fully automated, accurate CACS quantification from non-gated chest CT scans. This approach may serve as an effective method for streamlining clinical workflows for quantifying coronary calcification. Furthermore, DL-CACS exhibited an incremental prognostic value beyond the established model (FRS) for predicting MACEs in patients with DM over long-term follow-up. This enhanced predictive capability enables greater risk stratification accuracy and potentially facilitates personalized treatment strategies for high-risk patients with DM.

Despite the aforementioned findings, this study has two limitations. First, our study enrolled in-hospital patients with T2DM, who were at more advanced stage of the disease. Additional studies are required to assess whether these findings can be generalized to individuals in the earlier phases of DM or those with milder clinical manifestations. Furthermore, DL-CACS can be used as an indicator of the overall coronary atherosclerosis burden in patients. However, DM inherently accelerates the development of coronary plaques, including noncalcified lesions. Therefore, the risk of patients with DM and coronary atherosclerosis burden may be underestimated by the proposed model. In conclusion, DL-CACS derived from chest CT was an independent predictor of MACEs in the cohorts with DM. The combined FRS + DL-CACS model provided incremental value for CAD risk stratification.
