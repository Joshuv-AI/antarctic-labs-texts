Bladder metastasis arising from right: sided invasive lobular carcinoma in bilateral synchronous discordant primary breast cancers—a rare case
Authors: Ting Wen, Shiying Liu, Xiaomei Li, Yingjie Qi, Jintao Hu, Minjie Yang, Donghong Peng, Guijing Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bladder metastasis of breast cancer is clinically rare, and there is no report on simultaneous bilateral breast cancer with different pathological types complicated by bladder metastasis. This study reports a case of bladder metastasis in a patient with simultaneous bilateral breast cancer of different pathological types. The patient is a 71-year-old woman who was diagnosed with simultaneous bilateral breast cancer six years ago (invasive lobular carcinoma in the right breast and invasive carcinoma of no special type in the left breast). She received adjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and endocrine therapy after surgery. Four years ago, she developed vertebral metastasis. In January 2022, she presented with intermittent gross hematuria. Imaging studies showed diffuse thickening and enhancement of the bladder wall with surrounding infiltration on CT and hydronephrosis on ultrasound. Biopsy confirmed metastasis of invasive lobular carcinoma of the breast. The patient was diagnosed with stage IV Her2-positive breast cancer with bone and bladder metastasis. This paper aims to explore its diagnostic features, treatment strategies, and prognostic factors.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a prevalent global malignancy among women, with common metastases to bone, lung, liver, and brain. However, bladder metastasis is extremely rare, and there has been no report on simultaneous bilateral breast cancer of different pathological types with bladder metastasis. Understanding breast cancer metastasis characteristics is vital for diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis prediction. The diversity of breast cancer subtypes classified by hormone receptor (HR) status and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression complicates metastatic disease management. Here, we report a unique case of a 71-year-old woman diagnosed with simultaneous bilateral breast cancer (right invasive lobular carcinoma and left invasive carcinoma of no special type) eight years ago. After adjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and endocrine therapy, vertebral metastasis occurred six years ago. In January 2022, intermittent gross hematuria led to the discovery of bladder metastasis. This case broadens our knowledge, offers insights into diagnostic features, treatment strategies, and prognostic factors, and contributes to the literature for managing similar patients.

Data collection

Case

A 71-year-old female patient presented to our hospital on January 18, 2022, with intermittent gross hematuria. Three months prior, she experienced urinary frequency, urgency, and incomplete voiding without any obvious cause. Ten days before presentation, she had intermittent gross hematuria with dark red urine. To better evaluate the patient’s condition, a multi-disciplinary team (MDT) consisting of urologists, oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists was assembled. The MDT decided to conduct a PET-CT scan, which provided comprehensive information about the distribution of possible metastases in the body. The tumor marker CA15-3 was significantly elevated at 350 ng/mL (normal range: 28 ng/mL).

The patient was hospitalized in our hospital eight years ago due to “discovering masses in both breasts for 5 days”. Preoperative physical examination showed that a mass about 2.5 cm × 1.0 cm in size could be felt at the 10 o’clock position in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast. It was moderately firm, with an uneven surface, unclear boundaries, no tenderness, good mobility, and no adhesion to the chest wall. Local thickening with mild tenderness was found at the 3 o’clock position of the left breast, and no obvious mass was palpable in the left breast. Enlarged lymph nodes were palpable in the right axilla, and no enlarged lymph nodes were felt in the supraclavicular and infraclavicular regions on both sides.

Medical history revealed bilateral breast cancer diagnosed eight years ago. The right breast underwent right axillary sentinel lymph node biopsy and modified radical mastectomy, revealing invasive lobular carcinoma with hormone receptor (HR) status ER (+) 3%, PR (+) 3%, Ki-67 (+) approximately 5%, and HER2 (-). The left breast underwent left total mastectomy and left axillary sentinel lymph node biopsy, revealing invasive ductal carcinoma with HR status ER (+) approximately 90%, PR (+) approximately 90%, Ki-67 (+) approximately 10%, and HER2 (-). Six years ago, multiple vertebral metastases were discovered (Fig. 1D), confirmed as metastases from invasive lobular carcinoma with ER (-), PR (+), HER2 (1+), and Ki-67 (approximately 15%+) (Figs. 1, 2 and 3).

Breast MRI (as shown in Fig. 1A–C): There was extensive non-mass enhancement in the right breast, with an irregular shape, mainly involving the upper outer quadrant. The internal signal was heterogeneous on dynamic contrast-enhanced scanning, and the TIC curve showed a rapid - rise and slow - decline pattern.

Physical examination revealed no palpable masses in the renal areas, no tenderness upon percussion over the kidney areas, and no tenderness at the ureteral points. The bladder was not distended, and no enlarged lymph nodes were palpated throughout the body. Ultrasound of bladder: A localized elevation was observed on the right posterior wall of the bladder, measuring approximately 42 × 13 mm, with solid hypoechoic features and relatively smooth surface, with punctate blood flow signals seen around it.

CT (Fig. 2): Diffuse thickening of the bladder wall, notably affecting the left, posterior, and bottom walls, showed significant enhancement on contrast-enhanced scan. The lesion’s border with the cervix and vagina was unclear. Mild dilation of the left ureter with hydronephrosis was noted. Uneven thickening and enhancement were observed in the lower segment wall of the left ureter and pelvic peritoneum. Multiple lymph nodes were seen in the left iliac vessel area and bilateral inguinal areas, with the largest one measuring approximately 8 mm in short diameter. Conclusion: The findings in the bladder suggested bladder cancer, invading the lower segment of the left ureter, cervix, vagina, and peritoneum.

Bladder cystoscopy and biopsy revealed infiltrative carcinoma tissue consistent with metastatic lobular breast carcinoma. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed ER (-), PR (-), HER2 (30%, 1–2+), and Ki-67 (approximately 20%+). FISH analysis revealed HER2 amplification (Fig. 3). Final clinical diagnosis: Stage IV metastatic breast cancer to bone and bladder, HER2-positive subtype. Treatment included trastuzumab 400 mg/dL, albumin paclitaxel 300 mg/dl, and carboplatin 300 mg/dL for 4 cycles of chemotherapy.

Combined with the clinical history and immunohistochemistry, it was clearly confirmed that the patient had bladder metastasis from breast cancer. After 5 cycles of chemotherapy with paclitaxel, carboplatin, and trastuzumab, the patient reported poor tolerance to chemotherapy. Therefore, the treatment regimen was changed to a 13-cycle treatment of paclitaxel combined with HP dual-target therapy, followed by trastuzumab deruxtecan for injection. Since the diagnosis of bladder metastasis, continuous follow-up observations have shown that after 4 months of chemotherapy, the abdominal contrast-enhanced CT re-examination revealed that the thickening and enhancement of the bladder wall were reduced, and the lymph nodes were shrunk. In the subsequent 3 CT re-examinations, no obvious abnormalities were found in the bladder. After chemotherapy, the CA15-3 level gradually returned to normal and did not rise again. Treatment regimens

Six years ago: After the diagnosis of bilateral breast cancer, for the right breast invasive lobular carcinoma and left breast invasive carcinoma of no special type, considering the hormone receptor status and other pathological features, the patient might have received chemotherapy regimens such as anthracycline - based (e.g., doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide) followed by taxane - based (e.g., paclitaxel) chemotherapy. Endocrine therapy was also likely initiated, but due to the low expression of ER and PR in the right breast (about 3% for both), the endocrine therapy effect might be limited. Radiotherapy might have been carried out for the right breast and axillary area considering the axillary lymph node metastasis.

Six years ago: After the discovery of multiple vertebral metastases, in addition to continuing endocrine therapy, a new round of chemotherapy might have been adopted, such as a combination of platinum - based drugs (e.g., cisplatin) and other chemotherapy drugs. Radiotherapy was probably given to the metastatic vertebral sites to relieve pain and control local tumor growth. Endocrine therapy was adjusted according to the new hormone receptor status of the metastatic lesions. However, specific treatment details would need to be further confirmed from the patient’s complete medical records.

Discussion

The time interval between the occurrence of breast cancer on both sides being less than 6 months is defined as synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC). The incidence of SBBC among all breast cancer patients ranges from 1% to 3%, and the pathological types of breast cancer in SBBC are often the same [1]. This case presents a rare instance of breast cancer with different pathological types occurring simultaneously in both breasts. Common metastatic sites of breast cancer include bones, lungs, liver, and brain, while the bladder is an uncommon site for breast cancer metastasis. Bladder metastases are defined as secondary tumors involving the bladder through lymphovascular spread, excluding direct spread or invasion from adjacent organs such as the colon, rectum, prostate, and cervix [2]. A study that conducted autopsies on 1,000 cases of epithelial origin malignant tumors found that only 4 out of 167 breast cancer cases had bladder metastasis [3].

The clinical manifestations of bladder metastases are not specific, and the most common manifestation is microscopic or gross hematuria. The clinical symptoms are similar to those of primary bladder tumors, but their treatment methods are different. The main treatment method for primary bladder cancer is transurethral bladder tumor resection, while the treatment method for metastatic bladder cancer is comprehensive treatment focusing on treating the primary disease. Therefore, the differential diagnosis between the two has certain clinical significance.

In my country, the incidence of primary bladder cancer in women is low [4], but the incidence of bladder metastasis from breast cancer is also very rare. The identification of the two mainly relies on pathological diagnosis, and imaging mainly detects lesions. There have been no previous differential - diagnosis reports. This article reviews relevant literature in Chinese or English at home and abroad. A total of 28 case reports of breast cancer bladder metastasis include descriptions of B-ultrasound or CT images. Among them, 93% (26/28) of bladder metastases showed bladder wall thickening, and 32% of them (9/28) showed diffuse thickening, and 39% (11/28) showed localized thickening, which was common on the left wall and posterior wall. Mass formation was rare and was often accompanied by renal or hydroureter. Bladder metastasis in this case showed diffuse thickening and enhancement of the bladder wall, with involvement of the left and posterior walls, without the formation of a mass, and mild dilatation of the left urinary tract with water accumulation, which is consistent with the characteristics of metastatic bladder cancer. Primary bladder cancer often occurs in the trigone area, bladder neck and ureteral orifice area, and rarely occurs in the side wall of the bladder. It often presents as a localized thickening of the bladder wall or a mass protruding into the cavity, often accompanied by significant swelling of abdominal and pelvic lymph nodes.

In addition, WANG G et al. [5] and Khan, N. A. J. et al. [6] reported that the tumor indicator CA15-3 is closely related to breast cancer bladder metastasis. The level of CA15-3 changes with the progression and outcome of the disease, which is consistent with the performance of this case. No clear relevant tumor markers have been found for primary bladder cancer.

The pathological type of bladder metastasis in this patient is invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) [7, 8]. To better understand why ILC is more prone to metastasize to the bladder, a more in - depth exploration of the underlying mechanisms is necessary. ILC has a unique biological behavior compared to other breast cancer subtypes. The cells of ILC lack E-cadherin, a protein that plays a crucial role in cell - cell adhesion. The loss of E-cadherin leads to a decrease in cell-cell adhesion, enabling the cancer cells to detach more easily from the primary tumor mass [9]. Once detached, these cells can enter the lymphatic or blood vessels and circulate throughout the body. The bladder has a rich blood and lymphatic supply. The detached ILC cells may reach the bladder through the bloodstream or lymphatic system. Moreover, the microenvironment of the bladder may provide a suitable niche for the survival and growth of ILC cells. The bladder wall is composed of multiple layers of cells, including smooth muscle cells, epithelial cells, and connective tissue [10]. The extracellular matrix in the bladder wall contains various growth factors and cytokines that may promote the adhesion, proliferation, and survival of ILC cells. In addition, some studies have suggested that ILC cells may have specific chemotactic responses to certain factors secreted by the bladder tissue [11]. These factors can attract ILC cells to the bladder, leading to the establishment of metastatic lesions. In contrast, invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) manifests as discrete nodules and usually metastasizes to the lungs, liver, bones, and brain. IDC cells have relatively stronger cell - cell adhesion compared to ILC cells, which may limit their ability to detach and spread to the bladder [12]. The micro - environmental requirements for IDC cell survival and growth are different from those of ILC cells, and the bladder microenvironment may not be as favorable for IDC cell metastasis.

Breast cancer bladder metastasis occurs from 1 month to 30 years after the diagnosis of breast cancer [13]. Before bladder metastasis occurs, patients may have metastasis in other parts of the body. The most common ones are bone metastasis and lung metastasis, which is different from this case. Generally speaking, when breast cancer patients develop bladder metastasis, the prognosis is worse and the survival time is shorter than when it metastasizes to bones, and most patients die within 2 years [13]. This may be related to the fact that metastases that occur in the bladder are difficult to detect in the early stages and are often diagnosed in the late stages of the disease. At this time, they are often combined with metastases in other parts of the body, and tumor cells in the bladder are easy to spread and difficult to remove.

In addition, an MDT for breast cancer bladder metastasis comprises multiple specialists. Pathologists make accurate diagnoses of the pathological type, which is the basis for treatment. Radiologists use imaging to detect and assess lesions and metastases, providing reports for the team. Medical oncologists develop systemic treatment strategies like chemotherapy, targeted or endocrine therapy, choosing drugs according to the patient’s condition. Urologists handle local treatment, performing surgery when possible and managing post-op complications. Radiation oncologists offer local radiation for non-surgical patients or as adjuvant treatment, controlling tumors and relieving symptoms. Nurses take charge of patient care, monitoring vital signs, administering drugs, offering psychological support, and educating patients. MDT meetings enable in - depth communication. Team members review the patient’s condition, share research and experience, and jointly make treatment decisions, avoiding single - discipline limitations and ensuring comprehensive, personalized treatment. In summary, the MDT approach is crucial for diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of breast cancer bladder metastasis, enhancing diagnosis accuracy, optimizing treatment, and improving patient prognosis and quality of life.

Conclusion

Metastatic breast cancer to the bladder is rare, typically diagnosed years after breast cancer diagnosis. Imaging findings of diffuse or focal bladder wall thickening without mass formation, along with significantly elevated tumor markers such as CA15-3, in patients with a history of breast cancer, particularly invasive lobular carcinoma, should raise suspicion for metastatic breast cancer to the bladder. Early detection, diagnosis, and treatment may improve patient outcomes.
