Green-synthesized MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites: structural characterization, cytotoxicity, and potential for targeted cervical cancer therapy
Authors: Amany I Almars
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer remains a major global health challenge, with high mortality rates particularly in developing countries, and is often associated with poor prognosis in advanced stages. The limitations of conventional chemotherapeutics, such as systemic toxicity, poor targeting efficiency, and drug resistance, have prompted the exploration of nanotechnology-based therapeutic strategies. In this study, manganese ferrite (MnFe₂O₄) nanoparticles were synthesized via both chemical co-precipitation and a green hydrothermal method employing Urtica (nettle) extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. Graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets were prepared through a modified Hummers method and integrated with MnFe₂O₄ to form MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites. Physicochemical characterization using FT-IR, XRD, SEM-EDX, TEM, and DLS confirmed the successful synthesis of pure, crystalline spinel MnFe₂O₄ and its stable incorporation into GO matrices, with particle sizes in the nanoscale range and negative zeta potentials supporting colloidal stability. Magnetic measurements revealed superparamagnetic behavior for all samples, with saturation magnetization (Ms) values of 55 emu/g for pristine MnFe₂O₄, 40 emu/g for green-synthesized MnFe₂O₄, and 25 emu/g for MnFe₂O₄/GO, remaining within ranges suitable for magnetic targeting and hyperthermia. In vitro cytotoxicity against HeLa cells demonstrated enhanced anticancer activity for the MnFe₂O₄/GO composite (IC₅₀ = 120.7 µg/mL) compared to MnFe₂O₄ (IC₅₀ = 200.7 µg/mL) and GO (IC₅₀ = 1202 µg/mL), indicating a synergistic effect. qPCR analysis showed significant upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (BAX, Caspase-3) and downregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene (Bcl-2), confirming apoptosis induction as a key mechanism of action. The results highlight MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites as promising multifunctional platforms for targeted cervical cancer therapy, with combined magnetic responsiveness, structural stability, and potent pro-apoptotic activity.Keywords: MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles, Graphene oxide, Cervical cancer, Green synthesis, Nanocomposites, Cytotoxicity, Apoptosis

Introduction

Cervical cancer originates from the uncontrolled proliferation of epithelial cells in the cervix [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), this form of cancer is a major contributor to mortality worldwide among women globally. In 2020, It is estimated that cervical cancer resulted in approximately 604,000 newly diagnosed cases and 342,000 deaths. Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer affecting women worldwide, preceded only by breast, colorectal, and lung cancers. The worldwide incidence rate is approximately 13.1 per 100,000 women [2]. This cancer poses a significant health challenge, especially in developing nations where 80% of the cases are reported. Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women globally, with its impact being particularly severe in developing nations [3]. The outlook for individuals with advanced or recurrent cervical cancer is especially bleak, with only around 10–20% surviving beyond one year [4]. Treatment approaches for cervical cancer are primarily based on the stage of the disease and typically involve a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapies, and localized interventions [5]. In the initial phases of cervical cancer (stages IA to IIA), surgery is often the preferred approach for treatment. To minimize the risk of recurrence or metastasis, this is usually accompanied by the direct administration of drug formulations to the cervical region. However, the clinical application of some chemotherapy drugs, like paclitaxel, gemcitabine, and cisplatin is restricted due to various side effects. These include neutropenia, hypersensitivity reactions, low blood pressure, peripheral neuropathy, gastrointestinal issues, and hair loss. Such adverse effects can lead to poor patient adherence and ultimately, treatment failure [6, 7].

Recently, innovative strategies using metallic, magnetic, and semiconductor nanoparticles (NPs) of various sizes and shapes have emerged, significantly advancing cancer nanomedicine. Cancer nanotechnology has been crucial in not only limiting the growth of primary tumors but also in potentially eliminating malignant tissues that have spread to other parts of the body through metastasis and are unresponsive to conventional treatments. Because of their diminutive dimensions, nanoparticles possess an extensive surface area, which enhances their binding affinity with various classic chemotherapy drugs, ligands, and antibodies. This characteristic facilitates targeted delivery and controlled release of these therapeutic agents in a precise and safe manner [8]. Magnetic NPs are particularly notable among the diverse nanomaterials explored in nanomedicine, owing to their distinctive characteristics. These properties encompass superparamagnetic behavior, an extensive specific surface area, remarkable colloidal stability, straightforward surface functionalization, and exceptional compatibility with biological systems. These attributes make them highly attractive for research and development in advanced drug delivery systems [9]. Spinel-type ferrites have attracted considerable interest from researchers because of their wide range of applications and favorable properties [10]. These ferrites possess a cubic spinel lattice structure, typically expressed by the formula MFe2O4, where M stands for a divalent metal ion like Zn, Mn, Ni, Co, or Cu. MFe₂O₄ NPs offer several benefits, including low toxicity, high biocompatibility, and excellent redox activity. These properties are largely attributed to the ability of their spinel structures to facilitate electron exchange between different valence states, which also contributes to their favorable electrical performance [11]. Manganese ferrite (MnFe₂O₄) is distinguished from other ferrites due to its remarkable magnetic and electromagnetic properties [12]. This material, in the form of NPs, nanostructures, or thin films, displays a range of intriguing features. These include a high anisotropy constant, superparamagnetic behavior, size-dependent variations in saturation magnetization, and a notably elevated Curie temperature. The unique characteristics of MnFe₂O₄ NPs have made them suitable for a variety of applications.

These encompass various applications such as magnetic recording, microwave technology, contrast agents for MRI, ferrofluids, systems for targeted drug delivery, magnetic tunnel junction sensors, photocatalytic processes, gas detection, and the absorption of heated gases [13]. Graphene, a material made up of a single layer of carbon atoms connected by sp² bonds in a two-dimensional lattice, offers significant promise for various applications [14]. Its excellent electrical and thermal conductivity has opened up possibilities for applications such as inducing hyperthermia. Furthermore, due to its expansive surface area and exceptional chemical stability, graphene serves as an excellent base for the incorporation of metal oxide NPs [15]. Graphene and graphene oxide (GO) at the nanoscale are recognized for their compatibility with biological systems and their low toxicity, which makes them ideal for application as effective nanocarriers in drug delivery and gene therapy applications [16]. In recent times, considerable focus has been placed on creating magnetic nanocomposites by combining superparamagnetic NPs with GO nanosheets. This method has attracted attention because of the distinctive characteristics and promising applications of these composite materials [18–20]. Integrating MnFe₂O₄ NPs with GO merges the unique characteristics of both materials into a unified system. The GO/MnFe₂O₄ magnetic composites have attracted considerable interest in the biomedical field because of their multifaceted functionality, particularly as drug carriers [17]. The carboxyl groups present on the surface of GO sheets are capable of forming robust coordination bonds with MnFe₂O₄ NPs. By functionalizing or anchoring these magnetic NPs onto the carbon-based platform, which offers a large surface area, facilitating the creation of NPs clusters is effectively minimized, resulting in a more stable hybrid nanostructure. The numerous functional groups found on the surface of GO offer numerous possibilities for additional modifications. The uniform distribution of MnFe2O₄ NPs on the GO platform enhances both circulation time and thermal efficiency, particularly in magnetic hyperthermia therapy, highlighting its promising potential for applications in the biomedical field [18, 19].

Materials and methods

Materials

The study employed the following resources and materials. ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6 H₂O), manganese chloride (MnCl₂), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), graphite, sulfuric acid, sodium nitrite, potassium permanganate, hydrogen peroxide, ethanol, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Additionally, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, streptomycin, and sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis reagents were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). These materials were utilized for NPs synthesis, graphene oxide production, and biological assays.

Preparation of nettle extract

Nettle (Urtica) was gathered from nearby plants and left to dry naturally at room temperature. Once dried, the leaves were ground into a fine powder. To prepare the extract, a solution of 200 mL ethanol mixed with 200 mL deionized water was added to 5 g of the powdered nettle leaves. The mixture was then heated and stirred continuously on a hotplate for 3 h at 60 °C to facilitate extraction. Following the extraction procedure, the mixture was passed through filter paper to separate and eliminate any solid residues. The liquid extract obtained was then kept refrigerated for future utilization [20].

Synthesis of mnfe₂o₄ nanoparticles

Chemical co-precipitation method

MnFe₂O₄ NPs were synthesized via a conventional chemical co-precipitation method. First, 1 mmol of ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6 H₂O, 0.270 g) was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water under constant stirring at 50 °C for 30 min to form the iron precursor solution. Separately, 0.5 mmol of manganese chloride (MnCl₂, 0.063 g) was dissolved in another 25 mL of deionized water and stirred under the same conditions to prepare the manganese precursor solution. In parallel, 20 mL of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 0.1 M) solution was prepared in deionized water to act as a surfactant, then stirred for 15 min until fully dissolved. After preparation, the CTAB solution was added dropwise to the iron precursor solution at room temperature under continuous stirring to ensure proper dispersion. This was followed by the gradual addition of the manganese precursor solution into the same mixture. Subsequently, 20 mL of 2 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was introduced slowly drop by drop over 10 min, adjusting the pH to approximately 11. The mixture was then stirred for an additional 30 min at room temperature to allow for nucleation and growth of NPs. The resulting black precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted components, and dried in an oven at 90 °C for 12 h. This process yielded chemically synthesized MnFe₂O₄ NPs for further characterization [21, 22].

Green hydrothermal synthesis

In the green hydrothermal method, MnFe₂O₄ NPs were synthesized using plant-derived reducing and stabilizing agents from nettle (Urtica) extract. To begin, 1 mmol of FeCl₃·6 H₂O (0.270 g) and 0.5 mmol of MnCl₂ (0.063 g) were each dissolved separately in 25 mL of deionized water. Both solutions were stirred at 50 °C for 30 min to ensure complete dissolution. The iron precursor solution (FeCl₃·6 H₂O) was then combined with 20 mL of freshly prepared nettle extract, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 15 min to allow phytochemical interaction. Next, the manganese chloride solution was slowly introduced into the mixture while stirring continuously.

To initiate NPs formation, 20 mL of 2 M NaOH solution was added dropwise over 10 min, bringing the pH of the mixture to approximately 11. The resulting homogeneous mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 140 °C for 8 h. After the reaction was complete and the system cooled to room temperature, the black precipitate was collected using magnetic separation. The product was thoroughly washed with ethanol and deionized water to remove any residual impurities and then dried in an oven at 90 °C for 12 h. This method offered an environmentally friendly approach to synthesizing MnFe₂O₄ NPs with enhanced biocompatibility, attributed to the presence of plant-derived capping and reducing agents [23].

Synthesis of graphene oxide nanosheets

Graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets were synthesized through an adapted version of the Hummers method [24]. To begin, 0.5 g of graphite (particle size ≤ 2 mm) was dispersed in concentrated sulfuric acid (98% v/v) and stirred for 10 min while maintaining the temperature below 0 °C using an ice bath. Sodium nitrite was subsequently introduced in a quantity matching that of the graphite, and this was followed by the slow incorporation of 3 g of potassium permanganate. The reaction mixture was taken out of the ice bath, and the temperature was gradually increased to 30 °C while stirring continuously for a period of 4 h. Next, 100 mL of water was introduced into the mixture, and the stirring process was maintained for an additional 30 min, during which the temperature was raised to 90 °C. The resulting solution was subjected to exfoliation using ultrasonication (120 W) to separate the GO layers. The mixture was subsequently rinsed several times with water until the pH of the GO suspension stabilized at a neutral level (pH 7). The graphene oxide nanosheets were then placed in an oven and dried at a temperature of 50 °C for a duration of 24 h.

Synthesis of GO–MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles

To synthesize GO–MnFe₂O₄ nanocomposites, 100 mg of MnFe₂O₄ and 2 mL of GO suspension (25 mg/mL) were mixed in a 70 mL solution prepared by combining ethanol and deionized water in equal parts. The mixture was subjected to sonication for 30 min to achieve a consistent and uniform dispersion. After sonication, the solution was placed into an autoclave and subjected to a temperature of 180 °C for a duration of 18 h. After the reaction was complete, the product was purified and freeze-dried, resulting in the formation of GO–MnFe₂O₄ nanocomposites.

Structural and morphological characterization of nanoparticles

FT-IR spectroscopy, utilizing a Nicolet Magna-IR550 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), was used to investigate in the samples the chemical composition and functional groups. The spectra were captured within the wavelength range of 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹. XRD analysis was conducted with an X-Pert-MPD diffractometer (Philips) to identify the crystalline phases present. The test was conducted using Cu-Kα₁ radiation with a wavelength of 1.54060 Å, and it was performed at operating settings of 40 kV and 30 mA. Data were gathered within a 2θ range of 20° to 80°, using a step interval of 0.05° and a dwell time of 1 s for each step. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a VASCO analyzer (Cordouan Technologies, France) was employed to determine the hydrodynamic size and size distribution of the nanoparticles, along with their zeta potential. The microstructural features, elemental distribution, and composition of the NPs were examined using SEM (Mira 3-XMU, TESCAN, Czech) integrated with an energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrometer. Moreover, the particle size and shape were analyzed using TEM with a Philips EM208 TEM instrument. The magnetic characteristics of the prepared NPs were assessed at ambient temperature using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM), specifically the Kavir-Magnet model from Iran.

Cell culture

HeLa cell lines and the HFF-2 (Human Foreskin Fibroblast 2) cell line as a normal cell were maintained in a monolayer culture using DMEM. The medium was enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin to promote cell proliferation. The cells were kept in an incubator with controlled humidity at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. For MTT cytotoxicity assays, HeLa cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h prior to treatment.

Cytotoxicity evaluation of nanoparticles

The cytotoxic effects of the synthesized NPs on cervical cancer cells were evaluated using the standardized MTT assay protocol. HeLa cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with various concentrations of MnFe₂O₄-GR, GO, and MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites (ranging from 0 to 1300 µg/mL) and incubated for a fixed period of 24 h. After treatment, 20 µL of MTT stock solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well containing 100 µL of medium, resulting in a final MTT concentration of approximately 0.83 mg/mL. The cells were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to allow formazan crystal formation. Thereafter, the culture medium was carefully removed, and 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan. Absorbance was measured immediately at 570 nm using a microplate reader. All time points, including treatment duration and MTT incubation, were standardized across all experimental groups to ensure consistency and reproducibility. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

Evaluating the gene expression in the apoptosis pathway

To assess the transcriptional expression levels of the genes Bax, Bcl-2, and Caspase-3 related to apoptosis, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was utilized. RNA extraction kit was utilized to isolate total RNA from the treated cells, and the RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For each sample, 200 nanograms of total RNA were converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) through reverse transcription using the kit. The reverse transcription process utilized oligo-dT and random primers to ensure efficient synthesis of cDNA. The synthesized cDNA was utilized to conduct real-time PCR with primers specifically designed for the target genes, and SYBR Green dye as the fluorescence marker. The thermal cycling program was configured into three phases. Initially, denaturation was conducted at 95 °C for 5 min to separate the cDNA strands. Next, the process involved 45 amplification cycles, with each cycle comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 9 s. Reactions were performed in triplicate for all samples to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The GAPDH gene, a housekeeping gene, served as the internal control to normalize the expression levels of the target genes. This approach offered a dependable way to assess the relative mRNA expression of genes that are implicated in the apoptosis pathway.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0) and SPSS (v16), with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from triplicate experiments. For cytotoxicity assessment (MTT assay), dose-response curves were fitted using nonlinear regression (log(inhibitor vs. response) to calculate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀), with goodness-of-fit determined by R². Comparisons of cell viability across nanoparticle concentrations (0–80 mg/mL) were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to account for multiple comparisons. Gene expression data (Bax, Bcl-2, Caspase-3) were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with log-transformed fold changes analyzed via one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s correction. Normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) were confirmed; nonparametric alternatives (Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc) were applied if assumptions were violated. Particle size distributions (DLS, TEM) were described using descriptive statistics, and Pearson’s correlation assessed agreement between techniques. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

FT-IR analysis

The FTIR spectra were analyzed to determine the chemical functional groups present in MnFe₂O₄ NPs synthesized by both green and chemical routes, GO, and their composite (MnFe₂O₄/GO). As shown in Fig. 1, each sample exhibits characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to its unique composition and method of synthesis.

FTIR spectra of MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles synthesized through chemical (MnFe₂O₄-CH) and green (MnFe₂O₄-GR) methods, GO, and the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite

In the spectrum of green-synthesized MnFe₂O₄ (MnFe₂O₄-GR), a broad absorption band near 3390 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the O–H stretching vibrations, indicative of hydroxyl groups originating from absorbed water molecules or phenolic compounds in the nettle extract. The bands at ~ 1400–1450 cm⁻¹ are assigned to symmetric and asymmetric stretching of –COO⁻ (carboxylate) groups, confirming the presence of organic molecules from the plant extract, which play a role in stabilizing and functionalizing the NPs. Strong peaks observed in the region 500–700 cm⁻¹ are attributed to Fe–O stretching vibrations, confirming the formation of a spinel ferrite structure, where Fe³⁺ ions occupy both tetrahedral and octahedral sites. The chemically synthesized MnFe₂O₄ (MnFe₂O₄-CH) spectrum also displays O–H (~ 3400 cm⁻¹) and Fe–O (500–700 cm⁻¹) bands, but lacks peaks in the 1400–1500 cm⁻¹ region, indicating the absence of organic surface groups. This reflects the purity of the product and the lack of biological capping agents in conventional synthesis [25–27].

For GO, characteristic peaks are observed at 3400 cm⁻¹ (O–H stretching), 1720 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching of carboxylic acid), 1620 cm⁻¹ (C = C stretching of unoxidized graphitic domains), and 1050–1250 cm⁻¹ (C–O–C and C–OH stretching). These oxygen-containing functional groups enable strong interaction with metal oxide NPs and improve dispersibility in aqueous media [28, 29]. The MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite spectrum shows a combination of the characteristic peaks of both GO and MnFe₂O₄. The O–H and Fe–O peaks remain visible, with slight shifts in peak positions and intensities, likely due to chemical interactions and bonding between MnFe₂O₄ NPs and GO sheets. The persistence of C = O and C–O–C bands also supports successful anchoring of NPs onto the GO surface [30, 31]. These observations confirm not only the successful synthesis of each component but also the functional integration of MnFe₂O₄ onto the GO matrix, which is critical for biomedical applications where surface chemistry affects biological interactions. XRD analysis

The structural properties of the synthesized MnFe₂O₄ NPs via green synthesis (MnFe₂O₄-GR) and the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite were investigated through X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 2). The XRD pattern of MnFe₂O₄-GR displays sharp and well-defined peaks at 2θ values of 30.2°, 35.6°, 43.3°, 53.6°, 57.2°, and 62.8°, corresponding respectively to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes of a cubic spinel ferrite structure. These reflections match well with standard JCPDS card No. 74-2403, confirming the successful formation of single-phase MnFe₂O₄ with high crystallinity and no observable secondary or impurity phases [36–38]. where K is the shape factor (taken as 0.9), λ is the X-ray wavelength (1.5406 Å for Cu Kα), β is the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the most intense peak (311), and θ is the Bragg angle. The calculated crystallite size of MnFe₂O₄-GR was approximately 12 nm, consistent with nanoscale dimensions necessary for biomedical applications.

The XRD pattern of the MnFe₂O₄/GO composite shows the characteristic peaks of the spinel MnFe₂O₄ phase, confirming that the core structure of the NPs remains intact upon incorporation into GO. A broad diffraction feature around 2θ ≈ 10.5° is additionally observed, corresponding to the (001) plane of graphene oxide, indicating successful inclusion of GO sheets. A noticeable reduction in the intensity of MnFe₂O₄ peaks in the composite is evident, likely due to decreased crystallinity or diffraction shielding by the GO matrix. This may also reflect partial structural disorder caused by NPs anchoring on the GO surface [32, 33].

The average crystallite size for the MnFe₂O₄/GO sample, calculated using the same (311) peak, was approximately 16.7 nm, slightly larger than that of MnFe₂O₄-GR. This increase may be attributed to the aggregation or mild recrystallization during composite formation, although no peak broadening indicative of strain or significant lattice distortion was observed. Importantly, no new peaks or foreign phase reflections are detected in the composite, indicating excellent phase purity in both systems. Notably, no significant lattice parameter shift or peak position change was observed between MnFe₂O₄-GR and MnFe₂O₄/GO. This suggests that the incorporation of GO does not lead to doping or substitutional changes in the MnFe₂O₄ crystal lattice. The overall results confirm that the structural integrity and phase purity of the MnFe₂O₄ NPs are preserved, and the composite formation does not compromise their crystalline framework. These findings validate the successful green synthesis and structural compatibility of MnFe₂O₄ and GO in the nanocomposite, making them highly promising for further biomedical applications requiring pure, stable, and nanostructured materials.

DLS analysis

To evaluate the colloidal properties and dispersion behavior of the synthesized nanomaterials under physiologically relevant aqueous conditions, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3, the MnFe₂O₄ NPs exhibited a narrow hydrodynamic size distribution centered around ~ 75 nm, indicating uniform dispersion and minimal aggregation in aqueous suspension. In contrast, the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite showed a broader size distribution with a dominant peak between 650 and 700 nm. This increase in hydrodynamic size can be attributed to the lateral extension of GO sheets and their interaction with the magnetic NPs, which enhances the composite’s effective particle size in solution.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles and MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites

The surface charge characteristics of the samples were also consistent with colloidal stability requirements. MnFe₂O₄ NPs exhibited a moderately negative surface charge (~ − 27.4 mV), while the MnFe₂O₄/GO composite showed a slightly more negative zeta potential (~ − 31.6 mV), which is likely due to the presence of carboxyl and hydroxyl functional groups on the GO surface. Zeta potential values in this range (|ζ| >25 mV) generally indicate sufficient electrostatic repulsion to prevent NPs aggregation, promoting colloidal stability in aqueous environments [34, 35].

The long-term stability of the dispersions was further assessed by storing the NPs suspensions in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C for up to 14 days. No significant sedimentation, turbidity changes, or visible aggregation were observed during this period, indicating that both MnFe₂O₄ and MnFe₂O₄/GO formulations retain good dispersion stability under storage conditions relevant to biomedical use. Moreover, no appreciable changes were detected in either the hydrodynamic particle size or the zeta potential values throughout the storage period, confirming the structural integrity and electrostatic stability of the NPs suspensions. These findings support the potential applicability of these nanomaterials in biological systems, where consistent colloidal behavior is critical for effective delivery, biodistribution, and cellular interaction.

SEM and EDX analysis

The surface structure and elemental makeup of MnFe₂O₄ NPs were examined through SEM and EDX techniques. The SEM images (Fig. 4) reveal that the MnFe₂O₄ NPs exhibit an aggregated and roughly spherical morphology with a relatively uniform distribution. The particles are nanoscale in size, with some aggregation likely resulting from the magnetic interactions between the NPs. The high-magnification image provides a detailed view of the surface texture, showing the presence of fine structures and individual particles within the aggregates. These observations confirm that the MnFe₂O₄ NPs are successfully synthesized and exhibit a morphology typical of magnetic NPs prepared through a green synthesis method.

SEM images of MnFe₂O₄/GO nanoparticles at different magnifications

The EDX spectrum confirms the elemental composition of the MnFe₂O₄ NPs (Fig. 5). The prominent peaks corresponding to manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), and oxygen (O) indicate the formation of MnFe₂O₄, consistent with the expected stoichiometry of the spinel ferrite structure. The absence of significant peaks for other elements demonstrates the high purity of the NPs, with no detectable contamination from precursor materials or other impurities. The presence of oxygen further supports the formation of a metal oxide structure. Overall, the SEM analysis highlights the nanoscale morphology and aggregation behavior of the MnFe₂O₄ NPs, while the EDX results confirm their elemental composition and purity. These findings are in alignment with the intended synthesis of MnFe₂O₄ NPs and support their suitability for subsequent composite formation and biomedical applications.

Examination of nanoparticles using transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

A Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) investigation was conducted to assess the structural characteristics, morphological features, and distribution patterns of the synthesized MnFe₂O₄ NPs, GO, and the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites. Each material displayed distinct characteristics, confirming their successful synthesis and structural integrity. The TEM images of MnFe₂O₄ NPs (Fig. 6a) synthesized through the green method reveal roughly spherical NPs with a narrow size distribution. The particles exhibit some degree of aggregation, which is common for magnetic NPs due to strong magnetic dipole-dipole interactions. The individual particle sizes are in the nanoscale range, consistent with the crystallite size calculated from the XRD analysis, which confirms the formation of MnFe₂O₄ NPs with a uniform morphology and structural integrity. For GO NPs, the TEM images (Fig. 6b-c) display thin, transparent, and wrinkled sheet-like structures, characteristic of exfoliated GO layers. The sheets appear to overlap in some regions, which is consistent with the layered nature of GO. The transparency of the sheets indicates their nanoscale thickness, and the observed wrinkles are a result of mechanical flexibility and distortions caused during synthesis and drying processes. These characteristics validate the effective production of graphene oxide, demonstrating a significant level of layer separation. For the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites (Fig. 6d-e), the TEM images provide clear evidence of the successful deposition of MnFe₂O₄ NPs onto the GO nanosheets. The NPs are uniformly distributed over the GO surface, indicating a strong interaction between MnFe₂O₄ and GO throughout the synthesis process, which facilitated their effective integration. The wrinkled structure of GO remains visible, indicating that the layered nature of GO is retained in the composite. The dispersion of NPs across graphene oxide sheets significantly increases the overall surface area and improves the functional characteristics of the composite, rendering it highly effective for applications like targeted drug delivery and cancer therapy. Therefore, the TEM analysis confirms the successful synthesis of MnFe₂O₄ NPs, graphene oxide, and their composite. The structural features observed in the images align with the expected characteristics of each material, supporting their suitability for further investigations and biomedical applications.

The magnetic characteristics of mnfe₂o₄ nanoparticles

The magnetic behavior of the pristine MnFe₂O₄, green-synthesized MnFe₂O₄ (MnFe₂O₄-GR), and MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite was evaluated using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) at room temperature, and the resulting M–H curves are shown in Fig. 7. All samples exhibit narrow hysteresis loops with negligible coercivity and remanence, indicative of superparamagnetic behavior, which is desirable for biomedical applications due to the absence of remanent magnetization after removing the external magnetic field. The saturation magnetization (Ms) values were found to be approximately 55 emu/g for pristine MnFe₂O₄, 40 emu/g for MnFe₂O₄-GR, and 25 emu/g for the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite. The reduction in Ms in the green-synthesized sample compared to pristine MnFe₂O₄ can be attributed to surface disorder, smaller particle size, and possible cation redistribution induced by the green synthesis process [36–39]. Further reduction in Ms upon GO incorporation is likely due to the non-magnetic nature of graphene oxide and partial surface shielding of magnetic domains by the GO sheets [40, 41]. Nevertheless, all values remain within the acceptable range for magnetic biomedical applications.

M–H curves of pristine MnFe₂O₄, green-synthesized MnFe₂O₄, and MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite

The coercivity (Hc) values of all samples were below 50 Oe, and the loops show a sharp rise in magnetization at low fields, which further confirms the high magnetic susceptibility and rapid response of the NPs to external magnetic fields [40, 42]. These properties are highly suitable for magnetic drug targeting, where responsiveness to low magnetic fields ensures effective navigation in vivo, and for magnetic hyperthermia, where the heating efficiency correlates with the superparamagnetic relaxation behavior.

Evaluation of nanoparticle-induced cytotoxicity in HeLa cell lines using the MTT assay

The cytotoxic effects of MnFe₂O₄ NPs, GO, and their MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite were assessed on HeLa cells using the MTT assay to evaluate their potential anticancer activity (Fig. 8). Cell viability was measured across a range of NPs concentrations, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was determined for each formulation. The MnFe₂O₄ NPs (Fig. 8a) displayed notable cytotoxicity with an IC₅₀ of 200.7 µg/mL, suggesting a strong concentration-dependent inhibition of HeLa cell proliferation. This cytotoxic response is likely attributed to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction, as observed in similar magnetic NPs, which may lead to apoptotic pathways being triggered in cancer cells. In contrast, GO alone (Fig. 8b) exhibited significantly lower toxicity, with an IC₅₀ of 1202 µg/mL, indicating its relative biocompatibility at low concentrations. However, at higher concentrations, GO has been reported to disrupt cellular membranes and induce oxidative stress, both of which may contribute to its mild cytotoxic effect.

Dose-response curves showing the cytotoxic effects of (a) MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles, (b) GO, and (c) MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite on HeLa cell viability, as determined by the MTT assay

The MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite (Fig. 8c) demonstrated superior cytotoxicity, with a markedly lower IC₅₀ of 120.7 µg/mL. This enhanced cytotoxic effect can be attributed to a synergistic mechanism involving multiple factors: the magnetic core (MnFe₂O₄) likely facilitates improved endocytic uptake via magnetic interactions and cellular internalization pathways, while the GO matrix provides a large surface area that enhances cellular contact and delivery [43, 44]. Furthermore, both components are known to contribute to elevated intracellular ROS levels, which can trigger oxidative damage, mitochondrial membrane potential loss, and apoptosis [45, 46]. Although direct ROS quantification or endocytosis assays were not performed in this study, the significantly lower IC₅₀ of the composite compared to individual components supports a mechanism involving both enhanced intracellular delivery and ROS-mediated cytotoxicity. These findings emphasize the potential of MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites as effective therapeutic agents in cervical cancer treatment by leveraging dual-action mechanisms [47–49].

We addressed the sensitivity of normal cells in the context of our study. We observed no cytotoxic effects on HFF-2 cells in the treatment groups (Fig. 9). We emphasized that this does not necessarily imply a complete lack of sensitivity in all normal cells. We clarified that differential toxicity can occur and discuss the implications of our findings regarding normal cell responses (Fig. 9).

Effects of nanoparticles on apoptosis-related gene expression

The effects of MnFe₂O₄ NPs, GO, and their composite (MnFe₂O₄/GO NPs) on apoptosis-related gene expression in HeLa cells was conducted by examining the expression patterns of BAX, Caspase-3, and Bcl-2 (Fig. 10). These genes play a crucial role in controlling the process of programmed cell death. BAX and Caspase-3 facilitate apoptosis by activating cell death pathways, whereas Bcl-2 functions as a protective factor, preventing apoptosis from occurring. The findings reveal a notable increase in the expression of genes associated with programmed cell death, specifically BAX and Caspase-3, while simultaneously demonstrating a marked suppression of the Bcl-2 gene, which is known for its anti-apoptotic function. This regulatory shift occurs as a direct consequence of exposure to MnFe₂O₄/GO NPs. For BAX (Fig. 10a), the expression level was markedly increased in the MnFe₂O₄/GO-treated group compared to untreated cells and cells treated with MnFe₂O₄ NPs or GO alone. The MnFe₂O₄/GO-treated group exhibited a fold change that was statistically significant (P < 0.0001), highlighting the synergistic effect of the composite in activating apoptotic pathways. Similarly, Caspase-3 expression was significantly higher in the MnFe₂O₄/GO-treated group, with a similar trend observed when compared to the individual components (Fig. 10b). The results suggest that the composite induces enhanced activation of caspase-mediated apoptosis, which is critical for cell death in cancer therapy. Conversely, the levels of the Bcl-2 gene, which plays a role in preventing apoptosis (Fig. 10c), were markedly diminished in cells exposed to MnFe₂O₄/GO NPs. This suppression of Bcl-2 further supports the pro-apoptotic potential of the composite, as reduced Bcl-2 levels lead to increased mitochondrial outer membrane permeability and promotion of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. The extent of Bcl-2 downregulation was greater in the MnFe₂O₄/GO-treated group than in cells treated with either MnFe₂O₄ NPs or GO alone, emphasizing the superior efficacy of the composite. The enhanced performance exhibited by MnFe₂O₄/GO NPs arises from the interplay between the unique characteristics of the magnetic NPs and the distinctive properties of graphene oxide. MnFe₂O₄ NPs enhance cellular uptake due to their magnetic properties, while GO provides a large surface area and functional groups that facilitate cellular interactions and delivery. The enhanced capability of the composite to regulate the expression of genes associated with apoptosis, surpassing the effectiveness of its separate constituents, highlights its promise as a potent anticancer therapeutic. The exposure of HeLa cells to MnFe₂O₄/GO NPs results in a substantial upregulation of genes associated with apoptosis, specifically BAX and Caspase-3, while concurrently downregulating the expression of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2. This indicates the NPs’ potent capability to enhance programmed cell death. These results underscore the potential of MnFe₂O₄/GO NPs as an innovative approach for cervical cancer therapy, primarily by modulating apoptotic signaling pathways [50–52].

Relative gene expression levels of apoptosis-related genes in HeLa cells treated with GO, MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles (MnFe₂O₄ NPs), and MnFe₂O₄/GO composite. (a) BAX (pro-apoptotic), (b) Caspase-3 (pro-apoptotic), and (c) Bcl-2 (anti-apoptotic) gene expression levels are presented as log2 fold changes relative to untreated cells

Although the present study focused on evaluating the effects of MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites in a two-dimensional (2D) monolayer model of HeLa cells, which is commonly used for preliminary anticancer screening, it is acknowledged that such models do not fully recapitulate the complexity of in vivo tumor microenvironments. The gene expression data, demonstrating significant upregulation of pro-apoptotic markers (BAX, Caspase-3) and suppression of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2, provide mechanistic insight into the therapeutic potential of the composite. These early findings justify the composite’s progression to more advanced models. While three-dimensional (3D) spheroid cultures or animal studies were not included in the current work due to the study’s focus on synthesis and mechanistic exploration, future investigations will incorporate such models to better assess tumor penetration, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic efficacy under more physiologically relevant conditions.

Discussion

The green synthesis of MnFe₂O₄ NPs using nettle extract offers several advantages over conventional chemical methods. The FT-IR analysis validated the existence of organic functional groups associated with the green-synthesized MnFe₂O₄ NPs, which likely contribute to enhanced biocompatibility and stability. This aligns with findings by Katubi, Alsaiari [31] and Peng, Choo [30], who highlighted the functionalization benefits of natural extracts in NPs synthesis. Additionally, the integration of MnFe₂O₄ NPs with GO through a green hydrothermal method resulted in a stable nanocomposite structure, where the abundant oxygen-containing functional groups of GO facilitated uniform NPs dispersion. These structural improvements echo results from Yang, Shi [17] and Zheng and Li [19], who demonstrated the advantages of GO-based composites for enhanced stability and functional properties.

The XRD analysis confirmed the successful synthesis of single-phase MnFe₂O₄ NPs with a cubic spinel structure and high crystallinity. The absence of impurity peaks in both MnFe₂O₄-GR and MnFe₂O₄/GO samples strongly indicates excellent phase purity and structural integrity. The calculated crystallite size of ~ 12 nm for MnFe₂O₄-GR and ~ 16.7 nm for MnFe₂O₄/GO, using the Debye–Scherrer equation, confirms their nanoscale dimensions, which are critical for biomedical applications such as drug delivery and hyperthermia. Importantly, no observable peak shifts or lattice distortions were detected upon composite formation with GO, suggesting that the MnFe₂O₄ crystal lattice remains unaffected. This indicates that the interaction between the NPs and the GO matrix is likely limited to surface-level physical anchoring rather than substitutional doping or structural intercalation [53, 54]. The slight increase in average crystallite size in the composite may be attributed to mild NPs aggregation or the reduction in lattice strain due to the presence of GO sheets. Therefore, the preservation of the spinel structure, combined with the enhanced surface interaction potential provided by GO, supports the suitability of MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites for multifunctional biomedical use. These structural characteristics closely align with previous studies demonstrating the importance of nanocrystalline spinel ferrites in targeted therapeutic applications due to their stable crystallinity and favorable size range [55, 56].

The TEM and SEM analyses further supported these observations, revealing uniform dispersion of MnFe₂O₄ NPs on GO sheets and reduced aggregation compared to standalone MnFe₂O₄ NPs, which aligns with results from Lan Huong, Tu [32]. The DLS results confirmed that both MnFe₂O₄ and MnFe₂O₄/GO nanomaterials maintain particle sizes suitable for biomedical applications, with the GO-containing composites exhibiting larger hydrodynamic dimensions due to the extended structure of graphene oxide [30, 42]. Additionally, the negative surface charge of both systems supports their electrostatic stabilization in aqueous media. The stable behavior observed in PBS over two weeks further highlights their potential for use in physiological environments where NPs aggregation can compromise therapeutic efficacy or biodistribution. These colloidal characteristics are essential prerequisites for clinical translation and reinforce the functional advantages of the composite design.

The magnetic evaluation of the synthesized materials provides crucial insight into their applicability for biomedical technologies. While the pristine MnFe₂O₄ exhibited the highest magnetic saturation, the green-synthesized and composite forms showed a gradual decrease in Ms, a trend commonly observed due to surface modifications, particle size effects, and the dilution of magnetic components when hybridized with non-magnetic substrates like GO. Despite the reduction in saturation magnetization, the magnetic response remained within an effective range for biomedical functions, particularly in magnetic targeting and hyperthermia, where materials with moderate magnetization (~ 20–40 emu/g) and superparamagnetic behavior are preferred to avoid magnetic retention in tissues post-treatment [57]. These properties ensure that the NPs can be guided using an external magnetic field and dispersed efficiently once the field is withdrawn, minimizing agglomeration and off-target effects. Furthermore, the superparamagnetic character observed across all samples aligns with current requirements for minimally invasive therapeutic interventions, such as magnetic drug delivery and magnetic fluid hyperthermia. The minimal coercivity and remanence eliminate the risk of residual magnetization, which is critical for safe circulation in the bloodstream and efficient clearance [58]. Comparing the synthesized materials, the MnFe₂O₄/GO composite offers a balanced profile, although its Ms is lower, the improved colloidal stability, potential surface functionalization by GO, and retained magnetic responsiveness make it highly promising for multifunctional nanomedicine platforms. These results echo previous findings in the literature where GO-based ferrite composites demonstrated superior in vivo distribution, biocompatibility, and combined imaging-therapeutic functions [50, 59]. Thus, while pristine MnFe₂O₄ offers stronger magnetization, the green-synthesized and GO-integrated forms provide a more application-tuned profile, supporting their use in magnetically driven diagnostics and therapy.

The enhanced cytotoxic activity observed for the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite, compared to pristine MnFe₂O₄ and GO, likely stems from synergistic mechanisms beyond simple additive effects. Notably, the integration of MnFe₂O₄ with graphene oxide may facilitate more efficient intracellular delivery via magnetically assisted endocytosis, a phenomenon reported for various superparamagnetic nanostructures [60]. The GO component, with its two-dimensional morphology and oxygen-containing functional groups, likely improves dispersion and stability in the cellular environment, thereby increasing the likelihood of NPs–cell membrane interactions [61]. Moreover, previous studies have suggested that both MnFe₂O₄ and GO can independently generate ROS, which are key initiators of oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in cancer cells. The combination of these materials may therefore result in elevated intracellular ROS levels, contributing to mitochondrial damage and the activation of programmed cell death pathways [62]. Although this study did not directly quantify ROS production or endocytosis, the significantly improved cytotoxic effect of the composite compared to its individual constituents supports the involvement of such mechanisms.

Comparable findings have been reported in hybrid nanocomposites, such as CoFe₂O₄/GO and Fe₃O₄/rGO systems, where enhanced cellular uptake and ROS-mediated damage were identified as principal mechanisms of cytotoxicity [45, 60]. These results suggest that the MnFe₂O₄/GO composite leverages both physicochemical and biological properties, magnetic responsiveness, high surface area, and chemical reactivity, to achieve superior therapeutic potential. The mechanistic interplay between magnetic targeting, oxidative stress, and GO-facilitated transport positions MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites as promising candidates for future application in targeted cancer nanotherapy. Further mechanistic investigations, including ROS assays, intracellular uptake tracking, and apoptosis pathway analysis, would strengthen the understanding of their therapeutic mode of action and optimize their design for clinical use. While HeLa cells are widely used and serve as a reliable in vitro model for studying cervical cancer biology and screening therapeutic agents, we acknowledge that they represent only one subtype of cervical cancer. Therefore, the cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects demonstrated in this study, although promising, should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of therapeutic potential. Further validation using additional cervical cancer cell lines, such as SiHa and C33A, as well as 3D spheroid or patient-derived models, would be essential to comprehensively establish the nanocomposite’s efficacy across heterogeneous cervical cancer types. Nonetheless, the significant reduction in IC₅₀ and the activation of apoptosis-related pathways observed in HeLa cells provide a strong foundation for future translational investigations.

Analysis of gene expression revealed an increased expression of pro-apoptotic indicators, including BAX and Caspase-3, while simultaneously demonstrating a reduced expression of the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl-2 in cells exposed to MnFe₂O₄/GO. This modulation of apoptotic pathways aligns with findings by Ganjouzadeh, Khorrami and Gharbi [63], who reported similar effects for GO-based composites in cancer therapy. The ability of MnFe₂O₄/GO to effectively induce apoptosis underscores its potential as a robust anticancer agent. The MnFe₂O₄/GO composite exhibited performance that was either on par with or exceeded that of other nanomaterials documented in previous studies. For instance, studies on CoFe₂O₄/GO composites highlighted their efficacy in magnetic hyperthermia and targeted drug delivery [24]. The enhanced apoptotic effects observed in this study are consistent with the findings of Ganjouzadeh, Khorrami and Gharbi [63], where similar nanocomposites were shown to induce significant mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis in cancer cells. Additionally, the MnFe₂O₄/GO composite’s biocompatibility and scalability through green synthesis provide a distinct advantage over chemically synthesized alternatives, which often involve toxic reagents [31]. In comparison to silver-based NPs, which have demonstrated potent anticancer activity but are often limited by dose-dependent toxicity [64], MnFe₂O₄/GO composites offer a safer and more sustainable alternative. Furthermore, the functional groups on GO enhance drug-loading capacity and delivery efficiency, as reported by Zheng and Li [19], making MnFe₂O₄/GO a versatile platform for cancer therapy. This study’s findings underscore the promising capabilities of MnFe₂O₄/GO composites for medical treatments. Their ability to modulate apoptotic pathways, combined with their favorable magnetic and structural properties, positions them as promising candidates for targeted cancer therapies. The environmentally friendly synthesis approach further supports their alignment with sustainable nanotechnology practices. Despite the promising in vitro performance of MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites demonstrated in HeLa monolayer cultures, it is acknowledged that 2D models do not fully replicate the complex tumor microenvironment, including gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and drug penetration barriers. However, monolayer models are a necessary and widely accepted first step for high-throughput screening and mechanistic understanding. The significant decrease in IC₅₀ values, alongside the clear upregulation of apoptosis-related genes and potential involvement of ROS generation and magnetic-mediated uptake, provide strong rationale for proceeding to 3D spheroid and in vivo models. Future work will focus on these higher-order systems to evaluate NPs distribution, penetration efficiency, and therapeutic efficacy under more clinically relevant conditions. Such studies would be instrumental in validating the translational potential of MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites for cervical cancer therapy and optimizing their design for future preclinical development. Future studies should focus on in vivo evaluations, exploring the long-term safety and therapeutic efficacy of MnFe₂O₄/GO composites. Additionally, integrating these composites with other therapeutic agents, such as doxorubicin, could enhance their applicability in combination therapies, as demonstrated in studies by Bahmani, Banihashem [65].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful green synthesis of biocompatible MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles using Urtica extract and their integration with graphene oxide to yield MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites with enhanced physicochemical and biological performance. Comprehensive characterization confirmed the preservation of the cubic spinel structure, nanoscale particle size, and surface functionalization enabling stable dispersion. The magnetic analysis revealed that, despite a reduction in saturation magnetization upon GO incorporation, the composite retained superparamagnetic behavior and Ms values sufficient for efficient magnetic targeting and hyperthermia applications, while avoiding remanent magnetization that could cause in vivo aggregation. Biological evaluations demonstrated that the MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposite exhibited superior cytotoxicity toward HeLa cervical cancer cells compared to its individual components, achieving a substantially lower IC₅₀ value. This enhanced efficacy is attributed to synergistic effects involving magnetic-facilitated cellular uptake, improved dispersion and membrane interaction via GO, and the generation of reactive oxygen species triggering mitochondrial damage. Gene expression studies further revealed significant induction of pro-apoptotic markers and suppression of anti-apoptotic signals, confirming apoptosis as the primary mode of cytotoxicity. The environmentally friendly synthesis route provides an additional advantage over conventional chemical approaches, aligning with sustainable nanotechnology principles. While these findings establish a strong foundation for MnFe₂O₄/GO composites as potent anticancer agents, the current results are based on 2D in vitro models. Future investigations should incorporate 3D tumor spheroids and in vivo systems to evaluate biodistribution, tumor penetration, pharmacokinetics, long-term safety, and therapeutic performance under clinically relevant conditions. Furthermore, coupling these composites with established chemotherapeutics or photothermal agents could expand their therapeutic scope in combination cancer therapy. Overall, MnFe₂O₄/GO nanocomposites synthesized via a green route offer a promising, multifunctional platform for targeted cervical cancer treatment and warrant further preclinical development.
