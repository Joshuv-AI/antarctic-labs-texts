Trends in childhood mortality from external causes of death in Hungary between 2000 and 2022: a retrospective cohort study of the nationwide population register data
Authors: Zsanett Urfi, Tibor András Nyári, Tamás Lantos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe highest childhood mortality rates are observed from external causes of death. Our study aims to investigate the trends in deaths from external causes among children aged 0–14 years between 2000 and 2022, comparing the pre-pandemic (2000–2019) and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022) periods.DesignThis is a population-based retrospective cohort study.SettingsFrom the ‘Dissemination Database’ of the Hungarian Central Statistical Office, we collected the monthly and annual mortality of all external causes (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision: V01–Y89) and the two most common external causes (traffic accidents and drownings) for the period 2000–2022.ParticipantsChildren aged under 15 years, and the relevant population, were available from this database by gender, 5-year age group and region.Outcome measuresThe trends were characterised by the incidence rate ratio (IRR) and its 95% CI.ResultsDuring the study period, 1813 deaths (1142 boys and 671 girls) occurred. There was a significant decreasing trend in all external-cause mortality (IRR=0.94; 95% CI 0.93 to 0.95; p<0.001). A similar trend was detected for mortality in traffic accidents and drownings. For external-cause mortality, significant seasonality was found in the pre-pandemic (July; p<0.001) and pandemic (May; p=0.041) periods. Similar seasonality (July–August) characterised mortality from traffic accidents and drownings. The highest mortality in the pre-pandemic period was observed in Northern Hungary and Southern Transdanubia regions. In contrast, these regions had the lowest external-cause mortality rates during the pandemic period, while the regions of the Northern Great Plains and the Southern Great Plains (HU33) had the highest external-cause mortality rates. The highest mortality rate from traffic accidents was found in the HU33 in both periods.ConclusionsThere is a significant decreasing annual trend in childhood external-cause mortality in Hungary. Mortality from drowning was significantly higher around our larger natural water bodies in the summer months, while mortality from traffic accidents was the highest in the HU33.

Introduction

Mortality rates in Hungary have been high for decades. Among European Union (EU) countries, Hungary ranks in the top five, shared with Bulgaria, Croatia, Latvia and Lithuania (2023).1 In Hungary, cardiovascular diseases account for around 50% of deaths, followed by cancers (25%). These are followed by external causes of death, with suicides and accidental falls in the elderly being the most common.2

External-cause mortality is a very heterogeneous group of death causes. The most common external causes of death in the total population in recent decades have been suicides, followed by accidental falls and traffic accidents.3 These are also the leading causes of death in many other European countries. In addition, in recent decades, Hungary has recorded one of the highest mortality rates for both accidental falls and suicides on average in Europe.3 Among children under 15 years in Hungary, mortality has dropped dramatically, but external causes remain among the leading causes of death, alongside cancer and nervous system disorders.2 Thus, in this study, we focus exclusively on the two most common accidental deaths, which are traffic accidents and accidental drowning in children aged under 15 years.

Road traffic accidents are one of the leading causes of death among children under the age of 15 years, accounting for 34% of childhood deaths.4 Accidental drowning is a major problem worldwide, especially among children aged 0–14 years.5 Children aged under 15 years are exposed to the risk of road traffic accidents for several reasons.6 7

Drowning also occurs often in children. A major problem for young children is their lack of swimming ability. In the past decades, external causes were the third most common cause of childhood death in Hungary, after certain conditions in the perinatal period (42% of all deaths) and congenital malformations (19%). In general, around 80 (13%) childhood deaths from external causes were annually recorded in Hungary.2

Previous studies have shown that the pattern of death from external causes differs between men and women and within different age groups. Additionally, seasonal variation might also occur in the aetiology of death from external causes.8

During the coronavirus pandemic, there was a significant excess mortality. However, this goes (far) beyond the direct deaths caused by the virus.

Our study aims to investigate both the annual and seasonal trends in all external-cause mortality rates in Hungary over the 23-year interval between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2022 and to compare mortality rates before and during the pandemic.

Materials and methods

Study population

From the ‘Dissemination Database’ published online by the Hungarian Central Statistical Office (HCSO), we obtained data on all external-cause mortality (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision: V01–Y89) registered in the age group under 15 years of age, as well as the monthly and annual numbers of deaths caused by the two most common external causes in this age group (traffic accidents (V01–V99) and accidental drownings (W65–W74)) for the period 2000–2022.2 The annual population data were retrieved from the HCSO database. Since monthly data on the childhood population aged 0–14 years were not available, we extrapolated from the monthly numbers of deaths and births to estimate the monthly population of children in this age group. Both data on population and external causes of deaths were broken down by gender, age group and region. The age groups were (1) under 5 years, (2) 5–9 years and (3) 10–14 years. Thus, age-specific death rates were calculated. Furthermore, the mortality rates of external causes were directly standardised using the European Standard Population to make comparisons easier.

Territorial analysis

The regional territorial distribution of childhood deaths from external causes was also compared. During the study period, the regional administration in Hungary changed: the region of Central Hungary was divided into two regions (Budapest (HU11) and Pest County (HU12)). According to the territorial division of the EU (abbreviated from the French version ‘Nomenclature des unités territoriales statistiques’, level 2 (NUTS2)), the following eight regions were defined in Hungary: HU11, HU12, Central Transdanubia (HU21), Western Transdanubia (HU22), Southern Transdanubia (HU23), Northern Hungary (HU31), Northern Great Plain (HU32) and Southern Great Plain (HU33).

Trend analyses

Annual and seasonal trends were analysed using negative binomial (NB) regression. The trends were characterised by the incidence rate ratio (IRR) and its 95% CI. In the case of traffic accidents, we can expect the phenomenon called ‘overdispersion’ (as independence of observation is compromised), which does not fit the model assumptions of Poisson regression. In such cases, a model based on an NB distribution (Poisson-Gamma mixture distribution) is more appropriate.9 Geometric methods were used to confirm the observed seasonality results: the Edwards test10 and its generalisation, the Walter-Elwood method.11 All analyses were carried out for all external-cause mortality (V01–Y89) and the most common external causes of death: traffic accidents (V01–V99) and accidental drownings (W65–W74). Due to the low number of cases in the studied population (aged 0–14 years), all the remaining external causes of death (including suicide) were not analysed separately. The analyses were performed using the statistical software package STATA V.16.1. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

During the study period, there were 1813 deaths (1142 boys: 63% and 671 girls: 37%), of which 614 (374 boys: 60.9% and 240 girls: 39.1%) were caused by road traffic accidents and 268 (198 boys: 73.9% and 70 girls: 26.1%) by drowning.

The age-standardised total external-cause mortality rate was 5.23 per 100 000 children during the study period, while the age-standardised total external-cause mortality rate was 5.58 per 100 000 children in the pre-pandemic period and 2.77 per 100 000 children in the pandemic period. The standardised traffic accident death rate was 1.78 per 100 000 children during the period of the study, while the standardised traffic accident mortality rate was 1.89 per 100 000 children in the pre-pandemic period and 0.98 per 100 000 children in the pandemic period. The standardised rate of drowning deaths was 0.77 per 100 000 children in the study period, while the standardised drowning death rate was 0.83 per 100 000 children in the pre-pandemic period and 0.35 per 100 000 children in the pandemic period.

There was a significant downward trend in all external-cause mortality (IRR=0.94; 95% CI 0.93 to 0.95; p<0.001). A similar trend was observed in mortality from traffic accidents (IRR=0.93; 95% CI 0.92 to 0.94; p<0.001) and drownings (IRR=0.93; 95% CI 0.91 to 0.95; p<0.001). For external-cause mortality, significant seasonality was also observed in the pre-pandemic (July; p<0.001) and pandemic (May; p=0.041) periods. Similar seasonality (July–August peak) characterised mortality from traffic accidents and drownings.

Table 1 shows that the highest mortality rates in the pre-pandemic period were observed in the regions of HU31 and HU23. In contrast, during the pandemic period, these regions had the lowest external-cause mortality, while the highest external-cause mortality was found in the regions of the HU32 and HU33. The highest mortality from traffic accidents was found in the HU33 in both periods (table 2). Additionally, the highest mortality from accidental drowning was found in the HU32 and HU31 regions during the study period (table 3).

For total external-cause mortality, we found significant seasonality in both pre-pandemic (July; p<0.001) and pandemic (May; p=0.41) periods. Similar significant seasonality also characterised the mortality from traffic accidents (August; p<0.001) and drownings (July; p<0.001).

Discussion

In Hungary, both traffic accidents and accidental drownings show a decreasing annual trend between 2000 and 2022. Additionally, the regional distribution of death rates was different for traffic accidents and accidental drownings.

The lowest childhood mortality rates from traffic accidents were observed in HU21. By contrast, HU33 had the highest mortality rate (per capita), while HU23 and HU32 had the highest childhood mortality rates.

The regions with the highest standardised mortality rates for accidental drownings were HU23 and HU31, which are associated with the geographical location of the two largest lakes of Hungary (Lake Balaton and Lake Tisza). In contrast, HU21, which includes the northern shore of Lake Balaton, had the lowest mortality rate from accidental drowning among children. This may be related to the geological (bathymetric) structure of the northern and southern shores of Lake Balaton.12 The north shore has a more balanced water depth, with a slow deepening; the south shore, on the other hand, is very abrupt. Presumably, this lesser-known geological variation may also account for the higher fatality rate of accidental drownings on the south shore.

In terms of seasonality, most traffic accidents occur in late August and early September, coinciding with the start of the school year. For accidental drownings, the maximum mortality is observed in late June and early July at the start of summer break.

A cyclical trend in childhood deaths from road traffic accidents and accidental drownings, with a peak in July during the summer holiday period, has been described in previous studies.13 14 Our results show a similar seasonal trend for children aged 0–14 years: after the end of the school year, early summer (July) has the highest accidental drowning mortality rate, and late summer (August), at the start of the new school year, has the highest traffic accident mortality rate.

In our study, we investigated the most common external causes of death among children (aged 0–14 years) in Hungary. The 23-year period under study includes the first 2 years of the coronavirus pandemic. Previous studies have mainly focused on the pre-pandemic period and examined mortality in a more heterogeneous population (aged 0–19 years).41522 Similar to our results, it has been observed that child and adolescent mortality from non-natural causes in Europe has declined significantly over the past two decades. As with other causes of death among young people (eg, cancers and infectious diseases), geographical differences have emerged, which are multifactorial and involve the performance of the health system. In addition, cultural and socioeconomic characteristics may also play an important role in non-natural childhood deaths.17 In a recent study,20 the Eastern European countries (Romania, Bulgaria, Poland, Slovakia and the Czech Republic) had very high rates of non-natural causes of death among 0–19-year-olds, while Spain, Denmark, Italy and the UK had the lowest. Switzerland, France and Hungary had rates similar to the EU28.20 Over the almost two decades studied (2000–2018), Spain, Ireland and Portugal have seen the largest reductions in death rates from non-natural causes. Poland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia and Romania have also seen decreases, but these countries are still above the European average.16 20 By contrast, Bulgaria had the highest rate of deaths from non-natural causes between 2003 and 2007.20 Reductions in accidental deaths have also been recorded in Northern Europe19 and other European countries.4 15 16

In Western European countries, non-natural childhood mortality rates were already declining before 2000,4 and in some of these countries (Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Switzerland) the rate continues to fall, while in others (Spain, Portugal, the UK and Denmark) the rates have remained stable at low levels since 2010.4 15 17 This confirms that attention to injury prevention and safety standards remains an effective way to prevent injury-related deaths.18 22 This sustained reduction can be attributed to specific efforts to reduce traffic crashes21 23 through the implementation of legislation that has increased the use of child safety belts, developed safer road designs and improved vehicle safety.18 20 22 24 However, our study does not account for changes in traffic regulations and water safety measures over the study period, which may also impact mortality rates. There was a restriction on the traffic law, with substantial changes in May 2008. One of the main elements of this regulation is that drunk driving is (even in international terms) strictly punishable (‘zero tolerance’), and a high fine can be imposed on the spot, even in the case of low alcohol consumption. We speculate that further speed limitations in the Highway Code would have similar (positive) consequences.

Limitations

In this retrospective study, we have investigated the annual and seasonal patterns of mortality in children aged 0–14 years. One of the strengths of this research is the comprehensive analysis of a 23-year period, which allows for robust conclusions regarding trends in external-cause mortality among children in Hungary.

The data were obtained from vital registers. The reliance on official mortality statistics may not capture all cases of external-cause deaths, particularly those that are misclassified or under-reported. However, the HCSO’s ‘Dissemination Database’ on mortality statistics is highly accurate. Nevertheless, for privacy reasons, the more detailed data structure cannot be displayed directly but can be approximated relatively well by extrapolation. Thus, the use of a well-defined dataset from the HCSO ensures high data quality and reliability. Consequently, we are confident that our results reflect real trends.

Additionally, over-dispersion did not influence our results, as the NB regression method was employed in the investigation of the annual mortality trend, and the application of this method is appropriate for enhancing the accuracy of the statistical analysis. One of the additional advantages of the present study is the analysis performed by the NUTS2 regions. Furthermore, the study’s regional analysis offers a detailed understanding of geographic disparities in mortality rates, which can guide localised public health interventions.

Examining seasonal trends provides valuable insight into the timing of mortality peaks, which can inform targeted prevention strategies.

The possibility of ecological fallacy is another limitation of our analysis: individual-level associations may not always be reflected in ecological-level associations. Finally, while the study effectively compares pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, it may not fully elucidate the multifactorial causes behind the observed trends, necessitating further qualitative research to explore underlying risk factors and prevention opportunities.

Conclusions

There is a significant decreasing annual trend in childhood external-cause mortality in Hungary. Mortality from drowning was significantly higher around our larger natural water bodies in the summer months, while mortality from traffic accidents was the highest in HU33.

Our ecological study presented the seasonal pattern of some external-cause mortality that might be related to environmental factors. We found seasonal effects related to both traffic accidents and accidental drowning, with peaks in July and September, respectively. These causes of death account for most of the external-cause mortality.

These findings demonstrate how the COVID-19 pandemic affected external-cause mortality and underscore the need for targeted prevention strategies that address the specific causes and regional disparities of childhood external-cause mortality. We may speculate that an increased number of controls by both the traffic and water police during the summer would be beneficial in this respect. Continuous monitoring and intervention efforts are essential to maintain the downward trend and protect vulnerable populations, particularly in the face of evolving public health challenges. Further studies are needed to investigate these results using detailed individual data.
