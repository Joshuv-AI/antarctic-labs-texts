Precision-guided heart failure interventions: The expanding role of advanced cardiac imaging and artificial intelligence
Authors: Arif Albulushi, Hatem Al-Farhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure remains a major driver of hospitalization, morbidity, impaired quality of life, and healthcare expenditure. As disease phenotypes become more diverse and therapeutic options expand beyond pharmacological treatment alone, contemporary heart failure care increasingly includes transcatheter valve therapies, cardiac resynchronization and pacing strategies, complex coronary interventions, invasive hemodynamic assessment, implantable pressure sensors, and advanced heart failure therapies. This evolution has made precision in three domains increasingly important: anatomy, myocardial substrate, and physiology. Advanced cardiac imaging is central to this transition. Echocardiography remains the front-line modality for functional assessment, hemodynamics, and procedural guidance. Cardiac magnetic resonance refines etiologic diagnosis through ventricular quantification, scar assessment, and tissue characterization. Cardiac computed tomography provides high-resolution anatomic planning for structural and coronary interventions. Nuclear imaging offers selected value in perfusion, viability, inflammation, and sympathetic innervation. In parallel, remote hemodynamic monitoring supports earlier recognition of congestion, while artificial intelligence may improve segmentation, standardization, workflow efficiency, and risk prediction. This narrative review synthesizes the role of advanced cardiac imaging and artificial intelligence across the pre-procedural, intra-procedural, and post-procedural pathway in heart failure interventions. We propose a pragmatic precision-care framework that integrates multimodality imaging, invasive physiology, remote monitoring, and patient-centered outcomes. We also highlight current limitations, including heterogeneous evidence, variable access, procedural non-response, data fragmentation, and the need for external validation before broad implementation of artificial intelligence-supported decision-making.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) affects more than 64 million people worldwide and remains one of the leading causes of hospitalization, morbidity, and health-system expenditure [1]. Contemporary HF is not a single disease entity, but a clinical syndrome shaped by aging, cardiometabolic disease, coronary artery disease, valvular disease, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, inflammatory myocardial disease, renal dysfunction, pulmonary vascular disease, and genetic substrates [2], [3]. This heterogeneity has made a purely generic approach to HF management increasingly insufficient.

Guideline-directed medical therapy remains the foundation of care, particularly in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). However, many patients now require decisions that go beyond medication titration: whether a valve lesion is driving symptoms, whether mitral or tricuspid regurgitation is proportionate to ventricular remodeling, whether ischemic myocardium is likely to recover after revascularization, whether dyssynchrony is modifiable by resynchronization, whether pulmonary hypertension reflects left-sided congestion or pulmonary vascular remodeling, and whether a patient has crossed the threshold from intervention-responsive HF to advanced HF requiring mechanical circulatory support or transplantation.

These decisions depend on three practical domains of precision: anatomy, myocardial substrate, and physiology. Imaging biomarkers can help translate these domains into actionable HF phenotypes, particularly when routine clinical assessment does not fully explain the patient's symptoms or trajectory [4]. Echocardiography provides real-time assessment of ventricular function, valve disease, filling pressures, right ventricular (RV) function, and procedural guidance [5], [6]. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) adds accurate ventricular quantification and tissue characterization, including scar, edema, infiltrative disease, and diffuse fibrosis [7].

Cardiac computed tomography (CCT) provides high-resolution anatomic definition for coronary evaluation, valve intervention planning, device sizing, vascular access assessment, and three-dimensional procedural simulation [8]. Nuclear imaging remains important in selected patients, particularly for perfusion, viability, inflammation, and sympathetic innervation [9].

At the same time, HF care is becoming more longitudinal and data-driven. Implantable pulmonary artery pressure monitoring can detect hemodynamic congestion before overt clinical deterioration and may support earlier adjustment of therapy in selected patients [10]. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning may reduce variability, improve throughput, and integrate imaging with clinical, biomarker, and hemodynamic data [11]. Yet these tools also introduce new challenges, including uncertain generalizability, data fragmentation, workflow disruption, medico-legal questions, and the risk of overinterpreting algorithmic outputs without adequate clinical context.

The central aim of this review is to propose a practical precision-care framework for HF interventions. Rather than presenting imaging modalities as isolated diagnostic tools, we organize them according to how they change clinical decisions across the intervention pathway: patient selection, procedural planning, intra-procedural guidance, post-procedural assessment, and longitudinal monitoring. We also emphasize areas where evidence remains uncertain, because a mature precision framework must define not only where imaging helps, but also where it should be interpreted cautiously.

Scope and evidence selection

This is a narrative review focused on advanced cardiac imaging, invasive physiology, remote monitoring, and AI in interventional and device-based HF care. The review was designed to be clinically translational rather than encyclopedic. We prioritized literature that directly informs decision-making in HF interventions, including randomized trials, large prospective studies, major registries, guideline documents, expert consensus statements, multicenter imaging studies, and contemporary reviews relevant to imaging-guided HF care.

The evidence base was considered across five practical domains: baseline phenotyping and etiologic diagnosis; structural valve and coronary intervention planning; resynchronization, device therapy, and advanced HF selection; invasive and remote hemodynamic monitoring; and AI-supported quantification, triage, and prediction. We gave particular attention to studies that changed clinical practice or clarified uncertainty, including trials of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) in secondary mitral regurgitation (MR), CRT lead-targeting studies, viability and revascularization trials in ischemic cardiomyopathy, pulmonary artery pressure monitoring trials, and randomized or externally validated AI imaging studies.

Because this is a narrative review, no formal pooled analysis was performed. Instead, the manuscript aims to provide a structured, clinically usable synthesis with a balanced discussion of evidence strength, unresolved questions, and implementation barriers.

A precision-care framework for imaging-guided HF interventions

A practical HF imaging strategy should begin with the clinical question rather than the modality. In daily practice, the most important first step is to define the dominant problem: congestion physiology, valve-driven HF, ischemic burden, arrhythmic substrate, unexplained cardiomyopathy, pulmonary vascular disease, or advanced HF trajectory.

Echocardiography usually answers the first layer of uncertainty: ventricular function, chamber size, valve severity, pulmonary pressure estimates, RV function, filling pressures, and pericardial disease. When the question shifts toward myocardial substrate, CMR becomes central. It can differentiate ischemic from non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, detect myocarditis or infiltrative disease, quantify biventricular remodeling, and characterize scar patterns relevant to arrhythmic risk and device response. When the question is procedural feasibility, CCT often provides the most actionable information, particularly for TAVR, LAAO, complex structural interventions, and selected coronary workflows. Nuclear imaging adds value when perfusion, viability, inflammation, or cardiac sympathetic innervation is the primary uncertainty.

This framework is not intended to encourage indiscriminate multimodality imaging. Its purpose is to select the lowest-burden test that answers the highest-impact question (Table 1). In stable patients with a clear HF phenotype and good-quality echocardiography, further imaging may not be necessary. Core imaging modalities in contemporary HF care

Echocardiography

Echocardiography remains the front-line modality in HF because it is widely available, repeatable, portable, and uniquely suited to dynamic hemodynamic assessment. It provides rapid evaluation of left ventricular ejection fraction, RV function, diastolic indices, valvular disease, pulmonary pressure estimates, pericardial disease, and volume status. Three-dimensional echocardiography improves chamber quantification and valve anatomy assessment, while strain imaging can detect subtle dysfunction in HFpEF, cardio-oncology, amyloidosis, and other cardiomyopathy phenotypes.

Its main strength is clinical immediacy. It can be performed at the bedside, repeated after decongestion or intervention, and used during procedures. However, echocardiography remains operator-dependent, image-quality dependent, and vulnerable to inter-observer variation, particularly for diastolic grading, RV assessment, and quantification of complex regurgitant lesions. These limitations explain why echocardiography should often be the entry point rather than the endpoint in complex HF phenotyping.

Cardiac magnetic resonance

CMR is the reference standard for ventricular volumes, mass, and ejection fraction, and it provides unmatched non-invasive tissue characterization. Late gadolinium enhancement identifies myocardial scar distribution, helping distinguish ischemic from non-ischemic cardiomyopathy and supporting arrhythmic risk assessment. Native T1 mapping, extracellular volume quantification, and T2 mapping extend evaluation to diffuse fibrosis, edema, inflammation, and infiltrative disease.

In HF interventions, CMR is particularly useful when the substrate determines expected benefit. In ischemic cardiomyopathy, scar transmurality can help estimate regional recovery potential, although modern trial data caution against using viability as a simple binary gatekeeper for revascularization. In CRT, scar location and burden can help avoid placing the LV lead over non-viable myocardium and may explain non-response. In advanced HF, accurate RV assessment by CMR may influence LVAD and transplant risk stratification, especially when echo windows are limited or RV geometry is complex.

The limitations of CMR include access, scan duration, device compatibility, patient tolerance, renal considerations for gadolinium in selected patients, and variability in mapping techniques across vendors. Therefore, its best use is targeted: to clarify etiology, quantify remodeling, and define myocardial substrate when these findings will influence management.

Cardiac computed tomography

CCT has evolved from a coronary imaging modality into a core structural planning tool. It provides high spatial resolution, three-dimensional reconstruction, calcification assessment, vascular access mapping, and device landing-zone evaluation. In TAVR, it is central to annular sizing, valve selection, coronary height assessment, calcium distribution, and access planning. In LAAO, it can define appendage morphology, ostial dimensions, landing zone, lobar anatomy, and device sizing. In selected TEER or tricuspid workflows, CCT may support procedural planning when echo anatomy is incomplete.

CCT also helps evaluate coronary disease in HF patients with intermediate pre-test probability or unclear ischemic contribution. In advanced structural programs, CT-derived models may support simulation, fusion guidance, and procedural efficiency. Its limitations include radiation exposure, iodinated contrast use, heart-rate dependence, and reduced image quality in severe arrhythmia or advanced renal dysfunction.

Nuclear imaging

Nuclear imaging has a more selective but important role in HF. PET and SPECT can evaluate perfusion, ischemia, viability, inflammation, and myocardial sympathetic innervation. FDG-PET is valuable in suspected cardiac sarcoidosis and inflammatory cardiomyopathy when combined with appropriate suppression protocols. Perfusion PET can quantify myocardial blood flow and coronary flow reserve, which may be relevant in ischemic cardiomyopathy, microvascular dysfunction, and HFpEF phenotypes.

Nuclear imaging is not required for all HF patients, and its role should not be overextended. It is most useful when the clinical question is metabolic or inflammatory activity, viability, flow reserve, or denervation rather than anatomy alone. Access, cost, radiotracer availability, radiation exposure, and local expertise remain major determinants of real-world use.

Structural and interventional HF therapies

Structural heart and device therapies have improved outcomes for selected HF populations, but benefit is highly dependent on correct patient selection, procedural feasibility, and post-procedural response. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement

In patients with severe aortic stenosis and HF, imaging must confirm valve severity, define LV and RV consequences, and determine procedural feasibility. Echocardiography remains essential for diagnosing severity, assessing flow state, estimating pulmonary pressures, and evaluating concomitant valve disease. CCT is the cornerstone of procedural planning because it defines annular dimensions, calcium burden, coronary heights, sinus anatomy, and vascular access [12].

The evidence supporting TAVR is robust across multiple risk groups, but HF patients require special attention to low-flow states, pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction, frailty, renal dysfunction, and mixed valvular disease. Post-procedural echo confirms gradient reduction, paravalvular leak, and ventricular response. Selected patients may require post-TAVR CCT to evaluate hypoattenuated leaflet thickening or suspected valve thrombosis [13].

Practical takeaway

In HF patients undergoing TAVR, echocardiography defines severity and hemodynamic consequence, while CT defines feasibility and procedural risk. The highest-value decisions occur when these data are reviewed within a heart team rather than interpreted in isolation.

Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair for mitral regurgitation

TEER has become a major intervention for selected patients with symptomatic secondary MR despite optimized medical therapy. The imaging challenge is not simply to grade MR severity, but to determine whether MR is a treatable driver of HF or a marker of advanced ventricular disease. This distinction partly explains the different results of COAPT and MITRA-FR [14], [15]. Long-term COAPT follow-up supports durable benefit in appropriately selected patients, whereas MITRA-FR reminds clinicians that correcting MR does not necessarily improve outcomes when ventricular remodeling is advanced or MR is proportionate to LV dilation [16].

Echocardiography is central throughout the TEER pathway. Baseline transthoracic echocardiography evaluates MR severity, LV size and function, RV function, pulmonary pressure, and tricuspid disease. Transesophageal echocardiography, particularly three-dimensional TEE, defines leaflet anatomy, coaptation, jet location, mitral valve area, and procedural suitability. During TEER, TEE guides transseptal puncture, device orientation, leaflet grasping, residual MR assessment, and transmitral gradient monitoring.

CMR is particularly valuable when echo quantification is discordant or limited. It can quantify LV and RV volumes, regurgitant volume and fraction, myocardial scar, and remodeling pattern [17]. In functional MR, this helps clarify whether the patient has severe MR with a potentially modifiable valve target or severe ventricular disease with limited procedural reserve.

Practical takeaway

TEER should be guided by anatomy, MR severity, ventricular remodeling, pulmonary pressure, RV function, and patient-centered goals. Tricuspid interventions

Tricuspid regurgitation is common in advanced HF and is associated with poor outcomes. It often reflects right-sided remodeling, pulmonary hypertension, atrial fibrillation, device leads, or left-sided valve disease. Imaging is challenging because the tricuspid valve has complex geometry, variable leaflet anatomy, and limited fluoroscopic landmarks.

Three-dimensional echocardiography and TEE are increasingly important for defining leaflet anatomy, coaptation gap, annular dilation, lead interaction, and device feasibility. CCT may provide complementary information on annular geometry, right-sided chambers, caval anatomy, and spatial relationships. Emerging tricuspid TEER and replacement systems have shown improvements in TR severity and quality of life in selected patients, but longer-term outcome data remain less mature than for left-sided valve interventions [18], [19].

Practical takeaway

In tricuspid intervention, imaging must move beyond grading TR severity. It should define mechanism, RV reserve, pulmonary vascular load, and whether reducing TR is likely to improve congestion and functional status rather than simply change valve appearance.

Left atrial appendage occlusion

Atrial fibrillation is common in HF and increases thromboembolic risk. In patients who are poor candidates for long-term anticoagulation, LAAO may be considered. CCT can define appendage morphology, ostial dimensions, landing zone, lobar anatomy, and device sizing. TEE remains widely used for procedural guidance and thrombus exclusion, while intracardiac echocardiography may allow minimalist workflows in experienced centers [20], [21].

For HF patients, the procedural decision should consider frailty, renal function, anticoagulation tolerance, atrial size, and the broader rhythm-control strategy. Imaging should confirm that the appendage anatomy is suitable and that the expected clinical benefit justifies procedural risk.

Practical takeaway

In LAAO, CT and TEE or ICE are complementary. CT improves pre-procedural planning, while TEE or ICE ensures procedural safety and immediate assessment of leak, position, and complications.

Resynchronization, revascularization, and advanced HF escalation

Cardiac resynchronization therapy

CRT remains a high-impact therapy in selected HFrEF patients with electrical dyssynchrony, particularly left bundle branch block and wide QRS duration. Despite guideline-based selection, non-response remains common. Imaging can refine, but not replace, standard electrical criteria.

Echocardiographic strain can identify the site of latest mechanical activation, while CMR can identify scar burden and scar location. Studies such as TARGET and STARTER suggested that placing the LV lead at or near the latest activated viable segment can improve remodeling and clinical response compared with conventional lead placement [22], [23]. CCT can support venous anatomy mapping when coronary sinus anatomy is complex or prior implantation has failed [24].

However, imaging-guided CRT has not eliminated non-response. Response depends on electrical substrate, scar burden, lead position, myocardial viability, atrial rhythm, device programming, RV function, MR, and adherence to GDMT. Therefore, imaging should be used to improve the probability of response, not to promise response.

Practical takeaway

Imaging-guided CRT is most useful when it identifies avoidable failure modes: lead placement over scar, inaccessible target veins, severe RV dysfunction, untreated MR, or advanced remodeling that makes reverse remodeling unlikely.

Revascularization in ischemic cardiomyopathy

In ischemic HF, imaging is used to define coronary anatomy, ischemic burden, scar, viability, and recovery potential. CMR late gadolinium enhancement can quantify scar transmurality, while PET and SPECT can assess perfusion and viability. Historically, viability imaging was used to select patients for revascularization. However, contemporary evidence has made this approach more nuanced.

The STICH extended follow-up supported survival benefit with surgical revascularization in selected patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy, but viability testing did not clearly identify a subgroup with differential long-term survival benefit [25], [26]. REVIVED-BCIS2 showed that PCI added to optimal medical therapy did not reduce death or HF hospitalization in patients with severe ischemic LV dysfunction and viable myocardium, and subsequent analyses questioned the role of viability testing as a simple selector for PCI benefit [27], [28].

This does not mean viability imaging has no role. Rather, its role is more clinically specific: clarifying scar burden, estimating likelihood of regional functional recovery, identifying ischemic territory, and informing heart-team discussions when symptoms, coronary anatomy, and myocardial substrate are discordant.

Practical takeaway

Viability should not be used as a stand-alone binary test for revascularization benefit. Revascularization decisions in ischemic HF should integrate anatomy, symptoms, ischemia, scar burden, surgical risk, comorbidity, patient goals, and likelihood of improving survival, symptoms, or HF trajectory.

Advanced HF therapies

In advanced HF, imaging helps identify when conventional interventions are likely to be futile and when referral for LVAD or transplantation should be accelerated. Echocardiography assesses LV size, RV function, TR severity, pulmonary pressures, valve disease, and hemodynamic response to therapy. CMR can quantify RV volumes and function, scar burden, infiltrative or inflammatory disease, and diffuse fibrosis. RHC remains essential for filling pressures, pulmonary vascular resistance, cardiac output, and transplant risk assessment [29], [30].

A key clinical risk is delaying advanced HF referral while repeatedly attempting interventions unlikely to reverse end-stage remodeling. Imaging can help distinguish patients who may improve after valve, coronary, or device therapy from those with advanced myocardial disease, RV failure, severe pulmonary vascular remodeling, or end-organ dysfunction who require advanced therapy evaluation.

Practical takeaway

In advanced HF, imaging should not only ask “Can this procedure be performed?” but also “Will this procedure meaningfully change the patient's trajectory?”

Invasive hemodynamics and real-time image-guided procedures

Non-invasive imaging cannot replace invasive hemodynamics in all HF patients. RHC remains central in advanced HF, pulmonary hypertension phenotyping, transplant evaluation, cardiogenic shock, and selected HFpEF patients with unexplained exertional symptoms. It defines right atrial pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, cardiac output, pulmonary vascular resistance, transpulmonary gradient, and RV-pulmonary arterial coupling surrogates.

In HFpEF, resting hemodynamics may be normal despite significant exertional limitation. Exercise or volume challenge can unmask abnormal filling pressure responses and prevent misclassification. Echocardiography complements these data by assessing LV diastolic function, LA remodeling, RV reserve, TR, and pulmonary pressure estimates. CMR may identify the underlying substrate, such as amyloid, myocarditis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or diffuse fibrosis. In selected patients, CMR-guided catheterization has been shown to integrate anatomy, flow quantification, and invasive physiology in a radiation-sparing workflow [31].

Real-time image-guided procedures are also expanding. Intracardiac echocardiography can support transseptal puncture and device guidance, while echo-fluoroscopy fusion improves spatial orientation during structural interventions. Hybrid laboratories increasingly combine fluoroscopy, echo, CT-derived roadmaps, and hemodynamic data to support complex interventions [32].

In coronary intervention, intravascular ultrasound and optical coherence tomography are particularly relevant in complex HF patients, where diffuse disease, calcification, left main disease, and prior revascularization are common. Intravascular imaging can optimize lesion preparation, stent sizing, expansion, and failure assessment [33].

Practical takeaway: Invasive physiology is most powerful when paired with an imaging-defined question. This approach avoids treating numbers in isolation and improves decision-making in borderline, discordant, or high-risk HF phenotypes.

Remote hemodynamic monitoring

A major limitation in HF management is that clinical deterioration often becomes obvious only after congestion is established. Implantable hemodynamic monitoring aims to detect rising filling pressures before overt symptoms, allowing earlier adjustment of diuretics and disease-modifying therapy.

CardioMEMS pulmonary artery pressure monitoring has the largest evidence base. Post-approval data supported effectiveness in real-world practice among selected patients managed with pulmonary artery pressure-guided therapy [34]. The GUIDE-HF trial was neutral for its overall primary endpoint, but the study was affected by pandemic-related changes in care delivery; pre-pandemic analyses suggested benefit in reducing HF events [35], [36]. The MONITOR-HF trial subsequently showed improved quality of life and fewer HF hospitalizations in a European chronic HF population [37].

Newer systems such as the Cordella pulmonary artery pressure sensor aim to integrate pressure monitoring with patient-facing biometric data and digital workflows. Early and pivotal data suggest feasibility and clinical promise, but long-term scalability, workflow burden, and health-system integration remain important questions [38]. Investigational left atrial pressure monitoring systems may be particularly relevant in selected patients with HFpEF or discordance between right-sided pressures and symptoms, although their evidence base is still evolving [39].

Remote monitoring is not simply a device intervention; it is a care model. Its success depends on thresholds, staffing, responsibility, patient engagement, medication protocols, and response time. Without a defined clinical workflow, pressure data can increase workload without improving outcomes.

Practical takeaway

Remote pressure monitoring works best when embedded in a protocolized HF program with clear response pathways, medication adjustment authority, and integration with imaging follow-up.

Artificial intelligence in imaging-guided HF interventions

AI is moving from experimental promise toward practical cardiovascular imaging applications. Its most immediate value is not replacing clinicians, but reducing variability, improving efficiency, and extracting reproducible measurements from large imaging datasets.

Automation and reproducibility

Echocardiography is vulnerable to variability in chamber quantification, EF estimation, strain analysis, and diastolic grading. Deep learning methods can automate segmentation and functional assessment, and randomized data suggest AI-assisted LVEF assessment can be non-inferior to sonographer-based assessment while improving workflow efficiency [40], [41].

Procedural planning and decision support

In structural interventions, AI may assist with annular detection, calcium quantification, device sizing, risk prediction, and procedural simulation. In CRT, automated integration of scar and activation patterns may improve lead targeting. In coronary intervention, AI-enabled OCT and IVUS tools may support real-time plaque characterization and stent optimization.

Predictive analytics

The larger promise of AI is multimodal risk prediction. Imaging features can be integrated with biomarkers, hemodynamics, rhythm data, remote pressure trends, laboratory results, and electronic health records to predict HF hospitalization, arrhythmia, mortality, or likelihood of response to intervention [42], [43] (Fig. 3). Barriers to implementation

The AI section must be approached with caution. Models trained in homogeneous populations may underperform in different ethnic, geographic, technical, or health-system settings. Black-box outputs may be difficult to justify in high-stakes procedural decisions. Regulatory approval does not necessarily prove outcome benefit. AI tools may also increase rather than reduce workload if they are poorly integrated into reporting systems or generate excessive alerts.

Transparent reporting, external validation, prospective testing, audit trails, clinician-in-the-loop design, and governance frameworks are essential. AI should be deployed where it solves a defined clinical problem: reducing measurement variability, improving reporting throughput, triaging high-risk patients, or identifying patterns that would otherwise be missed. It should not be added as a novelty layer without measurable clinical value.

Practical takeaway

AI should be treated as an implementation tool, not a clinical substitute. Its value depends on whether it improves decisions, workflow, equity, and outcomes in real practice.

Case-based illustration: Imaging-guided TEER in secondary mitral regurgitation

A practical example helps illustrate how a precision-care pathway works. Consider a patient with HFrEF, recurrent HF admissions, persistent symptoms despite optimized GDMT, and at least moderate-to-severe secondary MR on transthoracic echocardiography. The first imaging step is not referral directly to TEER, but confirmation of mechanism and proportionality. Echocardiography defines LV size and function, MR severity, pulmonary pressure, RV function, TR, mitral valve anatomy, and transmitral gradient.

If MR severity is discordant or echo windows are limited, CMR can clarify LV volumes, regurgitant volume, regurgitant fraction, scar burden, and whether ventricular remodeling is advanced. This is important because TEER benefit is most likely when MR is an important driver of HF rather than simply a marker of end-stage ventricular dilation.

If the patient remains anatomically suitable, three-dimensional TEE then defines leaflet length, coaptation depth, coaptation gap, jet origin, commissural involvement, calcification, and valve area. During the procedure, TEE guides transseptal puncture, clip orientation, leaflet grasp, MR reduction, pulmonary vein flow, and transmitral gradient. After TEER, follow-up echocardiography assesses residual MR, transmitral gradient, LV and RV remodeling, pulmonary pressure, and TR.

Patient-centered follow-up should include NYHA functional class, HF hospitalization burden, quality of life, exercise tolerance, diuretic requirement, and ability to continue GDMT. This case demonstrates the core principle of precision-guided intervention: the goal is not merely to perform a technically successful procedure, but to select a patient in whom anatomic correction is likely to produce meaningful clinical benefit.

Patient-centered endpoints and real-world value

A limitation of many imaging-guided pathways is that they focus heavily on anatomy and procedural success while giving less attention to what matters most to patients. HF is a chronic symptomatic condition. For many patients, the most meaningful outcomes are fewer admissions, improved breathlessness, better functional capacity, preserved independence, less diuretic instability, fewer clinic visits, and better quality of life.

This is particularly important in TEER, tricuspid intervention, remote monitoring, and advanced HF care. A technically successful valve repair may not represent clinical success if the patient remains severely limited due to RV failure, pulmonary vascular disease, frailty, renal dysfunction, or advanced myocardial disease. Conversely, a modest imaging improvement may be valuable if it reduces hospitalizations or improves functional status.

Therefore, future imaging-guided HF programs should embed patient-centered endpoints into routine follow-up. Imaging findings should be interpreted alongside quality-of-life scores, NYHA class, 6-min walk distance, HF hospitalization burden, medication tolerance, renal function, and patient goals. Precision care should mean better alignment between intervention and patient benefit, not simply more sophisticated testing.

Limitations of current evidence

Several evidence limitations should be acknowledged. First, imaging-guided selection is often supported by observational or single-center data rather than large pragmatic trials. Second, trials of interventions may not fully capture the incremental value of imaging because imaging is embedded in selection and procedure rather than randomized as a separate strategy. Third, response definitions vary across studies, particularly for CRT, valve intervention, and remote monitoring. Fourth, some imaging thresholds are vendor-dependent or technique-dependent, limiting generalizability across centers.

Fifth, the evidence is uneven across modalities. Echocardiography and CT are strongly represented in structural intervention workflows, while CMR and nuclear imaging are often underused despite their value in substrate characterization. Sixth, many AI studies remain retrospective, single-system, or focused on technical performance rather than clinical outcomes. Finally, implementation studies from low- and middle-resource settings remain limited, even though access and scalability are central to real-world impact.

Barriers to implementation

Access and equity

Advanced imaging and structural intervention services remain concentrated in specialized centers. This can delay diagnosis, restrict treatment access, and widen outcome disparities. Practical solutions include referral networks, standardized protocols, tele-imaging review, shared reporting templates, and regional heart-team models.

Standardization and training

Precision imaging requires consistent acquisition, interpretation, and communication. Variation in strain measurement, MR quantification, RV assessment, CT sizing, scar quantification, and hemodynamic interpretation can directly affect procedural decisions. Training programs must increasingly include multimodality interpretation, procedural imaging, invasive physiology, and basic understanding of AI limitations.

Workflow integration

Even high-quality imaging can fail to improve outcomes if reports are not actionable. Imaging reports should state mechanism, severity, procedural feasibility, limitations, and the next decision point. Remote monitoring and AI tools also require ownership: who receives alerts, who adjusts therapy, and how quickly intervention occurs.

Data fragmentation

HF patients often generate data across echo, CMR, CT, catheterization, device platforms, laboratory systems, and electronic health records. These data are frequently stored in separate systems. Future HF care will require interoperable platforms that integrate anatomy, substrate, physiology, therapy, and outcomes into a single clinical timeline.

Regulation and governance

AI-supported tools must be validated across populations, scanners, vendors, and health systems. Research priorities

Several research priorities are important for the next phase of imaging-guided HF care.

First, pragmatic trials should test whether structured multimodality imaging pathways improve outcomes compared with usual care. Relevant endpoints should include time to diagnosis, intervention selection, procedural safety, HF hospitalization, quality of life, cost-effectiveness, and equity.

Second, standardized quantitative thresholds are needed across vendors and laboratories. This includes strain, RV function, scar burden, diffuse fibrosis, CT sizing outputs, MR quantification, TR grading, and pulmonary vascular imaging markers.

Third, studies should clarify how imaging identifies responders and non-responders after interventions. For TEER, CRT, tricuspid intervention, revascularization, and TAVR, the field needs better predictors of sustained clinical benefit rather than only immediate technical success.

Fourth, remote monitoring trials should be embedded within contemporary GDMT pathways, with clear response protocols and attention to patient burden. The key question is not only whether pressures can be measured, but whether measuring them reliably changes care and outcomes.

Finally, AI tools should move beyond retrospective performance. Prospective, externally validated, clinician-in-the-loop studies are needed to prove that AI improves workflow, accuracy, risk prediction, decision-making, and patient outcomes without worsening inequity or alert fatigue.

Conclusion

Advanced cardiac imaging has evolved from a diagnostic adjunct into a strategic framework for contemporary HF intervention. Echocardiography, CMR, CCT, nuclear imaging, invasive hemodynamics, remote pressure monitoring, and AI each provide different pieces of the same clinical puzzle. Their greatest value emerges when they are used in a question-driven and patient-centered pathway: define the dominant mechanism, clarify anatomy and substrate, confirm physiology, select the right intervention, and monitor whether the patient truly benefits.

The future model of HF care should not be “more imaging for every patient,” but better imaging at the right decision point. To achieve this, the field needs standardized workflows, stronger prospective evidence, equitable access, and trustworthy AI implementation. Precision-guided HF intervention will be successful only if it improves not just procedural accuracy, but symptoms, quality of life, hospitalization burden, and long-term patient outcomes.
