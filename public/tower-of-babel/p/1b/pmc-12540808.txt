Exploring the impact mechanisms of EEG signals and emotional intelligence levels on language learning efficiency
Authors: Jun Yang, Jinlan Duan, Chenhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving language learning through a better understanding of how brain activity and emotional intelligence interact is a promising research direction with practical value in education. Traditional methods in this area often use static models, which struggle to capture how cognitive and emotional states change over time. To address this, we propose a new machine learning framework that adapts to both neural signals and emotional cues during language learning. It consists of two main components: the context-aware relational encoder (CARE) and the adaptive contextual enhancement (ACE) strategy. The CARE module represents sentence structure and meaning using attention mechanisms and semantic embeddings. ACE complements this by adjusting learning content in real time using data augmentation, meta-learning, and reinforcement feedback. Together, these components help the system adapt to individual learning needs and emotional responses, improving both engagement and learning outcomes. Our experiments show that this approach improves language understanding and adaptability across different emotional and linguistic scenarios, outperforming existing models on standard benchmarks. These results show the value of combining brain and emotional data to build smarter and more personalized language learning tools for education.Keywords: Language learning, Emotional intelligence, Neural networks, Contextual encoding, Adaptive strategiesSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The task of understanding the interplay between EEG signals and emotional intelligence (EI) levels in enhancing language learning efficiency is of significant importance, as it bridges neuroscience, psychology, and education1. only does this exploration provide insights into cognitive processes underlying learning, but it also paves the way for personalized educational interventions2. Previous research has indicated that EEG signals can serve as effective biomarkers for cognitive engagement and emotional states, while emotional intelligence is linked to adaptive learning strategies and resilience3. The integration of these two domains holds promise for optimizing language acquisition4. Furthermore, addressing this topic not only deepens our understanding of neural and emotional contributions to learning but also advances tools and methodologies that foster equitable and effective educational outcomes5.

To address the limitations of symbolic AI approaches, early studies focused on rule-based models and knowledge representations for understanding the role of EEG signals and EI in language learning6. Symbolic AI, rooted in structured knowledge bases and logical reasoning, attempted to create explicit mappings between physiological signals, such as EEG, and language learning metrics7. Techniques included feature engineering to extract meaningful EEG signal patterns and mapping these patterns to predefined emotional states, which were hypothesized to influence learning. Similarly, models of EI were formalized through knowledge graphs, linking behavioral markers to learning outcomes8. While these approaches succeeded in providing a conceptual framework, they often lacked robustness due to their reliance on handcrafted features and static representations, which failed to capture the dynamic interplay of neural and emotional factors9.

Building on the limitations of symbolic approaches, the advent of data-driven methods and machine learning revolutionized the analysis of EEG signals and EI in the context of language learning10. These methods leveraged large datasets to model the nonlinear and complex relationships between EEG features, emotional intelligence metrics, and learning outcomes11. Algorithms such as support vector machines, random forests, and ensemble models showed promise in classifying emotional states from EEG data and predicting language learning efficiency12. Furthermore, machine learning facilitated the automated analysis of self-reported EI measures and their correlation with learning metrics13. However, these models often suffered from interpretability challenges and overfitting issues, particularly when the dataset size was insufficient or lacked diversity14.

The emergence of deep learning and pre-trained models marked a significant evolution in understanding the impact of EEG signals and EI on language learning15. Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), enabled end-to-end processing of raw EEG signals, capturing temporal and spatial dependencies that were previously unattainable16. These models were also applied to analyze textual and behavioral data related to EI, benefiting from transfer learning approaches that utilized large-scale, pre-trained language models17. The integration of multimodal data–EEG signals, EI scores, and language learning metrics–into unified frameworks offered unprecedented precision and adaptability18. Despite these advances, challenges remained in ensuring data privacy, addressing biases in training data, and achieving generalization across diverse populations and contexts19.

Based on the aforementioned limitations of symbolic, machine learning, and deep learning methods, we propose a novel approach that integrates real-time EEG signal processing, adaptive emotional intelligence modeling, and personalized language learning frameworks. This approach leverages cutting-edge advances in neural decoding, dynamic EI assessment, and reinforcement learning to create a holistic system. Our method addresses the limitations of static modeling by incorporating continuous learning paradigms and real-time feedback loops, enabling adaptive interventions tailored to individual learners. Furthermore, it mitigates interpretability challenges by employing explainable AI techniques to make predictions and recommendations transparent to educators and learners alike.

Related work

Neural mechanisms in language learning

Research on neural mechanisms in language learning has provided valuable insights into how brain activity influences cognitive abilities related to language acquisition20. Electroencephalography (EEG) studies have been instrumental in mapping neural patterns associated with phonetic recognition, syntax processing, and semantic integration21. Early studies primarily focused on event-related potentials (ERPs), such as the N400 and P600 components, which are markers of semantic and syntactic processing respectively. These findings have deepened our understanding of how neural activity underpins language comprehension22. Recent advancements emphasize the role of dynamic neural interactions in language learning, incorporating tools like functional connectivity analysis and neural oscillation studies23. Connectivity-based approaches highlight the interaction between the language network (e.g., Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas) and executive control systems, such as the prefrontal cortex, during language tasks24. Oscillatory mechanisms, particularly theta and gamma rhythms, have also been implicated in language processing and learning25. Emerging machine learning methods, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have been employed to analyze EEG signals, uncovering complex patterns of neural engagement during language acquisition26. These tools offer novel perspectives for linking brain activity to language learning outcomes. Despite these advancements, challenges remain in translating neural insights into practical applications for language education. Current efforts aim to integrate real-time EEG-based feedback systems into adaptive learning platforms. Such approaches promise personalized interventions that align with individual neural profiles, enhancing efficiency in language learning. Future work should explore the convergence of neural and affective data to refine these systems, bridging the gap between neuroscience and educational technology.

Emotional intelligence in language learning

Emotional intelligence (EI) plays a pivotal role in language acquisition by influencing motivation, resilience, and interpersonal communication skills27. Theoretical frameworks, such as Salovey and Mayer’s EI model, suggest that emotional perception, regulation, and utilization are critical for effective learning28. Language learning environments that foster positive emotional states have been shown to improve engagement and retention29. Empirical studies have linked high EI levels with superior performance in second language acquisition (SLA)30. Learners with strong EI are better equipped to manage anxiety, maintain motivation, and navigate culturally diverse communication settings. These findings have driven interest in designing curricula and interventions that enhance EI alongside linguistic skills31. For instance, collaborative learning approaches and role-playing activities have been employed to develop empathy and emotional awareness in language learners. Technological advancements offer new avenues for integrating EI into language learning. Virtual reality (VR) platforms and affective computing systems enable immersive experiences that simulate real-world emotional and linguistic challenges. Similarly, mobile applications equipped with sentiment analysis algorithms can provide personalized feedback, encouraging emotional growth alongside linguistic development. Despite these innovations, challenges persist in quantifying EI and tailoring interventions to individual needs. Future research should focus on combining biometric data with self-report measures to enhance the reliability of EI assessments. Moreover, exploring the interplay between EI and neural activity could uncover deeper insights into their joint impact on language learning.

Adaptive learning systems for language acquisition

Adaptive learning systems represent a transformative approach in language education, offering personalized pathways based on learners’ unique needs and preferences32. Traditional static models often fail to account for the dynamic nature of language learning, which involves continuous interaction between cognitive, emotional, and contextual factors33. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled the development of adaptive systems that leverage real-time data to optimize learning experiences34. Key innovations in this domain include the use of deep learning architectures, such as transformers, for language modeling35. Models like BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) and GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) have revolutionized natural language processing, offering nuanced representations of linguistic context36. Adaptive systems build on these advancements by incorporating user-specific data, such as performance metrics and behavioral patterns, to tailor content delivery. Techniques like reinforcement learning enable these systems to refine recommendations over time, ensuring sustained learner engagement. Another critical aspect of adaptive learning is the integration of affective computing, which allows systems to respond to learners’ emotional states. For example, facial expression analysis and sentiment tracking can inform adjustments in difficulty levels or feedback styles. meta-learning strategies, which involve training systems to adapt quickly to new tasks, have proven effective in enhancing cross-domain language learning. Despite these strides, challenges remain in balancing scalability with personalization. Developing systems that cater to diverse linguistic and emotional profiles requires robust datasets and sophisticated algorithms. Future work should prioritize the ethical use of learner data, ensuring privacy and equity in adaptive education. By combining advancements in neural and emotional intelligence research, adaptive systems have the potential to revolutionize language learning on a global scale.

The combination of a bi-directional Transformer for sentence-level encoding and a GRU for document-level modeling reflects a deliberate architectural trade-off. Transformers are highly effective in capturing contextual dependencies within sentences, where the length of sequences is relatively manageable and full attention can be computed without prohibitive memory cost. This is particularly beneficial in our scenario, where semantic disambiguation within a sentence is crucial for aligning multimodal inputs with language-related brain signals. However, when dealing with document-level representations–i.e., sequences of multiple sentence embeddings–the input length increases significantly. Using Transformers at this level would introduce substantial computational overhead due to their self-attention mechanism’s quadratic complexity. Additionally, in the presence of high-dimensional and noise-prone EEG data, Transformers can be prone to overfitting and instability, especially with limited training data. To address this, we adopt a GRU for document-level modeling. GRUs are well-suited for capturing long-range sequential dependencies with fewer parameters, and they have demonstrated robustness in modeling temporal structures in physiological signals. In our experiments, the GRU consistently provided better generalization and smoother representations across sentence sequences, contributing to the system’s overall performance and stability. This hybrid approach thus capitalizes on the strengths of both architectures: rich local context encoding via Transformers, and efficient global sequence modeling via GRUs. Our ablation study in Section 4.4 supports this design choice by showing performance degradation when either component is removed or replaced.

Experimental setup

Dataset

The AMIGOS Dataset37 is a widely used collection designed for emotion analysis. It includes physiological and audiovisual data collected from participants watching videos designed to elicit various emotional states. This dataset facilitates multimodal emotion recognition and provides extensive annotations for emotional labels. It is highly valued for its combination of EEG, ECG, and facial expressions, enabling diverse research directions. The Physionet MI Dataset38 focuses on motor imagery-based brain-computer interface research. It comprises EEG data recorded during motor imagery tasks where participants imagine movements of their hands or feet. This dataset is crucial for developing advanced brain-computer interfaces and includes well-structured signals with detailed task annotations, supporting reproducibility and comparative studies in neurophysiological research. The Age and Gender Dataset39 provides annotated images of individuals with corresponding age and gender information. It is widely used in demographic analysis, computer vision, and biometric authentication research. The dataset offers significant diversity in terms of age ranges, ethnicities, and environmental conditions, enabling robust model training for age and gender prediction tasks. The AutoTherm Dataset40 is a novel dataset dedicated to thermal imaging and automated thermal pattern recognition. It contains thermal video sequences of subjects performing specific activities, along with annotations for regions of interest. This dataset is instrumental in developing models for medical diagnostics, activity recognition, and environmental monitoring using thermal data. While the AMIGOS dataset does not contain explicit language learning tasks, it provides rich EEG and emotional data during multimodal stimulus processing, including video clips with spoken and visual language content. We consider this a cognitively relevant proxy for real-world language learning conditions, where learners are often exposed to emotionally charged audiovisual content. Our model is designed to adapt to the neural and emotional states evoked in such contexts, which is central to understanding and optimizing engagement during language-related cognitive tasks. Therefore, although the dataset is not a conventional language-learning corpus, it serves as a viable benchmark to test the adaptability and emotional sensitivity of our proposed architecture, which are key components for building future personalized language learning systems.

Experimental details

In our experiments, we utilized the AMIGOS Dataset, Physionet MI Dataset, Age and Gender Dataset, and AutoTherm Dataset to validate the effectiveness of our proposed method. For preprocessing, all datasets underwent normalization to ensure consistent input scale across experiments. EEG and ECG signals were band-pass filtered between 0.5 Hz and 50 Hz to remove noise, while image datasets were resized to for compatibility with state-of-the-art deep learning architectures. The model architecture comprised a multimodal framework incorporating convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for image and thermal data and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) for time-series data like EEG and ECG. For training, the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of and a batch size of 32 was employed. The loss function was task-dependent, using categorical cross-entropy for classification tasks and mean squared error for regression tasks. All experiments were conducted using PyTorch, and computations were performed on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. To evaluate performance, 5-fold cross-validation was conducted for all datasets to ensure robust generalization. Evaluation metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score for classification tasks and mean absolute error (MAE) for regression tasks. Baselines were implemented from state-of-the-art methods cited in literature, ensuring a fair comparative analysis. Data augmentation strategies were applied, such as random cropping and flipping for image data and synthetic signal generation for physiological datasets, to prevent overfitting. Each dataset’s specific characteristics were leveraged to tailor augmentations and preprocessing pipelines. For example, the Physionet MI Dataset benefited from windowed segmentation of EEG signals to enhance temporal feature extraction, while the AutoTherm Dataset utilized frame differencing to emphasize motion patterns. Training was conducted over 50 epochs with early stopping based on validation performance. Each dataset’s specific modalities were fused using attention mechanisms to prioritize the most salient features. The experiments demonstrated the scalability and versatility of our method across various data types, achieving competitive results compared to existing benchmarks (Algorithm1).

Discussion

In this work, we introduced a novel framework that integrates neural and emotional dynamics into adaptive language learning. The complexity of our architecture–comprising CARE and ACE–is motivated by the multifaceted nature of language acquisition, which involves not only linguistic comprehension but also emotional engagement and neural adaptation. Traditional models often overlook this interplay, leading to static learning experiences that lack personalization and adaptability. The CARE module plays a critical role by capturing relational and semantic nuances in sentence structure through attention-based encoding. This allows the model to better align with learners’ cognitive representations. The ACE strategy, on the other hand, dynamically refines the learning process using emotional and neural feedback through meta-learning and real-time augmentation. Together, these components form a complementary system: CARE handles linguistic structure while ACE ensures contextual adaptation. Our experimental results demonstrate that this dual-module architecture significantly improves language comprehension across diverse emotional and neural states, achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks. These findings affirm the value of a multimodal, adaptive design that responds to individual learner variability. We acknowledge the complexity of the model may raise concerns for real-world deployment. However, each architectural choice is driven by pedagogical relevance and backed by empirical improvements. Future work could explore lightweight variants to enhance practicality while preserving the adaptive capabilities that are central to our approach.

Conclusions and future work

This study investigates the complex interaction between neural mechanisms and emotional intelligence in enhancing language learning efficiency, a vital issue in educational psychology. Traditional methods often fall short due to their static modeling of the dynamic relationships between cognitive and emotional states. To address this, the study introduces a dual-layer framework integrating the Context-Aware Relational Encoder (CARE) model and the Adaptive Contextual Enhancement (ACE) strategy. The CARE model captures intricate linguistic structures via hierarchical encoding and relational reasoning, utilizing advanced techniques like multi-head self-attention and semantic embeddings. Meanwhile, the ACE strategy enhances real-time adaptability through dynamic data augmentation, meta-learning for domain adaptation, and reinforcement signals. The experimental results illustrate that this innovative framework significantly improves language comprehension and adaptability across varied linguistic and emotional settings, outperforming conventional approaches. These findings emphasize the value of combining cognitive and emotional dimensions in scalable language learning systems.

Despite its promising results, the study has limitations. the reliance on specific benchmarks for evaluating the framework’s effectiveness may not generalize well to real-world scenarios where linguistic and emotional contexts are more nuanced and variable. Future research should explore more diverse datasets and environments to ensure broader applicability. Second, while the ACE strategy’s real-time optimization shows potential, the computational overhead associated with dynamic adjustments can hinder practical deployment. Subsequent studies might focus on developing lightweight algorithms or hardware optimizations to alleviate these constraints. Overall, the integration of neural and emotional intelligence dimensions opens exciting avenues for adaptive language learning systems, paving the way for more personalized and effective educational technologies.
