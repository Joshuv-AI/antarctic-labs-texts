Laparoscopic Assisted Abdominal Perineal Resection (APR) in a Low‐Resource Setting: A Case Report From Muhimbili National Hospital, Tanzania
Authors: Rajabu Athumani Bakari, Karushaija Mujwahuzi, Ahmed Abubakar Ahmed, Aidah Kenseko, Godbless Exaud Massawe, Kitembo Salum Kibwana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbdominoperineal resection (APR) is a popular surgical method for rectal malignancies, offering benefits such as reduced blood loss and faster recovery. However, its application in low‐resource settings is challenging due to limited access to advanced laparoscopic equipment. This case report points out a successful laparoscopic management of a case of low rectal tumor in a resource‐limited setting.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains a significant health burden worldwide, with rectal cancer requiring complex surgeries [1]. Abdominoperineal resection (APR) is the standard approach for low rectal cancers, particularly when sphincter preservation is not feasible [2]. While laparoscopic surgery has revolutionized colorectal procedures, its application in low‐resource settings is often hindered by limited infrastructure, a lack of trained personnel, and financial constraints [3, 4, 5, 6]. This case report aims to illustrate the successful implementation of a laparoscopic‐assisted APR in a hospital with restricted resources, emphasizing cost‐effective modifications and strategies.

Laparoscopic APR is a type of laparoscopic colon resection that involves creating a permanent end colostomy following the perineal resection under laparoscopic assistance. Low‐resource settings, also referred to as low‐income countries, are characterized by limited financial, human, and infrastructural resources, particularly in healthcare and education. It has several advantages, including decreased pain, smaller incision, early recovery of postoperative ileus, less hospital stay, fewer intraoperative and postoperative complications, less blood loss, reduction in injury to blood vessels, and it has fewer abdominal wound infection [1, 3, 7]. A multidisciplinary approach has been designed to treat rectal cancer, with curative surgery being the best choice for locally advanced rectal cancer with TME.

Case History and Examination

A 54‐year‐old female, known to be hypertensive and asthmatic and controlled on medications presented to our facility with 1‐year history of passing fresh blood in her stool, sometimes with blood clots. It was associated with pain during defecation, a feeling of incomplete defecation, and significant weight loss, though she denied any history of a mass protruding per anus. There was no history of alcohol intake, cigarette smoking, or trauma to the perineum with occasional intake of red meat and fatty foods. On physical examination, the patient was clinically stable with vitals within the normal range. The abdominal examination was unremarkable with no palpable abdominal mass. During the digital rectal examination (DRE), a mass was detected 3 cm from the anal verge on the anterior rectal wall that was mobile and not obstructing the lumen but could not go above it.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Diagnosis and Differentials

The diagnosis of a low rectal tumor was reached after a colonoscopy and examination under anesthesia (EUA), with the biopsy taken reporting adenocarcinoma that was moderately differentiated.

Metastatic Workups and Staging

A thoraco‐abdominopelvic CT scan done showed irregular wall thickening involving the rectum with peri‐rectal infiltration, with the impression of a rectal tumor (Figure 1). No features of metastasis picked up from CT scan of the chest and abdomen. Based on CT scan, we clinically staged the patient as cT3N1M0. MRI was not done due to financial constraints.

Management and Operative Details

The patient did not receive neoadjuvant therapy as the disease was considered as early cancer and she was scheduled for a laparoscopic‐assisted APR. The heart seemed structurally normal on echocardiography with an ejection fraction of 61%. The preoperative labs were within the normal ranges.

Under general anesthesia, a skilled colorectal surgeon and experienced laparoscopic surgeon with the team position the patient in a modified lithotomy position with 3 laparoscopic ports (Figure 2). Intra‐abdominal findings revealed no ascites, peritoneal carcinomatosis, or liver metastasis (Figure 3). The rectosigmoid colon was mobilized after dissection following TME principles despite the lack of high‐definition imaging, and the proximal sigmoid colon was applied as a permanent end colostomy in one of the ports on the left iliac region (Figure 2). The perineal dissection was completed in the lithotomy position (Figure 4) and the specimen for histopathology was delivered per anally (Figures 5 and 6). The perineal wound was closed in layers after a drain was applied (Figure 7).

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient is still being followed up at our surgical clinic with an end colostomy well‐functioning and during the last visit she was doing fine with no new complaints reported.

Discussion

For low rectal tumors that cannot be treated with sphincter‐preserving techniques, APR is still the accepted surgical method [8, 9, 10, 11]. The use of minimally invasive procedures like laparoscopy, which are typically carried out through an open approach, has demonstrated notable advantages in terms of postoperative recovery, pain control, and cosmetic results [12, 13, 14]. However, there are particular difficulties and factors to take into account while implementing laparoscopic‐assisted APR in low‐resource settings, which call for careful attention [15].

The expensive cost of laparoscopic towers and equipment, a shortage of skilled workers, erratic power supplies, and poor maintenance services are some of the obstacles that have hindered the adoption of laparoscopic procedures in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). Despite these obstacles, minimally invasive colorectal operations have been selectively integrated as a result of recent developments and the strategic use of laparoscopic surgeries, particularly in teaching hospitals and urban tertiary institutions [15, 16]. In order to optimize available resources, laparoscopic‐assisted APR was executed in this instance using a hybrid approach that combined open perineal resection with laparoscopy for the abdominal dissection [17, 18].

Comparing laparoscopic APR to open surgery, numerous studies have confirmed its oncological safety in high‐resource settings, demonstrating comparable oncologic clearance with less blood loss and shorter hospital stays. The key to guaranteeing curative intent in this low‐resource setting was meticulous patient selection and adherence to oncologic principles, such as obtaining negative circumferential resection margins and complete mesorectal excision. The surgical team balanced procedural safety and cost‐effectiveness by using reused tools and a single‐monitor system. Due in significant part to instrumentation restrictions and ergonomic issues, the operation took longer than a traditional open APR.

Conventional open APR has disadvantages, including long abdominal incision and a high risk of infection, both of which laparoscopic techniques make up these limitations [1]. Laparoscopic APR does not need the same laparotomy incision as open APR and has advantages in dissecting adhesion [1]. Average operating time was 221 min and a conversion rate of 4.0%, and the most commonly identified injury was to the sacral plexus [6]. Neo‐adjuvant therapy is not offered to most patients, as its effectiveness is not yet proven, and for patients with low rectal cancer within 5 cm from the anal verge APR is still considered the standard surgical option by most colorectal surgeons [4]. Although it is said that neo‐adjuvant therapy has advantages in decreasing the local recurrence rate, increasing sphincter‐saving procedures, and achieving complete regression of some cancers [19].

The significance of continuous surgical training and capacity‐building in LMICs is shown by this case. Prior exposure to fundamental laparoscopic procedures, including appendectomy and cholecystectomy, followed by practical workshops and supervision, helped the surgical team successfully complete laparoscopic‐assisted APR. The example provides a model for the gradual development of skills in challenging laparoscopic colorectal procedures in settings with limited resources. Additionally, access to simulation‐based training and collaborations with foreign surgical programs might quicken the learning curve for intricate oncological treatments.

Finding a balance between cost and benefit is one of the biggest problems in LMICs. Although the initial cost of purchasing laparoscopic equipment may seem high, cost analysis studies have demonstrated that these costs can eventually be covered by shorter hospital stays, faster return to work, and fewer wound infection rates. The patient's smooth postoperative recovery in this instance, which included early ambulation and no need for analgesics, demonstrated the long‐term benefits of investing in laparoscopic infrastructure.

Better postoperative pain management, reduced immunosuppression, and a quicker return of bowel function have all been linked to minimally invasive surgery [20, 21, 22]. In the example at hand, the patient returned to oral intake on the second postoperative day and was released on the sixth day without experiencing any significant difficulties. Preoperative counseling and continuous stoma care instruction helped to lessen the psychological effects of a permanent colostomy. Although early indicators were encouraging, long‐term follow‐up is required to assess local recurrence and quality of life outcomes.

Patient safety, informed consent, and equity are ethical issues that arise when high‐complexity operations are performed in settings with limited resources. In this instance, the surgical team maintained a safety net for conversion to open surgery, ensured that comprehensive counseling was provided, and obtained informed consent, understanding the risks and limitations. To reduce surgical risk, cooperation with the perioperative and anesthetic teams was essential, as was making sure the intensive care unit was available after surgery. This case demonstrates that, when carried out by a skilled team with sufficient planning and institutional support, laparoscopic‐assisted APR is both possible and safe in a low‐resource situation. Even if there are still logistical and technical obstacles, little investments in infrastructure, tools, and training can help laparoscopic colorectal surgery become more widely used in LMICs, which will ultimately improve patient outcomes and surgical equity.
