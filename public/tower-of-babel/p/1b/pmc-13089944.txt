Fecal Material of Captive Wild Animals as Source of CAZymes With Application Potential
Authors: Julia Bechtner, Jiri Hosek, Clarissa Schwab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal systems of mammals and birds host taxonomically complex and functionally diverse microbial communities. Microbial activities contribute to community functioning and interaction with the host but can also be exploited as a source of novel enzymes or other industrially relevant microbial traits. With the overall goal to identify new resources for carbohydrate‐active enzymes (CAZymes), we bioprospected fecal microbial communities of the little‐explored source of captive wild animals. Using dbcan3, we identified a CAZyome dominated by glycosyl hydrolases (GHs) specialized in degrading oligo‐ and polysaccharides with much lower diversity and abundance of glycosyl transferases, carboxyl esterases, polysaccharide lyases, and redox enzymes with auxiliary activity. CAZyome profiles differed between animals depending on gut physiology and diet. Crude cell extracts conferred hydrolytic activity against compositionally and structurally diverse polysaccharides and nitrophenyl‐sugar analogs. We identified five candidate GH68 and GH70 enzymes with the potential to produce oligo‐ and polysaccharides from sucrose, highlighting that fecal metagenomes are a source of rare CAZymes with industrial relevance. Taken together, we exemplify the functional potential captive wild animal fecal microbiota and suggest such a gene pool as a largely untapped resource for the discovery of novel biotechnological applications.Keywords: bioprospecting, carbohydrate‐active enzymes, glycansucrases, gut microbiota, metagenomics

Introduction

Carbohydrate‐active enzymes (or CAZymes) encompass a diverse class of enzymes and support proteins assigned to six functional groups, e.g. glycoside hydrolases (GH), glycoside transferases (GT), polysaccharide lyases (PL), carbohydrate esterases (CE) and redox enzymes with auxiliary activities (AA), and noncatalytic carbohydrate‐binding modules (CBM) that act together with CAZymes. CAZymes carry out diverse reactions often with selectivity and rate enhancements. GH are mainly involved in carbohydrate degradation and are often specific for a substrate and the stereo‐ and regiochemistry of the glycosidic linkage. Certain retaining GH can also built polymers [1]. GH have found a number of applications at an industrial level, e.g. for the saccharification of plant biomass for biofuel production using enzymatic cocktails (e.g. Celluclast, Cellic CTec2), as feed enzymes [2], for the production of prebiotics [3], and for designer food sugars [4]. GT synthesize oligo‐ and polysaccharides through the transfer of sugar moieties from activated donor molecules to acceptor compounds including carbohydrates, proteins, or lipids. GT are industrially employed for example in the production of human milk oligosaccharides [5] or the glycosylation of natural products. PLs cleave uronic acid‐containing polysaccharide chains via a β‐elimination mechanism, while CEs deacylate O‐ or N‐groups from sugar molecules. Enzyme mixtures such as pectinase include GH, PL, and CE and are used industrially for juice extraction and wine clarification. CBM often co‐occur with catalytic CAZy and enhance their activity. AA include oxidative/redox active enzymes responsible for lignin depolymerization [6], which support GH, PL, and CE to gain access to recalcitrant plant cell wall polysaccharides. Due to their supportive function, the AA group of lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases is incorporated into commercial cellulase cocktails such as the Cellic series [7].

Due to their relevance in several industries, there is a high interest for novel microbes with the potential for CAZyme activity. A habitat rich in taxonomically and functionally diverse microbes is the gastrointestinal tract of mammals and birds [8]. Mammalia and Aves rely on the enzymatic activity of gastrointestinal microbes to degrade complex plant‐derived carbohydrates and gain substrates for fermentation. Intestinal microbiota composition and activity depend on diet and gut physiology [8]. In addition to classifying animals as herbivores, omnivores, or carnivores, animals have been categorized as foregut or hindgut fermenters based on gut physiology. Briefly, the foregut fermenters have a vessel attached to the acid‐secreting part of the stomach that hosts a numerous microbial community. Foregut fermenters include ruminants and some monkeys. In hindgut fermenters, the cecum and large colon serve as chambers housing microbial communities (e.g. rodents, horses, elephants). Herbivores are found among the foregut and hindgut fermenters, while hindgut fermentation is common among the omnivores and carnivores.

Domesticated ruminants were carriers of microbes with diverse CAZymes representing all major classes GH, GT, CE, PL, CBM, and AA [9]. Protein identity to enzymes present in databases was generally less than 60% in 2019 suggesting that ruminants host alternative CAZymes [9]. Wild animals have been identified as a rich source of gut microbes with novel microbial functions such as toxin‐metabolizing proteases [10]. Intestinal microbiota of wild (captive) animals can also be a source of CAZymes with few available studies, for example, on the herbivorous capybara (Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris) [11], the Giant Panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) [12], or baleen whales [13]. The CAZyme profiles of baleen whale microbiota were characterized by high abundance of GH families that are likely involved in animal polysaccharide [13] indicating that diet is an important determinant in CAZyme distribution. These studies suggest captive wild animal microbiota as a habitat of diverse and industrially relevant enzymatic activities. With the aim to further explore the potential of gut microbes of the underinvestigated group of wild‐captive animals [14] as a source of novel CAZymes, we screened fecal material of animals differing in gut physiology and diet. We combined metagenome mining, which was previously successfully applied to identify the potential for CAZymes of animal intestinal microbiota [9, 10, 11, 12, 13], and enzyme activity assays to test for the presence of CAZymes that could find application in industrial applicants in the future.

Results and Discussion

Captive Wild Animal Metagenomes Are a Rich Source of CAZymes

In previous work, we sequenced the metagenomes of fecal samples (n = 25) of captive wild animals representing different diet types (17 herbivores, three carnivores, five omnivores) and gut physiology (11 foregut and 14 hindgut fermenters) [8]. We generated metagenome‐assembled genomes (MAGs) representing 4150 bacteria and archaea species [8]. In order to determine the potential for CAZymes in MAGs, we used the automated CAZyme annotation tool dbcan3 [15]. dbcan3 incorporates the alignment tools HMMER, Diamond, and dbCAN_sub and provides substrate predictions.

CAZymes that degrade carbohydrates include the GH, PL, and CE. Based on dbcan3 analysis of MAG gene content, we identified 153 GH families, with most subfamilies assigned to GH13 (α‐glucosidases including amylases, n = 36 subfamilies), GH5 (β‐glucosidase and β‐mannosidases, n = 34), GH43 (β‐glucosidase, n = 32), GH31 (α‐glucosidases and α‐galactosidases, n = 17), and GH130 (β‐mannosidases, n = 12) highlighting the richness of GH in fecal metagnomes of captive wild animals. The most abundant GH families were the amylases of GH13 (11.8% relative abundance including subfamilies), GH2 and GH3 (5.4 and 3.5%) encoding β‐glucosidase, β‐xylosidase, β‐galactosidase, and β‐mannosidase. GH2 and GH3 were also highly abundant in cecal and rectal samples of the capybara, possibly contributing to plant cell wall degradation [11]. In our metagenomes from captive wild animals, GH109 and GH179 contributed 6.2%. There are few characterized enzymes of GH109 and GH179 [16, 17]. Members of this group employ an unusual redox mechanism with NAD+ as cofactor [16].

In total, 33 PL families were detected. The most abundant PL were assigned to hyaluronan and chondroitin lyases of PL8 (15.5% of all PL) and PL8_2 (8.2%), and to pectate lyases PL1_2 (11.6%). The detection of these PL families suggests for the potential of captive wild animal microbiota to degrade the vertebrate‐derived glycosaminoglycans hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate and the plant polymer pectin. The dataset encompassed 18 CE families that belonged mainly to CE1 (27.3% of all CE) and CE4 (26.1%), which catalyze the deacetylation of polysaccharides. Degradation activity of GH and PL is supported by AA. There were representatives of eight AA families; the most abundant was AA4 (34.5% of all AA), which contains vanillyl‐alcohol oxidases that catalyze the conversion of phenolic compounds followed by AA7 enzymes (20.3%) belonging to the glucooligosaccharide oxidases. Together, these data indicate that fecal material of captive wild animals provides a reservoir of diverse and uncharted CAZymes that confer the potential to decomposes plant and animal derived polysaccharides.

We recovered 71 GT families, which include the primary enzymes involved in carbohydrate synthesis. Together, GT2 and GT represented more than 50% of all GT. Certain microbial GT2 are capable of producing β‐glucans with different linkages including bacterial cellulose, which might be released into the gut environment [17]. Often, GT and GH are connected to CBM, which supports catalytic activity. In our captive wild animal metagenomes, CBM48 (34.7% of all CBM), which bind various linear and cyclic α‐glucans, and CBM67 (21.1%) with rhamnose binding activity were predominant. CBM48 has not only been linked to GH and GT but also to CE activity on arabinoxylan indicating diverse binding capacity [18]. Our observations highlight that captive wild animal fecal microbiota has the potential to express a diverse CAZyome covering all major enzyme and protein classes. The Carbohydrate Active Enzyme database (http://cazy.org) is constantly evolving [19], and the CAZymes of captive wild animal microbiota covered a major proportion of CAZy families, indicating the richness of catalytic potential.

Impact of Gut Physiology and Diet on Occurrence of CAZYmes

Next, we determined how gut physiology and diet are linked to the presence of different CAZymes. There were significantly more CBM, AA, and GT in hindgut than foregut fermenters, while the abundance of GH, CE, and PL involved in the degradation of plant carbohydrates was not significantly different (Figure 1). As both the most abundant GH and CBM families (GH13 and CBM48) were linked to α‐glucan degradation, these data indicate efficient starch/glycogen binding and degradation capacity of fecal metagenomes especially of hindgut fermenters.

AA includes oxidative enzymes responsible for lignin depolymerization that have been frequently identified in fungi [6]. In our dataset, 712 MAGs harbored at least one AA; 92% of these MAGs were assigned to the domain Bacteria and 8% to Archaea. Among the bacteria MAGs, most belonged to the phyla Bacillota (59%), Verrucomicrobiota (13%), Actinomycetota (9%), and Pseudomonadota (9%). MAGs with highest occurrence of AA (ten or more) were mainly annotated to the phyla Actinomycetota and Pseudomonadota and included Rhodococcus sp. (Supporting Information Table S1), which are known for their capacity to degrade lignin [20]. These observations point to captive wild‐animal fecal metagenomes as a source of bacterial hosts that possess diverse AA, which can support the degradation of complex, lignin‐containing plant material.

Despite variations in dietary plant content, there was no significant difference in CAZymes abundance between samples derived herbivores, omnivores, and carnivores based on gene content (Figure 1), suggesting that the potential for overall carbohydrate activity was not or little affected by diet. In another study, the relative abundance of genes encoding cellulases and hemicellulases was higher in herbivores and omnivores than carnivores in agreement with higher enzymatic activity [12], highlighting that additional differences in CAZymes abundance can occur on GH family level.

Therefore, we determined the abundance of the understudied GH109 and GH179 families, which were highly prevalent in fecal metagenomes of captive wild animals. Both GH families encode β‐N‐acetylhexosaminidases and were significantly more abundant in foregut fermenting herbivores than in hindgut fermenters (Figure 2). While characterized GH109 enzymes have been associated with taxa that colonize the mammalian intestine, e.g. Akkermansia muciniphila (ACD03864.1) or Bacteroides spp. (CAJ01379.1, AAO79348.1), GH179 has been characterized for an uncultured archaeon (QFQ13829.1) [21]. So far, GH109 and GH179 have been mainly linked to the degradation of host‐derived glycosylated molecules such as mucin [22, 23], but their presence in environmental isolates [21] suggests a broader functionality. Our results point at the gut microbiome of foregut fermenting herbivores as a possible source of novel GH109/GH179 enzymes.

Activity Profiles of Fecal Crude Extracts

To relate genetic functional potential predicted with dbcan3 with GH activity, we tested for degradation/hydrolysis activity of prominent GH families present in fecal crude cell extracts using nitrophenyl (NP) linked glycosides and agar plate assays. Oligosaccharide degradation activity conferred by GH2/3 family enzymes was detected mostly in hindgut fermenters based on the release of NP from NP‐glycosides, but more sparsely distributed in other fecal crude cell extracts (Table 1). Preferred substrates were β‐glucosides and β‐galactosides with activities ranging from 0.02 to 0.74 µmol released NP per g fecal material in 90 min. Activity on α‐glucosides and α‐fucosides was observed less frequently and at lower levels (0.02–0.13 µmol released NP per g fecal material in 90 min). None of the crude extracts hydrolyzed α‐mannoside or β‐xyloside.

In accordance with the high abundance of genes encoding GH13, starch and resistant starch were degraded by crude extracts obtained from most samples with the exception of extract from D6 (African lion) (Table 1). There was little difference in the hydrolysis of starch and resistant starch. Cellulose, xyloglucan, and inulin degradation activity was also observed for most samples from all gut physiology/diet types, while ararbinogalactan was less accessible. Pectin hydrolysis, which requires a combination of GH, PL, and CE, was observed across gut physiology and diet types with the highest activity in hindgut fermenting omnivores (Table 1). These data show that fecal material of captive wild animals provides a broad GH activity spectrum targetting common dietary oligo‐ and polysaccharides in accordance with activity potential predicted by metagenome analysis.

A Closer Look into the Potential for Sucrose‐Based Polymerization Activity

Next, we more closely investigated the presence of genes encoding GH68 and GH70. Enzymes belonging to these GH families are of particular biotechnological interest due to their capacity to synthesize oligo‐ and polysaccharides from sucrose, rearrange linkage types within existing polysaccharides, and catalyze the glycosylation of diverse acceptor molecules [24, 25]. Members of both GH families employ a retaining catalytic mechanism with initial substrate hydrolysis, followed by transfer of the fructosyl (GH68) or glucosyl (GH70) moiety to an acceptor molecule [26]. Such glycansucrases produce either fructans or α‐glucans.

GH68 enzymes have been most extensively studied for their ability to produce high‐molecular‐weight levans and inulins, as well as fructooligosaccharides [27, 28]. GH68 accepts alternative substrates such as raffinose, a plant‐derived trisaccharide, and many possess intrinsic activity toward fructan‐type polysaccharides [29]. GH68 homologs were identified in both bacteria and archaea isolated from a wide range of ambient (e.g. animals, plants, soil) and extreme habitats such as highly saline environments or hot springs [30]. This broad ecological distribution highlights their potential for industrial applications, as catalytic stability often reflects the physicochemical conditions of their environment. In fecal metagenomes of captive wild animals, we identified four MAGs encoding candidate GH68 enzymes (Table 2). These sequences were incorporated into a phylogenetic reconstruction including well‐characterized GH68 representatives (Figure 3A,B). The protein tree was calculated using the maximum likelihood method with the Jones–Taylor–Thornton model. We additionally compared protein sequences with the NCBI database using BLASTx (Table 2). A GH68 protein, associated with Limosilactobacillus reuteri, was detected with high similarity of the amino acid sequence to the characterized inulosucrase of L. reuteri 121 (AAN05575.1) (Table 2). A second GH68 enzyme was identified in a Lactobacillus sp. and clustered closely with the inulosucrase of Lactobacillus crispatus L49 (RXF55115.1) (Figure 3A). GH68 enzymes from Ligilactobacillus sp. and Psychrobacillus sp. clustered distantly from previously characterized proteins in the protein tree (Figure 3A) and had lower sequence similarity to protein homologs in NCBI (Table 2), suggesting that they may represent enzymes with novel specificities and application potential.

So far, GH70 enzymes have been identified exclusively in bacteria, with most characterized representatives originating from the taxonomically broad group of lactic acid bacteria (LAB). LAB encoding GH70 enzymes are commonly associated with animals or food‐related environments [31]. GH70 enzymes are currently divided into four subgroups: GtfA enzymes catalyze the synthesis of large α‐glucans from sucrose. A subset of GtfA enzymes, the branching sucrases (Brs), does not extend polymer chains but introduces branch points into existing polysaccharides. The GtfB, GtfC, and GtfD groups instead utilize maltooligosaccharides or amylose as donor substrates and catalyze the transfer of individual glucose units or larger glucan fragments, thereby modifying α−1,4 linkages into, for example, α−1,6 linkages [1, 25]. In this study, only a single GH70‐encoding MAG was identified (Table 2). The corresponding protein was assigned to Weissella cibaria, with 99% amino acid sequence identity to the GH70 enzyme of W. cibaria LBAE‐K39 and close proximity to other W. cibaria enzymes in the phylogenetic tree (Figure 3B). The metagenome derived GH70 is therefore most likely a dextransucrase that predominantly synthesizes α−1,6‐linked glucans from sucrose [32].

Microbial Lifestyle Relates to Recovery of MAGs Encoding GH68 and GH70

LAB‐producing glycansucrases have been associated with biofilm formation on nonglandular stratified squamous epithelium in the upper gastrointestinal tract of rodents, equines, and porcines, as well as in the crop of birds and in the terminal esophagus of macaques [33, 34]. These anatomical sites typically provide abundant substrates such as sucrose, raffinose, and amylose through the host diet. We used sylph [35] to profile for the presence of MAGs with GH68 and GH70 in fecal metagenomes. Abundance was generally low (<0.2%) but in agreement with LAB biofilm lifestyle in certain animals, three and two MAGs with potential for GH68 or GH70, respectively, were discovered in metagenomes of the Burchell‘s zebra (D4) and the Przewalski‘s horse (D46) (Figure 4). The GH68 of Lactobacillus and a L. reuteri were recovered from a Barbary macaque (D29) or a Red‐shouldered macaw (D9, respectively (Figure 4). The GH70‐encoding W. cibaria was mainly identified in the banded mongoose (D11) (Figure 3), a carnivorous species that does not directly consume plant‐derived carbohydrates. Because of its insect prey (e.g., millipedes, beetles, ants) [36] feeds on plant material, trace amounts of sucrose or related sugars may enter the mongoose digestive tract, enabling W. cibaria to form biofilms. Moreover, plant and soil material itself often carries microorganisms encoding GH68 enzymes [30], which might contribute to the recovery of relevant MAGs in feces. Indeed, the GH68 of Psychrobacillus sp. was recovered in 48% of the samples across gut physiologies and diets. While this taxon has been reported for a carnivore (red fox) before, it is also linked to different environmental habitats [37]. Altogether, this shows that knowledge about microbial lifestyles can aid in finding rare enzymes.

Abundance of of GH68 and GH709 enzymes in fecal metagenomes of captive wild animals. The abundance of MAGs that possessed GH68 and GH70 enzymes in fecal metagenomes was determined using sylph [35].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the fecal microbiomes of captive wild animals represent a rich and underexplored reservoir of CAZymes with biotechnological potential. By combining metagenomic mining with functional activity assays, we uncovered a highly diverse CAZyome, which is dominated by GH involved in the degradation of dietary and host‐derived carbohydrates. While the overall abundance of major CAZyme groups was largely independent of diet, gut physiology significantly shaped the distribution of specific enzyme classes, in particular AA, CBM, and GT families, underscoring the influence of host anatomy on microbial functional capacity. Functional assays confirmed broad hydrolytic activities across animals, validating predictions from metagenomic data and revealing widespread capability to degrade structurally diverse polysaccharides. Notably, we identified five candidate GH68 and GH70 glycansucrases, which are enzymes with considerable value for the synthesis of oligo‐ and polysaccharides. Two glycansucrases showed limited similarity to characterized homologs and may represent novel enzyme specificities. Altogether, our findings highlight captive wild animal fecal microbiomes as a promising source of CAZymes, including GH families with unusual catalytic mechanisms and rare glycansucrases with potential industrial relevance. This work underscores that microbial lifestyle information can aid in predicting suitable host animals to find specific enzymes, expand the current enzyme repertoire and support further targeted bioprospecting efforts aimed at discovering new functionalities for food, feed, and industrial biotechnology applications.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

Chemical and reagents were obtained from Merck unless indicated otherwise.

Sample Collection

Fecal matter was collected by zookeepers at Givskud and Copenhagen Zoo, Denmark, as part of a previous study [8]. No contact with animals occurred during sample collection. In this study, we focused on samples from 25 animal species, whose fecal microbiota were analyzed by metagenomic sequencing [8].

Metagenome Processing and CAZy Annotation

Metagenomic reads from 25 samples collected in [8] were quality controlled, and the MAGs were assembled as described in [8]. The MAGs were annotated using Prokka [38], and the outputs from Prokka were further processed using run_dbcan (ver. 2.0.11), which is the standalone version of the dbCAN3 annotation tool for CAZymes. The use of dbCAN3 and subsequent abundance calculation was done in accordance with dbCAN's reference (https://dbcan.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_guide/run_from_raw_reads.html). We followed the document starting from the Prokka and dbCAN3 annotation. In order to estimate the CAZyme abundances, the reads were mapped to the Prokka outputs and to the scaffolds from metaspades assemblies that were generated previously [8]. The sam files were sorted, and the read count was calculated using bedtools (ver. 2.31.1) as recommended by dbCAN3. Finally, the script dbcan_utils was used to calculate the abundances in transcripts per million. Additional details are provided in the supplementary material.

Protein Tree with GH68 and GH70 Enzymes

Amino acid sequences of characterized GH68 and GH70 enzymes were retrieved from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using accession numbers provided by the Carbohydrate‐Active enZYmes Database (CAZy) (www.CAZy.org). Additional sequences were added through an extensive literature search (October 2025) obtaining sequences of enzymes that were characterized more recently. Based on these sequences, a phylogenetic tree for GH68 or GH70 family enzymes was calculated, respectively, using MEGA ver. 12.0.15 and the maximum likelihood method with the Jones–Taylor–Thornton model [39]. The trees were visualized using iTOL v.7.2.2 [40]. Additional information on the isolation sources of the source microorganisms was obtained in batch using the rentrez package for R (v. 4.5.1) [41] along with the corresponding NCBI protein accession numbers.

Preparation of Fecal Crude Cell Extracts

Fecal material of 24/25 samples with available metagenome sequences [8] were thawed after storage at −80°C. No more fecal material was available from the reindeer. Approximately 250 mg fecal matter was resuspended in 1 mL phosphate buffer pH. Zirconia silica beads (approx. 200 μL) were added, and the sample was processed in a bead beater for 60 s at 40 m/s. Samples were centrifuged twice (10.000 rpm, 10 min), and supernatants were collected for further activity assays.

CAZy Activity Testing

Hydrolysis activity of the fecal material crude cell extract was tested using agar plate assays and high‐throughput assays in 96‐well microtiter plates with nitrophenyl (NP)‐glycosides as substrates. To test degradation activity on agar plates, 1% agar and 0.1% target compound were resuspended in 50 mM PB and heated to boiling. We included cellulose, xyloglucan, xylan from corncob, starch and resistant starch, inulin from chicory, arabinogalactan from larch wood, and pectin from citrus peel and apple. Plates were dried, and 5 µL supernatant crude extract was spotted. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Congo red solution (0.1%) was poured on the plate, the plates were incubated under slight agitation for 30 min, and Congo red solution was replaced with 1 M NaCl and incubated another 30 min. Degradation activity was identified as a halo around the spotted crude enzyme extract (Supporting Information Figure S1), and the diameter was measured. The minimum detection limit was 0.5 cm, and crude extracts without visible halo or with an average diameter < 0.5 cm were considered “no activity” indicated by “0′. Activity of most fecal crude extracts against the provided polysaccharides was tested at least two to four times with some extracts tested only once as indicated Table 1. To test for hydrolysis activity of NP‐glycosides, 5 µL crude extract was mixed with 95 µL NP‐glycoside resuspended in PB (2 mM) and incubated in a Clariostar plate reader at 37°C for 60 min. Hydrolysis activity was determined through NP release at 405 nm, and activity was calculated as µmol NP released/g feces/90 min. The tested NP‐glycosides were p‐Nitrophenyl β‐D‐glucopyranoside (NP‐β‐gluc), o‐Nitrophenyl β‐D‐galactopyranoside (oNPG), p‐Nitrophenyl β‐D‐galactopyranoside (pNPG), p‐nitrophenyl β‐D‐xylopyranoside (NP‐β‐xyl), p‐Nitrophenyl α‐D‐glucopyranoside (NP‐α‐gluc), p‐Nitrophenyl α ‐L‐fucoside (NP‐α‐fuc), and p‐Nitrophenyl α‐D‐mannopyranoside (NP‐α‐man). Each crude extract was tested two to three times.

Statistical Analysis

Abundance of CAZy groups was statistically analyzed using Sigma Plot (v. 15.0). Every dataset was tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on normality, differences in abundance were determined using Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's Method or one‐way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak method and Mann–Whitney Rank Sum test or t‐test. A p‐value p < 0.05 was considered significant.
