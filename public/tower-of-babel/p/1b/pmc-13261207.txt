Cardioprotective therapy in type 2 diabetes guided by a proteomic risk model: A randomized trial
Authors: Rosalynn Gill, Meredith A Carpenter, Amy Holstein, Ariadne Reyes Garcia, Ayman Alkholder, Aanchal Gupta, Vatsala Singh, Arshed A Quyyumi, Neda Rasouli, Ian J Neeland, Stephen A Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardioprotective (CP) therapies such as GLP-1 RA, SGLT2i, and PCSK9i medications remain underutilized in cardiovascular (CV) risk management. Individualized risk assessment using blood-based proteomic testing, such as Residual Cardiovascular Risk (RCVR) scores, may help guide treatment decisions.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether access to RCVR scores improves risk-concordant prescribing of CP medications in clinical practice.MethodsIn this multicenter, open label, behavioral randomized trial, adults with type 2 diabetes eligible for CP medications underwent SomaScan™ proteomic testing. Participants were randomized to an informed (RCVR results shared with patient and clinicians) or uninformed arm (results withheld until study end). CP prescribing rate and sensitivity-to-change of RCVR to CP initiation were assessed by trial arm. Chi-square trend in proportions, one-sided 2-sample tests for equality of proportions, and one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for statistical analysis.ResultsAmong 377 baseline participants analyzed (59 % informed; mean age 66 ± 10.3 years; 45 % female), CP prescribing was significantly higher in the informed versus uninformed arm (32.1 % vs 9.6 %; OR 5.45; 95 % CI: 2.31–12.89; p < 0.001); per-risk-bin rates were also significantly higher in all elevated risk bins (p<0.05). Among 173 participants with paired RCVR scores from baseline-6-months, CP initiation (n = 24) in the informed arm (n = 92) corresponded to a 3.9 % reduction in proteomic-predicted CV risk (p = 0.036).ConclusionsDisclosure of proteomic RCVR scores was associated with higher rates of CP prescribing and short-term reductions in proteomic risk scores among CP medication users; proteomics-based risk assessment may support personalized CV prevention.Keywords: Cardiovascular risk stratification, Residual cardiovascular risk, SomaScan, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Cardioprotective therapy, Risk-aligned prescribing, Proteomics

Introduction

Almost 38.4 million people in the US (11.6 %) and nearly 600 million globally have type 2 diabetes (T2D) [1,2]. In patients with T2D, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a leading cause of complications and death, with an estimated $37.3 billion spent in the US alone to treat cardiovascular (CV) related complications annually [3,4]. Approximately one third of patients with diabetes will die of CVD despite the development of novel lipid lowering [5,6], anti-inflammatory [7,8], antithrombotic [9], dual antiplatelet [10], and novel glucose lowering medications [11,12].

In healthcare settings, the utilization of novel glucose lowering therapies with cardioprotective (CP) benefits, specifically Sodium-Glucose Co-Transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and Glucagon-like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, remains low despite proven efficacy [13,14], professional society guideline endorsement [15], and regulatory labels for CV benefit. This low utilization has led to a “call for action to the cardiology community”, raising utilization from less than 10 % of eligible patients in 2021 [13] to approximately 23 % of eligible patients in 2023 [16]. Patients receiving CP medications tended to be younger, with lower hospitalization rates, while over 75 % of eligible patients were still not receiving these medications [16].

Many factors have contributed to underutilization of CP medications, including lack of physician awareness, variability in insurance coverage, out-of-pocket costs [13], and variable treatment responses [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Prescribing CP medications to all eligible patients regardless of CV risk could strain the health systems and expose lower-risk patients to unnecessary costs and adverse effects without clear benefit.

Health care providers (HCP) underutilization of these therapies may also be limited by effective metrics for tracking cardiovascular response to therapy. Traditional CV risk calculators, based on risk factors including age, sex, race, diabetes status and hypertension history, as well as coronary imaging measures may not be capturing the full cardiovascular benefit of agents like SGLT2is and GLP-1 RAs.

Novel proteomic tools may help bridge the gap in cardiovascular therapy monitoring. The Residual Cardiovascular Risk (RCVR) – 4-year SomaSignal™ Test is a 27-protein model developed to predict the risk of a composite cardiovascular event (myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for heart failure, or all cause death) within 4 years from blood sample collection in individuals ≥40 years with one or more known factors of elevated cardiovascular risk [21]. The test reports absolute cardiovascular risk score and relative risk category [21], which can be used in managing CV care. The RCVR test has been evaluated in over 45,000 patient samples, with an AUC of 0.73(0.72-0.74) [21] and is currently available as a Laboratory Developed Test (LDT) through the reference laboratory in Boulder, CO on a limited basis for patients to order through HCPs at private practices. Test Information Guide included in Supplemental Files.

The primary aim of this randomized controlled study was to determine whether the addition of the RCVR score to existing clinical measurements leads to risk concordant prescribing, where physicians prescribe CP medications more frequently to patients with higher RCVR scores. RCVR testing was repeated in a subset of patients at a single follow-up visit occurring within a 6–12-month window post-baseline to detect changes in the RCVR score. Changes in RCVR predictions were compared with changes in ASCVD scores to evaluate the sensitivity of each measure to change. An overview of the trial design, endpoints, and principal findings is shown in the Central Illustration (Fig. 1).

Central Illustration: Proteomic risk disclosure aligns therapy with risk and detects change: Cardioprotective (CP) therapies remain underused in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D). In a multi-center, randomized trial, adults with T2D were randomized to RCVR informed (results disclosed to participants and HCPs after baseline clinical visit) vs. uninformed (RCVR results withheld until end of study) arms. CP prescribing was higher with disclosure (32.1 % vs 9.6 %; OR 5.45, 95 % CI 2.31–12.89; p < 0.001), consistent with risk-aligned treatment. Among informed participants who initiated CP therapy, RCVR decreased 3.9 % from baseline to follow-up (6-12 months) (p = 0.036). Findings support proteomic-guided, risk-aligned prescribing and early risk reduction.

Methods

Study participants and randomization

Three academic clinical centers - Emory University School of Medicine (Emory), University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center (UHCMC), and University of Colorado Anschutz (CU Anschutz) - recruited participants (≥40 years old, diagnosed with T2D, and eligible for but not previously prescribed qualifying CP medications (SGLT2i, GLP-1 RA, PCSK9i)), along with their HCPs into the trial. Participant exclusion criteria included use of qualifying CP medications within 3 months of enrollment, clinically significant malignancy (active, treated, untreated, in remission for <5 years), Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), end-stage renal disease (ESRD), self-reported pregnancy, and inability to read English.

All study procedures were conducted in accordance with applicable institutional guidelines, Institutional Review Board (IRB) requirements, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The three randomized trials protocols were approved by the Emory University IRB (STUDY00003362), University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center IRB (SPARTA IRB# STUDY20211340), and the Colorado Multiple IRB (COMIRB# 21-4510), University of Colorado Anschutz, and were registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05256706, NCT05182359, NCT03599531). Study data were de-identified prior to analysis to protect participant privacy. Minor differences between site protocols, including randomization and outcome statements, reflect trial preferences of institutional PIs; these variances did not limit the ability to compile and analyze results across centers. Site protocols are included in Supplemental Files.

Due to early study termination by the sponsor, sites were not able to complete 12-month follow-up or obtain full intended enrollment. However, the data collection prior to termination enabled sufficient statistical power to warrant proceeding with analysis. Participants were followed for up to 12 months at Emory University and CU Anschutz and for 20 weeks at UHCMC. Follow-up data was collected for 186 participants, with an additional blood sample and RCVR score being obtained from 173 participants (Emory = 101, CU Anschutz = 72). No follow-up sampling was completed at UHCMC.

Prior to the randomization, all providers received training on interpretation of RCVR test results. Randomization occurred to ensure equal distribution of informed and uninformed HCP-participant pairs and was adapted to site workflow while preserving core study structure. Participants and HCPs randomized to the informed arm received the RCVR results following the baseline visit (results reported to HCPs and shared with patients), while participants and HCPs randomized to the uninformed arm both remained blinded to the results until the end of the study (EOS). At CU Anschutz, randomization was performed at the provider level with 1:1 informed to uninformed providers, and patients assigned according to their provider’s allocation. At Emory, randomization was 1:1, but was conducted at the patient level, with providers assigned according to their patient’s allocation. At UHCMC, randomization was conducted at the patient level, with providers assigned according to their patients, but the allocation rate was 2 informed to 1 uninformed patient to expedite trial recruitment at the site.

Prescribing decisions for the use of CP medications (SGLT2i, GLP-1 RA, or PCSK9i) were assessed after the baseline visit in both informed and uninformed arms. The HCP’s decision to prescribe or not prescribe CP medication, along with whether the patient’s prescription change was implemented, was documented in the patient medical record and reviewed by study personnel. The trial designs for all three sites are summarized in the CONSORT diagram (Fig. 2).

Residual cardiovascular risk (RCVR) SomaSignal™ Test

The Residual Cardiovascular Risk (RCVR) – 4 years test predicts the likelihood of a CV event (defined as a myocardial infarction [MI], stroke, transient ischemic attack, hospitalization for heart failure [HF] or all-cause death) within 4 years in individuals ≥40 years with one or more known causes of elevated CV risk, including individuals with: stable CVD (history of MI or stroke [>6 months prior], hospitalization for HF, revascularization, T2D, chronic kidney disease, or over 65 years old without known elevated CV risk. The test was developed on the SomaScan™ 5K (∼5000 protein) multiplexed assay using 32,130 plasma samples from 22,849 participants across multiple clinical studies (including HUNT3, ARIC, BASEL, EXSCEL, CRIC) and independently validated in 11,609 participants including 6,514 participants with T2D aged 25-95 [21]. The 27-protein model had an AUC of 0.73(0.72-0.74), c-statistic of 0.71(0.69-0.72), and net reclassification index of +0.43.

The RCVR test output is expressed as an absolute predicted risk percentage (0–100 %) and relative risk category (low risk: 0-7.5 %, medium-low risk: 7.6-25 %, medium-high risk: 26 %-50 %, and high risk: 51 %-100 % risk of a cardiovascular event). The relative risk categories were determined based on maximal stratification of results on a Kaplan-Meier curve during the training and development of the original RCVR test model. Event rates from test development were 5.6, 11.2, 20.0, and 43.4 % within 4 years in the low, medium-low, medium-high, and high risk categories, respectively, with a median time to event of 1.71 years [21].

Participant EDTA plasma samples were collected, frozen at -80 degrees Celsius, and shipped weekly on dry ice to SomaLogic’s CLIA licensed/CAP-accredited laboratory for processing (Boulder, CO). Samples were run on SomaScan™ assay version 4.1 (7K), and RCVR risk scores and relative risk categories were generated. Test results were delivered electronically back to study sites and disclosed according to study randomization. This procedure was implemented both for the baseline and follow-up samples (where applicable).

Study endpoints

The primary stated measure in this study was the concordance of CP prescribing rates to randomization arm and RCVR score. Qualifying CP medications included SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, ertugliflozin), GLP-1 RAs (liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide), and PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab, alirocumab). This aim captured the number of participants who had physician prescribed changes in CP medication within 6-12 months for CU Anschutz and Emory, and at 20 weeks for UHCMC (consistent with the single-visit design). All physician prescribing decisions were recorded via HCP questionnaires and adjudicated by staff through documentation review.

The secondary outcome was assessing the change in RCVR and ASCVD scores from baseline to follow-up. These risk score changes were assessed alongside the initiation, or non-initiation, of qualifying CP medication between the baseline and 6-12 month-follow-up visit, to assess the sensitivity of RCVR and ASCVD to any measurable CV risk changes associated with the initiation of the CP medications. CP medication initiation was defined as a secondary variable distinct from CP prescribing rate, as not all participants who were prescribed CP medications initiated treatment in follow-up period. Medication initiation was evaluated through participant medication administration record (MAR) and adjudicated by staff via chart review during study participation.

Statistical methods

Risk concordant prescribing

To assess differences in CP medication prescribing rates relative to RCVR scores and relative risk bins, the rate of CP prescribing (#PatientsReceivingNewCPMedRxTotal#PatientsinStudyArm)*100, standard error of proportion √(RateofCPRx*1−RateCPRxTotal#PatientsinStudyArm), and 95 % CI for proportions (RateofCPRx±1.96*SE) were calculated.

The rate of prescribing CP medications was calculated by RCVR relative risk category (low, medium-low, medium-high, high) in both the informed and uninformed groups, and in all participants combined. A trend analysis was performed using the chi-squared test for trend in proportions (significance at p < 0.05). One-sided 2-sample tests for equality of proportions with continuity correction (proportionality test) were used to assess whether prescribing probability rates in the informed arm were significantly greater (p < 0.05) than the uninformed arm in each relative risk bin, as well as cumulatively. A generalized linear mixed-effects model with Poisson distribution and log link of CP medication prescription (Y/N) with a fixed effect of treatment arm and random effect of study site was additionally included to evaluate any bias in prescribing rates due to site-specific allocations.

Longitudinal sensitivity to change

To assess whether the initiation of CP medications led to improvement in the RCVR test results within 6-12 months of initiation, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared RCVR predictions between paired baseline and follow-up samples. The alternative hypothesis tested that the mean difference in RCVR prediction within group (treatment arm and CP medication status) from baseline to follow-up was less than zero (decrease in score).

Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) Risk scores (2013) were calculated according to the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Pooled Cohort Equations [22] for 121 participants with blood samples at baseline and follow-up, and required clinical metadata (sex, ethnicity, age, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, hypertension, and tobacco use). To assess whether the initiation of CP medications had a significant effect on ASCVD risk scores within 6-12 months of medication initiation, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared ASCVD scores at baseline and follow-up. The alternative hypothesis tested that the mean difference in ASCVD score within group (treatment arm and CP medication status) from baseline to follow-up was greater than zero (increase in score).

The difference in directionality of the hypothesis testing for RCVR (decrease) and ASCVD (increase) is due to the prediction that the RCVR model has increased sensitivity to proteomic changes secondary to the initiation of CP medications, while the ASCVD model is less sensitive to these changes and more driven by age and clinical factors that tend to increase over time.

Software and reproducibility

Analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.2) using a reproducible script-based workflow. Statistical analyses were performed with base R stats (prop.trend.test, prop.test, wilcox.test) and glmmTMB::glmmTMB for sensitivity analysis.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 9,730 patients screened, 395 participants were accepted and randomized for the study initiation (baseline). A total of 233 participants were randomized into the informed arm (CU Anschutz = 58, Emory = 72, UHCMC = 103), and 162 into the uninformed arm (CU Anschutz = 40, Emory = 71, UHCMC = 51). Participants missing RCVR score (n = 12 [7 informed, 5 uninformed]), and/or CP prescribing information (n = 6 [5 informed, 1 uninformed]) were excluded from baseline analyses (n remaining = 221 informed, 156 uninformed). Follow-up data was collected for 97 informed (CU Anschutz = 44, Emory = 53, UHCMC = 0) and 89 uninformed (CU Anschutz = 38, Emory = 51, UHCMC = 0) participants. UHCMC was designed as a cross-sectional study with only baseline data collection. Longitudinal analysis was limited to participants with RCVR scores at both baseline and follow-up and CP medication initiation information following the baseline visit, leaving 173 participants (CU Anschutz = 41 informed, 31 uninformed; Emory = 51 informed, 50 uninformed) for this aim. Missingness of participant data at follow-up was primarily due to early study termination and missed visits.

Demographic characteristics of participants in the informed and uninformed arms were well balanced at baseline (Table 1); no significant differences were identified in RCVR score (mean RCVR score: 34.4 % (31.0-37.7 %) in the informed arm, and 32.6 % (28.9-36.3 %) in the uninformed arm, p=0.49), or any other demographic feature pertinent to ASCVD calculations included in Table 1 (all p>0.05).

Cardioprotective medication prescribing rates

Looking across all subjects and sites, CP prescribing rates were significantly higher in the informed arm compared with the uninformed arm (32.1 % vs 9.6 %, respectively, χ2equality of proportions p < 0.001). This finding remained robust when accounting for site-level heterogeneity using a Poisson mixed-effects model (SD = 0.539, p < 0.001). In this model, participants in the informed arm had an estimated 3.8-fold higher rate of CP medication prescription than those in the uninformed arm (p < 0.001). Risk-concordant prescribing of CP medications was observed only in the informed group where more prescriptions were provided to those in higher risk bins. Thus, participants classified as high-risk by their RCVR score were prescribed CP medications more frequently in the informed (38.6 %) compared to the uninformed arm (12.1 %, p = 0.0076). Similarly, 35.7 % of informed medium-high risk participants were prescribed CP medications, compared to 6.4 % of uninformed (p < 0.001), and 27.1 % of informed medium-low risk were prescribed CP medications, compared to 11.3 % of uninformed (p = 0.015). The difference in CP prescribing rates in the low-risk bins (informed = 25.5 %, uninformed = 7.1 %, p = 0.24) did not reach significance. CP medication prescribing rates along with number of participants per rate calculation are shown in Table 2. A positive trend in CP medication prescription across RCVR relative risk bin was observed in the informed arm from the low-risk to high-risk bins (p = 0.105); this trend was not observed in the uninformed arm (p = 0.886) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Risk-concordant prescribing

This study examined the real-world application of using a proteomic cardiovascular risk score (RCVR) to help providers with prescription decisions for CP medications. The results suggest that informing HCPs of RCVR scores can aid in risk-concordant prescribing of these medications. This was observed as significantly higher prescribing rates in the high-risk, medium-high risk, and medium-low risk bins of the informed arm, compared to the uninformed arm. Additionally, though not significant, there was a notable upward trend in the proportion of prescribing rates across risk bins in the informed arm, suggesting participants at higher residual proteomic CV risk, indicated by higher RCVR scores, were being prescribed CP medications more frequently than those with lower risk.

These results are similar to those previously reported in a virtual trial, in which 241 HCPs (161 primary care physicians, 80 cardiologists) used QURE Healthcare’s Clinical Performance and Value (CPV®) vignettes (synthetic patient case) to survey treatment decisions that might be made when presenting a selection of patient cases to the HCP. The study reported physicians using standard clinical measures to risk stratify a vignette of 9 patient cases. Each patient case was designed to describe an individual with T2D along with a collection of other risk factors seen in a typical multi-morbid patient. The first phase of the study showed that with various traditional risk factors supplied, only 38.9 % of physicians were able to correctly stratify risk in the study using the standard clinical tools, including the AHA/ACC ASCVD risk calculator [23]. HCPs were then trained on the RCVR test and surveyed online about how they might treat the patient vignettes with variable RCVR and ASCVD risk scores provided. This study showed both improved risk stratification (which was not part of the current study design) and increased rates of virtual prescribing of CP medications in patients with intermediate and high-risk scores [24].

Longitudinal testing

Follow-up testing assessed the changes in RCVR scores compared to ASCVD scores over 6-12 months after initiation of CP medications. During follow-up, mean ASCVD risk increased across all groups, whereas change in mean RCVR predictions differed by group. As hypothesized, RCVR predictions decreased significantly from baseline to follow-up among informed participants who initiated CP medication (mean difference = -3.92, p = 0.036).

These results suggest that the RCVR test may be more sensitive than the ASCVD risk score to short-term improvements in CV risk following CP medication use. Although the RCVR SomaSignal™ Test was not developed as a marker for therapeutic response, the proteomic score has been repeatedly demonstrated to be sensitive to multiple mechanistic levers of change. In a recently reported study using semaglutide, even after adjusting for weight loss and glycemic changes (associated with T2D), the circulating proteome showed modulation of lipid and inflammatory pathways, and CV-risk-related proteins; individuals with and without T2D taking semaglutide showed a reduction in RCVR risk scores [25]. More broadly, these proteomic risk predictions, shown to be directionally concordant with clinical outcomes, reflect changes across key biological pathways including those related to inflammation, metabolism, vascular biology, and cardiac stress [21,26]. While these previous studies support the mechanistic plausibility for CP therapy associated with proteomic changes, pathway analyses were not evaluated in this clinical application analysis, and further studies would be required to expound on the relationship between CP medication and proteomic CV risk.

Comparatively, ASCVD scores are intended for long-term risk estimation. The scores are calculated from broader risk factors, some of which are fixed (biological sex, ethnicity), categorical (hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use), or reflective of general systemic characteristics (age, cholesterol, HDL, LDL). These model factors are not expected to change over the short-term, thus small increases in ASCVD could be reflective of the inclusion of age in the predictive model [22].

Implications

Providing clinicians with individualized proteomic residual cardiovascular risk (RCVR) scores was associated with higher rates of CP prescribing in adults with type 2 diabetes. This protein-based risk assessment complements traditional clinical factors, enabling more precise cardiovascular monitoring and helping close gaps in use of guideline-recommended SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and PCSK9 inhibitors.

Integrating validated proteomic risk tools into clinical workflows could enhance care efficiency and value by targeting therapies to those patients most likely to benefit. While this study demonstrates an association between proteomic risk assessment and guideline-concordant prescribing, larger pragmatic trials and health economic analyses are warranted to determine whether this translates into reduced cardiovascular events and improved overall care value in real-world settings. Key implementation challenges include availability of the tests, clinician awareness and training, seamless integration with electronic health records, and payer coverage policies that support proteomic, risk-based cardiovascular prevention.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this study include variations between site protocol, as well as shortened follow-up time and data limitations due to early termination of the trial. Study differences in randomization are captured in the CONSORT flowchart (Fig. 2), as well as the description of participant characteristics. Baseline participant demographics were assessed across all sites and found to be balanced (not significantly different) between the informed and uninformed arms, suggesting that randomization differences did not strongly impact the study cohort.

With respect to CP medication initiation, information on medication adherence, including dates of exposure, was limited to medical record review and self-report.

The early trial termination impacted the number of participants, length of follow-up (6-12 months), and amount of data collected on participants. Specifically, as noted in the conclusions, the short trial length precluded the collection of CV outcome data, limiting the ability to draw conclusions on the relative directionality of change in the RCVR and ASCVD outcomes. Additionally, the ASCVD sensitivity-to-change cohort was a subset of the RCVR sensitivity-to-change cohort, as not all clinical metadata required for ASCVD was available for the larger cohort. Increasing the number of participants, time of follow-up, and completeness and breadth of participant health data (including the RCVR scores, ASCVD metadata, and primary outcome data) would improve the power to detect and draw generalizable conclusions from the trial.

Because providers and patients in the informed arm were aware of test results and study objectives, the Hawthorne Effect may have influenced prescribing behavior, lifestyle changes, adherence, or other nonpharmacological factors contributing to observed RCVR changes. Although this represents a study limitation, such awareness-driven behavior changes reflect real-world clinical practice and may be one mechanism by which proteomic testing influences clinical decision-making and patient behavior.

As disclosed, several authors, employed by the study sponsor, were involved in study design, data analysis and interpretation, and manuscript preparation. Though not involved in conducting the trial, collecting, or adjudicating results, the sponsor relationship may have introduced bias into the study and independent validation of results is warranted.

Conclusion

In this multicenter randomized study, disclosure of proteomic RCVR scores was associated with higher rates of cardioprotective therapy initiation and modest short-term reductions in proteomic risk estimates among CP medication users. Longer-term studies are needed to determine whether these changes translate into improved clinical outcomes, thus supporting proteomic RCVR scoring as a method for identifying patients who are most likely to benefit from CP medication use.

Glossary

Any drug or therapeutic agent that reduces the risk of cardiac injury, preserves myocardial function, or prevents progression of cardiovascular disease. Common examples include antiplatelet agents, lipid-lowering agents, beta-blockers, and, as defined in this analysis, Sodium-Glucose Co-Transporter 2 Inhibitors (SGLT2i), Glucagon-like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RA), and Protein Convertase Subtilisin/Kexin Type 9 Inhibitors (PCSK9i).

A group of disorders affecting the heart and blood vessels, including coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, heart failure, and other conditions resulting from atherosclerosis, structural, or functional abnormalities of the cardiovascular system.

An individual licensed or credentialed to deliver medical, diagnostic, therapeutic, or preventative services, including physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and other qualified clinicians involved in patient care.

A proteomic assay based on the SomaScan™ platform that quantifies circulating proteins using modified DNA aptamers (SOMAmers) to generate a Residual Cardiovascular Risk (RCVR) score. The test integrates multi-protein signatures to estimate an individual’s remaining cardiovascular risk beyond traditional clinical factors, providing a molecular assessment of cardiometabolic disease activity and risk stratification. The test predicts the likelihood of a cardiovascular (CV) event (defined as a myocardial infarction [MI], stroke, transient ischemic attack, hospitalization for heart failure [HF] or all-cause death) within 4 years in individuals ≥40 years with one or more known causes of elevated CV risk, including individuals with: stable CVD (history of MI or stroke [>6 months prior], HF, peripheral artery disease, revascularization, abnormal stress test or imaging suggesting coronary heart disease), T2D, chronic kidney disease, a history of cancer, symptoms consistent with chronic coronary syndromes, or over 65 years old without known elevated CV risk.

A chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and progressive impairment of insulin secretion, leading to elevated blood glucose levels and associated cardiometabolic complications. Typical laboratory criteria for diagnosis of T2D include Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5 %, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose on oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) ≥ 200 mg/dL, and random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia (polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia) or hyperglycemic crisis.
