Associations of altruistic factors and perceptions with intention to receive Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) vaccination among older adults in China
Authors: Lizhen Liu, He Cao, Ziqing Ou, Weijun Peng, Hongbiao Chen, Yuan Fang, Siyu Chen, Shaojian Xu, Zixin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRespiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) vaccination may become available for older adults in China soon. This study was to investigate behavioral intention to receive self-financed and fully subsidized RSV vaccination among older adults in China. Associations of altruistic factors and perceptions with behavioral intention were also explored. This is a cross-sectional survey conducted in Shenzhen, China between August and October 2024. The sampling frame was Shenzhen residents aged ≥60 y with administrative health records in community health centers (0.94 million in 2021). Multistage random sampling was used. Ten community health centers were first randomly selected. Each selected community center then randomly selected 200 administrative health records of residents aged ≥60 y. A total of 1556 participants completed face-to-face interviews at community health centers. Among the participants, 15.4% and 60.1% intended to receive self-financed and fully subsidized RSV vaccination in the next year. After adjusting for significant background characteristics, general altruism was associated with higher odds of intending to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination, but not a self-financed vaccine. Family-oriented altruism (belief that receiving vaccination could protect family members), perceived more benefits, barriers, cue to action, and self-efficacy related to RSV vaccination were associated with higher odds of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under both cost scenarios. In addition, perceived susceptibility and severity of RSV were associated with higher intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination. The findings had implications for RSV vaccination service planning and health promotion among older adults before and after free vaccines are available in China.KEYWORDS: Respiratory Syncytial Virus vaccination, behavioral intention, altruistic factors, perceptions, older adults, China

Introduction

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) infection causes respiratory tract diseases such as infection of the airway, lungs and middle ear.1 Mild cold-like symptoms are typical among adults with RSV infection.1 However, adults aged 60 y or above are at increased risk of having severe consequences following RSV infection, such as bronchiolitis, pneumonia and even respiratory failure.2 Older adults with chronic medical conditions are more vulnerable to RSV-associated hospitalization and mortality.2 In the United States, it is estimated that there are 60,000–160,000 hospitalizations and 6,000–10,000 deaths annually caused by RSV infection among adults aged 65 y or above.3 In mainland China, RSV infection accounted for 16.8% of medically attended acute respiratory infections.4 In northern part of China, the peak of RSV infection occurs during winter and spring, whereas in southern China, RSV infection occurs throughout the year with the peak in spring and summer.5

Two RSV vaccines, the AREXVY (GSK, Durham, NC, USA) and the ABRYSYOTM (Pfizer Inc., New York, NY, USA), were both effective in preventing RSV-related emergency department visits and hospitalizations in adults aged 60 or above without safety concerns.3 The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved RSV vaccination for individuals aged 60 y or above in May 2023.6 The approval was expanded to cover adults aged 18–59 y at increased risk for disease in October 2024.7 The Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends all adults aged 75 y or above and adults aged 60–74 y who are at higher risk of severe RSV diseases receive one dose of RSV.2 RSV vaccines are approved for the use in older adults aged 60 y or above in Macau and Hong Kong in 2024,8 but are not yet available in mainland China. Several clinical trials on RSV prevention products, including vaccines, are ongoing in mainland China.9 It is expected that the RSV vaccines would become available in the country in 2026.

Most studies investigating uptake or acceptance of RSV vaccination were conducted among pregnant women and caregivers of infants, whereas relatively few studies investigated these topics among older adults. Three studies reported RSV vaccination uptake rate among adults aged 60 y or above in the United States.10–12 The uptake rate ranged from 7.6% to 26.7% in 2023–2024.10–12 Older age was associated with a higher uptake rate of RSV vaccination.10–12 Older adults who were Asian, non-Hispanic Black or Native American and had higher respiratory risk reported lower uptake of RSV vaccination.10–12 In our literature review, 47% and 51.1% of adults aged 60 y or above in the United States and Arab countries intended to receive an RSV vaccination.13,14 Previous vaccination history (e.g., seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination), feeling at risk of RSV, belief in safety and efficacy of RSV vaccination, and advice from doctors were facilitators of behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination.13–15 These determinants were considered by our study. To our knowledge, only one study investigated behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination among adults in mainland China.9 However, this study included small number of older adults (<5% of participants were 50 y or above) and did not mention any cost scenario when measuring the behavioral intention.9 It is expected that the RSV vaccination will be provided at market rate without any subsidization in the early phase of rollout in mainland China, which is similar to the situation of seasonal influenza vaccination for older adults.16 Therefore, understanding behavioral intention to receive RSV vaccines under different cost scenarios and its determinants are important to inform service planning and health promotion in mainland China.

A behavior is described as altruistic when it is motivated by a desire to benefit someone other than oneself for that person’s sake.17 Triggering altruism and altruistic behaviors may be useful approaches to reduce vaccine hesitancy.18 Achieving herd immunity through vaccination could indirectly protect people who have not yet received or could not receive a vaccine.19 Relatively few studies examined the association between altruistic factors and vaccination uptake. One survey showed that altruism could influence the decision to receive seasonal influenza vaccination.20 Another national survey among older adults in Italy suggested that altruism was associated with better trust of the national vaccination plan and higher uptake rate of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination.21 Furthermore, a behavioral experiment showed that providing vaccination messages targeting altruism could increase vaccination intention.18 A randomized controlled trial showed that altruism-eliciting short, animated video intervention was more effective than the control condition in increasing intention to receive a COVID-19 vaccination among younger adults.22 At the time when this study was conducted, RSV vaccination is only available for young children aged 0–12 months in mainland China. Moreover, the coverage of RSV vaccination is limited among children of this age group due to the high cost (CNY3,000 or US$413.2 per dose). Recent data showed that about 41% of older adults in China are living with their children and grandchildren, and over 30% of them played important roles in taking care of their grandchildren.23,24 It is possible that protecting family members, especially children who are more vulnerable to RSV, would motivate them to receive an RSV vaccination. However, no study has tested such a hypothesis.

To address the knowledge gap, this study investigated the level of behavioral intention to receive self-financed or fully subsidized RSV vaccination among older adults in Shenzhen, China. Determinants of behavioral intention under the two cost scenarios were also investigated. Potential determinants considered by this study included altruistic factors and perceptions related to RSV. We hypothesized that the level and determinants of behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination would be different between the two cost scenarios.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is cross-sectional survey among people aged 60 y or above in Shenzhen between August and October 2024. Shenzhen is a city and special economic zone in the southern Chinese province of Guangdong, bordering Hong Kong to the south. Shenzhen is one of the four most developed cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen) in mainland China, with a population of 17 million in 2021. At the time when this study was conducted, RSV vaccine is available for infants aged 0–12 months in mainland China with a market rate of CNY3,000 (US$413.2) per dose. However, RSV vaccines are not yet available for other age groups, including older adults.

Participants and data collection

Participants were: (1) aged 60 y or above, (2) living in Shenzhen at the time of the survey, and (3) had established administrative health records in community health centers. Since 2014, all Chinese residents who have lived in the same region for more than 6 months, regardless of age or permanent residency, should establish administrative health records in local community health centers. The health records contain basic sociodemographic information, children development indicators, information related to pregnancy and childbirth (for women), chronic disease diagnosis and management, and vaccination history. It is expected that most older adults who have lived in Shenzhen for more than 6 months have established administrative health records.

Multistage random sampling was used to recruit participants. In the first stage, names of all community health centers in Shenzhen (926 in total) were entered in an Excel spreadsheet. Using the function of “selecting random cells,” ten community health centers were randomly selected. Within each selected community health center, 200 residents were randomly selected from administrative health records of people aged 60 y or above. Staff in the selected health centers approached prospective participants through telephone at different timeslots during weekdays and weekends, briefed them about the study, and invited them to visit the community health center to complete a face-to-face interview. On site, staff of the community center obtained their written informed consent, and performed the interview in Mandarin or Cantonese, which took about 30 minutes to complete. A cash coupon of CNY25 (US$3.5) was given to each participant after completion of the interview. The current study obtained ethical approval from the Longhua District Center for Disease Control and Prevention (reference: 2024008).

Sample size planning

The target sample size was 1,500. Assuming the level of behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination in the reference group (without a facilitating condition) to be 10–50%, the sample size could detect the smallest odds ratios of 1.34 between participants with and without a facilitating condition, with a power of 0.80 and an alpha of 0.05 (PASS 11.0, NCSS, LLC). We assumed the response rate to be 75%. Therefore, we needed to invite 2,000 older adults (200 in each selected community health center) to join the study.

Measurements

Development of the questionnaire

A panel consisting of experts in public health, vaccination behaviors and health psychology, CDC workers and older adults was formed to develop the questionnaire. The questionnaire was in simplified Chinese. We tested the readability and length of the questionnaire by conducting face-to-face interviews among ten residents aged 60–84 y. All participants in the pilot study reported that the questions were easy to understand, and the duration of interview was acceptable. The panel finalized the questionnaire based on minor suggestions made by the participants. The English version of the questionnaire used in this study is shown in Appendix 1.

Background characteristics

Participants reported sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex assigned at birth, education level, relationship status, employment status, and living arrangement. In addition, information on lifestyles (smoking and binge drinking in the past year), presence of chronic conditions, history of confirmed RSV and SARS-CoV-2 infection, and history of other vaccination (e.g., seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination) were collected.

Behavioral intention to receive RSV vaccination under different scenarios

Participants were briefed about RSV vaccines: “Receiving RSV vaccination could reduce the risk of RSV infection by 80% among people aged 60 or above. In older adults with chronic diseases, RSV vaccination could reduce RSV infection by 95%. Older adults only need to receive one dose of RSV vaccination, and the duration of protection is at least two years.” They were then asked about the likelihood of receiving RSV vaccination in the next year under two cost conditions: (1) self-financed and without any subsidization at CNY3,000 or US$413.2 per dose, which was the same as the cost of RSV vaccines for infants in mainland China, and was similar to that for older adults in Hong Kong (HK$2,400–3,500 or US$307.7–448.7), and (2) fully-subsidized vaccination provided by the government (response categories: 1 = very unlikely to 5 = very likely). Many published studies used similar Likert scale to measure behavioral intention to receive a vaccination and treated intention as a binary outcome in data analysis.25–28 Participants who answered “likely” or “very likely” were defined as having a behavioral intention to receive RSV vaccination. Such definition of behavioral intention was commonly used in published studies.27–29 This study measured behavioral intention under the condition that participants knew the efficacy and cost of the vaccine, which ensured uniform information and better interpretation of results. Pre-Survey Briefings and is widely used in survey design to investigate attitudes or intentions.27,28

Altruistic factors

General altruism was measured using the validated Chinese version of the Elder Care Research Center Altruism Scale (five items, Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87). In addition, family-oriented altruism was measured by asking participants whether they agreed that receiving vaccination could protect their family members “You can protect your family members by receiving vaccination (e.g., seasonal influenza vaccine, COVID-19 vaccine),” with response categories from 1 = disagree, 2 = neutral, to 3 = agree.

Perceptions related to RSV vaccination based on the Health Belief Model (HBM)

The HBM, a commonly used behavioral change theory to explain vaccination behaviors,30–32 was used as the theoretical framework for variable selection. Four scales were constructed for this study to measure perceived susceptibility to RSV (two items, e.g., “The older you are, the higher the risk of contracting RSV”), perceived severity of RSV (two items, “Older adults will experience severe symptoms caused by RSV infection”), perceived benefits of RSV vaccination (two items, “RSV vaccination is effective in protecting you against RSV”), and perceived barriers related to RSV vaccination uptake (two items, “RSV vaccination has severe side effects”). The Cronbach’s alpha of these scales ranged from 0.70 to 0.81. Two single items measured perceived cue to action (“Doctors or your family will suggest you receive RSV vaccination”) and perceived self-efficacy (“You are confident to receive RSV vaccination if you want to”). Responses to the aforementioned measurements were 1 = disagree, 2 = neutral, and 3 = agree.

Statistical analysis

Mean and standard deviation (SD) of the scale or item scores were presented. Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was calculated using reliability tests. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was applied to evaluate the dimensional structure of the HBM items. Sampling adequacy was examined using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) statistic and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Factors were extracted through principal component analysis with varimax rotation. Items were considered acceptable if their standardized loadings were ≥0.60 and the corrected item total correlation was ≥0.30.33 Items showing substantial cross-loadings, defined as a loading difference of less than 0.10 between two factors, were considered for exclusion.33 In confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including relative Chi-square (χ2/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Non-Normed Fix Index (NNFI/TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMA). Generally, a model showed good fit to data, if the following criteria were met: CFI > 0.90,2 TLI > 0.90,34 RMSEA < 0.0635 and SRMR < 0.08.36

Behavioral intention to receive self-financed and fully subsidized RSV vaccination were dependent variables. Univariate logistic regression models were used to investigate the associations between background characteristics and the dependent variables. After adjusting for significant background characteristics, associations between independent variables of interest (altruistic factors and perceptions based on the HBM) and the dependent variables were assessed using multivariate logistic regression models. Each multivariate logistic regression model contained one independent variable of interest and all significant background characteristics. Crude odds ratios (ORs), adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and their 95% confidence interval (CI) were obtained. Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests were conducted to evaluate the strength of the logistic regression models in this study.37,38 The insignificant Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > .05) indicates that the model’s estimates fit the data at an acceptable level. Additional analyses were performed to estimate the effect per SD for independent variables of interest (altruistic factors and perceptions based on the HBM). Standardized scale/item scores were used in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The standard scale scores were calculated by subtracting the mean of raw scores and dividing by the standard deviation of the raw scores. This process converted different scales into a common z-score scale with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. The AOR in these analyses represented the change in the odds of the outcome (behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccine under different cost scenarios) for an increase in one standard deviation of the z score of an independent variable of interest. Moreover, interaction terms were created by multiplying income level and independent variables of interest (altruistic factors and perceptions based on the HBM), and these terms were included in multivariate logistic regression models to test their significance. SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, SPSS Inc., United States) was used for data analysis, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. A forest plot illustrating AORs with 95% CIs for key predictors under both scenarios (self-financed vs. fully subsidized) was generated in R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.1) and the dplyr package (v1.1.4).

Results

Background characteristics

Among 2,000 people aged 60 y or above approached by the research team, 74 had migrated to other cities at the time of this study, 370 refused to participate in the study due to lack of time and other tangible reasons, and 1,556 completed the face-to-face interviews. The response rate, defined as number of participants who completed the survey (n = 1,556) divided by number of eligible participants (n = 1,926), was 80.8%. Among the participants, 69.6% were under 70 y old, 61.4% were female, 84.2% did not receive tertiary education, 90.1% were married, 81.4% were retired, and 55.4% reported a monthly income lower than CNY5,000 (US$689). The proportion of participants who were living with spouse and children was 59.6% and 71.6%, respectively. In the past year, 9.6% and 8.6% of the participants reported smoking and binge drinking, respectively. Among the participants, 59.5% had at least one chronic disease, 70.2% reported a history of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, 46.1% received seasonal influenza for the 2023/24 flu season, and 68.8% took up a COVID-19 vaccination booster dose (Table 1).

Altruistic factors and perceptions based on the HBM

The means and SDs for scales and items related to altruistic factors and perceptions based on the HBM were presented in Table 2. Regarding general altruism, 60.1–85.0% of the participants agreed or strongly agreed with items in the Elder Care Research Center Altruism Scale. Regarding family-oriented altruism, 73.9 of them agreed that they could protect their family members by receiving vaccination. Regarding perceptions related to RSV and RSV vaccination, 59.1–62.7% of the participants agreed with the items representing perceived high risk and severe consequences of RSV among older adults, and the effectiveness of RSV vaccination. Among the participants, 41.3% and 40.2% concerned about the side effects and short duration of protection of RSV vaccination, respectively; 57.6% of them believed that their significant others would suggest them to receive RSV vaccination, and 57.1% of them were confident to receive such vaccination. In EFA, the KMO value was 0.833, indicating meritorious sampling adequacy, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ2 = 4334.146, df = 15, p < .001), suggesting the data were suitable for factor analysis. Principal component analysis extracted one factor with an eigenvalue of 3.546, accounting for 59.1% of the total variance. All six HBM items demonstrated strong factor loadings ranging from 0.660 to 0.825, exceeding the prespecified criterion of 0.60. In CFA, the model fit results were χ2 (9) = 607.98, p < .001, CFI = 0.862, TLI = 0.769, RMSEA = 0.207, and SRMR = 0.071. The SRMR fell within the acceptable range (<0.08). However, the CFI, TLI and RMSEA did not meet the recommended threshold of good model fit.

Factors associated with behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination under different scenarios

In the self-financed scenario, participants who received seasonal influenza vaccination in the 2023/24 flu season had higher likelihood of intending to receive an RSV vaccination (OR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.10, 1.91, p = .01). If fully subsidized vaccines are provided by the government, participants who were married (OR: 1.63, 95% CI: 1.14, 2.34, p = .01), living with children (OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.07, 1.68, p = .01) or grandchildren (OR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.16, 1.75, p < .001), with a history of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.64, p = .01), and received seasonal influenza vaccination (OR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.58, p = .02) and ≥2 doses of COVID-19 vaccination (2 doses: OR: 1.53, 95% CI: 1.05, 2.24, p = .03; ≥3 doses: OR: 1.49, 95% CI: 1.08, 2.07, p = .03; reference: 0–1 dose) were more likely to have intention to receive an RSV vaccination. Higher monthly income level (≥CNY10,000 [US$1379]: OR: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.58, 0.91, p = .01; reference: ≤CNY5,000 [US$689]) and smoking in the past year (OR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.49, 0.96, p = .03) were associated with a lower likelihood of intending to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination (Table 3).

The associations of altruistic factors and perceptions with intention to receive an RSV vaccination under different scenarios were presented in Table 4, after adjusting for significant background characteristics listed in Table 3. Regarding altruistic factors, perceived that they could protect their family members by vaccination were associated with higher likelihood of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under both the self-financed and fully subsidized scenarios (AOR: 1.53, 95% CI: 1.13, 2.06, p = .01 & AOR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.44, 2.18, p < .001). However, higher score in the Elderly Care Research Center Altruism Scale was associated with higher odds of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under the fully subsidized scenario (AOR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.11, p < .001), but not under the self-financed scenario. Perceived more benefits (AOR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.34, p = .01 & AOR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.44, 1.72, p < .001), barriers (AOR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.27, p = .02 & AOR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.26, p < .001), cue to action from significant others (AOR: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.33, 2.10, p < .001 & AOR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.74, 2.35, p < .001), and self-efficacy (AOR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.15, 1.77, p = .001 & AOR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.95, 2.66, p < .001) related to RSV vaccination were associated higher odds of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under both scenarios. Perceived higher susceptibility to RSV (AOR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.30, 1.56, p < .001) and more severe consequences caused by RSV (AOR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.33, 1.58, p < .001) were associated with higher odds of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under the fully subsidized scenario, but not under the self-financed scenario. A forest plot showing these AORs and their 95% CIs is shown in Appendix 2. The changes in the odds of the behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccine, under different scenarios, for each one SD increase in the z-score of the independent variables of interest were presented in Appendix 3.

Moderation effects of income level on the associations between independent variables of interest and behavioral intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination

The interaction term of income level and general altruism was significantly associated with behavioral intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination (AOR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.92, 0.99, p = .03). Among participants with relatively lower monthly income (CNY ≤ 4,999 [US$689] or CNY5,000–9,999 [US$690–1,378]), higher level of general altruism was associated with higher likelihood of intending to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination. However, the association between general altruism and behavioral intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination was not statistically significant among those with higher monthly income (≥CNY10,000 [US$1,379]). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies investigating the associations between altruistic factors and behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination among older adults, which suggested a new angle of vaccination promotion for older adults. The findings also expanded the application of the HBM in explaining vaccination behaviors. A relatively large study sample recruited through multi-stage random sampling is another strength of this study.

Our findings provided some practical implications to inform RSV vaccination service planning for older adults in China. Only 15.4% of older adults intended to receive self-financed RSV vaccination. It is expected that very few of them would receive an RSV vaccination under such a delivery model and is unlikely to create an impact on RSV transmission. Similar to other types of vaccination (e.g., herpes zoster vaccination), intention to receive an RSV vaccination was cost-sensitive. About 60% of older adults intended to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination provided by the government. Such level of intention was higher than that reported among older adults in the United States (47%) and was similar to the level observed in general population in mainland China (68.4%).9,13 However, the availability of fully subsidized RSV vaccination may not guarantee a high uptake rate. Health promotion is needed even after fully subsidized RSV vaccination becomes available. Take United States as an example, although the FDA approved two RSV vaccines for adults aged 60 y or above in May 2023 with most cost of the vaccines covered by private insurance and Medicare Part D, only 16.4% of adults of this age group received an RSV vaccination by 2025.39 Given the health threat of RSV, health authorities and experts in mainland China may consider cost-effectiveness analysis to decide the level of subsidy for RSV vaccination for older adults.

History of seasonal influenza vaccination in the 2023/24 flu season was associated with higher odds of behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination under both cost scenarios. Receiving more doses of COVID-19 vaccines was associated with higher likelihood of intending to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination. Such finding was similar to studies conducted in United States.13 Those who have received these vaccines might be more motivated to use vaccines for additional protection against infectious diseases. Health authorities in China may consider including RSV vaccination as a part of comprehensive immunization program for older adults. Higher income level was associated with lower odds of intending to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination. Similar finding was observed among general population in mainland China.9 Previous studies suggested that systematic advantages (e.g., wealth, high-quality healthcare) can lead to a lower risk perception and hence a higher vaccine hesitancy.40 Smokers were less likely to report an intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination. A previous study conducted among older adults in the United States observed a similar trend.12 Therefore, more attention should be given to older adults with higher income and those who are smokers during the rollout of fully subsidized RSV vaccination program in China. Majority of the participants were living with their children and grandchildren, and participants with such living arrangements were more likely to have intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination. It is possible that protecting these family members was a motivation to receive a fully subsidized RSV vaccination.

Older adults in our study had a high level of general altruism, as about 80% of them agreed that they enjoyed helping others and truly cared about the needs of others. In line with previous findings, older adults with a higher level of general altruism were more likely to have the intention to receive a fully subsidized RSV.21 However, the association between general altruism and intention to receive a self-financed RSV was statistically non-significant. Similar to previous studies, family-oriented altruism (i.e., belief that receiving a vaccine could protect household members) was associated with higher odds of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under both scenarios.41 It is possible that older adults would out receive an expensive vaccination such as self-financed RSV vaccination for the sake of protecting people other than their family members. Furthermore, higher level of general altruism was associated with higher likelihood of intending to receive a fully subsidized RSV among participants with lower monthly income, but not among those with higher monthly income level. Previous studies also observed a complex relationship become income and altruism.42

Different strategies are needed to promote RSV vaccination before and after fully subsidized RSV vaccination program becomes available, as factors associated with behavioral intention to receive self-financed and fully subsidized RSV vaccination are different. Targeting perceived susceptibility/severity might be useful to promote RSV vaccination when fully subsidized vaccines are available, as both perceived susceptibility and perceived severity were significantly associated with behavioral intention to receive a free RSV vaccination.43 Health communication messages should emphasize that older age and presence of chronic conditions would increase both risk of RSV infection and its severe consequences to increase perceived threat of RSV. However, these approaches might not be helpful when the vaccines are available at market rate, as none of these two constructs was significantly associated with behavioral intention to receive a self-financed RSV vaccination.

Enhancing perceived benefits and self-efficacy, and providing strong cue to action related to RSV vaccination, are potential useful promotion strategies before and after fully subsidized RSV vaccination becomes available, as these constructs were significantly associated with behavioral intention under both cost scenarios. Health communication messages should explain the vaccine efficacy in reducing both infection risk and severe consequences of RSV in the way that can be easily understood by older adults. Health authorities should also consider involving family doctors in the community health centers in the health promotion. In China, a family doctor is assigned to each resident to provide individualized health services, including health management, health education, and vaccination services.44 The community health center makes an ideal setting to initiate discussion related to vaccination with older adults. Majority of the older adults in this study were living with their spouse and children. Health promotion should involve these family members as their suggestions as a facilitator. Facilitating older adults to develop a concrete action plan and providing outreach vaccination service in the community may be useful to increase perceived self-efficacy to receive an RSV vaccination. In contrast to our hypothesis, perceived barriers were associated with higher odds of intending to receive an RSV vaccination under both cost scenarios. One possible explanation is that older adults who are actively considering vaccination may also seek more information about it, which increases their awareness of potential side effects or the limited duration of protection. Rather than deterring them, such awareness could coexist with a stronger motivation to get vaccinated, reflecting a more engaged decision-making process. Another interpretation is that individuals with risk awareness may simultaneously recognize barriers and benefits, but ultimately weigh the benefits more heavily, leading to stronger vaccination intentions despite acknowledging obstacles. Thus, barriers may serve not only as deterrents but also as markers of informed deliberation. Nevertheless, reverse causality cannot be ruled out, and the cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference.45

This study had several limitations. First, this study focused on community living older adults, and did not include older inpatients or residents of residential care homes. Therefore, the findings might not be generalized to all older adults in Shenzhen. The distribution of gender and age was similar to the census data in Shenzhen. The prevalence of chronic conditions among our participants was similar to that observed by a large-scale survey targeting older adults in the same city.46 Second, we were not able to collect information from older adults who refused to join the study. Therefore, we could not compare the characteristics between participants and refusals. It is possible that participants and refusals would have different characteristics. Self-selection bias existed. Third, due to the difference in healthcare system, vaccination delivery models, health literacy and cultural factors, the findings might not be applicable to other cities in China. Shenzhen is one of the most developed cities in mainland China. It is expected the performance of its healthcare system and residents’ health literacy would be higher than less developed regions of China. Therefore, more older residents in Shenzhen might intend to receive an RSV vaccination compared to their counterparts living in less developed regions. Fourth, data were collected through face-to-face interviews in this study. Participants might over report behavioral intention and positive attitudes toward RSV vaccination due to social desirability bias. We did not use any indirect measure of the social desirability bias. Anonymous surveys should be considered in future work. Moreover, scales measuring HBM constructs were constructed for this study and were not validated by separate study. The reliability of these scales was acceptable in this study. Future validation studies are needed. Furthermore, causality could not be established as this was a cross-sectional survey.

In conclusion, the study provided useful data to prepare the anticipated RSV vaccination rollout for older adults in 2026. As compared to the self-financed delivery model, behavioral intention to receive an RSV vaccination was much higher of fully subsidized vaccines provided by the government. To facilitate the RSV vaccination rollout, health authorities and experts in mainland China may conduct cost-effectiveness analysis to decide the level of subsidy for RSV vaccination for older adults.47,48 Early experience in other countries suggested that fully subsidized RSV vaccination might not guarantee a high uptake rate among older adults.11,39 Health promotion is needed even in the presence of subsidized vaccines, and our findings provided some implications to inform such health promotion. General altruism and family-oriented altruism were associated with higher intention to receive a fully subsidized vaccine. In the future, studies comparing the efficacies of altruism messages with standard messages in improving RSV vaccination uptake among older adults in China should be considered. Health communication messages targeting perceived susceptibility and severity of RSV and perceived benefits and self-efficacy related to RSV vaccination, and involving family doctors and family members to provide a strong cue to action may be useful health promotion strategies.
