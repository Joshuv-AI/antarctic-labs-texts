Cardiac Surgery in Sub-Saharan Africa

Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

While most of Africa is undergoing the epidemiological transition that has resulted in a significant increase in lifestyle-related noncommunicable diseases, Sub-Saharan Africa continues to have a high burden of rheumatic heart disease1 and congenital heart disease. Although cardiac surgery has progressed in SSA, it is still inaccessible, expensive, and bereft of trained health care personnel and governmental commitment.

Various models have been developed for the provision of cardiac surgical care in SSA. One model was the transfer of patients to surgical centers in high-income countries (HIC). Benefiting only few patients, the general problem of lack of services back home remains unanswered.2 Furthermore, surgical pathology is different in SSA, and few HIC surgeons have the skillset for patients with endomyocardial fibrosis, advanced congenital heart disease, and rheumatic heart disease. However, in countries such as Cape Verde and São Tomé and Príncipe, with small populations on geographically remote islands, this may be the only practical solution.

Another model is a visiting international surgical team that conducts a short-term mission. This model risks leaving behind complications of procedures to be handled by inexperienced, under-resourced local teams. Furthermore, little training is possible during short irregular visits carried out by multiple teams. Experience from Nigeria shows that this model is difficult to translate into self-reliance in the long run.3

A more effective way for the development of local self-sustaining centers is a mentorship model. A robust example of this model is the Aswan Heart Centre in Egypt, set up by Professor Magdi Yacoub.

Various models for provision of cardiac surgery may coexist, as the Tenwek hospital experience from Kenya illustrates.4 During COVID-19, the absence of missions provided the impetus for self-sufficiency, resulting in local surgeons now working independently. Despite this progress, local capacity remains limited with overseas transfers still assuming a preponderant role.

The Cardiac Surgery Intersociety Alliance has shown that a mentorship model from a high-volume center in a middle-income country can foster independence, as was the case with Maputo Central Hospital in Mozambique partnering with the University of Cape Town/Groote Schuur Hospital, which assumed a shepherding role while enhancing surgical autonomy. An alternative arrangement was shown by Team Heart, an American nongovernmental organization that took on the mentorship role for the King Faisal Hospital in Kigali, Rwanda, and has run missions with the explicit aim of achieving surgical autonomy. The Cardiac Surgery Intersociety Alliance has also acknowledged that training of surgeons in SSA may be best accomplished in other non-HIC with substantial similarity in cardiovascular pathology such as India and China.5 Advances in telemedicine are increasingly enabling “telementoring,” where a remote, experienced center can serve as a source of guidance for centers in SSA.

Besides overseeing the steps of surgical craftsmanship and the formation of the multidisciplinary clinical team, mentors should sow the seeds for scientific research. HIC journals of quality, with significant readership and citation prowess, can be a valuable partner in this exercise by providing a platform for the divulgation of outcomes. Most research is contextual and, in that framework, authors from SSA, should have some dedicated space to present their work in high-quality journals. Scrutiny of their work without judgmental overtones provides the impetus to maintain databases and compare outcomes and benchmark against external standards, improving transparency. It also provides a platform for recognition and an incentive for collaboration. The latter is of importance with the increasing human migration, where experience reported from SSA can contribute significantly to knowledge of conditions that are increasingly seen in HIC.

How can investment in a resource-intense enterprise like cardiac surgery be justified when communicable diseases are still the bane of SSA? Was the first cardiac transplant justified when there were other pressing problems? Such events garner a lot of media attention and serve as a source of national pride, and the ensuing surge in collective confidence that follows results in many parallel accomplishments. The new Groote Schuur hospital in Cape Town, where the world’s first heart transplant was performed, opened in 1986 as a multiracial hospital contravening the laws of apartheid. Its superintendent, Hannah-Reeve Saunders, wrote that this was partially possible because of the international standing it achieved through its achievements in cardiac surgery, including the excellent clinical expertise and outstanding research output. Having a cardiac surgery program has significant downstream effects that can positively impact local health care. In Ethiopia, universities started to open other training programs, private health institutions engaged in cardiac surgery and interventional cardiology services, and people demanded better cardiac services.6 In countries where health care workers (HCW) are sparse, these initiatives can inspire youngsters to train in the health professions. Setting up cardiac surgical services serves as a rising tide that can lift many other boats like laboratory services, radiology, blood bank, and intensive care units that will also serve other areas in the hospital. However, HCW need to be paid justly for their skills with ringfenced governmental funding earmarked. Governments in Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, and Ghana have taken this step.7

Centers should develop their own sustainable financing models without relying on donors or even governmental funding, which cannot be taken for guaranteed after initial infrastructure development. For example, in Ghana, in the benefits package of the National Health Insurance Scheme, echocardiography, angiography, and cardiac surgery are excluded, reflecting the low priority attributed to cardiovascular care. With restricted fiscal space, which is the margin to attribute funding to specific causes without jeopardizing the remaining budget, and a limited tax base due to the large informal economies in SSA, government commitment to continued funding of cardiac surgery consumables will be insufficient. Leaders of cardiac programs need to acquaint themselves with taxation structures, health care policy, and principles of management, bring competitive negotiating attitudes for innovative financing, and arbitrate for patient care needs with policymakers. Financial sustainability enables the increase in case volumes and local expertise, which can then be leveraged to train HCW, guaranteeing self-sufficiency and less reliance on third-party providers, resulting in strategic purchasing based on value in the future.

For sustainability to be ensured, value-based health care should be the operating paradigm in each center.8 This involves systematic tracking and reporting of outcome metrics. Comparison of outcomes and benchmarking with other centers in SSA provides transparency, which should be a prerequisite for value-based payments by funding stakeholders. Consideration can be given for innovative financing vehicles like social impact bonds, where nongovernmental investment, including private investors, can provide funding with the government returning money to investors if certain goals are met by the entities that were funded. Donors and philanthropic entities can find these instruments acceptable because of the possibility of return on capital coupled with obligations to improve outcomes.

A mindset fueled by innovation and creativity is crucial in resource-limited settings to re-engineer, repurpose, and redefine surgical material. The medical device industry should abandon the current approach of linear supply chains exemplified by disposable surgical material and adopt the principles of a circular economy by manufacturing products that are durable, recyclable, and simple to run and repair. COVID-19 exposed the fragility of linear supply chain economies. Value-based purchasing of medical supplies where the full life-cycle costs and not just upfront purchase costs ought to be the aim.

Health care institutions alone cannot do everything required to increase patient value. In many countries in SSA, unlike in HIC, a strong sense of community can be leveraged in multiple ways to improve outcomes. Providing services that facilitate transitioning patients from the hospital and home, including assistance with daily living, access to medication, and education on healthy lifestyles, should ideally be community-based initiatives. Collaborating with community-based players like faith-based organizations who are significant providers of health care in SSA can increase the network of cardiovascular care beyond government services (a variant of the Memphis model). Political stability and absence of conflict are paramount in maintaining centers open, as is evident from recent experience in Cameroon and Sudan.9 Establishment of regional centers of excellence that serve multiple smaller nations for complex cases should be considered in nations with a long history of peace and sustained development.

For ownership to be assumed by the local community and governmental policymakers, a sense of purpose derived from African culture and values is important. The core principles of uniquely African frameworks such as Ubuntu, which endorses values of survival, solidarity, compassion, respect, dignity, and ownership should be adopted, coupled with actions that exemplify Umuganda (coming together for a common purpose in Rwanda) and Bulungi Bwansi (for the good of the earth in Uganda) (Figure 1).

Operational Framework for the Development of a Cardiac Surgery Program in SSA

A background of political stability and peace with core principles of African culture forms the bedrock of a cardiac surgery program. Sustainable funding through donors, the government, and the banking sector for infrastructure and program development is needed. Continued sustainability requires self-financing projects bringing in diversified income streams to reduce financial risk. Social impact financing can link both the government and private investors with returns coupled to guaranteed outcomes. External international stakeholders can support training and research, and industry can enhance their value proposition through context-specific initiatives. The cardiac surgery program works toward improved clinical outcomes and financial indicators that are audited and benchmarked against other centers.
