Structural Changes in Pellets of Calcium Phosphate Ester Salts after Exposure to Simulated Body Fluid with the Addition of Alkaline Phosphatase
Authors: Wanyu Dong, Yuko Matsukawa, Taishi Yokoi, Kazumasa Suzuki, Chikara Ohtsuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Salts composed of calcium and phosphate esters (SCPEs) have been proposed as potential components for developing next-generation artificial bones, known as bioresponsive materials, due to their ability to transform into hydroxyapatite (HAp) in the physiological environment via enzymatic mediation by alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Previous studies have investigated the reaction behavior of the SCPE powders in simulated body fluid (SBF) modified with ALP. The reaction rates were influenced by the particle size of the SCPEs, specifically, the specific surface area, which in turn affected the morphology of the reaction product, HAp. Therefore, pellet samples, characterized by relatively constant surface areas, are more suitable for elucidating the fundamental reaction mechanisms. In this study, compact powder pellets of calcium salts composed of methyl phosphate (CaMeP), ethyl phosphate (CaEtP), butyl phosphate (CaBuP), and dodecyl phosphates (CaDoP) were utilized to investigate and evaluate their surface reactions in solution. The addition of ALP to SBF accelerated the detection of surface structural changes, even on pellet surfaces. The morphological changes observed on the surfaces of pellets exposed to either SBF or SBF/ALP followed trends similar to those previously reported for powder samples; however, the phase transformation occurred at a slower rate on the pellet surfaces. Although the quantity of the reaction product was insufficient for definite identification of the crystalline phase, the observed morphology suggested the formation of HAp. In the case of CaDoP, the formation of fibrous materials became prominent after 7 d of immersion in SBF/ALP. It was observed that molecules with higher hydrophobicity, generated from the decomposition of phosphate esters in SBF, influenced the development of fibrous materials during the dissolution–precipitation process.

Introduction

Calcium phosphate compounds have been widely used as materials for artificial bones. − Among them, hydroxyapatite (HAp) − and β-tricalcium phosphate (TCP) − ceramics are well-established materials for bone repair. HAp ceramics with a stoichiometric composition are bioactive materials exhibiting low biodegradability in vivo, ,, enabling direct bonding to living bone and promoting bone-bonding properties upon exposure to the physiological environment. Although β-TCP ceramics exhibit direct bonding to living bone, they are considered biodegradable materials, ,, owing to their gradual replacement by newly formed bone tissue. The biodegradability of β-TCP is attributed to its higher solubility compared with HAp, indicating that the biological behavior of calcium phosphate ceramics is largely governed by their interactions with body fluids.

The bone bonding of HAp ceramics, which are classified as bioactive materials, is attributed to their osteoconductive properties. This osteoconduction occurs through the formation of a biologically active HAp layer that closely resembles the natural bone when the material is exposed to physiological conditions. Previous studies on bioactive glasses and glass-ceramics have demonstrated that the formation of bone-like apatite on the surface of these materials is a critical factor for eliciting osteoconductive responses. − The formation of bone-like apatite is governed by the chemical interactions between the material and body fluids. Moreover, the potential of a material to form bone-like apatite can be evaluated using simulated body fluid (SBF), an aqueous solution designed to replicate the ionic composition of human blood plasma. ,

Despite their high biological affinity for living bone, calcium phosphate ceramics are mechanically inferior to autogenous bone. , These materials exhibit a high Young’s modulus and intrinsic brittleness. In addition to their mechanical limitations, their biological properties, specifically their ability to actively promote tissue regeneration in living bones, are also less effective compared with autogenous bone. ,, Consequently, there is growing interest in developing materials with mechanical properties that more closely mimic those of natural bone, as well as in designing materials with enhanced biological functions to promote bone regeneration. To incorporate additional functionality into osteoconductive materials, calcium salts of phosphate esters have garnered attention owing to their potential to enhance the formation of bone-like apatite in response to the local concentration of alkaline phosphatase (ALP). ALP is an enzyme that plays an important role in osteoid formation and mineralization and known as one of bone formation markers. Recent studies suggest that salts composed of calcium and phosphate esters (SCPEs) should be considered key components in the design of enzyme-responsive materials for novel artificial bone substitutes. −

Previous studies have demonstrated that SCPE powders are converted into HAp upon exposure to ALP-modified SBF. SCPEs contain organic components and are synthesized through the reaction of calcium phosphate ceramics with organic molecules under appropriate conditions, resulting in the formation of a coating layer on the SCPE particles. The reactivity of this coated layer is influenced not only by the type of organic modification, specifically, the nature of the alkyl groups in the phosphate esters but also by the microstructure formed through the aggregation of SCPEs particles. However, limited information is available regarding how microstructural variations in SCPE particle aggregates influence their chemical reactivity in biomimetic environments, such as immersion in ALP-modified SBF. To gain deeper insights into the chemical reactions of SCPE materials under these conditions, alternative approaches are required to achieve a fundamental understanding of their reactivity of SCPEs.

In this study, SCPE powder compacts (pellets) were immersed in SBF, following the protocol established by Kokubo et al., with a modification incorporating ALP to create ALP-modified SBF/ALP. The pellet form was used as a model to represent the microstructural aggregation of SCPEs coated on a substrate, enabling straightforward observation of surface structural changes on SCPEs. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the chemical reactions of compacted powders of calcium salts derived from methyl phosphate (CaMeP), ethyl phosphate CaEtP), butyl phosphate (CaBuP), and dodecyl phosphate (CaDoP) following exposure to SBF and ALP-modified SBF.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of SCPEs and Their Pellets

The SCPE powder was prepared according to a previously reported method. A CaCl2 solution was prepared using a chemical reagent (Nacalai Tesque Inc., Kyoto, Japan) to achieve a final concentration of 200 mol·m–3 (mM) CaCl2 solution at pH = 10. The pH of both the CaCl2 and phosphate ester solutions was adjusted to 10 using 28% ammonia (NH3) water (Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) at 37 °C. For the preparation of CaMeP, CaEtP, and CaBuP, 500 cm3 of a 200 mM CaCl2 solution was mixed with 500 cm3 of a 200 mM phosphate ester solution. The phosphate ester solutions were prepared by dissolving the respective commercially obtained chemicals: methyl phosphate (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), ethyl phosphate (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd.), or butyl phosphate (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The pH of each solution was adjusted to 10 using 28% ammonia solution, and the mixtures were maintained at 37 °C for 2 h. The phosphate ester concentration (200 mM) was calculated based on the assumption that it was a monoester. The monoester content of methyl phosphate, ethyl phosphate, and butyl phosphate was 50.0–55.0 mass%, 35.0–47.0 mass%, and 50 mass%, respectively. During the preparation of CaDoP, the concentrations of both the calcium source and dodecyl phosphate were reduced to 50 mM owing to the limited solubility of dodecyl phosphate. Accordingly, 500 cm3 of a 50 mM CaCl2 solution was mixed with 500 cm3 of a 50 mM dodecyl phosphate solution (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan) at pH 10 and maintained at 37 °C for 2 h. The pH was adjusted using 28% ammonia solution. Subsequently, four types of SCPEs, abbreviated as CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP, respectively, were obtained by filtering the resulting precipitates, washing them with ultrapure water, and drying them at 40 °C for 48 h.

The dried powders were manually ground using a mortar and pestle, followed by uniaxial pressing. Approximately 0.35 ± 0.05 g of each SCPE powder was pressed at 250 MPa for 1 min to form a compact pellet with a diameter of 12 mm and a thickness of approximately 1.6 mm.

Soaking of Powder Compacts in SBF and SBF/ALP

The SBF used in this study was prepared according to the method reported by Kokubo et al. in a previous study. The following reagents, all obtained from Nacalai Tesque Inc., Japan, were used: NaCl (7.996 g, ≥ 99.5%), NaHCO3 (0.350 g, ≥ 99.5%), KCl (0.224 g, ≥ 99.5%), K2HPO4·3H2O (0.228 g, ≥ 99%), MgCl2·6H2O (0.305 g, ≥ 98%), 1.00 mol·dm–3 HCl (aq) with 40 cm3, CaCl2 (0.292 g, ≥ 95%), Na2SO4 (0.071 g, ≥ 99%), and (CH2OH)3CNH2 Tris (6.057 g, ≥ 99.9%).

The aforementioned chemicals were sequentially added, in the specified quantities, to 1 dm–3 ultrapure water maintained at 37 °C without further purification. The solution was then adjusted to pH 7.40 using 1.00 mol·dm–3 HCl (aq) to obtain the SBF used in the experiments. The prepared pellets were individually immersed in 15 cm3 of SBF. For immersion in ALP-modified SBF (SBF/ALP), the ALP solution was initially dissolved in SBF at a concentration of 33 units·dm–3, following appropriate modifications based on previously reported methods. (Note: ALP concentration was fixed in this experiment because the effects of ALP concentrations on a SCPEs reaction was already discussed in the previous report. To compensate for the gradual reduction in ALP activity as a hydrolyzing agent, 1.0 mm3 of ALP solution containing 0.5 units was added daily. The ALP solution, adjusted to pH 8, was prepared by mixing 0.02 cm3 of a commercially obtained ALP stock solution containing 1000 units (derived from bovine intestinal mucosa, Sigma–Aldrich Japan Co., Tokyo, Japan) with 1.98 cm3 of a mixture solution. This mixture solution consisted of 10 mM of Tris, 1 mM of MgCl2·6H2O, 0.1 mM of ZnCl2 (Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd.), and 50 vol % glycerol (Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd.). The dilution procedure followed the method proposed by André Rosenthal and colleagues. The pH of the mixture solution was adjusted to 8 using 1 mol·dm–3 of HCl solution. This mixture was used to maintain ALP activity. When not in use, the prepared ALP solution was stored in a refrigerator at 5 °C. ,, Although the ALP solution contained Tris, MgCl2, ZnCl2 and glycerol, the amounts of added ALP solution to SBF was 1.0 mm3 and hence the difference of SBF and SBF/ALP can be regarded as presence and absence of ALP.

After immersion in SBF or SBF/ALP for predetermined durations (1, 3, and 7 d), the pellets were removed from SBF and SBF/ALP, gently rinsed with ultrapure water, and subsequently dried in an oven at 40 °C for 48 h. The specimen that was not subjected to any soaking treatment was designed as 0 d sample.

Characterization

The crystalline phases of the samples were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD; MiniFlex600, Rigaku Co., Tokyo, Japan) with Cu–Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 2° to 50° at a scan rate of 2°·min–1. The chemical structures were investigated using attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR–ATR; Nicolet iD5, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) in the spectral range of 520 to 4000 cm–1. The static water contact angles of the SCPE pellet surfaces were determined using the half angle method with a contact angle goniometer (USB Microscope M2, Scalar Co., Tokyo, Japan) using 1.5 mm3 of ultrapure water at room temperature. The sample surface was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JCM–7000, JEOL Co., Tokyo, Japan), under low vacuum conditions without conductive coating, at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The pH values of SBF and SBF/ALP solutions after sample immersion were measured using a pH meter (F–71, HORIBA, Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) at 37 °C.

Results

Characterization of As-Prepared SCPE Pellets

Figure presents the XRD patterns of CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets (powder compacts) in their as-prepared state, prior to immersion in SBF or SBF/ALP, denoted as “0 d.” The observed crystalline phases are consistent with those reported in previous studies. The crystalline phases of the CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets were attributed to calcium methyl phosphate, calcium ethyl phosphate, calcium butyl phosphate, and calcium dodecyl phosphate, respectively.

XRD patterns of CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets (powder compacts) in their as-prepared state prior to immersion in SBF or SBF/ALP, denoted as “0 d.” For clarity, the reflection intensities of CaMeP 0 d and CaBuP 0 d in the 2°–50° range are presented at 1/10 of their original intensity. The reflection intensity of CaEtP 0 d within the 2°–50° range is displayed at 1/100 of the original intensity.

Figure displays the FT-IR–ATR spectra of the surfaces of the as-prepared CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets. Absorption peaks observed in the ranges of 2980–2921 cm–1 and 2873–2843 cm–1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of the CH3 and CH2 groups, respectively. These peaks are associated with the CH3 and CH2 groups of the SCPEs and are particularly prominent in samples containing longer alkyl chains, such as butyl and dodecyl groups. Additionally, absorption peaks detected in the range of 1105–1095 cm–1 are assignable to P–O stretching vibrations, consistent with those observed in phosphate ions present in calcium orthophosphate compounds. In addition, the peak observed around 1030–1020 cm–1 is attributed to the superposition of absorption bands at 1027 and 1024 cm–1, corresponding to P–O–C stretching and P–O stretching vibrations, respectively. , Thus, this region reflects overlapping signals from both P–O and P–O–C functional groups. Comparison with previously reported results for powdered SCPEs indicates that the crystalline phase and chemical bonding of the samples remain unchanged during the pelletization process. This suggests that compaction does not alter the fundamental composition or crystalline structure of the materials.

FT-IR–ATR spectra of SCPE (i.e., CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP) pellets (powder compacts) in the as-prepared state prior to immersion in SBF or SBF/ALP, designated as “0 d.”.

Figure presents the results of the water contact angle measurements. No water droplet penetration into the sample pellets was observed within 1 min. The measured static water contact angles on the surfaces of the CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets were 31.4°, 60.3°, 80.1°, and 81.0°, respectively. An increase in the water contact angle on the pellets was observed with the extension of the alkyl chains in the phosphate esters.

Water contact angles measured on the surfaces of as-prepared CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets.

Figure shows SEM images of the surfaces of the as-prepared CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets prior to immersion in SBF or SBF/ALP (0 d). All SCPE pellets exhibited smooth, low-porosity surface morphologies that were consistent among the four compounds. The surface morphologies of CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP were similar, with deformation during compression molding progressing to the point where no surface pores were observed. Therefore, the observed trend of increasing hydrophobicity, i.e., CaMeP < CaEtP < CaBuP < CaDoP, can be attributed to the progressive increase in the length of the alkyl chains in the SCPE.

Changes in the Surface Structure of Pellets After Exposure to SBF and SBF/ALP

Figure presents SEM images of the surfaces of CaMeP pellets after immersion in SBF and SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d. On the CaMeP pellets, spherical particles that were several micrometers in diameter were observed on the surface after soaking not only in SBF/ALP for 1 d or more but also in SBF 1 d. The significant increase in number of these particles was not observed with longer immersion times, from 1 to 7 d, in both SBF and SBF/ALP solutions. Meanwhile, the surface roughness of the pellets increased with prolonged soaking time from 1 to 7 d, in both SBF and SBF/ALP solutions.

SEM images of CaMeP pellets after immersion in (a–c) SBF and (d–f) SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d. Red circles highlight the presence of spherical precipitates.

Figure presents SEM images of the surfaces of CaEtP pellets after immersion in SBF and SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d. After 1 d of soaking in SBF, fragmented structures and small spherical particles, a few micrometers in diameter, were observed on the surface. Particles larger than 10 μm appeared at 3 d and rod-like structures with several tens of micrometers in length were observed at 7 d. Under SBF/ALP conditions, sparsely distributed rod-like structures, several tens of micrometers in length, began to appear after 3 d, although such structure was not observed at 1 d. A slight increase in the number of these rod-like features was noted after 7 d of immersion.

SEM images of CaEtP pellets following immersion in (a–c) SBF and (d–f) SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d, respectively.

Figure shows SEM images of the surface of the CaBuP pellets after immersion in SBF and SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d. An increase in surface roughness of the pellet was observed after 1 d of immersion in SBF, which became more pronounced with longer immersion times. Additionally, spherical particles, several μm in diameter, were observed on the pellets after 3 d of immersion in SBF. The increase in the number of the spherical particles was observed at 7 d. Under SBF/ALP conditions, the large number of aggregates composed of spherical particles were observed in SBF/ALP for 1 d. Spherical particles, approximately 5 μm and larger than those observed at earlier stages, were observed in SBF/ALP for 3 d. The number of spherical particles was slightly reduced while the size of spherical particle was increased at 7 d. In addition, plate-like materials were observed on the surfaces of the spherical particles in SBF/ALP 7 d.

SEM images of CaBuP pellets after immersion in (a–c) SBF and (d–f) SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d, respectively.

Figure presents SEM images of the surfaces of CaDoP pellets after immersion in SBF and SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d. On the CaDoP pellets, fibrous structures were observed in both SBF and SBF/ALP for 1 d. Spherical particles were observed on the surfaces after 1 d of immersion in SBF. With increasing soaking time, both the number of fibrous structures and spherical particles increased on the pellets immersed in SBF. The diameter of the spherical particles exceeded 5 μm after 7 d of immersion in SBF. A comparison of the fibrous materials formed in SBF/ALP and those in SBF revealed that a greater quantity of fibrous structures was observed at each time point in SBF/ALP, whereas spherical particles were absent.

SEM images of CaDoP pellets after immersion in (a–c) SBF and (d–f) SBF/ALP for 1, 3, and 7 d, respectively.

Figure presents the XRD patterns of CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets after immersion in SBF and SBF/ALP for 7 d. Enlarged views of XRD patterns in the 25°–40° range are shown on the right side of Figure . Characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the starting materials of each SCPE were observed in both the SBF and SBF/ALP for 7 d. In addition, broad diffraction peaks corresponding to the crystalline phase of HAp, in accordance with the powder diffraction file (PDF #000–009–0432), were detected on CaMeP and CaDoP pellets after immersion in SBF and on CaMeP, CaBuP, and CaDoP pellets after immersion in SBF/ALP for 7 d. These findings suggest that CaMeP and CaDoP pellets are capable of forming an HAp layer on their surfaces under both immersion conditions (SBF and SBF/ALP) for 7 d.

The absorption peaks observed in the range of 1105 to 1020 cm–1 for CaMeP immersed in SBF exhibit markedly different shapes compared with those of the as-prepared SCPE pellets shown in Figure . In contrast, after immersion in SBF/ALP, the absorption bands within the same region of 1105–1020 cm–1, corresponding to overlapping vibrations of P–O and P–O–C bonds, display similar shapes across all SCPE types.

pH Changes of SBF and SBF/ALP Owing to Immersion of the Pellet

Figure illustrates the changes in pH of (a) SBF and (b) SBF/ALP solutions during the soaking of SCPE pellets. An initial increase followed by a continuous decrease in pH was observed for CaMeP pellets immersed in SBF, reaching a pH of 6.6 after 7 d. For CaEtP pellets, the pH of the SBF decreased during the initial 0–3 d, then stabilized around 7.1. In contrast, immersion of CaBuP and CaDoP pellets in SBF resulted in only a slight decrease in pH, with values of approximately 7.3 throughout the 7 d period. In the case of immersion in SBF/ALP, CaBuP pellets caused a continuous decrease in pH to 6.4, whereas the immersion of CaMeP, CaEtP, and CaDoP pellets in SBF/ALP resulted in a smaller reduction, with pH values stabilizing around 7.2.

Discussion

Based on the results described above, the SCPE powders were compressed into powder compacts, which exhibited smooth surfaces under SEM observation (Figure ). Following immersion in SBF and SBF/ALP, all four types of SCPEs (CaMeP, CaEtP, CaBuP, and CaDoP) demonstrated reactivity with the surrounding solution. This interaction led to an increase in surface roughness and the development of distinct surface morphologies, with each type of pellet exhibiting unique surface features: spherical particles several micrometers in diameter were deposited on CaMeP pellets; sparse rod-like structures, several tens of micrometers in length, appeared on CaEtP pellets; and fiber-like aggregates were observed on CaDoP pellets. The addition of ALP to SBF accelerated the surface reactions, as the morphological changes observed in SBF and SBF/ALP were similar for each SCPE pellet. In other words, the type of SCPE does not determine the phase of the precipitate formed after immersion in solution. The differences in pH reduction behavior among the various SCPE pellets possibly reflect the variations in the quantity of final products. Specifically, the marked pH decrease observed in SBF after 7 d of immersion with the CaMeP pellet, as well as in SBF/ALP with the CaBuP pellet, was caused by HAp formation. This observation suggests that these pellets generated greater amounts of HAp compared with the other pellets.

The morphological changes observed on the surface of SCPE pellets immersed in solutions are governed by the solubility of the SCPEs and the structural organization of the particle aggregates formed on the pellet surface, as these changes result from dissolution–precipitation reactions on the pellet surface. The solubility of the SCPE decreases in the following order: CaMeP > CaEtP > CaBuP > CaDoP. On the surfaces of the CaMeP pellets, spherical structures formed after immersion in both SBF and SBF/ALP. This morphology closely resembles that of bone-like apatite layers typically observed on bioactive ceramics following immersion in SBF. Accordingly, these spherical particles are identified as HAp, formed through the reaction between CaMeP and the surrounding fluid. The reaction was accelerated in the presence of ALP, even when the pellet surface appeared smooth owing to the compression of the CaMeP powders. On the surface of the CaEtP pellets, rod-shaped structures were observed, forming subsequent to the appearance of small spherical particles with diameters of a few micrometers. Detecting the formation of HAp using XRD is difficult owing to its low crystallinity under these conditions. Rod-shaped structures are characteristics of HAp formed under biomimetic conditions, particularly in environments with low supersaturation relative to HAp. Therefore, it is expected that crystal formation occurred in regions where the dissolution rate of the CaEtP pellets was relatively low, resulting in a modest increase in the supersaturation of SBF with respect to HAp. The densification of the CaEtP pellet surface likely contributed to the reduced dissolution rate. In contrast, the CaBuP pellets exhibited aggregates of spherical particles approximately 5 μm in diameter following immersion in both SBF and SBF/ALP. The presence of HAp was also confirmed by XRD analysis. Therefore, the CaBuP pellets underwent dissolution upon exposure to the solutions, leading to an increased degree of supersaturation with respect to HAp, which in turn promoted the deposition of bone-like apatite. On the surface of the CaDoP pellets, a distinctive morphology was observed, characterized by the aggregation of fibrous structures alongside spherical particles, the latter of which could be attributed to the bone-like apatite phase. The XRD patterns of CaDoP pellets immersed in SBF and SBF/ALP exhibited unidentified reflection peaks (labeled as “unknown” in Figure ). These peaks are likely attributable to the formation of complex structures resulting from the reaction between dodecyl phosphate and calcium ions, similar to the “CaDDP/CaP” mixtures reported by Ozin et al. However, the definitive assignment of these peaks requires further investigation.

A study comparable to this study is investigation about the reaction behavior of SCPEs powder in SBF or SBF/ALP. It is noteworthy that the reaction behavior and morphology of the precipitates formed from CaMeP, which has the highest solubility, and CaDoP, which has the lowest solubility, among the four SCPEs examined in this study, are similar in both powder and pellet forms (Figures and ). This suggests that the sample shape, i.e., powder or pellet, is unlikely to be a controlling factor in the morphology of the formed precipitates. On the other hand, for CaEtP and CaBuP, a large difference was observed in the reaction behavior between the powder and pellet forms. Rod-shaped structure was observed on CaEtP pellets in SBF and SBF/ALP (Figure ), but such precipitate and structures were not observed in CaEtP powder. In the case of CaBuP, the formation of a large amount of precipitates was observed on the pellets (Figure ), but no such significant reaction was observed in the case of the CaBuP powder. These results suggest that the material shape likely affect the reaction behavior as well as morphology of formed precipitates in the case of SCPEs with intermediate solubility. Further research is needed to understand this complex morphogenesis phenomena, particularly focusing on the effects of phosphate esters eluted from the SCPEs and alcohols produced with hydrolysis of the phosphate esters mediated by ALP. In addition, effects of ALP present in the reaction system on the precipitation reaction and morphogenesis of precipitates are also required to investigate.

Based on the above discussion, the precipitates formed on the SCPE pellets in this study are considered to be primarily HAp, occasionally incorporating organic molecules formed through dissolution and precipitation processes in the biomimetic solutions. The pronounced differences in surface morphology are likely attributed to the compact structure resulting from the compression of the SCPE powders and more detailed microstructure observation is essential. The surface structure of sample pellets may primarily influence the dissolution rate, thereby increasing the degree of supersaturation with respect to HAp, assuming that SCPE dissolution from the pellet is the rate-determining reaction under the biomimetic conditions examined. Future research will require a detailed investigation of the dissolution behavior of SCPEs, as well as a simultaneous investigation of the change in the supersaturation with respect to HAp. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of HAp formation on SCPE materials is essential for the controlled development of HAp microstructures and associated molecular assemblies.

Conclusion

This study investigated the behavior of four types of SCPEs, which were compressed into pellets and immersed in SBF and SBF/ALP, to evaluate their surface structures, particularly in relation to HAp formation. Distinct surface morphologies were observed, depending on the type of pellet: spherical particles several micrometers in diameter were deposited on CaMeP pellets; sparse rod-like structures, several tens of micrometers in length, formed on CaEtP pellets; aggregates of spherical particles, approximately 5 μm in diameter, were observed on CaBuP pellets; and fiber-like aggregates developed on CaDoP pellets. The addition of ALP to SBF accelerated surface reactions, as indicated by the similar morphological trends observed in both SBF and SBF/ALP for each SCPE pellet. The structural differences among the compressed powders played a significant role in determining the resulting surface morphologies following exposure to the biomimetic solutions. These findings offer valuable insights for the microstructure design of bioresponsive materials for artificial bone applications using calcium salts of phosphate esters.
