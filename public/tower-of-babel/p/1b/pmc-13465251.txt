Enhancing heme synthesis attenuates pathological α-synuclein propagation in vivo
Authors: Masami Masuda-Suzukake, Masato Hasegawa, Takashi Nonaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intraneuronal α-synuclein (αS) accumulation is a central event in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. The spread of αS pathology throughout the central nervous system contributes to disease progression via seed-dependent propagation, yet disease-modifying therapies remain unavailable. We previously reported that porphyrin compounds inhibit αS aggregation in vitro; however, because of their high molecular weight, porphyrins poorly penetrate the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Heme, a porphyrin derivative, is synthesized from 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) via the heme biosynthetic pathway. In this study, we investigated whether 5-ALA modulates αS accumulation and propagation. Although porphyrin compounds inhibited αS seeding in vitro, 5-ALA itself did not. However, treatment of mouse primary neurons with 5-ALA enhanced heme synthesis and suppressed seed-dependent αS aggregation, suggesting a potential role for intracellular heme in modulating αS propagation. We further evaluated the effects of 5-ALA in an in vivo αS propagation model. Preformed αS fibrils were unilaterally injected into the striatum of wild-type mice, followed by oral administration of 5-ALA at three doses (0, 1.7, and 20 mg/kg/day) for 4 weeks. Biochemical analyses demonstrated that 5-ALA significantly reduced the propagation of sarkosyl-insoluble αS to the contralateral hemisphere. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed a marked reduction in phosphorylated αS pathology in the amygdala and substantia nigra in the 5-ALA-treated groups. Collectively, these findings indicate that oral administration of 5-ALA suppresses αS propagation in vivo, potentially through enhancement of intracellular heme synthesis. Introduction

Intracellular accumulation of α-synuclein (αS) aggregates is a common pathological hallmark of synucleinopathies, including Parkinson’s disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies, and multiple system atrophy [31, 32]. In the diseased brains, αS undergoes structural changes and forms β-sheet-rich amyloid filaments [3, 32]. Progressive distribution of αS pathology correlates with the clinical stages of sporadic PD [5], suggesting that αS propagation contributes to disease progression. Therefore, targeting the propagation of pathological αS has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. However, no disease-modifying therapies have been developed to date.

Experimental evidence supporting the prion-like propagation of αS has been obtained in vivo. Intracerebral injection of preformed αS fibrils (PFFs) into nontransgenic animals induces conversion of endogenous αS into insoluble aggregates and promotes time-dependent propagation of αS pathology from the injection site to anatomically connected regions [10, 13, 18, 19, 26].

Previously, we reported that porphyrin compounds inhibit the aggregation of αS, tau, and Aβ in vitro [16, 17, 35]. However, their high molecular weight limits permeability across the BBB. Recently, a novel strategy employing porphyrins conjugated to BBB-permeable peptides has been proposed to enhance brain delivery, particularly as a therapeutic approach against viral infection [20, 36]. An alternative strategy to overcome this limitation is to enhance endogenous production of porphyrin-related molecules within the brain. All cells, including neurons, can produce heme, a porphyrin derivative, through the heme biosynthetic pathway. This pathway comprises eight enzymatic reactions spanning the mitochondria and cytoplasm. It is initiated in the mitochondria by the condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA to generate 5-ALA by ALA synthase. 5-ALA is then exported to the cytoplasm, where four consecutive reactions produce coproporphyrinogen III. This intermediate is transported back into the mitochondria and further processed by coproporphyrinogen oxidase and protoporphyrinogen oxidase to generate protoporphyrin IX (PPIX). In the final step, ferrochelatase catalyzes the incorporation of ferrous ion into PPIX, yielding heme. Overall, one molecule of heme is synthesized from eight molecules of 5-ALA and one ferrous ion [4].

5-ALA has been reported to cross the BBB, and its oral administration results in its distribution and PPIX production in the rat brain [37]. In addition, oral 5-ALA administration has been approved by the FDA for the photodynamic diagnosis of malignant gliomas, as it induces the accumulation of the photosensitizer PPIX within brain tumor cells [6]. Furthermore, 5-ALA has been proposed as a therapeutic candidate for neurological diseases [27].

Herein, we investigated whether 5-ALA suppresses neuronal αS aggregation by enhancing intracellular heme synthesis. We systematically evaluated its effects on αS aggregation in vitro and in cellular models and further assessed its therapeutic potential in vivo using an αS propagation mouse model following oral administration, with biochemical and neuropathological analyses.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

5-ALA was purchased from Cosmo Bio (Tokyo, Japan). Sodium Ferrous Citrate (SFC) was obtained from Mitsubishi Chemical Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). Protoporphyrin IX disodium salt (PPIX), hematin porcine (oxidized form of heme), rotenone, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and 3,3´-diaminobenzidine (DAB) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Thioflavin T (ThT) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo Japan).

Recombinant αS protein preparation and in vitro aggregation assays

Expression and purification of recombinant αS protein

The pRK172 plasmid encoding αS was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells and expressed and purified as described previously [15, 38]. Cells were grown at 37 °C for 4 h in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin, and protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside for 2 h. Cells were collected, suspended in Buffer A (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM dithiothreitol) and lysed by sonication. Lysates were centrifuged at 21,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were boiled in the presence of 1% 2-mercaptopethanol for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 21,000 g for 15 min. Heat-stable fractions were loaded onto a Q-Sepharose ion exchange chromatography column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA), and the αS protein was eluted with 0.35 M NaCl in buffer A. After precipitation with ammonium sulfate (50% saturation), αS was dialyzed against 30 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5. After ultracentrifugation at 113,000 g for 20 min, the supernatant contained soluble monomeric αS. Protein concentration was determined based on absorbance at 215 nm using reverse phase high-pressure liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) with an Aquapore RP300 column (PerkinElmer).

To prepare αS filaments, a solution of 5 mg/mL of purified αS in 0.1% NaN3 and 30 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) was incubated with shaking at 200 rpm for 5 days at 37 °C. Insoluble αS filaments were collected by ultracentrifugation at 113,000 g for 20 min at 25 °C, washed with saline, and ultracentrifuged again. Insoluble αS was suspended in saline, and protein concentration was determined by RP-HPLC after dissolving with 6 M guanidine-HCl. The samples were then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Prior to use, αS filament solution was sonicated for 180 s at 30% amplitude using a Sonifier SFX250 cup horn sonicator (BRANSON, Brookfield, CT) and then used as αS preformed fibrils (PFFs).

In vitro αS aggregation assay

The effects of porphyrins and 5-ALA on αS filament formation were analyzed as described previously [17]. Purified αS (2 mg/mL) in 30 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, and 0.1% NaN3 was incubated at 37 °C under shaking at 200 rpm in the presence of 200 μM compounds for 72 h. For ThT fluorescence, samples were mixed with 10 μM ThT, and the fluorescence intensity (excitation: 450 nm; emission: 480 nm) was measured using a microplate reader infinite200Pro (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). For sarkosyl insolubility, samples were mixed with 1% sarkosyl in 30 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Then, they were centrifuged at 113,000 × g for 20 min at 25 °C. Finally, the insoluble pellets were suspended in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer and analyzed via SDS‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. Images were acquired with Gel Doc EZ (Bio-rad, Hercules, CA) and quantified using ImageJ software.

In vitro seeding assay

Purified monomeric αS (1 mg/mL) in 30 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, in the presence of 40 μM ThT was incubated at 37 °C with 0.01 mg/mL αS PFFs in the presence of various concentrations of compounds. Fluorescence intensity at excitation 442 nm and emission 485 nm at 37 °C was monitored every 15 min using a microplate reader (TECAN).

Mouse primary neuronal culture

Primary mouse neuronal cells were prepared from embryonic day 15 (E15) ICR mice (Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) using Neuron dissociation solutions (FujiFilm-Wako, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were plated on poly-D-lysine coated 6 well plates at 1 × 106 cells/well in Neurobasal Medium supplemented with B27 and Glutamax (Gibco, Waltham, MA). At 14 days in vitro (DIV), cells were treated with 2 μg of αS PFFs. 5-ALA and SFC (at a molar ratio of 10:1) were added at DIV15, and cells were collected at DIV19. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (FujiFilm-Wako), permeabilized and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Primary antibodies (Table 1) were incubated in blocking buffer for 4 h, and Alexa488- or Alexa568-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) were incubated for 2 h. Coverslips were mounted with Vectashield with DAPI (Vector, Newark, CA). Images were acquired using a BZ-X710 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). For biochemical analysis, cells were collected with buffer B (10% sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.8 M NaCl, and 1 mM EGTA) containing 1% sarkosyl. Samples were lysed using sonication for 60 s using an Ultra S Homogenizer VP-5S (TAITEC, Saitama, Japan) and centrifuged at 113,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants containing the sarkosyl-soluble fraction were collected, and the insoluble pellets were washed with the same buffer. Immunoblotting

Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA). Then, membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies (Table 1) in blocking buffer overnight. Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000; Bio-Rad) for 1 h, the signal was developed using ECL™ Western Blotting Detection Reagents (Cytiva) or SuperSignal™ West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and detected using ImageQuant 4000mini (Cytiva). Band intensity was quantified using Image J software.

αS uptake assay

αS PFFs in saline were labeled with DyLight-488-NHS ester (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse primary neurons were plated on poly-D-lysine coated 8 chamber slides (Matsunami glass, Osaka, Japan) at 1.5 × 105 cells/well. Cells were treated with 500 nM DyLight488-labeled αS PFFs at DIV14 and 5-ALA and SFC (at a molar ratio of 10:1) at DIV15. After 4 or 24 h of incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA, and mounted with Vectashield with DAPI. The uptake of DyLight488-αS PFFs was analyzed with BZ-X710 microscope and a BZ-X analyzer.

Measurement of intracellular ATP and heme concentrations

At DIV15, mouse primary neuronal cells grown on poly-D-lysine coated 6 well plates were treated with 5-ALA and SFC (at a molar ratio of 10:1), or rotenone for 4, 24, 48, and 72 h. Cells were harvested with 0.05% trypsin–EDTA. Intracellular ATP levels were measured using an ATP Assay kit-Luminescence (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) and heme levels were analyzed by using a heme assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich).

Oral 5-ALA administration to the αS propagation mouse model

Wild-type mice (C57BL/6 J, SLC Japan) aged 3–4 months were used and randomly assigned to experimental groups. Body weights were measured prior to the experiments and confirmed to be comparable between groups. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and immobilized in a stereotaxic frame (Kopf, Tujunga CA). Two microliters of αS PFFs (0.5 mg/mL) were injected unilaterally into the right striatum (AP: 0.2 mm; ML: 2 mm; DV: − 2.6 mm). From the day after stereotaxic injection, mice were divided into three 5-ALA dosing groups: 0, 1.7, and 20 mg/kg/day. The number of animals used in the experiments is shown in Fig. 3a. Mice were orally administered with 5-ALA and SFC (at a molar ratio of 8:1) in sterile water once a day for 4 weeks. Health status and body weight were monitored daily and weekly, respectively. Animals were group-housed (4–5 per cage) with free access to food and water. After 4 weeks of administration, mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a combination of midazolam, medetomidine, and butorphanol tartrate and perfused with PBS. For immunohistochemistry, the brains were fixed in 10% formalin neutral buffer solution (Fujifilm-Wako). For biochemical analysis, brains were divided into the right and left hemispheres and stored at − 80 °C. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments (Science Council of Japan) and ARRIVE guidelines 2.0, and all experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Science (Approval no. 20-009, 21-037, 22-020 and 23-010).

Biochemical analysis of mouse brains

Sarkosyl extraction of the mouse brain tissue was performed as previously described [19]. Brain tissue samples were homogenized in buffer B using a Polytron. Then, the homogenates were centrifuged at 100,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were kept as soluble fractions. The pellets were homogenized in buffer B containing 1% sarkosyl and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min before centrifuged at 100,000 g for 30 min. Finally, pellets were resuspended in 30 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, and centrifuged again to remove contamination of soluble proteins. Buffer-soluble and sarkosyl-insoluble fractions were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Immunohistochemistry

Fixed brain tissue samples were sectioned into coronal slices of 40 μm-thickness and stored in PBS containing 0.1% NaN3. Then, free-floating sections were mounted on CREST-coated glass slides (Matsunami glass), dried, and subjected to antigen retrieval at 100 °C for 10 min in 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). To inactivate endogenous peroxidases, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, and blocking with blocking buffer (0.3% Triton X-100, 10% fetal calf serum, and PBS). Primary antibodies (Table 1) in blocking buffer were incubated at room temperature overnight; after washing, biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Vector) were incubated for 2 h. Sections were then incubated with Vectastain ABC kit (Vector) and developed with 0.4 mg/mL DAB. The sections were thoroughly washed multiple times to ensure that NaN3 was completely removed prior to DAB staining. Counter staining with hematoxylin (Muto chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) was performed when required.

Quantitative analyses were performed using three consecutive sections. For pS129 staining, sections located 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma for the striatum and S2 + insular cortices, 1.5 mm posterior to the bregma for the amygdala and PRh, 2.9 mm posterior to the bregma for the substantia nigra were analyzed. For tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) staining, sections at 0.14 mm anterior to the bregma for the striatum and at 3.52 mm posterior to the bregma for the substantia nigra were analyzed. Images were acquired using a BZ-X710 microscope and the positive area in regions of interest was measured using a BZ-X analyzer software.

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical methods were selected based on data distribution and experimental design. Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA).

Results

Porphyrins, but not 5-ALA, inhibit αS filament formation and seed-dependent aggregation in vitro

We previously reported that porphyrin compounds inhibited αS filament formation in vitro [17]. In this study, we first investigated the effects of 5-ALA on αS filament formation. Figure 1a shows the chemical structures of the compounds. Unlike porphyrin compounds such as PPIX and hematin, 5-ALA did not have an inhibitory effect on αS filament formation (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Next, we evaluated the effect of these compounds on seed-dependent aggregation. Soluble monomeric αS itself does not aggregate under static conditions at 1 mg/mL and 37 °C for 40 h, but the addition of small amounts of αS PFFs dramatically promotes seed-dependent aggregation (Fig. 1b). PPIX and hematin inhibited seed-dependent aggregation in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1c and d), whereas 5-ALA and SFC (a ferrous ion donor) showed no inhibitory effect (Fig. 1e and f). 5-ALA inhibits seed-dependent αS aggregation in mouse primary neuronal cells

Next, we investigated whether activating the heme biosynthesis pathway in a cellular model would inhibit αS aggregation. Mouse primary neuronal cells were seeded with αS PFFs, and at 24 h after seeding, 5-ALA and SFC were added to the culture media. SFC was added to enhance heme production from PPIX. Phosphorylated αS aggregates were observed after 72 h after addition of αS PFFs (Fig. 2a). 5-ALA treatment reduced phosphorylated αS aggregates in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2a and b). Furthermore, sarkosyl-insoluble αS decreased in a 5-ALA concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2c and d). This effect was not caused by a decreased uptake of αS PFFs by 5-ALA (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). As 5-ALA did not inhibit the seeding of αS in vitro (Fig. 1e), the inhibitory effect observed in the cell model is not due to a direct action of 5-ALA. Notably, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), a heme-degrading enzyme, was markedly increased in a 5-ALA concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2c and e), suggesting enhanced heme metabolism in the treated neurons. To further examine the metabolic consequences of 5-ALA treatment, we measured intracellular heme and ATP levels based on the hypothesis that both would increase following stimulation of the heme biosynthesis pathway. Intracellular heme levels were significantly elevated 24 h after the addition of 10 μM 5-ALA, and this increase was sustained for up to 72 h (Fig. 2f). In contrast, ATP levels did not significantly increase under these conditions, whereas a marked decrease was observed from 4 h after treatment with rotenone, a mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I inhibitor used as a control (Fig. 2g). Discussion

Previously, we reported that porphyrin compounds inhibit αS amyloid filament formation in vitro [17]. Based on this finding, we hypothesized that enhancing intracellular heme synthesis might suppress αS propagation. To test this hypothesis, we focused on 5-ALA, a precursor in the heme biosynthetic pathway. Although 5-ALA did not inhibit filament formation or seed-dependent αS aggregation in vitro (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and Fig. 1), it significantly suppressed seed-dependent aggregation in both cellular and mouse models (Figs. 2, 3, 4). Given the absence of inhibitory activity in vitro, this effect is unlikely to be mediated by a direct action of 5-ALA. Instead, in 5-ALA-treated neurons, we observed upregulation of HO-1, a heme-degrading enzyme, accompanied by increased intracellular heme levels (Fig. 2c, e, and f), whereas ATP levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2g). These findings suggest that 5-ALA modulates αS propagation through activation of heme metabolism. Notably, in the animal study, no reduction in pathological burden was observed at the injection site (Fig. 4a and b), indicating that 5-ALA does not affect intracellular PFF uptake. This interpretation is further supported by experiments in mouse primary neurons (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

In this study, in vivo analyses were conducted at a relatively early time point (1 month) following αS PFF injection, at which neurodegeneration was not observed (Additional file 1: Fig. S3a, b and d). Consequently, no neuroprotective effects of 5-ALA were detected (Additional file 1: Fig. S3a, c, and e); therefore, behavioral tests were not performed. However, our findings indicate that 5-ALA suppresses αS propagation at least during the early stages of disease progression, prior to the onset of neurodegeneration. Since neurodegeneration in this model occurs at later time points (e.g., 2 months post-PFF injection) [28], future studies involving longer-term administration will be required to evaluate the potential neuroprotective effects of 5-ALA.

The mechanism by which 5-ALA inhibits αS seeding remains unclear; however, several possibilities have been considered: (1) a direct effect of heme, (2) indirect effects mediated by heme, (3) indirect effects of 5-ALA, or (4) a combination of these mechanisms.

Our previous study supports the first possibility [17]. Porphyrin compounds inhibited filament formation and seed-dependent aggregation of recombinant αS in vitro (Figs. 1 and S1), suggesting a direct interaction with αS. Furthermore, porphyrin stabilized soluble αS oligomers, likely by interacting with oligomeric intermediates and preventing further polymerization [17]. Notably, these oligomers were not cytotoxic [17]. In contrast, in the present study, no such stabilized oligomers were detected in soluble fractions following 5-ALA treatment in either cellular or mouse models (data not shown), suggesting that such stabilized oligomers may be rapidly degraded within cells. Overall, however, our data provide only limited support for a direct mechanism.

Second, indirect effects mediated by heme may involve multiple pathways. As a prosthetic group in hemoproteins, heme is essential for diverse biological processes, including oxygen transport (hemoglobin and myoglobin), electron transfer (cytochromes), and redox regulation (catalases and peroxidases). Heme deficiency has been linked to impaired mitochondrial function, increased oxidative stress, and neuronal cell death, which may contribute to neurodegeneration [2, 7]. Conversely, increased heme availability may enhance the activity of these proteins, particularly cytochromes, thereby improving mitochondrial function. However, ATP levels remained unchanged in the present study (Fig. 2g), suggesting that enhanced energy production is unlikely to be the primary mechanism underlying the suppression of αS seed-dependent aggregation.

In addition to its role in mitochondrial function, elevated heme levels induce the expression of HO-1, a heme-degrading enzyme. HO-1 cleaves heme to generate bilirubin, carbon monoxide, and ferrous ions. It belongs to the stress protein superfamily and is upregulated in response to heme, oxidative stress, and heat shock [14]. Consistent with this, we observed a marked increase in HO-1 expression following 5-ALA treatment (Fig. 2c and e), indicating activation of heme metabolism. Although increased HO-1 expression and its colocalization with pathological structures—such as Lewy bodies, neurofibrillary tangles, and senile plaques—have been reported in neurodegenerative diseases [25, 29], and HO-1 overexpression has been shown to promote proteasomal degradation of αS [30], we did not observe a reduction in soluble αS levels (Fig. 3c, e, f and h). These findings suggest a limited contribution of this pathway under our experimental conditions.

Third, indirect effects of 5-ALA should also be considered. 5-ALA has been reported to exert beneficial effects in several diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis [8], chronic fatigue syndrome [12], and type 2 diabetes mellitus [11]. Clinical trials are also ongoing for adult-onset Still’s disease, a systemic inflammatory disorder, and Leigh syndrome, a mitochondrial neurological disorder [1, 33]. In addition, 5-ALA exhibits antiviral activity against feline infectious peritonitis virus, classical swine fever virus, and SARS-CoV-2 [9, 21, 22, 34]. Although the molecular mechanisms underlying these antiviral effects are not fully understood, increased PPIX levels induced by 5-ALA have been shown to inactivate viral particles and interfere with viral replication [9]. Alternatively, 5-ALA may reduce viral entry by decreasing ACE2 expression [21]. Furthermore, 5-ALA administration has been reported to enhance cytochrome c oxidase activity and increase ATP levels in the liver of mice [24], and in Drosophila, it extends lifespan and preserves muscle structure during aging [23]. Collectively, these pleiotropic effects suggest that the inhibitory action of 5-ALA on αS propagation is likely mediated by a combination of systemic and cellular mechanisms rather than a single direct pathway.

However, a limitation of this study is that the mechanistic relationship between enhanced heme synthesis and the suppression of αS propagation remains indirect. In addition, the possibility that 5-ALA promotes the clearance of αS aggregates cannot be excluded. Further studies are required to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these effects.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that oral administration of 5-ALA suppresses αS seed-dependent propagation in vivo without altering total αS protein levels. This effect is unlikely to result from direct inhibition of aggregation or enhanced ATP production, suggesting the involvement of indirect cellular mechanisms. Collectively, these results highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting heme biosynthesis as a novel strategy for modulating αS propagation.
