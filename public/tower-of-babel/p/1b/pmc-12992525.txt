Advances in In Vitro Vascularization of Engineered Tissues: From Microvessel Formation to Hepatic Tissue Integration
Authors: Yen-Hsiang Huang, Akira Ono, Ryo Sudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in tissue engineering have enabled the construction of two-dimensional tissues such as skin and cornea, as well as avascular three-dimensional (3D) tissues such as cartilage. However, building fully functional 3D tissues and organs remains a major challenge. In 3D constructs, microvascular networks are indispensable for delivering oxygen and nutrients to the inner regions; thus, the formation of capillaries and microvessels and their integration with 3D parenchymal tissues––so-called vascularization––are of critical importance. In this review, we discuss the current status and future perspectives of in vitro tissue engineering from a bioengineering standpoint, with a particular focus on the vascularization of 3D tissues. We first summarize recent progress in the in vitro construction of capillaries and microvessels, including microfluidic- and bioprinting-based approaches. We then focus on the liver as a representative parenchymal tissue, given its vital metabolic functions and the potential of human cell-derived liver models for drug discovery and as alternatives to animal testing. Finally, we highlight emerging strategies for vascularizing liver and other organ models and outline future directions in this evolving field. We conclude that bioengineering approaches for designing biomimetic systems, when integrated with vascular biology, organ-specific cell and stem cell biology, pathology, multiphoton lithography, and materials science, will enable the construction of in vitro 3D tissues with stable vasculature and functional integration with parenchymal structures, opening new avenues for drug discovery, disease modeling, and regenerative medicine.Keywords: Vascularization, Capillary, Microvessel, Liver, Microfluidic platform, Multiphoton lithography, Tissue engineering

Introduction

Tissue engineering, a cross-disciplinary field that aims to construct tissues and organs from individual living cells, was recognized early on as a vital and emerging technology with significant potential for industrial development [1]. In 1987, the Division of Emerging Technology of the U.S. National Science Foundation held a Forum on Issues, Expectations, and Prospects for the Emerging Technology Research Initiations, which strongly supported the organization of a dedicated workshop. Consequently, a workshop on tissue engineering was held in the United States in 1988.

For the purposes of that workshop and subsequent discussions, the term “Tissue Engineering” was defined as “the application of principles and methods of engineering and life sciences toward fundamental understanding of structure–function relationships in normal and pathological mammalian tissues and the development of biological substitutes to restore, maintain, or improve tissue functions” [1]. Research in tissue engineering has therefore been ongoing since at least the 1980s.

The foundations and challenges of this field were later summarized in a seminal review published in 1993 [2]. The general strategies were proposed for tissue construction: (1) the use of isolated cells or cell substitutes, (2) tissue-inducing substances, and (3) cells placed on or within matrices. Building on these strategies, researchers have since attempted to engineer virtually every type of mammalian tissue and organ.

Advances in tissue engineering have enabled the successful construction of two-dimensional (2D) tissues such as skin and cornea, as well as avascular three-dimensional (3D) tissue such as cartilage [3,4]. However, the construction of fully functional 3D tissues and organs remains a major challenge. In 3D tissues, microvascular networks are indispensable for delivering oxygen and nutrients to the inner regions. Therefore, the construction of capillaries and microvessels and their integration with 3D parenchymal tissues––so-called vascularization––is of critical importance.

In this review, we discuss the current status and future perspectives of in vitro tissue engineering from a bioengineering standpoint, with a particular focus on the vascularization of 3D tissues. We first summarize recent progress in the in vitro construction of capillaries and microvessels. We then focus on the liver as a representative parenchymal tissue, as this organ performs vital metabolic functions that cannot be replicated by artificial materials. Moreover, the ability to construct liver tissue using human cells holds great promise for applications in drug discovery and as an alternative to animal testing. Finally, we review efforts toward the in vitro construction of liver tissues and vascularization of liver tissues, and we conclude by discussing vascularization strategies for other organs and outlining future directions in this evolving field.

In vitro construction of capillaries and microvessels

Capillary networks play indispensable roles in sustaining cellular metabolic activities by supplying oxygen and nutrients. Accordingly, the ability to reconstruct 3D capillary networks within engineered tissues in vitro is fundamental to ensuring adequate transport of oxygen and nutrients to their internal regions. Over the past decades, a wide range of studies have investigated the formation of capillaries and microvessels in vitro, leading to significant progress in our understanding of vascular morphogenesis. In this section, we summarize the current conceptual framework and representative strategies for constructing in vitro capillary and microvascular networks.

Self-assembled vasculature in conventional culture systems

Early investigations of in vitro capillary formation were conducted in 3D culture environments employing hydrogel scaffolds within culture dishes or well plates. These conventional models have provided foundational insights into the mechanisms of vascular morphogenesis and the cellular responses to various external cues. Within such 3D culture systems, two principal paradigms have been established for capillary construction: angiogenesis-based and vasculogenesis-based models [5].

In the angiogenesis-based model, endothelial sprouts elongate and branch from a monolayer of endothelial cells (ECs) seeded on the surface of hydrogels (Fig. 1 Angiogenesis)––typically composed of collagen or fibrin––thereby forming interconnected vascular networks within the scaffold [6,7]. This configuration recapitulates in vivo angiogenesis, in which new vascular sprouts develop from pre-existing vessels, with the initial endothelial monolayer serving as the equivalent of the native vasculature.

In an in vitro angiogenesis model, ECs cultured on the surface of a hydrogel scaffold invade the underlying hydrogel to form vascular sprouts, which subsequently develop into microvascular networks. In an in vitro vasculogenesis model, ECs embedded within the hydrogel self-assemble into microvascular networks. Adapted from Watanabe et al. [7], J Biomech Sci Eng (2021), licensed under CC BY 4.0.

By contrast, the vasculogenesis-based model recapitulates the de novo formation of primitive capillary plexuses during embryonic development. In this approach, ECs are embedded within a hydrogel matrix, where they spontaneously aggregate, establish cell–cell junctions, and self-organize into interconnected vascular networks (Fig. 1 Vasculogenesis) [[8], [9], [10]].

Across both models, diverse biochemical and biophysical cues have been identified as key regulators of vascular formation. Biochemical factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and basic fibroblast growth factor drive endothelial sprouting and network formation [[11], [12], [13]], whereas biophysical factors including shear stress and matrix stiffness modulate vascular morphogenesis [6,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Collectively, these conventional 3D culture systems have served as essential experimental platforms for elucidating the fundamental principles of in vitro vascular formation, laying the groundwork for more advanced approaches, such as microfluidic-based and bioprinting-based vascular engineering, described in the following sections.

Vascular formation in microfluidic platforms

Conventional culture systems described above have provided foundational insights into vascular morphogenesis and EC responses to biochemical cues. Compared with these earlier models, microfluidic systems enable more precise spatial and temporal control of mechanical and biochemical stimuli, allowing detailed characterization of EC dynamics under physiologically relevant conditions. For example, EC migration was extensively investigated in conventional culture models prior to the advent of microfluidic platforms; however, these systems often lacked precise control of gradient formation and real-time visualization under well-defined flow conditions. Microfluidic devices address these limitations by enabling stable gradient generation, precise application of shear and interstitial flow, and high-resolution imaging at the single-cell level.

Microfluidic devices facilitate cell culture within confined spaces ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers [18]. Fluid behavior at these length scales exhibits distinctive characteristics. In fluid mechanics, the Reynolds number (Re) is a dimensionless number representing the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in a flowing fluid. In microfluidic systems, Re is typically very low because of the small characteristic length scale. Consequently, fluid flow is viscosity-dominated and remains laminar. This regime allows highly precise control of the chemical and mechanical microenvironment surrounding cells and has been a key factor driving advances in microfluidic-based experimental platforms [19]. These features make microfluidic platforms well suited for recapitulating the complex vascular microenvironment observed in vivo. Furthermore, such devices offer excellent optical accessibility and can be integrated with perfusion systems to apply well-defined flow stimuli, thereby enabling precise regulation of mechanical and biochemical cues acting on cells [20,21]. Both angiogenesis-based [[22], [23], [24], [25]] and vasculogenesis-based models [[26], [27], [28], [29]] of capillary formation have been successfully established in microfluidic culture systems (Fig. 2), similar to those observed in conventional 3D culture models described above [7,[30], [31], [32], [33]].

Representative illustrations of self-organization approaches in microfluidic platforms. Both in vitro angiogenesis and vasculogenesis models are constructed using microfluidic culture systems. Adapted from Watanabe et al. [7], J Biomech Sci Eng (2021), licensed under CC BY 4.0.

This microfluidic culture system has greatly advanced our understanding of vascular formation by enabling the application of controlled chemical and physical stimuli to ECs within microchannels. Regarding chemical stimulation, the ability to generate stable concentration gradients of biochemical factors has facilitated investigations into how such gradients influence vascular morphogenesis. For example, Chung et al. established a VEGF concentration gradient within a microfluidic device and demonstrated that ECs migrated through the hydrogel toward regions of higher VEGF concentration [34]. Furthermore, Shin et al. quantitatively analyzed the combined effects of VEGF and angiopoietin-1 on angiogenesis [35]. They found that a VEGF gradient alone was sufficient to induce 3D angiogenesis, whereas the addition of angiopoietin-1 enhanced the connection between tip and stalk cells, resulting in the formation of more stable capillary networks.

Regarding physical stimulation, experiments have been conducted by controlling pressure and flow within microfluidic devices by connecting tubes and perfusion systems to the culture ports [[36], [37], [38]]. In our previous study, we investigated the effects of interstitial flow on angiogenic vascular morphogenesis. In this study, interstitial flow through collagen hydrogels inside the microfluidic device was generated by creating a height difference in the culture medium between the reservoirs connected to the microchannels. The results demonstrated that interstitial flow through the collagen gel significantly enhanced vascular sprouting, network extension, and the formation of branched microvascular networks in a flow-magnitude-dependent manner [39].

In addition to chemical and physical factors, cellular interactions play crucial roles in vascular morphogenesis. Building on insights from in vivo studies, microfluidic systems emulate key aspects of the physiological microenvironment to recapitulate cell–cell interactions. Coculture with supporting cell types such as pericytes, mesenchymal stem cells, and fibroblasts––key components of the vascular microenvironments––has been demonstrated in microfluidic platforms [18,[40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. These studies have enabled investigation of the signaling pathways involved in the interaction between endothelial and supporting cells. Microfluidic systems provide precise control over various elements of the culture microenvironment, including cell density, positioning, and scaffold architecture, all at high spatial resolution. Therefore, these platforms facilitate mechanistic analyses that are difficult to achieve in vivo or in conventional culture systems.

In addition, organ-specific cells and microenvironments have been considered in vascular formation and maturation [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. For example, brain microvascular ECs exhibited significantly higher endothelial barrier functions compared to human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) [47]. Furthermore, brain ECs were cultured with primary brain pericytes and astrocytes to recapitulate blood-brain barrier function [46]. Decellularized kidney cortex hydrogels enhanced kidney microvascular EC maturation [45].

Future studies are expected to investigate microvessel properties under more physiologically relevant conditions by combinatorial manipulation of chemical factor gradients, scaffold stiffness, shear stress, hydrostatic pressure, and cocultured cell types within microfluidic platforms. As our understanding of microvascular responses to various stimuli deepens, further exploration of the underlying signaling pathways will become increasingly important. Achieving this goal requires the ability to retrieve cells from microfluidic devices for molecular and cellular analyses. However, conventional microfluidic devices, which are typically fabricated by bonding polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) to a coverslip, make cell retrieval challenging. Therefore, it will be essential to integrate established microvessel construction techniques with device designs that allow disassembly and cell recovery [[49], [50], [51], [52]]. Moreover, because the number of cells cultured in a microfluidic device is inherently limited, highly sensitive and quantitative analytical methods for assessing cell functions are also required.

Effect of shear stress on ECs

The effects of mechanical stimuli on blood vessels have attracted attention due to flow-dependent phenomena observed in vivo [[53], [54], [55]]. For example, Flaherty et al. demonstrated that when an incision in a dog's aorta was sutured perpendicular to the original vessel axis, EC nuclei, which were initially oriented perpendicular to the blood flow, became rounded after 3 days and subsequently reoriented along the direction of flow after 10 days [56]. These pioneering observations suggested that ECs dynamically alter their morphology in response to fluid mechanical stimuli. Subsequent studies further elucidated how blood flow induces morphological adaptations in ECs and the associated functional changes in vascular tissues. Numerous in vivo studies have shown that alterations in blood flow induce morphological changes in ECs, thereby highlighting the intimate relationship between hemodynamic forces and vascular function.

Research on the effects of shear stress on ECs has advanced rapidly with the development of in vitro culture techniques that enable controlled flow to be applied to cells. Representative systems include parallel-plate flow chambers and rotating disk apparatuses [57]. Using these 2D experimental setups, a variety of shear stress-induced alterations in vascular functions, such as cell alignment [58], migration, proliferation [59], and barrier function [60], have been elucidated. The flow-induced responses of blood vessels investigated in numerous studies have been largely characterized using these conventional 2D culture systems. More recently, research has expanded beyond morphological analyses to encompass comprehensive genetic and even epigenetic investigations aimed at identifying key genes and transcription factors involved in flow-mediated mechanotransduction [61].

Furthermore, the development of microfluidic systems has enabled more precise control of fluid flow and advanced imaging and analytical capabilities. These advancements have facilitated detailed analyses of EC orientation based on the relative positioning of the Golgi apparatus and nucleus, as well as the reorganization of the cytoskeleton and cell–cell junctions, under experimental conditions in which the magnitude and gradient of shear stress are precisely defined [[62], [63], [64], [65]]. Such flow-induced alterations collectively contribute to the maintenance of vascular barrier integrity, reduction of hydrodynamic resistance, promotion of directional migration, and redistribution of intracellular mechanical stress, all of which are essential for vascular homeostasis.

The accumulated findings underscore the critical role of flow stimulation in maintaining the fundamental functions of ECs. At the same time, it is essential to consider the differences between 2D and 3D culture environments. In multicellular organisms, ECs, as integral components of vascular tissue, sense blood flow while adhering to adjacent ECs and the extracellular matrix (ECM) in a 3D context, thereby determining tissue organization and function. Building on insights obtained from 2D culture systems, future studies should elucidate how flow governs endothelial behavior and tissue organization in 3D vascular constructs.

Perfusion culture and vascular remodeling

The development of microfluidic experimental systems has enabled the construction of perfusable microvessel models [66]. Subsequent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of shear stress in vascular maturation and remodeling. Perfused vasculature has been shown to dynamically modulate its functional state in response to shear stress, exhibiting alterations in barrier integrity and in the expression of angiogenesis-related proteins, including matrix metalloproteinase-1 [[66], [67], [68], [69], [70]]. Furthermore, Zeinstra et al. demonstrated the importance of perfusion by showing that perfused vascular networks display gene expression profiles that promote vascular development compared with their non-perfused counterparts, and that they integrate more rapidly and effectively with host vessels upon transplantation [50].

In vivo, blood vessels are constantly perfused with blood to maintain homeostasis, while during developmental processes or wound healing, changes in flow patterns trigger vascular remodeling to optimize network architecture. Vascular remodeling is largely driven by shear stress, and numerous in vivo observations in experimental animals such as mice and zebrafish have demonstrated this phenomenon [[71], [72], [73], [74], [75]]. These studies indicate that vascular remodeling involves processes such as regression, pruning, enlargement, and shrinkage [[65], [66], [67], [68], [69]]. The vascular network is continuously optimized through various cellular mechanisms, including EC migration, fusion, proliferation, hypertrophy, and other morphological changes [74,75].

In vitro models that reproduce vascular remodeling have also been reported, demonstrating that the structure of vascular networks changes in response to shear stress and the underlying cellular mechanisms are now being elucidated [44,[76], [77], [78], [79], [80]]. For instance, we utilized a microvessel model constructed in a microfluidic device to show that fluid perfusion induces the enlargement of capillary-scale vessels (diameters <20 μm) into intermediate-scale vessels (40–150 μm) [79]. Similarly, Shafiyi et al. applied flow to a microvessel model using a perfusion system and observed pruning of low-flow regions accompanied by increased flow velocity in adjacent areas, resulting in more efficient overall perfusion [78].

These findings indicate that blood vessels possess a shear stress “set point” for maintaining homeostasis and that vascular remodeling represents an adaptive response to restore the applied shear stress toward this set point when deviations occur. To obtain deeper insights into vascular remodeling, it will be essential to integrate experimental approaches such as bioprinting techniques that enable precise definition of local shear stress, with computational analyses that simulate wall shear stress distributions.

Bioprinting approaches for constructing microvascular networks

Bioprinting, an extension of 3D printing technology, enables the fabrication of living tissues, organs, and ECM hydrogels into predefined architectures. Several printing modalities have been developed, including inkjet-, extrusion-, and laser-assisted techniques. Among these, multiphoton lithography (MPL) offers the highest spatial resolution and is therefore particularly suitable for constructing capillary-scale vascular structures [81].

MPL is a laser-based microfabrication method capable of producing structures with micrometer- or even sub-micrometer-level precision. In this technique, a femtosecond laser is focused inside a hydrogel, such as Matrigel, collagen, or fibrin, where multiphoton absorption occurs. This nonlinear optical process triggers localized photochemical and photophysical events, including ablation, cavitation, polymerization, and degradation at the focal volume. These physical effects can be tuned by adjusting laser power, scanning speed, and repetition rate. Consequently, hollow microchannel templates can be generated within the hydrogel with exceptionally high resolution (Fig. 3A).

Following microchannel fabrication, ECs are seeded into or onto these channels, in which they adhere, migrate, and form perfusable vascular structures guided by the predefined templates. MPL has been applied with various hydrogel matrices for vascular tissue construction; however, collagen gels are most commonly used because of their intrinsic bioadhesive properties and ability to support endothelial morphogenesis [80,[82], [83], [84], [86], [87], [88], [89], [90], [91], [92], [93]].

The principle of MPL enables the creation of microstructured templates with virtually arbitrary geometries by precisely controlling the laser irradiation trajectory. In theory, if cells can be introduced into these structures, microvessels of any desired shape can be constructed. Using MPL, user-defined microstructures with dimensions on the order of several tens of micrometers can be fabricated. Although straight geometries are most commonly employed (Fig. 3B), tapered (Fig. 3C) and bifurcated microvessel models (Fig. 3D) have also been realized at this scale [80,85,87]. Furthermore, the high degree of shape controllability afforded by MPL has facilitated the construction of organ-specific capillary architectures. Notably, microvessel models mimicking the kidney glomerulus (Fig. 3E) and liver lobule (Fig. 3F) have been successfully fabricated, demonstrating the potential of this technique for replicating organotypic microvascular geometries [88,93]. These examples highlight the potential of MPL not only for structural replication but also for exploring functional vascularization under perfused culture conditions.

Despite the high spatial resolution achievable with MPL, reports of successful construction of vessels with diameters below 20 μm––the scale of true capillaries––remain limited. Given the remarkable advances in microfabrication and other technologies that recapitulate the in vivo microenvironment, there is an increasing need for methodologies that enable precise cell introduction into such minute templates, as well as subsequent cell spreading and growth to achieve functional vascular tissue formation. In our recent study, we demonstrated successful vascular formation within templates containing 50-μm channels; however, vascular formation failed in 20-μm channels under static culture conditions. These findings suggest that the mode of vascular formation shifts as the channel diameter decreases to a scale comparable to that of individual cells. We further demonstrated that flow stimulation promoted vascular formation along these minute templates by enhancing collective EC migration and adhesion [80].

To date, the primary novelty of vascular models created using MPL has been associated with their breakthroughs in microvessel construction and scaffold fabrication techniques [94]. However, future studies are expected to further integrate vascular geometry with biological function, thereby enabling investigations into how specific structural parameters influence endothelial behavior, perfusion dynamics, and tissue morphogenesis.

Moreover, MPL's superior capacity for geometric control makes it particularly suitable for designing perfused microvessel models. By defining the inlet, outlet, and intermediate vessel diameters, the local shear stress distribution along the vascular network can be qualitatively regulated. Such precisely defined flow environments will be invaluable for future studies on the mechanotransduction and flow-mediated signaling in engineered microvessels. These developments collectively underscore the importance of perfusion dynamics in guiding vascular maturation and remodeling under physiological flow conditions.

Beyond vascular applications, MPL can be extended to virtually any cell type. It has been employed to precisely control the spatial arrangement of fibroblasts [[95], [96], [97]], cancer cells [98], and stem cells [99,100], as well as to modulate the architecture of complex tissues such as the small intestine [101], bile ducts [102], bone [103,104], and nerves [105]. Thus, MPL is increasingly recognized as a powerful platform for controlling the geometry and spatial organization of diverse cell types, including endothelial, epithelial, and stromal cells, through user-defined scaffold architectures at the single-cell scale [104]. With its ability to guide cellular positioning and tissue morphology, MPL is expected to play a central role in recapitulating the structural and functional complexity of native tissues in vitro. Collectively, these advances highlight MPL's versatility as a bridge between microfabrication and cell biology, paving the way for studies on perfusion culture and vascular remodeling.

In vitro construction of 3D hepatic tissues

Building on the development of capillary network construction described in the preceding section, research is now shifting toward the ultimate goal of supplying oxygen and nutrients to 3D parenchymal tissues, rather than culturing vascular structures alone. Among various organs, the liver has attracted particular attention because it plays vital roles in sustaining life––including detoxification, metabolism, and protein synthesis––and serves as a key target in regenerative medicine as well as drug discovery research.

3D culture systems are essential for maintaining the differentiated functions of hepatocytes. Hepatocytes represent a classic example: they undergo rapid dedifferentiation within approximately one week under conventional 2D monolayer conditions, whereas in 3D environments they retain their phenotypic and functional characteristics for substantially longer periods. This functional preservation arises because 3D architectures reconstitute essential cell–cell and cell–ECM interactions and reproduce the spatial organization that closely mimics the native hepatic microenvironment. The following sections summarize representative 3D hepatic culture strategies and their applications in liver tissue modeling [106].

Spheroid culture of hepatocytes

Spheroid culture is a scaffold-free 3D culture system in which cells spontaneously self-aggregate to form compact, spherical constructs (Fig. 4, Spheroid). This approach allows cells to establish close intercellular contacts, thereby enhancing cell–cell communication and generating tissue-like organization [107,108].

Organoid culture utilizes stem cell self-organization to produce organ-like structures through cyst formation or coculture with supporting cell types. The fundamental concept of spheroid formation dates back to 1894, when it was demonstrated that isolated embryonic cells possess intrinsic self-organization capabilities [109]. In hepatic research, early studies revealed that isolated rat liver cells can spontaneously re-aggregate and undergo histotypic reorganization into functionally organized spheroids [110]. Subsequent investigations established that cell–cell interactions, rather than cell–ECM adhesion, are critical for maintaining differentiated hepatocyte functions [111]. Functional analyses further demonstrated that hepatocyte spheroids restore structural polarity and maintain bile canalicular networks, hallmark features of in vivo liver tissue [112].

While early research demonstrated the potential of hepatic spheroids as functional 3D culture systems, their practical applications faced significant challenges, including non-uniform size distribution and difficulties in scalable production. Microfabrication technologies have been introduced to address these issues. For instance, chemically modified culture surfaces have enabled the formation of hepatocyte spheroids with uniform diameters ranging from 32 to 220 μm [113]. In addition, PDMS-based honeycomb microwell arrays have been developed to improve oxygen transport, enabling high-throughput spheroid production with precise control over spheroid formation [114]. These technological advancements have transformed spheroid culture from a simple biological observation into a practical platform for tissue engineering and drug testing.

However, spheroid culture inherently suffers from diffusion limitations, leading to nutrient and oxygen gradients within 3D constructs. When the spheroid radius exceeds approximately 150–200 μm, mass transport to the core becomes insufficient, often resulting in central necrotic cell death [108,115]. The 150–200 μm threshold specifically applies to spheroids cultured under standard submerged conditions, in which oxygen availability is limited by the relatively low solubility of oxygen in culture medium and the increased diffusion distance to the spheroid core.

This limitation can be partially alleviated under alternative culture configurations, such as air–liquid interface (ALI) culture. To mitigate diffusion-induced hypoxia, ALI systems have been employed in organoid, liver slice, and epithelial culture models [[116], [117], [118]]. In ALI culture, direct exposure of cells to atmospheric oxygen enhances oxygen availability and can delay the onset of central necrosis in larger constructs. However, nutrient delivery remains dependent on diffusion from the liquid side; therefore, diffusion-related constraints are reduced but not completely eliminated [118,119]. In addition, prolonged air exposure may increase the risk of tissue dehydration [120].

These physical diffusion constraints restrict the formation of larger and more structurally complex tissues and underscore the critical need for vascularization to sustain cell viability in thicker constructs. Accordingly, overcoming this diffusion limit through vascularization strategies represents the next frontier in spheroid-based tissue engineering.

While spheroid culture represents a fundamental scaffold-free 3D approach, its intrinsic diffusion limitations restrict tissue size and long-term functionality. To overcome these challenges and enable large-scale, planar 3D constructs with improved nutrient and oxygen transport, multilayer culture systems have been developed as an alternative strategy for hepatocyte culture.

Multilayer culture of hepatocytes

Multilayer culture employs engineered cellular stratification to construct 3D tissue models through the controlled deposition of cellular and ECM components (Fig. 4, Multilayer). This approach establishes distinct functional compartments by positioning cells within defined layers, thereby allowing precise regulation of the cellular microenvironment and intercellular interactions.

Early developments in multilayer hepatic culture primarily relied on matrix-dependent configurations. The sandwich culture, developed in 1989, demonstrated that hepatocytes cultured between two collagen layers maintained liver-specific functions by mimicking native ECM encapsulation [[121], [122], [123]]. However, this system was inherently limited to monolayer configurations. Building on this concept, the stacked-up 3D culture approach incorporated microporous membranes to enable the vertical stacking of multiple hepatocyte layers, facilitating direct cell–cell contact across layers [124,125]. This architecture successfully reconstituted bile canalicular networks spanning multiple tissue layers, thus more closely recapitulating in vivo hepatic organization.

Another milestone was the development of cell sheet technology, which utilized temperature-responsive poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) surfaces to harvest intact hepatocyte sheets without enzymatic digestion, thereby preserving cell–cell junctions and endogenously deposited ECM [126]. This approach minimized inflammatory responses associated with synthetic scaffolds while enabling direct endothelial–hepatocyte contact in multilayered coculture systems [127,128]. Despite these advances, nutrient and oxygen diffusion limitations remain a major bottleneck in thick multilayered constructs, emphasizing the necessity of incorporating vascularization strategies to sustain long-term hepatocyte viability and function.

Liver organoid culture

Liver organoid culture represents a new generation of 3D hepatic culture technology that enables the long-term expansion and functional maturation of hepatocytes and their progenitors (Fig. 4, Organoid). Unlike spheroid or multilayer cultures, which rely on the aggregation or stacking of mature hepatocytes, organoid culture exploits the self-organization potential of stem or progenitor cells within well-defined 3D ECM environments.

Organoid culture systems generate organ-like structures through self-organization processes that recapitulate key aspects of developmental morphogenesis. Broadly, organoids can be categorized into two distinct types: (1) cystic organoids, which are derived from epithelial stem or progenitor cells and form hollow spherical structures, and (2) coculture organoids, which incorporate multiple cell types to reconstitute complex tissue microenvironments. A pioneering early study demonstrated that coculture of hepatic progenitors with non-parenchymal cells is critical for constructing hepatic organoids [129]. In both types of organoid culture, cells spontaneously organize into 3D architectures that exhibit structural and functional hallmarks of native organs [130,131].

A major conceptual breakthrough in organoid biology was achieved by Sato et al. [132], who established the first cystic organoid culture using three essential niche factors that activate the Wnt signaling pathway. Specifically, R-spondin 1 stabilizes β-catenin to promote stem cell renewal, epidermal growth factor drives cell proliferation, and Noggin inhibits differentiation by blocking BMP signaling. This combination enabled single Lgr5+ stem cells to form fully self-organizing intestinal structures in vitro without the need for non-epithelial support cells.

Building on this concept, organoid culture methodologies have been extended beyond stem cell systems to include mature cell-derived organoids. Igarashi et al. [133] demonstrated that cryopreserved primary human hepatocytes can be reprogrammed into functional liver organoids through the combined activation of Wnt and STAT3 signaling pathways, achieving enhanced proliferative capacity while maintaining hepatic identity. However, the proliferative lifespan of these hepatocyte-derived organoids remains constrained by telomere shortening, which ultimately limits indefinite expansion and poses challenges for long-term experimental and translational applications.

Takebe et al. [134,135] pioneered the generation of liver organoids including nascent microvascular networks by co-culturing induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived hepatic endoderm cells with HUVECs and mesenchymal stem cells. Within 48 h, these heterogeneous cell populations self-organized into 3D liver buds that closely recapitulated early liver organogenesis. Upon transplantation into mice, the liver buds rapidly anastomosed with host vasculature and matured into functional vascularized liver tissue, establishing a foundational model for in vivo-integrated organoid transplantation.

More recently, Takebe's group developed an inverted multilayered air–liquid interface (IMALI) culture system to further refine liver bud organoid formation. This approach enables the coculture of iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial progenitors (iLSEPs), hepatocyte-like cells (iHep), and induced septum transversum mesenchyme within a spatially organized, air–liquid interface environment [136]. The study demonstrated that iLSEPs within the organoids formed fenestrated vessels and secreted Wnt2, which in turn promoted hepatic maturation through endothelial–parenchymal crosstalk. Collectively, these studies represent a major advance toward reconstructing perfusable, developmentally patterned liver tissues in vitro, bridging the gap between organoid biology and vascular tissue engineering.

Hepatic tissue formation in microfluidic platforms

Advances in spheroid, multilayer, and organoid cultures have demonstrated the importance of 3D architecture and cellular self-organization in maintaining hepatic functionality. However, static culture environments cannot fully reproduce the dynamic biochemical and biomechanical cues of the liver microenvironment in vivo. Consequently, the next frontier in hepatic tissue engineering lies in microfluidic culture systems, which integrate controlled perfusion, oxygen and nutrient delivery, and intercellular communication to drive in vitro hepatic tissue formation.

Microfluidic culture systems employ engineered microscale channels to precisely regulate the cellular microenvironment. These systems enable well-controlled mass transport, allowing the generation of stable concentration gradients and the application of laminar perfusion flow for enhanced exchange of nutrients, growth factors, and metabolic waste [137]. Furthermore, spatial compartmentalization of different cell types within designated regions allows the recreation of tissue-specific architecture and microenvironmental heterogeneity, resulting in culture conditions that are more physiologically relevant than those achievable in conventional static systems.

Early microfluidic liver models focused on engineering microchannel geometries and flow dynamics to recapitulate key aspects of hepatic physiology. Lee et al. [138] constructed hepatic plate-like structures within a microfluidic device by employing parallel microchannel barriers that mimic liver sinusoidal architecture. This design established diffusion-dominated mass transport within a physically confined, high-density hepatocyte culture region, recapitulating the spatial organization of hepatic plates. Toh et al. [139] developed a 3D microfluidic perfusion culture platform incorporating micropillar arrays for cell immobilization and polyelectrolyte complex coacervation to generate cell-conformal matrices. This configuration supported long-term hepatocyte functionality by maintaining cell–cell and cell–ECM interactions under perfused conditions. In another study, Choi et al. [140] utilized a microfluidic platform to culture primary rat hepatocytes under controlled flow, demonstrating that optimized perfusion rates markedly improved hepatocyte viability and metabolic activity compared with static culture.

In recent years, microfluidic platforms have been increasingly employed to culture various types of 3D tissues, including hepatic constructs (Fig. 4, Microfluidics). Choi et al. [141] developed a microfluidic platform incorporating pyramidal microwell arrays to facilitate hepatocyte spheroid formation and examined hepatic functions under different microfluidic chamber volumes. Spheroids cultured within smaller chamber volumes exhibited significantly enhanced hepatic functions, suggesting that microenvironmental confinement plays a key regulatory role in hepatocyte behavior. Similarly, de Hoyos-Vega et al. [142] designed a microfluidic device equipped with a valve-controlled injection port, enabling the direct transfer and culture of needle-harvested liver tissue fragments within the chip. Both studies demonstrated that reducing microfluidic volume enhances the accumulation of autocrine signaling molecules, notably increasing hepatocyte growth factor expression while suppressing transforming growth factor-β1 levels. These findings underscore the importance of spatial confinement and local signaling dynamics in promoting hepatic functionality within microfluidic culture environments.

Engineering vascularized 3D hepatic tissues and extending to the other organ models

Advances in microfluidic liver models have highlighted the critical interplay between tissue architecture, fluid dynamics, and biochemical signaling in maintaining hepatic function. Yet, without a functional vascular network, these models remain limited in their ability to fully recapitulate the physiological complexity of liver tissue.

Vascular integration is a key determinant of engineered tissue function. Vascularization provides 3D tissues with sufficient mass transport capacity to overcome diffusion limitations, while pre-vascularized constructs facilitate rapid integration with host vasculature on transplantation. Moreover, ECs actively secrete angiocrine factors that regulate tissue maturation, morphogenesis, and functional development.

The next frontier in hepatic tissue engineering thus lies in reconstructing vascularized 3D liver tissues within microfluidic platforms, where perfusion flow and endothelial–parenchymal crosstalk can be precisely regulated to guide tissue growth and regeneration.

Hepatocyte-EC coculture in microfluidic platforms

Early approaches toward the construction of vascularized hepatic tissues involved coculturing hepatocytes and ECs in close proximity. Microfluidic platforms provide separate culture regions and allow precise control of coculture conditions. Several studies have integrated multilayer culture systems with microfluidic platforms for liver coculture. These platforms employ dual-channel architectures, in which hepatocytes are cultured in an ECM sandwich configuration in the upper channel, while liver non-parenchymal cells are cultured in the lower channel, separated by a porous membrane. This multicellular arrangement effectively recapitulates the complex cellular composition of the liver [143,144]. However, these multilayer systems still lack direct vascular integration within the engineered tissue constructs.

In our previous study, we applied interstitial flow to promote the formation of 3D primary hepatocyte tissues, followed by static coculture with microvascular ECs [145]. Hepatocyte-secreted paracrine factors induced ECs to form 3D capillary-like structures that extended toward the hepatocyte tissues. Although the microvessels did not penetrate the 3D hepatocyte constructs, this study established an important foundation for the development of vascularized hepatocyte tissues (Fig. 5A).

Advanced vascularization strategies for hepatic tissues in microfluidic platforms

Over the past few years, several studies have fabricated vascularized hepatic tissues using microfluidic platforms. Bonanini et al. [146] developed a two-stage tissue grafting strategy for hepatic tissue vascularization. In the first stage, they fabricated spheroids containing immortalized human hepatocytes and HUVECs. In parallel, they established VEGF gradients within the microfluidic device to guide HUVEC growth toward the central region, forming a microvascular bed. The spheroids were then grafted onto this microvascular bed, where the two constructs fused through cellular anastomosis to form functional vascularized tissue (Fig. 5B). However, hepatic cell lines exhibit continuous proliferation and outward migration, which may attenuate cell–cell adhesion and compromise tissue organization. In a subsequent study, the same group cocultured multiple liver cell types in a central channel with HUVECs in adjacent side channels. The mixed cells self-assembled into vascularized hepatic tissues with integrated microvascular networks [147]. Although these tissues exhibited vascularized structures, this cell-mixing approach, in which dispersed single cells were directly embedded within a hydrogel, led to a relatively loose tissue organization. This was mainly because hepatocytes exhibited limited migratory capacity after encapsulation and consequently remained spatially isolated, rather than forming compact 3D tissue architectures.

Unlike immortalized or stem cell-derived hepatocytes, vascularization of primary hepatocytes poses formidable challenges due to their strong cell–cell adhesions and limited migratory capacity. In a recent study, we incorporated human lung fibroblasts (hLFs) as paracrine sources of angiogenic factors within freshly isolated primary hepatocyte spheroids [148]. We demonstrated that increasing hLF density and reducing diffusion distances generated steeper paracrine factor gradients, thereby promoting microvessel penetration into the spheroids (Fig. 5C). The resulting vascularized spheroids exhibited hepatic sinusoid-like structures in which microvessels were in close contact with hepatocytes. Nonetheless, excessive hLF proliferation eventually compromised microvascular perfusion.

Chhabra et al. [149] developed a vascularized liver model to investigate hepatocyte regenerative responses mediated by endothelial–hepatocyte interactions. They embedded hepatocyte–fibroblast spheroids within a fibrin hydrogel and cocultured them with HUVEC-lined channels under oscillatory flow conditions. They supplied exogenous interleukin-1β (IL-1β), which activated HUVECs to secrete prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and induced hepatocyte cell-cycle reentry (Fig. 5D). However, direct microvessel penetration into hepatocyte tissues was not observed in this model, likely limiting regenerative potential, as the spatial gap between microvessels and hepatocytes substantially attenuates diffusional molecular exchange. These findings underscore the necessity of developing perfusable, vascularized hepatic tissues that faithfully recapitulate sinusoid-like architecture and support regenerative responses in vitro.

Our recent study [150] addressed this longstanding challenge by developing a paracrine factor local gradient (PFLG)-generating system for engineering perfusable vascularized hepatocyte tissues (Fig. 5E). In this system, fibroblast-loaded cryogels were integrated with microfluidic platforms to generate PFLG, in which spatially controlled gradients directed microvessel penetration into 3D hepatocyte tissues. The resulting constructs formed perfusable vascular networks with sinusoid-like architectures. Importantly, this approach prevented uncontrolled hepatocyte–hLF contact, which often occurs in conventional coculture systems, by maintaining physical separation between cell populations while enabling sustained, localized paracrine stimulation. Notably, under perfusion conditions, the vascularized constructs exhibited robust hepatocyte proliferation characterized by active mitosis (Fig. 5E). Moreover, hepatocytes preserved functional polarity and differentiated hepatic functions. Collectively, this work represents a significant advance in engineering liver tissues that simultaneously achieve vascularization, perfusion, and regenerative capacity.

Vascularization of other organ models in microfluidic platforms

In parallel with advances in hepatic vascularization strategies (Fig. 6, Liver tissue), these approaches have been extended to organoids derived from other organ systems. Although the overarching goal of achieving perfusion is shared, each organ presents distinct structural and biological constraints that require specialized strategies for vascular integration.

Kidney organoids, in particular, face vascularization challenges due to their complex multicellular architecture and highly specialized vascular–epithelial interfaces. Homan et al. [151] cultured kidney organoids on a gelatin-fibrin matrix within microfluidic devices, where medium flow generated controlled shear stress over organoid surfaces. This shear stress activated endogenous endothelial progenitor cells within organoids, promoting their differentiation into functional vascular networks with perfusable lumens. In parallel, shear stress upregulated endogenous VEGF expression, establishing concentration gradients that guided vascular growth toward glomerular structures (Fig. 6, Kidney spheroid). Furthermore, disruption of these gradients impaired proper vascular targeting, confirming that endogenous VEGF gradients are essential for directed angiogenesis. This microfluidic approach enables the vascularization of kidney organoids and provides physiologically relevant models for studying kidney development and disease.

Cardiac tissues require extensive vascularization due to their exceptionally high metabolic demands. Arslan et al. [152] cocultured pre-vascularized cardiac microtissues with human iPSC-derived ECs and pericytes in a fibrin hydrogel, resulting in the formation of vascular networks both within and around the microtissues that connected with surface microvessels. The integration of these vascular networks enhanced cellular interactions and modulated the contractile properties of the cardiac tissues. Alternatively, Di Cio et al. [153] employed cardiac spheroids composed of cardiomyocytes, ECs, and fibroblasts at a 4:2:1 ratio. Simultaneous embedding with additional vascular cells enabled internal ECs to form microvessels within the spheroids, while the external vascular cells established surrounding networks. This dual-level vascular integration maintained synchronous beating for 25 days, accompanied by perfusable blood flow throughout the spheroids (Fig. 6, Cardiac spheroid). Such internal vascular incorporation yields more durable and physiologically relevant cardiac models. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that cardiac vascularization not only supplies oxygen and nutrients but also creates microenvironments essential for cardiomyocyte functionality and long-term tissue viability.

Isolated pancreatic islets rapidly undergo capillary network regression, presenting significant challenges for revascularization. Li et al. [154] generated reprogrammed vascular endothelial cells (R-VECs) by inducing ETV2 transcription factor expression. Unlike HUVECs, which fail to form stable vessels and deteriorate within 24 h, R-VECs established perfusable vascular networks that directly penetrated into islet tissues, guided by islet-derived VEGF signals. When cocultured with islets, R-VECs acquired islet-specific endothelial characteristics and formed native-like capillary architectures. This vascular integration restored glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and calcium influx patterns that were absent in non-vascularized islets. Moreover, subcutaneous transplantation of R-VEC vascularized islets achieved sustained glycemic control in diabetic mice, whereas non-vascularized islets failed to engraft (Fig. 6, Pancreatic islet tissue). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that engineered adaptive ECs can reconstruct the vascular microenvironment essential for islet metabolic function and long-term engraftment.

Together, these organ-specific studies underscore that vascular integration not only sustains perfusion but also enhances paracrine signaling, thereby promoting functional maturation across engineered tissues.

Future perspectives and concluding remarks

Over the past three decades, research in tissue engineering has progressed remarkably, and techniques for in vitro microvascular formation have advanced substantially. These techniques constitute a fundamental basis for the 3D tissue construction in vitro. Although the vascularization of epithelial tissues such as the liver is of great importance, challenges remain even in studies focusing solely on vascular structures. One major issue is the long-term stability of vascular networks. Under perfusion culture, blood vessels dynamically respond to mechanical stimuli and undergo morphological changes, a process known as vascular remodeling. Therefore, even when vascular geometries are pre-designed through bioprinting approaches, their structural integrity is not necessarily maintained during culture. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms governing vascular remodeling is thus essential, and achieving long-term vascular stability will require careful consideration of these mechanisms.

In addition to pre-designing vascular geometries using bioprinting, it is crucial to enhance the physiological relevance of engineered capillaries and microvessels. Vascular structures constructed by MPL approaches have been composed solely of ECs [80,88,92]. Future research should therefore aim to construct vascular tissues incorporating perivascular cells, such as pericytes and vascular smooth muscle cells, to more closely reproduce the multicellular organization of native vessels.

Bioprinting-based approaches also hold great promise for the vascularization of hepatic tissues. MPL enables not only the pre-design of vascular geometries but also the precise patterning of 3D epithelial tissue architectures. This capability allows the coculture of microvascular networks and epithelial tissues in predefined configurations, facilitating the analysis of endothelial–epithelial crosstalk. Such crosstalk is expected to promote the formation of more physiologically relevant and highly organized 3D tissues with differentiated functions. In particular, blood vessels not only supply oxygen and nutrients but also serve as continuous sources of angiocrine factors secreted by ECs under perfusion culture [[155], [156], [157]]. This sustained paracrine signaling may support the long-term maintenance and maturation of epithelial tissues, ultimately enabling the construction of more functional and stable in vitro models.

Recent advances in tissue-construction technologies that faithfully recapitulate human physiological characteristics have also driven important regulatory changes. In April 2025, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) announced a plan to phase out the requirement for mandatory animal testing in the drug approval process [158]. As organoid culture systems, organ-on-a-chip technologies, and their integrated applications employing human cells continue to evolve, it is becoming increasingly feasible to predict human-specific responses, including toxicities that may not be evident in conventional animal models. Consequently, advanced tissue-engineering-based in vitro systems are expected to serve as promising alternatives to animal experimentation.

Furthermore, recent advances have enabled the creation of highly physiologically relevant 3D tissues with precisely controlled structural organization. While such engineered tissues are valuable for drug discovery and disease modeling, their use in regenerative medicine faces the major challenge of scaling up tissue size. To address this limitation, engineering strategies that enable the stacking, integration, and fusion of vascularized tissue units into larger constructs will be essential. Alternatively, it may be necessary to establish culture environments that promote self-organized tissue growth, for example through perfusion-induced cues. These strategies could facilitate the fabrication of large-scale, functional tissues suitable for transplantation.

In conclusion, the bioengineering approaches for the design of in vitro systems described above, when integrated with vascular biology, organ-specific cell and stem cell biology, pathology, MPL, and materials science, will enable the construction of in vitro 3D tissues that exhibit vascular stability and functional integration with epithelial structures. Such interdisciplinary approaches will not only faithfully recapitulate both the structural complexity and physiological functions of native organs but also open new avenues for drug discovery, disease modeling, and regenerative medicine.
