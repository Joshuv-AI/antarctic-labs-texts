Modified Amberlyst 15 for continuous glycerol-free biodiesel production from rice bran oil fatty acid distillate
Authors: Tiprawee Tongtummachat, Maturada Suksaard, Kanisorn Toponwised, Kanyanat Prompen, Atchariya Muadchan, Sirinthon Khamphimai, Surachai Chatpatumtong, Attasak Jaree, Nattee Akkarawatkhoosith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study developed a modified Amberlyst 15 (A15) catalyst for the production of glycerol-free biodiesel from rice bran oil fatty acid distillate (RBOFAD), a low-quality feedstock with high free fatty acid content. A15 was selected as the catalyst platform because of its strong Brønsted acidity and good stability; however, its direct application is limited by textural constraints and restricted access to acid sites. To overcome these limitations, A15 was modified through thermal treatment followed by acid-site regeneration to enhance both pore accessibility and catalytic functionality. The optimized modified catalyst exhibited a 140.5% increase in surface area and an 11.6% increase in acid density compared with the as-received A15. The catalyst was then applied to the continuous production of biodiesel, using dimethyl carbonate as a glycerol-free acyl acceptor. The effects of key operating variables were systematically investigated and optimized using a design-of-experiments approach. Under optimal conditions of 120 °C, a residence time of 60 min, and a DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio of 3 : 1, the modified catalyst achieved a % FAME of 87.5%. In addition, the catalyst showed good preliminary operational stability over 30 h of time-on-stream. The influence of Zn loading on the modified A15 catalyst was also evaluated, but it did not further improve its catalytic performance. Benchmarking against related catalyst-based biodiesel production systems showed that the modified A15 catalyst provided competitive performance under substantially milder operating conditions. These findings demonstrate that catalyst modification combined with acid-site regeneration is a practical and effective strategy for improving continuous glycerol-free biodiesel production from low-quality, high-FFA feedstocks.

Introduction

Currently, conventional biodiesel production faces high production costs, particularly raw material costs, which account for 70–80% of the total production cost.1 This green and renewable energy has not been as cost-competitive as diesel. Hence, low-cost feedstocks, particularly those with high free fatty acid (FFA) content, such as crude vegetable oils2 and waste-based oils,3 have been explored for biodiesel production. In addition, algae-derived oils have also attracted attention as sustainable lipid-rich feedstocks for biofuel production.4 For example, rice bran oil fatty acid distillate (RBOFAD) is a byproduct of rice bran oil refining. RBOFAD is a low-cost feedstock with a high FFA content (approximately 60%), promising for value-added applications when used as a raw material for biodiesel production. Unfortunately, the direct use of these feedstocks is not practical with conventional transesterification due to the high FFA content. Pretreatment processes for feedstocks are required to purify them prior to use, incurring additional production costs.5 For example, in the work of Cao et al. (2022),6 the FFA content in waste edible oils was removed by esterification prior to the main transesterification. This technique is well known as a two-step process. However, the product still needs to be purified before proceeding to the next step.

Supercritical biodiesel technology can overcome this issue; however, it requires extreme operating conditions, such as high reaction temperatures and pressures, to achieve high production performance. A promising approach is to identify a simple, effective, highly efficient catalytic process that can be used directly with high-FFA raw materials without further pretreatment steps and can be operated under conventional (subcritical) conditions. Recent studies have further highlighted the importance of catalyst design for biodiesel production from low-quality oils. For example, advanced photothermal catalysts have enabled the one-step conversion of high-acid-value non-edible oils under mild conditions,7 while lignin-derived biochar catalysts with controllable acidity have improved the catalytic upgrading of Jatropha oil to biodiesel.8 These studies demonstrate that controlling catalyst acidity and pore structure is essential for improving biodiesel production from high-FFA feedstocks.

Amberlyst 15 (A15) is among the most widely studied cation-exchange resin catalysts for acid-catalyzed esterification in biodiesel production, particularly for high-FFA feedstocks. Its popularity arises from the presence of strong sulfonic acid sites. Beyond biodiesel, A15 has also been used in esterification, etherification, condensation, and hydrolysis reactions, making it a versatile solid-acid catalyst. However, its practical use is limited by issues related to accessible surface area, diffusional resistance, and the thermal sensitivity of the sulfonated polymer framework.9,10 Mass transfer rate can be improved via various techniques, such as co-solvents and ultrasonication. For example, in the work of Bejarano-Alva et al. (2024),11 the ultrasound-intensified transesterification of refined palm olein (FFA 0.27%) and ethanol was synthesized under an anion exchange resin. High biodiesel yield was observed. Another piece of evidence was confirmed by the work of Khomane et al. (2024),12 which demonstrated ultrasound-assisted synthesis of methyl acrylate using A15 as a catalyst. 61.02% conversion was obtained. However, these methods may incur additional costs, which reduced their attractiveness.

A micro-scale fixed-bed reactor has been identified as a promising approach to enhance mass transfer rate without additional equipment or solvents. As implemented in our previous work,13 a microscale fixed-bed reactor with ion-exchange resin achieved superior biodiesel production performance (97% yield) compared to conventional reactors (60–92% yield). Unfortunately, the internal mass-transfer limitations due to the catalyst characteristics (diffusion) remained apparent.14 These limitations have motivated attempts to modify the catalyst to improve its performance.

Among the reported modification strategies, thermal treatment is one of the simplest approaches for tuning its physicochemical properties. Evidence from the literature15 shows that mild heating (<200 °C) can enhance catalyst accessibility and improve biodiesel performance while retaining the sulfonic acid groups, whereas excessive heating (>200 °C) leads to desulfonation and a severe loss of catalytic activity. For example, Gupta and Deo (2023)15 showed that preheating A15 at 200 °C improved catalyst performance by increasing surface area while preserving its sulfonic acid groups, thereby increasing biodiesel production from Karanja oil. By contrast, Boz et al. (2015)16 reported that heat treatment at temperatures of ≥220 °C reduced the active –SO3H surface and lowered biodiesel activity. Thus, thermal treatment alone is usually insufficient to achieve both high catalytic activity and long-term stability. Further post-treatment strategies, particularly acid-site regeneration, are therefore needed to recover acidity and improve catalyst performance. In addition, previous studies have mainly focused on conventional transesterification for biodiesel production, while the applicability of this technique to non-conventional biodiesel systems (glycerol-free processes) remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

A non-conventional biodiesel production route has been explored as a cost-effective route for future biodiesel production.17 Switching from a low-value byproduct (glycerol) to a higher-value one (such as triacetin, glyoxal, or glycerol carbonate) is a promising way to improve cost competitiveness. To achieve this, alternative acyl acceptors (dimethyl carbonate (DMC) and methyl acetate) are used instead of alcohol (methanol). Among these pathways, biodiesel production using DMC has gained attention as a green process because DMC is a non-toxic reagent and can be applied to renewable and bio-based substrates.18 In FFA-based DMC routes, glyoxal can be generated as a value-added by-product and serve as a useful chemical intermediate, further enhancing the process value.19 Hence, the development of this pathway was the focus of this work.

Overall, this study demonstrates the potential of catalyst modification (A15) as a practical and promising strategy for enhancing continuous glycerol-free biodiesel production from low-quality RBOFAD in a mini fixed-bed reactor using DMC as the acyl acceptor, while also providing a basis for evaluating further bifunctional catalyst development through zinc loading and benchmarking the production performance against previously reported systems. Moreover, the effects of operating variables, including reaction temperature, DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio, and residence time, were systematically studied and optimized using response surface methodology to maximize biodiesel content (% FAME). This work could offer a simple yet effective strategy to improve biodiesel productivity in continuous fixed-bed systems while retaining the advantages of cleaner byproduct formation and low-cost raw material utilization.

Materials and methods

Materials

RBOFAD was provided by Surin Bran Oil Company and used as received without further purification. The physicochemical properties of the raw materials were reported in our previous work.19 DMC (AR grade, 99%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Amberlyst 15 was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Ethanol (99.9%), HPLC-grade acetonitrile (99.9%), and acetone (99.8%) were purchased from RCI Labscan. Zinc acetate dihydrate (99.5%) was obtained from KEMAUS. Analytical standards, including glyoxal and fatty acid methyl ester (FAME; AOCS No. 6), were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich.

Biodiesel production

In this work, biodiesel synthesis was carried out in a mini fixed-bed reactor where the modified catalyst was packed. First, the RBOFAD was premixed with DMC at the desired DMC-to-oil weight ratio in a beaker to obtain a homogeneous substrate solution. The stream of RBOFAD (or refined RBO) and DMC was then introduced into a mini fixed-bed reactor via an HPLC pump at the desired residence time. The mini fixed-bed reactor was filled with 2 g of catalyst (A15/modified A15). Quartz wool was packed at the reactor inlet and outlet to prevent catalyst loss. The transesterification/esterification reactions took place only in the catalyst zone. This reactor was placed inside a convection oven to control the desired reaction temperature. The reactor outlet was connected to a back-pressure regulator to maintain a pressure of 6.8 MPa and prevent DMC vaporization. After exiting the reactor, the product was collected for product analysis with HPLC. The purity of biodiesel (% FAME) was used to evaluate the reaction performance. Schematic illustration of the continuous biodiesel production process from RBOFAD.

Product analysis

The biodiesel products obtained were analyzed for fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) content (% FAME) using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a refractive index (RI) detector. Separation was performed on a C18 column (ACE excel super C18; 250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm). The mobile phase consisted of acetone and acetonitrile (70 : 30 v/v) and was delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Standard compounds were used to determine retention times and assign detected components. Residual reactants and by-products were simultaneously monitored under the same analytical conditions.

Catalyst thermal treatment

A15 was thermally treated in a furnace at 180–280 °C for 4 h at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 to modify its textural properties. The treated catalysts were then characterized using various physicochemical techniques. The resulting thermally treated catalysts were denoted as TT-A15-X, where X represents the treatment temperature (180, 200, or 280 °C).

Catalyst acid-site regeneration

Acid-site regeneration was carried out by suspending the thermally treated A15 catalyst in 0.1 M H2SO4 solution under stirring at 250 rpm for 5 h. After treatment, the catalyst was separated by filtration, washed five times with 100 mL of deionized water, and dried at 105 °C for 2 h. The resulting catalysts were designated as TT-S-A15-X, where X denotes the treatment temperature (180, 200, or 280 °C).

Catalyst characterization

The morphology of the catalysts before and after modification was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), while surface elemental composition was analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy. The textural properties, including BET surface area, pore size, and pore volume, were determined from N2 adsorption/desorption measurements. Pore size and pore volume were calculated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method based on the desorption branch. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the surface functional groups, with spectra recorded over the range 500–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 and averaged over 32 scans. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to assess the catalyst's thermal stability at a nitrogen flow rate of 20 mL min−1 and a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from 50 to 500 °C. The acid density, corresponding to the concentration of accessible acid sites, was quantified by acid–base back-neutralization titration.

Results and discussion

Thermal behavior of A15

The thermal behavior of A15 was evaluated by TGA, and the corresponding profile is shown in Fig. 2. The TGA curve exhibited three distinct mass-loss regions, corresponding to the removal of adsorbed moisture, the decomposition of sulfonic acid groups, and the degradation of the polymeric backbone, consistent with previous reports on A15.20 The first mass-loss region, observed below 180 °C, was assigned to the release of physically adsorbed water and residual volatile species trapped within the resin matrix. This behavior is consistent with the hydrophilic nature of A15, arising from the presence of sulfonic acid groups (–SO3H), which strongly interact with moisture. The relatively limited mass loss in this region indicates that no significant structural degradation occurred below 180 °C. The second region, spanning 180–380 °C, corresponded mainly to the thermal decomposition of –SO3H groups, together with the onset of deterioration of the organic resin network. This stage is commonly associated with desulfonation, which directly reduces the density of Brønsted acid sites responsible for catalytic activity.21 A detailed examination of the weight-loss behavior in this range showed that the additional mass loss at 200 °C was still below 1 wt% relative to that at 180 °C, whereas at 220 °C it slightly exceeded 1 wt%. This observation suggests that the catalyst remained relatively stable up to about 200 °C, while decomposition of the acid functionality became more pronounced above this temperature. A similar trend has been reported previously, where the activity of A15 was found to change after heat treatment at temperatures of 220 °C and above due to the reduction of active –SO3H groups.16 At 380 °C, the cumulative mass loss reached approximately 33.5 wt% relative to that at 180 °C, confirming substantial degradation of the sulfonated resin structure. These findings indicate that the temperature window of 180 to 200 °C may be suitable for mild thermal treatment to modify textural properties while limiting excessive loss of active acid sites. A third, more severe mass-loss region was observed above 380 °C and was attributed primarily to the decomposition of the crosslinked polystyrene–divinylbenzene framework. In this region, degradation of the polymer backbone became dominant, leading to extensive structural collapse of the resin matrix. The total weight loss reached approximately 55 wt% at 500 °C, confirming severe destruction of the organic support at elevated temperature.

TGA profile of as-received A15.

Accordingly, the TGA analysis identifies 180–200 °C as a critical thermal window in which surface modification may be achievable with minimal destruction of the catalytically active sulfonic groups, whereas treatment at higher temperatures leads to irreversible degradation of both the acid functionality and polymer framework.

The FTIR results demonstrate that thermal treatment at 180–200 °C preserves most of the structural features of A15, albeit with a reduction in acid-site intensity, whereas treatment at 280 °C causes a pronounced loss of both the sulfonic acid functionality and the polymeric support structure.

The textural properties, sulfur content, and acid density of the as-received A15 and modified catalysts are summarized in Table 1. Thermal treatment markedly altered the physicochemical structure of A15. The BET surface area increased substantially from 33.1 m2 g−1 for the as-received A15 to 74.8 m2 g−1 after treatment at 200 °C (TT-A15-200), and further to 89.7 m2 g−1 at 280 °C (TT-A15-280). Such enhancement is attributed to the removal of adsorbed moisture and volatile species, as well as to thermal restructuring of the polystyrene–divinylbenzene framework, thereby increasing the accessibility of the internal porous network.24 A concomitant increase in pore volume (0.35–0.37 cm3 g−1) after thermal treatment was also observed, further supporting partial pore development and improved diffusion pathways within the resin structure. In contrast, the sulfur content and acid density decreased markedly with increasing treatment temperature, from 18.6 wt% and 4.3 mmol g−1 for the as-received A15 to 3.74 wt% and 0.8 mmol g−1 for TT-A15-280, respectively. This result confirms the progressive loss of sulfonic acid groups (corresponding to the loss of Brønsted acid sites) during thermal treatment. This behavior is consistent with the thermal degradation and desulfonation of sulfonated polystyrene-based cation-exchange resins (PS-DVB-type resins). These findings are consistent with the TGA and FTIR results, which confirmed the degradation of sulfonic functionalities and structural deterioration of the sulfonated polymer backbone at high temperature. The acidic functional groups lost during thermal treatment could be partially regenerated by subsequent acid-site regeneration, thereby restoring the catalyst's active acid sites.25

FE-SEM analysis was used to examine the surface morphology of the catalysts (Fig. 4). As-received A15 retained its characteristic bead-like morphology, and after thermal treatment, the particles still preserved their overall spherical shape, indicating that heating did not severely damage the macroscopic resin structure. However, the thermally treated samples exhibited a noticeably rougher surface, with more pronounced surface defects or cracks, suggesting that heating altered the resin's external texture. A similar trend has been reported for heat-treated A15, in which the catalyst surface changed from relatively smooth to increasingly cracked.16 This morphological change is consistent with the N2 adsorption–desorption results, which showed a higher BET surface area and greater pore accessibility after heating, indicating that thermal treatment modified the resin texture, improving access to the internal porous network.

Characterization of thermally treated A15 after acid-site regeneration (TT-S-A15)

After acid-site regeneration, TT-S-A15 was characterized by FTIR to evaluate the recovery of acidic functional groups. As shown in Fig. 5, the treatment increased the intensity of the band in the 1030–1050 cm−1 region, which is characteristic of sulfonic-group stretching in sulfonated styrenic resins.25 This result suggests the recovery or improved accessibility of sulfonic-acid-related functionalities after acid-site regeneration. The strengthened FTIR band, together with the increased acid density (Table 1), indicates that the treatment effectively restored the availability of Brønsted acid sites in TT-A15. The elemental sulfur contents summarized in Table 1 are also consistent with the FTIR results, as sulfonic functionalities in PS-DVB-type resins are closely associated with their ion-exchange capacity.26 In all cases, the sulfur content increased markedly after acid-site regeneration compared with the corresponding thermally treated samples, providing further evidence of the recovery of acid functionality. This improvement can be attributed to the enhanced surface area and pore accessibility of TT-A15, which may have facilitated the recovery or improved availability of sulfonic-acid-related Brønsted acid sites. Notably, TT-S-A15-180 exhibited the highest sulfur content and acid density, reaching 20.9 wt% and 4.8 mmol g−1, respectively. In contrast, both the sulfur content and acid density of TT-S-A15 decreased with increasing pretreatment temperature, indicating that harsher thermal treatment, particularly above 220 °C, reduced the resin matrix's ability to recover acid functionality effectively. This behavior is reasonable because PS-DVB-type resins are known to undergo progressive desulfonation and acid-site loss at elevated temperatures.27

Comparison of FTIR spectra of TT-A15 with and without acid-site regeneration at different treatment temperatures: (a) 180 °C, (b) 200 °C, and (c) 280 °C.

The textural properties also changed after acid-site regeneration. The BET surface areas of TT-S-A15-180, TT-S-A15-200, and TT-S-A15-280 were 79.6, 83.7, and 80.4 m2 g−1, respectively, which were substantially higher than that of the as-received A15 (Table 1). This indicates that the improved pore accessibility generated during thermal treatment was largely retained after acid-site regeneration. Compared with the corresponding TT-A15 catalysts, the BET surface area increased after acid-site regeneration for the samples pretreated at 180 and 200 °C, suggesting that the acid treatment may have further exposed accessible pore channels within the resin matrix. This behavior may be associated with swelling and rearrangement of the PS-DVB polymer network during acid treatment, thereby enhancing the accessibility of internal pores.25 In addition, the pore volumes of TT-S-A15-180 and TT-S-A15-200 increased to 0.48 and 0.43 cm3 g−1, respectively, further supporting improved pore accessibility after acid-site regeneration. The pore size of the TT-S-A15 catalysts decreased slightly to 30.8–31.5 nm compared with approximately 34 nm for the TT-A15 catalysts. This slight decrease may be related to the recovery or improved exposure of sulfonic-acid-related functionalities within the pore structure, which could partially narrow the accessible pore channels while still maintaining high overall pore accessibility. However, TT-S-A15-280 showed a lower BET surface area than TT-A15-280, suggesting that excessive thermal treatment at 280 °C may have caused irreversible shrinkage or partial collapse of the polymer network. Therefore, although acid-site regeneration improved the acid functionality and textural properties of the thermally treated catalysts, severe thermal treatment limited the resin matrix's ability to recover effectively. Based on the combined FTIR, sulfur content, acid density, and textural results, TT-S-A15-180 was selected for biodiesel production because it exhibited the highest regenerated acid density while maintaining substantially higher BET surface area and pore volume than the as-received A15.

Biodiesel production with as-received A15

Continuous biodiesel production from RBOFAD using DMC and A15 was first conducted in a mini fixed-bed reactor to establish a benchmark for subsequent catalyst modification. Experiments were performed at 120 °C using DMC : RBOFAD weight ratios of 1 : 3, 1 : 1, 3 : 1, and 5 : 1 and residence times between 35 and 103 min (Fig. 6). The results show that the FAME content increased rapidly up to 52 min and reached a maximum of 79% at a DMC : RBOFAD ratio of 1 : 1, after which further increases in residence time produced no appreciable improvement, indicating that the reaction approached a steady conversion limit under these operating conditions.28

% FAME obtained using as-received A15 as the catalyst.

The % FAME was slightly lower at higher DMC ratios. This behavior is attributed to a dilution effect at high acyl-acceptor levels, which reduces the effective concentration (activity) of fatty acids/glycerides at the catalytic sites and can inhibit the oil–catalyst interaction in continuous-flow systems.29 In addition, A15 is a solvent-responsive polystyrene–divinylbenzene resin that can swell in organic media. Under DMC-rich conditions, such swelling may reduce bed voidage and increase intraparticle diffusion resistance, while simultaneously lowering the local concentration of RBOFAD-derived reactants near the –SO3H active sites. These effects can aggravate transport limitations in the packed bed and contribute to the lower % FAME observed at high DMC fractions.21

Biodiesel production with TT-A15-180

TT-A15-180 was subsequently evaluated for continuous biodiesel production from RBOFAD using DMC in a mini fixed-bed reactor under the same operating window as the as-received A15 catalyst. As shown in Fig. 7, the effect of thermal treatment was more clearly observed under certain operating conditions. For example, at a DMC weight ratio of 3 : 1 and a residence time of 52 min, the % FAME increased from 72% for the as-received A15 to 75% for TT-A15-180. In contrast, under the optimal condition, at a DMC weight ratio of 1 : 1 and a residence time of 52 min, TT-A15-180 achieved 80% FAME, which was only slightly higher than that obtained with the as-received A15 catalyst (79%). This small difference indicates that both catalysts were approaching their maximum catalytic performance under the optimal condition, making the improvement from thermal treatment less pronounced. However, under less favorable conditions, TT-A15-180 showed slightly better performance, likely due to the enhanced surface area and improved pore accessibility after thermal treatment, which facilitated reactant diffusion and access to the remaining acid sites.

Comparison of % FAME obtained with different catalysts and residence times under DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratios of (a) 1 : 3, (b) 1 : 1, (c) 3 : 1, and (d) 5 : 1.

Although thermal treatment caused a partial reduction in sulfonic acid functionality (Table 1), the catalyst still maintained comparable or slightly improved activity. This suggests that catalytic performance in the present system was governed not only by the total density of Brønsted acid sites but also by their accessibility and utilization. In polymeric sulfonic acid resins, catalytic effectiveness is often strongly influenced by textural properties, pore accessibility, and diffusional constraints, particularly when bulky molecules are involved.26 In this study, thermal pretreatment at 180 °C likely improved the surface area and accessibility of the internal porous structure, thereby enhancing the utilization of the remaining –SO3H groups and alleviating mass-transfer limitations for RBOFAD-derived species. As a result, TT-A15-180 delivered slightly higher % FAME despite its lower sulfur content.

Biodiesel production with TT-S-A15-180

Continuous biodiesel production using TT-S-A15-180 was carried out under the same operating conditions as those used for the A15 and TT-A15-180, and the results are presented in Fig. 7. Across the investigated conditions, TT-S-A15-180 exhibited the highest catalytic performance, particularly at higher DMC-to-RBOFAD ratios and longer residence times. At a DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio of 1 : 1, TT-S-A15-180 achieved % FAME values of approximately 82.5, 84.8, and 85.5% at residence times of 35, 52, and 103 min, respectively, consistently outperforming both TT-A15-180 and A15. The enhancement became more evident under DMC-rich conditions, where TT-S-A15-180 produced % FAME values above 80% at a DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio of 3 : 1. The superior performance of TT-S-A15-180 relative to both A15 and TT-A15-180 can plausibly be attributed to the combined effects of (i) the recovery or improved availability of sulfonic-acid-related Brønsted acid sites, leading to higher total acidity while retaining the textural accessibility created by thermal pretreatment, (ii) improved effective utilization of acid sites because swelling and solvent sorption govern site accessibility in polymeric resins, and (iii) reduced intraparticle transport resistance for bulky RBOFAD-derived species due to more accessible pore pathways.30,31 Therefore, TT-S-A15-180 appears to provide the most favorable balance between acid site density and accessibility, leading to improved utilization of active sites and enhanced biodiesel productivity in the continuous fixed-bed system.

Effect of reaction temperatures on % FAME

Temperature is one of the most critical parameters in biodiesel production because it strongly affects both reaction kinetics and catalyst stability. In this study, the effect of temperature on % FAME was evaluated using TT-S-A15-180 over the range of 110–180 °C at a fixed DMC : RBOFAD weight ratio of 3 : 1, with residence times ranging from 21 to 52 min. As shown in Fig. 8, the highest % FAME (85.3%) was obtained at 120 °C, indicating that this temperature provided the most favorable balance between reaction rate and catalyst stability in the present system. The beneficial role of moderate temperature is consistent with previous fixed-bed studies using A15, in which % FAME increased as temperature rose within the resin's low-temperature operating range.32

Effect of reaction temperatures on % FAME.

Further increasing the temperature above 120 °C resulted in a gradual decline in % FAME, reaching 64.5% at 180 °C and 52 min. This trend suggests that the beneficial effect of faster intrinsic kinetics at elevated temperature was outweighed by catalyst deactivation under more severe conditions. A plausible explanation is the limited thermal stability of A15, in which elevated temperatures can promote partial degradation or loss of sulfonic acid groups, thereby reducing catalytic activity.33

In a continuous packed-bed system, these thermal effects may become more pronounced as residence time increases because the catalyst remains exposed to hot reacting media for longer. This interpretation is also in line with recent continuous esterification work, in which A-15 was limited to 120 °C, whereas a more thermally robust carbon-based solid acid catalyst could be operated at 150 °C with better time-on-stream performance.21 Accordingly, although elevated temperature generally accelerates esterification and transesterification reactions, the present results indicate that catalyst instability became the dominant factor above 120 °C. Therefore, from both performance and catalyst stability perspectives, the temperature range selected for subsequent optimization was narrowed to 110–130 °C, while the residence time range was set to 35–100 min.

Effect of Zn-doped TT-S-A15-180 on % FAME

To investigate the potential enhancement of catalytic performance by introducing Lewis acid functionality, Zn was loaded onto TT-S-A15-180 at 5–10 wt% and evaluated for biodiesel production. The presence and loading of Zn on the catalyst surface were confirmed by SEM-EDX analysis. Zinc was selected because Zn2+ can act as a Lewis acid and potentially activate the carbonyl (CO) group of fatty acids or ester intermediates,34,35 thereby increasing the electrophilicity of the carbon center and facilitating esterification/transesterification reactions. In this study, the reaction was tested at the previously optimized temperature of 120 °C under various DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratios (1 : 3 to 3 : 1) and residence times (35–103 min). Two Zn-containing catalysts, Zn/TT-A15-180 and Zn/TT-S-A15-180, were prepared, and their performance was compared with that of TT-S-A15-180.

As shown in Fig. 9, Zn incorporation did not improve biodiesel production performance. On the contrary, the Zn/TT-S-A15-180 catalyst containing 10 wt% Zn consistently gave lower % FAME than TT-S-A15-180 without Zn under all tested DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratios. The same negative trend was also observed when the Zn loading was reduced to 5 wt% (data not shown), although the decrease in performance was less pronounced. In the case of Zn/TT-A15-180, which contained a higher Zn content on the catalyst surface, the catalytic activity declined even more severely. These results clearly indicate that Zn loading had a detrimental effect on the catalytic system rather than promoting biodiesel formation. This behavior may plausibly arise from several possible factors. First, Zn2+ may interact strongly with or partially exchange with the sulfonic acid groups (–SO3H) in the A15 matrix, leading to a reduction in the density of Brønsted acid sites.36 This effect is particularly unfavorable because the DMC-based conversion of high-FFA feedstocks is expected to depend predominantly on Brønsted acidity, particularly during esterification of free fatty acids and in the overall acid-catalyzed conversion pathway. Therefore, partial neutralization or blockage of these acid sites would directly suppress catalytic performance. Second, Zn2+ may react with free fatty acids to form zinc carboxylate species, which could deposit on the catalyst surface and hinder access of reactants to the active sites.37 Such surface species may also contribute to diffusional resistance within the resin structure. Third, excessive Zn loading may alter the pore accessibility of the polymeric resin, thereby weakening mass transfer and reducing the effective utilization of internal active sites.30 This explanation is consistent with the more severe loss in activity observed for Zn/TT-A15-180, where the Zn content was higher.

Effect of 10 wt% Zn loading on the catalyst surface on % FAME at different DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratios; (a) 1 : 3, (b) 1 : 1, and (c) 3 : 1.

The present results therefore suggest that, for DMC-based biodiesel production from high-FFA feedstocks, preserving the Brønsted acidity of TT-S-A15-180 is more important than introducing additional Lewis acid sites through Zn loading. In other words, the catalytic function required in this system appears to be dominated by strong acid sites rather than by Lewis acid-assisted carbonyl activation. Within the Zn loading range investigated in this study, metal incorporation did not increase % FAME and instead adversely affected catalytic performance. Although further physicochemical characterization and mechanistic investigation would be needed to confirm the exact origin of this deactivation, such detailed analysis is beyond the scope of the present work. Nevertheless, these findings provide useful guidance for future catalyst design, indicating that Zn doping is not an effective strategy for improving TT-S-A15-180 in this reaction system.

Interaction effects of process variables on % FAME

The interaction effects of the DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio, residence time, and reaction temperature on biodiesel purity are presented in Fig. 10. The response surfaces reveal that % FAME was governed by a clear interplay among these variables rather than by the isolated effect of any single parameter, highlighting the coupled roles of reaction severity, reactant availability, and catalyst stability in this continuous DMC-based biodiesel system. This interpretation is consistent with previous biodiesel studies showing that alcohol/acyl-donor ratio, temperature, and reaction time act in a strongly coupled manner during esterification and transesterification.38

Interaction effects of two process variables on % FAME; (a) DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio and residence time, (b) temperature and residence time, and (c) DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio and temperature.

In Fig. 10a, the increase in % FAME with residence time was markedly more pronounced at higher DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratios, indicating a positive interaction between these two variables. This result suggests that excess DMC amplified the beneficial effect of longer residence time by providing a greater driving force toward biodiesel formation. The presence of excess DMC likely promoted more effective reactant contact and alleviated mass-transfer constraints, thereby allowing RBOFAD conversion to proceed more efficiently as residence time increased.39 Consequently, the gain in % FAME was substantially greater under DMC-rich conditions than at low DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratios.

The interaction between residence time and temperature shown in Fig. 10b further demonstrates that the influence of residence time depended strongly on thermal conditions. At low temperature, increasing residence time significantly improved % FAME, reflecting the need for longer reaction periods to achieve effective conversion under kinetically limited conditions.32 However, at higher temperatures, the beneficial effect of residence time diminished and became unfavorable with prolonged exposure. This trend suggests that although higher temperatures accelerated the reaction, excessive reaction severity, due to the combination of high temperatures and long residence times, may have reduced the overall benefit through progressive catalyst deactivation and reduced accessibility of the acid-active sites.33

A comparable interaction was observed between temperature and DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio in Fig. 10c. Simultaneous increases in these variables generally promoted % FAME, confirming that both kinetic enhancement and reactant excess contributed positively to biodiesel formation. Nevertheless, the response surface became flatter at the highest combined levels, indicating that once sufficient temperature and DMC excess were achieved, further intensification no longer yielded proportional improvement. This behavior suggests that the system had approached its practical optimum, and additional increases in temperature and DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio provided only limited benefit to biodiesel formation.14

Taken together, these results indicate that the optimum region for biodiesel production was located within an intermediate-to-high range of temperature, residence time, and DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio, where the positive effects of faster reaction kinetics and an enhanced reaction driving force were maximized without incurring excessive thermal or catalytic penalties. Therefore, the interaction analysis confirms that process optimization in this system must consider not only the magnitude of each variable, but also its combined effects on reaction performance and catalyst stability.

Catalyst stability

The stability of TT-S-A15-180 was preliminarily investigated in the continuous production of biodiesel from RBOFAD under the optimal conditions of 120 °C, a residence time of 60 min, and a DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio of 3 : 1 over a time-on-stream of 30 h. The effluent was collected every 2 h for % FAME analysis. The results showed that the % FAME remained nearly constant at approximately 87.5% throughout the experimental period, demonstrating the good operational stability of TT-S-A15-180. To further confirm the catalyst stability, the acid density of the used catalyst was also determined after the reaction. The acid density slightly decreased from 4.8 to 4.6 mmol g−1 after 30 h of time-on-stream. This minor decrease may be attributed to the partial loss of acid sites during the post-reaction washing step with an isopropanol/water (1 : 1 v/v) mixture, used to remove impurities deposited on the catalyst surface prior to analysis. A similar trend has been reported in our previous studies using related cation-exchange resin catalysts. For example, in biodiesel production via interesterification of refined palm oil with ethyl acetate over RCP160M, stable catalytic performance was observed for 72 h with nearly constant biodiesel content.13 In addition, Amberlyst-15 has been reported to maintain stable catalytic activity for more than 60 h in other applications.40 Therefore, these results indicate that TT-S-A15-180 exhibited good preliminary operational stability over 30 h in continuous glycerol-free biodiesel production from RBOFAD.

However, these advantages are offset by the need for high-pressure, high-temperature equipment, which increases capital cost, energy consumption, and operational safety concerns.

The second and third cases involved subcritical DMC systems operated under similar conditions but with different catalyst types. Conventional acid/base catalysts (no. 4–7) generally gave higher % FAME than solid heterogeneous catalysts (no. 8–10) and were effective at lower temperatures, typically below 100 °C. However, their performance strongly depended on the quality of the feedstock. As FFA content increased, biodiesel content decreased, and acid catalysts became necessary to promote esterification. In addition, conventional acid/base systems suffer from poor catalyst recovery and complex downstream separation, resulting in large amounts of wastewater generated.

In contrast, solid heterogeneous catalysts offer clear advantages in catalyst separation and reuse but typically require higher temperatures (>100 °C) due to mass-transfer limitations and limited access to active sites. A similar decline in performance was observed as FFA content increased, highlighting the difficulty of efficiently processing low-quality feedstocks. Compared with these systems, the modified A15 catalyst developed in this work improved catalytic performance and reduced the reaction time to 60 min under continuous-flow operation, while giving % FAME comparable to that of a homogeneous catalytic system (no. 7). Nevertheless, the obtained % FAME remained lower than values reported for feedstocks with lower FFA content.

These results indicate that the modified A15 catalyst (TT-S-A15-180) offers a promising balance between catalytic activity and process practicality under subcritical DMC conditions. The process offers several practical advantages, including relatively mild operating conditions and easier catalyst separation owing to the catalyst's heterogeneous nature. However, the overall environmental benefit of this approach should be further validated through life cycle assessment, considering key factors such as feedstock source, DMC consumption, catalyst modification, energy input, and downstream purification.49 Further improvement is also required, particularly through catalyst development to enhance simultaneous esterification and transesterification of high-FFA feedstocks.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates that A15 can be effectively engineered to enable continuous biodiesel production from high-FFA feedstocks, offering a practical alternative to supercritical processing. Thermal treatment at 180 °C (TT-A15-180) improved the catalyst's textural properties, increasing the surface area by 48.6%. However, excessive thermal treatment led to a 44.2% reduction in acid density relative to the as-received A15, indicating a trade-off between structural enhancement and acid-site preservation. To address this limitation, acid-site regeneration was introduced after thermal treatment to restore catalyst acidity while retaining the structural benefits of thermal modification. Among the investigated catalysts, TT-S-A15-180 provided the best balance between surface area and acid functionality, with a BET surface area of 79.6 m2 g−1 and an acid density of 4.8 mmol g−1. Compared with the as-received A15, this catalyst exhibited increases of 140.5% in BET surface area, 12.3% in sulfur content, and 11.6% in acid density, confirming successful regeneration of sulfonic acid sites and improved textural properties.

In continuous-flow biodiesel production from RBOFAD using DMC, TT-S-A15-180 achieved the highest % FAME of 87.5% under the optimal conditions of 120 °C, a residence time of 60 min, and a DMC-to-RBOFAD weight ratio of 3 : 1. The catalyst also exhibited good operational stability over 30 h of time-on-stream. Its catalytic performance was comparable to that of homogeneous catalytic systems, while avoiding the severe operating conditions required for supercritical processes. Moreover, as a heterogeneous catalyst, TT-S-A15-180 offers practical advantages, including easier separation and potential reusability. These findings highlight the strong potential of the modified A15 catalyst for valorizing low-quality, high-FFA feedstocks under relatively mild continuous-flow conditions.

Taken together, this study demonstrates that catalyst modification combined with acid-site regeneration is an effective strategy for expanding the applicability of A15 in continuous biodiesel production. Further improvements should focus on enhancing the catalyst's ability to promote both esterification and transesterification reactions simultaneously. In particular, the use of suitable co-reactants and/or co-catalysts may further improve process efficiency and advance biodiesel production from challenging low-quality, high-FFA feedstocks.
