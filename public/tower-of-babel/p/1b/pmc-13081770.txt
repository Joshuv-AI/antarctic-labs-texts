Diagnosis of Mucormycosis: Current Situation, Challenges and Future Prospects
Authors: Yixuan Qu, Yuling Xiao, Lina Liu, Junyan Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMucormycosis, an aggressive fungal infection caused by members of the order Mucorales, progresses rapidly and is associated with high mortality, particularly in immunocompromised hosts such as patients with uncontrolled diabetes, transplant recipients or those with COVID‐19‐associated immunosuppression. Early diagnosis remains challenging with current clinical methods, yet it is essential to reduce mortality. This review examines the evolution of diagnostic strategies for mucormycosis, ranging from conventional techniques such as histopathology, culture and microscopy to advanced and emerging methods including molecular assays, serological testing, imaging and metabolomics. We also explore the ongoing transition towards integrated, rapid and non‐invasive diagnostic platforms that leverage novel biomarkers, portable devices and artificial intelligence. These new technologies have the potential to facilitate early diagnosis, thereby enabling early treatment and reducing the mortality rate of mucormycosis.

Introduction

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, a marked increase in the global incidence of mucormycosis was reported [1]. This aggressive opportunistic infection, caused by fungi of the order Mucorales, progresses rapidly and is associated with high mortality, a pooled mortality rate of 48.8%, attributable largely to delays in diagnosis [2, 3]. However, current diagnostic methods remain suboptimal for early detection. Fungal cultures often perform poorly on standard media, and histopathological identification in scant tissue samples requires specialised expertise [4]. Although novel rapid detection techniques are moderately recommended in guidelines, they lack standardisation [5, 6]. To address these challenges, this study summarised the advances in existing and emerging diagnostic technologies and explored their potential and limitations in clinical applications, aiming to improve early diagnosis of mucormycosis. However, due to the rarity of this disease, high‐quality studies are limited and most evidence derives from case reports. Although these limitations restrict the universality of the research results, the existing evidence still provides valuable clinical insights.

Literature Search Strategy

We searched PubMed and Web of Science for literature on the diagnosis of mucormycosis published from January 2000 to July 2025, using MeSH terms and keywords related to imaging, pathogen detection, molecular diagnostics, serology and artificial intelligence. Reference lists of key reviews and guidelines were manually searched.

The two authors independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts, with any disagreements resolved by a third author. Inclusion criteria were: (1) studies focused on mucormycosis diagnosis; (2) studies addressing conventional or emerging diagnostic techniques, including original research (cohort, case–control, case series, case reports), clinical guidelines, systematic reviews and meta‐analyses with complete clinical and methodological data; (3) English‐language articles. Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies only covering pathogenesis, treatment or epidemiology without diagnostic data; (2) preclinical animal or in vitro studies; (3) duplicate or non‐English publications; (4) studies with incomplete data or unclear diagnostic criteria; (5) studies lacking reliable clinical insights. Given the limited availability of high‐quality large‐sample studies on mucormycosis, according to our criteria, we prioritised studies with clear diagnostic standards and synthesised findings according to evidence levels to ensure objective conclusions.

Clinical Diagnosis

Diagnostic Classification

Current diagnostic criteria for mucormycosis incorporate a four‐tiered framework based on updated consensus guidelines and clinical practice: (1) risk factors; (2) clinical manifestations; (3) microbiological evidence; and (4) histopathological confirmation. Diagnostic classifications are defined as follows: possible mucormycosis (1 + 2), probable mucormycosis (1 + 2 + 3) and confirmed mucormycosis (1 + 2 + 3 + 4) [5, 6, 7], as summarised in Figure 1.

Risk Factors

The pathogenesis of mucormycosis is closely associated with the host's immune status and underlying comorbidities, which must be considered in clinical diagnosis. Poorly controlled diabetes, particularly when complicated by ketoacidosis, is the predominant risk factor, promoting infection through hyperglycemia‐induced neutrophil dysfunction [8]. Other high‐risk groups include recipients of haematopoietic stem cell or solid organ transplants [9, 10], patients on long‐term corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy [11] and those with haematological malignancies [12]. Notably, COVID‐19 has become a major risk factor, leading to a surge in mucormycosis cases that may be driven by virus‐induced immune dysregulation [13].

Clinical Manifestations

In immunocompromised patients, pulmonary mucormycosis (PM) is the most common presentation. Rhino‐orbital‐cerebral mucormycosis (ROCM) typically affects individuals with diabetes mellitus, whereas cutaneous and soft tissue infections are more frequent in immunocompetent hosts [5]. ROCM commonly presents with facial edema, pain, nasal congestion, ocular symptoms and palatal necrosis, while PM is characterised by cough, dyspnea and chest pain [14]. Disseminated mucormycosis often involves systemic fever and site‐specific dysfunction [15]. Cutaneous and soft tissue infections manifest as edema, necrotic ulcers with crusting or necrotising lesions [16]. Gastrointestinal involvement differs by age. Adults typically present with abdominal distension, fever, hemorrhagic symptoms such as hematemesis, melena or hematochezia and acute abdomen secondary to perforation. Neonates and children most frequently exhibit vomiting, fever and marked abdominal distension [17]. Renal mucormycosis commonly presents with fever, oliguria, hematuria and flank pain [18]. Liver mucormycosis presents with fever and abdominal pain [19], and may present as Budd‐Chiari syndrome or veno‐occlusive disease [20].

Imaging

Computed Tomography

CT is rapid and widely accessible. In resource‐limited settings where MRI is unavailable, CT can serve as a valuable first‐line diagnostic tool [21, 22, 23, 24]. Common CT findings in ROCM include bone erosion, hard palate destruction, hyperdense sinus contents and orbital or vascular invasion, with intrasinus gas observed notably more frequently than in other infections [25]. However, these features are not specific, as they may also be present in acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis caused by other fungi [26, 27]. In contrast, loss of contrast enhancement on CT or MRI reflects fungal vascular invasion and tissue ischemia, such as the ‘black turbinate sign’, a finding classically associated with mucormycosis [28]. Furthermore, while CT offers detailed bone information for surgical planning, it is less effective than MRI, specifically for identifying vascular invasion, perineural spread, cavernous sinus involvement or tissue necrosis [29]. PM typically presents on CT as consolidation, frequently with the reversed halo sign (RHS), which is more common than in invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) (47.1% vs. 11.7%; p < 0.01) [30]. The PM lesions are usually larger than those of IPA (median 5.0 cm vs. 3.0 cm; p < 0.01) [30] and often characterised by a large perilesional ground‐glass halo (53%), peripheral distribution (87%) and occasional perivascular ground‐glass precursors preceding nodular lesions by 1–2 weeks (20%) [31]. In contrast, the air crescent sign is present in 13% of the PM patients, which was not significantly different from that of IPA (13% vs. 6%; p = 0.38), limiting its specificity [32]. Moreover, the airway‐invasive features of PM were significantly less (29.4% vs. 56.7%; p = 0.01) [30].

Cases of mucormycosis involving other organs are extremely rare, and their CT manifestations have been reported to be diverse. Gastrointestinal involvement may include gastric ulcers, perforation, bowel ischemia, obstruction, wall edema, venous engorgement and mesenteric infiltration [33, 34]. Hepatic infection can appear as mass‐like infarcted lesions or abscesses [20, 35], renal disease as bilateral nephromegaly with infarcts and thrombosis [36] and splenic involvement as patchy hypodense areas with wedge‐shaped changes [37]. Although CT findings may provide important clues and guide biopsy, they are non‐specific and require microbiological or histopathological confirmation.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

MRI offers superior soft tissue resolution compared to CT, making it the preferred modality for suspected intracranial or intraorbital involvement [5]. However, its clinical utility is constrained by high cost, longer acquisition time and the requirement for specialised expertise [38]. In patients with ROCM, MRI shows features such as the ‘black turbinate sign’ and large‐vessel occlusion. In patients with COVID‐19, common findings include thickening of the nasal mucosa or gas–fluid levels; some patients show invasion of the periorbital soft tissue and bone destruction, while a few present with involvement of the brain parenchyma or optic nerve [39]. By quantifying bone marrow edema, sinus‐intracranial invasion and soft tissue extension, an MRI‐based scoring system was established to evaluate the severity of ROCM lesions [39]. Additionally, a four‐stage classification system categorises the regional spread of mucormycosis to predict mortality risk, ranging from localised sinus involvement to extensive intracranial extension [40]. The MRI findings of rare clinical types of mucormycosis mainly come from case reports or small series. Hepatic mucormycosis on MRI often presents as heterogeneous lesions with intralesional fluid levels and peripheral rim enhancement [19]. Renal involvement is characterised by nephromegaly, hydronephrosis and pyelonephritis‐like features [41].

Endoscopy

Endoscopy is strongly recommended for patients with suspected sinonasal or pulmonary mucormycosis, as it enables direct visualisation of mucosal abnormalities and obtaining tissue specimens or secretions for further analysis [5]. Bronchoscopy detects endobronchial masses, mucosal abnormalities and stenosis [42]. When bronchoscopy is nondiagnostic, thoracoscopic pleural biopsy can be considered [43]. Endobronchial ultrasound‐guided aspiration and CT‐guided FNA improve diagnostic yield compared to conventional bronchoscopy [44, 45]. Compared to bronchoscopy, transbronchial lung cryobiopsy can obtain larger specimens by cryotechnology for precise diagnosis [46]. Nasal endoscopy facilitates minimally invasive mucosal sampling [26]. In the lower gastrointestinal tract, emergency gastroscopy enables rapid localisation of bleeding sites [47] and colonoscopy may reveal fistulas with whitish secretions [48]. Despite its diagnostic advantages, endoscopy has certain limitations. Its invasiveness, as well as the requirement for specialised equipment and trained personnel, restricts its widespread use, especially in resource‐limited settings [49].

Ultrasonography

Ultrasound is not the main primary diagnostic tool in current guidelines, but it can be used for initial screening and biopsy guidance [5, 6]. Compared to CT and MRI, its lower cost and wider availability help to narrow the accessibility gap in LMICs. A global survey found that 88% of healthcare providers in LMICs have access to ultrasound equipment [50]. Sonographic findings such as hepatic artery stenosis may raise suspicion of mucormycosis [51]. The ultrasound features of liver mucormycosis also include an irregular, extensive parenchymal area with increased echogenicity, indicating multiple liver abscesses [52]. It has been reported that the ultrasound manifestations of renal mucormycosis are bilateral renal hypertrophy accompanied by complete loss of vascular perfusion signals [53].

Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography

Although not a primary diagnostic method [5, 6], 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F‐FDG PET/CT) demonstrates high diagnostic value in mucormycosis by identifying characteristic metabolic patterns, including peripheral hypermetabolism with central photopenia in fungus balls, thereby enabling localisation of deep infections and assessment of treatment response [54]. In patients with ROCM, the uptake of FDG may be increased in bone and soft tissue, including the sinus and orbital regions [54]. A prospective study involving 47 patients reported that PET/CT detected metabolically active disease in 35 patients, among which 6 cases (12.8%) of the lesions could not be well visualised on conventional MRI or CT, indicating that PET/CT has a higher sensitivity in identifying occult lesions [55]. However, for other clinical types, evidence remains limited to individual case reports describing PET/CT findings in gastrointestinal, femoral and disseminated mucormycosis [56, 57]. Notably, 18F‐FDG‐labelled autologous leukocyte PET/CT has been recently described as a potential technique for mapping of occult fungal infections [58]. However, FDG uptake is nonspecific and must be differentiated from increased metabolic activity due to malignancies [59], other invasive fungal infections [60] or post‐radiotherapy inflammation [61]. Further limitations include high cost and limited availability [62].

Pathogen Detection

Direct Microscopy

For the rapid diagnosis of mucormycosis, potassium hydroxide (KOH) wet mount microscopy remains the primary method. Supplementation with fluorescent brighteners such as Calcofluor white is recommended to improve fungal visualisation under fluorescence microscopy [5, 16, 63]. Staining techniques, including May‐Grünwald‐Giemsa and Papanicolaou, can be integrated with KOH preparations to enhance sensitivity [64]. Under the microscope, Mucorales exhibit wide, ribbon‐like, non‐septate or pauci‐septate hyphae with right‐angle or irregular branching, unlike Aspergillus and other filamentous fungi. Emerging technologies, such as ex vivo confocal microscopy, can examine specimens with simple processing [65], possessing advantages such as rapid diagnosis and high biological safety. However, all microscopic methods can only provide morphological evidence and carry a risk of misdiagnosis, particularly in non‐sterile samples [66].

Culture

Fungal culture serves as a crucial component in pathogen diagnostics, enabling species‐level identification and antifungal susceptibility profiling [6]. The Sabouraud dextrose agar and potato dextrose agar are preferred for Mucorales culture [67], avoiding tissue homogenisation and suggesting separate cultivation at 30°C and 37°C [5, 6]. Despite being considered a gold standard, fungal culture is limited by the long culture time and the operator, which delays the early diagnosis [68, 69]. Of note, characteristic hyphae identified microscopically in sterile‐site specimens can still be significant even if cultures yield negative results [70]. To improve the speed and accuracy of identification, the culture can be combined with MALDI‐TOF MS, although the limitations of the database may lead to false negatives [71, 72].

Histopathology

While fungal culture enables species identification and antifungal susceptibility testing, histopathological examination plays an equally crucial role in the diagnosis of mucormycosis, typically involving microscopic evaluation of tissue biopsies or cytological specimens [73]. Direct KOH wet mount serves as a rapid initial assessment tool, enabling prompt detection of fungal elements. For more reliable identification and detailed morphological characterisation, various staining techniques are employed to enhance fungal visualisation. Commonly used stains include haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) and Grocott's methenamine silver (GMS) [5, 6]. PAS staining provides clear delineation of fungal structures and offers certain diagnostic value [73], whereas GMS staining has relatively low sensitivity for Mucorales [74]. Lactophenol cotton blue can also be used to highlight spores [75], and Gram stain, though not routinely recommended, may assist in narrowing the differential diagnosis during preliminary assessment [76]. In addition to conventional staining, intraoperative frozen section analysis offers a rapid alternative, with high specificity for diagnosing invasive fungal rhinosinusitis but only moderate sensitivity (83.4%). Its sensitivity for mucormycosis (75.4%) is notably lower than that for aspergillosis [77].

Despite its essential role, histopathology alone has notable limitations. The morphological features of Mucorales include broad, ribbon‐like, pauci‐septate hyphae with right‐angle branching. These characteristics can overlap with those of other angioinvasive moulds, such as Aspergillus, making definitive genus‐level identification challenging [68, 78]. Moreover, diagnostic accuracy is highly dependent on sample quality, fungal burden and the pathologist's experience [73]. Histopathology alone has a reported sensitivity of only 20% for diagnosing mucormycosis [4], and the positivity rate of Mucorales in culture is similarly low [68]. These constraints highlight the value of integrating multiple diagnostic modalities.

The diagnostic value of histopathology is particularly evident when culture results are negative, as characteristic hyphae observed in sterile‐site specimens retain diagnostic significance even in the absence of culture confirmation [70]. Integrating histopathology with fungal culture may therefore improve diagnostic yield [4], and this complementary relationship is further supported by distinct histopathological patterns that correlate with culture outcomes. Specifically, culture positivity is associated with high fungal burden and extensive tissue necrosis, whereas culture negativity tends to correlate with a higher degree of vascular invasion. Although the overall extent of tissue invasion was comparable between groups, minimal invasion was more frequently observed in culture‐negative cases [79]. Given these inherent limitations and complementary strengths, the integration of histopathology and fungal culture represents a valuable diagnostic strategy.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) using specific antibodies has emerged as a valuable adjunctive tool. The commercially available anti‐Rhizopus monoclonal antibody (WSSA‐RA‐1, LSBio) enables specific detection of mucormycetes via DAB chromogenic deposition with no cross‐reactivity against Aspergillus species, showing 100% sensitivity and specificity for mucormycosis in a study of culture‐confirmed cases (n = 13 mucormycosis, n = 20 aspergillosis) and demonstrating excellent agreement with culture results [80]. Histomorphologic assessment also showed high agreement with IHC and culture, with 97% concordance in morphologically diagnosed mucormycosis [81]. However, the sensitivity of IHC may vary with antibody selection and tissue processing. A study reported a lower sensitivity of 41.18% in formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissues using the anti‐Rhizopus antibody [82]. In a case report, the direct immunofluorescence analysis of skin biopsy specimens reveals smooth IgG, weak IgA, faint IgM and C3 deposition along hyphal surfaces, effectively distinguishing fungal invasion from vasculitis [83]. Although these findings are promising, they still need to be further validated in different clinical settings.

Serology

There are currently no clinically available serological tests for diagnosis of mucormycosis; however, several promising assays are in development. Novel ELISA techniques show promise for early detection of Rhizopus‐specific antigen [84] or anti‐Rhizopus IgG/IgM antibodies, with IgG offering higher sensitivity for ROCM and PM [85] Moreover, both assays show no cross‐reactivity with Aspergillus or Candida, demonstrating their specificity. Emerging flow cytometric quantification of mould‐reactive T cell subsets has demonstrated higher sensitivity and specificity than established diagnostics in a prospective cohort and has effectively differentiated Aspergillus from Mucorales [86]. Although these approaches are non‐invasive and rapid, more data are still needed.

Molecular Detection

Quantitative PCR

Compared with conventional PCR, real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR) offers high sensitivity for detecting degraded DNA in FFPE tissues, faster turnaround with real‐time monitoring and reduced contamination risk through closed‐tube amplification [87]. The ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region is recommended as the gold standard for species‐level identification of Mucorales, as it exhibits high interspecies variability and multiplicity of copies within the rDNA gene cluster [6]. Therefore, multiplex qPCR assays can simultaneously detect multiple pathogens and identify mixed infections that might be missed by histopathology or conventional PCR [87]. Additional targets such as the rnl gene offer a detection limit as low as 1 pg [88], and assays for the multicopy cytochrome b gene demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity in cultured samples [89]. Alternative molecular markers further enhance diagnostic performance, including probes targeting 18S or 28S rDNA that significantly reduce turnaround time [90], as well as TaqMan MGB probe‐based qPCR against 28S rRNA for RNA‐level quantification [91].

A recent meta‐analysis not only confirmed the high diagnostic performance of qPCR for mucormycosis but also advocated the inclusion of PCR detection of free DNA in blood, BALF and tissues into updated diagnostic definitions and guidelines [92]. The types of sample also affect the performance of qPCR. The diagnostic efficacy is the highest in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), with a sensitivity of 97.5% and a specificity of 95.8%, followed by fresh tissue and blood samples. In FFPE tissues, there is high specificity (96.4%), but the sensitivity is lower (73.0%) [92]. Several commercial kits, including MucorGenius, MycoGENIE and Fungiplex, are not yet available in many countries. qPCR is rapid and has high sensitivity and specificity; however, its cost and requirement for equipment limit its widespread use in resource‐limited settings [93, 94, 95, 96, 97].

Emerging and Adjunctive Molecular Techniques

Beyond qPCR, several molecular techniques have shown diagnostic potential but they are still in the stages of clinical validation.

Conventional PCR and its variants serve as complementary tools, particularly when qPCR is unavailable or yields negative results [98]. Studies have explored various targets and formats, including FTR1‐based PCR [99], CotH gene detection in urine and plasma [100], rnl gene [101], semi‐nested PCR targeting 18S rDNA/ITS regions [102], PCR‐restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR‐RFLP) [103, 104] and PCR with melting curve analysis [105], digital high‐resolution melt (dHRM) [106] and PCR coupled with electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (PCR/ESI‐MS) [107, 108]. While these assays demonstrate high sensitivity, there is still a considerable distance to clinical application.

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive pathogen detection in culture‐negative or diagnostically challenging cases. Metagenomic NGS (mNGS) enables rapid detection in various samples, including plasma, BALF, CSF, hydrothorax and peripheral blood, especially in patients with negative results from conventional methods [109]. Despite declining sequencing costs, the high expense of mNGS remains a barrier to clinical application [110]. Targeted NGS (tNGS) demonstrates higher sensitivity than Sanger sequencing in formalin‐fixed tissues, compensating for the limitations of traditional methods [111]. However, the evidence mainly comes from single‐centre studies. Although NGS is currently not applicable for diagnosing mucormycosis, it can provide important clues for the early diagnosis of this disease.

Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) targets ribosomal RNA in tissue samples, but its utility is limited by technical complexity and the risk of false‐negative results [82, 112]. It may serve as a complementary tool for species identification when culture and PCR are inconclusive, but further optimisation and validation are required.

Isothermal amplification techniques, such as loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) and rolling circle amplification (RCA), enable nucleic acid amplification at constant temperature, reducing operational complexity and cost, an advantage for resource‐limited settings. A novel magnetic nanoparticle‐based LAMP assay targeting the CotH gene showed > 83% sensitivity in blood and urine samples from 30 patients [113]. RPA enables diagnosis within minutes [88], and RCA has been evaluated on reference strains of six common Mucorales species [114]. However, the existing data are mainly based on laboratory evaluations or small clinical cohorts and further validation is needed before clinical application.

Future Prospects

The future of diagnostic development lies in accessible, integrated, rapid and non‐invasive platforms, including novel biomarkers, advanced molecular platforms, portable diagnostic devices and artificial intelligence. Although these innovations have not yet been applied in clinical settings, they hold potential for early, rapid and non‐invasive diagnosis, as shown in Figure 3.

Novel Biomarkers

Serological biomarkers for mucormycosis hold considerable potential for early, non‐invasive diagnosis. The TG11 monoclonal antibody specifically binds to Mucorales hyphal walls without cross‐reacting with Aspergillus fumigatus in murine lungs, suggesting potential for early detection [115]. Furthermore, preliminary studies have shown that species‐specific volatile organic compounds (VOCs) detected through breath analysis may enable non‐invasive differentiation between mucormycosis and aspergillosis. Distinct sesquiterpene profiles have been identified in exhaled breath from murine models and five patients infected with Mucorales species such as Rhizopus microsporus [116]. These findings indicate that VOC characteristics can distinguish between these infections and potentially reflect treatment response, as concentrations were observed to decrease following antifungal therapy [116]. The emerging biosensor technologies, including electronic nose systems, have demonstrated the ability to detect fungal‐specific VOC profiles in conditions such as invasive aspergillosis in proof‐of‐concept studies [117]. If successfully applied to Mucorales, this method may facilitate early diagnosis, but the diagnostic accuracy may be affected by confounding factors, including diet, smoking and co‐infections [118].

Future Developments in Molecular Diagnostics

Emerging molecular diagnostic platforms show potential for improving early detection of mucormycosis. Digital PCR platforms, such as those based on microfluidic chip‐based and droplet‐based systems, separate samples through nanoliter chambers and water‐in‐oil droplets with optical detection, respectively [119], surpass qPCR in accurately quantifying trace targets without standard curves [120]. Nanopore sequencing offers performance comparable to mNGS with a streamlined workflow and shorter turnaround times than blood culture, particularly for detecting Candida and fastidious pathogens [121, 122]. Furthermore, the FilmArray Blood Culture Identification 2 panel (BCID2; bioMérieux), a fully automated PCR‐based assay, can identify pathogens and markers in positive blood cultures within 1 h [123]. These technologies can also be attempted to detect Mucorales‐specific markers such as CotH for early diagnosis.

Portable Diagnostic Devices

Emerging portable diagnostic methods also have the potential to achieve early diagnosis of mucormycosis through rapid, point‐of‐care testing. A new lateral‐flow device (LFD) that targets and binds to extracellular polysaccharide antigens through an IgG1 monoclonal antibody can enable rapid detection of mucormycosis caused by Rhizopus arrhizus [124]. Another LFIA detects a conserved α‐1,6‐mannan epitope recognised by the 2DA6 monoclonal antibody, broadly detecting fungi of Ascomycota and Zygomycota [125]. However, its clinical sensitivity and specificity remain unclear and the antibody lacks specificity due to cross‐reactivity with conserved α‐1,6‐mannan epitopes in other pathogenic yeasts and moulds. Recently, a murine IgG2b monoclonal antibody (TG11) targeting the extracellular polysaccharide antigen of Mucorales was developed and incorporated into a competitive lateral‐flow device (TG11‐LFD). This study has shown that it has no cross‐reactivity with other clinically relevant fungi, such as Aspergillus, Candida, Cryptococcus, Fusarium or Scedosporium species [126]. A subsequent multicentre retrospective study evaluating its performance demonstrated a sensitivity of 76.92% and a specificity of 75.51%, with a negative predictive value of 92.5% [127]. These findings suggest that TG11‐LFD may be a promising rapid diagnostic tool, particularly in resource‐limited settings [127]. In addition, CRISPR‐based systems such as CRISPR‐Cas12a facilitate highly specific identification of pathogens and can distinguish single‐nucleotide polymorphisms more effectively than qPCR [128]. Low‐cost biosensors and microfluidic platforms improve point‐of‐care applicability, including electrochemical impedance sensors capable of detection within 1 h [129] and μReaCH‐PAD systems that integrate RT‐RAA with CRISPR‐Cas12a for the identification of Candida/Aspergillus species [130]. However, these technologies are still in the laboratory stage and require further clinical validation.

Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence research in the diagnosis of mucormycosis is still at the proof‐of‐concept stage, but it has certain application prospects in the imaging, histopathology and molecular testing for early diagnosis. In the imaging field, the deep learning models for the automated detection of mucormycosis in paranasal CT images were validated in a single centre with 794 CT images, achieving 100% accuracy on internal test sets and a mean five‐fold cross‐validation accuracy of 98.9% [131]. A multicentre CT segmentation model for pulmonary mucormycosis using a 3D U‐Net architecture achieved an internal validation Dice coefficient of 93.38% and an external validation Dice coefficient of 77.53%, suggesting preliminary cross‐centre generalisability [132]. A U‐Net model applied to whole‐slide images for detecting fungal elements in nail specimens demonstrated sensitivity (90.5%) comparable to that of 11 board‐certified dermatopathologists (89.2%), though false positives due to staining artifacts and focal plane shifts remain a concern [133]. A hybrid learning classifier based on ocular photographs reported an accuracy of 99.5%, but it relies on a small, non‐public dataset and lacks gold‐standard comparators, which limits its reliability [134]. In histopathology, an AI recognition system utilising hyphal branching angle and tortuosity enabled differentiation between Mucorales and Aspergillus, with two‐dimensional scatter plot analysis of 147 Mucorales and 67 Aspergillus image samples showing clear separation between the two genera [135]. In molecular diagnostics, a machine learning‐based approach for interpreting qPCR curves extracted 14 features from amplification and melting curves and reduced indeterminate results from 14.8% to near zero, with six meta‐classifiers achieving perfect agreement on external validation [136].

However, local deployment of AI systems demands substantial computational resources and specialised maintenance, far exceeding those typically available in public hospitals [137]. These findings, while promising, should be interpreted with caution given that most studies are preliminary, with limited sample sizes and a lack of prospective external validation. Nevertheless, AI still holds the potential to serve as a rapid and accessible auxiliary tool for early diagnosis of mucormycosis.

Conclusion

The early diagnosis of mucormycosis remains challenging. Histopathology and culture are still the gold standards, but their limited sensitivity and long turnaround time can lead to missed diagnoses and delayed diagnoses. Mucorales‐specific qPCR assays testing has clinical application value, and commercial kits are now available in some countries. Digital PCR, CRISPR‐based detection platforms, novel biomarkers, portable diagnostic devices and artificial intelligence are still in the research stage and may become new tools for the diagnosis of mucormycosis in the future.
