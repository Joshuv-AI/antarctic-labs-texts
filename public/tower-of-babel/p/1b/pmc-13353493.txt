Neonatal genetic sequencing as a first-tier option: a real-world clinical implementation study in Northern China
Authors: Jialin Mu, Meng Sun, Yulin Li, Panpan Li, Hui Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: To evaluate real-world implementation of newborn genetic screening (NBGS) in terms of positivity rate, carrier frequency, and diagnostic accuracy for inherited metabolic disorders (IMDs), and to explore feasibility and challenges in regional clinical application.Methods: This study enrolled 1590 newborns (August 2023–November 2024) whose parents opted for NBGS. A targeted sequencing panel covering 465 genes for 596 diseases alongside traditional biochemical screening for 46 disorders were performed. Variants were classified per ACMG guidelines. Positive cases were recalled for confirmatory Sanger sequencing and auxiliary biochemical tests. We calculated uptake, carrier frequency, identified hotspot variants, and compared allele frequencies with gnomAD_EAS. Diagnostic performance was compared with biochemical screening.Results: Uptake was 10.41% (1590/15,272). Overall positivity was 7.74% (123/1590). Among these, 10 were positive for IMDs‑related genes, with 8 confirmed, giving a positive predictive value (PPV) of 80.00% for IMDs – significantly higher than biochemical screening’s 5.71%. We detected 2,354 variants, with carrier frequency 70.94%. Hotspot IMDs variants included MMACHC c.609G>A, c.658_660del, and MUT c.1286A>G, whose minor allele frequencies differed from gnomAD, indicating regional specificity. High carrier rates were also seen for lysosomal storage genes (GALC c.1901T>C, 53.57%; c.2041G>A, 32.14%).Conclusion: NBGS offers high PPV and specificity, reducing false positives and providing early molecular evidence for IMDs. Regional hotspot and MAF differences underscore the need for a local genetic database. Although the acceptance of NBGS is increasing, factors such as cost and varying levels of awareness among healthcare providers remain barriers to its broader implementation. This study provides preliminary data supporting the implementation of regional newborn genetic screening programs and informs secondary and tertiary prevention strategies.Keywords: Newborn screening, genetic sequencing, real-world implementation, Northern China

Introduction

Traditional newborn screening (NBS) is the early identification and diagnosis of newborns with inherited metabolic disorders through the detection of specific biochemical indicators in the blood of newborns, thus identifying population health problems such as intellectual disabilities, growth retardation, and even death. The global NBS program originated in the 1960s when Dr. Guthrie in the United States successfully established the bacterial inhibition method for the semi-quantitative determination of phenylalanine in the blood; in particular, he created the dried blood spot method, which uses filter paper for blood sample collection, enabling the realization of NBS in phenylketonuria (PKU) [1]. NBS in China began in 1981, with the first screening and treatment studies of PKU and congenital hypothyroidism pioneered in Shanghai and Beijing [2]. After forty years of development, screening techniques have evolved from bacterial inhibition and fluorescence analysis for biochemical indicators to tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), which has greatly expanded the types of diseases screened [3].

In recent years, next-generation sequencing and other genetic testing technologies have provided an important basis for the diagnosis of diseases, especially for difficult cases and high-risk infants, and have increasingly become a rapid diagnostic tool for various types of diseases [4–6]. With the popularization and application of the NBS program, domestic and foreign countries have accumulated more experience in newborn genetic screening (NBGS). Genetic testing technology applied to NBS can not only flexibly determine the types of diseases and the number of genes to be tested according to the needs and compensate for the shortcomings of biochemical screening but also helps to rapidly diagnose patients with genetic diseases, improve screening efficiency, and expand the types of screening diseases, and has certain advantages in terms of diagnostic sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value. In terms of sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value, it has certain advantages [7–9]. Genetic screening in newborn screening programs initially served as a secondary screening procedure [10,11]. In recently published studies, it was shown that, as a primary screening tool, it improves detection ability in traditional screening and could be a key method for first-line screening in NBS [12]. However, as an emerging program, genetic screening still has some shortcomings in terms of acceptance in real-world populations, ethics, and report interpretation [13]. Currently, most studies on NBGS are prospective or retrospective clinical trials, and very few analytical studies have reported on the value of their practical application in clinical settings. This report applies a neonatal targeted sequencing screening technology containing 465 gene variants for 271 diseases; summarizes the real clinical application of NBGS in our center; and provides a summary of hotspot genes associated with inherited metabolic diseases, the carrier frequencies of the variant loci, and relevant information about diagnosed patients.

Results

Newborn genetic screening in a clinical context

The NBGS panel was designed to include genes associated with conditions that align with the Wilson and Jungner screening criteria (Figure S1). This screening program constitutes a real-world clinical-grade study rather than a clinical trial. This study is a prospective clinical implementation research project, and all participants provided written informed consent for research participation, which included the return of research results.

From 1 August, 2023, to 15 November, 2024, a total of 15,272 newborns were delivered at our center, the families of 1590 of which opted for the newborn genetic screening program, yielding a screening rate of 10.41%. Of the 1590 newborns who elected to undergo genetic screening, 865 were male and 725 were female, with a mean (±SD) birth weight of 3325 g (±505 g) and a mean (±SD) gestational age of 39.0 weeks (±1.2 weeks). Of these newborns, 123 (7.74%) screened positive, 462 (29.05%) screened negative, and 1005 (63.21%) carriers for recessive disorders, as shown in Figure 1.

The number of completed newborn genetic screenings per month demonstrated an upward trend between August 2023 and November 2024, with the lowest number recorded in September 2023 (49 screenings) and the highest in September 2024 (148 screenings), indicating a growth rate of 66.89% over this period. It is acknowledged that the number of deliveries exhibits variation from month to month, which may result in significant fluctuations in the number of newborn screenings performed, as demonstrated in Figure 2(a). Consequently, an examination of the number of completed newborn screenings per month as a percentage of overall deliveries revealed a stable trend until April 2024 (7.38%–10.76%) and an escalating trend thereafter (9.76%–21.47%), as demonstrated in Figure 2(b). A statistical survey of our inpatient centers involved in the delivery of neonatal specimens shows that there are differences in the delivery rates of the inpatient centers. Analysis of positive NBGS results

Of the 123 newborns who screened positive, 34 (27.64%, 34/123) had congenital disorders related to deafness. Audiological screening tests were conducted on all 34 newborns, and 4 (3.25%, 4/123) were found to be positive. Reexaminations were performed on these 4 newborns, and 1 (0.81%, 1/123) remained positive in the retest. Another 10 (8.13%, 10/123) had inherited metabolic disorders (phenylketonuria, methylmalonic acidemia, glucose-6-dehydrogenase deficiency, etc.); 8 of these 10 newborns (6.50%, 8/123) were eventually clinically diagnosed, treated, and managed with long-term follow-up, while the remaining 2 were followed up for monitoring of the appropriate indicators. The remaining 79 of the 123 newborns were diagnosed with hematological disorders (thalassemia, porphyria, hereditary abnormalities of erythrocyte morphology, etc.), musculoskeletal disorders (chondrodysplasia, osteogenesis imperfecta, peroneal muscular dystrophy, epiphyseal dysplasia, etc.), urological disorders (Alport syndrome, cystinuria, nephrotic syndrome, etc.), and cardiovascular disorders (hypertrophic/dilated cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, etc.). Diseases related to the respiratory system (primary cilia dyskinesia, etc.), the eye (oculocutaneous albinism, cataract, etc.), and the immune, nervous, gastrointestinal, and other systems are being monitored for as part of regular follow-up, either because the relevant clinical manifestations do not appear in the neonatal or infantile stage or because there is no specific test index.

Among the 123 newborns who were screen-positive, 140 genetic variant loci involving 84 genes were detected. Of these, 65 (46.43%), 54 (38.57%), and 15 (10.71%) loci corresponded to the autosomal dominance (AD), autosomal recessive (AR), and AD/AR (SLC3A1, TNFRSF13B, GJB2, PROC, SERPINC1, EPB41, GDAP1, and LAMB3) patterns of inheritance, respectively. In total, 1.43% (two patients), 0.71% (one patient), and 0.71% (one patient) conformed to the X-linked incomplete dominance (G6PD), unknown (HBA1 and HBA2), AD/somatic variant (RB1), and X-linked recessive (XLR) inheritance patterns, respectively, as shown in Figure 3.

Genetic burden in newborns

A total of 2,354 genetic variants were identified in 1,590 newborns, with the top 11 genes being GJB2 (110, 4.67%), MMACHC (63, 2.68%), and ATP7B (51, 2.17%), PAH (50, 2.12%), MYO7A (41, 1.74%), MYH14 (38, 1.61%), MUT (36, 1.53%), SLC26A4 (32, 1.36%), MYBPC3 (31, 1.32%), GALC (28, 1.19%), and PKHD1 (28, 1.19%). The genes in question include four genes responsible for inherited metabolic disorders (MMACHC, ATP7B, PAH, and MUT), four deafness-related genes (GJB4, MYO7A, MYH14, and SLC26A4), one gene associated with the cardiac system (MYBPC3) and one gene linked to the renal system (PKHD1).

Among the inherited metabolic disease genes, 519 gene variants were detected, and the top 10 genes were MMACHC (11.05%), ATP7B (8.95%), PAH (8.77%), MUT (6.32%), GALC (4.91%), GAA (3.16%), SLC3A1 (3.16%), SLC22A5 (2.98%), SLC25A13 (2.81%), and ETFDH (2.46%), as illustrated in Figure 4. Diseases with high rates of gene carriage included two organic acid metabolic disorders [MMACHC gene variant leading to methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) type cblc, MUT gene variant leading to MUT-type MMA, and ETFDH gene variant leading to glutaric acidemia type II], two amino acid metabolic disorders [a variant in the PAH gene leading to hyperphenylalaninemia (HPA), and a variant in the SLC25A13 gene caused citrin protein deficiency]. The following lysosomal disorders (LSDs) were identified: Krabbe disease, which is caused by variants in the GALC gene, and glycogen storage disease type II, which is caused by variants in the GAA gene. One fatty acid metabolism disorder was also identified—systemic primary carnitine deficiency, which is caused by variants in the SLC22A5 gene. Finally, one copper metabolism disorder (hepatolenticular degeneration caused by variants in the ATP7B gene) and one renal injury disease (cystinemia caused by variants in the SLC3A1 gene) were identified, as seen in Figure 4.

Analysis of hotspot variants of genes for inherited metabolic diseases in newborns

Among the inherited metabolic disease genes, the disease with the highest carrier frequency was MMA (especially MMACHC gene variants, the rest including MUT, MMADHC, MMAB, and MMAA). The three MMACHC gene variants with the highest carrier frequencies were c.609G > A (25.40%, 16/63), c.658_660del (20.63%, 13/63), and c.482G > A (12.70%, 8/63), respectively. Among the MUT gene variant loci, the three with the highest carrier frequencies were c.1286A > G (38.89%, 14/36), c.1663G > A (16.67%, 6/36), and c.729_730insTT (11.11%, 4/36), and the rest of the genes carried lower frequencies. In addition to the lysosomal disease-related genes and the MMACHC, MUT, ATP7B, and SLC3A1 genes, we performed variant site counts in the PAH, SLC22A5, SLC25A13, ETFDH, and ACADVL genes, which have high carrier frequencies. The most frequent variant sites in the PAH gene were c.728G > A (14.00%, 7/50), c.721C > T (14.00%, 7/50), c.158G > A (8.00%, 8/50), and c.1400C > G (94.12%, 16/17) in the SLC22A5 gene. The SLC25A13 gene variant c.852_855delTATG (37.50%, 6/16) and the ETFDH gene variant c.770A > G (35.71%, 5/14) were the most common, while the ACADVL gene was scattered.

To explore the possibility of universal screening for these high-carrier-rate loci in the population genome, we calculated their minor allele frequencies (MAFs) and compared them with the carrier rates reported in gnomAD_exome_EAS (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). Comparison of the diagnostic efficacy of biochemical screening and genetic screening for inherited metabolic diseases

The 1,590 newborns were selected for the genetic screening program while the traditional newborn screening program for 40 metabolic diseases was refined. 10 metabolic disease gene-positive newborns were identified by the genetic screening program, 140 screen-positive newborns were identified by the traditional screening program, and the final diagnosis of 8 children was positive by both biochemical and genetic screening. We compared the diagnostic efficacy of biochemical and genetic screening and showed that the combined diagnostic efficacy of genetic screening was still higher than that of biochemical screening, especially in terms of specificity (99.87% vs. 92.30%) and positive predictive value (80.00% vs. 5.71%).

Case studies on confirmed diagnoses of inherited metabolic diseases

Of the 123 newborns who screened positive, a total of 10 screened positive for genes related to inherited metabolic diseases (three PAH, two MUT, two G6PD, one MMACHC, one PCCA, and one ACADSB gene). Parental inheritance patterns were determined by Sanger sequencing of parental samples. These samples were collected during the clinical follow-up process after a neonatal result was reported, not at the time of the initial newborn screening; biochemical tests aided in the clinical diagnosis of 8 cases, which had a positive predictive value of 80%. Five of the eight diagnosed patients had organic acidemia (two MMA-MUT, one MMA-cblC, one PA, one 2-MBDD, two G6PD, and one HPA gene). The remaining two neonates with positive PAH gene screening results were excluded after recall and Sanger sequencing, which confirmed they both had compound heterozygous variants. However, biochemical index tests were not available for clinical diagnosis. Of the 8 diagnosed cases, 5 (2 MMA-MUT, 1 MMA-cblC, 1 PA, and 12-MBDD) received treatment and underwent regular follow-up. 2 neonates with G6PD deficiency received prophylactic guidance and were scheduled for regular follow-up. The remaining one neonate with HPA had not yet met the criteria for clinical intervention and was therefore maintained on a normal diet with regular follow-up. Discussion

With the rapid development of sequencing technology and the growing depth of medical genetics research, the application of genetic testing technology in NBS has been explored in depth worldwide, and genetic screening and related clinical studies for many hereditary diseases have been extensively conducted [14]. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) carried out four projects, including BabySeq, to explore the application of genome sequencing in NBS, followed by a number of studies on NBGS in many countries including the UK and Germany [15–17]. Many multicenter, prospective studies have also been initiated in China to explore the prospects and strategies for the application of genetic screening and diagnostic systems for newborns with inherited diseases [12,18–20]. In view of the disadvantages such as long testing times and high costs associated with whole-exome sequencing or whole-genome sequencing, targeted sequencing panel testing has become the next best testing method for NBGS [11]. In a multicenter study, NBGS panel testing for 155 variants in 35 genes showed an accuracy of 99.65% (286/287) [21,22]. Our center has also participated in large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies showing that targeted sequencing panel testing improves early and accurate identification of newborns with congenital diseases [19].

Even so, the practical application of genetic screening in clinics is hampered by numerous obstacles, including but not limited to cost, feasibility, testing time, reporting requirements, ethics, and the law. For the provider perspective, this includes the need for specialized training in genomic medicine, the time constraints associated with pre- and post-test counseling, and the challenge of managing and communicating clinical uncertainty. For the patient/family perspective, the encompasses difficulties in understanding complex results (such as variants of uncertain significance and carrier status), the potential for psychological anxiety, and the implications of findings for other family members. For the public health or programmatic perspective, this involves the infrastructure required for data storage and interpretation, the need to ensure equitable access to screening, the importance of cost-effectiveness analyses, and the establishment of robust long-term follow-up frameworks.

After preliminary research and preparation, neonatal targeted sequencing technology has been widely promoted and applied in our center. This study is the first study conducted after the actual clinical application of this technology in our center, and to the best of our knowledge, it is also the first study reporting the clinical application of NBGS as a primary screening tool in Shandong Province, China. This study summarizes the overall acceptance of NBGS in our geographic region and shows that only 10.41% of families were willing to allow their newborns to undergo a genetic screening program, which overall translates to a low level of screening. However, the data demonstrate a consistent upward trend over time, both in terms of the number of screenings and the screening rate. It is important to note that the NBGS has only been in clinical practice for a relatively brief period, which may suggest a positive start and demonstrate the program’s potential for application. According to Chinese hospital regulations, each obstetric ward is responsible for newborn screening, rather than pediatricians. Another issue that must be fully considered is whether medical personnel have effectively achieved their goals for communication and educational efforts. In this study, the genetic screening rate for each neonatal specimen delivery center in our institution was statistically analyzed. The results showed that the test delivery rate varied greatly among centers, ranging from 1.48% to 38.68%. We have to consider whether this difference is due to variations in the understanding and acceptance of genetic screening among medical staff at each center. The emergence of this problem is likely inevitable. However, it should be the main focus of the clinical application of genetic screening programs. Specifically, it is necessary to improve the awareness and acceptance of these programs by healthcare professionals to promote its integration into clinical practice. In addition, we also need to consider the cost of genetic screening tests. Because this test is not a public welfare project, the cost is entirely borne by individuals. The cost of the NBGS may be a significant factor in guardians’ decision-making regarding whether to proceed with screening for their newborns. According to official statistical data, the per capita annual disposable income of local urban residents is 7,887 USD. In contrast, the cost of this test is 354 USD, which represents a significant financial outlay (approximately 4.5% of the annual per capita income) for an average family. The economic burden likely acted as a barrier to access, potentially limiting the choice for this test and creating a selection bias towards families with a higher socioeconomic status. We tried to explain the relevant factors that may affect the guardian’s choice for their newborn to undergo genetic screening, such as knowledge and education level, income level, and family genetic status. However, based on the initial application of this detection technology, we could not conduct sufficient research on whether guardians ultimately chose for their newborns to undergo genetic screening, and more extensive related research may be needed in the future.

A multicenter nationwide study including our center was conducted using targeted region capture sequencing for the genetic screening of newborns for 142 genes associated with 128 disorders; this study showed that 813 (2.7%) of 29,601 newborns had positive genetic screening results, and 402 (1.4%) ultimately received a diagnosis [12]. The targeted sequencing panel technology applied in this study had previously been used in a retrospective study of 11,484 newborns, which showed a positive initial screening rate of 7.85% (902/11,484) [20]. In the present study, among 1590 newborns whose parents opted for genetic screening, the screening positivity rate was found to be 7.74%, which is in line with previous reports of this study but significantly higher than other studies, and this may be related to the disease inclusion criteria and the scoring principles for reporting loci. An inescapable question is what exactly the population carrier rate of genetic variants is, and this is an important question that needs to be answered by clinicians during genetic counseling with families of newborns. Previous studies have shown population carrier rates of genetic variants ranging from 59.27% (2315/3249) to 26.59% (1326/2986) [23], and in the present study, a total of 1,128 newborns carried at least one genetic variant, with an overall carrier rate of 70.94% (1128/1590). The reason for this consideration is that high carrier rates of genetic variants are associated with disease inclusion and pathogenicity ratings of variant loci. An increase in screen-positive and gene carrier rates will undoubtedly increase the psychological burden of newborn guardians and clinicians who conduct genetic counseling. We also found that it is difficult to confirm the diagnosis of screen-positive children, mainly due to the fact that the selection of disease types includes some diseases with no relevant auxiliary diagnostic indicators or late-onset diseases; this causes unnecessary distress for the family members of newborn infants and poses a moral ethics challenge. According to a recently published expert consensus on neonatal genetic screening, disease selection and scoring criteria still need to be refined for NBGS in order to prevent unnecessary moral and ethical issues and psychological burden on families [24].

In our study, we started with a large group of preventable and treatable genetic metabolic diseases and found 10 positive newborns, all of whom were successfully recalled and examined; 8 children were finally diagnosed. The advantage of genetic screening over biochemical screening in the detection of inherited metabolic disorders is mainly reflected in its higher specificity and significantly higher positive predictive value. In the practical application of genetic screening, clinicians may prefer to focus on preventable and treatable diseases with early and clear diagnostic aids. Therefore, while it should be ensured that a broader range of diseases can be screened, panel genetic screening by targeted sequencing should be strictly limited to disease inclusion criteria. While NBGS showed higher specificity in our cohort, larger multi-center studies are warranted to establish sensitivity across diverse disorders.

We compiled and analyzed genes related to IMDs and found that, in our geographical region, MMA-cblC was the disease with the highest gene carrier rate due to variants in the MMACHC gene, followed by phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency due to PAH gene variants and then MMA-MUT due to MUT gene variants; each of these three genes have their hotspot variants. The MMACHC gene variants c.609G > A, c.658_660del, and c.482G > A showed higher carrier rates, which is basically consistent with our previous description of the gene carrier rates of MMA in our region; this indicates that the population included in this study reflects the gene carriage in our geographic region to a certain extent and the presence of organic acid metabolic diseases and amino acid metabolic diseases. We found a high prevalence of ATP7B and SLC3A1 gene variants in our region. However, biochemical screening indicators for the neonatal period are not yet available, making diagnosis, genetic counseling, and follow-up difficult. In addition, we found that the carrier rates of glutaratemia type II in organic acid metabolism, citrin deficiency in amino acid metabolism, and systemic primary carnitine deficiency in fatty acid metabolism are high, and all have their hotspot variants. It is worth mentioning that the PCD gene c.1400C > G carrier rate was 94.12% (16/17), which was much higher than our expectations. A count of patients diagnosed with primary carnitine deficiency at our center revealed that more than 73.91% of patients carried this locus, in addition to three cases with pure variants inherited from the parents, verified by Sanger sequencing; these compel us to be vigilant in the screening and prevention of this disease (unpublished data).

In order to distinguish whether the genes tested in this study population are common or rare variants, we introduced the concept of minor allele frequency (MAF). A minor allele is the frequency with which the second most common allele occurs in a given population. In this study, the MAFs of genes that carry high frequencies, such as the MMACHC, MUT, and PAH genes, were all <5%, suggesting that these genes are all rare variants in the study population. On the other hand, we compared the MAFs of these genes with the gnomAD_exome_EAS database. In the MMACHC gene, the MAFs were higher than those in gnomAD_exome_EAS. But in the MUT gene, except c.1741C > T, c.1159A > C, and c.1295A > C were not included in the gnomAD_exome_EAS, the MAFs of other loci were lower than those in gnomAD_exome_EAS. The MAFs of c.158G > A, c.1174T > A, and c.516G > T in the PAH gene were lower than that in gnomAD_exome_EAS. This shows that the variant loci of the above genes, despite being rare-disease-associated, have frequencies that are heterogeneous across populations. The existence of population heterogeneity in the frequencies of these loci also reminded us to take this variability into account in the subsequent screening and data analysis and not to simply refer to the uniform standards of international databases when conducting clinical counseling; we also had to refer to established local databases on populations for geographic analysis and interpretation. It must be taken into account that, in addition to ethnic heterogeneity, this difference may be related to our insufficient population number. We will also continue to expand the number of people included in our screening, so that our results more closely reflect the true and objective mutation site frequency and provide reliable support for genetic counseling and the construction of local databases.

On the other hand, we were surprised to find that LSDs had a high rate of gene carriage; the carrier frequencies of two gene variant loci, GALC and GAA, were second only to those of the MMACHC, PAH, and MUT genes. Therefore, we summarized the carrier frequencies of genes related to LSDs and found that the GALC gene variants c.1901T > C and c.2041G > A had high carrier rates in the population, which were 53.57% (15/28) and 32.14% (9/28). The variant sites of the GAA gene were instead more dispersed, and the NPC1 and SMPD1 genes had lower carrier rates. Lysosomal enzyme activity screening has been carried out for a relatively short period of time in China. A multicenter prospective study conducted by our center showed that the overall prevalence of LSDs in newborns in our province was about 1:3000, and only single heterozygous gene variants were detected in some of the children with reduced enzyme activity, which indicated that there was a certain degree of false negativity in gene sequencing for the identification of some LSDs and that an LSD screening program based on tandem mass spectrometry is of great practical significance [25].

In this real-world implementation cohort of newborns, we observed a striking contrast in the diagnostic performance between NBS and NBGS. While both modalities achieved 100% sensitivity in detecting the 8 confirmed cases, the specificity and positive predictive value (PPV) differed substantially. NGS demonstrated an excellent PPV of 80.00% (8/10), meaning that 8 out of 10 screen-positive infants were true positives. In contrast, biochemical screening yielded a PPV of only 5.71% (8/140), indicating that over 94% of initial biochemical positives were false positives requiring unnecessary recall and follow-up testing. These findings highlight a key advantage of genomic screening, the significant reduction in false-positive results. From a clinical and public health perspective, this reduction has tangible benefits, including decreased parental anxiety, lower healthcare utilization for confirmatory testing, and reduced burden on follow-up systems. In our cohort, NGS would have avoided 132 unnecessary recalls compared to biochemical screening alone. However, whether these data support the widespread implementation of genomic NBS requires critical examination. First, while the PPV of 80% is impressive, it must be interpreted within the context of the study design. The true-positive cases were confirmed through biochemical abnormalities and clinical evaluation, meaning that NGS alone, without biochemical correlation, might have identified variants whose clinical penetrance or expressivity remains uncertain. Second, the two false positives from NGS (the PAH compound heterozygous cases without biochemical abnormalities) raise important questions about variant interpretation and the potential for overdiagnosis. These infants carry genetic variants consistent with a diagnosis of phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency, yet they show no biochemical evidence of disease. Whether they represent cases of non-penetrance, mild variants, or will develop biochemical abnormalities later in life remains unknown, underscoring the challenges of predicting phenotype from genotype alone. While our data demonstrate that NGS can achieve excellent diagnostic accuracy and dramatically reduce false positives compared to traditional biochemical screening, they do not yet provide conclusive evidence for immediate, widespread implementation. The true clinical utility of genomic NBS will depend on long-term follow-up to assess outcomes in both true-positive and false-positive groups, ongoing refinement of variant interpretation to minimize uncertainty, and rigorous cost-effectiveness analyses that account for both healthcare savings from reduced recalls and the additional costs of genomic infrastructure. Future research should focus on these areas to determine whether the promise of genomic screening can be translated into sustainable, equitable public health practice.

In our region, the cost of NBS is approximately $55 per infant, whereas NBGS used in this study costs $354 per infant—approximately 6.4 times higher. While the upfront cost of NBGS is substantially greater, a comprehensive cost-effectiveness comparison must consider both the direct savings from reduced false-positive recalls and the long-term benefits of early diagnosis. In this cohort, traditional NBS generated 132 false-positive results, each requiring confirmatory testing, pediatric specialist consultations, and often generating parental anxiety and lost work time. Although we did not systematically quantify these costs, prior studies estimate that each false-positive recall can cost several hundred dollars when accounting for laboratory tests, clinical visits, and family burden. Thus, the 132 unnecessary recalls potentially consumed resources that could offset a portion of the higher sequencing cost. Quantifying the net cost-effectiveness would require modeling long-term outcomes, but a preliminary comparison is illustrative. If even a single case of severe organic acidemia is prevented from progressing to irreversible brain damage, the lifetime savings in medical and social costs could far exceed the incremental investment in sequencing for the entire cohort. Nevertheless, formal health economic analyses—incorporating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained, caregiver burden, and healthcare utilization—are needed to determine whether NBGS meets traditional cost-effectiveness thresholds in our setting.

The newborns in this study who screened positive for genes related to inherited metabolic diseases were recalled for Sanger sequencing and biochemistry-assisted testing, and we ultimately diagnosed eight newborns. Interestingly, all 10 newborn babies with positive screening results were male. We believe that this may be a coincidental finding, because no gender differences were observed in the selected population, and their guardians did not describe a family history of the diagnosed disorders. Of the eight newborns diagnosed, three had MMA (two cases of the MUT type and one case of the cblC type) and two had G6PD. HPA, PA, and ACADSB were diagnosed in one case each. All of the above locus variants were reported loci, and all of the diagnosed newborns were under regular treatment and follow-up. We found that 3 of the 10 screen-positive newborns were positive for the PAH gene and all carried the c.158G > A locus; however, only 1 presented with elevated blood phenylalanine and was diagnosed with the disease, while the remaining 2 had normal biochemical parameters. The interpretation of this locus is controversial, as the variant is located in exon 2 and is a missense variant, resulting in the substitution of arginine for histidine at position 53. In vitro expression experiments have shown that the activity of the PAH enzyme in the c.158G > A variant is approximately 79% of that of the wild type [26]. Therefore, the c.158G > A variant may be associated with a mild HPA phenotype, and the clinical phenotype of an affected child is related to the type of variant in the other allele. In MMA, two newborns with MUT gene mutations were diagnosed, and although both of them had mildly elevated biochemical levels, there was no tendency for them to be elevated when observed at follow-up under normal feeding conditions. Both patients carried a variant at the c.1286A > G locus of the MUT gene, which is a missense variant resulting in the substitution of tyrosine for cysteine at position 429, as suggested by Multiple Protein Prediction analysis. Several protein prediction software suggests that this variant will disrupt MUT protein function. In combination with the related cases reported in this study, it is believed that this locus may be associated with delayed-onset or mild forms of methylmalonic acidemia.

A limitation of this study is that we only performed statistical analysis on inherited metabolic diseases and did not summarize the results of genetic screening for other systemic diseases. In fact, another collaborator of ours has summarized data on genes for deafness and eye diseases, and we look forward to a larger summary of the results for valuable clinical applications. Another limitation of this study lies in the lack of in-depth exploration of gene data analyses and related interpretation guidelines. This is because this study focuses on delineating the practical implications of gene sequencing in the clinical application of newborn screening, rather than highlighting technical development aspects. Another limitation of this study is that the 79 newborns who screened positive for genetic variants associated with other systemic disorders did not have definitive clinical diagnoses. The unconfirmed cases have been clearly listed in the Table S2. For the 79 newborns screen-positive children under surveillance, the benefit lies in avoiding a diagnostic odyssey and enabling proactive management.

In conclusion, this study shows that the diagnostic efficacy index of the newborn genetic screening program in clinical application has more significant advantages, but the positive screening rate and gene carrier rate are detected to be higher, which may be related to the principles for selecting genetic screening diseases and pathogenicity evaluations of the variant loci, and so on. Neonatal genes for inherited metabolic diseases have high carrier rates, which emphasizes the necessity of prenatal carrier screening and postpartum neonatal genetic screening for inherited metabolic diseases. LSDs show a high frequency of gene carriage in our geographic population, and given that variant site mining of lysosomal storage disorder-related genes has not yet been conducted in sufficient depth, the incidence of this major group of disorders may be higher than we expected, and enzyme activity screening programs for lysosomal storage disorder are of great significance. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first in Shandong Province, China, to analyze the application of newborn genetic screening in clinical practice. It also summarizes the genetic carrier frequencies of inherited metabolic disease genes in newborns undergoing targeted sequencing at our center; this provides some data to support secondary and tertiary prevention and control of this disease on the regional and provincial levels.

Methods

Clinical data collection

From 1 August 2023 to 15 November 2024, pregnant women were provided with prenatal education in all obstetric wards at our center. After an understanding of NBGS was gained, the pregnant women, their relatives, or guardians were required to sign an informed consent form. This form acquired consent for the collection of a dried blood spot (DBS) 48 h after the delivery of the fetus for the testing program, as well as the synchronized collection of plantar blood for the screening of 40 inherited metabolic disorders (IMDs) in the women’s newborns. The inclusion criteria for participation in this program are as follows: firstly, the availability of DBS specimens collected within 48 h of birth; secondly, the collection of a minimum of four 8 × 8 mm DBS samples; and thirdly, the acquisition of written informed consent from the participants. The exclusion criterion was the receipt of a blood transfusion. All participants or their legally authorized representatives provided informed consent before enrollment. If they agree to participate, they will sign an informed consent form and provide a blood sample for screening using the NESTS panel, which includes 465 genes covering 596 disorders. Clinical reports are issued within 42 days, and post-test genetic counseling is provided. Regardless of their decision on genetic screening, all newborns undergo traditional newborn screening, which includes inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs) by MS/MS, as well as phenylketonuria (PKU), congenital hypothyroidism (CH), and hearing loss (HL). All newborns are required to receive a routine physical examination follow-up at 42 days. Based on the screening results, some cases may require further CNV-seq testing to detect copy number variations. Parental Sanger sequencing may be performed when necessary. If no further evaluation is needed, the current screening process is concluded. Additional genetic counseling and follow-up are available, and all families are eligible to receive ongoing genetic counseling and systematic follow-up services. The research plan was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinan Maternity and Child Care Hospital (approval no. IRB KY-22-29).

Sample collection

Blood was collected from the inner or outer side of the heel using a disposable needle, to a depth of less than 3 mm, on a special Scheicher and Schuell 903 filter paper sample card (“Guthrie,” >8 mm in diameter), 48 h after birth, following adequate breastfeeding (at least 3 times). The collection of some blood samples was delayed for various reasons (preterm birth, low body mass, newborns undergoing treatment, early discharge, etc.), but not later than 20 d after birth. All samples were air-dried, collected by designated personnel, sealed, and delivered to Jinan Newborn Disease Screening Center, where they were stored in a refrigerator at 2–8 °C prior to testing.

Neonatal targeted sequencing methods

When designing the panel, the priority was given to genes with early-onset, relatively high incidence and potentially actionable. severe diseases. Gene curation was performed according to ClinGen Gene-Disease Validity Standard Operating Procedure, version 7(clinicalgenome.org/curation-activities). After curation, 465 disease genes that are related to 596 OMIM phenotypes were included in this panel. For those genes with Gene-Disease relationship classification level lower than “strong”, the reasons for the inclusion were also provided (Table S1). A detailed description is shown in Supplementary data. The samples were collected within a two-day period and dispatched to Foton Laboratories in Beijing for testing. About 50 ng genomic DNA was extracted from dried blood spots by drilling holes. The size, concentration and total amount of DNA fragments were detected by electrophoresis, NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, USA) and Qubit (Thermo Scientific, USA). The quality control data of genomic DNA was checked. DNA samples were fragmented by Covaris M220 (Covaris, USA). Target area was captured using liquid hybrid technology of Agilent Sure-Select™ (Agilent, USA). The DNA library was purified by Ampure XP beads (Ampure, China). After end repairing and adenylation, adapters were ligated. Probes were hybridized and captured after DNA fragment enrichment. The size of library fragment was detected by Agilent 2100 (Agilent, USA), and the library concentration was detected by qPCR. Next-generation sequencing was performed on Illumina Hiseq Xten (Illumina, USA), with a mean depth of 200X and raw base of > 1.5 GB for each sample. Quality criteria are: (1) 99.7% of the targeted regions are captured and sequenced; (2) over 95% of the coverage is sequenced more than 50×; (3) 99% of the SNP can be confirmed; (4) peak depth shift < 50%; (5) Q20 > 90% and Q30 > 80% and (6) targeted area repetition rate < 20%, with targeted area average depth > 80×. Variants with reads < 4× or allele fraction < 5% were filtered automatically. Variants annotation included gene annotation (NCBI, RefSeq), disease annotation (Human Gene Mutation Database, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, ClinVar), frequency annotation (1000 Genomes, gnomAD, our in-house database) and mutation prediction (SIFT, PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster, CADD).

Newborn screening methods for inherited metabolic diseases

The NeoBase™ non-sterile MS/MS assay kit (PerkinElmer, Wallac Oy, Turku, Finland) was used for MS/MS analysis. Spots with a diameter of 3.2 mm (equivalent to 3.2 µL of blood) were extracted from each DBS and placed in U-96-well microplates. Then, 100 µL of extraction solution containing an internal standard was added to each well. After incubation in a shaker for 45 min (45 °C, 700 rpm), 75 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a fresh V-96-well microplate. Quality control adopts three levels of internal quality control (blank, low, and high). The indicators related to amino acids, organic acids, and fatty acids were quantitatively analyzed using MS/MS (Xevo TQD, Applied Biosystems, Waters, USA). Analysts conducted quality control checks and ensured that the total ion chromatogram (TIC) was normal, after which they uploaded the data to the Anaconda system for data analysis. This method is used to detect 46 diseases involving metabolic disorders of amino acids, organic acids, and fatty acids. The specific diseases are listed in the attached Table S3.

Interpretation of newborn genetic screening results

Detected gene variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines (Figure S1). (1) The disease mode of inheritance is dominant or unspecified, with heterozygous or hemizygous, pathogenic or possibly pathogenic variants detected. (2) The disease mode of inheritance is recessive or unspecified, with purely heterozygous or hemizygous, pathogenic or possibly pathogenic variants detected. (3) The disease mode of inheritance is recessive, with two or more variants of the same gene detected and at least one heterozygous pathogenic or possibly pathogenic variant detected. (4) The disease mode of inheritance is dominant or unspecified, with unknown heterozygous or hemizygous variants detected. (5) The disease inheritance pattern is recessive or unspecified, pure or hemizygous variants of unknown significance are detected, or two or more variants of unknown significance in the same gene are detected. (6) The disease inheritance pattern is recessive, and heterozygous disease-causing or probable disease-causing variants are detected. Of these descriptors, 1, 2, and 3 indicate a screen-positive result; 4, 5, and 6 indicate a ‘carrier-only’ result; and the absence of any detected variant is considered a screen-negative result. Detailed phenotype information, family history and traditional newborn screening results were collected for further evaluating variants of uncertain significance. Uncertain significance were not routinely returned unless there was strong phenotypic correlation or family history. All returning cases received genetic counseling.

Follow-up and treatment

Families of newborns who received a positive result from the genetic screening test were advised to attend the center’s outpatient clinic for a consultation and to complete the relevant auxiliary diagnostic tests; however, they were also informed that participation was ultimately voluntary. The parents ‘carrier-only’ newborns could choose, at their own discretion, whether or not to attend the clinic for consultation on-site or to communicate with the clinic by telephone for consultation. Children with clearly diagnosed hereditary metabolic diseases were then followed up in the center in accordance with the consensus and guidelines for the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of related diseases. These follow-up tests were not part of the research study and were arranged through routine clinical care, with associated costs typically covered by the families’ health insurance or paid out-of-pocket.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed with the SPSS 26.0 statistical package (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). For normally distributed data, values are presented as the mean + standard deviation; for non-normally distributed data, values are presented as median and interquartile range for continuous variables and number (%) for categorical variables. Figures were plotted via https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn (last accessed on 10 Dec 2024), an online platform for data analysis and visualization.
