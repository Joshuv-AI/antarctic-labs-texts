Concurrent validity of wearable IMUs for sagittal plane lower-limb range of motion during walking and estimated ground reaction forces: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Azamat Bissenov, Esra Zhubi, Marie Anne Engh, Orsolya Gresits, Réka Tóth, Tamás Terebessy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInertial measurement units (IMUs) offer a portable, affordable alternative to optical motion capture (OMC) systems for gait analysis. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the concurrent validity of IMUs against gold-standard systems (OMC and force plates) for the lower-limb sagittal plane range of motion (RoM) during walking. We also summarized evidence on estimating vertical and anterior–posterior ground reaction forces (GRFs).MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central were searched on December 10, 2023 and updated on August 26, 2025. We included studies of ambulatory human participants where IMUs were compared with optical motion capture or force plates for sagittal plane RoM or on estimating vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs. Both observational and randomized designs were eligible. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted data, and assessed study quality (QUADAS-2). Pooled root mean square error (RMSE) values were calculated using random-effects meta-analysis for RoM and GRFs.Results27 studies met inclusion criteria; 22 were included in meta-analysis. IMUs showed concurrent validity (RMSE) within the commonly cited threshold ≤ 5° for ankle (RMSE = 4.62°, 95% CI 3.34–5.90) and knee (RMSE = 4.60°, 95% CI 3.60–5.60) sagittal plane RoM, especially during overground walking. Hip concurrent validity was borderline (RMSE = 5.79°, 95% CI 4.39–7.19), with errors amplified on treadmills. Variability (I2 = 90–96%) reflected differences in hardware, placement, calibration, and processing. Estimates of GRFs, particularly vertical (RMSE = 8.37%BW; 95% CI 5.14–11.61) and anterior–posterior components (RMSE = 5.50%BW; 95% CI 2.90–8.09), were less consistent.ConclusionIMUs provide acceptable concurrent validity (RMSE ≤ 5°) for ankle and knee sagittal plane RoM during walking. Concurrent validity decreases for the hip and treadmill conditions. Standardized calibration, sensor placement, and processing protocols are essential for reliable clinical use, and until further validation, IMUs should be considered complementary rather than stand-alone tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06322-w.Keywords: Inertial measurement unit, Gait analysis, Joint angle, Biomechanics, Motion capture

Background

Accurate measurement of lower-limb kinematics and kinetics during gait is critical for clinical judgment and rehabilitation planning. These measures are also essential for outcome evaluation across diverse populations, including orthopaedic, neurological, and sports-medicine groups [1, 2]. In particular, sagittal plane range of motion (RoM) and ground reaction forces (GRFs) are widely used in clinical practice: RoM guides gait retraining, impairment screening, and recovery tracking, while GRFs quantify external loading relevant to injury screening, load management, and return-to-sport decisions [1, 3–5]. Although vertical GRF is most frequently studied, anterior–posterior forces related to braking and propulsion are also clinically important for stability and efficiency [6]. This review, therefore, focused on sagittal plane RoM and inertial measurement unit (IMU)-based estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs, which are both clinically relevant and most consistently reported in IMU validation studies [3–5]. Three-dimensional (3D) optical motion capture (OMC) systems, such as Vicon or Qualisys, combined with force plates, have long been the gold standard for measuring kinematics and kinetics [5, 7]. OMC offers high precision but suffers from marker placement errors, high cost, limited portability, and dependence on lab facilities [8]. Therefore, the limited applicability of OMC systems for everyday clinical practice and field-based measurements justifies the need for low-cost and mobile alternatives. Moreover, objective monitors demonstrably outperform self-report for free-living activity and sedentary time, and early-life cardiometabolic disparities further underscore the value of objective movement assessment during adolescence [9, 10].

In recent years, inertial measurement units (IMUs) have emerged as a promising solution. They are portable, lower-cost, commercially available, easy to use, and suitable for real-world environments [11]. IMUs are wearable sensors attached to body segments to capture motion data. They allow clinicians and researchers to derive gait and joint kinematics in both controlled and real-world settings. IMUs estimate segment orientation, and sensor-fusion algorithms with biomechanical models reconstruct joint angles [12, 13]. Due to their accessibility, IMUs are advantageous for clinical gait analysis in a diverse setting, including rehabilitation centres, hospitals, sports training settings, and real-world community environments [8], and beyond measurement, accelerometry-based feedback has been shown to improve lifestyle awareness and modestly reduce anthropometric risk in high-risk patients [14]. After total hip arthroplasty, accelerometry has shown increases in moderate–vigorous activity intensity despite little change in total activity time, illustrating how wearable sensors capture qualitative improvements in mobility [15].

Despite these advantages, the concurrent validity of IMUs varies substantially, particularly for multi-degree-of-freedom joints (e.g., hip) [16]. We define clinically acceptable concurrent validity as root mean square error (RMSE) ≤ 5°, a commonly cited threshold in lower-limb kinematics [3]. RMSE was chosen because it summarizes absolute differences in clinically interpretable units, assigns greater weight to larger errors, and is the most consistently reported outcome across IMU validation studies [12, 17, 18]. Although this benchmark facilitates comparison with OMC, its clinical relevance may vary by joint. A number of studies have demonstrated acceptable concurrent validity for ankle and knee joints’ RoM [19–21], others have reported clinically relevant inaccuracies, especially when evaluating proximal joints or estimating kinetic parameters such as estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs [22, 23]. Variability issues can arise from the variance in sensor hardware and placement, calibration methods, gait context (i.e., treadmill vs. overground), and processing algorithms [24–26]. Such variability contributes to differences in IMU measurements within and among studies and influences the accuracy of the IMU-based measurements.

Several studies have evaluated the validity and reliability of IMUs to quantify the kinematic parameters of human gait and other functional movement tasks [12, 27, 28]. In addition, several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have synthesised IMU-derived lower limb kinematic data, and confirmation was provided that IMUs could be used as accurate structural tools in movement analysis [17, 18, 25, 29, 30]. While previous reviews have been beneficial, none specifically analyzed IMU concurrent validity using RMSE for sagittal plane RoM of the ankle, knee, and hip joints, or for estimating vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs. A systematic evaluation is needed to test whether IMUs meet the commonly cited RMSE ≤ 5° threshold. It should also identify methodological factors that influence accuracy for both kinematics and estimates of GRFs. Consequently, it is essential to quantitatively establish the validity of IMUs before they can be recommended for routine use in gait evaluations. Although our search included both walking and running, not enough studies investigated sagittal plane RoM during running to allow synthesis. Therefore, our RoM analysis focuses on walking, the primary gait task assessed clinically [8]. The primary aims of this systematic review and meta-analysis were: (1) compile and synthesize evidence on the accuracy of IMU-derived sagittal plane joint RoM (ankle, knee, hip) and estimates of anterior–posterior, vertical GRFs compared with gold-standard systems; (2) evaluate this concurrent validity against established commonly accepted clinical thresholds (e.g., RMSE ≤ 5° for RoM); (3) examine the influence of walking conditions (overground vs. treadmill) on concurrent validity.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [31], and the protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews on December 10, 2023 (PROSPERO number: CRD42023492248).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they included ambulatory human participants of any age or health status (Population) and assessed gait using IMUs to measure sagittal plane RoM or estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior ground reaction forces (Index test). Eligible studies were required to use a gold-standard comparator, specifically 3D OMC systems or force plates (Comparator). The primary outcomes operationalizing concurrent validity RMSE values for ankle, knee, and hip sagittal plane RoM (°), and for estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs (%Body Weight) (Outcomes). We included observational or randomized controlled study designs that collected concurrent IMU and reference-system data (Study design). Exclusion criteria were case reports, case series, abstracts, letters, non-peer-reviewed sources, and studies using depth-camera systems (e.g., Kinect) or those without concurrent gold-standard measurements.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search consistent with PRISMA guidelines was carried out on December 10, 2023, and updated on August 26, 2025, across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials to locate studies that examined lower limb sagittal plane joint RoM and estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs with IMUs and simultaneously reported on the analyses from a gold-standard gait analysis system. This search was carried out without restrictions on language, year of publication, or participant characteristics in order to maximize sensitivity. The only search filter applied was to limit results to human studies. The search terms grouped concepts for inertial sensors, biomechanics and gait and used the following Boolean string: ("IMU" OR ("inertial" AND "measurement" AND "unit") OR inertia* OR acceler* OR "sensor" OR gyroscop* OR magnetomet*) AND ("capture" OR "analysis" OR "treadmill" OR ("force" AND "plate")) AND (biomechanic* OR kinematic* OR kinetic* OR "force" OR load* OR "moment" OR torque* OR "power") AND ("gait" OR "locomotion" OR ambula* OR walk* OR "run" OR "running" OR "jog" OR "jogging"). The reference lists of all eligible studies and their citing articles were manually screened to identify additional relevant publications. The full database search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Selection process

The search results were imported into EndNote 20 and then Rayyan QCRI [32], and duplicates were eliminated automatically. Two reviewers (AB, EZ) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts, and disagreements were resolved by consensus between them or consultation with a third reviewer (OG). Cohen’s kappa was used to quantify inter-rater reliability [33].

Data collection process

Two independent reviewers (AB, EZ) extracted the data to put it into a pre-designed Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The extracted variables were (1) study characteristics (author, year, and sample size); (2) IMU specifications (brand, sampling rate, data fusion algorithm or filtering method, calibration method, and sensor placement); (3) reference system; (4) joints and assessed parameters (sagittal plane RoM of the ankle, knee, hip, and estimates of vertical/anterior–posterior GRFs); (5) statistical results. Only studies reporting the RMSE with corresponding mean ± standard deviation (SD) were included for quantitative synthesis. The RMSE was selected, as it provided a differential quantitative measure of deviation from reference values, with emphasis on larger errors to allow for assessment of clinically significant errors [3].

We categorized RMSE as follows: ≤ 2° = good validity (within natural variability); 2–5° = commonly accepted threshold; 5–10° = tolerable but should be interpreted with caution; > 10° = unacceptable [3, 34]. Risk of bias was assessed using a structured accuracy-based framework (QUADAS-2), as our aim was to evaluate how well IMUs reproduced reference-standard values. The domains of patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow/timing were applied and considered appropriate for the methodological validation studies included.

Statistical synthesis

Substantial between-study heterogeneity was assumed considering the variable sensor numbers and locations, as well as estimation methods from IMUs between studies. Thus, random-effects models were used. The mean RMSE of RoM joints and GRFs was used as an effect size measure with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Sample size, mean and corresponding standard deviation (SD) were extracted or calculated from CI or SE. The t distribution was assumed when calculating the SD from the CI.

The inverse variance weighting method was used to calculate the pooled mean. To estimate the heterogeneity variance measure (τ2), we used the restricted maximum-likelihood estimator with the Q profile method for confidence intervals [35, 36]. We applied the Hartung-Knapp adjustment[37, 38]. For subgroup analysis, we used random-effects models within subgroups, assuming different τ2, pooling subgroups with a fixed-effects model. To assess the difference between subgroups, we used a “Cochrane Q” test at 5% significance level[36].

We summarised the meta-analysis findings in forest plots. Between-study heterogeneity was described by Higgins & Thompson’s I2 statistics [39]. Small study publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of Funnel-plots and Egger’s test if there were at least 10 studies, with small study bias considered if the p-value was less than 0.1. Potential outlier publications were explored using different influence measures and plots as recommended by Harrer et al. [36].

All statistical analyses were performed with R [40] using the meta (Schwarzer 2022, v6.5.0) package for basic meta-analysis calculations and plots, and the dmetar (Cuijpers, Furukawa, and Ebert 2023, v0.0.9000) package for additional influential analysis [41].

Results

Results of literature search

The updated search identified 19,580 records. Of these, 15,186 were removed as duplicates or overlaps with the previous search, leaving 4394 unique records for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 4374 records were excluded. 20 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 16 were excluded (10 for non-GRF or non-RoM outcomes, 4 for use of different reference systems, 2 abstracts only). 4 new studies were included and added to the 23 studies from the previous review. In total, 27 studies meeting the eligibility criteria, with 441 participants (across studies, the mean age ranged from 20 to 32 years old), were included in the systematic review; 22 of the studies (357 participants) were included in the meta-analysis. Although our eligibility criteria allowed for both healthy and clinical populations, all included studies investigated healthy participants, typically young adults, with most studies reporting a balanced distribution by sex. Interrater reliability for the selection of studies was high, with Cohen's kappa coefficients of 0.90 and 0.97, which indicates almost perfect agreement. The overall process of study selection is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA 2020 flowchart representing the study selection process

Basic characteristics of studies included

Xsens (n = 9) was the most frequently used commercial IMU. Opal/APDM (n = 2) and Perception Neuron (n = 2) were also reported. The remaining studies used a variety of systems, including WAA-006, RehaGait, iSen/iSens, Portabiles, BNO055, MotionSense, and BlueTrident. Reported sampling rates ranged from 20 to 1000 Hz, with most using 100–240 Hz (18/26 among studies reporting a numeric rate). Standard sensor locations targeted the pelvis, thigh, shank, and foot segments. Calibration protocols were described in 23 studies: static/anatomical was most common (n = 17), while functional/dynamic methods were reported in 6 studies, with some studies applying both. Reference standards were predominantly Vicon (n = 15), with OptiTrack (n = 2), Qualisys (n = 1), and other validated optical or force-based systems (n = 9). All included studies used observational designs; no randomized controlled trials were identified. Across studies, most participants were healthy young adults (mean age 20–32 years), with limited representation of older adults, children, or clinical populations. Lower limb sagittal plane RoM and estimates of GRFs

Data from 22 studies, which included a total of 357 participants were pooled to compare the (concurrent) validity of IMUs against the gold standard of either OMC or force plate technology. Pooled results were expressed as RMSE (degrees for RoM; %BW (Body Weight)) for GRFs) using a random-effects model (95% CI). Across outcomes, heterogeneity was very high (I2 typically 90–96%); therefore, pooled RMSEs should be interpreted as between-study averages rather than universal benchmarks, and study-specific context (hardware, placement, calibration, and walking condition) should be considered.

Ankle joint RoM (sagittal plane)

In ten studies, IMUs showed acceptable concurrent validity in estimating ankle sagittal RoM compared with OMC, with a pooled RMSE of 4.62° (95% CI 3.34–5.90; I2 = 91%, p < 0.001) [19–21, 28, 42–47]. This substantial heterogeneity indicates that the pooled RMSE is not a stable single value; protocol and device differences likely account for much of the spread, so the estimate should be interpreted cautiously. Subgroup analysis found rates of error to be lower for overground walking (4.15° [95% CI 2.49–5.81]; I2 = 89%, p < 0.001) as compared to treadmill walking (5.46° [95% CI 2.20–8.72]; I2 = 94%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). This pooled value falls within the ≤ 5° commonly accepted threshold, supporting acceptable concurrent validity for ankle sagittal RoM, particularly in overground conditions.

Forest plot of the RMSE for the ankle joint RoM in the sagittal plane, comparing IMUs to OMC systems. Random-effects model. CI = confidence interval

Knee joint RoM (sagittal plane)

Across nineteen studies, IMUs demonstrated acceptable concurrent validity for knee sagittal RoM compared with OMC, with a pooled RMSE of 4.60° (95% CI 3.60–5.60; I2 = 96%, p < 0.001)[19–21, 28, 42–56]. Given the very high I2, the pooled RMSE reflects an average across diverse protocols rather than a universal benchmark; study context remains critical. When examined by walking condition, the RMSE values were 4.51° (95% CI 3.14–5.89; I2 = 96%, p < 0.001) for overground walking and 4.78° (95% CI 2.88–6.67; I2 = 90%, p < 0.001) for treadmill walking (Fig. 3). This pooled value also falls within the ≤ 5° acceptable threshold, indicating acceptable concurrent validity for knee sagittal RoM, though context (e.g., treadmill vs. overground) remains important given the high heterogeneity.

Forest plot of the RMSE for the knee joint RoM in the sagittal plane, comparing IMUs to OMC systems. Random-effects model. CI = confidence interval

Hip joint RoM (sagittal plane)

Fourteen studies reported on hip sagittal RoM, where IMUs showed reduced concurrent validity compared with OMC, with a pooled RMSE of 5.79° (95% CI 4.39–7.19; I2 = 92%, p < 0.001) [19–21, 28, 42–47, 49, 51, 53, 57]. When stratified by walking condition, the RMSE for overground walking was 5.08° (95% CI 3.23–6.92; I2 = 90%, p < 0.001), and for treadmill walking, it was 6.94° (95% CI 4.33–9.55; I2 = 94%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). This pooled value exceeds the ≤ 5° acceptable threshold, suggesting lower concurrent validity for hip sagittal RoM—especially on treadmills—and underscoring the need for cautious interpretation.

Forest plot of the RMSE for the hip joint RoM in the sagittal plane, comparing IMUs to OMC systems. Random-effects model. CI = confidence interval

Estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs

Four studies assessed the concurrent validity of IMU-based estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs, derived from inverse dynamics or machine learning models, relative to laboratory-based force plate measurements. [20, 44, 58, 59]. For vertical GRFs, the pooled RMSE was 8.37%BW (95% CI 5.14–11.61) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86%, p < 0.001). For anterior–posterior GRFs, the pooled RMSE was 5.50%BW (95% CI 2.90–8.09), also with high heterogeneity (I2 = 88%, p < 0.001). These high heterogeneity values underscore that pooled GRF errors are exploratory and highly sensitive to modelling approach and task context, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Given the small sample sizes, wide confidence intervals, and methodological heterogeneity, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and are presented in Supplementary Figures S1 and S2.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias (QUADAS-2). Reference Standard and Flow & Timing were consistently low risk (27/27 each). Index Test was predominantly low risk (24/27 low; 3/27 unclear; 0/27 high). Patient Selection showed the greatest concerns (14/27 low; 6/27 unclear; 7/27 high), making it the primary driver of bias across studies. Applicability concerns were generally low, with relatively higher concern in Patient Selection (9/27 low; 10/27 unclear; 8/27 high). Full domain counts and study-level ratings are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized current literature on the concurrent validity of IMUs for quantifying sagittal plane RoM at the ankle, knee, and hip joints and provided preliminary evidence for vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs. Across 27 studies involving 441 participants, 22 provided data suitable for quantitative analyses. While previous reviews have addressed IMU validity for either kinematic or kinetic outcomes, this study is the first meta-analysis to jointly evaluate sagittal plane RoM of the ankle, knee, and hip together with IMU-based estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs, benchmarked against gold-standard OMC and force plate systems. By integrating both domains, our findings provide a more comprehensive assessment of IMU validity for clinical gait analysis.

Validity of IMUs for sagittal plane joint RoM

Overall pooled findings

The pooled results indicate that IMUs demonstrate concurrent validity within the commonly accepted threshold (RMSE ≤ 5°), particularly for distal joints such as the ankle and knee during overground walking. However, precision varies significantly by joint and walking condition, with reduced concurrent validity at the hip and during treadmill walking, which has been similarly noted by Kobsar et al. [18] and Poitras et al. [17] in their reviews of lower-limb kinematics. Ankle RoM was shown to have a pooled RMSE of 4.62° (95% CI 3.34–5.90), which falls within the commonly accepted threshold during overground (RMSE: 4.15°), and was just outside the acceptable range in the treadmill conditions (i.e., RMSE: 5.46°). For the knee joint, the pooled RMSE was 4.60° (95% CI 3.60–5.60). Subgroup analysis showed acceptable accuracy during overground walking (4.51°; 95% CI 3.14–5.89) but slightly higher error under treadmill conditions (4.78°; 95% CI 2.88–6.67). The most significant error was seen at the hip, with the pooled RMSE of 5.79° (95% CI 4.39–7.19), which is well above the threshold rating of 5° and further increased to 6.94° in treadmill conditions. The larger errors at the hip likely stem from increased soft-tissue artefacts and joint complexity [60, 61]. Given that the typical total sagittal plane RoMs during normal walking are ~ 30° at the ankle, ~ 60° at the knee, and ~ 40° at the hip[62], even small absolute RMSE values may partly reflect the relatively small overall excursions of these joints. Similarly, treadmill-related errors may partly result from magnetometer interference, since treadmill motors generate hard-iron disturbances and steel frames produce soft-iron distortions, both of which bias heading estimates [46, 63, 64].

Subgroup by walking condition (overground vs. treadmill)

Subgroup analysis yielded two robust patterns: (1) IMUs are more accurate during overground walking (ankle 4.15°; knee 4.51°; hip 5.08°) rather than treadmill walking, with reduced validity under treadmill conditions likely related to magnetic disturbances generated by treadmill hardware and potentially also to differences in walking speed, which can influence joint excursions and interact with IMU sampling frequency[52, 63, 64]; and (2) accuracy declined proximally in the kinematic chain, with the most remarkable RMSE differences observed at the hip. These results corroborate patterns demonstrated in prior reviews (Kobsar et al. [18]; Zeng et al. [29]), which revealed distal segments to be the most reliable and proximal joint kinetic measures to be the most complex to assess.

Clinical context and sources of variability

Sagittal plane RoM errors were generally < 5°, within the commonly accepted threshold for both the ankle and knee, supporting their potential clinical applicability. This threshold is meaningful because sufficient ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion are important for shock absorption and propulsion during gait [65], while sufficient knee motion allows for load absorption at initial contact and toe clearance in the swing phase [66]. Nevertheless, the variability across studies was high (I2 = 90–96%). Many of the contextual factors that probably influence variability include IMU hardware variability (sampling rates 20–1000 Hz), sensor location (pelvis vs. sacrum vs. thigh), calibration methods (17 static vs. 6 dynamic vs. 4 no calibration), number of sensors, and data fusion methods (Kalman, complementary, and machine learning). Past reviews [17, 18] reported that inconsistencies can result in errors exceeding 3–4°, which, when considered collectively, may account for much of the variability observed in the current review. These methodological factors, summarized in Table 2, likely explain much of the heterogeneity observed in RMSE values. Therefore, calibration methods need to be standardized, and researchers need to report the data fusion method to decrease variability across studies to strengthen the generalizability of IMU-based kinematic data. Finally, it is important to recognize that validity also depends on the chosen reference system. Most studies compared IMUs against OMC for kinematics and against force plates for GRFs, both of which are considered gold standards but are not free from error. For example, OMC is affected by soft-tissue artefact and marker placement variability [3, 60, 61], while force plate estimates can be influenced by foot placement and experimental setup [5]. Such factors likely contributed to variability across studies and should be considered when interpreting RMSE values.

Methodological factors influencing IMU validity

Higher RMSE values in specific studies can be explained by clear methodological choices. Dorschky used a simplified planar musculoskeletal model and indirect GRF estimation, which limited concurrent validity for the hip and ankle [44]. Sy relied on only three IMUs with strong joint constraints, increasing hip and knee errors [51]. Shuai used a commercial system with proprietary calibration and model mismatches, leading to systematic offsets, especially at the ankle [28]. These factors explain the outliers while our pooled results remain within clinical thresholds.

Validity of IMU-based estimates of vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs

Unlike kinematic outcomes, IMU-based estimation of GRFs remains underdeveloped. Only four small studies (n = 45) reported both vertical and anterior–posterior components. Pooled RMSEs were 8.37 and 5.50%BW, respectively, with wide confidence intervals and high heterogeneity. Current approaches typically rely either on model-based inverse dynamics pipelines (using IMU-derived segment kinematics with anthropometric assumptions and contact detection) or on data-driven methods such as neural networks to predict GRFs directly from IMU signals [22, 67]. Both approaches face important challenges, including susceptibility to drift and soft-tissue artefacts, dependence on accurate anthropometric modelling, and poor generalizability across walking speeds, footwear, and surfaces [68–70]. Accordingly, IMU-based GRF estimation is promising but remains exploratory and considerably less validated than kinematic measures, which justifies the limited depth of analysis in this review.

Clinical implications

The RMSE values (generally < 5° at the ankle and knee in most conditions) indicate that IMUs offer acceptable concurrent validity. However, high heterogeneity and reduced hip concurrent validity mean that findings should be interpreted cautiously, where a high standard of precision is particularly critical. Although high heterogeneity prevents defining a single ‘optimal’ IMU setup, our synthesis points to several configurations that appear most reliable across studies: (1) sampling rates of 100–240 Hz, (2) placement on pelvis, thigh, shank, and foot, (3) static anatomical calibration, and (4) magnetometer-free or magnetically robust fusion when treadmill interference is likely. These should be regarded as evidence-informed recommendations rather than universal standards. Future work should systematically test joint- and task-specific optimization to establish reproducible best practices. Their portability, low cost, and flexibility support routine clinical gait assessments, sports monitoring, and community-based research. While IMUs have demonstrated great potential for examining joint kinematics in clinical practice and within research, IMUs are premature for GRFs. They should be thought of as investigational until validated within larger, higher-quality cohorts. The prompt translation of scientific results into everyday clinical practice is a priority for Academia Europaea and is paramount in the current medical research landscape [71, 72]. Therefore, to maximize the clinical applicability of IMUs, careful calibration protocols, appropriate sensor placement, and clear data processing protocols must be prioritized, especially when measuring hip motion or walking on a treadmill and where demands for precision are high.

Limitations and future directions

This review has several important limitations. Firstly, the majority of studies included only healthy young adults in this review, which limits generalizability for older populations or clinical groups. Sample sizes were also small, which raises concerns about statistical power and a possible underestimation of error rates. Secondly, heterogeneity was very high, largely driven by IMU hardware differences, sensor placement, calibration, sampling rates (20–1000 Hz) and data-fusion algorithms. Inconsistent or poorly reported methods also limit comparisons to the literature and reproducibility. Furthermore, the QUADAS-2 assessment indicated high or unclear risk in patient selection (restricted to healthy young adults) and in the index test domain (incomplete reporting of calibration and signal processing). These biases likely limited generalizability and may have contributed to heterogeneity in pooled RMSE values. Finally, our reliance on RMSE as the primary outcome provides information on average error but not on error direction or limits of agreement. This choice may introduce selection bias toward studies that report RMSE and underrepresent studies using alternative validity metrics. Future research should therefore incorporate complementary metrics such as intraclass correlation coefficients or Bland–Altman analysis to allow for more clinically meaningful interpretation.

The evidence bases for kinetic outcomes, particularly vertical and anterior–posterior GRFs, is still limited and methodologically inconsistent. Standardized calibration and modelling pipelines and larger and better-powered studies will be needed to strengthen confidence in these measures. Additional priorities include validation of IMUs for frontal and transverse plane kinematics, systematic evaluation of optimal sampling frequencies, and testing in clinical population (older adults, neurological or orthopedic patients) as well as free-living environments. Emerging technologies—such as flexible nanocomposites, tendon-focused wearables, and multimodal sensor systems—may further enhance gait assessment in the future [73, 74].

Finally, our analysis was limited to walking. No eligible studies examined sagittal plane RoM during running. Broader validation across tasks, environments, and patient groups will be essential before IMUs can be recommended as reliable stand-alone tools for clinical gait analysis.

Conclusion

This review shows that IMUs achieve acceptable concurrent validity (RMSE ≤ 5°) for ankle and knee sagittal plane RoM during walking, whereas performance declines at the hip and under treadmill conditions. Because the total sagittal RoM during walking is modest (≈30° ankle, 60° knee, 40° hip), even apparently small absolute errors may represent a meaningful proportion of joint excursion. The substantial heterogeneity across studies highlights the urgent need for standardized calibration, sensor placement, and reporting protocols. Evidence for GRF estimation remains preliminary. Taken together, IMUs are promising adjuncts for gait assessment but cannot yet replace gold-standard systems as stand-alone clinical instruments.
