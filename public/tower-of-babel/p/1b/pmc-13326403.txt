A de novo genome assembly of an Angelman syndrome pig (Sus scrofa domesticus) model to resolve SNHG14
Authors: Alasdair J Taylor, Nicole M Foley, Isabella R Childers, William J Murphy, Scott V Dindot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPigs (Sus scrofa domesticus) are used as biomedical and preclinical models for their comparable anatomy, physiology, and neurodevelopment to humans. Angelman syndrome is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder, caused by the loss of the maternal Ubiquitin protein ligase E3A (UBE3A) allele, that was recently modeled and characterized in Yorkshire/Landrace pigs. The paternal UBE3A allele is intact but silenced in neurons of the central nervous system by the long non-coding RNA and polycistronic transcription unit Small Nuclear Host Gene 14 (SNHG14). Targeting SNHG14 to reactivate the paternal UBE3A allele is a therapeutic approach for Angelman syndrome currently under investigation. However, the current pig reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) misassembles the highly complex SNHG14 locus, hindering accurate targeting and regulatory studies in this model. Using PacBio HiFi and Hi-C sequencing, we generated a chromosome-level assembly for an Angelman syndrome pig model and fully resolved the SNHG14 locus without gaps. We show that the current pig reference genome contains extensive tandem repeat collapse within SNHG14, misrepresenting both its size and organization. The resolved locus reveals a previously unknown bipartite architecture of SNORD116 tandem repeats, comprising two distinct groups in both sequence and structure. This resolution and genome resource remove the genomic hindrance to SNHG14-focused studies in pigs.

Introduction

Pigs (Sus scrofa domesticus) have been used as biomedical models for centuries. Their comparable anatomy, physiology, genetics, and developmental trajectory to humans are particularly advantageous when compared to rodent model (Lunney et al. 2021). A pig’s relatively low cost of care and multiple large litters a year makes pigs significantly more accessible than non-human primate models (Walters et al. 2017; Loewa et al. 2023).

In the age of genomic medicine, accurate assembly of genomic regions of biomedical importance is crucial for therapeutic design and preclinical studies in models such as pigs. The current pig reference genome (Sscrofa11.1), while contiguous, still contains unresolved disease-relevant loci. One such region, small nuclear host gene 14 (SNHG14), is associated with several neurodevelopmental disorders, including Angelman syndrome and Prader–Willi syndrome (Bieth et al. 2014; Meng et al. 2014).

A pig model of Angelman syndrome (Fig. 2A) with a complete heterozygous deletion of the maternal ubiquitin protein ligase E3A (UBE3A) allele was recently characterized and presents a new modality for investigating this genetic neurodevelopmental disorder (Myers et al. 2025). Angelman syndrome is caused by the loss of function or expression of the maternal UBE3A allele (Kishino et al. 1997; Matsuura et al. 1997). The paternal UBE3A allele remains intact but is silenced in neurons of the central nervous system (CNS) by the transcriptional elongation of SNHG14, forming an antisense transcript (Vu and Hoffman 1997; Rougeulle et al. 1997; Chamberlain and Brannan 2001; Runte et al. 2001). SNHG14 is therefore under investigation as a therapeutic target for Angelman syndrome to reactivate the paternal UBE3A allele (Huang et al. 2011; Meng et al. 2013).

SNHG14 is polycistronic, encoding multiple different transcripts from a single promoter. The long non-coding RNA and acts as a single transcriptional unit, hosting both protein-coding and non-coding genes (Runte et al. 2001). Two C/D box small nucleolar RNAs (SNORDs) hosted by SNHG14, SNORD109A, and the tandemly repeated SNORD116, form the critical region for the neurodevelopmental disorder Prader–Willi syndrome (Bieth et al. 2014). Snhg14 in murid rodents has significantly diverged from other species belonging to the mammalian superorder Euarchontoglires (e.g. primates, rodents, colugos, treeshrews, and hares), losing Snord109 and only expressing Snord116 in CNS neurons (Dindot et al. 2023; Baldini et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2014). These differences may explain why mouse and rat models of Prader–Willi syndrome fail to recapitulate some patient phenotypes and justify the need for alternative translational models to investigate the disease, such as pigs (Kummerfeld et al. 2021).

Although it is well characterized in humans and mice, SNHG14 is often misassembled in published genomes due to its large size (~800 Kb in humans) and highly repetitive structure from both segmental duplications and transposable elements. In Sscrofa11.1, homologs to genes found in human/mouse SNHG14/Snhg14 (Fig. 1A) are split between Chromosome 1 and an unplaced scaffold (Fig. 1B). It has been assumed that the resolved structure would resemble a combination of the two (Fig. 1C), but the extent of the repetitive collapse of the SNORD tandem repeats found within SNHG14 remains unclear. An unresolved SNHG14 in pigs introduces an additional hurdle to the study of, and therapeutic development for, Angelman syndrome and Prader–Willi syndrome.

Here, we present a de novo S. scrofa domesticus genome assembly of the Angelman syndrome pig model. We leveraged highly accurate long-read DNA and RNA sequencing to ensure the resolution and characterization of the SNHG14 locus and identified novel structures within it.

Biological materials

We collected all biological materials incidentally as part of other studies under TAMU IACUC protocol 2023-0179.

For PacBio HiFi DNA sequencing, a veterinary technician collected EDTA blood samples via the vena cava from a juvenile male Paternal UBE3A deletion (UBE3A+/−) Yorkshire/Landrace pig, part of a research herd held at Texas A&M University, Texas, USA. We collected paternal UBE3A+/− pig blood and maternal UBE3A+/+ (WT) fibroblasts from the same herd for Illumina sequencing. For PacBio long-read RNA-seq, we took 4 mm brain frontal cortex punches from two juvenile female pigs post-mortem from the same litter, one UBE3A−/+ and one UBE3A+/+. All samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at −80 °C, and shipped on dry ice.

DNA library preparation and sequencing

We submitted blood samples and fibroblasts to Maryland Genomics at the University of Maryland Institute for Genome Sciences for library preparation and sequencing. Their methods were as follows: high-molecular-weight DNA was extracted and HiFi libraries were prepared before sequencing on a single PacBio Revio SMRT Cell (HiFi/CCS mode, 24-h movie). Illumina UDI Library Prep and Illumina NovaSeq6000 paired-end 150 bp sequencing were used for short read parental DNA sequencing.

DNA sequence processing

HiFiAdapterFilt was used with default settings to remove any PacBio adapter sequences from HiFi reads. FastQC was used to evaluate sequence quality and generate quality metrics. We generated Kmer counts with Meryl (k = 21), for GenomeScope2.0 to estimate genome size and heterozygosity.

Hi-C sequencing reads

We downloaded publicly available Hi-C sequencing data from a male Landrace pig (SRR24831862) through the NCBI SRA database using SRA Toolkit. The reads were generated using paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The data were trimmed and quality controlled FastQC before use.

Genome assembly

An initial assembly was generated using HiFiasm using Hi-Fi reads with self-scaffolding (--dual-scaf) to produce primary and alternate assemblies. Phasing with trio binning was attempted with parental short read data in HiFiasm trio mode. Pseudohaplotypes were assembled with HiFiasm in Hi-C mode with publicly available Hi-C data. Hi-C reads were aligned to the primary/alternate and haplotypes 1/2 assemblies with BWA-MEM2 and scaffolded with YaHS without contig error correction (--no-contig-ec). Hi-C contact maps were generated with JuicerTools before visualizing and manually curating each assembly in JuiceBox. Finally, we screened the assemblies for contamination and adaptors using the NCBI FCS-GX and FCS-adaptor. The completeness of the resulting assemblies were assessed using a combination of Quast, Merqury, and the % of intact BUSCO genes.

Annotation

First, we identified and annotated repeat elements using RepeatModeler and, in combination with the Cetartiodactyla repeat dataset from Repbase (Downloaded 12 August 2025), soft masked repeats with RepeatMasker. We applied pig reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) annotations to the masked assemblies using LiftOff. We identified novel SNHG14 SNORDs by BLAST using annotated SNORDs from other placental mammal genomes with a 90% similarity threshold. Finally, we cross-referenced annotated SNORDs to further characterize them as functional or a pseudogene using SnoBIRD. Based on training data of validated SNORDs, SnoBIRD uses machine learning to predict the presence and functionality of SnoRNAs in any given genome.

RNA sequencing and alignment

Pig frontal cortex brain samples were submitted to Maryland Genomics, their sequencing methods were the following: PacBio Kinnex RNA-Seq libraries were prepared for PacBio Revio SMRT Cell Sequencing run (HiFi/CCS mode, 24 h movie).

To process the sequencing data, we used Lima (--isoseq). Next, we assessed quality and adapter presence with FastQC. We used Pbmm2 (--preset ISOSEQ), and Iso-Seq (--do-not-collapse-extra-5exons) to segment, demultiplex, cluster, and map the consensus sequences to the primary assembly.

Results

The PacBio HiFi sequencing generated 5.7 million reads covering 103.5 Gb at an average of 39.8× coverage based on the reference genome size of 2.6 Gb. Genomscope 2.0 kmer-based size estimation predicted a genome size of 2.2 Gb and a 0.63% heterozygosity rate. A histogram of kmer distribution (Fig. 2B) shows a bimodal distribution of two peaks at 19× and 38×, as is expected from the homozygous and heterozygous states of a diploid genome.

Sequencing and assembly of the Angelman syndrome pig model. (A) A pig model of Angelman syndrome colony member. (B) GenomeScope2.0 kmer histogram of PacBio HiFi sequencing. (C) Proximity ligation contact map of primary assembly. (D) Blobtools plot of primary assembly statistics.

Assembly with Hifiasm produced a primary haplotype with 163 contigs spanning a total of 2,713 Mb, and an alternative assembly with 28,814 contigs spanning 2,578 Mb (Table 2). After Hi-C scaffolding and manual curation, the primary assembly consists of 169 contigs in 152 scaffolds with a scaffold L50 of 9 and a scaffold N50 of 142 Mb (Fig. 2D). Curation of the primary haplotype introduced three breaks and six joins to resolve BLAST-validated misassemblies, such as a join between the X and Y chromosomes (Fig. 2C). The resulting assembly is highly contiguous, with all chromosomes represented in single scaffolds. The alternate assembly is significantly fragmented, containing 25,745 scaffolds after Hi-C scaffolding with a scaffold L50 of 1,892 and a scaffold N50 of 0.345 Mb.

To phase the assemblies, Hifiasm was run in trio mode with maternal and paternal short reads. Both haplotypes contained over 2000 contigs each even after the additional step of Hi-C scaffolding in YaHS. The lack of contiguity and a failure to correctly assemble the locus of interest led us to abandon this approach and phase with Hi-C to assemble pseudohaplotypes instead. Hifiasm was run in Hi-C mode to produce Haplotype 1 with 312 contigs and Haplotype 2 with 246 contigs spanning 2,696 and 2,446 Mb, respectively. After scaffolding, both haplotypes were manually curated in Juicebox to resolve validated misassemblies, resulting in 257 scaffolds for Haplotype 1, and 180 for Haplotype 2.

The final primary assembly has a BUSCO completeness of 98.1% for Cetartiodactyla (Odb10) gene sets (Fig. 1D) and a combined k-mer completeness score of 99.45%. Likewise, Haplotype 1 and 2 had scores of 98.1% and 94.6% in BUSCO, respectively, and a combined k-mer completeness of 99.76%.

Annotation identified a fully assembled SNHG14, with all homologs on a single contig in the expected order. SNURF/SNRPN, SNORD109A, and SNORD116 were found between LOC10216334 and SNORD115, as they are in humans. Sscrofa11.1 SNORD116 copies aligned with the first 210 of the 317 copies annotated by BLAST (E-value = 0), but not to the 107 that follow (Fig. 3). Of those 317 SNORD116 copies, 315 are predicted to be functional C/D box snoRNAs by snoBIRD, with two pseudogenes being found amongst the unaligned 107 copies. Although six copies of SNORD115 can be annotated by BLAST, as with Sscrofa11.1, only one is predicted to be expressed and functional.

Annotation of the region revealed repetitive collapse in the reference genome and identified novel structures within. Compared to the reference genome, we identified nearly 16× the number of SNORD116 copies (317 copies versus 20), including novel variants and groups. A self-alignment heat map (Fig. 4A) visualizes grouping within the tandem repeats of SNORD116, from here on referred to as Group I (proximal) and Group II (distal). The groups differ consistently in repeat unit structure (Fig. 4B), SNORD116 sequence (Fig. 4,C), and host gene exon sequence (54.6% similarity). SnoRNA sequences are highly consistent within groups (99.8% similarity), in contrast to between them (93.6% similarity).

Discussion

Highly accurate long-read sequencing technologies, such as PacBio HiFi, have enabled the characterization of repetitive genomic regions (Wenger et al. 2019). We generated a highly contiguous genome with the goal of resolving a disease-relevant locus incorrectly assembled due to the repetitive collapse that is found in most published short-read reliant pig genomes, including the current reference genome Sscrofa11.1. Although this assembly does not meet the current “telomere-to-telomere” gold standard for model organism reference genomes, it provides a high-quality resource for comparative and disease modeling studies.

The 15q11-q13 region that contains SNHG14 is heavily imprinted, with both paternal- and maternal-specific allelic expression being found throughout (Chamberlain and Lalande 2010). To aid in identifying maternal and paternal specific RNA transcripts, we used trio-binning with parental short-read sequencing. The resulting phased assemblies were highly fragmented (2000+ contigs), failed to resolve SNHG14, and only improved marginally with Hi-C scaffolding. This may have occurred due to a lack of genetic distance between parents in the research colony, or from insufficient short-read sequencing coverage at ~15×. For these reasons, we proceeded without trio binning, producing a highly contiguous primary assembly, and with Hi-C-based phasing to produce pseudohaplotypes as a resource for further study.

We chose to present analysis and characterization of SNHG14 from the primary assembly as it is the most contiguous and scores marginally better than Haplotype 1 on completeness metrics. To avoid bias and to confirm our findings, we compared this to the other assemblies. The primary assembly’s SNHG14 is the same length as Haplotypes 1 and 2 and contains the same copy number of SNORDs, Repeating the pairwise consensus alignments for Haplotypes 1 and 2 returned identical percentage variation for SNORD116 (99.8% similarity within groups, 93.6% within groups) and host gene exons (54.6% similarity). Haplotypes 1 and 2 differ significantly notably in the deletion of UBE3A in Haplotype 2, and Haplotype 1 containing an additional Group I SNORD116 (211 total), and one fewer Group II SNORD116 (106 total). This level of homozygosity was expected, given the pedigree of the research colony.

Characterization of SNHG14 in pigs revealed a novel structure in the SNORD116 tandem repeats that divides it between two groups. The highly conserved bipartite nature of SNORD116 Groups I and II implies a recent and rapid expansion from two diverged ancestral repeat units, and a high degree of positive selection and gene conversion over time to maintain each group and its identity. Further analysis and practical studies would be required to determine whether these two groups are independent in function, or whether their mechanism of action is conserved and their divergence incidental to the expansion of the host gene instead. The copy numbers of SNORD116 and SNORD115 in this assembly (317 and 6, respectively) differ significantly from other well characterized species such as Homo sapiens (29 and 48, respectively) and Mus musculus (69 and 133, respectively), implying significant lineage specific expansions or contractions of the locus across placental mammals (Dindot et al. 2023). Although the timing and frequency of these events are currently unknown, recent efforts to assemble highly accurate long-read based genomes of other model and non-model species, such as ours, make an evolutionary study of complex loci like this more feasible than ever before.

In conclusion, we present a highly contiguous genome assembly for the biomedically and agriculturally relevant S. scrofa domesticus. As a genomic resource, this genome is the first genome of a pig biomedical model and joins the small but growing number of highly contiguous pig genomes now available for pre-clinical, comparative, and pangenomic studies. This genome was assembled with the primary goals of resolving and characterizing the SNHG14 locus in pigs to support their current and future potential as preclinical models for Angelman syndrome and Prader–Willi syndrome, respectively.
