A Response to: Letter to the Editor Regarding “Aflibercept 8 mg Versus Faricimab Treat-and-Extend for Diabetic Macular Edema or Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration: A Bayesian Fixed-Effect Network Meta-analysis of Clinical Trials”
Authors: Scott M Friedman, Yingxin Xu, Steven Sherman, Andreas Kuznik, Ali Mojebi, Sam Keeping, Keith Chan, Theodore Leng, Nimesh Patel
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Dr. Panos for his careful reading of our article, Aflibercept 8 mg versus Faricimab Treat-and-Extend for Diabetic Macular Edema or Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration: A Bayesian Fixed-Effect Network Meta-analysis of Clinical Trials [1], and for his thoughtful comments [2]. We welcome the opportunity to clarify methodological considerations and to further contextualize our findings.

Differences in Injection Frequency

With respect to differences in injection frequency between aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab treat‑and‑extend (T&E) regimens, we agree that cumulative injection numbers observed in clinical trials are influenced by protocol‑specific design features, and this consideration was directly addressed in the original article. As outlined in both the Discussion and Key Summary Points, the observed difference in injection numbers reflects, in part, the higher number of initial loading injections required in the faricimab trials and the longer maximum extension intervals permitted in trials of aflibercept 8 mg. We stated that “differences in aflibercept 8‑mg and faricimab trial designs influenced the number of injections observed in the trials” and emphasized that a network meta-analysis (NMA) cannot separate treatment effects from protocol-driven features.

Importantly; however, the interpretation of injection frequency should not be considered in isolation from efficacy outcomes. Across the respective pivotal trial programs, the two treatment approaches yielded efficacy results that were broadly comparable in terms of visual and anatomic outcomes. Within this context, it is notable that such comparable efficacy was achieved with fewer injections for aflibercept 8 mg. Furthermore, if one were to shorten the extension intervals of aflibercept 8 mg, it is reasonable to expect that efficacy outcomes could be maintained or potentially further optimized, underscoring the interplay between dosing frequency and treatment effect.

The objective of the NMA was not to ascribe causality to a single mechanistic explanation (e.g., biological durability versus protocol structure), but rather to compare treatment exposure as observed under the respective pivotal trial paradigms, for which injection burden within approved dosing algorithms remains a clinically relevant outcome. To account for differences in the duration of trials, injection counts were standardized to a 104-week period as described in the Supplementary Appendix. Within this context, the key finding of our analysis is that treatment with aflibercept 8 mg is associated with a lower number of injections over 2 years while similar visual and anatomic efficacy outcomes are achieved. However, our conclusions were framed cautiously and were not intended to attribute the difference solely to an inherent durability advantage independent of trial design.

Network Structure and Model Choice

We acknowledge the sparse nature of the evidence networks and the absence of closed loops. As detailed in the Methods and Supplementary Appendix, both fixed-effect and random-effects models were evaluated in accordance with established National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Decision Support Unit and International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) guidance. Given the limited number of trials contributing to each disease-specific network and lack of stable heterogeneity estimates, fixed‑effect models were deemed the most appropriate under these conditions. We agree that clinical heterogeneity across trials represents an inherent limitation of indirect comparisons, and this was directly acknowledged in the manuscript.

Evidence Sources and Data Provenance

We appreciate the comment regarding the use of peer-reviewed publications for faricimab trials and clinical study reports for the 96-week PHOTON and PULSAR data at the time of analysis. This distinction was clearly stated to ensure transparency. Clinical study reports represent prespecified, regulatory-grade data sources and are commonly used in evidence synthesis when peer-reviewed publications are not yet available. We would note that the 96-week results from PHOTON and PULSAR have since been published [3, 4], further supporting the robustness of the data included in the analysis.

Additional Limitations

We agree that differences in prior exposure to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors across the diabetic macular edema (DME) trials, outcome definitions, and the derivation of percentage changes in central subfield thickness (CST) represent important limitations. Adjustment for prior treatment status was not performed because subgroup data were not consistently available across trials; however, any resulting bias would be expected to favor faricimab, given the higher proportion of previously treated patients in the trial of aflibercept 8 mg (PHOTON) compared with those of faricimab (YOSEMITE and RHINE) [5], rendering the current conclusions conservative. CST and central retinal thickness (CRT) were considered to be equivalent on the basis of shared optical coherence tomography-based measurement of the central 1-mm subfield, consistent with prior NMAs in DME and neovascular age-related macular degeneration although potential differences in definitions or measurement protocols were acknowledged as a limitation. Further to this, percentage change from baseline in CST had to be derived for faricimab since these values were not directly reported. The assumptions made were tested in sensitivity analyses to help contextualize observed anatomic outcomes given the baseline imbalances, rather than replace the primary analyses. As noted in the Supplementary Appendix, these derivations required assumptions, and the resulting estimates were therefore interpreted with appropriate caution.

In summary, we are grateful for the thoughtful feedback provided by Dr. Panos. Our original article proactively addressed the comments within the limitations section and ensured that our conclusions were carefully and responsibly framed to reflect these considerations. The analyses were presented as hypothesis‑generating and were intended to inform discussion in the absence of direct comparative evidence. In line with the original manuscript, we reiterate that “NMAs have inherent limitations and cannot replace head-to-head trials.” Accordingly, further head-to-head or real-world comparative studies are warranted to confirm these observations.
