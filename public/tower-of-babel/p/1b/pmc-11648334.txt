Delusional Parasitosis in a Patient With a History of COVID-19 and Substance Use Disorder
Authors: Jami Wang, Brian Kato, Samantha Li, Davin A Agustines
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDelusional parasitosis is a psychotic disorder that manifests as a belief that the skin is infested with parasites. This case report documents the association between COVID-19 infection and new-onset delusional parasitosis superimposed on preexisting psychosis, which has not been previously documented in the current literature. A 28-year-old male presented with new-onset delusional parasitosis superimposed on existing psychosis following COVID-19 infection. Computed tomography scan of the head was unremarkable. He was treated with olanzapine, which appeared to lessen the intensity of the delusions. This case report should encourage increased monitoring of COVID-positive patients with psychosis.

Introduction

Delusional parasitosis is an uncommon psychotic disorder that presents with the belief that the skin is infested with parasites. These claims are not corroborated by medical evidence and often lead to symptoms of depression. Therefore, delusional parasitosis is often misdiagnosed for depression. Many patients with this condition refuse psychiatric medications due to feeling confident that the root cause is infestation rather than psychosis.1

Substance use disorder is known to be associated with delusional psychosis, as various substances can trigger or exacerbate psychotic symptoms.2 The interplay between substance use disorder and psychotic disorders is complex because substances can both induce psychotic episodes and be used by individuals to self-medicate their symptoms. In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, there have been instances of new-onset psychotic symptoms, including delusions and hallucinations, following infection.3 In this case, the patient had a history of substance use disorder, but with new onset of COVID-19. This suggests that psychosis can arise from either substance use disorder, COVID-19 infection, or a combination of both factors.

Although COVID-19 infection has been associated with new-onset psychotic symptoms, such as delusions and auditory/visual hallucinations, this is the first case of a patient with new-onset delusional parasitosis superimposed on existing psychosis following COVID-19 infection.3 Informed consent was provided by the patient.

Case Patient Presentation

A 28-year-old male with a history of polysubstance use disorder and bipolar and psychotic disorder presented to the psychiatry emergency department due to suicidal ideations with a plan to run into traffic. The patient had a history of multiple incarcerations, psychiatric hospitalizations, homelessness, and demonstrated danger to self and others. When discussing why the patient wanted to jump into traffic, he described pain from a parasite, radiating “from head to toe.” Per the chart, the patient described it as a “giant worm in my stomach.” He described being affected by the parasite about 4 weeks prior and had tried over-the-counter supplements for pinworms and parasites. However, the pain remained ongoing. The patient’s drug screen came back positive for cannabis. For the first time, the patient tested positive for COVID-19 despite being asymptomatic. On the mental status examination, the patient was disorganized and hostile, with suicidal ideation, paranoia, delusions, and poor insight, judgment, and impulse control. No other neuropathy symptoms were noted on examination.

On the most recent admission, nearly 4 months after the first presentation, the patient presented again to the psychiatry inpatient unit following suicidal ideation and a self-inflicted laceration to his forehead with a knife 2 weeks prior. He was visibly anxious, pacing and moving extremities, picking at his skull, and actively discussed suicidal ideations due to the parasite. He maintained the thought content that the parasites were still in his bloodstream and that he had to cut his forehead to “let the parasites out.” His perverseness of the parasites was evident as he stated, “the parasites have been so bad I might have to kill myself.” The patient believed the incident caused him to lose his job. The forehead wound was 8 cm × 4 cm on the forehead, nonerythematous, with granulation tissues but no pus. A computed tomography (CT) scan and ova parasite stool test were ordered, and both tests returned negative for parasites. An updated urine toxicology result showed positive for amphetamine, benzodiazepine, and cannabis. In addition, his COVID-19 test was negative.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient’s presentation was complex, with a history of existing psychiatric diagnoses, including bipolar and psychotic disorder. His new symptom of pain due to parasites all over his body, so bothersome to the point that he cut himself trying to get rid of them, prompted the possibility that there might have actually been parasites. However, imaging was negative for parasites. Although the patient had a long history of psychiatric hospitalizations due to unspecified psychosis and polysubstance use disorder, this was the first time he reported parasitosis. His conviction for his parasitosis remained high, to the point that he felt the need to scalp his forehead. The only difference between his previous hospitalization and the current presentation was the diagnosis of COVID-19. It is also possible that substances, such as amphetamines, were the cause of the delusions in the initial presentation but were undetected in the urine test due to the time elapsed since the substance was taken. However, the patient had a history of amphetamine use by urine test prior without symptoms of delusional parasitosis. As a result, the likelihood of COVID-19–induced parasitosis was more likely than substance-induced parasitosis.

Clinical Reasoning

No cases of new-onset delusional parasitosis superimposed on existing psychosis following COVID-19 infection have been reported in the literature to date. One case of new-onset delusional parasitosis following COVID-19 infection was reported in a patient with no history of psychosis. However, this patient had a subacute infarct in the basal ganglia associated with secondary delusional parasitosis.4 A head CT scan of the patient did not show any infarcts or structural abnormalities and did not reveal any evidence of parasites. Furthermore, testing the stool for ova and parasites yielded no positive results.

It has been hypothesized that the high affinity of COVID-19 for angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 receptors, which are expressed in the central nervous system, can cause neurologic disease. This can manifest as cerebrovascular accidents, seizures, and encephalopathy, possibly contributing to secondary delusional parasitosis.5 The fact that a head CT scan of the patient did not reveal any of these pathologies may suggest that COVID-19 can exacerbate existing psychosis through an unknown process.

Treatment Pathway

During the patient’s first visit to the psychiatry emergency department due to suicidal ideations with a plan to run into traffic, the patient was given daily olanzapine intramuscular injections. However, over time, he grew more upset because the “worm” was still there. As a result of his mental status examination, the patient was prescribed olanzapine 5 mg daily. He was disorganized and hostile, with suicidal ideation, paranoia, delusions, and poor insight, judgment, and impulse control.

Four months later, he was again admitted to the psychiatry inpatient following suicidal ideation and a self-inflicted laceration to his forehead with a knife 2 weeks prior. The superficial cut was then treated with bacitracin. However, the cut was large enough that it required a plastic surgery follow-up. During this admission, olanzapine was titrated up to 15 mg twice a day.

Outcomes

Despite being treated with olanzapine 15 mg twice a day at his second admission, he continued to ruminate about the parasite for the first week during his hospitalization. Throughout the treatment, the patient said the parasite was still there but less severe than before. Toward the end of his treatment, the patient mentioned that he was “feeling a lot better.” There have been no follow-ups after the second admission.

Key Learning Takeaways

This case report contributed to practice change by considering COVID-19 as a contributor to psychiatric diagnoses. The present case is the first to suggest the possibility that the neurologic sequelae of COVID-19 may intensify delusional symptoms in patients with existing psychosis without altering brain structure. Current literature indicates an association between COVID-19 and psychosis, but often in the presence of an underlying brain structure abnormality and without preexisting psychotic symptoms. The absence of brain infarcts and other abnormalities in this patient suggests an alternative pathway in the aggravation of psychosis, resulting in delusional parasitosis.

There are still many unknown effects of COVID-19 that are slowly unraveling. It is important to document all the possible effects that it may have on one’s central nervous system. There have been documented cases of new-onset psychosis following COVID-19 infection in patients without a history of psychosis. However, the present paper is the first to discuss COVID-19 being associated with delusional parasitosis in a patient with a history of schizophrenia. It is important to consider the possibility of heightened psychotic symptoms in patients with preexisting psychosis after infection with COVID-19. Perhaps there should be an emphasis on closely monitoring COVID-positive patients with psychosis and documenting the specific type of psychosis patients tend to experience. This may help to prevent self-harm and potential danger to others. The patient provided informed consent for the case study.
