Functional characterization of PpGS2 promoter in response to hormonal and environmental signals in Kentucky bluegrass
Authors: Shasha Liu, Xiashun Liu, Chen Wang, Mengyao Sang, Qinyi Wang, Qiyun Wei, Lulu He, Fuchun Xie, Jia Jiang, Yajun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis) is an important cool-season turfgrass that requires high nitrogen (N) fertilizer inputs for optimal density, and turf quality. PpGS2 is a key gene involved in N uptake and assimilation; however, the core regulatory region of its promoter remains unclear. To characterize the transcriptional regulation of the PpGS2 promoter, a series of 5’-terminal deletion fragments were generated, each fused to the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene and introduced into Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) and tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum). The transcriptional activity of all fragments decreased as fragment length shortened. The full-length promoter drove the strongest GUS expression in the leaves of transgenic Arabidopsis. Furthermore, exogenous hormone treatment, low-N stress, and high-light stress all significantly enhanced promoter activity. Among these treatments, methyl jasmonate (MeJA) elicited the strongest induction of promoter activity. Deletion analysis revealed distinct distributions of responsive elements of the PpGS2 promoter: gibberellin (GA) and abscisic acid (ABA) responsive elements were primarily located between − 2000 bp and − 1600 bp and − 1200 bp to -800 bp, respectively; auxin (IAA) and MeJA responses relied on the synergy of multiple regions. The low-N response was mediated by both positive and negative cis-acting elements. Specifically, the − 2000 bp to -1200 bp region likely contained repressive elements, whereas the − 1200 bp to -500 bp region harbored enhancing elements. Light-responsive elements were mainly concentrated within the − 1600 bp to -800 bp regions. In summary, this study provides a preliminary characterization of the regulatory pattern of the PpGS2 promoter, providing insight into the synergistic regulatory mechanisms of PpGS2 in response to hormonal and environmental signals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09056-x.Keywords: PpGS2, Promoter, Hormone, Low-nitrogen, Light stress

Introduction

Kentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis), native to Eurasia, is a globally important perennial cool-season turfgrass species [1]. Valued for its desirable characteristics, including strong cold tolerance, dark green foliage, and rapid establishment, it is widely used in golf courses, sports fields, and recreational green spaces [2]. However, Kentucky bluegrass has a high nitrogen fertilizer requirement during growth; excessive nitrogen application not only increases turf maintenance costs but also leads to a range of environmental issues [3]. Therefore, developing nitrogen-efficient cultivars is essential for achieving sustainable turf management.

In soils, inorganic nitrogen primarily exists as ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻), however, plants cannot directly utilize these inorganic forms for metabolic processes and must assimilate them into organic nitrogen compounds to support growth and development [4]. Glutamine synthetase (GS) plays a central role in the nitrogen assimilation. Plant GS enzymes are classified into GS1 and GS2, both encoded by nuclear genes but differing in subcellular localization and physiological functions. GS1 is localized in the cytoplasm and is predominantly present in non-photosynthetic tissues such as roots and stems, where it is responsible for primary nitrogen assimilation, long-distance nitrogen transport, and nitrogen remobilization [5]. In contrast, GS2 is primarily localized in chloroplasts (plastids) and plays a crucial role in the efficient assimilation of excess ammonia which is generated from nitrate reduction and photorespiration [6].

The activity and expression level of GS2 directly influence nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) and photosynthetic capacity in plants. For example, in rice (Oryza sativa ), overexpression of GS2 enhanced nitrogen uptake and significantly improved NUE [7]; overexpression of NtGS2 in tobacco promoted amino acid accumulation, increases NUE, accelerates seedling growth, and raised plant fresh weight by 20–30% [8]. Similar outcomes of GS2 overexpression were also observed under low-nitrogen conditions. For example, studies have found overexpressing GS2 in Arabidopsis plants and wheat (Triticum aestivum) exhibited enhanced nitrogen uptake efficiency and increased grain nitrogen remobilization capacity [9–10]. More recently, overexpression of GS2 in duckweed (Lemna minor) increased biomass and protein content by 10% and 24%, respectively [11]. Collectively, these findings indicate that elevated GS2 expression enhances nitrogen assimilation and metabolic efficiency, thereby promoting biomass accumulation. Given that GS2 expression is regulated by its upstream promoter region, functional characterization of the GS2 promoter is essential for a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying GS2 mediated nitrogen assimilation and utilization in plants.

The promoter region of the GS2 gene not only contains low-nitrogen-responsive elements, but also other abiotic stress-responsive elements (e.g., high light intensity, drought, low temperature) [12–15]. In barley (Hordeum vulgare), HvGS2 mutants exhibited a reduced maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) under high-light conditions [16]. In tea plants (Camellia sinensis), dark conditions significantly inhibit CsGS2 expression, consistent with the presence of light-responsive elements in its promoter [13]. Additionally, the drought responsiveness of the GS2 promoter appears to be variety-specific in rice, with stable expression observed in drought-tolerant varieties but reduced expression in drought-sensitive ones [14].

Critically, nitrogen availability and signaling interact extensively with phytohormone networks, forming an integrated system that coordinates the expression of genes involved in nitrogen metabolism [17–18]. Most previous studies have primarily focused on the responses of the GS2 promoter to environmental signals, while the specific hormone-responsive elements within the GS2 promoter, as well as the mechanisms by which they mediate hormone signaling and synergistically regulate transcription in response to environmental signals, remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the functional characteristics of the PpGS2 promoter in Kentucky bluegrass. The upstream promoter region at the 5’ end of the PpGS2 gene was cloned, and its cis-acting elements were analyzed. A series of GUS reporter constructs carrying truncated promoter fragments were generated and introduced into Arabidopsis and tobacco. By integrating GUS histochemical staining, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and GUS enzymatic activity assays, we systematically characterized promoter activity, tissue-specific expression patterns, and transcriptional responses to various phytohormones, low-nitrogen stress, and light stress. Collectively, this study provides a theoretical basis for elucidating the transcriptional regulatory networks of nitrogen metabolism in turfgrass and identifies potential molecular targets for breeding nitrogen-efficient turfgrass germplasm.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions and treatment

Two-year-old ‘Maoershan’ Kentucky bluegrass turf was collected from the experimental field of Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China; 44°29′N, 127°17′E) and transplanted into plastic pots (9.5 cm length × 11.3 cm width × 10.5 cm height). Each pot contained 30 seedlings and 500 g of growth substrate composed of black soil (pH = 7.0, organic matter content 16.8 g/kg), and vermiculite, at a ratio of 3:1:1 (v/v/v). Plants were grown in a controlled-environment growth chamber under the following conditions: temperature 25 °C ± 2 °C (day) /18°C ± 2 °C (night), relative humidity 65% ± 5%, photoperiod 13 h (h) (day) /9 h (night), light intensity 700 ± 10 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. Plants were irrigated every 2 days (d) to maintain adequate moisture conditions and trimmed every 3 d to maintain a turf height of 4–5 cm. After 45 d of growth, aerial tissues were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for promoter cloning.

Wild-type Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) was used for genetic transformation of PpGS2 promoter. Seeds were sterilized, rinsed in sterile distilled water, and sown on solid Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium. After cold stratification at 4 °C for 3 d, seedlings were grown for 12 d under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 70% relative humidity, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod). Seedlings were then transplanted into pots containing a mixture of perlite and peat moss (1:2 ratio) (top diameter 9.8 cm × bottom diameter 6.5 cm × height 8.2 cm), with 120 g of substrate per pot. Plants were placed in an artificial climate chamber (temperature 23 °C, light intensity 700 ± 10 µmol m⁻²·s⁻¹, 16 h (day) /8 h (night) photoperiod, humidity 70%), and watered regularly to keep the soil moist. Plants were used for genetic transformation approximately four weeks after germination. Nicotiana benthamiana culture methods were identical to those for Arabidopsis.

Homozygous T3 transgenic Arabidopsis plants were cultured under the same conditions as wild-type plants. After 4 weeks of growth, plants were subjected to low-nitrogen, high-light, and hormone treatments. For low-nitrogen treatment, plants were cultured in Hoagland nutrient solution containing 0.5 mM NaNO₃ for 7 d, while plants grown in standard Hoagland solution served as controls. For high-light stress, plants were exposed to a light intensity of 1000 ± 10 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 4 h at 22 °C; control plants were maintained at a light intensity of 700 ± 10 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ under the same temperature conditions. For hormone treatments, leaves were sprayed with 100 µM abscisic acid (ABA), 1 mM indole-3-butyric acid (IAA), 1 mM gibberellic acid (GA), or 1 mM methyl jasmonate (MeJA), with deionized water used as the control. Following treatment, plants were cultivated for an additional 24 h. GUS transcript levels and GUS enzymatic activity were subsequently analyzed. The treatment method of Kentucky bluegrass is the same as that of Arabidopsis. Three independent potted seedlings (each containing 10–12 plants) were selected for each treatment as biological replicates, the plants in each pot were mixed into one sample, and three technical replicates were analyzed for each sample.

Cloning of promoters and bioinformatics analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from frozen aboveground tissues of Kentucky bluegrass using a plant genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Based on the PpGS2 coding sequence available in NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the published Kentucky bluegrass reference genome, the promoter region was defined as the 2000 bp sequence immediately upstream of the translation start codon. Primers specific to the putative promoter region were designed for PCR amplification (Table S1). PCR products were purified and sequenced, and the correctly assembled promoter sequence (PR0) was analyzed for cis-acting elements using the PlantCARE online tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Promoter truncation and vector construction

PCR amplification yielded promoter fragments with 5’-end deletions (Table S1). The resulting fragments were designated PR1, PR2, PR3, and PR4, with lengths of 1600 bp, 1200 bp, 800 bp, and 500 bp, respectively. The full-length promoter (PR0) and truncated fragments were individually cloned upstream of the GUS reporter gene in the pCAMBIA3301 vector, replacing the CaMV 35 S promoter, using homologous recombination (Table S2). The ligation products were transformed into DH5α competent cells, and positive clones were screened on plates containing 100 mg/L kanamycin (Kan) and verified by colony PCR. Sequencing performed by Kumei, (Jilin, China) confirmed the correct recombinant plasmids, designated pCAMBIA3301-PR0, pCAMBIA3301-PR1, pCAMBIA3301-PR2, pCAMBIA3301-PR3, and pCAMBIA3301-PR4.

Acquisition and identification of transgenic plants

The recombinant plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and subsequently transformed into Arabidopsis using the floral dip method [19]. First, T0 seeds were disinfected and sown on medium containing 0.001% Basta for preliminary screening. Healthy green seedlings were transplanted, and T1 seeds were harvested from individual plants. Subsequently, PCR molecular validation was performed on T1 plants to confirm transgenic positivity, and their T2 seeds were collected. Subsequently, T2 seeds were sown for Basta resistance screening. Lines exhibiting a 3:1 resistance segregation ratio were selected, cultured, and harvested for T3 seeds, which were subsequently validated for resistance. Those with 100% seedling survival were identified as homozygous transgenic lines. For each promoter construct PR0-PR4, three independent homozygous T3 lines were selected as biological replicates for subsequent GUS assays, hormone treatments, and qRT-PCR analyses. For each biological replicate (one T3 line), three individual plants were pooled to generate a single sample, and three technical replicates were performed per sample.

Transient expression assays were performed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves using Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration. Agrobacterium cultures harboring the indicated constructs were resuspended in infiltration buffer and introduced into the abaxial surface of fully expanded leaves using a needleless syringe until the leaf tissue was completely infiltrated. For each construct, five independent tobacco plants were used as biological replicates, and four to five leaves were infiltrated per plant. Leaves infiltrated with Agrobacterium carrying the empty pCAMBIA3301 vector were used as negative controls. After infiltration, plants were maintained in darkness for 2–3 d and subsequently transferred to normal growth conditions for further analyses.

GUS staining and activity assay

GUS histological staining was performed using a GUS staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing) following the manufacturer’s instructions.The staining solution was prepared by mixing X-Gluc solution (50x) with GUS buffer at a 1:50 ratio. Transgenic tobacco leaves and 4-week-old PR0 transgenic Arabidopsis plants were immersed in GUS staining solution (three independent T3 lines of PR0 were used, and three individual plants from each line were stained separately to verify consistency). The reaction proceeded continuously at 37 °C in a constant-temperature metal bath for 8–12 h. Subsequently, the staining solution was removed and 75% ethanol was added for decolorization. The ethanol solution was replaced every 5–10 min until endogenous pigments in the plant tissues were completely removed. After decolorization, samples were prepared for temporary mounting. Specific blue precipitate distribution patterns were observed and documented using an E200MV optical microscope (Nikon, Japan).

GUS enzyme activity was measured using an ELISA kit (SolaBio, Beijing). Proteins extracted from the test samples were mixed with the GUS detection buffer and incubated at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated with 0.2 M termination buffer (NaCO₃) and fluorescence intensity was measured using a Varioskan Flash (Thermo, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 365 nm and an emission wavelength of 455 nm. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford protein assay [20].

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR Validation

Total RNA was extracted from transgenic Arabidopsis using the EasyPure® Plant RNA Kit (TianGen, Beijing). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Japan). Gene expression levels were measured on a Bio-Rad CFX 96 real-time PCR system using Vazyme’s ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Low ROX). Primers used are listed in Table S3. The reaction program was set as follows: 10 min pre-denaturation at 95 °C; followed by 40 cycles of amplification, each cycle comprising 10 s denaturation at 95 °C, 30 s annealing at 58 °C, and 10 s extension at 72 °C; post-cycle extension at 72 °C for 10 min, then final storage at 4 °C. Experiments were conducted with three independent biological and technical replicates. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCT) method with AtACTIN as the internal control [21].

Data processing and analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For comparisons involving more than two groups (Figs. 4 and 6), one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Different letters above the bars indicate significant differences at p < 0.05, and bars sharing the same letter are not significantly different. For comparisons between two groups (Figs. 3B and 5, each treatment vs. its respective control), Student’s t-test was applied. Significance is indicated by *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Cloning and bioinformatics analysis of the PpGS2 promoter

To investigate the transcriptional regulation mechanism of the GS2 gene, a 2000 bp promoter fragment upstream of the translation start site (ATG) was cloned from genomic DNA and designated PR0 (Fig. 1). Analysis of this sequence via the PlantCare online platform revealed that, in addition to core promoter elements such as the TATA-box and CAAT-box, it contained multiple functional cis-acting elements associated with stress response, hormone signaling, and light response. Among these, abiotic stress-related elements included the drought response element DRE, the low temperature response element LTR, the TATABOX5 involved in low-nitrogen response, and multiple MYB/MYC binding sites (Table 1). Hormone response elements comprised the abscisic acid (ABA) response element ABRE, the gibberellin (GA) response element GARE-motif, and the auxin (IAA) response element AuxRR-core. Additionally, multiple light response elements such as G-Box, GATA-motif, and GT1-motif were identified (Table 1). These findings suggest that PpGS2 gene expression may be co-regulated by multiple environmental stresses, plant hormones, and light signals.

Analysis of the transcriptional activity of the PpGS2 promoter

To evaluate the transcriptional activity of the PpGS2 promoter, the full-length PR0 fragment was used as a template to generate a series of 5’-end deletion constructs, designated PR1, PR2, PR3, and PR4, with lengths of 1600 bp, 1200 bp, 800 bp, and 500 bp, respectively (Fig. 2A). These constructs were introduced into tobacco leaves by Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation, followed by histochemical GUS staining. Leaves expressing the full-length PR0 promoter exhibited strong blue staining compared with the empty-vector control, indicating robust activation of GUS expression by PR0. In contrast, PR1, PR2, and PR3 drove progressively weaker GUS staining as the promoter length decreased, whereas the shortest fragment, PR4, displayed only faint staining (Fig. 2B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the five PpGS2 promoter fragments (PR0-PR4) exhibited distinct transcriptional activities, with promoter activity positively correlated with fragment length, suggesting that upstream regulatory regions are required for full promoter function.

Characterization of PpGS2 promoter expression in different tissues of Arabidopsis

To investigate the expression properties of the PpGS2 promoter in different tissues of Arabidopsis, histochemical staining was performed on 4-week-old WT and PR0 transgenic Arabidopsis plants. The experimental results showed that the PpGS2 full-length promoter drove the transcriptional activity of the GUS gene in all tissues examined compared with WT, with particularly strong activity in leaves, followed by stems and roots (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analysis of GUS gene expression driven by the PpGS2 full-length promoter using qRT-PCR showed that the GUS gene was expressed at the highest level in transgenic Arabidopsis leaves, approximately 1.41-fold higher than in stems (Fig. 3B). This result is consistent with the histochemical staining, which indicated that the PpGS2 promoter drives GUS reporter gene expression primarily in Arabidopsis leaves and secondarily in stems.

Expression analysis of GUS genes driven by PpGS2 promoter under abiotic stresses

To further investigate the response of the PpGS2 promoter to exogenous hormones, low-nitrogen stress and light stress, PR0 transgenic Arabidopsis plants were used to detect the relative expression levels of GUS genes following these treatments. The results showed that all four phytohormones (GA, IAA, ABA, and MeJA), as well as low-nitrogen stress and light stress, significantly induced the expression of GUS genes compared with the control, although the intensity of induction varied among treatments (Fig. 4). More specifically, GUS expression was highest under the MeJA treatment, approximately 10.12-fold higher than the control, followed by the low-nitrogen treatment, which increased GUS gene expression by about 4.32-fold. The PpGS2 promoter was less sensitive to IAA, ABA and light stress, with GUS gene expression levels of approximately 2.24-, 2.35- and 1.76-fold of the control, respectively, and the PpGS2 promoter was the weakest in response to GA, which was about 1.42-fold of the control. These results indicated that the PpGS2 promoter could respond to a variety of hormone signals, low-nitrogen stress and light stress, with the response to MeJA being particularly significant.

Analysis of transgenic Arabidopsis GUS enzyme activity under abiotic stresses

To identify the key cis-regulatory regions within the PpGS2 promoter that mediate responses to phytohormones, low-nitrogen stress, and light signals, GUS enzymatic activity was quantified in PR0-PR4 transgenic Arabidopsis lines following treatments with GA, IAA, ABA, MeJA, low-nitrogen, and high light conditions (Fig. 5). Following GA treatment, a pronounced increase in GUS activity was observed exclusively in the PR0 transgenic line, with an approximately 1.27-fold induction relative to the control, and no significant changes were detected in PR1, PR2, PR3, or PR4 (Fig. 5A). This pattern suggests that GA responsiveness of the PpGS2 promoter is primarily dependent on cis-acting elements located within the − 2000 to -1600 bp region. IAA (Fig. 5B) and MeJA (Fig. 5D) treatments significantly enhanced GUS activity in all transgenic lines compared with the control. However, GUS activity progressively declined with successive promoter truncation, indicating that IAA and MeJA responsive regulation involves multiple cis-acting elements distributed across the promoter and these elements likely function synergistically to regulate PpGS2 transcription. Under ABA treatment, GUS activity was significantly increased in PR0, PR1, and PR2 transgenic lines, whereas no significant differences were detected in PR3 and PR4 compared with the control (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that ABA responsive cis-acting elements are mainly localized within the − 1200 to -800 bp region of the PpGS2 promoter.

Low-nitrogen stress led to increased GUS activity in all transgenic lines (Fig. 5E); among them, PR1 and PR2 showed the highest activity under low-nitrogen conditions, reaching 1.63-fold and 1.72-fold that of PR0, respectively. The absence of the − 2000 to -1200 bp region in PR1 and PR2, coupled with their high expression under low-nitrogen treatment, indicated that this region typically inhibit low- nitrogen-induced expression, suggesting it contains negative regulatory elements. In contrast, further deletion to -500 bp reduced GUS activity, indicating that the − 1200 to -500 bp region harbors positive regulatory elements required for complete low-nitrogen response. In response to high light conditions, no significant change in GUS activity was detected in the PR0 transgenic line compared with the control, whereas PR1 and PR2 lines, corresponding to the − 1600 to -800 bp region, exhibited significantly elevated GUS activity (Fig. 5F), indicating that this region likely represents an important light-responsive domain of the PpGS2 promoter.

Validation of PpGS2 expression in native Kentucky bluegrass

To validate the regulatory patterns observed in heterologous systems, we treated wild-type Kentucky bluegrass seedlings with the same phytohormones (GA, IAA, ABA, MeJA), low-nitrogen, and light stress conditions. qRT-PCR analysis showed that PpGS2 transcript levels were significantly induced under all treatments (Fig. 6), and its response pattern was similar to the GUS activity driven by the PpGS2 promoter fragment in Arabidopsis, although the induction fold different. Specifically, MeJA treatment exhibited the strongest inductive effect, with PpGS2 expression levels approximately 10.44-fold higher than those of the control, followed by low-nitrogen treatment, which resulted in an approximately 7.8-fold induction. GA and IAA treatments also showed strong induction, increasing expression by approximately 7.7-fold and 7.04-fold, respectively. In contrast, ABA and light stress induced relatively weaker responses, with expression levels approximately 4.6-fold and 3.4-fold those of the control, respectively. These findings further suggested a functional similarity of the PpGS2 promoter in regulating gene expression.

Discussion

GS2 plays a critical role in plant nitrogen metabolism, and its expression is regulated by multiple environmental factors, such as light and nitrogen availability, as well as developmental signals [6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that transcriptional regulation mediated by promoter sequences is a key determinant of gene expression dynamics [22]. Most reported studies on GS2 promoter functions have focused on food crops such as rice and barley, whereas research on important turfgrass and forage species such as Kentucky bluegrass remains limited [16, 23]. In this study, we successfully cloned the 2000 bp promoter sequence (PR0) upstream of the PpGS2 coding region from Kentucky bluegrass. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that this promoter contains a variety of cis-acting elements associated with hormone responsiveness, light signaling, and abiotic stress (Table 1). In addition to core promoter components such as TATA-box and CAAT-box, we also identified functional elements involved in abiotic stress responses, including LTR, DRE, and TATABOX5, as well as TATABOX5, which is implicated in low-nitrogen responses. These findings are consistent with the regulatory characteristics of GS2 promoter reported in other plant species [12–15]. Furthermore, we identified hormone-responsive elements, including ABRE, GARE-motif, and AuxRR-core (Table 1). Collectively, these results suggested that PpGS2 transcription may be synergistically regulated by multiple endogenous and environmental signals.

The number, type, and location of response elements within a promoter collectively determine its activity [24–25]. Previous studies have shown that promoter truncation can lead to either enhanced or reduced transcriptional activity, depending on the functional organization of regulatory elements. For instance, the activity of the GI promoter in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas ) initially increased and then decreased upon progressive 5’-end deletions [26], whereas the activity of the F3H promoter in mountain grape increased with promoter shortening [27]. In this study, a series of 5’-end deletion fragments (PR1-PR4) were generated and introduced into tobacco. GUS staining intensity gradually weakened as promoter length decreased (Fig. 2B), indicating that the transcriptional activity of the PpGS2 promoter depends on its integrity; deletion of specific fragments likely results in the loss of critical or synergistic elements, thereby impairing its driving capacity. Previous studies have demonstrated that GS2 expression is predominantly localized in green tissues, particularly leaves [28–29]. Consistent with these findings, the PpGS2 promoter drove GUS expression in the roots, stems, and leaves of transgenic Arabidopsis, with the strongest activity observed in leaves (Fig. 3). This expression pattern aligns well with the established role of GS2 in chloroplasts, where it functions in photorespiratory ammonium reassimilation and nitrogen metabolism associated with photosynthesis [11]. Collectively, these results further support the critical contribution of GS2 to leaf nitrogen assimilation [28–29].

Gene expression is often dynamically regulated by phytohormones and environmental stresses [21, 30–31]. In wheat, NH₄⁺ preferentially induces GS expression in roots, whereas NO₃⁻ promotes GS expression in leaves [32]; additionally, nitrate signaling mediated by the transcription factor NLP7 can directly induce GS2 expression [33]. In the present study, transgenic Arabidopsis plants harboring the full-length PpGS2 promoter exhibited significant induction of GUS expression in response to GA, IAA, ABA, MeJA, low-nitrogen stress, and light stress. Among these treatments, MeJA exerted the strongest inductive effect (Fig. 4), likely due to the high redundancy of MeJA-responsive elements (e.g. CGTGA-motif and TGACG-motif) distributed across the promoter (Table 1). These results suggest that GS2 is involved in MeJA-mediated defense responses. Under conditions such as insect damage or mechanical injury in turfgrass, GS2 may be activated to enhance nitrogen metabolism, thereby alleviating ammonia toxicity, providing substrates for defense protein synthesis, and promoting regeneration and repair of damaged tissues. In addition, low-nitrogen stress markedly enhanced promoter activity, implying that PpGS2 may be transcriptionally upregulated to facilitate nitrogen reassimilation under nitrogen-limited conditions. These findings are consistent with the presence of multiple hormone- and stress-responsive elements within the promoter and further confirm the responsiveness of the PpGS2 promoter to diverse regulatory signals.

Promoter regions often exhibit functional partitioning, with distinct segments responsible for specific regulatory responses. For example, in moso bamboo, the − 578 to -493 bp region of the PheMADS47a promoter was identified as critical for MeJA responsiveness [34]. Similarly, analysis of the Jatropha curcas JcGASA6 promoter revealed discrete regions harboring negative regulatory elements responsive to salicylic acid (SA) and abscisic acid (ABA) [35]. Consistent with these observations, our deletion analysis revealed clear regional functional differentiation within the PpGS2 promoter (Fig. 5). The GA response was primarily dependent on the − 2000 to -1600 bp region, suggesting the presence of cis-acting elements involved in gibberellin signal perception, which may be recognized by MYB transcription factors [36]. ABA responsiveness was mainly associated with the − 1200 to -800 bp region, which may integrate stress-related signals through bZIP factors (e.g., ABF/AREB binding to ABRE elements) [37]. In contrast, IAA and MeJA responses showed a gradual decline with promoter truncation, indicating that their regulation likely relies on the synergistic action of multiple cis-acting elements distributed across the promoter. The CGTCA/TGACG motif can recruit MYC2 for MeJA signaling [38], whereas the AuxRR core element binds ARF/NAC transcription factors to mediate auxin response [39]. Under low-nitrogen conditions, GUS activity exhibited bidirectional regulation: the − 1200 to -500 bp region appeared to contain positive regulatory elements enhancing transcription, including the TATABOX5 element potentially targeted by NLP factors [40]; in contrast, the − 2000 to -1200 bp region may contain repressive elements that modulate transcriptional output under nitrogen deficiency, possibly involving NIGT1/HRS1 repressors [41]. Additionally, an important light-responsive region was localized to -1600 to -800 bp, which may be enriched in light-responsive motifs such as G-box and GT1-motif, potential binding sites for HY5 and trihelix transcription factors, respectively, thereby integrating light and nitrogen signals [42]. This modular organization shares conserved features with the GS2 promoters of other plant species [12–15]; however, certain aspects may reflect species-specific adaptations of Kentucky bluegrass to its perennial growth habit and high nitrogen requirements. Notably, in this study, the GA response region was confined to the − 2000 to -1600 bp fragment (Fig. 5A), in contrast to the GA-responsive elements which were confined to the − 174 to -108 bp reported in annual grasses [36], which may be related to the importance of GA-mediated regeneration after mowing in perennial turfgrass management. Similarly, to our knowledge, no bidirectional low-nitrogen regulatory architecture has been described in other GS2 promoters, suggesting the existence of a specialized mechanism for fine-tuning nitrogen assimilation in perennial turfgrass under fluctuating nitrogen availability. Together, these results provide important insights into the modular organization of the PpGS2 promoter and lay the foundation for future mechanistic validation of the proposed transcription factor interactions.

The promoter deletion analysis in this study was performed using heterologous systems in Arabidopsis and tobacco, a widely accepted and effective approach for promoter characterization. While these heterologous systems may not fully recapitulate the native chromatin environment and transcriptional regulatory network of Kentucky bluegrass, we addressed this limitation by treating wild-type Kentucky bluegrass seedlings under the same conditions. qRT-PCR analysis showed that endogenous PpGS2 expression was significantly induced under all treatments, and the expression patterns were consistent with the GUS activity driven by the PpGS2 promoter fragments in Arabidopsis and tobacco (Fig. 6). Nevertheless, the precise cis-elements and interacting transcription factors remain to be identified. Future research will combine molecular biology approaches to further characterize these functional cis-elements and their interacting transcription factors, along with homologous validation in Kentucky bluegrass. Such efforts will help elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying nitrogen metabolism in turfgrass and provide a robust theoretical foundation for the practical application of this promoter in molecular breeding.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a preliminary characterization of the modular regulatory features of the PpGS2 promoter in Kentucky bluegrass. Through 5’-end deletion analysis, the specific distribution regions of response elements for GA, ABA, IAA, MeJA, low-nitrogen, and light signals were delineated, revealing the multi-regional synergy underlying hormonal regulation and a possible dual (positive/negative) regulatory mode for low-nitrogen responses. Additionally, the preferential expression of the promoter in Arabidopsis leaves was confirmed, consistent with the chloroplast-localized function of GS2. Overall, these findings fill a critical knowledge gap regarding the synergistic regulation of the PpGS2 promoter by hormonal and environmental signals in turfgrass and provide a molecular basis for breeding turfgrass varieties with enhanced nitrogen use efficiency and stress tolerance.
