Pharmacophore Identification and Structure–Activity Relationship Analysis of a Series of Substituted Azaindoles as Inhibitors of Trypanosoma brucei

ABSTRACT
Human African trypanosomiasis is among the World Health Organization’s designated neglected tropical diseases. Repurposing strategies are often employed in academic drug discovery programs due to financial limitations, and in this instance, we used human kinase inhibitor chemotypes to identify substituted 4-aminoazaindoles, exemplified by 1. Structure–activity and structure–property relationship analysis, informed by cheminformatics, identified 4s as a potent inhibitor of Trypanosoma brucei growth. While 4s appeared to be fast acting and cidal in the in vitro assays, it failed to cure a murine model of infection. Preliminary efforts to identify the potential mechanism of action of the series pointed to arginine kinase, though, as we demonstrate, this does not appear to be the sole target of our compounds. This comprehensive approach to drug discovery, encompassing cheminformatics, structure–potency and structure–property analysis, and pharmacophore identification, highlights our multipronged efforts to identify novel lead compounds for this deadly disease.

Introduction

Human African trypanosomiasis (HAT), caused by the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma brucei, is one of 20 neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) designated by the World Health Organization (WHO).1 Fatal if untreated, HAT has two stages. In the first stage, the parasite resides in the blood and lymphatic systems; infections at this stage are often not diagnosed due to nondescript symptoms.2,3 The second stage of HAT infection, during which the parasite crosses the blood–brain barrier of an infected individual, is most devastating as it leads to disrupted sleep patterns, brain damage, and eventual death.4,5

Current treatments for HAT pose challenges due to complex regimens, leading to the emergence of long-term health issues and an increasing incidence of resistance.4 In 2018, fexinidazole emerged as a groundbreaking orally administered treatment for stage 1 and 2 HAT caused by T. brucei gambiense, gaining approval in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and later in Uganda in 2021.6 Acoziborole, formerly SCYX-7158, demonstrated promising results in phase II/III trials for stage 2 HAT caused by T. brucei gambiense in 2022, with efficacy comparable to that of the current gold standard nifurtimox–eflornithine combination (94%).7 However, the imperative for alternative therapeutics persists due to attrition in clinical trials,8 the potential for resistance, and the need for drugs targeting T. brucei rhodesiense-induced HAT. Fexinidazole’s phase II study for T. brucei rhodesiense HAT was completed in October 2022, though results are currently pending.9

NTD drug discovery takes place largely in the nonprofit sector given that there is little financial incentive for profit-driven companies. Such settings tend to be resource-limited, and repurposing strategies are often employed. Lead repurposing relies on the identification of a human target family that is also present in the parasite; it has been shown that T. brucei expresses several essential kinases10,11 and that human kinase inhibitors can be optimized for these parasites.12 Inspired by this, Diaz et al. performed a whole-organism high-throughput screen (HTS) of 42 444 inhibitors from the GSK kinase-focused screening collection.13 This initial screening set was narrowed to 797 compounds that had pEC50 values of >6 and were >100-fold selective for T. brucei brucei over HepG2 cells. These hits were then clustered on the basis of structural similarity, leading to the identification of a series of substituted 4-aminoazaindoles, typified by 1. The structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies and pharmacophore identification around this cluster that is associated with high potency and good absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties (see Table 1) will be discussed. We are grateful to have benefited from a long-standing, in-kind collaboration with AstraZeneca that provides routine access to tier 1 ADME data, including human liver microsome (HLM) and rat hepatocyte (RH) intrinsic clearance, thermodynamic aqueous solubility, human plasma protein binding, and LogD. AstraZeneca has historically used this combined data package for early information on the “in vitro to in vivo” scaling for their programs.

In general, this cluster displayed a good ADME profile and a small topological polar surface area (TPSA) (Table 1), suggesting brain penetration may be achievable, which is required for the treatment of stage 2 HAT. When the original screen was run, we also considered the CNS-multiparameter optimization score14 as a predictor of brain penetration, though more recently the blood–brain barrier score15 replaced this. A retrospective analysis suggested that brain penetration may still be possible, though it was less likely than the MPO score predicted. While we work to further improve the overall potency of the series, the other most significant factors to address included cidality and off-target activity of 1, which has been shown to be a potent inhibitor of human ROCK1 kinase (Table S2).

Results and Discussion

We chose to first examine replacements of the pyrazole with five- and six-membered aromatic groups, which would enable rapid generation of the SAR. Given the acceptable ADME profile of the initial hit, we used cheminformatics to ensure that we were not introducing liabilities. We enumerated a virtual library of analogues using JChem Reactor (ChemAxon, Inc.) with a set of heterocyclic amines that were commercially available in preweighed quantities. Using Vortex (Dotmatics, Inc.), we filtered the library on the basis of the number of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs), hydrogen bond donors (HBDs), halogens, molecular weight (MW), cLogP, and TPSA (cutoffs shown in Figure 1), such that the chosen analogues were predicted to have a favorable ADME profile while also ensuring that we did not limit the chemical space that we were able to sample significantly. This gave ∼800 analogues from which to select our initial library. The compounds were then clustered using K-means clustering and manually examined to identify analogues for synthesis (Figure 1).

Informed analogue selection to probe the “drug-like space”. The gray points are the synthetic possibilities from commercially available starting materials, and the green points indicate the compounds chosen for synthesis.

The synthesis of these compounds was accomplished via Buchwald–Hartwig coupling,16 starting from 4-chloro-7-azaindole and the relevant amine (Scheme 1a). This led to the isolation of the desired product in highly variable yields and required alteration depending on the R2 substituent. When R2 ≠ H, the reaction, under previous conditions, led to the formation of multiple side products. Close monitoring of the reaction led to the conclusion that the side products were being formed simultaneously with the desired product, though the formation could be minimized by replacing tert-butyl XPhos with XPhos, decreasing the reaction temperature (from 100 to 75 °C), and monitoring the progress of the reaction.

In all cases, replacement with aromatic and benzylic functional groups led to a complete loss of activity in our assay (Table 2). Surprisingly, 2h and 2i both showed activity losses, suggesting that the HBD on the pyrazole was critical for potent activity. The in vitro ADME properties of a selection of analogues were determined, and they were generally found to have good aqueous solubility, good to excellent human PPB (plasma protein binding), and medium to high clearance in human liver microsomes (HLMs) and rat hepatocytes (RHs), highlighting the value of using predicted properties for library design (see Table S3). These analogues were generally shown to be nontoxic to mammalian cells with the exception of 2c that, despite its lack of activity against T. brucei brucei, demonstrated low micromolar activity against MRC5 cells.

We shifted our focus to the substituents on the pyrazole and examined alternative alkyl chains at positions R1 and R2 (Table 3). Initially, both methyl substituents were removed (3a), which led to a decrease in activity when compared to that of the parent compound (1); in addition, 3b (pEC50 = 6.0) exhibited a significant loss of activity, suggesting substitution was required for potent T. brucei brucei activity. Extension of R1 to ethyl (3c; pEC50 = 6.2) afforded similar activity, while the propyl group (3i; pEC50 = 6.8) afforded an improvement in the activity compared with that of 3b; however, it was still a half-log unit less active than the parent. The introduction of a bulky tert-butyl substituent at this position was not tolerated (cf. 3d; pEC50 = 4.7), a trend also confirmed with 3e (isopropyl; pEC50 = 4.6) and 3f (cyclopropyl; pEC50 = 5.1). Insertion of a methylene between the isopropyl (3g; pEC50 = 5.4) and the cyclopropyl (3h; pEC50 = 6.1) restored the potency, though not to the levels of that of the parent. Given the ethyl and propyl substituents at R1 were most favorable for activity, we used these to explore R2.

When R1 was kept constant as an ethyl group the most favorable R2 substituent was the methyl (3k; pEC50 = 6.7), with the ethyl (3l; pEC50 = 5.9) and propyl (3n; pEC50 = 6.0) derivatives both exhibiting a decrease of almost one log unit in potency. Bulk at this position was again not well tolerated with the isopropyl (3m; pEC50 = 4.6) losing activity. A similar SAR pattern was observed when R1 was the propyl with analogues exhibiting potency equal to those of their matched analogues. We also tried tying the two methyl groups together in bicyclic analogues 3s and 3t, though these led to decreases in potency of approximately a half-log unit and 2 log units, respectively, compared to that of the parent.

Replacement with the indole [4e (Table 4)] led to a modest increase in potency. We explored the 5- and 6-regioisomers of the azaindole (4a and 4d, respectively), and both were found to be unfavorable for activity. To probe the SAR at the azaindole -NH group, we prepared N-alkylated analogues. Methylation (4g) was well tolerated, and the ethyl led to a modest increase in potency (4h), suggesting the HBD was not essential for activity. However, there was a limit to the size and bulk of the alkyl group that was tolerated given the relative decrease in the activity of 4i. Truncation to the phenyl (4m) and 4-pyridyl (4n) led to a loss of activity. Reintroduction of a hydrogen bond acceptor (4p) to mimic the substitution pattern of the 7-azaindole led to a restoration of activity, though this was metabolically unstable in HLMs [250 μL min–1 (mg of protein)−1]. Interestingly, dimethyl amine 4q exhibited a loss of potency, though this could be due to the significant decrease in the aqueous solubility of the compound (<5 μM). Replacement with the benzofuran (4s; pEC50 = 8.7) led to a 2-fold improvement in potency compared to that of 1. None of these analogues showed mammalian toxicity.

To improve our understanding of the three-dimensional SAR, a seven-feature pharmacophore model was built with Phase (Schrödinger release 2018-2: Phase, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY) (Figure 2). As the HTS was performed using a kinase-targeting screening collection, it was assumed that the pharmacophore would have a hinge binding motif. Given the importance of the pyrazole from the initial SAR analysis, the pharmacophore models were filtered such that only those that had a donor–acceptor–donor hydrogen bonding motif from the pyrazole would be considered. Including the excluded volume from the inactive compounds and overlaying the active compounds onto the most appropriate model, we fould strong steric constraints on the pyrazole and substitutions at positions 4 and 5. Furthermore, rotation about the amine linker could explain the potency of compounds with hydrogen bond acceptors at position 1 or 7 of the heterocycles that substitute for the azaindole of 1. Additionally, the lack of excluded volume around the azaindole suggests that there is further chemical space to be explored here.

Compound 4s became our hit of interest, and we sought to optimize the metabolic profile and continue to explore the chemical space using alternative heterocycles. In silico metabolite prediction (using MetaSite, Molecular Discovery, Inc.) indicated that the 5-methyl on the pyrazole and position 2 of the benzofuran would be metabolically labile. As such, we synthesized 5m (Table 5), which resulted in a 2-log unit loss of potency, and while metabolism in human liver microsomes was improved, this was not reflected in rat hepatocytes. The addition of methyl groups at positions 2 and 3 of the benzofuran failed to yield an improvement in metabolism despite retaining potency. The lactam (5d) and its methylated derivative (5e) yielded the most stable compounds in both human liver microsomes and rat hepatocytes, though this was accompanied by a one-log unit decrease in potency. The dihydrobenzofuran (5i) yielded a slight improvement in potency and showed improved stability in HLMs, though it is known to be susceptible to oxidative metabolism.24 Two quinoline derivatives (5k and 5l) were synthesized. Compound 5l retains the spatial relationship between the HBA and the pyrazole and is equipotent with 4s, while 5k demonstrated an almost 2.5-log unit decrease in potency, which could be due to the difference in the vector between the HBA (of the methoxy) and the pyrazole or a change in the dipole moment. The activity of 5s was surprising given that the sulfur would be unable to participate in a hydrogen bonding interaction, though it does maintain a dipole consistent with 4s. When we examined the data holistically, there was no clear correlation with regard to the impact of electronics on the activity of the analogues. For example, the electron rich heterocycles, i.e., benzothiophene (5a), benzofuran (4s), and indole (4e), all had potent inhibition, and the electron-poor quinazolines (4l and 5l) demonstrated a similar level of potency. Upon examination of the overall dipole moment, there is also a lack of correlation with some analogues; for example, compare 5b and 4t.

Given the improved potency against T. brucei brucei and the acceptable ADME profile, we sought to further characterize the biological activity of the series and assess the rate of action. Compounds were defined as fast-acting if they reached a pEC50 of >6 after incubation for <18 h. All analogues that were tested from this series were found to be fast-acting (Figure 3). It is noteworthy that only partial inhibition is achieved for several compounds at shorter time points. On the contrary, incubation for ≥18 h with 1, 5d, and 5e was needed to achieve >90% growth inhibition at the highest concentration tested (40 μM).

Rate of action of selected analogues. All compounds in this cluster were found to be fast-acting.

The target candidate profile for HAT requires a compound that is cidal,25 and this is defined by the minimal trypanocidal concentration [pEC99 (Table 6)], which is the compound concentration that inhibits 99% of growth recovery when compared with DMSO controls. Compounds with pEC99 values of >6.0 after incubation for 18 h and subsequent washing are considered cidal. Although cidal behavior was not observed for some of the analogues in this series, 4s and 5a were classified as cidal, due to their potent pEC99 values. However, in all cases, growth was effectively restored at concentrations that previously arrested growth <80% after exposure for 18 h (Figure S3).

A selection of analogues were further profiled for CYP inhibition and/or induction, Caco-2 permeability, mouse liver microsome (MLM) stability, and mouse plasma stability (see Table S5). Of the compounds tested, all exhibited high clearance in MLMs yet were stable in mouse plasma, and only 5a showed the potential for induction of human CYP450 3A4.

Because the compounds in the original HTS were designed as human kinase inhibitors and 1 had potent kinase activity against ROCK1, we assessed the selectivity of 4s in a panel of 46 human kinases at 1 μM (see Figure S4). None of the kinases tested, including ROCK1, were found to be inhibited by 4s to any appreciable degree, demonstrating that it is possible to improve the selectivity profile of these inhibitor chemotypes. Given all of the data, we sought to improve our understanding of the mechanism of action for this series.

Compound 1 was subjected to chemoproteomic profiling of human and T. brucei ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors using kinobeads (results not shown). Potential targets of 1 were identified as those that showed >50% inhibition at 5 μM. In addition to ROCK1 that was identified in the preliminary kinase panel on 1, two human kinase targets were identified as potential targets using this approach, RIPK2 and ERN1, yet only one T. brucei kinase was identified, arginine kinase. Arginine kinase is an ATP phosphotransferase that catalyzes the phosphorylation of arginine to generate phospho-arginine, a highly abundant phosphagen widely distributed in invertebrates and protozoa.26−28 The phosphoarginine/arginine kinase system of T. brucei consists of three highly similar arginine kinase isoforms: TbAK1 found exclusively in the cytosol (Tb927.9.6290), TbAK2 found in the glycosome (Tb927.9.6250), and TbAK3 found in the flagellum (Tb927.9.6210). They differ in the N- and C-terminal regions, where localization signals responsible for the intracellular accumulation of each isoform are present, but the ATP binding domains are highly conserved.

TbAK1 meets certain criteria as a theoretically suitable target for therapeutic purposes. It has been described as being essential in T. brucei under oxidative stress conditions26 and is not present in vertebrates. Therefore, we sought to evaluate whether TbAK1 could be the target of compounds related to 1. Full-length TbAK1 (Tb927.9.6290) was expressed as a His-tagged fusion protein in Escherichia coli. The activity of the enzyme was evaluated by measuring the kinetic parameters using the coupled-enzyme assay29 yielding Km values of 1.38 ± 0.26 and 0.67 ± 0.35 mM for arginine and ATP, respectively, which is consistent with previously described values.26 The Vmax value, however, was somewhat higher than formerly reported (144 ± 14 μmol min–1 mg–1). We used this recombinant protein to assess the in vitro specific inhibition of 1 and selected derivatives at 1 mM with the Kinase Glo assay using 40 mM d-arginine as the inhibition control.

Significant in vitro inhibition was observed at 1 mM (100-fold excess of ATP) for only 4c and 4m, with 55% and 35% decreases in activity, respectively, compared to controls (Figure 4).

Inhibitory effect of derivatives of 1 on TbAK1 kinase activity. The asterisk denotes a significant reduction (p < 0.05) in activity in a two-tailed Student’s t test.

A dose–response analysis was performed for 4c, the most active compound, and the obtained IC50 value was 1.3 ± 0.3 mM (Figure 5). Indeed, no correlation was found between the percentage of enzyme inhibition at 1 mM and the EC50 value in culture for the different compounds (Pearson’s coefficient = −0.041). Taken together, all of these data strongly suggest that TbAK1 is not the sole target for this series, as extremely low EC50 values in culture do not correlate with the weak inhibitory effect in vitro against the enzyme. While binding to TbAK1 of the 1 compound series may occur, it is improbable that this enzyme is the primary site of action.

Given the balance of potency and a good ADME profile, we opted to progress 4s into a pharmacokinetic (PK) study. The mean plasma concentration–time profile of 4s following a single intraperitoneal (ip) administration in female NMRI mice and the simulated PK profile following b.i.d. dosing are presented in Figure 6. Parameters calculated from this curve, including Cmax, tmax, and t1/2, are presented in Table S7 and demonstrate that 4s has excellent exposure levels in both the blood and brain and exceeds 10 × EC50 (our targeted value) for >8 h in the blood. With an increase in dosing to twice daily, 4s was predicted to exceed our targeted range for 24 h.

As a result of these analyses, 4s was progressed into an in vivo efficacy study. Mice treated with 4s (dosed ip, 10 mg/kg, twice daily) showed an 11-day increase in life span when compared to the control (vehicle only) group but failed to elicit a cure, which is consistent with a trypanostatic agent (Figure 7). Analysis of plasma samples collected during the efficacy study revealed that lower levels of exposure were obtained than the initial PK study suggested (see Figure S5 and Table S8), which could be a due to differences in physiology between the healthy and infected animals. Additionally, the cidality assay revealed that parasite growth was restored in cells treated with 4s at concentrations greater than its pEC99. The lower level of exposure achieved in the efficacy study translated to exposure > pEC99 for <4 h, which could have contributed to the outcome of the efficacy study. Finally, we do not have data on the plasma protein binding of 4s in mice to determine an adjusted in vivo exposure value that would effectively lower the concentration of the compound in blood.

We have previously found value in testing compounds against other protozoa, specifically Trypanosoma cruzi (the causative agent of Chagas disease) and Leishmania donovani (a causative agent of visceral leishmaniasis).30−36 Hit criteria for these parasites are less stringent, yet it is often challenging to identify starting points for optimization. Hits for T. cruzi and L. donovani are considered for EC50 values of <10 μM.37 However, none of the analogues showed significant activity against either parasite or toxicity in the THP-1 or L6 mammalian cell line (Table S4).

Conclusion

Through carefully filtering a library of potential analogues for synthesis on the basis of the number of HBAs, HBDs, halogens, MW, cLogP, and TPSA, we selected analogues that would have a greater likelihood of possessing a favorable ADME profile for synthesis. The subsequent SAR studies around 1 led us to elucidate the pharmacophore for potent inhibition of T. brucei brucei that is typified by 4s. Given the lack of cidality for this series, and the failure of 4s to elicit a cure in an acute mouse model of infection, we are halting our optimization efforts on this chemotype. However, we are currently exploring the synergistic potential of this chemotype in combination with trypanocidal agents and will report our findings in due course.

Experimental Section

General Chemistry

All starting materials were commercially procured and used without further purification, unless specified. Reaction solvents were purified by being passed through alumina columns on a purification system manufactured by Innovative Technology (Newburyport, MA). General Procedure A (synthesis of primary, aromatic amines)

4-Chloro-7-azaindole (1 equiv), potassium carbonate (3 equiv), and the amine (3 equiv) were combined in tert-butanol (0.5 M). The reaction mixture was degassed for 2 min. Pd2dba3 (0.02 equiv) and XPhos (0.04 equiv) were added to the reaction mixture, which was again degassed. The reaction mixture was heated to 100 °C overnight on the shaker plate. The reaction mixture was filtered through Celite, and the crude material was purified by column chromatography with the eluent stated.

General Procedure B (synthesis of position 3-substituted amino pyrazoles)

4-Chloro-7-azaindole (1 equiv), sodium tert-butoxide (1.2 equiv), and the relevant, substituted aminopyrazole (1.2 equiv) were combined in tert-butanol (0.25 M). The reaction mixture was degassed for 2 min. Pd2dba3 (0.02 equiv) and tBu-XPhos (0.04 equiv) were added to the reaction mixture, which was again degassed. The reaction mixture was heated to 100 °C overnight on the shaker plate.

General Procedure C (synthesis of the β-keto nitrile)

Sodium hydride (2 equiv) was suspended in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (0.53 M). The mixture was warmed to 70 °C, and a solution of the appropriately substituted ester (1 equiv) and the appropriately substituted nitrile (2 equiv) in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (5.30 M) was added dropwise over 10 min. Heating was maintained throughout the addition and continued overnight. The reaction was checked by TLC analysis. The reaction was quenched by the addition of a 1 M aqueous citric acid solution, and the product was extracted with ethyl acetate (twice). The organic extracts were combined and washed once with a saturated solution of sodium chloride before being dried with magnesium sulfate. The crude material was purified by column chromatography, eluting with 0–65% ethyl acetate in hexanes.

General Procedure D (synthesis of the substituted aminopyrazoles)

The β-keto nitrile (1 equiv) was dissolved in toluene (0.16 M) in a sealed tube. To this were added hydrazine acetate (1 equiv) and diisopropylethylamine (1 equiv). The reaction mixture was stirred at 110 °C in a sealed tube overnight. LCMS analysis of the crude reaction mixture indicated a mass consistent with the desired product was present. The reaction mixture was concentrated in vacuo, and the crude material was purified by column chromatography, eluting with 15–75% ethyl acetate in hexanes before eluting the product with 0–5% methanol in dichloromethane.

General Procedure E (synthesis of position 3- and 4-substituted amino pyrazoles)

4-Chloro-7-azaindole (1 equiv), sodium tert-butoxide (1.2 equiv), and the substituted aminopyrazole (1.2 equiv) were combined in tert-butanol (0.25 M). The reaction mixture was degassed for 2 min. Pd2dba3 (0.02 equiv) and XPhos (0.04 equiv) were added to the reaction mixture, which was again degassed. The reaction mixture was heated to 75 °C on the shaker plate, and the progress of the reaction monitored by LCMS analysis for formation of the bis-Buchwald side product.

General Biology

Strains and Media

Bloodstream T. brucei brucei Lister 427 was the selected strain for performing dose–response experiments and the profiling assay. The cell strain was cultured in Hirumi’s modified Iscove’s medium (HMI-9),38 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a T-25 vented flask (Corning).

Determination of pEC50

Mid-log growing cultures of T. brucei brucei were dispensed at a cell density of 250 cells per well in Nunclon 96-well transparent plates. The maximum concentration for compounds was 40 μM in 0.4% DMSO per well, and serial 3-fold dilutions were used for dose–response curves.

Assay plates were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2; after incubation, 20 μL of a 440 μM resazurin solution in prewarmed HMI-9 was added. Fluorescence was read in an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan) at 550 nm (excitation filter) and 590 nm (emission filter). A four-parameter equation was used to fit the dose–response curves, and the EC50 as the concentration that reduces 50% viability compared to solvent-treated control was determined using SigmaPlot version 14.5. We used 4 μM pentamidine as the 100% inhibition control. Assays were performed in triplicate at least twice.

Rate of Action Assays

Mid-log T. brucei brucei cultures were diluted to the required cell density and dispensed in Nunclon 96-well solid white assay plates. Then, 10 μL of compound after serial 3-fold dilutions was added to each well to achieve a final maximum concentration of 40 μM. Four sets of assay plates were arranged in order to be sequentially stopped at their corresponding time point. Top and bottom rows were dismissed for the compound assay, to reduce evaporation effects.

Plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for the indicated time points; incubation was stopped by addition of 10 μL of Cell Titer Glo reagent (Promega). Plate luminescence was read on an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan), and raw data were processed and analyzed as described above.

Reversibility Assays

First, 3000 cells per well from mid-log T. brucei brucei cultures were dispensed in Nunclon 96-well transparent plates. The compound concentration and percentage of DMSO were used as described above. Plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 18 h, and their contents collected by centrifugation. Then, 90 μL of medium per well was manually discarded, and wells were refilled with fresh prewarmed medium. This procedure was repeated three times; finally, 10 μL per well was dispensed in a new assay plate with prewarmed medium, and cells were allowed to recover for 72 h. Resazurin readout was performed as previously described. The assay was performed in duplicate at least twice.

MRC5 Cell Culture and Growth Assays

MRC5-SV2 cells (SV40-transformed human lung fibroblast cell line) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in T-75 vented flasks (Corning).

Cytotoxicity Assay

Log phase MRC5 cells were removed from the T-75 TC flasks using TrypLE Express (Thermo) and dispersed by gentle pipetting. The cell density was adjusted to a working concentration with prewarmed DMEM medium; 25 000 cells per well were plated in 96-well transparent Nunclon plates and allowed to settle for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

After incubation, serial 3-fold dilutions of the compounds were added to each well to achieve a final maximum concentration of 50 μM. Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After incubation, 20 μL of a 500 μM resazurin solution was added. Fluorescence was read in an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan) at 550 nm (excitation filter) and 590 nm (emission filter). Data analysis was performed as described above.

Overexpression and Purification of Recombinant TbAK1

Full-length TbAK1 (Tb927.9.6290) was expressed as a His-tagged fusion protein in E. coli (BL21 DE3 strain). The DNA fragment was amplified from genomic DNA of T. brucei brucei and ligated into pET28a (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Transformed bacteria were induced with 0.3 mM IPTG for 18 h at 16 °C. Pellets were harvested and resuspended in complete lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 75 mM NaCl, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM PMSF, and 1× Protein Inhibitor Cocktail (PIC, Roche)], sonicated (five cycles of on and off pulses for 30 s each), and centrifuged to obtain the soluble fraction. The presence of protein in the soluble fraction was verified by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by Coomassie Blue staining. The Bradford assay was employed to determine the protein concentration.

Ni-Sepharose high-performance affinity medium (GE Healthcare) was used for recombinant protein purification. Medium was washed with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 1× PIC and further incubated with the soluble fraction. The recombinant protein was obtained by stepwise elution with increasing concentrations of imidazole (20, 100, 250, and 500 mM) in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 1× PIC. The enriched elution fractions were pooled and subjected to buffer exchange using PD-10 desalting columns (Cytiva). The protein (3.8 mg/mL) was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1× PIC, and 25% glycerol.

Enzyme Kinetics and Inhibition

TbAK1 assays were performed at increasing concentrations of ATP or l-arginine in a Versamax monochromator using the coupled-enzyme assay.29 Kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) were calculated using SoftMax Pro version 6.5 (Molecular Devices) by fitting the Michaelis–Menten equation to the sets of data. The kinase assay was performed in 1× kinase buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM MgCl2, 100 mM KCl, and 2 mM DTT], 1 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 0.14 mM NADH, 0.015 unit/μL LDH, 0.01 unit/μL PK, 1 ng/μL TbAK1, and l-arginine and ATP at variable concentrations. Measurements (absorbance at 340 nm) were performed every 15 s for 30 min at 37 °C. Reactions were performed in a 96-well plate in which 200 μL was the final volume per well.

To assess the inhibition of the NEU-1225 cluster compounds against recombinant TbAK1, the Kinase Glo assay (Promega) was selected. Reaction mixtures with 1 mM inhibitor dissolved in DMSO were incubated with 1× kinase buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM MgCl2, 100 mM KCl, and 2 mM DTT], 1 mM l-arginine, 2 ng/μL TbAK1, and 10 μM ATP. Incubation was performed for 5 min in a final volume of 50 μL at room temperature, and then Kinase Glo reagent was added. d-Arginine (40 mM) was used as the inhibition control. The percentage inhibition was calculated relative to assays containing only DMSO (100% inhibition), whereas the reaction with no enzyme was considered as 0% kinase activity.

For dose–response curves, the Kinase Glo assay was used with increasing concentrations of the compound, ranging from 4.5 nM to 10 mM. IC50 values were calculated using SigmaPlot version 14.5 by applying the four-parameter standard curve.

Pharmacokinetics Protocols

Compound 4s was administered ip to two groups of female NMRI mice (n = 3 for group 1, and n = 6 for group 2). The compound was prepared in a 1% (v/v) DMSO/99% (v/v) 20% (w/v) sulfobutyl ether-β-cyclodextrin (SBE-β-CD) (Captisol) mixture in water, and the dosing volume was 10 mL/kg for a total dose of 10 mg/kg. Food and tap water were available ad libitum. Following ip dosing, group 1 blood samples were collected from the tail vein in capillary tubes containing K2EDTA at the following time points: 0.0833, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h.

To obtain simultaneous blood and brain samples, group 2 mice were placed under terminal anesthetic (isoflurane) and blood samples (0.3 mL) collected from the retro-orbital sinus in K2EDTA tubes 0.5 h (n = 3) and 2 h (n = 3) after compound administration. Immediately following blood sample collection, death was confirmed by cervical dislocation and the brain removed. Aliquots of each blood sample were diluted in an equal volume of water. Mouse brain samples were weighed; water was added at a 1:2 (w/v) ratio (brain/water), and the samples were homogenized. Both blood and brain samples were stored −80 °C until they were analyzed.

Diluted blood and brain homogenates were processed using standard liquid–liquid extraction procedures using acetonitrile containing an internal standard (nifedipine) and analyzed by LCMS/MS. Noncompartmental analysis was performed using Phoenix pharmacokinetic software version 1.4 (Certara), and Cmax, tmax, AUClast, AUC, and t1/2 were estimated.

Efficacy Model

Procedures were performed at the Institute of Parasitology and Biomedicine Lopez-Neyra (Spanish National Research Council, CSIC) according to the European and Spanish Policy on the Care, Welfare and Treatment of Animals.

Female outbred mice (NMRI strain from Charles River Laboratories) were each infected ip with 104T. brucei brucei (STIB795) from a cryopreserved stock (day 0). Three days later, animals with confirmed parasitemia were distributed into two groups: control group {infected mice to which a vehicle [20% Captisol dissolved in doubly deionized H2O and 1% DMSO (n = 6)] had been administered} and treated group [infected mice treated twice daily with 10 mg 4s/kg (n = 6)]. An additional control of cure was used [a single dose of 40 mg Berenil/kg (n = 1)]. Parasitemia was periodically examined by diluting 2 μL of blood from an infected mouse tail in 100 μL of TDB glucose and counting in a Neubauer chamber in a light microscope. For ethical reasons, mice with severe parasitemia were euthanized. The day of death or euthanization was recorded (Table S8).

In addition, plasma samples from treated mice were collected to determinate the plasma concentration profile over time (Figure S4). Blood samples from the tail were taken on the first and last day of the first treatment 0.5, 2, 4, and 6 h after administration. Samples were mixed with sterile water (1:2 ratio). To eliminate the risk of infection, the samples were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles.

Pharmacophore Modeling

Small molecule preparation and pharmacophore mapping were carried out in Maestro 18.2 (Schrödinger, LLC). LigPrep with Epik39,40 was utilized to generate all possible tautomers and protonation states at pH 7.0 ± 0.5 for the compounds. MacroModel was then used to minimize each compound using the OPLS_2005 force field41 with an extended cutoff for nonbonded interactions. The Polak–Ribiere conjugate gradient (PRCG) method was employed with 2500 maximum iterations and a 0.05 kJ mol–1 Å–1 convergence threshold, while solvent effects were simulated employing the analytical generalized Born/surface area (GB/SA) model.42

Phase (Schrödinger, LLC) was used to generate pharmacophore hypotheses. The best align method was used with a pEC50 cutoff of >6.0 to define compounds that were active and <5.0 for those that were inactive. Fifty conformers were produced for each compound with each hypothesis needed to match at least 50% of the actives. Pharmacophore hypotheses were limited to five to seven feature sites, with at least two HBAs. Only pharmacophore hypotheses with a linear sequence of hydrogen bond donor–acceptor–donor features were selected. This filter was applied to mimic the formation of three hydrogen bonds from the nitrogen atoms of the amino-pyrazole to the hinge region of a kinase, the suggested targets of these compounds.

ADME Experimental Protocols

Aqueous pH 7.4 Solubility

Compounds are dried down from 10 mM DMSO solutions using a centrifugal evaporation technique. Phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) was added, and StirStix were inserted in the glass vials. Shaking was then performed at a constant temperature of 25 °C for 20–24 h. This step was followed by double centrifugation with a tip wash between the steps, to ensure that no residues of the dried compound were interfering. The solutions were diluted before analysis, and quantification using LC/MS/MS was performed.

LogD7.4

The distribution coefficient between 1-octanol and aqueous buffer, LogD, at pH 7.4, is based on the traditional shake flask technique, but with the modification of measuring compounds in mixtures of 10 at a time using UPLC with quantitative MS to measure the relative octanol and aqueous concentrations of compounds. The buffer solution was 10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4). The method has been validated for LogD7.4 values ranging from −2 to 5.0.

Human Plasma Protein Binding (PPB)

PPB is determined using equilibrium dialysis (RED device) to separate free compound from bound compound. The amount of compound in plasma (initial concentration of 1 mM in DMSO) and in dialysis buffer (pH 7.4 phosphate buffer) was measured by LCMS/MS after 18 h in a dialysis chamber at 37 °C. Sample levels were quantified using a seven-point calibration curve in plasma. The fraction unbound (fu%) was reported.

Human Liver Microsomal Clint

The in vitro intrinsic clearance was determined from human liver microsomes using a standard approach.43 Following incubation and preparation, the samples were analyzed using LC/MS/MS. Refined data were uploaded to IBIS and are displayed as Clint (intrinsic clearance) in microliters per minute per milligram.

Rat Hepatocyte Clint

The in vitro intrinsic clearance was determined from rat Han Wistar hepatocytes at a final concentration of 1 million cells/mL. The cells were preincubated at 37 °C for 15 min before the addition of the test compound (as a 50 μM stock solution) to a final concentration of 1 μM. The samples were analyzed using LC/MSMS at predefined time points. Refined data were uploaded to IBIS and are displayed as Clint (intrinsic clearance) in microliters per minute per 1 million cells.

Calculated LogP and LogD Values

Both LogP and LogD predictions are based on a modified version of the method44 in which the predicted partition coefficients are composed of the molecules’ atomic increments.

Matrix Stability

The stability in the mouse matrix, CD-1, was determined at 37 °C. The assay was performed by adding a 500 μM compound solution to the matrix to achieve a final concentration of 2 μM. Following incubation for 1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h, the reactions were quenched and analyzed using LC/MS to determine the percentage of drug remaining.

Caco-2 Permeability

The Caco-2 permeability of test compounds was measured using an established protocol at Charles River Laboratories. The concentrations of the test compounds were determined by measuring the peak area ratios at 0 and 2 h on the apical and basolateral sides of the basal plate.

CYP Induction

CYP induction was measured using established protocols at Charles River Laboratories. The assay was performed by adding the test compound to the positive or negative controls and incubating the samples for 3 h at 37 °C, following determination of the fold induction relative to the vehicle control by LC/MS/MS analysis.

CYP Inhibition

CYP inhibition was measured using established protocols at Charles River Laboratories. A 50 mM stock solution was prepared and serially diluted (1.5- and 4-fold); this was then combined with a buffer/cofactor/substrate solution before being combined with the human liver microsomes. The resulting mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle shaking before the percentage inhibition was calculated relative to zero inhibition by LC/MS/MS analysis.
