Beyond prediction: explainable machine learning for ferritin estimation from complete blood count
Authors: Ramazan Berk Us, Oguzhan Panatli, Mine Büşra Bozkürk, Atakan Öztürk, Suadiye Sağlam, Arzu Kösem, Alpaslan Öztürk, Gazi Erkan Bostanci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerum ferritin is a primary marker of body iron stores; however, its diagnostic utility is limited by cost and non-specific elevation during inflammatory states. This study aimed to develop and validate explainable machine learning (ML) models to estimate ferritin status using only widely available and cost-effective complete blood count (CBC) and demographic parameters.MethodsThis prospective study analyzed 12,450 adult laboratory records. To ensure ferritin reflected true iron status, cases with inflammation (elevated white blood cell count or erythrocyte sedimentation rate) were excluded. A balanced dataset of 7,200 samples was used to train five supervised ML algorithms, including Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and Artificial Neural Network (ANN), for both binary classification and regression tasks. Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to evaluate global feature importance.ResultsThe ANN model achieved the best classification performance, with an area under the curve of 0.837 and an F1-score of 0.760 in discriminating low ferritin levels (< 30 ng/mL) from normal values. Although the regression model demonstrated a moderate global R² of 0.32, it showed higher predictive accuracy in lower ferritin ranges critical for diagnosing iron deficiency. SHAP analysis revealed Sex, Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin, and Red Cell Distribution Width as the most influential predictors, consistent with iron-restricted erythropoiesis.ConclusionML models based solely on routine hemogram data provide a reliable, zero-cost screening approach for iron deficiency. The integration of explainable AI enhances clinical credibility and supports implementation as an accessible decision support tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03601-2.Keywords: Explainable artificial intelligence, Clinical decision support systems, Complete blood count, Iron deficiency, SHAP, Ferritin

Introduction

Ferritin is the primary intracellular iron storage protein, acting as a buffer against iron deficiency and overload by safely sequestering and releasing iron when needed [1]. In clinical practice, serum ferritin is widely used to assess body iron stores and is considered the most sensitive marker of iron deficiency, often preceding the development of anemia. However, ferritin’s role as an acute-phase reactant complicates its diagnostic utility, as its levels may increase independently of iron status in the presence of infection, inflammation, or chronic disease [2]. This overlap is particularly relevant in patients with chronic kidney disease, autoimmune diseases, or during pregnancy, where inflammation-driven hepcidin elevation can mask functional iron deficiency despite normal or elevated ferritin values [2, 3]. Moreover, global health data emphasize the ongoing burden of iron deficiency anemia (IDA), with millions affected across vulnerable populations, particularly women and children [4]. Despite WHO guidelines suggesting 15–30 ng/mL cutoffs, there remains no universally accepted ferritin threshold, further complicating early diagnosis and risk stratification [1]. This biological complexity and interpretive ambiguity underscore the need for more accessible, integrated approaches to iron deficiency screening in clinical settings.

Given these diagnostic challenges, complete blood count (CBC) parameters have emerged as practical, widely available tools to support iron deficiency assessment when ferritin interpretation is confounded. Iron deficiency directly affects erythropoiesis, leading to measurable changes in red cell indices such as mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), red cell distribution width (RDW), and hemoglobin (HGB) levels—long before overt anemia manifests [5, 6]. Studies have consistently shown that low MCV and MCH, coupled with elevated RDW, strongly correlate with depleted iron stores, even when ferritin is within normal or borderline ranges [6]. Moreover, age and sex are known to influence both ferritin and CBC values, especially in premenopausal women, the elderly, and populations with physiological or pathological chronic inflammation [7, 8]. This interplay between hematologic parameters and iron status forms the foundation for using hemogram-derived features in algorithmic prediction models. Leveraging this physiological relationship offers a cost-effective, data-driven path for early screening, particularly in resource-limited settings or when confirmatory biochemical tests are inaccessible or unreliable.

Machine learning (ML) offers a transformative opportunity to bridge the diagnostic gap created by ferritin’s cost, infrastructural dependency, and interpretive limitations. Ferritin assays, while informative, remain relatively expensive, subject to batch processing, and often inaccessible in rural, primary care, or emergency settings where rapid decision-making is required [3, 9]. Moreover, inflammatory interference further reduces test specificity, rendering ferritin suboptimal as a stand-alone diagnostic anchor in many real-world scenarios [2]. As routine hemogram testing is already deeply embedded in clinical workflows, utilizing these data for ferritin prediction via ML offers a high-yield, low-cost diagnostic alternative.

In this study, we propose and evaluate supervised learning models trained solely on hemogram parameters and basic demographic data to estimate ferritin status—without relying on biochemical assays. Beyond predictive accuracy, our emphasis includes model interpretability using explainable AI (XAI) techniques, ensuring the approach is clinically transparent and ethically aligned. Ultimately, our aim is to lay the groundwork for an affordable and scalable clinical decision support system capable of screening for iron deficiency in settings where traditional ferritin testing is constrained or unreliable.

Materials and methods

Study design and dataset description

In this study, four ML algorithms and artificial neural networks (ANN) were used for ferritin level classification, while only the ANN was employed to predict continuous ferritin values. Data preprocessing involved outlier detection, categorical variable encoding, and scaling. No missing data was found in the dataset. The models were developed using the training dataset and the optimized hyperparameters for the models were determined using 5-fold cross-validation (CV). The models were tested using the testing dataset. Classification performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC metrics. Subsequently, statistical analysis was performed to identify the best-performing model. Following classification, continuous ferritin values were predicted using an ANN. MAE, RMSE, and R² metrics were used to evaluate the regression ANN model. Finally, SHAP-based explainable artificial intelligence methods were applied to interpret feature contributions to model predictions. The study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

This prospective, single-center study was conducted at Ankara Etlik City Hospital between August 1st and November 1st, 2025. A total of 12,450 adult laboratory records (age range: 18–102 years) were initially collected. Cases with missing or incomplete data and those showing signs of inflammation—defined as a white blood cell count > 10,000/mm³ or elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate—were excluded to ensure that ferritin variation reflected iron status rather than acute-phase responses. After applying these exclusion criteria, the eligible dataset formed the basis for subsequent preprocessing and model development. All included individuals had CBC and serum ferritin measured on the same day. Ferritin was categorized as low (< 30 ng/mL) or normal (30–400 ng/mL). All data were anonymized, and the study received approval from the institutional ethics committee. Symbols and more detailed descriptions of the variables in the dataset are given in Table 1. An overview of the dataset through exploratory data analysis (EDA) is shown in Supplementary Figure S1 and S2.

Laboratory measurements

Serum ferritin levels were measured using the Cobas 8000 modular analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) with an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) method. Calibration and internal quality control procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. CBC parameters were obtained using the Sysmex XN-1000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan), which integrates impedance, flow cytometry, and fluorescence-based detection technologies to ensure high analytical precision. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was measured using the Vision C-1 automated analyzer (DIESSE Diagnostica Senese, Siena, Italy) based on the Westergren method. All analyses were completed within routine clinical laboratory workflows, and pre-analytical quality checks were performed prior to inclusion in the dataset.

Data preprocessing

Following exclusion of incomplete or inflammatory samples, the remaining dataset exhibited an imbalance between low ferritin (< 30 ng/mL) and normal ferritin (30–400 ng/mL) categories. To obtain equal representation for supervised learning and to prevent model bias, random undersampling was applied to the majority class, yielding a final balanced dataset of 7,200 samples (3,600 low and 3,600 normal). Among these, 5,240 participants were female and 1,960 were male. The dataset contained 11 variables in total: 9 numerical and 2 categorical. Independent variables were Age, Sex, Hemoglobin (HGB), Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV), Hematocrit (HCT), White Blood Cell Count (WBC), Mean Platelet Volume (MPV), Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH), and Red Cell Distribution Width–Coefficient of Variation (RDW-CV). Two dependent outcomes were defined: Ferritin (continuous) for regression tasks and Ferritin Level (binary; 1 = low, 0 = normal) for classification. All numerical variables were standardized using z-score normalization prior to model training. Following these steps, the interquartile range (IQR) method was used to detect outliers in the data preprocessing. Outliers were suppressed to minimize their possible negative impact on model performance. No missing data were found in the dataset. Thereafter, the sex variable was converted into numeric data using the label encoding technique to ensure compatibility with machine learning algorithms. After these steps, the dataset was randomly divided into a 70% training set and a 30% held-out test set. Lastly, all numerical variables were standardized using z-score scaling. All methods employed in data preprocessing were implemented using the Python (3.12.7) programming language along with the NumPy [10], Pandas [11], and Scikit-Learn [12] libraries.

Model development and hyperparameter tuning

Two predictive frameworks were constructed: binary classification models to differentiate low ferritin (< 30 ng/mL) from normal ferritin (30–400 ng/mL) and a regression model to estimate continuous ferritin values. For the classification, five different machine learning algorithms—Logistic Regression (LR) [13], Support Vector Machine (SVM) [14], Random Forest (RF) [15], eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [16], and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) [17] —were developed. To improve predictive performance, hyperparameter tuning was performed using 5-fold cross-validation (CV) on the training dataset. Subsequently, model performance was evaluated using 5-fold CV on the training dataset and then assessed on the test set. Accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) were calculated for each model. To obtain accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, a default classification threshold of 0.5 was utilized to convert the predicted probabilities into binary class labels. To address the architectural complexities of the ANN models for both classification and regression tasks, hyperparameter optimization was conducted using KerasTuner. The input layer dimension was dynamically configured to match the total number of features present in the dataset. As summarized in Table 2, a comprehensive search space was defined: hidden layers ranged from 1 to 10, neurons per layer from 32 to 512, and dropout rates from 0.0 to 0.5. Activation functions evaluated included ReLU, LeakyReLU, and PReLU, alongside L2 weight penalties (0.0001–0.01). Models were compiled using the Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 0.001, and training was executed with a standard batch size of 32. The loss function was set to binary cross-entropy for the classification model and mean squared error (MSE) for the regression model. The complete Python implementation, including the exhaustive search space configuration and model training scripts, has been made publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/oguzhanpanatli/ferritin-prediction-cbc).

Statistics

A non-parametric Friedman test was employed to compare the performance of the five machine learning classification models based on the 5-fold CV F1-scores. When a significant difference was detected, the Nemenyi post-hoc test was used to identify which model pairs differed. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed in Python using the SciPy [18] and scikit-posthocs [19] libraries.

Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI)

The explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) [20] method was used to enhance the comprehensibility of the best-performing model for the regression model. SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations) [21], which is a model-agnostic approach based on game theory, was applied for this purpose. SHAP values were primarily used to evaluate global feature importance and provided information about the features that had the most considerable impact on the model’s predictions. The XAI was conducted using Python and the SHAP library.

Results

Performance of classification models

A total of five supervised learning algorithms—LR, SVM, RF, XGBoost, and ANN—were trained using the training dataset, with hyperparameter optimization performed via 5‑fold CV. Classification performance was first evaluated using 5‑fold CV on the training set and subsequently validated on the held‑out test set. The ANN model resulted in a test accuracy of 0.752 and an F1-score of 0.760, while the XGBoost model yielded a test accuracy of 0.756 and an F1-score of 0.759, demonstrating comparable predictive capabilities. In contrast, the LR model exhibited the lowest performance metrics, achieving a test accuracy of 0.740 and an F1-score of 0.739. Specifically for the ANN model, following the hyperparameter optimization process, the optimal architecture for the binary classification task converged to a streamlined single-hidden-layer structure with 48 neurons utilizing a PReLU activation function. The optimal regularization parameters included a dropout rate of 0.40 and an L2 penalty of 0.00855. The Adam optimizer converged with momentum parameters of beta_1 = 0.920 and beta_2 = 0.999. The model was trained for a maximum of 250 epochs, with the early stopping mechanism triggered by monitoring the validation AUC (patience of 10 epochs). Statistical analysis

The classification models’ performances were compared using fold-wise F1-scores obtained through 5-fold cross-validation. The Friedman test indicated a statistically significant difference among the models (p = 0.0213). Subsequent Nemenyi post-hoc analysis revealed that the performance of Logistic Regression was significantly lower than that of XGBoost and ANN (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed among the remaining model pairs. Given that the statistical analysis showed no significant performance difference between the top-performing, the ANN was selected as the representative model for detailed error analysis, consistent with its application in the subsequent regression phase.

Performance of regression model

Following the binary classification, the ANN model was trained to predict continuous ferritin values using the same dataset. After the hyperparameter optimization, the optimal regression architecture was determined to be substantially deeper than the classification model, comprising 9 hidden layers. The number of neurons across these layers varied dynamically (ranging from 32 to 432), utilizing a combination of LeakyReLU, PReLU, and ReLU activations. Layer-specific dropout rates ranged from 0.0 to 0.40, and L2 penalties ranged from 0.00010 to 0.00270. The Adam optimizer converged with beta_1 = 0.87 and beta_2 = 0.995, and the model was trained for up to 500 epochs with early stopping monitoring the validation MSE (patience of 25 epochs). During the training using 5-fold CV, the model yielded an MAE of 30.82 ± 1.23 ng/mL and an R2 of 0.36 ± 0.01. Subsequently, on the test set, the model achieved an MAE of 31.63 ng/mL and an R² of 0.32. The close alignment between training and test performance metrics, as summarized in Table 4, indicated robust generalization capability with no substantial signs of overfitting.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed and validated ML models capable of screening for iron deficiency (ID) using solely CBC parameters together with basic demographic variables (age and sex), achieving a high discriminative ability (AUC of 0.837 for the ANN model). Our findings reinforce the growing body of evidence suggesting that routine hematological data contain significant latent information regarding body iron stores, which can be effectively extracted using non-linear algorithms. This aligns with recent large-scale studies, such as those by Y-H Chang, C-Y Chen, C-T Hsiao, Y-C Chang, H-Y Lai, H-H Lin, Y-L Wu, C-C Chen, L-C Hsu and T-T Chen [22] and O Efros, S Soffer, A Mudrik, R Robinson, G Kenet, GN Nadkarni and E Klang [3], which reported similar high diagnostic performance for CBC-based ID detection. However, unlike these previous approaches that often utilized broad inclusion criteria, our study specifically focused on a “clean” cohort by rigorously excluding inflammatory cases (elevated WBC and ESR), thereby ensuring that the predicted ferritin status reflects true iron stores rather than acute-phase fluctuations [1, 23]. This methodological refinement positions our model as a robust tool for identifying iron deficiency in its pure form, unconfounded by systemic inflammation.

A key aspect supporting the interpretability of our machine learning framework is the physiological coherence of the feature-importance hierarchy revealed by the SHAP analysis. The model consistently assigned strong positive contributions to MCH and MCV, whereas RDW-CV exerted a pronounced negative influence. These patterns reflect well-established mechanisms of iron-restricted erythropoiesis: declining iron stores impair heme synthesis, resulting in hypochromic (low MCH) and microcytic (low MCV) erythrocyte production, while the coexistence of older normocytic cells with newly produced iron-deficient cells increases size heterogeneity and elevates RDW (anisocytosis). Such physiologic correspondence indicates that the model’s learned relationships extend beyond statistical correlation and capture biologically meaningful erythropoietic responses.

These SHAP-derived patterns are consistent with recent clinical observations. OA Babah, CF Chieme, AI Abioye, SO Spaine, TA Adeyemo and BB Afolabi [24] identified RDW as the most sensitive discriminator of early iron deficiency, highlighting its responsiveness to subtle alterations in marrow iron availability. Likewise, D Županić, A Višnić, T Brenčić, G Juričić, L Honović and T Štimac [25] demonstrated that composite indices incorporating RDW and hematocrit outperform hemoglobin alone (AUC > 0.90), underscoring the diagnostic value embedded in simple hematologic parameters. The agreement between these empirical findings and our model’s internal weighting suggests that the algorithm is leveraging physiologic signal rather than spurious variance. Accordingly, the SHAP profiles provide an interpretable, white-box–like explanation of how the model integrates routine CBC metrics to infer ferritin status.

Our findings are concordant with recent advancements in hematology-centered machine learning, yet the methodological architecture of our approach distinguishes it from existing models. Y-H Chang, C-Y Chen, C-T Hsiao, Y-C Chang, H-Y Lai, H-H Lin, Y-L Wu, C-C Chen, L-C Hsu and T-T Chen [22] and O Efros, S Soffer, A Mudrik, R Robinson, G Kenet, GN Nadkarni and E Klang [3] have demonstrated that CBC-derived features can achieve high discriminative performance for iron deficiency, but their frameworks largely operate as opaque decision systems. A recurring critique of such models is the absence of transparent reasoning pathways, limiting their adoption in clinical workflows. By contrast, our study incorporates SHAP-based explainability at its core, directly addressing the need for interpretable AI highlighted by S Pullakhandam and S McRoy [26]. In addition, S Kurstjens, T De Bel, A Van Der Horst, R Kusters, J Krabbe and J Van Balveren [23] emphasize that ferritin-driven algorithms are particularly vulnerable to misclassification when inflammatory states are present due to ferritin’s acute-phase behavior. By systematically excluding samples with elevated WBC or ESR prior to model development, we ensured that predictions were trained on iron-dependent rather than inflammation-driven ferritin variability. This dual strategy—explicit explainability and stringent biological purification of the training set—positions our model as a more clinically coherent and mechanistically anchored alternative to previously published systems.

While our classification models achieved robust performance, the regression model designed to predict exact serum ferritin concentrations showed moderate efficacy, characterized by a physiological “saturation effect” where red cell indices plateau despite rising iron stores. Nevertheless, a detailed examination of model performance revealed that the ANN generally captured the majority of the data distribution successfully, as reflected in the percentile-based analysis (Table 5). Furthermore, the near-normal distribution of residuals indicates that the model generally produces unbiased predictions, with no evident systematic error. The absence of a systematic prediction error pattern suggests that errors are largely random rather than model-driven, supporting the overall reliability of the ANN predictions (Fig. 5b).

Beyond model performance, several limitations inherent to our study design warrant discussion. First, the strict exclusion of patients with leukocytosis (WBC > 10,000/mm³) or elevated ESR limits immediate generalizability to clinical scenarios involving active inflammation. However, this was a deliberate strategy to establish a “ground truth” dataset unconfounded by ferritin’s acute-phase reactant behavior. Second, while random undersampling effectively addressed class imbalance during training, this artificial equilibration of prevalence means the model’s positive predictive value (PPV) in a real-world setting—where iron deficiency prevalence is lower—requires careful calibration. Third, although we statistically filtered outliers, genetic confirmation for hemoglobinopathies was not performed. Given the regional prevalence of thalassemia, future iterations should integrate a multi-class output, a capability recently validated by W Tepakhan, W Srisintorn, T Penglong and P Saelue [27]. Finally, as a single-center study utilizing specific analyzer platforms (Sysmex XN-1000), external validation on diverse cohorts and device manufacturers is essential to confirm the model’s robustness against analytical variability.

Ultimately, the primary clinical implication of this model is its potential to serve as a high-yield, “zero-cost” screening tool. D Wang, M Sra, S Glaeser-Khan, DY Wang, R Moshashaian‐Asl, S Ito, A Cuker and G Goshua [28] recently highlighted that universal ferritin screening, while clinically beneficial, incurs significant costs that may be prohibitive in many healthcare systems. Since CBC is the most frequently ordered laboratory test worldwide, our model allows for opportunistic screening of iron deficiency without additional phlebotomy or reagents. This capability enables a tiered diagnostic workflow: identifying high-risk individuals for confirmatory biochemical testing while reducing unnecessary assays for the low-risk majority.

Although the present study evaluated individual classifiers and a single ANN architecture, recent advances in ensemble learning and mixture-of-experts (MoE) frameworks suggest that adaptively combining specialized sub-models through gating mechanisms can further improve predictive performance in complex biomedical tasks. For instance, fuzzy MoE-based approaches have shown promising results in aggregating multiscale features for biomedical image recognition under heterogeneous conditions [29]. Future work could explore whether such adaptive expert aggregation strategies, tailored to demographic or hematologic subgroups, may enhance CBC-based ferritin prediction, particularly in clinically challenging borderline ranges.

Conclusion

In summary, this study validates a machine learning framework that transforms routine hemogram data into actionable diagnostic insight. By deciphering the non-linear relationships between red cell indices and iron status with clinical transparency, the model offers a scalable, economically viable alternative for early ID screening, effectively bridging the gap between advanced data analytics and everyday clinical decision-making.
