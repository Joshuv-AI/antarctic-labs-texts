Association of adjuvant somatostatin analogue therapy with disease-free survival in patients with gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours: a Chinese multi-institutional propensity score matched and weighted analysis (CASSANET-GEP)
Authors: Huike Wang, Jing Hao, Yanwei Su, Qilong Chen, Enxiao Li, Yinying Wu, Dan Cao, Jie Yu, Haibo Lu, Guiqi Wang, Chengfeng Wang, Hong Zhao, Dongbing Zhao, Jian Wang, Xiangling Wang, Jiaqi Xu, Meichen Wang, Xiaofen Li, Yuanjie Sun, Jingrong Wang, Qiyun Tang, Lijie Song, Yihebali Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrence remains a major concern after curative-intent resection of gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (GEP-NETs), and evidence to guide postoperative adjuvant therapy is limited.MethodsWe performed a multicentre retrospective cohort study across nine university-affiliated hospitals in China, including patients with grade 1–3 GEP-NETs who underwent curative-intent resection between January 2007 and December 2024; follow-up ended on 1 October 2025. Exposure was postoperative adjuvant SSAs. The primary endpoint was disease-free survival (DFS) and the secondary endpoint was overall survival (OS). DFS and OS were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, and associations were evaluated using Cox regression after propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Time-related bias was addressed using time-dependent Cox and landmark analyses at 3 and 6 months after surgery.ResultsAmong 1602 patients, 358 received adjuvant SSAs. After 1:1 PSM, 560 patients were retained with improved baseline balance. Adjuvant SSAs were associated with a lower recurrence risk in the overall cohort in unadjusted (HR 0.466, 95% CI 0.323–0.673; p < 0.001) and IPTW-adjusted analyses (HR 0.419, 95% CI 0.278–0.631; p < 0.001), with similar directionality in G-NETs and P-NETs, whereas estimates in E-NETs were imprecise. In time-dependent Cox analyses treating SSAs as an ever-started time-varying exposure, adjuvant SSAs remained associated with longer DFS in the whole cohort (adjusted HR 0.519, 95% CI 0.353–0.764; p < 0.001), and findings were consistent in landmark analyses at 3 months (adjusted HR 0.523, 95% CI 0.339–0.806; p = 0.003) and 6 months (adjusted HR 0.537, 95% CI 0.356–0.811; p = 0.003). In the overall cohort, 24- and 36-month DFS rates were 92.4% and 89.3% in the SSAs cohort versus 86.9% and 84.2% in controls, respectively; corresponding 24- and 36-month OS rates were 99.1% and 98.7% versus 97.2% and 96.9%, respectively.ConclusionsIn this large multicentre real-world cohort, adjuvant SSAs were associated with longer DFS after curative-intent resection of GEP-NETs. The overall direction of association remained consistent in propensity-adjusted, time-dependent, and landmark analyses, supporting the robustness of the primary findings. Prospective studies are needed to confirm effectiveness and refine patient selection.

Background

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are heterogeneous malignancies that can arise throughout the body. The stomach, intestine, and pancreas are among the most common primary sites and account for a substantial proportion of cases [1, 2]. In the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, digestive system NENs are categorised as well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) or poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) [3–5]. Although NENs represent a small fraction of all malignant tumours, their incidence has increased markedly over recent decades [6, 7]. Clinical management remains challenging because disease behaviour ranges from indolent to aggressive and varies by primary site and functional status [8].

For localised gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (GEP-NETs), curative-intent resection is the mainstay of treatment [8]. However, most international guidelines remain cautious regarding postoperative adjuvant therapy. The 2020 European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guideline states that available data do not support routine adjuvant therapy for grade 1–2 NETs, and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline notes that the role of adjuvant therapy is unclear [9, 10]. In contrast, Chinese guidelines provide more practice-oriented suggestions for selected higher-risk patients. The 2024 Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guideline indicates that adjuvant therapy may be considered for grade 2 pancreatic NETs (P-NETs) with high-risk features after multidisciplinary discussion, including consideration of somatostatin analogues (SSAs) [11]. Collectively, these discrepancies underscore the need for higher-quality evidence in the postoperative management.

SSAs alleviate hormone-related symptoms by binding somatostatin receptors (SSTRs) and also exert antiproliferative effects [12, 13]. Randomised trials in advanced disease, including PROMID and CLARINET, have demonstrated prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) with SSAs therapy in selected populations [14–17]. These data have prompted interest in SSAs as postoperative treatment to suppress minimal residual disease and reduce recurrence risk. However, evidence supporting SSAs specifically in the adjuvant setting remains limited.

Therefore, using a large multicentre real-world cohort, we aimed to evaluate the association between postoperative adjuvant SSAs therapy and survival outcomes after curative-intent resection of GEP-NETs, and to assess the robustness of these associations in propensity-adjusted and time-aware analyses.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a multicentre retrospective cohort study across nine centres in China. Consecutive patients with primary GEP-NETs of grades 1–3 according to the 2022 WHO classification who underwent surgery between January 2007 and December 2024 were screened for eligibility (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria were a history of other malignancies, receipt of systemic antitumour therapy before surgery, or missing key recurrence or survival follow-up data (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The study was approved by the lead-centre ethics committee (Approval Number: 25/324-5270) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.Fig. 1Study flowchart. Definitions and staging

Curative-intent resection was defined as R0 or R1 resection with no radiographic evidence of residual disease after surgery. Tumours were staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition using the corresponding site-specific staging systems for gastric neuroendocrine tumours (G-NETs), enteric neuroendocrine tumours (E-NETs), and P-NETs. Patients with M1 disease were eligible only if metastatic lesions had been completely removed, either synchronously or in a staged procedure, such that no radiographic residual disease remained postoperatively.

Exposure and comparator

In the SSAs cohort, postoperative adjuvant therapy consisted of octreotide long-acting release (LAR) (20 or 30 mg deep intramuscularly every 4 weeks) or lanreotide autogel (ATG) (120 mg deep subcutaneously every 4 weeks). Treatment initiation and regimen selection were determined by the treating team, and the start and stop dates of postoperative SSAs therapy were recorded. The median duration of SSAs therapy was 12.27 months. The control cohort received standard postoperative surveillance without adjuvant systemic therapy.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint was DFS, defined as the time from surgery to the first documented recurrence (local or distant) or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Recurrence was defined as the first documented local or distant relapse after surgery on follow-up imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), with endoscopic, pathological, or operative findings used to support the diagnosis when available. The secondary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. Follow-up was performed according to institutional practice and typically included history taking, physical examination, and abdominal multiphasic CT or MRI, with chest CT and biochemical markers assessed when clinically indicated. Follow-up was generally conducted between 12 weeks and 12 months after surgery and then every 6–12 months for up to 10 years; surveillance beyond 10 years was performed as clinically indicated. Patients without events were censored at the last follow-up (1 October 2025).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using χ² tests. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression including prespecified covariates, namely age, sex, carcinoid syndrome, primary tumour site, tumour size, grade, vascular invasion, nerve invasion, Ki-67 index, and AJCC T, N, and M stage. Patients with missing key recurrence or survival follow-up data were excluded during cohort construction. For baseline covariates entering the propensity score and Cox models, unavailable values were retained as explicit “unknown” categories where recorded, rather than being imputed. We performed 1:1 nearest-neighbour propensity score matching (PSM) with a caliper of 0.05 and conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative matching ratios (1:2, 1:3, and 1:4). Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was performed using propensity score-derived inverse-probability weights, with truncation at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme weights. Covariate balance was assessed using standardised mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.10 indicating good balance. Kaplan–Meier methods were used to generate survival curves; unweighted comparisons used the log-rank test, and weighted comparisons used the Wald test in weighted Cox models. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the proportional hazards assumption was assessed.

Additional time-dependent sensitivity analyses were undertaken to address potential immortal-time and other time-related biases. Time-dependent Cox models were fitted using adjuvant SSAs as an ever-started time-varying exposure, whereby person-time accrued before treatment initiation was classified as unexposed and person-time accrued thereafter as exposed. Landmark analyses were also conducted at 3 and 6 months after surgery, restricted to patients who were alive and recurrence-free at each landmark time point, with follow-up recalculated from the corresponding landmark. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were fitted to evaluate potential non-linear associations between SSAs duration and recurrence risk. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.4.2, and all tests were two-sided with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1824 patients with grade 1–3 GEP-NETs who underwent curative-intent resection between January 2007 and December 2024 were screened. After exclusion of 222 patients with incomplete data, 1602 patients were included, comprising 358 in the SSAs cohort and 1244 in the control cohort. Primary sites included G-NETs (n = 370, 23.1%), E-NETs (n = 496, 31.0%), and P-NETs (n = 736, 45.9%). Association between adjuvant SSAs and DFS

Associations between clinical factors and DFS are presented in Fig. 2. In unadjusted Cox models, adjuvant SSAs were associated with a lower recurrence risk in the overall cohort (HR 0.466, 95% CI 0.323–0.673; p < 0.001; Additional file 1: Table S6), with similar directionality in G-NETs (HR 0.332, 95% CI 0.192–0.575; p < 0.001; Additional file 1: Table S7) and P-NETs (HR 0.402, 95% CI 0.198–0.816; p = 0.012; Additional file 1: Table S9). For E-NETs, the estimate was imprecise (HR 0.370, 95% CI 0.137–1.000; p = 0.050; Additional file 1: Table S8). In IPTW-adjusted Cox models, the association persisted in the overall cohort (HR 0.419, 95% CI 0.278–0.631; p < 0.001) and in G-NETs (HR 0.390, 95% CI 0.218–0.698; p = 0.002).Fig. 2IPTW multivariate Cox regression analyses of factors associated with DFS. In IPTW-adjusted multivariable models, gastric primary site (HR 3.126, 95% CI 1.869–5.230; p < 0.001), grade 3 histology (HR 2.336, 95% CI 1.011–5.400; p = 0.047), and distant metastasis (HR 4.594, 95% CI 2.424–8.703; p < 0.001) were independently associated with recurrence in the overall cohort (Additional file 1: Table S6). In E-NETs, recurrence was associated with age ≥55 years, Ki-67 5–10%, and distant metastasis (Additional file 1: Table S8). Subgroup and biomarker analyses

In the 1:1 PSM GEP-NETs cohort, longer DFS was observed among patients with genetic mutations, those not receiving concomitant non-SSAs medications, those not receiving radiotherapy, and those treated with SSAs for 7–12 months, whereas shorter DFS was observed in patients with MSI, grade 3 histology, Ki-67 > 10%, and a gastric primary site (Additional file 1: Fig. S12). In analyses restricted to SSAs-treated patients, younger age, female sex, absence of distant metastasis, no concomitant drug use, and no radiotherapy were associated with greater apparent benefit (Additional file 1: Fig. S13).

Organ-specific subgroup analyses suggested additional heterogeneity (Additional file 1: Figs. S14–S16). In the matched G-NETs, younger age, female sex, absence of vascular invasion, MSS status, SSAs duration of 7–12 months, MGMT expression 2+ or 3 + , and grade 1 histology were associated with longer DFS (Additional file 1: Fig. S14). In E-NETs, absence of distant metastasis, MSS status, and Ki-67 < 5% were associated with more favourable DFS (Additional file 1: Fig. S15). In P-NETs, T1 stage, SSTR2 expression 2+ or 3 + , absence of distant metastasis, and MSS status were associated with longer DFS (Additional file 1: Fig. S16).

Discussion

In this large multicentre real-world cohort of 1602 patients undergoing curative-intent resection for G1–G3 GEP-NETs, postoperative adjuvant SSAs were associated with longer DFS. This association was observed in the overall cohort in unadjusted and propensity-adjusted analyses and remained directionally consistent in additional time-dependent Cox and 3- and 6-month landmark analyses addressing delayed treatment initiation. Organ-specific analyses suggested similar directionality in G-NETs and P-NETs, whereas estimates in E-NETs remained imprecise. Although absolute differences in early DFS were modest and OS remained high in both groups, the overall pattern supports the robustness of the primary DFS findings. Given the non-randomised design, these results should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

SSAs are well established in advanced GEP-NETs, including as first-line or subsequent therapy and within combination regimens. The PROMID study showed that octreotide long-acting release delayed tumour progression in advanced metastatic NETs, and the CLARINET study demonstrated prolonged PFS with lanreotide in grade 1–2 advanced NETs [18]. Our findings are consistent with the possibility that a similar anti-proliferative association may extend to the postoperative setting. However, the adjuvant context differs substantially from advanced disease with respect to tumour burden, event rates, and biological heterogeneity, and dedicated postoperative evidence remains necessary. In this context, the present study provides real-world data supporting further prospective evaluation of adjuvant SSAs, particularly in clinically defined higher-risk groups.

Surgery remains the cornerstone of management for resectable disease [19, 20]. Current guidelines highlight the absence of high-level evidence supporting routine adjuvant systemic therapy after curative-intent resection of NETs, and data on efficacy, risk-benefit trade-offs, and cost-effectiveness remain limited [9–11, 21, 22]. Existing postoperative studies of SSAs have largely focused on symptom control and have not consistently demonstrated improvements in recurrence or survival outcomes, and these analyses are often constrained by small sample sizes and heterogeneous populations [23, 24]. Against this background, our multicentre cohort with complementary propensity-based adjustment and additional time-aware analyses provides further real-world evidence of an association between adjuvant SSAs and longer DFS, while residual confounding cannot be excluded.

We observed primary-site heterogeneity in the association between adjuvant SSAs and DFS. The more consistent associations in G-NETs and P-NETs are compatible with the biology of many well-differentiated NETs that retain SSTR expression, including SSTR2 [25–27]. In E-NETs, several factors could contribute to imprecision, including a predominance of rectal primaries, potentially lower receptor expression in a subset [28], an indolent natural history with low recurrence rates in lower-risk disease [29–31], and reduced effective sample size after matching. Accordingly, the enteric findings should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect; rather, they likely reflect limited power and underlying heterogeneity, and the postoperative role of SSAs in E-NETs warrants confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Among these site-specific signals, the P-NET findings merit particular attention because they were additionally supported by receptor-based biomarker analyses. Higher SSTR2 expression was associated with longer DFS, supporting SSTR2 as a candidate biomarker for postoperative benefit. This is biologically plausible, as SSAs bind somatostatin receptors and may exert antiproliferative effects through cell-cycle regulation, apoptosis-related signalling, and inhibition of growth-factor pathways [32]. Given the high affinity of octreotide and lanreotide for SSTR2, receptor abundance may influence pharmacodynamic effects and help refine postoperative patient selection. Differences by grade are also biologically plausible, as G1/G2 tumours more often retain receptor expression, whereas G3 NETs more frequently show reduced receptor expression and enrichment of alternative oncogenic drivers, including TP53 alterations and RB1 inactivation, which may limit responsiveness [3, 33].

This interpretation is also compatible with the broader clinical spectrum of P-NETs management, which may span surveillance, surgery, and systemic treatment according to tumour size, grade, biological behaviour, receptor profile, and patient preference. Consistent with this spectrum, a recent retrospective study comparing SSAs with active surveillance in small sporadic nonfunctioning G1–G2 P-NETs (≤2 cm) reported longer PFS with SSAs treatment, underscoring the importance of careful patient selection [34]. At a broader biological level, emerging evidence also suggests that lipid metabolism and homeostasis may contribute to NENs biology, treatment-response variability, and potential therapeutic vulnerability, providing additional context for the heterogeneity observed across patients and tumour subtypes [35].

In exploratory RCS analyses, we observed a non-linear association between SSAs duration and recurrence risk. This pattern may reflect differences in effective exposure, receptor internalisation or downregulation, and time-related bias inherent to retrospective duration analyses [36]. Because treatment duration is a post-baseline exposure, the interval estimates should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. These findings may still be useful for generating hypotheses for future prospective studies on treatment duration, but should not be used to infer an optimal postoperative treatment window from the present data alone.

Hereditary susceptibility contributes to a subset of NENs, and germline testing may be clinically relevant in selected patients. In our tested subset, pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variants were identified in approximately 25% of patients, including alterations in DNA damage repair genes such as BRCA1 and RAD50 (Additional file 1: Fig. S17). Because testing was not universal and may have been driven by clinical suspicion, this proportion should be interpreted cautiously. These findings suggest that inherited predisposition may be under-recognised in routine practice and support further evaluation of genetic counselling and appropriately selected testing in postoperative risk-stratification research.

Strengths of this study include its large sample size, multicentre design, and use of complementary propensity-based and time-aware analyses to reduce measured confounding and time-related bias. Several limitations should nevertheless be noted. First, the retrospective, non-randomised design cannot exclude residual confounding and selection bias, including confounding by indication; in addition, postoperative surveillance intensity was not fully standardised across centres and may also have differed between treated and untreated patients, potentially influencing recurrence ascertainment and the timing of DFS events. Second, although we performed time-dependent and landmark analyses to reduce immortal-time and related biases arising from delayed treatment initiation, such biases cannot be fully excluded in an observational dataset. Third, SSAs exposure was heterogeneous across agents, and dose intensity and adherence could not be characterised in detail, limiting agent and dose-specific inference. Fourth, median follow-up of approximately four years may be insufficient to capture late events in an indolent tumour population, particularly for longer-term OS comparisons. Finally, biomarker data were incomplete, which may introduce selection bias in correlative analyses and limit generalisability; biomarker-based subgroup findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Conclusions

Overall, this multicentre real-world study suggests that postoperative adjuvant SSAs were associated with longer DFS in selected patients with resected G1–G3 GEP-NETs. These findings support a more individualised postoperative strategy guided by recurrence risk, primary tumour site, and tumour biology, while prospective studies are required to confirm effectiveness, refine patient selection, and determine optimal treatment duration.
