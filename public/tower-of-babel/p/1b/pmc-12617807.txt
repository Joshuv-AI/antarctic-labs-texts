Gestational Weight Gain and Different Degrees of Preterm Birth: A Hospital‐Based Study in Chinese Singleton Pregnancies
Authors: Xiaomei Xiang, Weihua Wang, Yan Yu, Shaonong Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe link between gestational weight gain and preterm birth remains inconclusive. The present study examines the association between gestational weight gain (GWG) and preterm birth among the Chinese population.DesignA retrospective observational study design.MethodsData was from a hospital‐based sub‐study of the physical growth and development survey for Chinese newborns with different gestational ages, which was conducted from July 2015 to August 2017. Singleton newborns aged from 24 to 42 + 6 weeks' gestation and their mothers were investigated. A self‐designed questionnaire was used for the collection of information. 3203 participants were included in the analysis and a logistic regression model was adopted to explore the association of GWG and different degrees of preterm birth and odds ratios and 95% CI were estimated.ResultsThe mean GWG in the preterm group was significantly lower than that in the term group (13.00 ± 4.87 vs 15.72 ± 4.88 kg). After adjusting for potential confounders, the odds ratio of preterm birth was 2.13 (95% CI: 1.62–2.78) for mothers with low GWG and 0.42 (95% CI: 0.32–0.56) for those with excess GWG in comparison with those with adequate GWG. The odds ratio of low GWG was about 4.06 (95% CI: 2.87–5.72) for very preterm birth, 2.14 (95% CI: 1.47–3.10) for moderate preterm birth and 1.40 (95% CI: 1.03–1.91) for late preterm birth. The odds ratio of excess GWG was about 0.36 (95% CI: 0.24–0.54) for very term birth, 0.38 (95% CI: 0.25–0.57) for moderate preterm birth and 0.47 (95% CI: 0.35–0.64) for late preterm birth.Patient or Public ContributionAdverse impact of low GWG was dependent on the degree of preterm birth. Weight management and health education during pregnancy in maternal nursing practice should focus on the appropriate increase of GWG against preterm birth.Keywords: gestational weight gain, maternal nursing, preterm birthbirth outcome

Introduction

Preterm birth is one of the adverse birth outcomes, which takes place more than 3 weeks before the baby's estimated due date. Due to the immaturity of organs and systems, it is often accompanied by respiratory system abnormalities, metabolic abnormalities and other recent complications. It accounts for more than 70% of perinatal deaths and seriously endangers the safety and health of newborns (Chang et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2012; WHO ACTION Trials Collaborators 2019). Preterm birth is also prone to cause motor disorders and cognitive impairment on long‐term growth, development and health of newborns (Allotey et al. 2018; Hee Chung et al. 2020). The globally estimated preterm birth rate increased by 14.7% from 1990 to 2010 (Blencowe et al. 2012). The updated estimated global preterm birth rate in 2014 was 10.6% and varies across regions and countries. European countries had a lower rate of 8.7% while there was a higher rate of 10.4% in Asian countries (Chawanpaiboon et al. 2019). In China, the preterm birth rate increased slightly from 5.4% in 2012 to 5.7% in 2018 in singleton pregnancies, which was attributed to an increase in very and late preterm births (Deng et al. 2021). Generally, preterm is defined as babies born alive before 37 weeks of pregnancy are completed. Although moderate and late preterm birth (32–37 weeks) accounts for nearly 85% of all preterm births, there are still 15% of preterm births that are extremely or very preterm births (< 32 weeks) (Chawanpaiboon et al. 2019). Those newborns with extremely or very preterm births would face greater risks of death and long‐term impairment on growth and development (Glass et al. 2015; Geisler et al. 2021). Investigating measures against preterm birth is still an urgent issue in maternal nursing practice that needs to be addressed.

Background

Gestational weight gain (GWG) reflects maternal nutrition during pregnancy and fetal intrauterine growth, which may be one important factor contributing to maternal and infant outcomes (LifeCycle Project‐Maternal Obesity and Childhood Outcomes Study Group et al. 2019). Excess GWG was found to be associated with gestational diabetes mellitus and macrosomia while low GWG increased the risk of small for gestational age and low birth weight (Goldstein et al. 2017). However, the association between GWG and preterm birth is still controversial and inconclusive (Ozcan et al. 2017; Goldstein et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2020). Low GWG was regarded as a risk factor for preterm birth in some studies but associations between excess GWG and preterm birth varied (Ozcan et al. 2017). Previous studies found excessive GWG associated with a 27% increase in the risk of preterm birth among women in China but a recent study in south China indicated that excess GWG reduced the risk of preterm birth by 19% (Huang et al. 2016; Hu et al. 2020). Previous studies also reported that the association between GWG and preterm birth varied across cultures and ethnicities (Goldstein et al. 2018). Moreover, the few studies investigated associations between GWG and sub‐categories of preterm birth based on gestational age, especially the association of GWG with extremely or very preterm births (Freitas et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2020). Exploring the causes or risk factors related to preterm birth is key for the prevention of preterm birth and the reduction of neonatal mortality in the maternal nursing practice. Therefore, we used hospital‐based data to examine the association between GWG and preterm birth among the Chinese population living in northwest China. Especially, the association of GWG with different degrees of preterm birth was examined. Our study tried to help maternal nursing staff and policy‐makers guide pregnant women in maternal weight management and reduce the risk of preterm birth.

Method

Study Design and Participants

Data was from a sub‐study in Xi'an, which was one part of a physical growth and development survey for Chinese newborns with different gestational ages by the Ministry of Health of China, and it was a cross‐sectional study. Twenty‐one major hospitals with obstetrics departments in Xi'an, China were selected for the survey between July 2015 and August 2017. Singleton newborns aged from 24 to 42 + 6 weeks' gestation and their mothers were included. The mothers of newborns resided in Xi'an for greater than 2 years and were aged from 18 to 40 years. Due to the objective of determining the status of growth and development of the newborns in the survey, the newborns whose mothers were exposed to the potential high‐risk factors during pregnancy could not be included. Therefore, the mothers were excluded when they smoked, abused alcohol, or were drug‐dependent 3 months before pregnancy or during pregnancy, took adrenal cortical hormone or other immunosuppressive drugs for more than 1 month during pregnancy, and had severe anaemia, diabetes, hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, cardiac and renal dysfunction, or chronic hypertension. The newborns were excluded when they were born from artificial pregnancy and had serious congenital malformation, limb deformity, fetal edema or chromosomal abnormality at birth or had missing information on gestational age. Totally 3225 newborns and their mothers were included in this sub‐study. Due to missing values on such main covariates as family annual income, paternal age, paternal education, 22 participants were excluded and 3203 participants were included finally in the present study and we did not impute missing values because of less missing data.

A self‐designed questionnaire in the survey was used for the collection of information on birth outcome, family background, prenatal examination and anthropometric measurement for newborns. Before the investigation, a municipal‐level technical group was established. The heads of the investigation and their staff were trained and examined. Testing tools and investigation procedures were unified. The on‐site guidance was conducted per month during the investigation. All the investigation questionnaires were reviewed and checked and the qualified ones were coded and inputted into the standard database. Written informed consent was obtained from the mothers or parents of the newborns in the survey. The use of data was approved by Xi'an Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital which was responsible for the Xi'an survey and this study was approved by the institution's ethics committee.

Gestational Age and Preterm Birth

Gestational age was determined according to the mother's last menstrual period (LMP) and the ultrasonic examination during early pregnancy. When LMP was not clear, ultrasonic examination was used to determine gestational age. Preterm birth refers to delivery before 37 weeks of gestation and it was further categorised into three groups in the present study: very preterm birth (gestational age < 32 weeks), moderate preterm (32 weeks ≤ gestational age < 33 weeks and 6 days) and late preterm (34 weeks ≤ gestational age < 36 weeks and 6 days). This classification was slightly different from the traditional classification system (Glass et al. 2015). Due to the smaller number of extremely preterm births, we combined the newborns with less than 28 weeks of gestation and those with 28 to less than 32 weeks of gestation as the very preterm group.

Pre‐Pregnancy BMI and Gestational Weight Gain

Pre‐pregnancy BMI was classified into four groups according to the 2009 IOM standards: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m2). Gestational weight gain (GWG) was calculated by subtracting pre‐pregnancy weight from maternal weight at delivery. According to the 2009 IOM Guidelines, recommended GWG for underweight is 12.5–18 kg, normal weight is 11.5–16 kg, overweight is 7–11.5 kg, and obese is 5–9 kg (Rasmussen et al. 2009).

Covariates

Previous studies showed that a body of factors from the environment, family, individuals could be associated with preterm birth, which may confound the association between GWG and preterm birth (Goldenberg et al. 2008). The covariates considered in the present study included newborn gender (male and female), mode of delivery (vaginal delivery, caesarean section), obstetric complications (yes or no), pre‐pregnancy BMI, paternal age (years) (< 25, 25‐, 30‐, ≥ 35), paternal education level (postgraduate, college graduate, senior school, junior school and below), annual family income (◊thousands, CNY ¥) (< 50, 50‐, 100‐, 300‐), gravidity (1, 2, 3 or ≥ 4 times), and parity (0–1 or ≥ 2 times).

Statistical Analyses

The characteristics of participants were described with mean and standard deviation or numbers and percentages. Differences in characteristics and GWG between the newborns with and without preterm birth were compared with a t‐test or χ 2 test. The adjusted mean of GWG (kg) for potential covariates across the spectrum of preterm birth used a generalized linear model. A logistic regression model was adopted to explore the association of pregnancy weight gain and preterm birth and odds ratio and 95% CI were estimated. A multinomial logistic regression model was used to examine the association of GWG with different degrees of preterm birth. Considering the impact of potential covariates, three adjusted models were established. Model 1 was adjusted for the sex of newborns. Model 2 was adjusted further for delivery mode, ascertainment of gestational age, complications, gravidity, and parity based on Model 1. Model 3 was adjusted additionally for pre‐pregnant BMI, maternal age, paternal age, maternal education, paternal education and family annual income. Subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate possible heterogeneity of association according to main covariates. Considering the influence of gestation week on weight at delivery (Hutcheon and Bodnar 2018), two additional indicators reflecting GWG were established to adjust for gestation week in the present study. One was GWG per week which was determined with GWG divided simply by gestation weeks. Another was that GWG was adjusted for gestational age based on the residual error method. First, a linear regression model was set up between gestation week and maternal weight at delivery. The unstandardized residual from the regression model was the predicted value of maternal weight at delivery after eliminating the impact of gestation week. Then GWG adjusted for gestational age was calculated by adding these unstandardized residuals to the unstandardized predictive value of the linear regression model corresponding to the average gestation week (36.5 weeks of gestation) of the participants. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by regarding GWG per week or GWG adjusted for gestational age as continuous independent variables instead of GWG to repeat logistic regression and multinomial logistic regression analysis to examine the robustness of the association of interest. Another sensitivity analysis was to repeat the analysis on the association of interest by only controlling for the propensity score (PS) instead of all potential covariates. PS in the present study was the conditional probability that one participant was assigned to low GWG group, excess GWG group, or adequate GWG group under the given covariate conditions and it represented the joint effect of multiple covariates (Hullsiek and Louis 2002). PS of each participant was estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model, in which 11 selected covariates above were used. Stata 12.0 statistical software was used for all the analyses. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Participants

In total, 3203 participants were included in the final analysis, among them 1399 were preterm births and 1804 were term births, which were significantly different in the characteristics of newborns and their mothers (p < 0.05; Table S1). There were more males (55.3%) in newborns with preterm birth. The average gestational age for preterm birth was 33.05 ± 2.60 weeks. More mothers with premature babies tended to have caesarean sections and they underwent more gravidities and parities compared with those with term babies. The parents with premature babies tended to be older and have a lower education level. The families with premature babies tended to have a lower annual income.

Gestational Weight Gain

The mean BMI of mothers before pregnancy in the preterm group was slightly higher than that in the term birth group (21.49 ± 3.11 vs. 20.72 ± 2.74 kg/m2). The proportion of overweight and obesity was about 11.3% for the preterm birth group and 7.2% for the term birth group (p < 0.001). The mean gestational weight gain in the preterm group was significantly lower than that in the term group (13.00 ± 4.87 v.s 15.72 ± 4.88 kg). The proportion of those with lower weight gain in the two groups was 36.9% and 15.7%, respectively (Table 1).

Table S2 shows the increasing change of GWG across the whole gestation age. In the unadjusted model, GWG increased significantly from 11.19 kg (95% CI: 10.70–11.69) in very preterm birth to 15.72 kg (95% CI: 15.50–15.94) in normal term birth. After adjusting for the potential covariates, the tendency of GWG increase persisted and there was GWG of 11.12 kg (95% CI: 10.59–11.66) in very preterm birth and 15.89 kg (95% CI: 15.61–16.16).

Table 2 indicates the association between GWG and preterm birth. When controlling for newborn sex, the mothers with low GWG had a 2.58‐fold risk to give birth to a premature baby (95% CI: 2.15–3.09), but the mothers with excess GWG had a 36% decreased risk to give birth to a premature baby (95% CI: 0.53–0.75) compared with those with adequate GWG. After controlling for all potential covariates, the odds ratio of preterm birth was 2.13 (95% CI: 1.62–2.78) for the mothers with low GWG and 0.42 (95% CI: 0.32–0.56) for those with excess GWG in comparison with those with adequate GWG.

Subgroup Analysis and Sensitive Analysis

Across nearly all subgroups, low GWG was observed as a risk factor for preterm birth with low GWG (Figure 1) while the protective impact of excess GWG was found on preterm birth (Figure 2), which implied the robustness of the association of interest in the present study to some extent, and further confirmed the importance of appropriate gestational weight gain for a healthy pregnancy. Moreover, considering the influence of gestational age on birth weight, two additional indicators reflecting GWG were established instead of the original GWG to assess the association of interest as a sensitive analysis. Table S3 shows that the risk of preterm birth in offspring was reduced by 71% (OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12–0.68) when GWG per week of mothers increased by 1 kg. Similarly, the risk of preterm birth in offspring was reduced by 5% (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97) per 1 kg increase of GWG adjusted for the gestational age of mothers. Table S4 shows that the repeated multinomial logistic regression analysis using these two indicators confirmed a significant association between GWG and preterm birth. Table S5 also shows that all associations of interest were robust when only controlling for the propensity score (PS) instead of all potential covariates in logistic regression models.

Discussion

Main Findings

The pathogenesis of preterm birth remains completely unclear, and it could be a common end of multiple pregnancy complications involving the mother, uterus, placenta, fetal membrane and fetus (Freitas et al. 2018). Some risk factors could be related to preterm birth including race, genetics, maternal age and lifestyle behavior during pregnancy, and even socioeconomic status of parents (Goldenberg et al. 2008). Among them, maternal nutritional status could play an important role, and gestational weight gain is a key variable (LifeCycle Project‐Maternal Obesity and Childhood Outcomes Study Group et al. 2019). Unfortunately, the existing results seemed inconclusive due to different populations and varying measurements of GWG (Huang et al. 2016; Goldstein et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2020). The present study found that GWG in the preterm group was significantly 2 kg at least lower than that in the term group averaged in the Chinese population and the mothers with very preterm birth had lower GWG. After controlling for all potential covariates, the mothers with low GWG had a higher risk of giving birth to a premature baby while excess GWG showed a protective impact on preterm birth. Specifically, the adverse effect of low GWG on preterm birth seemed to be the largest for newborns with very preterm birth and decreased with the increase of gestational weeks. Moreover, sensitive analysis and subgroup analysis further confirmed the robustness of the association of interest.

Comparisons to Previous Studies

Body weight is one of the most important indicators of nutrition and health monitoring. GWG is a sensitive indicator representing nutritional status during pregnancy. Appropriate GWG is an important basis for the health of pregnant women and the growth and development of the fetus (Hutcheon and Bodnar 2018; Goldenberg et al. 2008). A recent study found that Chinese mothers with low GWG had a 92% increased risk of preterm birth compared with adequate GWG (Hu et al. 2020). While among women in Puerto Rico, low GWG was associated with a 34% increased risk of preterm birth (Zhang et al. 2016). A systematic review summarised that GWG below guidelines was associated with a higher risk of preterm birth in the USA/Europe (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.17–1.56) but not in Asia (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.78–1.44) than GWG within guidelines (Goldstein et al. 2018). The present study result on low GWG was consistent with previous studies but there was a larger effect size and the mothers with low GWG had a 2.58‐fold risk to be born to a premature baby. The present study further confirmed a close association between low GWG and the risk of preterm birth. However, the studies on the association of excess GWG with preterm birth were inconsistent. GWG above guidelines was associated with a lower risk for preterm birth than GWG within guidelines, a 21% lower risk for the USA/Europe and a 32% lower risk for Asia (Goldstein et al. 2018). For Chinese women, a similar reduced risk was observed (Hu et al. 2020) and the present study also showed a 58% lower risk of preterm birth for mothers with excess GWG after controlling for potential covariates. But there were not any associations observed between excessive GWG and PTB risk among women in Puerto Rico and women with twin pregnancies in the USA. These kinds of differences in effect size and controversial results could be due to ethnicity, selected population or analysis method (Goldstein et al. 2018). In the present study, the participants were selected rigorously, and the mothers with unhealthy lifestyles (such as smoking, alcohol drinking, etc.) and chronic diseases 3 months before pregnancy or during pregnancy were excluded. This meant that the mothers included in the study might be relatively healthy, which could make the link between GWG and preterm birth clearer in epidemiological analysis and partly interpret why the effect size of interest looked larger. Therefore, the present study provided more robust evidence on the link between GWG and preterm birth.

Present study especially investigated the association between GWG and severity of preterm birth. Regardless of severity of preterm birth, low GWG was found associated significantly with increasing risk of preterm birth. But it is noteworthy that adverse effect of low GWG on preterm birth seemed to be the largest for newborns with very preterm birth and decreased with the severity being lessened. The odds ratio of low GWG was about 4.06 for very preterm birth but 1.40 for late preterm birth, which further suggested appropriated GWG could play very important role in prevention or reduction of very preterm birth. Similar results were observed in other populations (Freitas et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2020) but their effect size looked smaller. Excess GWG was also observed associated significantly with decreasing risk of preterm birth, irrespective of severity of preterm birth. Not alike low GWG, the positive impact of excess GWG was similar across different degree of preterm birth, and the reduced risk of preterm birth was kept between 53% and 64%. However, a study found that excess GWG just was related to very preterm and moderate preterm birth but not to late preterm birth (Eick et al. 2020). And another study from Chinese indicated significantly higher risk of excess GWG on preterm birth (Zhang et al. 2016). These results implied that the association between excess GWG and preterm birth were unstable and could be easily modified by potential covariates as pre‐pregnancy weight (Huang et al. 2016; Hu et al. 2020; Faucher et al. 2016; LifeCycle Project‐Maternal Obesity and Childhood Outcomes Study Group et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2016). Moreover, although excess GWG was found associated with reduced risk of preterm birth, it should be interpreted as appropriate increase of GWG against preterm birth in maternal and child care practice, because excess GWG could bring about some adverse effects on perinatal and neonatal outcomes (Santos et al. 2019).

Modifiers for Link Between GWG and Preterm Birth

Although the subgroup analyses indicated a robust association of preterm birth and low GWG, some maternal characteristics, especially maternal age and pre‐pregnancy BMI, could modify such an association. Younger mothers could have a higher risk of preterm birth due to low GWG and older mothers followed. The studies found that adolescent pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of adverse birth outcomes (Chen et al. 2007). Younger mothers could be more likely to be intrinsic to maternal youth and have immaturity of the uterine or cervical blood supply in the first pregnancy (Erfina et al. 2019), and they also could lack enough good prenatal care due to low social economic status (Crequit et al. 2023). In the present study, half of the mothers with preterm birth owned an education level of senior school or less and more than 70% of mothers with preterm birth have lower family annual income (below 100 thousand Yuan). These results implied that premature pregnancy was not recommended. An interesting finding was that paternal age seemed to confound similarly the association of interest, which partly could be related to the effect of maternal age. However, potential paternal mechanisms might be involved and worthy of being explored in depth (Meng and Groth 2018). In the present study, we also observed a modification of pre‐pregnancy BMI on the association between low GWG and preterm birth and such a modifying impact just happened among underweight or normal weight mothers but diminished in the mothers with overweight or obesity, which was similar to results from the previous study (Viswanathan et al. 2008). Social‐demographical factors did not largely modify the association between excess GWG and preterm birth. It is worth noting that the protective impact of excess GWG on preterm birth just occurred among the mothers with normal pre‐pregnancy weight. Consequently, understanding the disparities in the impact of GWG on preterm birth among women with different characteristics could help to identify specific pathways for precise prevention against preterm birth in the practice of maternal nursing practice.

Potential Mechanism Linking GWG and Preterm Birth

Some potential reasons could account for the link between GWG and preterm birth although underlying mechanisms remained unclear. Low GWG could influence some pathogenic statuses of mothers such as anaemia and preeclampsia which increased the risk of adverse birth outcomes including preterm birth (Hutcheon and Bodnar 2018; Liu et al. 2022; LifeCycle Project‐Maternal Obesity and Childhood Outcomes Study Group et al. 2019). Moreover, some pathogenic mechanisms could be involved in the occurrence of preterm birth including stimulated cortisol, intrauterine vascular lesions, systemic inflammation, increased oxidative stress, increased susceptibility to uterine contractions and premature rupture of membranes (Hutcheon and Bodnar 2018; Goya et al. 2012; Wadhwa et al. 2004; Zhang et al. 2016). However, more importantly, weight gain during pregnancy may be reflective of maternal nutritional status and low GWG could deduce macronutrient and micronutrient deficiency, which was associated with reduced fetal growth (Han et al. 2011). This causal pathway could be significant from the perspective of prenatal care practice. Maternal weight management through balanced nutrition and necessary nutritional interventions could help pregnant women keep an appropriate increase of maternal GWG, which could prevent or reduce the risk of preterm birth of offspring.

Strengths and Limitations

An important strength of this study was that a series of potential confounders including smoking, alcohol drinking, abused or serious diseases such as anaemia and diabetes were excluded when selecting the participants in the present study, which made the present link less biased between GWG and preterm birth. This present study included premature newborns with a wide range of small gestational ages from very preterm to late preterm, which provided us the opportunity to investigate the impact of GWG on different degrees of preterm birth, especially very preterm birth. Moreover, the present study was hospital‐based and the key information on maternal weight and gestational age was collected accurately. The ways to estimate GWG varied by study. Most studies used the absolute difference between pre‐pregnancy weight and maternal weight at delivery but others used standardised GWG based on a 40‐week gestational duration (Eick et al. 2020). As a consequence, the effect size of the association of GWG with preterm birth could be affected. Considering the varying impact of different methods for GWG, besides absolute GWG, two additional indicators reflecting GWG were established to adjust for gestational week in the present study. GWG per week represented the rate of gestational weight gain across pregnancy and the other indicator was that GWG was adjusted for gestational age based on the residual error method. The results based on such three estimations of GWG were consistent. However, some limitations have to be acknowledged. Firstly, this was an observational study where a real causal relationship could not be presented completely due to potential confounders. Secondly, although a lot of confounders were controlled by statistical models, there were still some unobserved or unknown confounders that could not be rooted out. Thirdly, the information on maternal dietary patterns and physical activities was not collected in the survey, but these two factors could impact maternal weight gain or birth outcomes (Yan et al. 2020; Teede et al. 2022). Fourthly, the present study did not differentiate between spontaneous and medically indicated PTB and low GWG might contribute to PTB through separate pathways (Enomoto et al. 2016). Fifthly, the participants in the present study came from a single province in China, so the results may not be generalizable to other study populations directly.

Conclusion and Implications for Practice and Research

There is a significant association found between GWG and the risk of preterm birth in the Chinese population. Low GWG could increase the risk of preterm birth, especially very preterm birth but excess GWG could reduce the risk of preterm birth. An appropriate increase in GWG is recommended against preterm birth. Some maternal characteristics could modify the association of interest such as parents' age and pre‐pregnant BMI. The findings of this study underscore the critical role of nurses in maternal and child healthcare, particularly in enhancing health education for pregnant women. Special emphasis should be placed on assisting them in effectively managing their weight and dietary nutrition throughout pregnancy. It is imperative for nurses to comprehensively understand the varying impacts of maternal GWG on preterm birth among women with diverse characteristics, thereby facilitating the provision of individualised maternal care. Additionally, future research could explore the micro‐mechanisms through which GWG influences the severity of preterm birth, as well as paternal factors associated with adverse birth outcomes, including preterm birth.
