Prognostic Value of Patient‐Reported Outcomes in Predicting Long‐Term Mortality After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Hasan S. Alarouri, Ahmed El Shaer, Alejandra Chavez Ponce, Abdulah Mahayni, Samian Sulaiman, Sahar Samimi, Mohamad Alkhouli
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient‐reported outcome measures have been shown to have important prognostic value after various cardiac interventions. We assessed the association between the change in Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire 12 (KCCQ‐12) score after transcatheter aortic valve replacement and mortality.Methods and ResultsWe included patients who underwent transcatheter aortic valve replacement at Mayo Clinic between February 2012 to June 2022 and who completed a KCCQ‐12 before and 30 to 45 days after the procedure. Patients were categorized into 3 groups: those who experienced significant (>+19 points; group 1), modest (1–19 points; group 2), and no (≤0 points; group 3) improvement. A total of 1124 patients were included: 60.8% men; 97.6% White. Mean age was 79.4±8.3 years, baseline KCCQ‐12 score was 53.9±24.5, and median Society of Thoracic Surgeons score was 4.9% (interquartile range, 3.1–8.0). At 45 days, the mean change in KCCQ‐12 score was 19±24 points; 46.3% (n=520) of patients had a significant improvement in their KCCQ‐12 score, while 33.4% (n=375) and 20.4% (n=229) had modest and no improvement, respectively. Median survival was higher in group 1 (5.7±0.2 years) compared with groups 2 and 3 (5.1±0.3 and 4.1±0.4 years, respectively; P<0.001). Compared with patients in group 1, those in groups 2 and 3 had higher long‐term risk‐adjusted mortality (adjusted hazard ratios, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.20–1.96], and 2.30 [95% CI, 1.74–3.04], respectively).ConclusionsPatients who experience modest or no improvement in KCCQ‐12 score after transcatheter aortic valve replacement have substantially higher long‐term mortality. Delta KCCQ‐12 is a cost‐effective, efficient tool that can identify patients at increased risk of death at long‐term follow‐up post‐transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has revolutionized the management of severe aortic stenosis (AS) worldwide. With the continued demonstration of excellent outcomes, the indication for TAVR has expanded and the annual procedural volumes have grown exponentially. However, a considerable proportion of patients undergoing TAVR are either dead or experience no symptomatic improvement at 1 year, suggesting the need for additional strategies to identify and mitigate residual risks. 1

The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) is a commonly utilized instrument that assesses the quality of life in patients. Originally designed to be used in patients with heart failure (HF), the KCCQ has been validated as a prognostic tool in a variety of procedural settings. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The KCCQ was later abbreviated and extensively validated to allow its scalability and wide usage. 6 The modified version, the KCCQ‐12, has been adopted and validated to assess patients' quality of life before and after the treatment of various cardiac conditions, including AS. 7 , 8 , 9 Recently, the KCCQ‐12 demonstrated prognostic capabilities in predicting 1‐year mortality and HF hospitalization among patients undergoing TAVR. 10 , 11 However, to our knowledge, there are limited data on the association between delta KCCQ‐12 after TAVR and long‐term survival. Hence, we explored this association by leveraging prospectively collected data from a large tertiary valve center.

METHODS

Data Availability

As the study used data of a sensitive nature, any requests to access this data must be from qualified researchers who have received adequate training about human subject confidentiality protocols. Requests may be submitted to the corresponding author.

Study Population

We used the Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology's Transcatheter Valve Therapy (STS/ACC TVT) registry of patients who underwent TAVR at Mayo Clinic Hospital (Rochester, Minnesota) from February 8, 2012 to June 30, 2022. Eligibility for TAVR was determined by a heart team consisting of an interventional cardiologist and cardiothoracic surgeon and primarily guided by the US Food and Drug Association and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association's eligibility criteria. 12 , 13 We included patients who underwent the procedure and who had both a preprocedural and a postprocedural KCCQ‐12 administered (n=1124). The postprocedural KCCQ‐12 was administered 30 to 45 days after the procedure, which we defined as the early follow‐up visit. Patients whose vital status was censored or who had died within 45 days of the procedure (n=63) and those who did not have both questionnaires administered (n=1629) were excluded from our study. A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients included in our study and those who were excluded because of unavailable KCCQ‐12 data is summarized in Table S1. A dictionary of terms is provided in Data S1.

Study End Points and Design

The primary end point of our study was the association between delta KCCQ‐12 score and long‐term mortality in patients with TAVR. Secondary outcomes included: (1) association of baseline KCCQ‐12 score and long‐term mortality and the difference in median survival time between groups stratified based on the degree of change in the KCCQ‐12 score; (2) association of delta KCCQ‐12 score and long‐term mortality in valve‐in‐valve (ViV) TAVR versus native valve TAVR and in transfemoral access TAVR versus alternative access TAVR. We defined a significant improvement in the KCCQ‐12 score from baseline as one greater than the mean change in score at the early follow‐up visit (+19 points). Subsequently, we categorized patients into 3 groups; those who experienced a significant improvement (>+19 points; group 1), modest improvement (1–19 points; group 2), and no improvement (≤0 points; group 3). This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board, and a requirement for patient consent was waived as it was considered a minimal‐risk, retrospective research study.

Survey Instrument (KCCQ‐12)

The KCCQ‐12 is a health‐status assessment tool that consists of 12 items enclosed within the following 4 domains: symptoms, functional status, quality of life, and social limitations (Figure S1). 6 It produces a scaled score ranging from 0 (denoting the worst possible score) to 100 (denoting the best possible score) points.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages, while continuous variables were reported as either mean values with the SD or median values with interquartile ranges for skewed distributions. We compared procedural, in‐hospital, and early follow‐up outcomes across the 3 groups using either a Pearson Chi‐square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, and a 1‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. Median follow‐up time was computed from the date of the procedure to the date of death or censoring. Long‐term survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using a Cox‐proportional hazards regression model and were depicted with their 95% CI. The overall model assessed the association between delta KCCQ‐12 and long‐term mortality after adjusting for potential confounders and group differences in baseline characteristics with a P value <0.05; thus, the following covariables were adjusted for: age >65 years, atrial fibrillation or flutter, baseline aortic valve (AV) mean gradient, baseline left ventricular ejection fraction, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, baseline KCCQ‐12 score, body mass index (BMI), chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, hemodialysis, hypertension, immunocompromised status, postprocedural pacemaker requirement, primary TAVR indication, prior myocardial infarction, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack, sex, smoking status, Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk score, ViV procedure, and valve sheath access site. For the overall model, we stratified the baseline KCCQ‐12 score into tertiles as follows: lowest tertile: a score of <41; middle tertile: a score of 41 to 66; highest tertile (reference group): a score of >66. For the subgroup analysis, we adjusted for the same variables included in the overall model. We verified that the assumptions of the Cox‐proportional hazards regression model were met by plotting and inspecting the Schoenfeld residuals, Martingale residuals, and deviance residuals. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistics Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; version 28.0.0.0), R Statistical Software (version 4.1.2), and GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1).

RESULTS

Study Characteristics

Our study examined 1124 patients (39.2% women; 97.6% White) who underwent TAVR between February 8, 2012 to June 30, 2022. At the time of the procedure, the overall mean (±SD) age was 79.4±8.3 years, KCCQ‐12 score was 53.9±24.5 points, AV mean gradient was 42.7±13.2 mm Hg, and AV peak velocity was 4.1±0.7 m/s (Table 1). The median STS risk score was 4.9% (interquartile range, 3.1–8.0), and 67.0% of patients had an NYHA class of III or IV.

At the early follow‐up visit, 46.3% (n=520) of patients had a significant improvement in their KCCQ‐12 score, while 33.4% (n=375) and 20.4% (n=229) had modest and no improvement, respectively. The majority of the baseline characteristics were similar across the 3 groups; however, patients in group 1 had a higher median BMI (29.7; interquartile range, 25.7–34.1), a lower median left ventricular ejection fraction (61.0%; interquartile range, 50.0–65.0), and were more likely to have had a prior cardiac surgery (33.3%), a prior AV procedure (14.8%), and an NYHA class of III or IV (74.8%). Additionally, patients in group 1 had a significantly lower mean KCCQ‐12 score at baseline (40.7±18.7 points) compared with groups 2 and 3 (62.3±23.1 and 69.2±22.3 points, respectively). Patients in group 3 were significantly more likely to have AS as the primary indication for undergoing TAVR (95.6%) compared with patients in the other 2 groups.

Procedural and In‐Hospital Data

Overall implant success was 98.9% and was not significantly different between the cohorts (Table 2). Patients in group 3 had a significantly higher pacemaker requirement after the procedure relative to groups 1 and 2 (27.1 versus 17.5 versus 17.3, respectively; P=0.004). Patients in group 1 were less likely to undergo TAVR electively (86.2%) and require cardiopulmonary bypass during the procedure (0.2%) but were more likely to have undergone a ViV procedure (13.5%). The remaining procedural and in‐hospital characteristics were comparable among the groups.

Early Follow‐Up Visit

At the 45‐day follow‐up visit, the overall mean (±SD) KCCQ‐12 score was 72.8±22.3 points, with a mean increase of 19±24.0 points from baseline (Table 3). The mean KCCQ‐12 score was significantly different between the 3 cohorts after TAVR; patients in group 1 had a mean score of 80.2±15.5, while patients in groups 2 and 3 had a mean score of 72.4±22.6 and 58.3±10.8, respectively (P<0.001). The prevalence of NYHA classes III and IV decreased from 67% to 5.1% after TAVR overall, with patients in group 1 having the lowest prevalence compared with groups 2 and 3 (1.5% versus 5.1% versus 13.1, respectively; P<0.001). However, patients in group 1 had a significantly higher AV mean gradient (12.6±5.5 mm Hg) and a lower reduction in their AV mean gradient after TAVR (−29.4±14.0), relative to the other 2 groups.

Long‐Term Survival and the Prognostic Value of the KCCQ‐12 Score in TAVR

Over a median follow‐up of 3.0 years, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed an overall survival time of 5.4 years (95% CI, 5.0–5.7) for the entire study population. A significantly higher median survival time of 5.7 years (95% CI, 5.2–6.2) was observed for patients in group 1 compared with 5.1 years (95% CI, 4.5–5.8) and 4.1 years (95% CI, 3.4–4.9) for patients in groups 2 and 3, respectively (P by log‐rank <0.001) as shown in Figure 1A. After adjusting for multiple potential confounders, a Cox proportional hazards analysis showed an association between modest improvement in KCCQ‐12 and all‐cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio (HR), 1.54 [95% CI, 1.20–1.96]) or no improvement (adjusted HR, 2.30 [95% CI, 1.74–3.04]; Figures 1B and 2). Additionally, a baseline KCCQ‐12 score in the lowest (ie, <41) or middle (ie, 41–66) tertile was significantly correlated with long‐term mortality when compared with a baseline score in the highest tertile (ie, >66; adjusted HRs, 2.24 [95% CI, 1.65–3.03], and 1.48 [95% CI, 1.12–1.97], respectively). Other independent predictors of long‐term mortality included preprocedural atrial fibrillation or flutter, age, immunocompromised status, hemodialysis, chronic pulmonary disease, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, STS risk score, BMI, and valve sheath access site (Figure 2).

Long‐Term Survival and KCCQ‐12 in Valve‐in‐Valve Versus Native Valve TAVR

A ViV procedure (9.9%) was not associated with higher long‐term mortality even after adjustment (Figure 2). In a subgroup multivariable Cox regression analysis assessing the prognostic value of KCCQ‐12 in those who underwent a ViV procedure, a baseline KCCQ‐12 score in the middle tertile was associated with a higher long‐term mortality risk compared with one in the highest tertile (adjusted HR, 9.80 [95% CI, 1.55–61.80]; Tables S2 and S3). However, a baseline KCCQ‐12 score in the lowest tertile was not associated with a significant increase in long‐term mortality risk in this cohort. While no improvement in the KCCQ‐12 score after the procedure was associated with a higher mortality risk (adjusted HR, 5.88 [95% CI, 1.03–33.68]), a modest improvement was not associated with a similar increase in mortality risk in those who underwent a ViV procedure. Contrastingly, in those who underwent a native valve TAVR, the baseline KCCQ‐12 score was significantly correlated with long‐term mortality risk, in both the lowest and middle tertiles (adjusted HRs, 2.23 [95% CI, 1.63–3.06], and 1.43 [95% CI, 1.08–1.92], respectively). In addition, those who underwent a native valve TAVR and had modest or no improvement in their KCCQ‐12 score at the early follow‐up visit had a higher long‐term mortality risk (adjusted HRs, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.13–1.86], and 2.27 [95% CI, 1.70–3.02], respectively).

Long‐Term Survival and KCCQ‐12 in Transfemoral Versus Alternative Access Site TAVR

A total of 90.5% of the overall cohort had a transfemoral access site. After adjusting for potential confounders, alternative access sites, transapical or other (ie, access sites other than transfemoral or transapical), were associated with significantly higher long‐term mortality risk relative to a transfemoral access approach (adjusted HRs, 1.57 [95% CI, 1.16–2.13], and 2.11 [95% CI, 1.18–3.77], respectively; Figure 2). In a subgroup Cox regression analysis comparing the prognostic value of KCCQ‐12 in transfemoral versus alternative access site TAVR, the baseline KCCQ‐12 score was associated with long‐term mortality risk only in those who had a transfemoral access site (adjusted HRs for lowest and middle tertiles, 2.30 [95% CI, 1.66–3.19], and 1.46 [95% CI, 1.07–1.99], respectively; Table S2). Furthermore, a modest improvement in KCCQ‐12 score at the early follow‐up visit was only associated with a higher long‐term mortality risk in those who had a transfemoral TAVR approach (adjusted HR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.09–1.85]), while no improvement was associated with a higher long‐term risk in both the transfemoral and alternative access site cohorts (adjusted HRs, 2.11 [95% CI, 1.54–2.88], and 3.67 [95% CI, 1.63–8.24], respectively; Table S3).

DISCUSSION

Our study documents 2 key findings: first, it demonstrates that changes in the KCCQ‐12 score after TAVR are a significant, independent prognostic factor for long‐term survival, with modest and no improvement in KCCQ‐12 score after the procedure being associated with a considerably higher long‐term mortality risk. Second, a lower baseline KCCQ‐12 score alone was also associated with higher long‐term mortality risk after TAVR. Those findings are deserving of further elucidation.

The ability of patient‐reported outcome measures to yield critical prognostic information has previously been validated for various medical conditions such as AS, HF, and cancer. 6 , 10 , 14 In patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction and HF with preserved ejection fraction, KCCQ has consistently demonstrated excellent prognostic qualities with regard to all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular death, and hospitalization. 3 , 4 , 5 , 15 The KCCQ has also been shown to be a reliable predictor of 1‐year resource use and costs in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction after a myocardial infarction. 16 When compared with the NYHA classification system, the 6‐minute walk test, and the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire, KCCQ has demonstrated superior sensitivity to clinically meaningful changes in patients with HF while outperforming both, the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire and NYHA class, in predicting outcomes related to mortality and hospitalization. 15 , 17

The utility of KCCQ in risk stratification of patients undergoing TAVR has been explored previously. In a study by Arnold et al (2013), patients with severe, symptomatic AS who were randomized to either medical management, surgical AV replacement, or TAVR had a quality of life assessment with the KCCQ tool before and after the intervention. 7 Overall, different domains of the KCCQ were found to be a valid measure of change in symptoms, functional status, and quality of life when compared with other instruments that captured similar information. The study also evaluated the association between baseline KCCQ scores and 1‐year mortality in patients who were randomized to medical therapy only. In those patients, a low baseline KCCQ was found to be strongly associated with an increased risk of mortality. In a subsequent study by Arnold et al, patients who had poor (25–49 points), or very poor (<25 points) health status had much higher mortality than those with good baseline health status (KCCQ score ≥75 points; adjusted HR, 2.00, and adjusted HR, 1.54, respectively). 11 However, these studies only included baseline KCCQ and did not examine the association of its changes after the procedure with clinical outcomes.

Recently, Hejjaji et al attempted to bridge this gap by examining the prognostic importance of the pre‐ and postprocedural KCCQ‐12 (assessed at 30 days), and the change in KCCQ‐12 (from baseline to 30 days post‐TAVR) in predicting 1‐year mortality and HF hospitalization after TAVR. 10 In the risk‐adjusted model, the change in KCCQ‐12 score at 30 days was significantly associated with 1‐year mortality risk, with a larger change in score being more strongly associated with lower mortality (adjusted HR [per 5 points], 0.93 [95% CI, 0.92–0.94]). However, these data were limited to a 1‐year follow‐up and hindered by the heterogeneity of the KCCQ/KCCQ‐12's performance and reporting across sites. Our study corroborates those findings with a more extended follow‐up to a median of 3 years. We also further demonstrate that despite the prognostic value of the baseline KCCQ‐12 in the overall population undergoing TAVR, this score did not correlate with long‐term mortality risk in those who underwent TAVR through an alternative access site. In addition, a baseline KCCQ‐12 score in the lowest tertile (ie, <41) was paradoxically not significantly correlated with long‐term mortality in those who underwent a ViV procedure. Although those who experienced no improvement in their KCCQ‐12 score after TAVR had a higher mortality risk regardless of the access site utilized, those who underwent TAVR through an alternative access site and experienced no improvement in their KCCQ‐12 score postprocedurally had almost 1.5‐fold the mortality risk of their counterparts who also experienced no improvement in their score but underwent TAVR through a transfemoral approach. These findings have some practical implications. They illustrate the utility of serial measurements of KCCQ‐12 in the management of patients with AS following TAVR. Patients with limited changes in KCCQ‐12 could benefit from a targeted more intensive follow‐up protocol to address their residual risk. The changes in KCCQ‐12 after TAVR can further be used to study the utility/futility and the cost‐effectiveness of TAVR and may be other interventions in the future.

Another interesting observation in our study was the inverse association between baseline BMI and long‐term survival in patients with TAVR, a finding that is in line with prior research documenting this phenomenon. 18 , 19 Our study further identifies patients with a baseline BMI <23 to be at a notably higher risk of long‐term mortality after TAVR, which suggests that this could be a reasonable marker of frailty in patients undergoing the procedure. A similar association was also observed in Winter et al's (2014) meta‐analysis of the general population aged >65 years. 20

Finally, the results of our study must be interpreted with caution considering its several limitations. First, this is a single‐center study that was performed at an academic, tertiary center; thus, despite its large sample size, generalizability may still be limited. Second, although interest and emphasis on patient‐reported outcome measures have risen through the years, their incorporation in practice remains challenging and inconsistent. Therefore, we were unable to include all patients who underwent TAVR during that time because of missing KCCQ‐12 data, which may also impact the generalizability of our results. Third, although we attempted to adjust for multiple potential confounders and baseline differences between the groups, we cannot exclude the possibility of residual confounding attributable to variables that were not captured by the ACC/STS TVT registry.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients with a lower baseline KCCQ‐12 score and those who experience modest or no improvement in their KCCQ‐12 score after TAVR have a substantially higher long‐term mortality risk. The KCCQ‐12 is a cost‐effective, efficient tool that can help identify patients at increased risk of death after TAVR, thereby assisting clinicians and patients in making more informed decisions pre‐ and postprocedurally.
