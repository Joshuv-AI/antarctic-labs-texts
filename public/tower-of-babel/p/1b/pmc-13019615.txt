Transcriptomic analysis of chronic social instability stress during adolescence reveals major shifts in the extracellular matrix
Authors: Ming Yin Chu, Luca A Hategan, Amanda M Leonetti, Fiona O Ramnaraign, Samuel Holm, Savithru Kannuri, Timothy AB McLean, Laura Neira-Diaz, Meghana C Malagi, Cheryl M McCormick, Brandon J Walters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescence represents a sensitive window of neural plasticity, when social experiences exert enduring influences on emotional and cognitive development. Adverse social conditions, such as social instability stress (SIS), disrupt peer relationships and lead to long-term changes in social and affective behaviour. Here, we used RNA sequencing to characterize the immediate (P46) and enduring (P70) molecular consequences of adolescent SIS in male and female rats in two stress-sensitive regions: the dorsal hippocampus (dHPC) and basolateral amygdala (BLA). SIS produced region- and sex-specific transcriptional profiles. In males, SIS upregulated extracellular matrix (ECM) and perineuronal net (PNN)-related genes in the hippocampus, whereas females exhibited broad suppression of ECM-related genes in the amygdala. The opposing ECM trajectories between hippocampus and amygdala suggest that SIS drives premature structural stabilization in the male hippocampus and maintains heightened plasticity in the female amygdala. In both sexes, synaptic signaling pathways were consistently downregulated, whereas mitochondrial and metabolic programs were enhanced. Additionally, we observed sex and region-specific changes in neuroendocrine and hormonal gene expression, including oxytocin (Oxt), vasopressin (Avp), and testosterone signaling components. These transcriptional programs were largely distinct from normative age-dependent gene expression changes between adolescence and adulthood. Together, these findings identify adolescent SIS as a potent regulator of ECM and synaptic gene networks, revealing molecular pathways through which social stress may sculpt circuit maturation in a sex- and region-specific manner.

Introduction

Many neural circuits mature through temporally defined windows of heightened plasticity during which environmental experiences exert a long-term influence on neural connectivity and behavior. The perinatal period is a well-characterized critical period for shaping sensory systems (Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020); nevertheless, later-developing emotional and cognitive networks also experience phases of experience-dependent reorganization during adolescence (Blakemore et al., 2014; Graf et al., 2023; McCormick et al., 2010; Morrissey et al., 2011; Patel et al., 2024; Simone et al., 2022). In rodents, this sensitive period of adolescence spans approximately postnatal days (P) 28–59, with the onset of puberty as determined by physical markers occurring at ∼ P33 in females and ∼P40 in males. The shift from a non-reproductive to a reproductive state involves large-scale restructuring of the neural systems underlying social, affective, and cognitive behavior, such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex (Graf et al., 2023; McCormick et al., 2010; Morrissey et al., 2011; Patel et al., 2024; Mathews et al., 2012). Perturbations such as reduced social experience or exposure to chronic stressors during this period exert enduring effects on stress reactivity, cognition, and social behavior, reflecting the vulnerability of circuits that are still undergoing maturation.

Transitions into and out of sensitive periods are governed in part by remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM), a dense meshwork of collagens, laminins, and proteoglycans that surround neurons and constrain dendritic remodeling. Core structural components include fibrillar collagens (e.g., Col1a1, Col3a1, Col8a1) and glycoproteins such as tenascin-C (Tnc), which support matrix assembly and cell–matrix interactions. A specialized ECM structure, the perineuronal net (PNN), encapsulates fast-spiking parvalbumin interneurons and progressively consolidates across adolescence, stabilizing inhibitory tone and restricting further plasticity (Guadagno et al., 2021; Hirono et al., 2021; Wen et al., 2018). Experimental enzymatic degradation of PNNs in adulthood reopens juvenile-like plasticity (Willis et al., 2022; Lensjo et al., 2017), whereas stressors and concomitant hormonal cues can accelerate PNN formation, prematurely curtailing plasticity (Rahimian et al., 2024; Laham et al., 2021). ECM composition and PNN maturation therefore act as molecular gatekeepers of developmental plasticity, linking social experience and endocrine state to circuit stabilization.

Social instability or isolation during adolescence profoundly alters emotional and cognitive outcomes in both humans and rodents (Graf et al., 2023; McCormick et al., 2010; Morrissey et al., 2011; Patel et al., 2024; Simone et al., 2022; Mathews et al., 2012). In rodents, social instability stress involves repeated disruption of social partners and housing conditions, reliably evoking long-term alterations in anxiety-like behavior, social motivation, reward processing, and memory. These behavioral phenotypes are accompanied by structural and molecular changes in the hippocampus and amygdala, regions that continue to refine connectivity and receptor composition through late adolescence (Graf et al., 2023; McCormick et al., 2010; Morrissey et al., 2011; Patel et al., 2024; Mathews et al., 2012). Mounting evidence suggests that adolescent stress perturbs dendritic arborization and synaptic signaling (Hyer et al., 2021; McCormick et al., 2017), yet the transcriptional architecture remains incompletely defined. However, it remains unclear to what extent stress-induced transcriptional changes during adolescence reflect changes to developmental processes versus stress-specific molecular programs.

Here, we used RNA sequencing to examine the immediate (P46) and enduring (P70) molecular consequences of adolescent social instability stress in both sexes across two stress-sensitive regions: the dorsal hippocampus (dHPC) and basolateral amygdala (BLA). These regions were chosen because of lasting effects of SIS on behaviors they support, such as spatial memory and fear conditioning (McCormick et al., 2010; Morrissey et al., 2011). Our results reveal persistent and opposing ECM gene expression between hippocampus and amygdala, and sex-dependent suppression of synaptic signaling In addition, specific changes were observed in gene expression related to testosterone, oxytocin, and vasopressin pathways, implicating ECM and endocrine remodeling as central targets affected by social instability stress to shape adolescent brain development.

Methods

Animals

Female (n = 16) and male (n = 16) Long-Evans rats were obtained from Charles River (St. Constant, QC) at P22 and housed in same-sex pairs on a 12 h light–dark cycle (lights on 08:00 h), with ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines and were approved by the Brock University Animal Care and Use Committee. Due to the known sex-specific effects of the SIS procedure on rats, sexes was treated separately for these studies.

Social Instability Stress (SIS)

Rats were randomly assigned to SIS or non-stressed control groups. Beginning at P30, SIS rats were individually isolated for 1 h daily in ventilated containers located in a separate room and were then rehoused with a new cage partner that also underwent isolation. This procedure was repeated for 15 days (P30–P45). To study enduring effects of adolescent social stress, half of the rats were returned to their original partner on P46 and left undisturbed until sacrifice at P70. Control animals were not subjected to daily isolation, partner reassignment, or cage changes during this period.

Dissections

Brains were rapidly removed and flash-frozen in isopentane cooled on dry ice. Brains were sectioned (200 μM) on a vibrotome (Leica VT-1000s). dHPC and BLA were identified in sections and microdissected away from surrounding tissue and flash-frozen.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from tissue using Total RNA spin kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturers instructions and sent to CD Genomics (Shirley, NY, USA) for sequencing. RNA was poly-A selected and sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq4000 with PE setting, at a read depth of 30 - 35 million reads per sample. Raw FASTQ files were quality controlled using FastQC (version 0.11.9).

Bioinformatics

All sequenced reads were aligned to RNor7.2 genome (Ensembl). Briefly, reads were aligned to the genome, which was indexed in R using the subread library. Reads were aligned using Rsubreadalign function in R (version 2.12.3, R version 4.2.3) with paired-end settings. Adapters were trimmed using soft-clipping in Rsubread. Aligned reads were counted using featureCounts in the Rsubread library (version 2.12.3). Quality control on the counts was performed using RNASeqQC in R (version 0.1.4). Genes with a total count below 20 across all samples were removed prior to analysis.

Differential expression analysis

Differential Expression analysis was conducted with edgeR in R (version 3.42.4) using the GLM LRT method, with normalization factors calculated to the upper-quartile. Statistical model followed ∼ Batch + Condition within each comparison (e.g., Male P46 Naive vs Male P46 Stressed). We utilized the RUVseq package (version 1.34.0) in R to normalize variance discovered through PCA to a set of unchanging control genes. We reran differential expression with the following model ∼ W1 + Batch + Condition, where W1 represented the normalization matrix computed by RUVseq. Volcano plots were generated using a custom ggplot2 function in R (version 3.4.3), and significant DEGs were determined by absolute |Log2FoldChange| ≥ 1.0 and FDR <0.05. Venn diagrams of DEGs were either made manually using Biovenn (Hulsen et al., 2008), or computed using ggplot2 in R. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the clusterProfiler (version 4.8.1) gseGO function. Briefly, total genes of each comparison were ranked in decreasing order in R, GSEA function used a p-value cutoff of 0.05, a minGSSize of 5 and a maxGSSize of 500. GSEA plots of ontologies were plotted using the dotplot function in clusterProfiler.

Results

Immediate (P46) hippocampal transcriptional response to SIS

Immediately following SIS exposure in late adolescence (P46), male rats exhibited significant transcriptional alterations in the dorsal hippocampus (dHPC), with 97 upregulated and 52 downregulated genes relative to unstressed controls (FDR <0.05 and |LogFC| > 1; Fig. 1B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; Fig. 1D) revealed strong enrichment of extracellular matrix (ECM) pathways among upregulated genes, including structural ECM components (Col6a1, Lum) and perineuronal net (PNN)-related genes (Col3a1, Dcn). These ECM-related ontologies dominated the positively enriched categories, suggesting enhanced extracellular remodeling within the male hippocampus.

(F) Venn diagrams comparing shared and unique DEGs regulated by SIS between sexes. N = 4 rats per condition.

In contrast, negatively enriched ontologies centered on synaptic processes, including the synaptic vesicle cycle and regulation of postsynaptic membrane potential. Downregulated gene sets included key synaptic components such as the AMPA receptor subunit Gria1, the postsynaptic potassium channel Kcnq3, and the presynaptic calcium sensor Syt2. Collectively, adolescent SIS in males was associated with increased expression of ECM/PNN-related genes alongside reduced expression of genes involved in synaptic signaling.

In females, SIS produced a markedly different transcriptional profile at P46. The number of up- and downregulated genes was more balanced, with 150 genes upregulated and 118 downregulated in the hippocampus (FDR <0.05 and |LogFC| > 1; Fig. 1C). GSEA (Fig. 1E) indicated that upregulated genes were enriched for oxidative phosphorylation and energy production pathways, including electron transport-coupled proton transport and mitochondrial respiration. In contrast, downregulated ontologies were primarily associated with cellular growth and developmental morphogenesis, such as wound healing, cell differentiation, and developmental processes.

Direct comparison of male and female hippocampal responses at P46 revealed largely distinct transcriptional patterns (Fig. 1F), with only 20 shared differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Table 3 has the full gene lists used in Venn Diagrams). Moreover, no major gene ontologies were commonly enriched across sexes. One notable shared gene was Igf2, a well-established regulator of neural plasticity, along with its binding partner Igf2bp.

Together, these findings demonstrate that adolescent SIS induces immediate but sex-specific transcriptional remodeling in the hippocampus. In males, SIS is characterized by upregulation of ECM-related genes and suppression of synaptic signaling genes. In contrast, females exhibit minimal ECM involvement and instead show transcriptional changes primarily related to energy metabolism and developmental processes.

The enduring (P70) hippocampal transcriptional response to SIS

In the male hippocampus at P70, SIS continued to produce substantial transcriptional alterations, with a persistent bias toward gene activation (189 upregulated and 77 downregulated transcripts; FDR <0.05 and |LogFC| > 1; Fig. 2B). GSEA identified ECM-related ontologies among the top positively enriched pathways (Fig. 2D), consistent with sustained extracellular matrix remodeling beyond adolescence. Upregulated genes included structural ECM components (e.g., Col6a1, Col3a1) and PNN-associated regulators (e.g., Tnr, Mmp14), whereas downregulated genes were enriched for steroid- and hormone-related pathways, including those associated with testosterone signaling.

In females at P70, SIS induced more modest bidirectional transcriptional changes (42 upregulated and 35 downregulated genes; Fig. 2C). Upregulated ontologies emphasized metabolic and biosynthetic processes, including aerobic respiration, NADH dehydrogenase complex assembly, and ribosomal biogenesis. In contrast, downregulated ontologies were centered on synaptic function, including synaptic vesicle exocytosis and regulation of postsynaptic membrane potential. Representative genes included Grin2a and Grin3a, which encode NMDA receptor subunits.

Together, these findings indicate that the enduring hippocampal effects of adolescent SIS are strongly sex-specific. Males exhibit sustained upregulation of ECM/PNN-related gene networks alongside repression of hormone-associated pathways, whereas females show continued enhancement of metabolic gene expression accompanied by suppression of synaptic signaling programs. Only a small number of DEGs were shared between sexes at P70 (Fig. 2F), further emphasizing the divergence in enduring transcriptional response to SIS.

The immediate (P46) amygdala transcriptional response to SIS

SIS produced a markedly different transcriptional profile in the male basolateral amygdala (BLA) relative to the hippocampus. At P46, 298 genes were downregulated and 113 upregulated (FDR <0.05 and |LogFC| > 1; Fig. 3B), indicating a predominance of gene repression. GSEA (Fig. 3D) revealed that upregulated genes were enriched for energy production pathways, including mitochondrial and ATP synthesis processes. In contrast, downregulated ontologies were dominated by ECM-related processes, with structural ECM genes such as Col8a1 and Col1a2 prominently represented. Thus, in the male BLA, ECM genes were suppressed opposite to the pattern observed in the hippocampus indicating region-specific ECM regulation immediately following SIS.

In females at P46, SIS produced roughly balanced transcriptional changes in the BLA (142 upregulated and 118 downregulated genes; Fig. 3C). Similar to males, GSEA identified enrichment of energy production pathways among upregulated genes (Fig. 3E). Downregulated ontologies again centered on ECM and connective tissue processes, including both core structural components (e.g., Col6a1, Sdc1) and regulatory elements (e.g., Tll2).

Overall, both sexes exhibited similar ontology-level responses in the amygdala at P46, characterized by enhanced metabolic gene expression and suppression of ECM-related pathways. However, despite this shared functional pattern, the specific differentially expressed genes were largely distinct between males and females, with limited overlap (Fig. 3F). Notably, none of the shared genes were canonical ECM regulators.

Enduring (P70) amygdala transcriptional response to SIS

In the male BLA at P70, transcriptional changes remained biased toward repression, with 177 downregulated and 85 upregulated genes (Fig. 4B). Unlike P46, ECM-related perturbations were no longer prominent. Instead, GSEA revealed enrichment of developmental and differentiation-related processes among upregulated genes (e.g., organ induction and cell fate specification). In contrast, downregulated ontologies emphasized synaptic and axonal pathways, including synaptic transmission and axonogenesis (Fig. 4D). Notably, repressed genes included immediate-early genes such as Fos and Npas4, suggesting persistent alterations in activity-dependent transcriptional programs.

In females, the enduring (P70) transcriptional response to SIS shifted toward greater activation than repression, with 299 upregulated and 117 downregulated genes (Fig. 4C). Upregulated ontologies were enriched for signaling and regulatory processes, including endocannabinoid signaling, response to rapamycin, and regulation of H3K27 methylation. In contrast, downregulated terms again centered on ECM-related processes (Fig. 4E), with structural components such as Col6a1 and Col3a1 prominently represented.

Together, these findings demonstrate that the enduring amygdala response to adolescent SIS diverges sharply between sexes. In males, the transcriptional profile shifts away from early ECM suppression toward repression of synaptic and activity-dependent genes alongside enrichment of developmental programs. In contrast, females exhibit sustained suppression of ECM-related pathways accompanied by robust activation of intracellular signaling and regulatory networks. Only 33 DEGs were shared between sexes at P70 (Fig. 4F), reinforcing the divergence in enduring amygdala responses. Thus, while both sexes show enduring molecular consequences of adolescent SIS within the amygdala, the direction and functional categories of these changes are fundamentally distinct, indicating that adolescent SIS produces sex-specific regulatory responses in the amygdala.

Age-dependent transcriptional changes in naive rats and overlap with SIS-regulated genes

To determine whether the limited overlap in SIS-regulated genes across P46 and P70 reflected developmental differences in baseline gene expression, we directly compared naive P46 and naive P70 animals within each sex and brain region (Fig. 5). In the male hippocampus, comparison of naive P46 and P70 rats revealed minimal age-dependent transcriptional change, with only 10 differentially expressed genes (2 downregulated and 8 upregulated at P46 relative to P70; FDR <0.05 and |LogFC| > 1; Fig. 5A). Similarly, females exhibited modest age-related changes in the hippocampus (8 downregulated and 26 upregulated genes; Fig. 5B), indicating that baseline transcriptional maturation across this interval is limited in this region.

To assess whether SIS-regulated genes overlapped with those altered by maturation, we performed three-way comparisons among DEGs induced by SIS at P46, SIS at P70, and age (naive P46 vs. naive P70; Fig. 5C and D). In males, overlap between age-dependent and SIS-regulated genes in the hippocampus was minimal, with only a small number of genes shared across conditions and no substantial intersection between age-related DEGs and those induced by SIS at either time point (Fig. 5C). A similar pattern was observed in females (Fig. 5D), indicating that developmental transcriptional shifts do not account for the largely distinct SIS signatures observed at P46 and P70.

In the male amygdala, naive P46 versus P70 comparison identified modest age-dependent transcriptional changes (18 downregulated and 27 upregulated genes; Fig. 5E). As in the hippocampus, overlap between age-related DEGs and SIS-regulated genes was limited (Fig. 5G), further supporting the conclusion that the molecular effects of SIS are largely independent of baseline developmental transcriptional changes.

In contrast, the female amygdala displayed a more pronounced age-dependent transcriptional signature. Comparison of naive P46 and P70 females revealed 189 downregulated and 46 upregulated genes (Fig. 5F), substantially exceeding age-related DEG counts observed in the other three conditions. Despite this robust developmental shift, three-way analysis demonstrated minimal overlap between age-regulated genes and SIS-induced genes at either P46 or P70 (Fig. 5H).

Together, these findings indicate that while age-dependent transcriptional changes occur between adolescence and early adulthood these changes remain largely distinct from SIS-induced gene expression programs. Thus, the temporally divergent transcriptional effects of SIS cannot be attributed solely to maturation.

Summary of shared gene patterns

Across both brain regions, transcriptional responses to SIS were strongly influenced by developmental stage, with few shared DEGs between P46 and P70 within each sex and region. Shared genes most frequently participated in energy homeostasis, ECM/PNN organization, or synaptic plasticity-related pathways (e.g., Igf2), whereas region- and sex-specific signatures exhibited opposing regulation of ECM-associated genes. Overall, ECM genes were upregulated in the male hippocampus and downregulated in the female amygdala, indicating that extracellular remodeling is differentially regulated across sex and brain region following adolescent stress exposure. These ECM shifts frequently coincided with suppression of synaptic ontologies, consistent with coordinated alterations in extracellular structure and synaptic signaling in response to SIS.

To determine whether the limited overlap in SIS-regulated genes across ages reflected baseline developmental transcriptional shifts, we directly compared naive P46 and P70 animals. Age-dependent gene expression changes were minimal in the hippocampus of both sexes and in the male amygdala. Although more substantial in the female amygdala, age-regulated genes showed little overlap with SIS-induced DEGs at either time point. Thus, the distinct transcriptional signatures observed at P46 and P70 following SIS cannot be attributed solely to changes in the typical maturation program. Instead, adolescent SIS appears to engage its own transcriptional program that modifies the brain region.

Discussion

Sex- and region-specific ECM remodeling

SIS induced divergent ECM programs across brain regions in a sex-dependent manner. In the hippocampus, males exhibited robust activation of ECM and perineuronal net (PNN) genes at both P46 and P70, including ECM modulators, structural fibrillar collagens, and key PNN glycoproteins. These changes co-occurred with downregulation of excitatory synaptic genes, consistent with coordinated ECM consolidation alongside synaptic suppression. In contrast, females showed minimal ECM-related transcriptional activation in the hippocampus, although synapse-related genes were similarly downregulated.

In the amygdala, a distinct pattern emerged. Males displayed transient repression of ECM-related genes at P46 that was no longer evident at P70, whereas females exhibited persistent repression of ECM-related genes at both ages. Collectively, these findings reveal region- and sex-specific ECM trajectories: males demonstrate early ECM/PNN reinforcement in the hippocampus, whereas females, particularly in the amygdala, show sustained stress-induced repression of ECM-related genes. These ECM shifts occur alongside repression of synaptic genes and activation of metabolic and anabolic pathways, suggesting that these molecular programs are differentially sensitive to adolescent stress across circuits and sexes.

Importantly, direct comparison of naive P46 and P70 animals demonstrated that baseline developmental transcriptional changes between adolescence and early adulthood were minimal in the hippocampus of both sexes and in the male amygdala. Although more substantial age-related changes were observed in the female amygdala, these genes showed little overlap with those regulated by SIS at either time point. Thus, the distinct transcriptional signatures observed at P46 and P70 following adolescent SIS cannot be attributed solely to changes to normal maturation. Instead, SIS induces temporally distinct and largely non-overlapping gene expression programs that persist beyond adolescence, reflecting a stress-specific transcriptomic response.

Notably, although some ontologies were shared between regions or sexes (e.g., ECM-related pathways in the amygdala), the specific genes contributing to those categories differed across sex and brain region. This indicates that apparent functional similarities arise from distinct underlying transcriptional programs. Indeed, adolescent SIS exposure induces sex- and age-specific effects on diverse behavioral outcomes (Graf et al., 2023; Leonetti et al., 2025), and the present findings suggest that these outcomes are associated with largely unique gene expression programs in stress-responsive brain regions.

ECM and PNN remodeling as a mechanism for sensitive-period closure

PNNs act as molecular brakes on plasticity that define the closure of sensitive periods (Gallo et al., 2025). The transcriptional upregulation of PNN components in the male hippocampus is consistent with patterns that may reflect accelerated structural stabilization, potentially truncating the natural window for experience-dependent remodeling. Conversely, suppression of ECM ontologies in the female amygdala implies delayed PNN consolidation, prolonging circuit malleability and potentially altering emotional valence processing. This bidirectional regulation suggests that SIS does not uniformly dampen plasticity but instead exerts region-specific modulation of plasticity across circuits and sexes.

The role of gene expression differences in mediating behavioral outcomes following SIS is not necessarily direct, as transcriptional changes may not translate into immediate proteomic or structural alterations. Nevertheless, several connections are compelling. For example, transcriptional changes observed in the hippocampus of SIS-exposed male rats align with reports of impaired hippocampal-dependent memory performance and altered neurogenesis during adolescence and adulthood (Morrissey et al., 2011; McCormick et al., 2012; Green et al., 2013). Similarly, repression of testosterone-related genes following SIS may contribute to the reduced plasma testosterone concentrations and impaired sexual performance observed in adult SIS males (Green et al., 2013).

Additionally, prior work has demonstrated that SIS reduces oxytocin-immunoreactive cell counts in the paraventricular nucleus, which projects to multiple regions including the amygdala (Hodges et al., 2017). This finding is consistent with the decreased oxytocin receptor expression observed here in the female amygdala. Although increased oxytocin receptor densities were reported in the PVN of SIS males during adolescence (Hodges et al., 2017), we did not detect changes in oxytocin receptor expression in either the hippocampus or amygdala of male rats.

In adulthood, male and female rats exhibit divergent behavioral outcomes following SIS, including enhanced cue fear conditioning and decreased anxiety-like behavior in females (reviewed in (Hodges et al., 2019)). Whereas SIS males show no reduction in social motivation during adolescence or adulthood, SIS females display reduced social motivation in adulthood but not adolescence relative to controls (Leonetti et al., 2025). These sex-specific behavioral trajectories suggest differences in valence processing and are consistent with the persistent repression of ECM/PNN-related pathways observed selectively in the female amygdala, potentially contributing to these outcomes.

We also observed pronounced alterations in endocannabinoid signaling in the female amygdala. This finding aligns with recent unpublished work from the McCormick laboratory demonstrating that treatment with a cannabinoid type 1 receptor (CB1R) antagonist during SIS exposure alleviates deficits in adult social motivation in SIS females (PAPER IN PREP et al.; POSTER et al., 2025). Given that the endocannabinoid system undergoes extensive maturation during adolescence (Simone et al., 2022), these results suggest that endocannabinoid signaling contributes to the sex-specific effects of adolescent stress.

Previous studies have identified transcriptomic alterations in the amygdala and hippocampus associated with anxiety-like behavior following single-prolonged stress in adult male rats (Long et al., 2025). Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that even a relatively mild, chronic social stressor during adolescence induces immediate, persistent, and sex-specific transcriptional reprogramming. The present data suggest that ECM remodeling may represent a key molecular substrate underlying persistent behavioral consequences of adolescent SIS and highlight the ECM as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating stress-related alterations during development.

Conclusion

Adolescent social instability stress is associated with region- and sex-specific transcriptional changes across ECM/PNN, synaptic, and hormonal gene networks that may influence trajectories of neural development through region- and sex-specific coupling of ECM/PNN, synaptic, and hormonal gene networks. Rather than uniformly suppressing or extending plasticity, SIS appears to remodel ECM/PNNs in a region- and sex-specific manner, potentially stabilizing hippocampal circuits in males, while maintaining flexibility in female amygdala circuits. These findings identify ECM remodeling as a key mediator linking social experience during adolescence to long-term circuit architecture.
