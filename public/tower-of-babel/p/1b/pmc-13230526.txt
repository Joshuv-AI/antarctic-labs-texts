Human plasma extracellular vesicles as an exercise mimetic to preserve skeletal muscle plasticity during disuse
Authors: Alexander M Fliflet, Yanqi Tan, Takeshi M Barnes, Ane Nishitha Vijayan, Sung Jun Choi, Max T Deutz, Zan Zupancic, Ray A Spradlin, Benjamin F Miller, Nicholas A Burd, Jonathan V Sweedler, Marni D Boppart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study was to determine the extent to which extracellular vesicles (EVs) circulating in blood after exercise training act as an effective mimetic to maintain skeletal muscle mass during unloading and/or accelerate recovery after disuse. Ten healthy males (27.7 ± 7.1 y) were recruited for a 6-week progressive resistance and endurance training program. EVs were isolated from blood before (EVs) or immediately after training (ExerVs). EVs were intraperitoneally injected into male mice (4×; 3 × 108 particles/injection) during 14 days of hindlimb unloading (HU), then the muscles were collected immediately or 7 days after HU. ExerVs did not maintain muscle mass, fiber size (fCSA), or protein synthesis but significantly reduced collagen I during HU. ExerV administration rapidly restored Type I fCSA and capillary quantity concomitant with reduced collagen during the reloading period. Overall, this study demonstrates that ExerVs may represent a novel strategy to preserve skeletal muscle health during disuse.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cell biology, Physiology

Introduction

Spaceflight exposes astronauts to environmental hazards, such as radiation, microgravity, and isolation. Collectively, these stressors can induce DNA damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and cellular senescence, ultimately leading to degeneration of multiple tissues and physiological systems1. Decreased exposure to gravitational forces alone can induce rapid and profound reductions in skeletal muscle mass, with 30–40% decreases in fiber size observed in the soleus and vastus lateralis muscles of rats and humans within a few days of unloading2–5. Currently, there are no effective pharmacological therapies to completely prevent the loss of muscle mass or accelerate the recovery of muscle following microgravity-induced mechanical unloading. Therefore, the potential for long-term space travel and exploration is dependent on the discovery of innovative approaches to preserving tissue structure and function.

Exercise is commonly used as a countermeasure to attenuate microgravity-induced muscle atrophy. The development and advancement of in-flight exercise equipment, such as the Interim Resistance Exercise Device (iRED) and Advanced Resistance Exercise Device (ARED), are effective in reducing muscle loss and strength during short periods of spaceflight6. However, exercise cannot completely prevent these declines, especially in the lower limbs in both males and females during extended periods of space travel7,8. In addition, the inclusion of large exercise equipment on the new Orion capsule may be limited due to space constraints. Thus, complementary or alternative approaches are necessary to optimize skeletal muscle health in space.

Exercise can positively impact multiple tissues and organ systems9. Although the benefits of exercise to skeletal muscle mass and function are well studied, the mechanisms responsible for tissue crosstalk and integrative health remain unclear. Recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have been identified to serve a significant role in cell-to-cell communication10. EVs are nanosized, membrane-bound vesicles secreted by nearly all cells in response to physiological stressors, such as mechanical stress, hypoxia, and ROS10,11. EVs encapsulate cytoplasmic molecular cargo prior to release, including proteins, non-coding RNAs, and lipids, which are then distributed to nearby and distal cells. Recent studies indicate that an acute bout of high-intensity exercise is associated with a rapid and transient increase in EVs in the circulation (ExerVs)12–19. Additionally, EVs isolated from human blood after single or multiple bouts of exercise are enriched in exerkines that may promote whole body health13,15–17,19,20. Despite these intriguing findings, minimal information exists regarding ExerVs collected after multiple bouts of exercise (training), including the extent to which these EVs can preserve tissue structure and function during periods of disuse.

In this study, we investigated the extent to which systemically administered ExerVs derived from human participants after 6 weeks of exercise training maintain and recover skeletal muscle during and after hindlimb unloading in mice. We hypothesized that ExerVs would possess proteins that would provide protection against tissue loss during unloading. Our results indicate that ExerVs are enriched in metabolite interconversion enzymes, specifically oxidoreductases. Repeated, systemic administration of ExerVs did not prevent skeletal muscle atrophy during disuse but inhibited collagen I accumulation during this critical phase. In addition, ExerVs injected during disuse accelerated the ability for Type I oxidative fibers, which are most susceptible to atrophy21, to rapidly recover size during the reloading period. The results from this study represent an important first step in developing a complementary or alternative approach to preserve and rebuild mass during an extended period of disuse due to microgravity.

Methods

Participant recruitment and ethical approval

Ten healthy, physically active males (19- to 45-year-old) from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and the surrounding area volunteered to participate in this study. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. Baseline physical activity was assessed using the Godin-Shephard Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire (GSLTPAQ) and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Only participants scoring >14 (Godin-Shepherd) and >1500 (IPAQ) were considered for participation in the study. All participants were deemed healthy based on their responses to a health and medical screening questionnaire and were weight stable for at least the preceding 6 months prior to enrollment in the study. Prior to the 6-week exercise training program, participants underwent pre-intervention testing, including anthropometric measures (BMI, DEXA), strength training (hand grip, 10-repetition max load), and endurance capacity (VO2peak). All participants were informed about experimental procedures, the purpose of the study, and potential risks before giving written consent. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (IRB #24220). All procedures involving human subjects conformed to standards for the use of human participants in research, with the exception of pre-registration on a clinical trial registry, as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and exercise protocol

A schematic overview of the experimental design is depicted in Fig. 1A. Each participant completed a 6-week progressive, multicomponent exercise training program consisting of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic and strength-based exercises. Aerobic-based training was performed on a motorized treadmill, and intensities were determined by age-predicted heart rate max (HRmax = 220 beats per minute-age) and Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) using Borg’s original scale (6–20 points). The aerobic exercise training progression program is depicted in Table 2. Strength-based training was performed using guided-motion resistance exercise machines. Each training session consisted of 2 upper-body and 2 lower-body exercises with alternating arm pushing (chest press and shoulder press), arm pulling (seated pulldowns and seated row), and leg exercises (leg press, leg extension, and leg curl). Briefly, training consisted of a warm-up set of 10 repetitions at 50% 10-repetition max load (10RM), followed by training sets. Each exercise was performed at two sets of 10 repetitions (80% of 10RM) and a third set of as many repetitions as possible, with 2 min of rest between each set. A detailed training progression is depicted in Table 3. Training intensity was adjusted based on the individual’s training performance. During the intervention, the participants completed three 60–90-min training sessions per week for a total of 18 sessions. VO2peak was assessed before and after the 6-week training program via an incremental modified Bruce treadmill graded exercise test. The test was performed overnight fasted following a 72-h minimum rest period. Validity of the VO2peak was confirmed when meeting 3 of the 4 following criteria: (1) oxygen consumption reaches a plateau despite increases in workload; (2) respiratory exchange ratio ≥ 1.15; (3) heart rate within 10 beats of age-predicted maximum; and (4) a RPE of ≥ 18. Oxygen consumption was measured via indirect calorimetry (TrueOne, Parvo Medics, Sandy, UT), and heart rate was measured via a Polar heart rate monitor (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). Expired gases were recorded and analyzed breath by breath. Heart rate was recorded every minute during the test. Current diet intake was assessed prior to week 1, and during weeks 3 and 6 of the exercise intervention. Participants recorded 3 days/week in an online automated self-administered (ASA24) diet record system (9 total recorded days). To control for dietary variability, participants consumed a standardized meal providing ~30% of estimated caloric needs with 25% kcal from protein, 50% from carbohydrate, and 25% from fat. The standardized meal was consumed the evening before the first and final days of the 6-week exercise intervention. Within 5–7 days following completion of the exercise intervention, participants underwent follow-up testing mirroring tests of the pre-intervention.

Blood draw and plasma EV isolation

Blood was collected after an overnight fast at four different timepoints throughout the study: (1) before first exercise training session, (2) immediately after first exercise training session, (3) before final exercise training session, and (4) immediately after final exercise training session. Only two of the blood samples from each subject were used for the current study (before the first session and after the final session). Blood was drawn from either the antecubital or hand vein into 10 mL EDTA plasma tubes using 2-gauge butterfly needles. Blood was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Plasma was drawn from the top without disturbing the buffy coat, then centrifuged again at 2000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and stored in a low-protein binding tube at −80 °C. Plasma samples with signs of hemolysis were discarded from the study. EV isolation methods are consistent with MISEV guidelines22 and methods outlined in Wu et al.23. Briefly, aliquots of plasma samples were thawed on ice and EVs were isolated via size exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fractions #4–6 or Fractions #7–10; qEV single or original/35 nm; Izon Science, Medford, MA, USA) and ultrafiltration (Amicon® Ultra-2 Centrifugal Filter Unit—30 kDa cutoff; Millipore, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EVs were aliquoted and stored in low-protein-binding tubes with 25 mM D(+)-trehalose anhydrous (Chem-Impex International, Wood Dale, IL, USA) at −80 °C to be used within 2 weeks.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; Nanosight NS300, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) was used to measure concentration and size of isolated EVs. A subsample of EVs was diluted 1:1000 in sterile PBS to a final volume of 1 mL. Samples were loaded into the instrument using a syringe pump speed of 35. The camera level was set to 13 and detection threshold to 5 for sample analysis. A total of five 60 s videos were captured for each sample. Sample concentration (particle/mL) and size (nm) were calculated using NTA software.

EV protein extraction and trypsin digestion

EV samples (50 µL each) were lysed, and proteins were extracted using Perfect-FOCUS™ (G-BIOSCIENCES, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracted proteins were denatured in 100 µL of 6 M urea, reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at room temperature for 60 min, and alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 60 min. The reaction was quenched with 20 mM DTT, followed by incubation at room temperature for 60 min. MS Grade Trypsin Protease (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added at a 1:20 enzyme-to-protein ratio (w/w), and digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 16 h. The reaction was stopped with 1 µL of formic acid (pH 3–4), and samples were dried in a vacuum concentrator. The resulting peptides were reconstituted in 200 µL of 0.1% formic acid, desalted using Pierce™ peptide desalting spin columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and eluted with 600 µL of 50:49.9:0.1 (acetonitrile/water/formic acid) before being dried completely.

Quantitative EV protein LC–MS/MS analysis

Tryptic peptides were separated using a nanoElute LC system (Bruker Daltonics) with LC-MS grade water (0.1% formic acid, solvent A) and acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid, solvent B). Peptide digests were reconstituted in 30 µL of solvent A, and 1 µL (~100 ng peptides) was injected onto a PepMap trap column 300 µm × 5 mm, C18, 5 µm, 100 Å; (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Separation was performed on a nanoElute FIFTEEN column (75 µm × 150 mm, C18, 1.9 µm, 120 Å; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) at 40 °C, 300 nL/min, using a gradient from 2–40% B over 45 min, followed by a wash at 90% B. was coupled to a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) equipped with a CaptiveSpray ion source, operated in data-dependent acquisition with parallel accumulation–serial fragmentation (DDA-PASEF) mode and dynamic exclusion. The MS acquired 10 PASEF MS/MS scans per 1.9 s cycle with an m/z range of 100–1700 and an ion mobility range of 0.60–1.60 V s/cm2 (ramp time: 100 ms). The intensity threshold was 5000 units, target intensity was 20,000, and collision energy was ramped from 20–59 eV.

Database search and quantitation

Raw LC-MS data were analyzed using PEAKS Online (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.) with the Homo sapiens database from UniProt. Search parameters included a precursor mass error tolerance of 20 ppm, fragment mass error tolerance of 0.05 Da, trypsin digestion, a peptide length of 6–45 residues, and up to three missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation as a variable modification. Results were filtered at a 1% FDR with a protein threshold of −log10(P value) > 20.

For label-free quantitation, PEAKS LFQ was used to extract peptide peak areas with a 20 ppm mass tolerance, automatic retention time shift detection, and a CCS error tolerance of 0.05. Peptide selection criteria included an average area ≥300, peptide ID count ≥2, charge between 1 and 10, and at least 5 confident samples per group. Protein abundance was calculated from the top three peptide peak areas per protein. Missing values in the dataset were imputed via iterative imputation using a Bayesian Ridge regression on log-transformed data, with performance evaluated through root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Normalization was performed in Normalyzer (v1.14.0) using log2 transformation and variance stabilization normalization (VSN). Data normality was tested, and differential protein regulation was evaluated using Student’s t test (p < 0.05) after removal of immunoglobulins and keratins from the analysis. Protein enrichment was examined using the PANTHER Classification System (version 19.0).

Targeted protein LC-MS/MS analysis

The targeted method was developed by first analyzing human EV samples using DDA-PASEF. Raw LC-MS data were processed using PEAKS Online X for protein identification. The resulting mzXML and pepXML files were imported into Skyline (Version 23.1) to construct a targeted peptide library. This library consisted of proteins, including GPX1 with associated peptide information, including amino acid sequences, precursor m/z values, and retention times. The raw DDA-MS files were also imported into the Skyline project, and ion mobility values from the DDA runs were incorporated. The finalized targeted method was then exported from Skyline to the timsControl software on the instrument computer. The same human EV samples were re-injected into the nanoLC system using identical LC conditions and collision energy ramps as those used in the DDA-MS runs. Acquisitions were scheduled based on the defined retention times, precursors, and ion mobility windows. Peak areas were quantified in Skyline by manual integration, using the sum of peak areas of the top five MS2 fragment ions for each peptide. Protein quantification was based on the summed peak areas of the top three peptides.

Animals

All experimental animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC). Adult (4-mo-old) male C57BL6/J were ordered from Jackson Laboratory (Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA). Mice were housed at the Beckman Institute for Advanced Science and Technology at UIUC with ad libitum access to standard chow and water while maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on 07:00-19:00 h).

Hindlimb unloading and reloading

Mice were randomly assigned to the following experimental groups: weightbearing (WB), 14 days of hindlimb unloading (HU), and 14 days of HU with 7 days of reloading (RE). These time points were chosen based on previous HU studies focused on muscle recovery following disuse24. This model of disuse results in a rapid, consistent, and robust loss of muscle mass, particularly in the slow-twitch fiber-enriched soleus muscle21. The HU model was adopted from Morey-Holton & Globus25 with modifications. Mice were suspended by their tails at a 30° angle with a piece of athletic tape connected to a wire pulley system. Mice were not able to place weight on their hindlimbs for the entirety of the suspension. Two mice were suspended per cage and were monitored daily for body weight, water, and food intake to ensure no excessive weight loss due to dehydration or malnourishment. For reloading studies, mice were released from the HU apparatus and resumed normal cage activities for 7 days. WB mice served as loading and treatment controls. Suspended mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either PBS as a vehicle control (PBS), EVs isolated from human plasma prior to training as a condition control (EV), or EVs isolated from human plasma after training (ExerV) (n = 5/group). Mice were injected 2x/week during the unloading period, and the final injection was administered on Day 12 of HU (3 × 108 pooled particles in 100 µL PBS/injection). Additionally, HU mice were labeled with deuterium oxide (D2O) (Sigma-Aldrich Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA). Mice received a single i.p. injection bolus of filtered 99% D2O at 30 mL/kg during the third day of suspension and then were provided 8% D2O in their drinking water for 10 days26. After the experiments, mice were anesthetized under isoflurane and euthanized via cardiac puncture and cervical dislocation. Body weights were recorded. Tissues were excised, weighed and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane or optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at −80 °C. Spleens were excised and stored in formalin (formalin solution, neutral buffered, 10%; Sigma-Aldrich Inc.).

Immunofluorescence

OCT-embedded soleus muscles were sectioned at 10 µm at −20 °C using a CM3050S cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Sections were adhered onto charged microscope slides (29-107, Genesee Scientific, El Cajon, CA, USA) and stored at −20 °C until staining. Slides were thawed to room temperature prior to staining. Sections were hydrated for 5 min with PBS three times, blocked for 1 h at room temperature in blocking buffer (PBS with 5% BSA and 0.05% Triton X-100; Sigma-Aldrich Inc.).

To assess myofiber size and type, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-mouse dystrophin (1:200; ab15277, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), IgG1 anti-Type IIa MHC (1:50; SC-71, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank [DSHB], University of Iowa, Iowa City, IA, USA), mouse IgM anti-Type IIb MHC (1:50; BF-F3, DSHB), and IgG2b anti-Type I (1:50; BA-D5, DSHB) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Following washes with PBS, Cyanine5 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:100: A10523, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-mouse IgG1 (1:200; A-21124, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor goat 488 anti-mouse IgM (1:200; A-21042, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Alexa Fluor goat anti-mouse IgG2b (1:200; A-21140, Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies were applied simultaneously for 1 h at room temperature to visualize dystrophin and myofiber type-specific staining. After final washes with PBS, slides were sealed with Clear-Mount (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) and stored at 4 °C.

To assess capillary content, sections were incubated with rat anti-mouse CD31 (1:100; clone 390, No. 14-0311-85, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and rabbit anti-mouse dystrophin (1:200; ab15277, Abcam) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Following washes with PBS, Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-rabbit (1:100; A-21070, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rat IgG (1:200; A-11006, Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies were applied simultaneously for 1 h at room temperature to visualize dystrophin and CD31. Slides were sealed with Clear-Mount and stored at 4 °C.

To assess collagen I, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-mouse collagen type I (1:200; ab34710, Abcam) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Sections were washed with PBS, then incubated with Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-rabbit (1:200; A-21070, Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were sealed with Clear-Mount and stored at 4 °C. A sub-section of slides was co-stained with collagen I and CD31 to visualize collagen localization around capillaries. To co-stain, slides were prepared as previously described, but sections were also incubated with rat anti-mouse CD31 during the primary incubation period. Following primary incubation, slides were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-rabbit (1:200; A-21070, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rat IgG (1:200; A-11006, Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies were applied simultaneously at room temperature for 1 h. Slides were sealed with Clear-Mount and stored at 4 °C.

To assess immune rejection of human EV transplantation, excised spleens were weighed and fixed in 10% formalin. Embedded spleens were sectioned at 5 µm, and routine Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed. Spleen histology was evaluated for significant changes in red and white pulp structure and confirmed by the Veterinarian Diagnostic Laboratory in the College of Veterinary Medicine at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

Myofibrillar and mitochondrial protein fractional synthesis rate

Previously published methods were used to analyze protein synthesis26–28. Briefly, ~30–50 mg of skeletal muscle tissue was homogenized in a bullet blender with isolation buffer (1:20; 100 mM KCl, 40 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM Tris Base, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM ATP, pH = 7.6) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors. After homogenization, subcellular fractions were isolated by differential centrifugation to obtain myofibrillar and mitochondrial subcellular fractions, which were hydrolyzed and underwent cation exchange chromatography. Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectroscopy (7890 A GC-Agilent 5975 C MS, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) was used to determine the deuterium enrichment of alanine (Eproduct). To determine the body water enrichment of D2O, 50 μL of plasma was prepared for analysis of D2O enrichment as described in Abbott et al.27 and run on a liquid water isotope analyzer (Los Gatos Research, Los Gatos, CA, USA). The loss of muscle mass (i.e., protein mass) during the period of labeling is a non-steady state. Therefore, previously derived equations were used to calculate rates of synthesis and breakdown28. For the mitochondria fraction, it is not possible to use non-steady state equations because there is not yet an established sensitive measurement of mitochondrial protein mass29. Therefore, mitochondrial protein synthesis was calculated using standard precursor-product calculations30.

In vitro experiments

To assess myoblast viability, mouse myogenic progenitor cells (C2C12; Lonza #CC-2580; Cambridge, MA, USA) were cultured on 96-well polystyrene plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and incubated with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells grew to 30–40% confluency, serum was changed to 10% EV-free FBS, cells were treated with EVs, and viability was assessed via MTT assay 24 h later following manufacturer’s instructions (M6494, Thermo Fisher). Each plate was treated with either media only, EVs, or ExerVs (4 × 107 particles) for each subject in triplicate.

To assess myoblast differentiation and myotube diameter, mouse myogenic progenitor cells (C2C12) were cultured on 24-well 0.2% gelatin-coated polystyrene plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and incubated with DMEM, 1% PS, and 10% FBS until 70% confluency. Myoblasts were cultured in EV free media, EV and ExerVs (4 × 107 particles) for 4 days. Myotubes were fixed with 4% PFA, then stained with myosin heavy chain antibody (1:100; MF-20, Cat#MAB4470; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and Hoeschst 33342 (1:2000; H1399; Thermo Fisher Scientific). In a separate experiment, myoblasts grew to 90% confluency, then received differentiation media (DMEM + 2% horse serum) with 1% PS for 4–7 days. Differentiated myotubes were treated with either media only, EVs, or ExerVs (4 × 107 particles) for 24 h for each subject in triplicate, prior to assessing diameter.

To assess endothelial cell viability, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Lonza #CC-2519A) were cultured on 96-well polystyrene plates. HUVECs (1 × 104 cells/well) were incubated with Endothelial Cell Basal Medium (EBM-2; Lonza, Cambridge, MA, USA), 1% PS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10% FBS. Cells grew to 80% confluency, serum was changed to 10% EV-free FBS, cells were treated with EVs, then viability was assessed via MTT assay 24 h later. Each plate was treated with either media only, EVs, or ExerVs (4 × 107 particles) for each subject in triplicate.

To assess endothelial cell tube formation, 96-well polystyrene plates were coated with Matrigel (Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix, Phenol Red-free, LDEV-free; Corning) and allowed to solidify for 30 min at 37 °C. HUVECs (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on each plate and treated with either media only, EVs, or ExerVs (4 × 107 particles) for each subject in triplicate. Images for tube formation were obtained 8 h after treatment.

Imaging and analyses

Immunofluorescence stains were visualized using a Zeiss Axiovert inverted fluorescent microscope with Zeiss Axiocam digital camera and ZEN software (Zeiss, Thornwood, NY, USA) or a digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu NanoZoomer). Whole muscle section images were acquired at 20× for myofiber cross-sectional-area (CSA). Images were acquired at ×10 magnification for capillary analysis and ×20 magnification for collagen I deposition using Zeiss Axiovert. Images were acquired in separate color channels for independent analyses of images. To assess global myofiber CSA, dystrophin images of whole soleus cross-section images were analyzed using MyoVision31. Edge fibers were excluded during analysis. Parameters were set to exclude areas <200 µm2 and >6000 µm2. Fibers above 0.95 eccentricity or below 0.50 circularity were excluded from analysis. Fiber-type-specific CSA were automatically detected based on the intensity of different MHC antibodies by MyoVision software. Muscle capillarization was assessed using stitched together images of the entire muscle section. Punctate CD31-Alexa Fluor 488 dots were manually counted as capillary numbers. Myofiber numbers were manually counted, and edge fibers/capillaries were excluded from analysis. The capillary-to-fiber ratio was obtained by dividing the total number of capillaries by the total number of myofibers. A total of four regionally representative images of the soleus were obtained of collagen I and analyzed using the threshold intensity function within ImageJ (NIH).

All in vitro images were visualized and captured with a Zeiss Axiovert inverted fluorescent microscope with a Zeiss Axiocam digital camera and ZEN software. For analysis of myotube growth, three to five 20X images were obtained, and the width of myotubes was calculated using Adobe Photoshop software. Assessment of HUVEC tube formation, a single image was obtained in the center of the well at 5X and 10X objectives. Images were analyzed through the Angiogenesis Analyzer Plugin via ImageJ.

Investigators were blinded to sample identification during all assessments until data analysis was completed.

Statistics

All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Subject characteristics were analyzed with a student’s paired two-tailed t-test before and after exercise intervention. Student’s unpaired t-test was used to compare EV size and concentration. Differences between treatments (WB, PBS, EV, and ExerV) were determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD post hoc analysis. Statistical differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were completed using GraphPad Prism (10.1.2).

Results

Multicomponent exercise training preferentially increases strength

However, maximal strength significantly increased compared to baseline. Diet records from pre- to post-training are shown in Table 4. The impact of exercise training on EV quantity, size, and protein content

No significant changes in EV concentration or size were observed as a result of exercise training (Fig. 1B–D). The mean sizes of EVs before and after exercise training were 118.5 nm and 118.1 nm, respectively (range = 50-250 nm). EVs were then analyzed by nanoLC-MS (Supplementary Tables 1–3). A total of 462 proteins were identified in all samples, including several EV markers (CD9, CD81, HSPB1, ITB1) (Fig. 1E). EV markers SDCB1/Syntenin-1 and ANXA2 were only identified in ExerVs. Of the 462 proteins detected, the majority (322, 70%) were shared among EVs and ExerVs, whereas 128 proteins were unique to ExerVs compared to EVs (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). “Metabolite Interconversion Enzyme” was the most represented protein class for ExerVs, specifically “Oxidoreductases”, which included prostaglandin G_H synthase 1 (PTGS1), glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1), glucose-6-phosphate 1-dehydrogenase (G6PD), isocitrate dehydrogenase (mitochondrial, IDH2), malate dehydrogenase (mitochondrial, MDH2), GMP reductase 1 (GMPR), coagulation factor VIII (F8), and polyunsaturated fatty acid lipoxygenase (ALOX12) (Fig. 1G). Several antioxidants (PRDX1,2,5,6; SOD1,2; TXN; and CAT) and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins (COL1A2; COL6A1,A2,A3; LAMB1,C1; TNC; FN1; SPARC; VWF; VCAN) were identified in both EV and ExerVs, and GPX1 enrichment was validated with targeted analysis (Fig. 1H). Among the shared proteins between EVs and ExerVs, the top significantly upregulated proteins included integrin beta-1 (ITB1), calponin-2 (CNN2), fibronectin (FINC), myosin light polypeptide 6 (MYL6), alpha-enolase (ENOA), myosin regulatory light chain 12A (ML12A), and myosin regulatory light chain 12B (ML12B), thrombospondin-1 (TSP1), fibrinogen gamma chain (FIBG), integrin alpha-6 (ITA6), cofilin-1 (COF1), ras-related protein Rap-1b (RAP1B), vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein (VASP), (Fig. 1I and Supplementary Table 3). The significantly downregulated proteins included collagen alpha-2 (VI) (CO6A2), alpha-1-antichymotrypsin (AACT), hemoglobin subunit gamma-1 (HBG1), hemoglobin subunit gamma-2 (HBG2), apolipoprotein E (APOE), serum amyloid A4 (SAA4), triosephosphate isomerase (TSP1), and catalase (CATA) (Fig. 1I).

Systemic injection of human ExerVs prevents collagen I accumulation during hindlimb unloading

Human EVs were systemically administered to mice during 14 days of HU (Fig. 2A). Body weights were not significantly altered between groups (Fig. 2B). To ensure that human EVs did not evoke an immune response when injected into mice32, spleen weights were recorded at the end of the intervention. No differences in spleen weights or histology were observed (Fig. 2C). Soleus muscle weights and mean fiber CSA were significantly altered as a result of HU, with decreases observed between WB and all other groups (Fig. 2D–G). The myofibrillar fractional synthesis rate (FSR) was not altered during disuse (Fig. 2H). Gastrocnemius muscles were also analyzed for changes in weight, myofibrillar FSR, myofibrillar fractional breakdown rate (FBR), and mitochondrial FSR (Supplementary Fig. 1). Absolute and relative gastrocnemius muscle weights were significantly altered as a result of HU, with decreases observed between WB and treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). The myofibrillar FSR and FBR did not vary during HU (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). Mitochondrial FSR was not different at the end of two weeks of disuse (Supplementary Fig. 1E).

Systemic injection of human ExerVs during hindlimb unloading enhances recovery of Type I fibers during reloading

Based on the results above, we speculated that ExerV treatment during disuse may alter the ECM environment in a beneficial manner that may promote fiber growth during reload 33. Therefore, mice were injected with EVs during disuse and evaluated after 7 days of reloading (Fig. 3A). No changes in spleen weights, body weights, or muscle weights were observed between groups (Fig. 3B–D). However, a trend was noted for differences in mean fiber CSA (one-way ANOVA: p = 0.0849) (Fig. 3E). Upon close examination of fiber types, significant differences were detected in the size of Type I fibers, with decreases observed after PBS or EV injections, yet recovery noted after ExerV administration (Fig. 3F, G). No significant differences were detected in Type II fiber CSA due to treatment (data not shown). Capillary content was significantly decreased with PBS but also recovered with EV or ExerV treatment (Fig. 3H). Finally, similar to the maintenance study, differences were observed with collagen, such that collagen I remained increased around capillaries with PBS injection, but this effect was mitigated with systemic administration of EVs or ExerVs (Fig. 3I-J).

Discussion

Muscle contraction facilitates the release of proteins into the bloodstream that initiate adaptive responses in multiple tissues and organ systems, ultimately resulting in resilience to subsequent mechanical and metabolic stresses. The potential to identify and utilize these proteins, or exerkines, for the purpose of maintaining tissue structure and function under conditions of microgravity or disuse is an active area of investigation. The purpose of the current study was to isolate EVs from human blood after exercise training (ExerVs) and subsequently examine protein content and the potential to maintain or recover skeletal muscle during or after a period of hindlimb unloading in mice. Here, we demonstrate that ExerVs did not minimize the loss of skeletal muscle mass or fiber size during disuse but prevented the accumulation of collagen. Importantly, this modification to the ECM may have facilitated the recovery of Type I fiber size and capillary content observed during the reloading period after disuse. Thus, ExerVs may possess the potential to preserve skeletal muscle plasticity in the context of disuse during a period of microgravity.

Prior studies suggest that an acute bout of intense aerobic or resistance exercise can facilitate the release of EVs into blood when samples are collected within a short window after completion of exercise12–14,17,19,34. We are not aware of any studies that have specifically examined EV quantity and protein content immediately after a supervised, progressive exercise training program in healthy adults. Here, we report no change in EV quantity after progressive, multicomponent training, even when samples were collected immediately after the last exercise session. We predict that the progressive nature of training minimized stress during the adaptive period, resulting in gradual turnover of circulating EVs rather than an abrupt bolus release. Consistent with this hypothesis, EVs collected after the last bout of training possessed unique cargo, including metabolite interconversion enzymes, and induced beneficial changes in mouse models that are reflective of EV turnover. It is unclear whether the last bout of exercise was necessary to observe these effects or if the end-of-training baseline samples were endowed with similar cargo. Follow-up studies are in progress to address this important question.

Brief periods of unloading can result in dramatic loss of protein synthesis and mitochondrial content and function within skeletal muscle35–38. Kang and Li37 demonstrated a 50% reduction in mitochondrial density in mice subjected to two weeks of hindlimb immobilization. The loss of mitochondria within skeletal muscle can facilitate the formation of ROS during and after disuse37,39–42, which can then stimulate muscle degradation via initiation of calpains, autophagy, and the ubiquitin-proteosome pathway43–46. Based on the enrichment of antioxidants, specifically glutathione peroxidases and peroxiredoxins, in the current and prior studies13,15,20, we speculated that ExerVs may possess the capacity to restore redox balance and prevent muscle atrophy. We did not possess sufficient material to directly assess ROS in isolated mitochondria or fibers, but ExerVs did not prevent muscle atrophy in the soleus. Despite this outcome, administration of ExerVs during disuse effectively prevented collagen I accumulation, specifically in the perimysium around vessels, where it has been previously observed in rodent models during immobilization47. Closer evaluation of the protein classification list (Extracellular Matrix Protein) revealed unique enrichment of Latent Transforming Growth Factor Beta Binding Protein 1 (LTBP1) in ExerVs, a factor that maintains TGFβ1 in a latent or inactive state48. Thus, these data suggest that ExerVs isolated after exercise training may have prevented excess deposition of collagen or stimulated collagen remodeling in the later phase of disuse. These changes would be appropriate given the emphasis on strength training in the current study and the need to accommodate fiber growth.

Collagen I accumulation can inhibit load-induced growth in mice33, perhaps due to the inability of critical transmembrane mechanosensors in muscle fibers (dystrophin, integrins) to adhere to laminin in the basement membrane49,50. In addition, collagen I can inhibit angiogenesis due to the bioenergetic demands necessary for endothelial cell migration through a dense microenvironment51. Given the ability of ExerVs to prevent collagen accumulation during disuse, we predicted a more robust adaptive response during the reloading period. ExerV administration did indeed increase Type I fiber mechanosensing and growth. It is not clear whether resolution of collagen, preservation of capillaries, or both contributed to Type I fiber regrowth, but the in vitro experiments support the idea that ExerVs indirectly facilitated fiber recovery. Although both EVs and ExerVs equally reduced collagen I and enhanced capillary content during reload, only the ExerVs successfully recovered Type I fiber CSA. It is possible that EVs and ExerVs encapsulate some of the same beneficial cargo (GPX1, LTBP1), but the kinetics for fusion and release of cargo differ. For example, ExerVs may possess corona proteins that allow for rapid migration and integration within the interstitium, allowing for efficient collagen turnover during disuse, whereas EVs require mechanical stimulation associated with reload to properly localize and initiate remodeling. In fact, a 2.2-fold increase in the collagen-binding β1 integrin was detected in ExerVs compared to EVs (p < 0.05). Thus, ExerVs and EVs may possess similar therapeutic proteins, but perhaps only ExerVs can effectively preserve plasticity during disuse, and perhaps ExerVs are more efficient in rebuilding muscle mass during recovery. Future experiments are required to address these important questions. Despite a lack of precise mechanistic insight, this study is the first to demonstrate that systemically administered ExerVs can induce adaptation in muscle during and after a period of disuse.

There are several limitations of the current study. First, our relatively short-term multicomponent training protocol did not induce any significant changes in body composition or VO2peak when viewed as a group mean but did result in beneficial improvements in strength. Therefore, our training regimen aligns more with a standard resistance exercise training program. It is possible that endurance exercise training can result in antioxidant-rich ExerVs that can mitigate ROS, whereas resistance training results in ExerVs that remodel the ECM, and that a combination of both would be most effective. Direct comparison of the two types of training on plasma EVs is necessary to design ExerV therapies with a robust ability to prevent disuse atrophy. Second, we only isolated ExerVs from male subjects and administered the particles into male mice. Thus, this study cannot address potential sex differences that may exist with regard to ExerV content or efficacy. This is important since females are most impacted by long-duration spaceflight52 and may benefit the most from innovative countermeasures. We did collect ExerVs from female subjects, and future studies will address this question. Finally, we only administered one concentration of EVs four times during disuse. It will be important to follow up on these results and optimize the conditions that may allow for complete preservation of skeletal muscle mass and function during disuse, not just during the recovery period.

In summary, the current study provides the first evidence that EVs isolated from human subjects after exercise training possess the capacity to preserve skeletal muscle plasticity during disuse in a manner that allows for efficient and effective recovery during remobilization. Thus, ExerVs may represent a novel approach to ensure protection of human health during spaceflight or conditions that lead to significant muscle atrophy during periods of disuse.
