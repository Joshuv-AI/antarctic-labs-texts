Air- and Water-Persistent C‑Centered Radical Anion Based on FLP-Type-Activated CO2
Authors: Caroline Gonçalves, Agustín Morales, Léon Escomel, Hossam Farouk, Laure Vendier, Antoine Simonneau, Agustí Lledós, Olivier Baslé, Sébastien Bontemps
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic radicals play pivotal roles in chemistry, biology, and materials science. Although typically short-lived and highly reactive, considerable efforts have been devoted to stabilizing open-shell molecules to better understand their properties and expand their applications. Among them, air-persistent organic radicals (APORs) stand out as benchmarks of stability. Herein, we report the first air- and water-persistent radical anion derived from CO2. This species is generated from an FLP-type-activated CO2 adduct comprising an N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) as the Lewis base and a ditopic perfluorinated diborane as the Lewis acid. Combined experimental and theoretical studies reveal that both thermodynamic and kinetic factors contribute to the remarkable stability of this open-shell system.

Introduction

While organic radicals are typically highly reactive and short-lived intermediates, significant efforts have been devoted to stabilizing open-shell species through the design of new molecular frameworks. − Main-group element chemistry has been at the forefront of this effort, playing a particularly important role in stabilizing otherwise highly reactive carbon-centered radicals. ,− Such strategies have enabled deeper insight into their intrinsic electronic structures and their roles in biological processes, , while also expanding their applications in organic synthesis, materials science, and energy-storage technologies. The extent of radical stabilization and the conditions under which persistence is achieved are, therefore, defining features of these systems. Among them, radicals that remain stable in air constitute a benchmark of stability, making air-persistent organic radicals (APORs) particularly attractive targets. ,,

Over the past two decades, frustrated Lewis pair (FLP) chemistry has undergone tremendous development in the heterolytic activation of a wide range of small molecules. , Complementarily, frustrated radical pairs are formed, under suitable conditions, by an internal single-electron-transfer (SET) event between the Lewis base and Lewis acid, enabling homolytic activation of small molecules. , However, an external SET event onto a small molecule, which is already activated in an FLP-type manner, has been barely explored; − while APOR derived from such a transformation has, to the best of our knowledge, never been reported. Given the high tunability of FLP-type-activated small molecules, we anticipated that conducting SET onto these systems could enable access to APORs. A particularly attractive target would then be CO2 •– radical anion, a highly unstable yet fundamentally important species with notable distonic character − and growing relevance in synthesis. ,

Separately, Lewis base and Lewis acid reactants were used to impart air-persistence to open-shell species. For instance, the captodative properties of N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) − led to the synthesis of the carbonyl-based APORs of type [A] •+ , and [B] •+ , as well as to the neutral radical [C] • (Scheme a). , On another hand, the coordination of the Lewis acidic B­(C6F5)3 to an iminoxy radical led to the formation of the APOR neutral compound [D] • (Scheme a). However, those strategies never led to APOR based on CO2. We recently showed that the CO2-based radical anion, [E] •– , can be accessed via one-electron reduction of the corresponding FLP-type activated CO2 adduct E, and is persistent enough under an inert atmosphere for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. It represents only the second example of an argon-persistent CO2-derived radical anion, following the report of compounds [F] •– (Scheme b). ,

State of the Art of NHC-Stabilized APOR Carbonyl Radicals, of Argon-Persistent CO2-Based Radical Anion and General Scheme of the Present Work

Exploring further the boundaries of FLP systems, we report herein that variation of both the nature and denticity of the Lewis acid moiety enables us to expand the family of stabilized open-shell species and notably to synthesize the first air- and water-persistent CO2-based radical anion (Scheme c). Experimental and computational studies reveal that both thermodynamic and kinetic factors contribute to the remarkable persistence of this radical under oxidative (air, H2O2) and hydrolytic (H2O) conditions. This system thus expands the family of air-stable carbonyl radicals and constitutes the first example of an anionic species of this type along the cationic [A] •+ , [B] •+ , and neutral [C] • APOR. EPR and DFT analyses enabled to disclose an unusual through-space radical delocalization involving fluorine nuclei.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Neutral FLP-type Activated CO2 Adducts 2-4

We explored the reactivity of the CyCAAC–CO2 adduct 1 , toward perfluorinated Al-, Zn-, and B-based Lewis acids. Al­(C6F5)3(toluene), Zn­(C6F5)2(toluene), and the ditopic o-(B­(C6F5)2)2–C6F4 Lewis acids readily reacted with compound 1 at room temperature to afford the Lewis adducts 2-4, which were isolated by crystallization in 30, 66, and 63% yield, respectively (Scheme ). XRD analyses showed that compounds 2-4 feature similar FLP-type activation of CO2 between the Lewis basic carbene and the Lewis acids, yet with notable differences (Figure and Table ). In compound 2, Al­(C6F5)3 is κ1-O coordinated to the CO2 moiety, similarly to the analogous B­(C6F5)3 adduct E reported earlier (Scheme ), while compound 3 adopts a dimeric form with two Zn­(C6F5)2 moieties bridging two CO2 fragments (Figure ). The dihedral angle between the planes of the CAAC and the planes of the CO2 moiety is close to 90° (91.9(2)° and 101.2(4)° for 2 and 3, respectively).

Selected Structural Parameters for Compounds 2-4 and [2-4] •–

To promote the coordination of two Lewis acid units to the two oxygen atoms of CO2, we employed an excess of Al­(C6F5)3(toluene), Zn­(C6F5)2(toluene), or B­(C6F5)3. However, no double coordination was observed under these conditions. This coordination mode has only rarely been reported and typically with less sterically hindered Lewis acids. We therefore turned our attention to a ditopic Lewis acid. The 1,2-[B­(C6F5)2]2C6F4 bis­(borane), first reported in 1999 and recently used by some of us for O2 activation, belongs to a small family of highly Lewis acidic ditopic compounds. − XRD analysis of compound 4 confirmed that this scaffold enables a κ2-O,O coordination mode, where each boron atom binds to a different oxygen atom of the CO2 unit. Contrarily to 2 and 3, the CO2 and carbene planes in 4 are closer to being coplanar (D­(NHC–CO2) = 29.9(2)°). We reported earlier that while the orthogonal arrangement was the most encountered one in solid-state with different NHC units, the relative rotations of these two planes are rather accessible at room temperature in solution.

Compounds 2 and 3 were also characterized in solution by multinuclear NMR analyses. As expected, the 13C NMR characterization of the carbon atoms of the carbene and of the CO2 appears in similar areas (2: δ13C = 190.1 (CNHC), 159.7 (CCO2), 3: δ13C = 190.9 (CNHC), 161.9 (CCO2)) as in compound E (δ13C = 188.4 (CNHC), 158.0 (CCO2)). Moreover, the 1H NMR DOSY experiment conducted on compound 3 indicated that contrarily to its solid-state structure, it adopts a monomeric form in solution at room temperature (Table S1, Supporting Information). In this monomeric form, we questioned the denticity of the CO2 moiety toward Zn. DFT investigations indicated that the monodentated coordination mode is slightly favored by 1.9 kcal.mol–1 (Figure S1, Supporting Information, DFT). Both coordination modes are thus accessible at room temperature. As for compound 4, its insolubility in the tested solvents precluded NMR analysis in solution. Solid-state NMR was therefore employed for its characterization. The carbon atoms of the carbene and of the CO2 were assigned at δ13C = 183 (CNHC) and 166 (CCO2), while broad signals of the boron nuclei appeared at δ11B = −4 and of the F nuclei at δ19F = −130, −160, and −168.

Frontier Orbitals of Compounds 2-4

DFT investigations were performed on compounds 2-4. The initial geometries were taken from the XRD structures for 2 and 4. The M06-2X-D3­(SMD = DCM)/BS1 level of theory accurately reproduces the solid-state data. The localization and relative energy of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMOs) provide an initial measure of the influence of the nature and denticity of the Lewis acids on the behavior of such species under reductive conditions. The LUMO of compounds 2 and 3 are primarily localized on the CNHC fragment and the adjacent nitrogen atom (Figure a). The energy level is more stabilized in compound 2 (−0.97 eV) relative to compound 3 (−0.61 eV) (Figure a). In the case of compound 4, the LUMO is delocalized over both the CNHC and the CCO2 fragment, benefiting from the closer coplanar arrangement of the two planes. Moreover, the LUMO energy (−2.52 eV) is significantly stabilized by 1.55 eV compared to the LUMO of compound 2, which we attribute to the ditopic nature of the Lewis acid.

(a) LUMO of compounds 2-4, energies, and visualization (IBOview, threshold = 65) with color code: carbon (gray), nitrogen (light blue), boron (green), fluorine (dark yellow), oxygen (red), Al (dark gray), and Zn (deep red), (b) calculated reduction potentials vs Fc+/0 in DCM, and (c) experimental reduction potentials vs Fc+/0 in DCM with 0.1 M nBu4NPF6.

The reduction potentials E 1/2 of compounds 2-4 were calculated to be −1.45, −1.67, and −0.49 V in DCM vs Fc0/+, respectively (Figure b). The values for 2 and 3 are comparable to that reported earlier for compound E (−1.29 V in DCM vs Fc0/+), whereas the ditopic bis­(borane) in compound 4 has a marked effect, significantly shifting the reduction potential to less negative values. Cyclic voltammetry measurements of compounds 2-4 in dry dichloromethane (nBu4PF6, 0.1 M) at room temperature confirmed these trends. Irreversible reduction waves were observed at peaks at E pc = – 1.36, −1.72, and −0.92 V vs Fc+/0 for compounds 2-4, respectively (Figure c). Overall, compound 4 displays the least negative reduction potential among these species in agreement with its low-lying LUMO. The contrast with the reduction potential of free CO2 is notable (−2.7 V vs Fc+/0 in acetonitrile). The irreversibility and low intensity of the waves do not arise from instability of either the neutral adducts or the corresponding radical anions since the isolated species are stable in DCM in the presence of nBu4PF6 (Figures S53–S55, Supporting Information). However, we proved that the presence of nBu4PF6 is detrimental during the reduction step in DCM (Figure S56, Supporting Information).

Synthesis and Characterization of the Radical Anions [2-4]•– from the 1e– Reduction of the CO2 Adducts 2-4

The chemical one-electron reductions of compounds 2-4 were carried out using one equivalent of KC8, which resulted in immediate color changes to yellow, orange, and yellow, respectively. The reactions proceeded rapidly, as intense EPR signals were detected within the first 5 min. Notably, the suspension of the initially insoluble compound 4 dissolved instantaneously upon the addition of KC8, affording a clear yellow solution. The resulting radical anions [2-4] •– proved sufficiently stable under an inert atmosphere to allow their isolation by crystallization in 62%, 70%, and 40% yields, respectively (Scheme ). Consistent with their reduction potentials, compounds 2-4 could also be reduced by decamethylcobaltocene (CoCp*2, E° = – 1.9 V vs Fc+/0 in DCM, Figures S36,S38,S40). Furthermore, decamethylferrocene (FeCp*2, E° = – 0.6 V vs Fc+/0 in DCM) was found to be sufficiently reducing to reduce compound 4 (Figure S41, Supporting Information). Attempts to achieve double chemical reduction of compounds 2-4 to carbonite derivatives by adding up to 10 equiv of KC8 were unsuccessful.

XRD analyses revealed various features of the anionic radicals [2-4] •– . In the case of compound [2] •– , the Al­(C6F5)3 moiety is κ1-O coordinated to CO2, as it is in neutral compound 2. K+ is also κ1-O coordinated to the other O atom and to the phenyl ring of the 2,6-diisopropylphenyl (Dipp) substituent of the CAAC moiety. The molecule appears as a dimer with a center of inversion, with K+ serving as a bridge with additional K···F interactions (Scheme ). Compound [3] •– is a monomer in the solid state, and the Lewis acidic Zn­(C6F5)2 moiety is κ2-O,O bidentated to the two oxygen atoms of CO2. As in compound [2] •– , compound [3] •– features the K+ countercation coordinated to the O and F atoms as well as to the π-system of the Dipp substituent. The coordination sphere of K+ is further completed by two molecules of THF. We questioned whether Zn could also adopt a monodentate coordination mode, but no such minimum could be located by DFT means. The increased electronic density in [3] •– and a smaller OCO angle compared to 3 (117.9(3) vs 131.3(3) Å), therefore, strengthen the interaction with the Lewis acid, favoring a bidentate coordination mode. It is worth noting that the NHC and CO2-LA planes shifted from orthogonal in compounds 2 and 3 to coplanar in the related radical anions [2] •– and [3] •– , favoring charge communication between both parts.

In compound [4] •– , the diborane moiety remains dicoordinated to the two oxygen atoms of CO2 as it is in neutral compound 4. The countercation K+ is well separated from the anionic fragment as opposed to what is observed in compounds [2] •– and [3] •– . The higher steric hindrance of the diacid compared to the mono Lewis acids Al­(C6F5)3 and Zn­(C6F5)2 might explain such a feature. Selected parameters of compounds 2-4 and their corresponding radical analogues [2-4] •– are gathered in Table to analyze the impact of 1e– reduction on solid-state metrics. The CO2 moiety shows increased C–O distances and more acute OCO angles (Table , Entries 1 and 2). A stronger connection between the NHC and the CO2 moieties is also apparent in the three radicals [2-4] •– with shorter CNHC–CCO2 distances (Table , Entry 3) and relative orientations of the NHC/CO2 planes featuring coplanar arrangements (Table , Entry 4). These metrics are associated with increased CNHC–N distances (Table , Entry 5). Finally, the O-LA distances decrease for compounds [2] •– and [4] •– , which presumably indicates a stronger bond, as expected (Table , Entry 6). It is the contrary for the Zn compound [3] •– , but the comparison is not straightforward since the Zn coordination environments differ between the neutral (dimeric) and radical (bidentated monomeric) forms.

DFT investigations were performed at the M06-2X-D3­(SMD = THF)/BS2 level of theory to analyze the frontier orbital, as well as the electronic distribution in the radical species. The SOMO of [2-4] •– display similar topologies (Figures S6–S8 and ) and resembles the LUMO of compound 4. The SOMO of compound [4] •– is shown in Figure as a representative example. A predominant π-antibonding character, comparable to that of the free CO2 •– , is observed between the carbon and oxygen atoms of the CO2 unit, while a constructive overlap can be seen between CNHC and CCO2, explaining the shortening of the CNHC–CCO2 distances in each case. The α-SOMO energies for [2] •– and [3] •– are located at −5.00 eV and −4.84 eV, respectively, whereas in the case of [4] •– , the α-SOMO lies significantly lower at – 5.86 eV, indicative of a relatively greater thermodynamic stabilization. Normalized Mulliken spin densities indicate that the highest fractional spin density is mainly located on the CNHC in [2] •– and [3] •– , accounting for 43% and 51%, respectively, with much lower contribution for CCO2 (6% and 2%, respectively, Figure , red numbers). The situation is reversed for [4] •– , with 30% spin density on CCO2, with the density on CNHC accounting for 19%. For comparison, in [E] •– , the spin density is 36% on CNHC and 16% on CCO2. In all three radicals, Natural Population Analysis (NPA) indicates that the anionic charge is mainly localized on the oxygen atoms of the CO2 fragment (ranging from −1.3 to −1.6 e), followed by the nitrogen atom of the NHC moiety (around −0.4 e), while both CNHC (0.12 to 0.18 e) and CCO2 (0.67 to 0.75 e), bearing the Mulliken spin density, remain electropositive. It is particularly remarkable that CCO2 in compound [4] •– features the highest fractional spin density of 30% and the most electropositive charge of 0.75 e.

Overall, the three radical anions [2-4] •– are distonic anions since there is a clear separation between the negative chargemostly on the oxygen atomsand the radical charactermostly on the CNHC and CCO2. − While the CAACCO2 radical anion [1] •– is also a distonic anion, the presence of Lewis acid moieties and especially the ditopic boron-based Lewis acid leads to a significant delocalization of the spin density over the CCO2, making it more analogous to the CO2 radical anion in terms of electronic distribution.

Compounds [2] •– and [3] •– were characterized by CW X-band EPR spectroscopy, affording 3-line resonances at g = 2.0035 and 2.0032, respectively, with hyperfine coupling with the nitrogen nucleus of the CAAC (Figure , black signals). The couplings were simulated with values a N of 6.1 and 5.7 G, respectively (Figure , red signals). The EPR signal obtained for [4] •– at g = 2.0032 differs from the ones of compounds [2] •– and [3] •– because five hyperfine lines were observed. This signal was simulated with hyperfine couplings to one nitrogen and to two fluorine nuclei (a N = 6.6 G, a F = 7.3 G, Figure ).

In order to decipher the origin of the hyperfine coupling with fluorine atoms, single-point EPR calculations were performed with different functionals and basis sets (Tables S15 and S16, Supporting Information, DFT). In all cases, hyperfine coupling constants of significant intensities were found for nitrogen and fluorine atoms. The two fluorine atoms concerned are located in the ortho position of two C6F5 substituents of the diborane moiety. Among all tested combinations, TPSSh hybrid meta-GGA functional combined with IGLO-III basis set for nitrogen and fluorinechosen for its ability to accurately describe hyperfine interactionsand the def2-TZVPP basis set for the remaining atoms produced the best agreement with the experimental data (Table S16, Supporting Information, DFT). With this combination, very good agreement was obtained with a N‑calc = 5.9 G (a N‑exp = 6.6 G) and a F‑calc = 8.3 G (a F‑exp = 7.3 G). The fluorine hyperfine couplings are the result of spin densities of 1.1 and 0.3% on F1 and F2, respectively (Table S17, Supporting Information, DFT). The origin of this feature found explanation in a more detailed analysis of the XRD data, which indicates that these two atoms are spatially close to the carbon atom of CCO2. In the side view shown in Figure , F1 and F2 approach C1bearing the main spin density (30%)on the right and the left sides, respectively, with distances of 2.609(2) and 2.961(3) Å, which are shorter than the sum of the van der Waals radii for C and F (3.17 Å). This short contact enables through-space radical delocalization, explaining the computed spin density on F1 and F2 (Table S17, Supporting Information, DFT), responsible for the observed and computed hyperfine couplings. The observation of through-space radical delocalization is a rather rare event that was described with F in 1972 on a neutral iminyl radical and was more recently used with three Br to stabilize a triarylphosphine radical cation. An hypothetical compound [4*] •– was computed, in which the eight ortho fluorine atoms of the diborane moiety are replaced by hydrogen atoms to analyze the localization of the spin density in the absence of through-space delocalization. The geometry of [4*] •– was derived from the XRD structure of compound [4] •– and optimized at the same level of theory. Compared to [4] •– , the Mulliken spin density is slightly lower on CCO2 (28% vs 30%) and is slightly higher on CNHC (21% vs 19%).

Experimental and Theoretical Evaluation of the Stability of Radical Anions [2-4] •–

The possibility to run EPR and XRD analyses on [2-4] •– demonstrates that one-electron reduction of FLP-type activated CO2 produces stabilized radical anions. With multiple species in hand, we sought to identify the key factors governing this stabilization, particularly in the context of air-persistent organic radicals. We therefore evaluated their persistence under a range of conditions in solid and solution states (Figure ). In the solid state, radical anions [2-4] •– are stable for weeks when stored under Ar at −37 °C. In solution under Ar, the decay of [2-4] •– as well as that of [E] •– and [1] •– was monitored over 300 min by following the ratio I/I 0, where I is the integrated EPR signal at a given time and I 0 is the integration of the signal from the initial measurement (Figure a). I 0 thus corresponds to the EPR signal integrated approximately 10 min after sample preparation. Negligible decays were measured over 300 min for compounds [3] •– , [4] •– , and [E] •– (purple, orange, and green curves, Figure a), whereas in situ-generated [1] •– decays by ∼20% (yellow curve, Figure a). It thus proves that the presence of a Lewis acid in these compounds has a positive impact on their stability. However, this is not the case for the Al adduct [2] •– , which is the least stable, losing ∼60% of its signal during the same period of time (blue curve, Figure a). Over longer time scales, [3] •– and [E] •– fully decompose within 3–5 days in solution, while [4] •– remains stable for weeks.

The decays have been quantified by double integration of the signals.

Aerobic stability was then evaluated in both solid and solution states. Solid-state [3] •– decomposes almost completely after air exposure for 1 h (Figure b), whereas [E] •– retains 23% of its EPR signal under the same conditions and time period (Figure c). Strikingly, [4] •– persists for weeks under air, maintaining 60% of its initial signal after 1 month (Figure d). In solution, [3] •– and [E] •– are rapidly quenched upon exposure to air with no detectable EPR signal. By contrast, [4] •– remains markedly persistent (Figure e). A 5 mM THF solution of [4] •– was indeed exposed to air for ∼5 to10 min during the preparation and sealing of an EPR tube at the bench. Despite an estimated 9 equiv of O2 remaining in the tube headspace, the radical retains ∼90% of its signal during the first 100 min and is essentially unchanged thereafter (purple curve, Figure e). To further challenge the oxidative robustness of [4] •– , an aqueous solution of hydrogen peroxide was added to a 5 mM THF solution of [4] •– corresponding to 100 equiv of H2O2 and 400 equiv of H2O compared to the quantity of [4] •– . Although I/I 0 fluctuations were observed due to the difficulty to adjust the probe in the mixture of solvent, the overall decay profile closely matches that observed under air (blue curve, Figure e). These data collectively demonstrate that [4] •– displays exceptional persistence under both oxidative and hydrolytic conditions.

DFT Analysis of Radical Stabilization

Further insights into the factors governing radical stabilization were obtained from DFT calculations. Noting that conventional radical stabilization energy scales account only for thermodynamic parameters, Paton et al. proposed an alternative approach that also incorporates kinetic factors to more comprehensively assess radical stability. The method quantifies the stability of organic radical based (i) on the maximum fractional spin density and (ii) on the percentage buried volume (%Vburr) of the atom bearing this maximum spin density. These two parameters serve as thermodynamic (spin delocalization) and kinetic (steric protection) indicators of radical stability. As described earlier, the highest fractional spin density resides on the CNHC for [2-3] •– (43% and 51%, respectively), whereas for [4] •– it shifts to the CCO2 (30%; Figure ). Because [4] •– displays the lowest maximum spin density, it is the most thermodynamically stabilized species, according to Paton’s method. The corresponding %Vburr values associated with these carbon atoms (CNHC for [2-3] •– and CCO2 for [4] •– ) were computed at 82.9, 80.7, and 89.7%, respectively, computing [4] •– as the most kinetically protected radical anion. These data are reported in the diagram in Figure a. With both parameters, [4] •– is thus the most stabilized radical, in perfect agreement with the experimental data.

We also computed the values (%Vburr and maximum spin density) for radical compounds [E] •– , [1] •– , [4*] •– , [CPh 3 ] • , [CH 3 ] • , and [CO 2 ] •– at the same level of theory and reported the data in Figure a. As expected, the least stable radicals, [CH 3 ] • and [CO 2 ] •– , occupy the lower-right quadrant of the stability plot (minimal delocalization and steric protection). All of the remaining radicals exhibit substantial kinetic and thermodynamic stabilization.

Inspection of the CAAC–CO2 series ([1–4] •– and [E] •– ) reveals a clear increase in stability from the parent radical anion [1] •– to the adducts formed with Zn­(C6F5)2 [3] •– , Al­(C6F5)3 [2] •– , B­(C6F5)3 [E] •– , and o-(B­(C6F5)2)2–C6F4 [4] •– . This strengthening results from the simultaneous increase in both thermodynamic and kinetic stabilization factors. The ditopic Lewis acid moiety in [4] •– offers the strongest stabilization, comparable to the thermodynamic stabilization of triphenylmethyl radical (30% vs 28%) but much more kinetically stabilized than this known persistent radical (%Vburr = 89.7% vs 64.3%). Relative to [4] •– , the related [4*] •– is slightly more thermodynamically stabilized (28% vs 30%) but slightly less sterically shielded (%Vburr = 87.8 vs 89.7). To combine both descriptors into a single quantitative measure, we applied the radical stability score (RSS; eq ), which assigns equal weight to %Vburr (PVB) and the maximum fractional spin density (SD): 1 RSS=PVB+50(1−SD)

The resulting RSS values of the CAAC–CO2 radical anions (Figure b) span from 78.8 for [1] •– to 127.4 for [4] •– , further corroborating the progressive stabilization across the CAAC–CO2 series offered by the Lewis acid. This feature is particularly evident when examining the progressive thermodynamic and kinetic stabilization observed along the series [1] •– →[E] •– →[4] •– . The successive incorporation of B­(C6F5)3 and o-(B­(C6F5)2)2–C6F4 leads to an enhanced delocalization of spin density toward CCO2, concomitant with increased kinetic shielding of the radical center. A similar delocalization of spin is observed in the case of [2] •– and [3] •– compared to [1] •– but not to the same extent as the boron analogues.

The comparison with the APOR compounds of type [C] • shown in Scheme is informative. In that study, electron-withdrawing alkyl substituents connected to the carbonyl group were shown to promote APOR formation by diverting the spin density away from the CNHC moiety, an effect closely analogous to the Lewis-acid-induced redistribution observed here.

In both systems, however, the mechanism by which the alkyl substituent or Lewis acidwhose electronic influence is expected to be predominantly inductivemodulates the delocalization of spin density residing in an out-of-plane π-system remains unclear. Notably, the stabilization of the radical anion does not follow the trend expected from the fluoride ion affinity (FIA) or the Gutman–Beckett scales, − suggesting that additional factors are operative.

Furthermore, a clear discrepancy emerges between the computed stability of [2] •– , which is predicted to lie between that of [E] •– and [3] •– , and its experimental stability in solution under Ar, where it appears less stable than [1] •– . Although we do not yet have a definitive rationale for this behavior, we propose that the calculated stability reflects the intrinsic stabilization of the unpaired electron, whereas the experimentally observed decay reflects the stability of the entire molecule. The reduced persistence of [2] •– may therefore originate not from its open-shell nature but rather from the Lewis acid component itself. This would be in line with the higher reactivity of Al-based adducts relative to their B analogues observed in numerous closed-shell systems. , These features open new questions about the modulation of the stability of the open-shell species based on the nature of the Lewis acid.

Conclusions

We have shown herein that stabilized CO2-derived radical anions can be obtained from 1e– reduction of FLP-type activated CO2 using CAAC as the Lewis base and strong perfluorinated Lewis acids, extending beyond the B­(C6F5)3 adducts reported earlier. At first glance, these results prove (i) the generality of the process to stabilize open-shell compounds and (ii) that the extent of the thermodynamic stabilization by spin density delocalization is not directly correlated to the Lewis acidity. This latter observation highlights the need for more detailed investigations to understand and ultimately control the reactivity of these open-shell systems by the modulation of the nature of the Lewis acid.

In the context of APORs, we exploited the modularity of FLP-type activation to disclose the first air- and water-persistent CO2-based radical anion using a ditopic boron-based Lewis acid. This system was shown to significantly delocalize the spin density over the CCO2 center, resulting in an electronic distribution more closely resembling that of the free CO2 •– radical anion. Furthermore, comprehensive experimental and computational studies provide a clear rationale, indicating that both thermodynamic and kinetic factors contribute to this remarkable stability.

Ongoing work in our group focuses on modulating the reactivity of these distonic species, evaluating the stabilizing role of through-space spin delocalization, and extending the FLP strategy to access open-shell species from other small-molecule activations.

General Experimental Method

Manipulations were carried out following standard Schlenk line and glovebox techniques using argon as the inert gas. Solvents were dried by using a MBraun SPS column. Deuterated solvents were freeze-pump-thaw degassed and stored under Ar over 4 Å molecular sieves. NMR spectra were collected on a Bruker Avance III 400. Chemical shifts for 1H and 13C are relative to TMS. Chemical shifts are given in ppm and coupling constants in Hz. The following abbreviations are used: s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; q, quartet; sept, septet; and m, multiplet. Voltametric measurements were carried out with a potentiostat SP-300 (BioLogic) controlled by EC-Lab software. Experiments were performed in a homemade Schlenk three-electrode cell. The cell was prepared under argon in order to avoid the presence of moisture and air. The pseudoreference electrode consisted of a Ag/AgNO3 (10 mM) and nBu4PF6 (0.1 M) solution in CH3CN separated from the solution by a bridge compartment. The counter electrode was a platinum wire of ca. 1 cm2 apparent surface. The working electrode was glassy carbon microdisk (1.0 mm of diameter, BioLogic SAS). The glassy carbon working electrode was polished with alumina slurry and rinsed with distilled water before each cyclic voltammogram. The water content of the electrolyte was systematically controlled to be below 5 ppm. Blank experiments confirmed the absence of background electrochemical activity or oxygen contamination. The supporting electrolyte was nBu4PF6 (MERCK, for electrochemical analysis, ≥99.0%). The reduction potential of the redox couple, Fc+/0 is 0.13 V versus Ag/Ag+ in the presence of this supporting electrolyte. Computational Details

The calculations were carried out using Gaussian16 software. Geometry optimizations were performed using the M06-2X functional combined with Grimme’s D3(0) (zero-damping) correction to consider dispersion effects, using as a starting point the crystal structure obtained with single-crystal XRD analysis. The optimizations were carried out in solution (dichloromethane, DCM, ε = 8.93 and THF, ε = 7.4257) using the SMD continuum model , with BS1 as a basis set. BS1 uses the 6-311G++(d,p) basis set for the H, C, N, F, B, Al, and O atoms. − In the case of Zn, the Stuttgart/Dresden (SDD) effective core potential (ECP) and associated basis set were employed, augmented with one additional diffuse f-type polarization function (exponent = 3.031). Frequency calculations were performed with the aim of characterizing the optimized structures as minima. All energies in solution were corrected by single-point calculations with a larger basis set (BS2), including the def2-TZVP basis set for all atoms.

Gibbs energies in solution were calculated at 298.15 K, adding to the potential energies in solution, obtained with single-point calculations using BS2, the thermal and entropic corrections obtained from frequency calculations with BS1. To adjust the Gibbs energies from 1 atm to 1 mol·L–1 standard state in solution, a correction of RTln­(cs/cg) was added (ΔG 1 atm→1 M). The value 1.9 kcal·mol–1 was added to energies of all species, cs is the standard molar concentration in solution (1 mol·L–1), cg is the standard molar concentration in the gas phase (0.0446 mol·L–1), and R is the gas constant (8.314 J·mol–1·K–1). All reported Gibbs energies in the following discussion correspond to M06-2X-D3 energies in solution at 298.15K in kcal·mol–1, if not otherwise specified, calculated using the formula: G=E(BS2)+G(BS1)−E(BS1)+ΔG1atm→1M

The orbital energies were computed through a single-point calculation from their optimized structures with BS2 as basis set, using as input the optimized structures calculated at the M06-2X-D3­(SMD = DCM or THF)/BS1 level of theory. IboView software was used to visualize the orbitals. ORCA calculations were carried out to obtain the binary files (.gbw) that will be subsequently employed for the generation of molden.input files, readable files on IboView. These calculations at M06-2X were also combined with Grimme’s D3(0) (zero-damping) correction considering the original damping function (D3ZERO), , including def2-TZVP as a basis set for all atoms and in solution with SMD as solvation model. , NPA charges were calculated via a single-point calculation using the optimized geometry of the species and the NBO 3.1 package, included in Gaussian 16. The keyword pop = NPA was employed in order to request the NPA section of the Natural Bonding Orbital (NBO) analysis.

EPR calculations were carried out using ORCA 6.0 with the M06-2X-D3/BS1 optimized geometry in solution (THF, ε = 7.4257) in Gaussian16. Benchmarking of the methodology against the experimentally obtained isotropic g-value and the hyperfine coupling constant (HFCC) for all atoms was carried out for the functional and basis set. An additional keyword was used, including SOMF­(1X), which invokes the SOMF­(1X) treatment of the spin–orbit coupling (SOC) operator that is useful for EPR property calculations. TightSCF was also introduced to tighten the self-consistent-field (SCF) convergence criteria. In the %scf section, the number of SCF iterations was increased up to 1000 steps due to convergence issues. The soscfstart 1 keyword was also added, which controls the second-order SCF to also improve the SCF steps. In the %EPRNMR section, keywords such as GTENSOR TRUE were used to compute the isotropic g-value as well as AISO and ADIP for each atom, which request the isotropic part of the HFCC and its dipolar part, respectively.
