Presence of Clonal Hematopoiesis and Cardiac Events After Allogeneic Stem Cell Transplantation

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAllogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) has been associated with serious cardiotoxicity, including atrial fibrillation (AF) and other cardiac events. In preclinical models, posttransplant clonal hematopoiesis (CH) frequently parallels or precedes clinical AF, yet whether this is seen in patients or has implications for cardiotoxic risk in long‐term follow‐up is unknown.MethodsLeveraging consecutive patients treated with alloHSCT, genotyped using deep‐amplicon sequencing to detect the presence of CH, we assessed the development of new (incident) AF, by CH status. Secondary outcomes were the occurrence of major adverse cardiac events, defined as heart failure, myocardial infarction, AF, symptomatic ventricular arrhythmias, and cardiovascular death after alloHSCT. We also assessed the relation between CH and post‐alloHSCT cancer survival outcomes. Observed incident AF rates were compared with Framingham heart predicted rates. Multivariable regression and survival analyses were used to define the relation between CH and incident AF, major adverse cardiac events, progression‐free survival, and overall survival. We also explored these relationship by specific mutation and by varying variant allele frequency.ResultsOverall, 201 patients with deep sequencing after alloHSCT were identified (median age, 59 years; 46.3% with hypertension; and 42.3% with CH, including 29.5% with ≥2 mutations). Over a median follow‐up of 35 months, 11.5% of patients developed incident AF, and 29.9% experienced major adverse cardiac events. In those without prior AF, the weighted‐average incidence was 407 per 10 000 person‐years compared with the Framingham‐predicted 63 per 10 000 person‐years (risk ratio, 6.4; P<0.001; absolute excess risk, 344). In follow‐up, increased CH burden was associated with higher risk of incident AF (hazard ratio [HR], 4.0 [95% CI, 1.2–14.1]; P=0.03), cancer‐progression (HR, 2.6 [95% CI, 1.3–5.2]; P=0.006), and death (HR, 2.1 [95% CI, 1.0–4.3]; P=0.047). There was no difference in non‐AF major adverse cardiac events.ConclusionsCollectively, these data suggest among patients treated with alloHSCT, the presence of CH appears to portend increased AF risk, though validation in larger prospective studies is needed.

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) has strong efficacy against many hematologic malignancies and is used in nearly 60 000 patients every year. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Due to the expansion in donor eligibility, the number of alloHSCTs is expected to double by 2030. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Despite alloHSCT's benefits, unintended toxicity has become an important complication of this lifesaving therapy, as up to 15% to 20% of patients may experience nonrelapse death. 7 Increasing data suggest cardiovascular events, which may be a key source of this unintended morbidity and death. 8 , 9 , 10 However, as traditional cardiac and transplant factors only partially account for these events, the reasons and predictors of these events are largely unclear.

Although most available studies have focused on coronary events, emerging reports have suggested other cardiotoxic events, including atrial fibrillation (AF), may be substantially more common and associate with death. 8 , 9 , 10 Available data suggest electrocardiographic abnormalities and potentially life‐threatening arrhythmias are commonly seen in long‐term survivors of childhood cancers after alloHSCT, even beyond other events. 10 , 11 Further, anecdotal reports have suggested that these may be seen earlier than previously recognized. Because alloHSCT invokes a potent immune response, involving upregulation of proinflammatory factors increasingly recognized to potentially drive AF development (eg, clonal hematopoiesis [CH], interleukin‐6), 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 it may be reasonable to postulate that patients treated with alloHSCT may be at particularly elevated risk. This is supported by animal models, where the presence of CH after transplant precedes or predicts AF development. 14 Despite this, there are no data defining the relation between CH or other proinflammatory factors on cardiotoxic risk, yet whether CH holds prognostic implications on long‐term cardiovascular events after alloHSCT is unknown.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are not openly available for reasons of sensitivity and are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

Leveraging a US‐based comprehensive cancer center cohort of alloHSCT‐treated patients with a hematologic malignancy with deep sequencing following transplantation from 2015 to 2021, we evaluated the rate of new (incident) AF after institutional review board approval. Study patients included adults aged ≥18 years treated with alloHSCT for a hematologic malignancy who had deep sequencing for CH and any other biologic measures (eg, interleukin‐6) after transplantation. Patients with incomplete medical records for the variables of interest were excluded. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the institutional review board (IRB) due to the retrospective nature of the study. We manually identified all cardiac events after alloHSCT, inclusive of symptomatic arrhythmias (AF, ventricular tachycardia, and other arrhythmias), myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, coronary revascularization, and cardiac death. Cardiac deaths were adjudicated by cardiologists. Cardiac events were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0, followed by adjudication by independent cardiologists. 17 Cardiotoxicity‐related symptoms included chest pain, syncope, dizziness, palpitations, heart failure symptoms, and sudden or adjudicated cardiac death.

Sequencing and Other Inflammatory Data

Whole‐exome sequencing of blood DNA following alloHSCT was performed using a previously defined preidentified list of curated leukemogenic CH of indeterminate potential and CH‐related variations. 18 , 19 , 20 Specifically, an established 50‐gene panel (Table S1) was used. Processed libraries were sequenced using an amplicon‐based next‐generation sequencing strategy with mutation‐specific primers via a MiSeq system (Illumina) as previously described. 18 This panel focuses on base substitutions and small indels with full coding coverage for 36 genes and hotspot coverage for 14 additional genes. The mutant allele sensitivity is 99% at variant allele frequency (VAF) ≥2% and 100% at VAF ≥5%, with average read depth of 2100×. 18 Consistent with consensus definitions, CH was defined as the presence of somatic variations in leukemogenic CH driver genes at a variant allele fraction of ≥2%. 17 , 21 , 22 , 23 Receiver operating characteristic analyses were also performed to define the optimal VAF cutoff. Genetic data analyses were blinded to clinical characteristics and outcomes.

In those with available pre‐ and post‐alloHSCT biobank samples, prespecified proinflammatory proteins (eg, interleukin‐6) implicated in AF in preclinical models were also analyzed (see Data S1). 24 , 25 , 26 , 27

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was the development of incident AF. The secondary outcomes were the occurrence of major adverse cardiac events (MACEs), defined as any AF, heart failure, myocardial infarction, symptomatic ventricular arrhythmias, and cardiovascular death following alloHSCT, by CH mutation status were also assessed. Other outcomes including progression‐free survival (PFS), defined as the time from alloHSCT initiation to disease progression or death from any cause, and cumulative incidence of relapse and nonrelapse death were also considered. Furthermore, we explore the relation between alternative VAF thresholds (1%, 5%, 10%) and post‐alloHSCT AF, MACEs, and survival outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, using mean± SD, or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables due to non‐normal data distributions, and frequency counts with percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons were made using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Time‐to‐event analysis methods were used to summarize proinflammatory (eg, CH burden) and incident AF and MACE outcomes and to evaluate associations with these outcomes. In all analyses, death without the outcome event (incident AF or MACE) was considered a competing risk. Incident AF, MACE, or death were censored on the date of the last follow‐up. The relationship between CH burden and AF/MACE development was expressed using estimated hazard ratios (HRs). Variables with P<0.10 on univariate analysis were included in the multivariable regression model with each alloHSCT, cancer, and cardiovascular variable being evaluated independently in separate models. We also considered the implications of CH VAF status. In addition, in the multivariate analysis for death, we also included the development of AF as a predictor to better understand the effect of AF on survival. The relationship between CH mutation burden and development of AF, MACE, disease progression, and death were also assessed.

Univariable and multivariable Fine and Gray regression analyses accounting for competing risks were performed to determine the relationship between covariates and outcomes. Survival analysis techniques were used to estimate the cumulative incidence of AF, MACE, and both progression‐free and overall survival, by CH mutation status. Multivariable Cox modeling was used for development of AF, PFS and overall survival. Time to event was defined as the time from mononuclear cell sequencing to the first cardiac event. In addition, subgroup analysis, based on the presence or absence of multiple or high mutation burden was performed. When evaluating post‐alloHSCT AF, acute graft‐versus‐host disease, and chronic graft‐versus‐host disease were modeled with time‐to‐event outcomes and were treated as time‐varying covariates. Post‐alloHSCT AF, when used as a covariate in OS and PFS models, was also treated as time‐varying. Established clinical factors like GVHD were included in survival outcome modeling to minimize confounding.

To better understand the effect of alloHSCT itself on AF risk, observed rates of new AF were compared with calculated Framingham‐predicted rates of incident AF. 28 , 29 , 30 To provide a more vetted comparison with Framingham predictions, only those aged 45 to 90 years were included, matching the inclusion criteria used for the prediction model. Observed rates of new AF development were calculated as cumulative incidence, accounting for available follow‐up. The expected Framingham‐predicted rate of incident AF for this study cohort was calculated by averaging the individual patient probabilities obtained after applying the individual patient‐level risk factors. Absolute excess risk was calculated, by subtracting the expected number of cases from the number of observed, multiplying by 10 000, and finally dividing by person‐years at risk. All statistical analyses for the study were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Overall, 201 patients with a median age of 59±7 years (and 42.3% women, 46.3% with hypertension, 11.4% with diabetes, 8.5% with prior AF) with deep sequencing were identified. Most were treated for myeloid malignancies, and 97% had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 to 1; and 131 (65.2%) patients received prior anthracycline. Additional patient, oncologic, and treatment characteristics are described in Table 1. Among the cohort, 85 (42.3%) had detectable CH mutations after alloHSCT (Table S2). DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and TP53 were the most frequent mutations among 46 different total genes identified. Median time from alloHSCT to sequencing was 4 (IQR, 3–15) months.

At 3 years, the cumulative incidence of incident after alloHSCT AF in patients with no cardiovascular disease (CVD) history was 13.9%, and 7.8% in those patients with a history of CVD (P=0.48). In total, 60 (29.9%) patients developed any MACE, including symptomatic arrhythmias (56.7% of all MACEs), heart failure (33.3%), myocardial infarction (8.3%), and cardiac death (1.7%). MACEs are summarized in Table S4 and S5. No patients discontinued treatment due to MACEs. The cumulative incidence of MACEs by 2 years was 24%. Most arrhythmias were AF events (21/34 [62%]). Thirteen patients (6.5% overall) developed symptomatic ventricular arrhythmias after alloHSCT initiation; with a median time to event of 11.2 months. Cardiac death occurred in 6 patients, including 5 patients with a preceding MACE. Of those who developed MACEs, 43 (71.7%) occurred in patients without preexisting CVD. In those with MACEs, the median time to the first MACE was 9 (IQR, 1.3–19.7) months with 20%, and 32% of MACEs occurring within 1 and 3 years of alloHSCT treatment, respectively. The median time to any arrhythmia was 8.9 months, and cardiac death was observed at a median time of 20.4 (IQR, 9.7–30.4) months. Factors associated with MACEs accounting for competing risk of noncardiac death are shown in Tables S6–S11. On multivariable analyses, no alloHSCT‐specific treatment variables were associated with higher MACEs.

Relation between CH‐Related Mutations and AF Risk and Survival

On univariate analysis, prior hypertension, the presence of IDH1, and multiple (at least 2) CH mutations (ASXL1, FLT3, IDH1, TET2) at a VAF of ≥2% were associated with incident AF (Table S12). There was no difference in AF risk by donor CH status or individual VAF level (Tables S13 and S14). Among those with posttransplant CH mutations, there was no difference in posttransplant AF development based on recurrence versus de novo CH mutation development (12.5% versus 8.3%; P=0.71; Table S15) or need for subsequent cancer treatment. On multivariable analysis (Table 2), only the presence of ≥2 CH mutations (ASXL1, FLT3, IDH1, TET2) at a VAF of ≥2% consistently remained associated with incident AF in all models (HR, 4.05 [95% CI, 1.2–14.1]; P=0.03; Figure 1; Table 2).

Incident AF and Survival Outcomes

When comparing patients with new AF following alloHSCT to all other patients, those with incident AF have significantly worse PFS (HR, 3.05 [95% CI, 1.70–5.46]; P<0.001) as shown in Figure 2. In multivariate analysis, AF remained associated with worse PFS (HR, 2.74 [95% CI, 1.52–4.95]; P<0.001). Similarly, there was a significant association between the development of new AF and subsequent death (HR, 3.87 [95% CI, 2.28–6.59]; P<0.001). Incident AF did not associate with progression alone, and worse PFS was primarily driven by an increase in toxicity (nonrelapse death; HR, 10.23 [95% CI, 3.82–27.38]; P<0.001; Figure 3). Comparative Incidence of AF After AlloHSCT

In those without baseline AF, 11.4% developed new AF. Restricted to patients aged 45 to 90 years (n=152), the cumulative incidence of AF was 13.2%. This translated into an observed new AF cumulative incidence of 407 per 10 000 person‐years compared with the Framingham risk predicted rate of 63 per 10 000 person‐years. This resulted in an observed versus expected ratio of 6.4 (absolute excess risk, 344; P<0.001; Figure 4 and Figure S2).

Discussion

In this evaluation of the incidence, risk factors, and ramifications of CH after alloHSCT, nearly 12% developed incident AF and >25% developed a MACE after alloHSCT. In long‐term follow‐up, the excess risk of incident AF after alloHSCT was >6‐fold higher than expected. This pattern remained even after accounting for donor source and treatment‐related factors. Outside of age, the presence of multiple CH mutations was the strongest predictor of increased AF risk and corresponded to the risk of MACE. When accounting for the competing risk of noncardiac death, the presence of ≥2 CH mutations (ASXL1, FLT3, IDH1, TET2) was consistently associated with MACEs. This relationship remained, even after accounting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Furthermore, we observed that the presence of multiple CH mutations strongly associated with progression and overall mortality rate. These observations are important, given the growing number of alloHSCT‐treated survivors and the lack of available tools or early markers to guide the determination of long‐term cardiotoxicity risk.

The observation of increased AF, particularly in those with CH following alloHSCT, adds to a growing body of evidence linking immune‐based therapies with cardiac risk, yet no prior investigations have evaluated the effect of molecular drivers of inflammation on cardiotoxic risk. In an evaluation of patients with myeloma, the presence of pretreatment CH was linked with an increase in cardiac events. 10 However, the potential role of posttransplant CH on cardiac risk was not assessed. To our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation to define the relation between post‐alloHSCT CH and long‐term cardiovascular (and cancer) outcomes and the first to characterize the comparative risk of AF following alloHSCT.

The predictors of MACE following alloHSCT, including incident AF, are not well understood. Data from prior studies have suggested that traditional factors, including age and atrial size, may underlie at least in part, a significant proportion of the observed risk. 10 However, many of these events are not well explained by traditional factors alone, with many patients having almost no risk factors outside of alloHSCT treatment. 10 , 11 , 31 Although the presence of AF itself did not remain associated with death across multiple cardiac and cancer strata or factors, the presence of multiple clonal mutations led to a higher mortality rate. This may lend credence to the postulation that CH mutations and early inflammatory alterations may be a key driver of subsequent arrhythmia risk and cardiotoxicity development among patients requiring alloHSCT. Akin to other proarrhythmic cancer therapies (eg, ibrutinib), new AF development may serve as a key marker for understanding the cardiotoxic effects of alloHSCT on survival outcomes. 32

The effect of cardiac events following alloHSCT on survival outcomes is incompletely understood. Beyond traditional cancer risk factors, a high CH mutation burden is associated with an increase in incident AF and death. While the observed relationships between higher AF and subsequent death were reflective of those seen in noncancer populations, the relative risk of incident AF was substantially higher than the 2% to 3% rate anticipated from more traditional AF risk factors alone. Similarly, the magnitude of the effect of incident AF on the long‐term mortality rate was increased. Among patients with cancer, AF and other cardiac event development are a potent driver of transplant treatment alterations and transplant‐associated death. 7 Development of AF following alloHSCT has been associated with worse survival, 10 and the presence of CH mutations has been linked with cardiotoxic sequelae. 33 Our results support these links, but for the first time proinflammatory alterations (ie, CH mutations) are associated with cardiotoxic AF and long‐term mortality rate after alloHSCT initiation in patients with hematologic cancers and delineate a time‐dependent relationship. With alloHSCT, this may represent a potential opportunity for intervention, including early identification and potential targeted therapies; however, prospective studies are needed to delineate these effects.

Although the exact reasons for these observations are not clear, animal models and postmortem studies demonstrate that alloHSCT leads to myocardial remodeling mediated by immune activation. 13 In non‐alloHSCT models, proinflammatory alterations (eg, CH leads to an increase in adverse cardiac remodeling, arrhythmia susceptibility, and AF development) have been observed. 15 , 16 With alloHSCT, these processes are generally exaggerated, which may provide a key substrate for the initiation of unintended cardiac sequelae. Indeed, our correlative data, which will need validation in a larger data set, demonstrated a correlation between increased in interleukin‐6 and post‐alloHSCT cardiotoxicity. AlloHSCT‐induced aberrant inflammatory responses may lead to higher macrophage‐mediated inflammation and myocyte turnover. 12 Furthermore, as alloHSCT‐treated patients are now living longer, AF may increasingly be recognized as a common event following alloHSCT exposure. 9 , 34 These alterations have been recognized to drive disproportionate manifestations of CVD in other populations wherein potentially cardiotoxic therapy is needed. 35 However, with alloHSCT, prospective studies are needed to define the burden of CVD and elucidate preventive strategies for these events.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, available follow‐up and timing of deep sequencing were not uniform. Despite accounting for variation in follow‐up and traditional risk factors via extensive modeling, reliable observed rates from comparator oncologic populations were not routinely available. Compliance to preventative CVD therapies could not be determined. Similarly, the decision to initiate antiarrhythmic drugs was nonuniform. AF symptoms (eg, fatigue) can overlap with common toxicities that result from cancer therapy, and there is the potential to miss events, as a rigorous arrhythmia assessment is not routinely performed at all follow‐up visits. AF or other cardiac focused data were not routinely available in alloHSCT‐focused US‐based registries. Some patients may have relapsed near or following the time of sequencing, yet there was no association with subsequent treatments. Some cardiac drugs may have been given for nonarrhythmic reasons. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not routinely performed; but its use may allow an opportunity to more comprehensively and efficiently define cardiotoxicity risk. This is a single‐center study. Sample size was relatively smaller than larger cardiac‐focused cohorts. Some patients had primarily CH‐related mutations. CH was defined as measurable mutated cellular clones, irrespective of whether these clones were related to the primary disease for which transplant was done or emerged post‐transplant. As in animal models, DNA (cytosine‐5)‐methyltransferase 3A did not associate with AF. 14 Circulating biomarkers were not consistently available, although the correlative between increased levels of interleukin‐6, a cytokine known to affect cardiotoxicity in patients not undergoing alloHSCT to increased AF risk following alloHSCT provides potential direction for future mechanistic studies focused on targetable pathways to cardiotoxicity. However, the strengths of our study include the use of CH mutation sequencing following alloHSCT and the real‐world representation of our study population. Finally, some out‐of‐hospital events may have gone uncaptured, despite extensive search.

Conclusions

AlloHSCT is associated with a significantly elevated risk of new‐onset AF, even after accounting for traditional risk and treatment‐related factors. Beyond traditional risk, the presence of multiple CH‐related mutations is associated with an increased risk of incident AF and worse survival outcomes. Given the anticipated increased use of alloHSCT, further research into the mechanisms and potential targets for the prevention of these cardiotoxic events are needed.
