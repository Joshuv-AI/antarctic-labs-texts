Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is a commonly used diagnostic tool for assessing the mediastinum for malignant lymphadenopathy; it has demonstrated efficacy and safety. Published studies report sensitivities of 92% to 95% for a diagnosis of metastatic malignant lymphadenopathy from solid tumors using 21- and 22-gauge endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) needles.1, 2, 3, 4 For other diagnoses, including benign conditions such sarcoidosis, the reported sensitivities are lower at 83.3% in 1 randomized controlled trial.5 For an initial diagnosis of lymphoma, a sensitivity of 67.1% has been reported, with a sensitivity for recurrent lymphoma of 77.8%, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis.6

Although previous studies have reported some advantages of 21- over 22-gauge needles,7, 8, 9 other studies have failed to consistently demonstrate a difference in diagnostic yield (DY).10, 11, 12, 13 Several studies have been published on the use of a 19-gauge EBUS needle; however, these did not demonstrate an improvement in the DY, nor has a randomized clinical trial.14, 15, 16, 17 Conversely, a meta-analysis including 65 studies conducted over the past 2 decades found that the 19-gauge needle had a higher sensitivity for diagnosing sarcoidosis.18

A previous study conducted by our group using propensity score matching (PSM) methodology did not identify an advantage of the 19-gauge EBUS needle over the 21-/22-gauge EBUS needles in terms of DY or sensitivity for sarcoidosis or lymphoma in patients with mediastinal lymphadenopathy.19 In this study, our objective was to update our prior analysis with an expanded number of participants included in the cohort, and explore the application of using both PSM and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to minimize selection bias.

Study Design and Methods

We analyzed cases from the Stather Canadian Outcomes Registry for Chest Procedures (SCOPE) database20 to compare outcomes of EBUS-TBNA. Specifically, we evaluated procedures performed with 19-gauge needles and compared them with those using 21- or 22-gauge needles using PSM and IPTW analyses. Additional information on the registry design has been published previously.20 The registry and study protocol was approved by the Calgary Health research ethics board (protocol No. REB15-2843).

We selected all procedures in the database with an operator-assigned preprocedural diagnosis of mediastinal adenopathy not yet diagnosed, suspected sarcoidosis, or suspected lymphoma. EBUS needle gauge (19, 21, or 22) was recorded as were data on relevant potentially confounding factors. Cases were excluded if > 1 needle type was used in a single lymph node station or if 30-day follow-up diagnostic results were missing.

EBUS-TBNA Procedure

Procedures were performed at each institution according to local procedures and standards. Procedures were performed with moderate sedation without the use of laryngeal mask airway or endotracheal tube for most cases. Choice of sedating agent, dose, needle gauge, number of needle passes per lymph node station, use of rapid on-site evaluation, and cytopathology processing procedure were determined by the bronchoscopist according to personal and local practice.

Procedural diagnostic results were assessed at approximately 30 days after the procedure with an electronic chart review by the bronchoscopist to determine the final diagnosis made (by any means). This included review of consultant notes and additional pathology results if further nonbronchoscopic biopsies were performed. Diagnostic results could be entered beyond the 30-day window as needed. In the case of lymphoma, a positive diagnosis was made if a definitive diagnosis of lymphoma (regardless of subtype) was reported by the interpreting pathologist. A final diagnosis of sarcoidosis was made if EBUS-TBNA samples or other biopsy specimens exhibited noncaseating granulomas in the absence of an alternative explanation based on coexisting histology, microbiology, or alternative final diagnosis at 30-day follow-up. In the case of other causes of mediastinal lymphadenopathy, a positive diagnosis was defined as a diagnostic cytopathologic pattern (eg, non-small cell lung cancer) or positive microbiology (eg, TB). Specimens were deemed adequate if a specific diagnosis was made based on the sample or if evidence of lymph nodes/lymphocytes were seen on cytopathologic analysis. Inadequate samples were defined as those without a specific diagnosis made on pathology and without evidence of lymph node tissue/lymphocytes on pathologic examination.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were described by means and SDs or medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were described by frequencies and percentages. Cases were separated into a 19-gauge needle cohort and a 21-/22-gauge cohort. Proportions between these 2 groups were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Means were compared using a t test, and medians were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. PSM and IPTW analyses were performed using Python packages scikit-learn 1.5.121 and stats models 0.14.2.22

The propensity score (ie, the probability of receiving the 19- or 21-/22-gauge needle) was estimated using logistic regression and included the following potential confounding variables: preprocedural diagnosis (mediastinal lymphadenopathy not yet diagnosed, suspected lymphoma, or suspected sarcoidosis), lymph node station sampled, maximum lymph node size, number of targets sampled, presence of rapid on-site evaluation presence of trainee, operator, patient age, and sex. Cases for analysis using PSM were selected with a 1:2 ratio of 19- vs 21-/22-gauge needles, using random matching with caliper size of 0.1 without replacement. IPTW was carried out by weighing cases using the inverse probability of receiving the treatment they received (ie, in the 19-gauge group, cases received a weight of 1/p, where p is the propensity score, and cases in the 21-/22-gauge group received a weight of 1/[1 – p]). Weights were stabilized using the marginal probability of treatment (ie, multiplying weights in the 19-gauge group by 195/1,810, and weights in the 21-/22-gauge group by 1,615/1,810). Stabilized weights were then truncated at the 1st and 99th percentile to limit the influence of very small or large weights with no additional cases excluded. An uncorrected χ2 statistic was used to compare the aforementioned outcome measures between the PSM groups.

The primary outcome was DY for EBUS-TBNA. Secondary outcomes included the following: specimen adequacy for the procedure, sensitivity for diagnosis of sarcoidosis, and sensitivity for lymphoma (both initial diagnosis, relapse, and Hodgkin lymphoma [HL], and non-Hodgkin lymphoma [NHL] subgroups].

DY was defined on a per-patient basis according to the pathology report and ancillary test results on the specimen, such as flow cytometry or culture. A test result was considered diagnostic if noncaseating granulomatous inflammation or another specific diagnosis (eg, cancer, TB) was reported.23 A finding of benign lymphocytes only was not considered diagnostic. In the case of sarcoidosis, sensitivity was defined as the number of cases demonstrating noncaseating granulomas on EBUS-TBNA/the total number of cases with a final diagnosis of sarcoidosis at 30-day follow-up. In the case of lymphoma, sensitivity was defined as the number of cases with a definitive diagnosis of lymphoma by the reporting pathologist on EBUS specimens/the total number of cases with a final diagnosis of lymphoma at 30-day follow-up. Proportions of initial diagnosis and relapse of lymphoma, and HL and NHL subgroups are reported; however, the case numbers are small.

An a priori sample size calculation was not performed because the date for database extraction was based on other administrative factors. Data from previous studies suggest the DY on a per-patient basis of EBUS-TBNA with a 21-gauge needle in a similar patient population is 70% to 90%.6 Based on a sample size of 150 experimental patients and 300 control patients (in line with the PSM groups), we would be able to detect DY difference among groups of 0.679 or 0.901, with probability (power) of 0.8 and alpha of .05.

Results

The initial data set was extracted from the SCOPE database on July 1, 2023, and included 1,810 procedures after excluding 44 cases where (1) data were missing, (2) both needle types were used, or (3) a 25-gauge needle was used. A total of 195 procedures in the 19-gauge group and 1,615 in the 21-/22-gauge group were included (Fig 1). The PSM included a total of 474 procedures (19-gauge group: n = 158; 21-/22-gauge group: n = 316); although all 19-gauge cases are included in the IPTW analysis, after weighing, the total sum of weights for the IPTW was 1,729 (sum of weights of 122 in the 19-gauge group and 1,606 in the 21-/22-gauge group). Specimen Adequacy

The overall specimen adequacy was 1,556 of 1,615 (96.3%) in the 21-/22-gauge group vs 185 of 195 (94.9%) in the 19-gauge group (1.4% difference; 95% CI, −1.1% to 5.5%; P = .33). For PSM, the specimen adequacy rate was 307 of 316 (96.5%) in the 21-/22-gauge group vs 150 of 158 (94.9%) in the 19-gauge group (difference, 2.3%; 95% CI, −1.2% to 7.1%; P = .20). For IPTW, the specimen adequacy rate was 1,549.9 of 1,606.7 (96.5%) in the 21-/22-gauge group vs 119.6 of 122.3 (97.8%) in the 19-gauge group (difference, 1.3%; 95% CI, −3.1% to 3.1%; P = .44) (Table 3).

Sarcoidosis/Noncaseating Granuloma

A total of 1,007 cases (100 in the 19-gauge group and 907 in the 21-/22-gauge group) received a final diagnosis of noncaseating granulomas (55.6% of the total) (Table 3).

A final diagnosis of noncaseating granulomas was made in 833 of 907 cases (91.8%) in the 21-/22-gauge group vs 94 of 100 cases (94.0%) in the 19-gauge group (2.2% difference; 95% CI, −4.4% to 5.9%; P = .44). For the PSM analysis, a final diagnosis of noncaseating granulomas was made in 154 of 163 cases (94.5%) in the 21-/22-gauge group vs 82 of 86 cases (95.3%) in the 19-gauge group (0.8% difference; 95% CI, −6.4% to 6.2%; P = .78). For the IPTW analysis, a final diagnosis of noncaseating granulomas was made in 820.9 of 891.5 cases (92.1%) in the 21-/22-gauge group vs 57.7 of 62.3 cases (92.6%) in the 19-gauge group (0.5% difference; 95% CI, −8.9% to 5.2%; P = .88) (Table 3).

In the 19-gauge group, 100 patients had a final diagnosis of noncaseating granulomas; of these, 94 were diagnosed via EBUS-TBNA, yielding a sensitivity of 94.0%. Discussion

This study updates our initial analysis of the SCOPE database comparing the diagnostic performance and sensitivity of EBUS-TBNA for mediastinal lymphadenopathy not yet diagnosed, sarcoidosis, and lymphoma with a 19-gauge needle vs smaller gauge needles (21 or 22 gauge). The initial study included 138 19-gauge procedures and a trend toward improved DY. We aimed to reanalyze the SCOPE data set with a higher number of 19-gauge procedures (n = 195). We also analyzed the data using 2 different propensity matching methods: PSM and IPTW. This study supports our initial findings of no difference in DY nor specimen adequacy between the 19-gauge versus smaller gauge needles. We observed no difference in the sensitivity for sarcoidosis nor lymphoma between the 19-gauge needle and the 21-/22-gauge needles. The observed sensitivity for both needles for sarcoidosis and lymphoma was at the upper limit of what has previously been described.7,13,15,24 We did however observe a significant difference in the sensitivity for lymphoma when analyzing the data using IPTW, with the 19-gauge group counterintuitively having inferior results. The lack of clear advantage of the 19-gauge device is relevant given the higher cost of these needles and the apparent increased bleeding rate noted in our data. There are several possible reasons for our findings. First, increasing the number of participants may not permit detection of a difference as small as 5% to 10%. A yet larger sample size is likely needed to rule out a difference of that magnitude. Second, PSM captures only measured confounders, and it is possible that physicians selected a needle based on unmeasured factors. For example, the perception that a particular case may result in a lower-than-expected outcome may influence the operator’s inclination to use a 19-gauge needle in more difficult scenarios. Third, it is possible that analyzing smaller specimen samples may be sufficient to obtain a dichotomous result of diagnostic or nondiagnostic samples, despite evidence showing that larger needles yield samples with greater cellularity and size.25,26 This is supported by the very high sample adequacy rates (> 90%) observed for both groups, which we also found in our prior study.19 The lower sensitivity for lymphoma observed with the 19-gauge needle in the IPTW analysis is an unexpected result which should be interpreted with caution. The 19-gauge needles were used in a small number of lymphoma cases. The 19-gauge needle may have been selected for challenging cases with lower expected DYs. Residual confounding may persist despite adjustment, and IPTW may upweight a small subset of 19-gauge cases with false-negative results. This combination of nonrandom needle selection and a limited lymphoma sample make the weighted estimates particularly unstable. Furthermore, although 19-gauge needles may obtain larger samples, this does not necessarily translate into better preservation of nodal architecture or less blood contamination, both of which are important for lymphoma diagnosis. Taken together with the absence of any clear disadvantage of 19-gauge needles in overall DY, specimen adequacy, or sensitivity for a diagnosis of sarcoidosis, the lower weighted sensitivity for lymphoma most likely reflects methodologic and case-mix effects rather than true inferiority of the 19-gauge needle.

The current study has several strengths. First, we compared the 19-gauge and 21-/22-gauge needles using a multicenter registry reflective of real-world practice. Second, we used both PSM and IPTW, which allow assessment of the outcomes under different assumptions and sample size. Third, we increased the number of participants with a design focusing on sarcoidosis and lymphoma, diagnoses where EBUS results have been less favorable than for epithelial malignancies. Finally, to our knowledge, this sample of 195 procedures/patients undergoing EBUS-TBNA with a 19-gauge needle is 1 of the largest published studies on this topic.

The current study also has several limitations. First, as previously discussed, we anticipate considerable selection bias in needle size choice; however, PSM and IPTW were used to adjust for measured confounders in this database of sequentially recorded cases. Unmeasured factors may influence needle selection. Other procedural factors, such as the number of needles passes per lymph node station or the number of stations sampled, were not standardized but rather left at the discretion of the bronchoscopist. Second, we used the diagnosis of sarcoidosis at follow-up by an expert clinician as the gold standard. Some of the patients with a final diagnosis of sarcoidosis may have alternative diagnoses. Finally, the current study, like our previous study,19 may still have too few participants to detect small but potentially clinically significant differences in performance, particularly in subgroups such as lymphoma. Future studies should ideally be prospective and randomized with an adequate sample size to detect relatively small differences (5%-10%) in diagnostic results.

Interpretation

Use of a 19-gauge needle did not improve the diagnostic performance of EBUS-TBNA in patients with suspected sarcoidosis, lymphoma, or mediastinal lymphadenopathy compared with the 21- or 22-gauge needles in this updated analysis using 2 different propensity matching methods and increased participant numbers. Our paper suggests that when considering needle selection, operators may wish to consider factors other than diagnostic performance alone.
