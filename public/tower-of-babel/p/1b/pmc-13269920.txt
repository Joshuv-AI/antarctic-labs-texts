SLC25A1 upregulation promotes HNSCC cisplatin resistance via H3K27ac-mediated cellular senescence
Authors: Jing Li, Tao Yang, Yun Li, Xinjie Yang, Yaodong He, Yanbing Yao, Shengnan Zhao, Rong Liu, Zihui Yang, Hongxin Zhang, Qian Wang, Huan Li, Jianhua Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemoresistance to cisplatin is a major contributor to the progression of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC); however, the mechanisms underlying cisplatin resistance in HNSCC remain incompletely understood. Dysregulation of metabolite transport between mitochondria and the cytoplasm, mediated by solute carrier family 25 (SLC25), is closely associated with tumor progression. Solute carrier family 25 member 1 (SLC25A1) promotes malignant phenotypes in various cancers; however, its role in HNSCC remains unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that SLC25A1 is overexpressed in HNSCC and is closely associated with poor prognosis. SLC25A1 upregulation promotes cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells. Moreover, SLC25A1 enhances cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells by inducing cellular senescence. Mechanistically, SLC25A1 upregulates the expression of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 through histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation–mediated transcriptional activation. Furthermore, SLC25A1 interacts with HSPD1, a mitochondrial chaperonin protein, via its C-terminal region to increase citrate transport and cytosolic acetyl-CoA levels. Treatment with CTPI-2, a specific inhibitor of SLC25A1, exhibits therapeutic effects against cisplatin-resistant HNSCC. These findings establish SLC25A1 as a key regulator of cisplatin resistance in HNSCC, suggesting that it serves as both a predictive biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in chemoresistant HNSCC. From a translational perspective, these results support CTPI-2 as a promising therapeutic agent for overcoming cisplatin resistance.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) represents a major global public health burden characterized by a high mortality rate, with a particularly elevated prevalence in East and Southeast Asia1. Despite significant advances in the diagnosis and treatment of HNSCC, the processes driving tumor progression, particularly those associated with cisplatin resistance, remain poorly understood. Several signaling pathways, including the AKT/PI3K pathway, the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway, the JAK/STAT pathway, as well as cancer stem cells, have been implicated in the development of cisplatin resistance in HNSCC2,3; however, therapeutic agents targeting these pathways have demonstrated limited clinical benefit or have been associated with substantial toxicity. Consequently, there is an urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the malignant behavior of HNSCC to identify novel therapeutic strategies.

Cellular senescence is a state of irreversible growth arrest associated with the upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors (primarily p16 and p21), structural alterations, metabolic reprogramming, persistent DNA damage response, and increased secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors4,5. Cellular senescence was initially regarded as an effective antitumor mechanism. However, accumulating evidence indicates that senescent cancer cells may exert detrimental effects on antitumor therapy, including the exacerbation of adverse side effects, promotion of chemotherapy resistance, and association with poor prognosis4,6,7. For instance, persistent SASP in chemotherapy-treated cancer cells accelerates inflammation, thereby promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and facilitating chemotherapy resistance and tumor metastasis8. In addition, cancer cells that escape senescence may acquire stem-like properties that replenish the tumor population, ultimately leading to cancer recurrence. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

Mitochondrial citrate dysregulation contributes to tumor progression through metabolic reprogramming and metabolite-mediated signal transduction9,10. The key gene, solute carrier family 25 member 1 (SLC25A1), encodes a mitochondrial solute carrier protein that regulates citrate transport across mitochondrial membranes. Mutations in this gene are associated with combined D-2- and L-2-hydroxyglutaric aciduria11. SLC25A1 promotes tumorigenesis and tumor progression in pancreatic cancer12, colorectal cancer13, nasopharyngeal cancer14 through the orchestration of lipid metabolic reprogramming. In addition, SLC25A1 enhances cancer stemness and chemoresistance in non-small cell lung cancer by maintaining redox homeostasis15. Furthermore, the role of SLC25A1 in cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA production has been shown to regulate breast cancer progression through epigenetic histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac)–mediated estrogen receptor expression16. Although the biological functions and mechanisms of SLC25A1 in the malignant phenotypes of several cancer types have been elucidated, its specific role in HNSCC malignancy remains unclear.

In the present study, we systematically assessed the expression level and oncological significance of SLC25A1 in cisplatin-resistant HNSCC and elucidated the underlying mechanisms using multiple experimental approaches. Specifically, we demonstrate that SLC25A1 overexpression enhances cisplatin resistance in HNSCC through H3K27ac-mediated cellular senescence, thereby providing evidence of a mechanistic link between cellular senescence and cisplatin resistance. Moreover, targeting SLC25A1 with CTPI-2 offers a potential therapeutic strategy for HNSCC.

Results

SLC25A1 was frequently upregulated in HNSCC tissues

First, we compared the mRNA expression level of SLC25A1 in HNSCC tissues with that in peritumoral tissues using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-HNSCC) data and five Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE30784, GSE33232, GSE59102, GSE83519, and GSE107591). In addition, the GSE136037 dataset, which includes metastatic tumor samples, was analyzed to compare SLC25A1 mRNA expression between primary and metastatic tumors. SLC25A1 expression was significantly higher in HNSCC tissues than in peritumoral tissues, and its mRNA level was elevated in metastatic lesions compared with primary lesions (Fig. 1A–C). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot (WB) assays were performed to evaluate SLC25A1 expression in collected tissue samples (n = 12). SLC25A1 expression was increased in tumor tissues relative to peritumoral tissues (Fig. 1D, E). Compared with HaCaT cells (an oral mucosal cell line), SLC25A1 was overexpressed in nine HNSCC cell lines (Fig. 1F, G). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of 116 patient samples further confirmed the upregulation of SLC25A1 relative to peritumoral tissues (Fig. 1H). Survival analyses indicated that overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were significantly lower in patients with high SLC25A1 expression than in those with low expression (Fig. 1I). SLC25A1 enhances cancer stemness of HNSCC cells

To elucidate the role of SLC25A1 in HNSCC malignancy, we performed a series of cellular assays. SLC25A1 knockdown or overexpression did not affect cell proliferation, as determined by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and colony formation assays (Figure S1). Given the established role of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in tumor progression and the reported function of SLC25A1 in promoting cancer stemness in liver and lung cancers15,17, we investigated the association between SLC25A1 expression and the levels of CSC-related markers (EPCAM, SALL4, and SOX4). SLC25A1 expression positively correlated with the expression of EPCAM, SALL4, and SOX4 (Fig. 2A). IHC analysis revealed that the proportion of SLC25A1-positive cases was 77.6% among EPCAM-positive patients, 73.3% among SALL4-positive patients, and 68.1% among SOX4-positive patients (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, EPCAM was highly expressed in oncospheres derived from SLC25A1-positive cells isolated from patient-derived HNSCC samples, indicating a close association between SLC25A1 expression and CSC markers in HNSCC (Fig. 2C). Based on the strong link between cancer stemness and chemotherapy resistance, we further examined the role of SLC25A1 in CSC maintenance and cisplatin resistance. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that EPCAM, a recognized CSC biomarker, was highly expressed in CSCs isolated from CAL27 cells and cisplatin-resistant CAL27CDDP cells (Fig. 2D), suggesting that SLC25A1 may contribute to HNSCC stemness and cisplatin resistance. The efficiency of SLC25A1 downregulation in CAL27CDDP cells was confirmed by qRT-PCR and WB analyses (Figure S2A–B). Sphere formation and Transwell invasion assays were conducted to evaluate the malignant potential of SLC25A1 in HNSCC cells. SLC25A1 knockdown impaired CSC maintenance and invasion in CAL27CDDP cells, whereas SLC25A1 overexpression enhanced CSC maintenance and invasion in CAL27 cells compared with the control group (Fig. 2E, F). Additionally, the expression levels of several CSC-associated markers were reduced in SLC25A1-deficient CAL27CDDP cells, whereas SLC25A1 upregulation produced the opposite effect, indicating that SLC25A1 promotes cancer stemness (Fig. 2G). A limiting dilution xenograft assay in nude mice demonstrated that CAL27CDDP cells with SLC25A1 downregulation exhibited reduced tumorigenic capacity compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2H). SLC25A1 increases the cisplatin chemoresistance of HNSCC cells

Given the critical role of cancer stemness in promoting cisplatin resistance2, we investigated whether SLC25A1 contributes to cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells. To evaluate the clinical relevance of SLC25A1 in HNSCC chemoresistance, 48 paired resected tumor samples from patients with HNSCC were collected before and after chemotherapy (Table S4). Compared with their matched counterparts, tumors from patients who did not respond to chemotherapy exhibited elevated SLC25A1 expression (Fig. 3A). To further examine the role of SLC25A1 in chemoresistance, we established cisplatin-resistant HNSCC cells derived from parental CAL27 cells, characterized by the upregulation of P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1), designated as CAL27CDDP cells (Figure S3). Using CAL27CDDP cells with SLC25A1 knockdown, as described in Figures S2A, B, we evaluated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values (Figure S2C). SLC25A1-deficient CAL27CDDP cells treated with cisplatin exhibited reduced resistance and increased apoptosis (Fig. 3B, C). Restoration of SLC25A1 expression through ectopic overexpression largely attenuated the response to cisplatin chemotherapy (Fig. 3D, E). To investigate the function of SLC25A1 in vivo, shCtrl-treated and SLC25A1-knockdown CAL27CDDP cells were subcutaneously xenografted into BALB/c nude mice, followed by cisplatin treatment. Tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in mice bearing SLC25A1-deficient xenografts treated with cisplatin compared with those in mice bearing shCtrl xenografts treated with or without chemotherapy (Fig. 3F, G). Furthermore, SLC25A1 knockdown combined with chemotherapy resulted in a decreased proportion of Ki-67–positive cells and an increased proportion of cleaved caspase-3–positive cells relative to shCtrl tumors (Fig. 3H). SLC25A1 promotes HNSCC cell chemoresistance by enhancing cellular senescence

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the role of SLC25A1 in promoting chemoresistance, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of CAL27CDDP cells with SLC25A1 knockdown and corresponding control cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed a close association between SLC25A1 and cellular senescence (Fig. 4A). We subsequently evaluated the effects of SLC25A1 on cellular senescence in CAL27CDDP cells. Compared with shCtrl cells, SLC25A1 knockdown significantly reduced the proportion of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal)–positive cells (Fig. 4B), indicating that SLC25A1 enhances senescence-associated resistance to cisplatin in HNSCC. Additional senescence-associated phenotypes, including markers of cell cycle arrest (CDK inhibitors p21 and p16) and the SASP, were assessed by qRT-PCR, WB, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analyses. The expression levels of p16 and p21 were lower in shSLC25A1-transfected CAL27CDDP cells than in shCtrl cells (Fig. 4C, D). SASP analysis demonstrated that the mRNA levels of interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8, as well as their corresponding secreted protein levels in the culture medium, were also reduced in the shSLC25A1 CAL27CDDP group compared with the shCtrl group (Fig. 4E, F). Furthermore, compared with parental CAL27 cells, CAL27CDDP cells exhibited increased SA-β-Gal positivity, elevated p16 and p21 expression, and enhanced SASP-associated production of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 (Figure S4). Furthermore, among patients who did not respond to cisplatin chemotherapy, higher expression levels of p16 and p21 were observed in postchemotherapy samples compared with prechemotherapy samples (Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that cellular senescence may contribute to SLC25A1-mediated cisplatin resistance in HNSCC. To further demonstrate that SLC25A1 regulates cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells through enhanced cellular senescence, we employed tryptanthrin (TRYP), a known inducer of hepatocellular carcinoma cell senescence18, to determine whether TRYP could rescue the reduction in cellular senescence caused by SLC25A1 downregulation. We first evaluated the senescence-inducing effects of TRYP in CAL27CDDP cells. As shown in Figure S5, SA-β-Gal staining, cell cycle arrest analysis, and SASP characterization demonstrated that TRYP induced senescence in CAL27CDDP cells in a dose-dependent manner. TRYP rescue experiments combined with SA-β-Gal staining revealed that TRYP treatment significantly increased the proportion of senescent cells reduced by SLC25A1 knockdown in CAL27CDDP cells (Fig. 4H). Moreover, TRYP treatment significantly restored the decreases in p16 and p21 expression, as well as SASP production, induced by SLC25A1 knockdown in CAL27CDDP cells, suggesting that cellular senescence contributes to SLC25A1 upregulation–mediated cisplatin resistance in HNSCC (Fig. 4I–L).Fig. 4SLC25A1 upregulation promotes senescence in HNSCC cells.A KEGG pathway analysis was performed to identify SLC25A1-related signaling pathways in CAL27CDDP cells based on RNA-seq data following SLC25A1 knockdown (n = 3 per group). SLC25A1 mediates cellular senescence via H3K27ac-induced gene expression

To elucidate the mechanisms through which SLC25A1 induces senescence in CAL27CDDP cells, we first identified potential target genes regulated by SLC25A1 by intersecting data from the TCGA-HNSCC dataset (correlation coefficient with SLC25A1 > 0.3), the GSE30784 dataset (correlation coefficient > 0.4), and genes downregulated in shSLC25A1 RNA-sequencing data (|fold change (FC)| > 1.5 and P < 0.05). The associations between mRNA expression levels and overall survival were subsequently analyzed. RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 were identified as candidate downstream effectors modulated by SLC25A1, owing to their established associations with cellular senescence19–21(Fig. 5A). Next, we examined the effects of downregulating these three genes on HNSCC cell senescence and cisplatin resistance. As shown in Figure S6, knockdown of each gene significantly suppressed senescence-associated phenotypes and cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells. Notably, a recent study reported that CDC45 can induce replication stress, which is a critical driver of cellular senescence22. Therefore, we investigated whether downregulation of these three genes could influence DNA replication stress. As shown in Figure S7, the expression levels of γH2AX and phosphorylated RPA2, key regulators of replication stress, were reduced following silencing of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 in CAL27CDDP cells, respectively, suggesting that these candidate genes may promote cellular senescence by inducing replication stress. Additionally, significant correlations between SLC25A1 mRNA levels and those of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 in the TCGA-HNSCC and GSE30784 datasets are shown in Figures S8A, B. Consistently, SLC25A1 knockdown in CAL27CDDP cells reduced the expression of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1, whereas SLC25A1 overexpression in CAL27 cells increased their expression levels (Fig. 5B–E). Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial citrate efflux regulated by SLC25A1 supplies a cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool and induces H3K27ac signaling. Therefore, we investigated whether SLC25A1 promotes senescence in HNSCC cells through the enhancement of H3K27ac signaling. CAL27CDDP cells were treated with C646, a selective histone acetyltransferase inhibitor, to examine changes in the expression of the aforementioned genes. As shown in Figures S8C, D, C646 treatment markedly reduced H3K27ac levels as well as the expression of the three target genes. Similarly, C646 treatment attenuated the upregulation of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 induced by SLC25A1 overexpression in CAL27 cells (Fig. 5F, G). We further explored the specific regulatory regions of these three genes associated with enhanced H3K27ac using H3K27ac chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data from seven HNSCC cell lines available in the Cistrome Data Browser (Figure S8E). In addition, ChIP-qPCR analyses in CAL27CDDP and CAL27 cells demonstrated that RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 are transcriptionally activated by H3K27ac (Fig. 5H). Given the critical roles of p16 and p21 in senescence, we also assessed whether the SLC25A1–H3K27ac axis regulates the transcription of p16 and p21. The results showed that SLC25A1 upregulation or downregulation did not alter H3K27ac enrichment at the promoter regions of p16 and p21 (Figure S9A). Consistently, Cistrome Data Browser analysis revealed no significant enrichment of H3K27ac at the promoter regions of p16 and p21 (Figure S9B). These findings indicate that the SLC25A1–H3K27ac axis does not directly regulate p16 and p21 expression. Furthermore, the expression levels of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 were higher in postchemotherapy samples than in prechemotherapy samples among patients who did not respond to cisplatin chemotherapy (Fig. 5I). To elucidate how SLC25A1 maintains cytosolic acetyl-CoA levels to enhance H3K27ac signaling, immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry analyses were performed (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed the mitochondrial colocalization of SLC25A1 and HSPD1 (Fig. 6E). CTPI-2 could inhibit HNSCC cell malignancies in vitro and in vivo

CTPI-2 is a selective antagonist of SLC25A1 and has been shown to alleviate hepatic steatosis24. To evaluate the potential therapeutic effects of CTPI-2 in HNSCC, we assessed its impact on malignant phenotypes both in vitro and in vivo. First, we determined the IC50 of CTPI-2 in CAL27CDDP cells (Figure S10A). CTPI-2 treatment significantly increased apoptosis in CAL27CDDP cells, with or without concurrent cisplatin administration (Fig. 7A and Figure S10B). Treatment with CTPI-2 markedly reduced the number of CAL27CDDP oncospheres, invasive cells, and senescent cells (Figure S10C–E). Moreover, CTPI-2 in combination with cisplatin further suppressed malignant phenotypes, including oncosphere formation, cellular invasion, and senescence in CAL27CDDP cells (Fig. 7B–D). In vivo, CTPI-2 treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in xenografted nude mice compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 7E–G). As shown in the lower panel of Fig. 7E, CTPI-2 combined with cisplatin further inhibited tumor growth. Additionally, CTPI-2-treated mice exhibited lower proportions of Ki-67–positive cells and higher proportions of cleaved caspase-3–positive cells compared with control mice (Fig. 7H). No evident tissue or organ damage was observed in the CTPI-2 combination treatment group (Figure S11). Discussion

Cisplatin resistance is a major contributor to the low overall survival rate observed in patients with HNSCC; however, the mechanisms underlying chemoresistance and disease progression remain incompletely understood. In the present study, we investigated the role of SLC25A1 in mediating cisplatin resistance in HNSCC. Compared with peritumoral tissues, SLC25A1 was upregulated in tumor tissues, and its elevated expression was closely associated with poor prognosis in patients with HNSCC. Further analyses revealed that SLC25A1 enhances resistance to cisplatin therapy and promotes cancer stemness both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, SLC25A1 overexpression contributes to cisplatin resistance by promoting H3K27ac-mediated transactivation of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1, ultimately leading to cancer cell senescence. Moreover, CTPI-2, an inhibitor of SLC25A1, attenuated malignant phenotypes in HNSCC cells, indicating its potential as a therapeutic agent for patients with HNSCC.

Previous studies have explored the oncogenic role of SLC25A1 in various diseases. Tan et al. demonstrated that SLC25A1 upregulation accelerates hepatic steatosis, glucose intolerance, and inflammatory macrophage infiltration24. In pancreatic and colorectal cancer, SLC25A1 overexpression promotes tumorigenesis by enhancing de novo lipogenesis through mitochondrial citrate efflux12,13. Additionally, SLC25A1 facilitates the maintenance of redox homeostasis in cancer cells, thereby enabling evasion of cytotoxic effects and resistance to irradiation or anti–epidermal growth factor receptor therapies15,25. In liver cancer, parthenolide, a novel SLC25A1 inhibitor, suppresses stemness maintenance by inducing metabolic vulnerability17. Furthermore, mitochondrial citrate efflux supplies the cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool and activates downstream signaling cascades. Under serine starvation conditions, estrogen receptor (ER) signaling is impaired due to SLC25A1 downregulation–mediated reductions in nucleocytosolic acetyl-CoA levels, leading to decreased H3K27ac-induced ERα expression16. Similarly, the present study demonstrated SLC25A1-mediated H3K27ac epigenetic regulation in cisplatin-resistant HNSCC cells, suggesting the key role of SLC25A1 in modulating the nucleocytosolic acetyl-CoA content. Besides, HSPD1, the newly identified interacting protein of SLC25A1 in this study, unveiled a novel mechanism enrolled in citrate efflux, which may also participate in lipid metabolism and acetyl-CoA fueling in the progression of other diseases.

Given the role of SLC25A1 in mitochondrial metabolism, its involvement in cell death warrants further discussion. Dysregulated SLC25A1 has been reported to participate in multiple forms of cell death, including apoptosis17, necroptosis26 and ferroptosis27. Notably, Li et al. demonstrated that SLC25A1 suppresses ferroptosis by sustaining the cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool, further supporting its regulatory role in acetyl-CoA metabolic flux. In addition, recent studies have shown that supplementation with α-ketoglutarate, a precursor of D-2-hydroxyglutarate and a regulator of cuproptosis, sensitizes lung cancer cells with SLC25A1 inhibition to radiotherapy25,28,29, suggesting a potential link between SLC25A1 dysregulation and the cuproptosis process.

Cancer progression is driven by multiple factors that contribute to cisplatin resistance and cancer stemness maintenance. Cellular senescence is defined as a terminal growth arrest state associated with the upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors (primarily p16 and p21), structural alterations, metabolic reprogramming, and persistent DNA damage response4,5. The role of cancer cell senescence remains controversial30. As a common outcome of anticancer therapies, the accumulation and persistence of senescent cancer cells induced by treatment can promote metastasis, recurrence, and various adverse effects in patients30,31. Moreover, chemotherapy-induced senescent cancer cells exhibit a distinct phenotype characterized by the coexistence of stem-like and senescent features, which may contribute to therapeutic resistance and tumor repopulation following treatment32–34. In the present study, we observed that SLC25A1 simultaneously confers stemness maintenance and senescence-associated characteristics in HNSCC cells, supporting the hypothesis that chemoresistant HNSCC cells acquire senescence-associated stemness properties, as previously reported in colon cancer and esophageal adenocarcinoma32,35. Consistently, Milanovic et al. demonstrated that senescent cancer cells exhibit greater tumor-initiating potential than non-senescent cells exposed to chemotherapy4. Notably, therapy-induced senescent stem-like cancer cells display enhanced malignant traits, contributing to tumor invasiveness and metastasis36–38, suggesting that targeting these senescent stem-like cells may suppress tumor progression.

In addition, p53 signaling, which regulates p21 expression, contributes to cancer cell senescence. In a previous editorial comment, Dr. Chris Albanese proposed that SLC25A1 may function as a central hub at the intersection of multiple signaling pathways altered by mutant p5339. In the present study, SLC25A1 upregulation accelerated senescence in cisplatin-resistant HNSCC cells, providing evidence that senescent cells enhance chemoresistance and stemness maintenance and suggesting a potential interaction between SLC25A1 and p53/p21 signaling in cancer cells. Importantly, numerous studies have demonstrated that persistent SASP in chemotherapy-treated cancer cells promotes the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and proangiogenic factors, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that enhances chemotherapy resistance and tumor metastasis8,40. Our findings showed that SLC25A1 upregulation increased IL-1α, IL-6, and IL-8 levels, supporting SASP formation during the development of cisplatin resistance in HNSCC and thereby favoring SASP-dependent therapeutic resistance. Nevertheless, further investigations at the level of the tumor microenvironment are warranted. Moreover, the combination of MEK and CDK4/6 inhibitors has been shown to induce cellular senescence, while the SASP contributes to angiogenesis and enhances sensitivity to immunotherapy in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma41. Wang et al. proposed that the dual effects of cancer cell senescence on tumor progression may depend on specific biological contexts and cancer-related features30. Therefore, further investigations using lineage tracing, cell interaction analyses, and related approaches are required to elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

The present study identified RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 as SLC25A1-associated genes involved in H3K27ac-mediated senescence in HNSCC cells. RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 contribute to tumor progression and therapeutic resistance through their roles in cell cycle regulation42–45. It is therefore reasonable to speculate that these genes may participate in HNSCC cell senescence due to their involvement in cell cycle control. Our results provide evidence that RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 promote cellular senescence, consistent with previous reports19–21. Moreover, replication stress is widely recognized as a key driver of cellular senescence46,47. Our findings demonstrated that RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 can independently induce DNA replication stress, supporting recent evidence of CDC45’s role in promoting replication stress22. At the same time, given that our results did not demonstrate direct transcriptional regulation of p16 and p21 by the SLC25A1–H3K27ac axis, we propose that p16 and p21 may function within parallel pathways that reinforce senescence or may be indirectly regulated by downstream targets of the SLC25A1–H3K27ac axis or other regulatory factors. Notably, a recent study reported that a cluster of enhancers regulates INK4/ARF promoters independently of H3K27ac enrichment, which is consistent with our findings48. Recent studies have also demonstrated strong associations between cellular senescence and these three genes19–21. Furthermore, RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 may contribute to cancer stemness maintenance49–51. However, the roles of these genes in regulating both cell cycle progression and senescence appear paradoxical. In our view, this discrepancy may be attributed to differences in experimental context, including cancer type and pathological characteristics, which warrant further investigation. Additionally, the specific roles of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 in HNSCC tumor initiation and progression remain largely undefined and require systematic evaluation.

CTPI-2 is a selective inhibitor of SLC25A1 that has been shown to alleviate hepatic steatosis24. Previous studies have suggested that CTPI-2 treatment suppresses the maintenance of HNSCC stemness17. However, its effects on broader malignant phenotypes in HNSCC remain unclear. The present study demonstrated that CTPI-2 markedly attenuates cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells in vitro. In addition, CTPI-2 treatment at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in HNSCC xenograft models, supporting its therapeutic potential. Furthermore, our findings revealed that combined intraperitoneal administration of CTPI-2 and cisplatin produced synergistic anticancer effects without evident toxicity. Nevertheless, further detailed pharmacological investigations are required.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that upregulation of SLC25A1 enhances cisplatin resistance in HNSCC cells by promoting H3K27ac-mediated transactivation of RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1, thereby increasing cancer cell senescence. Moreover, CTPI-2 treatment significantly reduces malignant phenotypes in HNSCC both in vitro and in vivo. These findings suggest that SLC25A1 functions as a key regulator of cisplatin resistance in HNSCC and that targeting SLC25A1 with CTPI-2 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming cisplatin resistance.

Methods

Data resources

Gene expression datasets (GSE30784, GSE33232, GSE59102, GSE83519, GSE107591, and GSE136037) were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus database. Relevant platforms and sample information are summarized in Table S1.

Evaluation of SLC25A1 expression and survival correlation

HNSCC mRNA expression data from TCGA-HNSCC were analyzed using the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) platform, which includes 522 tumor samples and 44 peritumoral samples. SLC25A1 expression data from GEO datasets were used to compare mRNA expression levels between tumor and peritumoral specimens. The association between SLC25A1 expression and survival outcomes in the TCGA-HNSCC cohort was further evaluated using the GSCA platform52.

Cell line culture and tissue collection

The HaCaT human oral mucosal cell line was purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS). The FaDu, WSU-HN6, WSU-HN30, SCC-1, SCC-9, and SCC25 human HNSCC cell lines (Procell Biotech, Wuhan, China), the SAS cell line (Cobioer Biotech, Nanjing, China), and the HSC3 cell line (Jinyuan Biotech, Shanghai, China) were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% PS. Cisplatin-resistant CAL27CDDP cells, with an approximate resistance index of six, were established from parental CAL27 cells (Jinyuan Biotech, Shanghai, China) using a stepwise exposure method and were maintained in complete medium containing 150 ng/mL cisplatin. Specifically, the cisplatin concentration was initiated at 0.4 μg/mL and increased after every two consecutive passages by a two-fold increment until the cells stabilized and proliferated consistently at a final concentration of 4.0 μg/mL. Resistance was confirmed by an approximate resistance index of six and elevated expression levels of P-gp and MRP1. Specific information on the cell lines is provided in Table S2. In the present study, 116 paired HNSCC tumor and peritumoral samples, as well as 48 paired resected HNSCC tumor samples obtained before and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, were collected at the School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University. Sample collection was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University (Permission Number: KQ-YY-2025-011) (Tables S3–S4) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients’ responses to cisplatin chemotherapy were evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1. Based on post-treatment imaging, responders (R) were defined as patients achieving a partial response or better, whereas non-responders were defined as patients exhibiting stable disease or progressive disease. All patients in the 48-patient cohort were diagnosed with locally advanced HNSCC (M0) and received cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Prechemotherapy and postchemotherapy specimens were collected by biopsy and surgery, respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from human HNSCC tissues and cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 15596018CN, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissues or cell pellets were homogenized in TRIzol, followed by phase separation with chloroform. RNA was precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in RNase-free water. RNA concentration and purity were determined by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Cat. No. RR047A, Takara, Japan) in a 20 μL reaction volume. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Cat. No. RR820A, Takara, Japan) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) with a standard 20 μL reaction volume. Gene-specific primer sequences are listed in Table S5. The geometric mean of two stable reference genes, GAPDH and β-actin, was used for normalization. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the stable reference gene β-actin.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described53. Briefly, cells or homogenized tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Switzerland) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. A total of 40 μg of protein was separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies (listed in Table S6) diluted in blocking buffer. After washing, the membranes were incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were normalized to β-actin as an internal loading control.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry

H&E staining was performed to assess the histopathological characteristics of xenograft tumors. IHC analysis was conducted on 5 μm HNSCC tissue sections using a commercial IHC kit (Cat. No. KIT-5030, MXB Biotech, China). The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S6. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded xenograft tumor tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. For H&E staining, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and stained with hematoxylin (Beyotime, China) for 5 min followed by eosin (Beyotime, China) for 2 min according to standard protocols. The stained sections were subsequently dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam.

For IHC, tissue sections underwent antigen retrieval by heating in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0). After quenching endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min and blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies (listed in Table S6). The following day, sections were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody and subsequently with streptavidin-HRP using a commercial IHC kit (Cat. No. KIT-5030, MXB Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Diaminobenzidine was used as the chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The final IHC score was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score by the proportion score, yielding a scale from 0 to 12.

Knockdown and forced expression of target genes

Short hairpin RNA sequences targeting SLC25A1 were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Ltd., and the coding sequences of SLC25A1 were inserted into the pLKO.1 empty vector or the pLVX vector, respectively. The constructed vectors were cotransfected with pVSV-G, pLP1, and pLP2 into 293 T cells using Lipo2000 transfection reagent (Cat. No. 11668019; Invitrogen, USA). HNSCC cells were subsequently infected with lentiviruses in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Cat. No. E1299; Selleck, USA). Following transduction, HNSCC cells were treated with 2 μg/mL puromycin for one week to establish stable cell lines. All relevant sequences are listed in Table S7.

Cell proliferation, apoptosis and invasion assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 assay (Cat. No. HY-K0301; MCE, USA). Briefly, HNSCC cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium. After cell attachment, the cells were subjected to the designated treatments. At the indicated time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the control group (set as 100%). All experiments were performed with at least six replicates per condition.

Cell apoptosis was evaluated by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Cat. No. 40302ES60; Yeasen, China). After treatment, both adherent and floating cells were collected, washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of the cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL of propidium iodide solution in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Then, 400 µL of 1× binding buffer was added, and the samples were analyzed within 1 h using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCanto II). Data were processed using FlowJo software.

A Transwell Matrigel invasion assay was performed to assess the invasive capacity of HNSCC cells using a 24-well Transwell chamber system (8.0 µm pore size; Corning, USA) pre-coated with Matrigel (Cat. No. 356234, BD Biosciences)54. Briefly, Matrigel was diluted in cold serum-free medium at a 1:8 ratio, and 100 µL was added to the upper chamber and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 2 h. HNSCC cells (5 × 104), resuspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium, were seeded into the upper chamber, while 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 48 h at 37 °C, non-invading cells on the upper surface were gently removed. Invaded cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, and rinsed with PBS. The number of invaded cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Sphere formation assay

HNSCC cells cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) medium, as well as freshly isolated human HNSCC cells, were supplemented with 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (Cat. No. 10605-HNAE; Sino Biological, China), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (Cat. No. 10014-HNAE; Sino Biological, China), B-27 supplement (1:50; Cat. No. 17504044; Invitrogen, USA), and 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Cat. No. ST023; Beyotime, China) and seeded into ultralow-attachment plates for 7 days. The resulting oncospheres were subsequently subjected to immunoblotting or immunofluorescence analyses.

In vivo assays for tumor growth assessment

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Fourth Military Medical University (Permit No. KQ-2025-024) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male BALB/c nude mice (6-week-old, 18–22 g) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity (50 ± 10%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to autoclaved food and water. Mice were randomized into experimental groups using a computer-generated table, and all procedures and outcome assessments were performed blinded to group allocation. Mice were monitored daily for signs of distress, with predefined humane endpoints. At study completion, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with AVMA guidelines.

For the subcutaneous xenograft tumor formation assay, 1 × 107 CAL27CDDP-shSLC25A1 cells were injected subcutaneously into the mice. Tumor volume (mm3) was measured every 5 days, after which the mice were sacrificed. Ki-67 staining and cleaved caspase-3 staining were used to assess cellular proliferation and apoptosis, respectively.

To evaluate the effects of CTPI-2 (Cat. No. HY-123986; MCE, USA) on tumor growth, 6-week-old male nude mice were used to establish an in vivo xenograft tumor model. CTPI-2 at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight or cisplatin at a dose of 25 mg/kg body weight was administered via intraperitoneal injection twice weekly.

Diluted xenograft tumor formation

Cells with varying levels of SLC25A1 expression were subcutaneously injected into nude mice at concentrations of 5 × 105, 5 × 104, and 5 × 103. The mice were sacrificed two months later, and the number of tumor-free mice was analyzed using the extreme limiting dilution analysis (ELDA) tool55.

Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining

Cellular senescence was assessed using the Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Cat. No. K2181; APExBIO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 70–80% confluence. After treatment, cells were gently washed twice with PBS and fixed with 1× fixative solution (containing 2% formaldehyde and 0.2% glutaraldehyde in PBS) for 10–15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove residual fixative. For staining, cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a dry incubator (without CO2) with the X-gal staining working solution. The working solution was adjusted to pH 6.0 prior to use to ensure optimal enzymatic activity. After incubation, the staining solution was removed, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. Stained cells were observed under an inverted optical microscope. For quantification, bright-field images captured from five random fields per well were used to calculate the percentage of SA-β-Gal–positive cells.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the subcellular localization and expression of EPCAM, HSPD1, and SLC25A1 in HNSCC cells. Cells were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates and cultured until they reached 60–70% confluence. After the designated treatments, cells were gently washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and subsequently blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution at appropriate concentrations. After incubation, the washed cells were incubated with species-matched fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 µg/mL; Cat. No. D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at room temperature. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

ELISA for cytokines content measurement

HNSCC cell lysates were used to quantify several cytokines associated with the SASP using the following commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols: IL-1α (Cat. No. PI305; Beyotime, China), IL-1β (Cat. No. PI565; Beyotime, China), IL-6 (Cat. No. PI325; Beyotime, China), and IL-8 (Cat. No. PI640; Beyotime, China).

Metabolite measurement

A total of 1 × 108 CAL27CDDP-shSLC25A1 cells and corresponding control cells were used to determine citrate content. Mitochondria were isolated using a cell mitochondria isolation kit (Cat. No. C3601; Beyotime, China). A citric acid content detection kit (Cat. No. BC2155; Solarbio Biotech, China) was employed to measure citric acid levels in cytoplasmic and mitochondrial fractions according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To quantify acetyl-CoA levels, mitochondria-depleted supernatants were collected, sonicated, and precipitated with 1.0 M perchloric acid (PCA; Cat. No. 42403; Acros Organics, Belgium). After centrifugation, the supernatant was recovered and neutralized by the addition of potassium bicarbonate, followed by removal of the precipitate through centrifugation. The acetyl-CoA concentration in the supernatant was then measured using an acetyl-CoA assay kit (Cat. No. D751001; Sangon Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA sequencing and KEGG analyses

RNA sequencing analysis was conducted as previously described. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from CAL27CDDP-shCtrl (n = 3) and CAL27CDDP-shSLC25A1 (n = 3) cell samples using the TRIzol reagent kit (Cat. No. 15596018CN, Invitrogen, USA). The resulting cDNA fragments synthesized from quality-checked RNA were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Cat. No. 28104, QIAGEN, Germany). RNA sequencing was subsequently performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform to generate paired-end reads. Clean reads processed using HISAT2 software were mapped to the reference genome (GRCh38.112). Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.26.0) based on the criteria of a Bonferroni-adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |FC | > 1.5. The list of differentially expressed genes is provided in Data S1. KEGG pathway analysis was conducted using KOBAS software. A threshold of q-value < 0.1 was applied to identify statistically significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways.

Identification of SLC25A1-related genes

SLC25A1-related genes were identified using RNA expression data from the TCGA-HNSCC dataset available through the UALCAN resource and the GSE30784 dataset, applying correlation coefficient thresholds of > 0.3 and > 0.4, respectively, yielding 310 and 325 genes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the RNA-seq data of SLC25A1-knockdown CAL27CDDP cells were further incorporated to refine the selection of SLC25A1-associated genes. The cutoff criteria were defined as |FC | > 1.5 and P < 0.05. In addition, 11 candidate genes identified from the above datasets were evaluated for their association with overall survival in the TCGA-HNSCC dataset using the GEPIA2 survival analysis tool. Ultimately, RANBP1, CDC45, and PES1 were identified as SLC25A1-associated genes, as higher expression levels of these genes were associated with poorer overall survival. Furthermore, the correlation between SLC25A1 expression and several cancer stemness–related genes was analyzed using Spearman’s correlation via the cBioPortal platform.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assay

The Cistrome Data Browser was utilized to identify specific gene regions regulated by SLC25A1 using H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from seven HNSCC cell lines, including BICR16, BICR31, CAL33, and YD8 cells derived from tongue squamous cell carcinoma; BICR6 cells from pharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma; Detroit 562 cells from pharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma metastatic to pleural effusion; and HSC-4 cells from a primary tongue squamous cell carcinoma lesion with cervical lymph node metastasis56. ChIP experiments were performed using a SimpleChIP Plus Sonication ChIP Kit (Cat. No. 56383; Cell Signaling Technology, USA). HNSCC cells subjected to different treatments were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde and lysed using lysis buffer. Subsequently, the sonicated chromatin samples were immunoprecipitated with 5 μg of anti-H3K27ac antibody or normal rabbit IgG. DNA was then isolated for subsequent PCR amplification. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S5.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)–mass spectrometry (MS) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

A Capture IP & Co-IP Kit (Cat. No. 635721; Clontech, Japan) was used for immunoprecipitation. HNSCC cells were lysed using IP lysis buffer. Subsequently, 5 μg of the corresponding antibodies were used to immunoprecipitate protein samples overnight at 4 °C. Balanced protein A/G magnetic beads were then incubated with the immunoprecipitated complexes for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the bead-bound complexes were subjected to mass spectrometry for further protein screening. The normalized abundance of identified proteins was used to screen candidate SLC25A1-interacting proteins based on the overlap between CAL27CDDP and SCC25 cells with high SLC25A1 expression. Data S2 and S3 list the candidate interacting proteins of SLC25A1. SLC25A1-interacting proteins were further validated using Co-IP assays.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using the HDOCK server57. The cryo-electron microscopy structures of human SLC25A1 (AlphaFold: AF-P53007-F1) and human HSPD1 (PDB ID: 8G7M) were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database and the RCSB PDB, respectively. The protein structures were preprocessed by removing water molecules and correcting structural inconsistencies. The optimal docking pose was selected for interaction analysis and visualized using PyMOL.

SLC25A1-HSPD1 interaction domain analysis

Three solcar domains have been identified in SLC25A1. To determine the specific domain responsible for interaction with HSPD1, three truncated Flag-SLC25A1 mutants were generated based on these solcar domains, along with the HSPD1-His expression vector. Co-IP analysis was subsequently performed to identify the SLC25A1 domain that interacts with HSPD1.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise specified. Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze quantitative data. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to determine significant differences among multiple groups. Spearman or Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between two variables. SPSS 17.0 and GraphPad 7 software were used for statistical analyses, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
