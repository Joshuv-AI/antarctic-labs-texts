Hypochloraemia independently associates with short-term mortality in patients with acute heart failure: a study from Vietnam
Authors: Dieu Hien Tran, Thien Tan Tri Tai Truyen, Vu Ngoc Anh Pham, Chau-Minh Nhut Do, Phuc Dai Vo, Tri Cuong Phan, Han My Nguyen Le, Hoai Ngoc Thi Tran, Bao Minh Ton Luu, Thi Huong-Dung Nguyen, Thanh Phong Tran, Van Khoa Nguyen, Dieu Thi Truong, Thanh Phong Pham, Minh Vu Nguyen, Minh Nghiem Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSerum chloride has been recently recognised as a predictor of short-term mortality in patients hospitalised with acute heart failure (AHF). However, data from developing nations such as Vietnam remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study at Can Tho Central General Hospital, Vietnam, from May to December 2024. Adult patients aged ≥18 years, hospitalised with AHF and NT-proBNP levels ≥300 pg/mL, were included after providing informed consent. Exclusions were made for patients lacking 24-hour electrolyte panels, those transferred or deceased before blood collection and individuals with end-stage renal disease, on renal replacement therapy or with active malignancies. Postdischarge survivors were followed up via phone interviews for 30 days. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. Given the low in-hospital event rate (5%), logistic regression was used to estimate ORs, while modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was applied for 30-day mortality, where the event rate exceeded 10% and OR would overestimate true risk.ResultsThe final cohort included 423 patients (mean age 69.7±12.7 years; 48% male). During hospitalisation, 21 (5.0%) patients died and 402 were discharged alive. Of the discharged patients, 252 (62.7%) completed a 30-days follow-up, with 43 deaths (17.1% mortality rate). After adjusting for age, sex, coronary artery disease, chronic heart failure, left ventricular ejection fraction and serum sodium, hypochloraemia was an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 4.9; 95% CI 1.3 to 18.5) and 30-days mortality (adjusted RR 1.9; 95% CI 1.1 to 3.6). Additionally, each 1 mmol/L increase of chloride was associated with a 5% lower risk of 30-day death (RR per unit change 0.95; 95% CI 0.91 to 0.99; p=0.007).ConclusionAdmission serum chloride levels independently predicted both in-hospital and 30-days all-cause mortality in Vietnamese patients with AHF. Including this biomarker in risk-stratification tools could enhance short-term prognostication, while its longer-term predictive value warrants further investigation.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the prognostic value of hypochloraemia in patients with acute heart failure (AHF) in Southeast Asia, addressing a significant evidence gap in a region with a distinct phenotypic and aetiological profile compared with Western populations. Our study found that 19.4% of patients admitted with AHF in Vietnam had hypochloraemia. Hypochloraemia was an independent predictor of both in-hospital and 30-day all-cause mortality in these patients. After multivariable adjustment, each 1 mmol/L increase in admission serum chloride was associated with a 5% lower risk of 30-day all-cause mortality across the observed chloride range in our cohort, indicating a continuous dose–response relationship. Hyponatraemia was not associated with short-term mortality after adjusting for chloride levels. Additionally, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) was the predominant subtype in our cohort (59.9%), followed by heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) (23.0%) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) (17.1%), contrasting with contemporary Western registries where HFpEF now accounts for 39% to over 50% of hospitalised patients with heart failure—highlighting the distinct phenotypic profile of Vietnamese patients with AHF compared with Western populations.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our findings show particular significance for resource-limited healthcare settings in Southeast Asia, where advanced biomarkers such as NT-proBNP may not be routinely available. As the first study demonstrating the independent prognostic value of hypochloraemia in a Southeast Asian AHF population—where patients present younger and with a distinct phenotypic profile compared with Western cohorts—our findings suggest that serum chloride monitoring at admission should be considered in routine clinical practice given its simplicity and low cost. From a research perspective, multicentre prospective studies across Southeast Asian countries are needed to validate these results and establish region-specific thresholds. From a policy perspective, these findings highlight the potential for incorporating serum chloride into AHF risk assessment frameworks, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries where simple prognostic markers could meaningfully guide clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Heart failure is a significant global health challenge, with a particularly severe impact in Asia. Over 31 million individuals are affected, resulting in an age-standardised prevalence rate of 722.5 per 100 000 persons.1 2 In South-East Asia, where many countries are developing, the burden is even heavier and is on the rise; the prevalence has reached 755.9 per 100 000 and has been increasing by approximately 3.7% annually since 1990.1 2 Additionally, acute heart failure (AHF), a frequent complication, emphasises the gravity of the situation. Data from Asian registries indicate in-hospital AHF mortality rates between 2.8% and 8.5%, while short-term mortality (30–90 days) persists at levels ranging from 5% to 18.1%, despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment.38 The phenotypic distribution of heart failure also differs across regions; while HFpEF accounts for 39% and 24% of hospitalised patients in the USA and Swedish registries, respectively, Asian cohorts show a predominance of HFrEF—recently confirmed in a Vietnamese multicentre study where HFrEF comprised 49.5% of AHF admissions, with HFmrEF at 20.5% and HFpEF at only 30%.911 These statistics underscore the necessity for effective prognostic stratification. Rapidly identifying high-risk patients who could benefit from enhanced monitoring and follow-up is crucial for improving AHF outcomes.

Predictors for short-term and long-term mortality in patients admitted with AHF have been identified. The Organized Program to Initiate Lifesaving Treatment in Hospitalized Patients with Heart Failure (OPTIMIZE-HF) study highlighted several poor prognostic indicators for in-hospital mortality, including advanced age, increased heart rate, low systolic blood pressure, hyponatraemia and renal dysfunction.12 For 30-day mortality, prior studies reported significant predictors such as NT-proBNP levels at admission, age, creatinine clearance, hypertrophy on ECG, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and low output symptoms.13 14 These predictors are incorporated into predictive risk scores like MEESSI-AHF, which demonstrate strong discrimination with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.836.14 Research into new mortality predictors for patients with AHF is ongoing, and blood chloride levels have emerged as a clinically relevant indicator, with evidence accumulating over the past decade linking hypochloraemia to adverse outcomes in this population.15 16 Chloride levels have been shown to not only predict mortality but also provide insight into diuretic resistance, a challenging issue in managing patients with AHF.17 Specifically, chloride levels are inversely proportional to mortality, with a HR of 0.94 and a 95% CI of 0.92 to 0.95.18 Furthermore, hypochloraemia, defined as blood chloride levels below 96 mmol/L and reported in approximately 13% of cases in the Placebo-controlled Randomized Study of the Selective A1 antagonist Rolofylline for Patients Hospitalized with Acute Decompensated Heart Failure and Volume Overload to Assess Treatment Effect on Congestion and Renal Function trial (PROTECT),19 is associated with an increased risk of mortality for both short-term and long-term (HR ranging from 1.07 to 9.13), as well as more pronounced symptoms and a poor diuretic response.1825

In developing countries like Vietnam, despite the significant burden of AHF, the literature on AHF mortality and its predictors remains limited. Only one study has reported that patients with AHF have an in-hospital and 30-day all-cause mortality rate of 9.7% and 10.2%, respectively.26 Adding to this concern, heart failure accounts for approximately 15% of total hospital admissions in Vietnam, with inpatient treatment costs ranging from US$394 to US$714 per episode—a substantial burden for a resource-limited healthcare system where identifying simple, accessible prognostic markers is of particular clinical importance.9 27 Notably, there are no studies investigating the predictive value of chloride disorders in patients with AHF in our country. To address this knowledge gap in Vietnam, we conducted a study aimed at two objectives: (1) to determine the prevalence of hypochloraemia in patients hospitalised with AHF in Vietnam; and (2) to assess the prognostic predictive value of hypochloremia for 30-day all-cause mortality in these patients.

Methods

Study design and population selection

To address our primary objective, we conducted a cross-sectional study at Can Tho Central General Hospital, a tertiary care centre in southern Vietnam, from 1 May to 31 December 2024. The study design and patient selection criteria have been described in detail previously.28 Briefly, we enrolled all patients aged 18 years and older who were admitted to the cardiology department with a primary diagnosis of AHF. The diagnosis was based on the Framingham heart failure criteria and required an NT-proBNP level of at least 300 pg/mL.29 In addition to the initial criteria, patients were required to have a complete electrolyte panel, including serum chloride, drawn within 24 hours of admission, as well as an echocardiogram with a documented left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). If a patient was hospitalised more than once during the study period, only their first admission was included in the study. We excluded patients who died before blood samples could be obtained, those transferred to another facility, anyone who declined informed consent and patients with active cancer, end-stage kidney disease or indications for acute haemodialysis.

To achieve our secondary objectives, patients who survived until hospital discharge were followed up for 30 days postdischarge through telephone interviews. If the patient was alive, we interviewed them directly; if deceased, we gathered information from their family members. Based on a previously reported hypochloraemia prevalence of 13% among AHF admissions,19 along with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, we calculated a minimum sample size of 174 patients using the formula below. Ultimately, we recruited every eligible patient within the study period to meet or exceed this target.

Definition of demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and outcomes

Demographic data (age, sex) and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular accident (CVA), atrial fibrillation (AF) and prior congestive heart failure (CHF)) were extracted from our hospital electronic health record (EHR) during each patient admission. AHF was defined per Framingham criteria by the onset of new symptoms—dyspnoea, orthopnoea or lower-extremity swelling—and signs—elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary congestion or peripheral oedema—together with an NT-proBNP level ≥300 pg/mL.29 30 Serum chloride measured within 24 hours of admission was used to categorise chloride status as hypochloraemia (≤96 mmol/L), normochloraemia (97–107 mmol/L) or hyperchloraemia (≥108 mmol/L).1719 We also collected other laboratory information including complete blood counts, renal and liver function tests and echocardiographic parameters, including LVEF, from the EHR.

The primary short-term outcome was all-cause mortality within 30 days of discharge. Our physicians conducted follow-up calls—up to three attempts—to patients or their families. Subjects were considered as lost to follow-up if no contact could be made after three calls or if the family declined to provide survival information.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean±SD or median with first and third quartiles, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test; independent sample t-tests (or analysis of variance [ANOVA] test) were used for normally distributed continuous data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to non-normal distributions. Pearson’s χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test compared categorical variables. To evaluate the association between chloride disorders and in-hospital mortality in patients with AHF, we initially calculated OR via logistic regression, using patients with normal serum chloride as the reference group. Multivariable models were adjusted for age (rescaled per 10 years), sex, comorbidities (CAD, prior CHF), LVEF and sodium level. In the survivors, the primary short-term endpoint was 30-day all-cause mortality. Because the overall event rate exceeded 10%, the OR would overestimate risk ratio (RR). Accordingly, we implemented the modified Poisson regression with robust (‘sandwich’) variance estimation as described by Zou et al.28 31 This approach fits a log-linked Poisson model but replaces the model-based variance with a Huber-White estimator, yielding adjusted RR (Exp[B]) with valid 95% CI even when the Poisson variance assumption is violated, and ensuring stable convergence.

To evaluate potential bias introduced by loss to follow-up (LTFU), we examined the missingness mechanism using Little’s test to determine whether the missing outcome data were consistent with missing completely at random (MCAR).32 We then compared baseline clinical and demographic characteristics between patients with complete follow-up and those LTFU, using independent samples t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for all variables, with SMD >0.20 considered indicative of meaningful imbalance between groups.33 Additionally, a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating all baseline characteristics was fitted to identify independent predictors of LTFU and estimate each patient’s probability of being observed. Among all baseline characteristics examined, CVA was the only variable demonstrating meaningful imbalance between observed and lost groups (SMD=0.288) and the sole independent predictor of LTFU in multivariable logistic regression (OR 3.62; 95% CI 1.32 to 9.96; p=0.013) (online supplemental table S1). Importantly, admission serum chloride showed negligible imbalance between groups (SMD=0.005; p=0.958), indicating that LTFU was unrelated to the primary exposure variable. Together, these findings argue against MCAR and support a missing at random (MAR) assumption, whereby missingness is primarily explained by CVA status. However, as MAR is not formally testable, we performed sensitivity analysis using inverse probability weighting (IPW), in which each patient’s probability of complete follow-up was estimated based on CVA status. Stabilised inverse probability weights were calculated as the marginal probability of observation divided by the CVA-specific probability of observation and applied to the multivariable modified Poisson regression model to generate a pseudo-population in which follow-up was independent of CVA. Weight stability was verified by confirming that mean weights approximated to 1.0 and no extreme weights exceeding 5.0 were present. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p<0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, V.24 and R V.4.4.3.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between 1 May and 31 December 2024, a total of 675 patients admitted with AHF during the study period were screened. Following application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 423 participants were enrolled in the final analysis. Of these, 402 (95.0%) were discharged alive and entered the 30-day follow-up phase to assess all-cause mortality. Follow-up data were successfully obtained for 252 patients; the remaining 150 (37.3%) could not be reached or declined to participate and were classified as lost to follow-up. Among patients with available outcome data, 43 deaths were recorded, corresponding to a 30-day all-cause mortality rate of 17.1% (figure 1).

The mean chloride level was 101.1±6.0 mmol/L. Among the 423 patients with AHF, 82 (19.4%) presented with hypochloraemia. Their mean age was comparable to that of normochloraemic patients (69.1 vs 69.3 years). A history of CHF was more frequent in the hypochloraemic group (53.7%) than in the normochloraemic (49.8%) and hyperchloraemic cohorts (29.2%; p=0.02), whereas the prevalence of other comorbidities was similar across groups, except for a lower rate of cerebrovascular disease in those with hypochloraemia (table 1). On admission, mean systolic blood pressure tended to be lower in hypochloraemic patients compared with normochloraemic and hyperchloraemic individuals (120.6 vs 126.7 and 130.4 mm Hg, p=0.08, respectively).

Prognostic value for in-hospital mortality of blood chloride in patients with AHF

Using normochloraemic patients as the reference group, hypochloraemia was associated with a threefold increase in in‐hospital mortality (OR 3.1; 95% CI 1.2 to 8.0), whereas hyperchloraemia conferred no significant change in risk (OR 1.9; 95% CI 0.5 to 7.1). After adjusting for age, sex, CAD, CHF, LVEF and sodium level, hypochloraemia remained an independent predictor of in‐hospital death (adjusted OR 4.9; 95% CI 1.3 to 18.5). The model demonstrated adequate calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit: χ²₍₈₎ = 7.184, p=0.517). Additionally, this predictive model demonstrated strong discrimination for in‐hospital mortality, with an AUC of 0.742 (95% CI 0.629 to 0.855) (figure 2). Finally, patients with hypochloraemia experienced a longer median hospital stay compared with normochloraemic and hyperchloremic individuals (10.9 vs 9.4 and 8.6 days, respectively; p=0.02).

Discrimination ability for in-hospital death of multivariable predictive model including clinical features and hypochloraemia. A multivariable logistic regression incorporating demographic features (age, sex), comorbidities (coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure), left ventricular ejection fraction, sodium level and hypochloraemia demonstrated a good discrimination for in‐hospital mortality, with an area under the curve of 0.742 (95% CI 0.629 to 0.855). The model also exhibited adequate calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit: χ²₍₈₎ = 7.184, p=0.517).

Prognostic value for short-term outcome of blood chloride in patients with AHF

At 30‐day follow‐up, non‐survivors were more likely to be male, to have comorbidities such as CHF and CKD and to present with hypochloraemia; they also exhibited lower LVEF and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and higher N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) levels than survivors (table 3). Using a modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation, hypochloraemia predicted 30‐day all‐cause mortality (RR 1.9; 95% CI 1.1 to 3.5). After multivariable adjustment for demographics, hypertension, diabetes, CKD, AF, CAD, CHF, CVD, LVEF and sodium level, hypochloraemia remained independently associated with mortality (adjusted RR 1.9; 95% CI 1.1 to 3.6). Hyponatraemia, defined as sodium blood level <135 mmol/L, was not associated with 30-day all-cause mortality (adjusted RR 1.5; 95% CI 0.8 to 2.8). Meanwhile, other independent risk factors included male sex (adjusted RR 1.9; 95% CI 1.03 to 3.4), CKD (adjusted RR 3.6; 95% CI 1.8 to 7.3), cerebrovascular disease (RR 4.0; 95% CI 1.4 to 11.4) and each 10‐year increment in age (RR 1.4; 95% CI 1.1 to 1.8). Higher LVEF was the only protective factor (RR 0.03; 95% CI 0.002 to 0.36) (figure 3). The model converged without step‐halving, showed no numerical instability and exhibited acceptable fit (Pearson χ² = 199.7 with 238 df; χ²/df=0.84), indicating mild under dispersion. Finally, after multivariable adjustment, each 1 mmol/L increase in admission serum chloride was associated with a 5% lower risk of 30-day all-cause mortality across the observed chloride range in our cohort (RR per unit change 0.95, 95% CI 0.91 to 0.99, p=0.007), indicating a continuous dose–response relationship (figure 4).

In sensitivity analyses, the significant association between hypochloraemia and 30-day all-cause mortality observed in the complete-case analysis persisted after IPW adjustment based on CVA status (adjusted RR 2.00; 95% CI 1.09 to 3.67; p=0.026), representing a minimal 0.6% change from the complete-case estimate (RR 1.99; 95% CI 1.08 to 3.65; p=0.028). Discussion

Our study revealed that the prevalence of hypochloraemia in patients admitted with AHF in Vietnam was 19.4%. Patients with hypochloraemia exhibited not only higher in-hospital mortality rates but also experienced longer hospital stays. After conducting multivariable regression analysis, hypochloraemia was identified as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality, with an adjusted OR of 4.9. Moreover, hypochloraemia was associated with twice the risk of 30-day all-cause mortality, demonstrating a dose–response relationship where each 1-unit increase in chloride levels corresponded to a 5% reduction in mortality risk. In contrast, hyponatraemia was not linked to short-term mortality after adjusting for chloride levels. In our cohort, HFrEF was the predominant subtype (59.9%), followed by HFmrEF (23.0%) and HFpEF (17.1%), consistent with a recent Vietnamese multicentre study but contrasting with Western registries where HFpEF accounts for 39% to over 50% of hospitalised patients with heart failure .

Our study is the first to report the prevalence of chloride disorders in patients with AHF in Vietnam. We found that nearly 20% of these patients exhibited hypochloraemia, consistent with previous studies that have reported prevalence rates between 11% and 36%.1821 Despite being younger, patients with hypochloraemia in our study tended to be sicker, with a higher incidence of prior CHF, lower systolic blood pressure and sodium levels at admission, aligning with previous findings.18 19 Our study confirmed that hypochloraemia is a significant predictor of both in-hospital mortality and longer hospital stays in the Vietnamese population. Notably, hypochloraemia was associated with a threefold increase in the risk of in-hospital mortality, which exceeds the OR of 1.3 reported by Testani et al (Beta-Blocker Evaluation of Survival Trial - BEST trial) in 2017.20 However, this risk increase aligns with findings from a similar study in Ethiopia, which reported an adjusted OR of 2.8 for in-hospital mortality among hypochloraemic patients.34 The discrepancy between our results and those of the BEST trial might be attributed to differences in target populations; the BEST trial focused solely on patients with CHF, while our study included individuals with and without a history of CHF. This broader inclusion could lead to lower baseline mortality and consequently a more pronounced risk increase associated with hypochloraemia.20 Additionally, these findings might highlight the importance of accounting for regional and demographic differences when evaluating the impact of hypochloraemia on patient outcomes.

Our study further demonstrated that hypochloraemia independently increased the risk of 30-day all-cause mortality in patients admitted with AHF, nearly doubling the risk. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported an increased risk ranging from 1.4 to 1.7 times.23 25 Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis by Stankowski et al encompassing 15 studies and 25 848 patients reported that hypochloraemia was independently associated with a 56% increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.6; 95% CI 1.4 to 1.8) across follow-up periods of 6–67 months.16 Our study extends this evidence to a short-term outcome framework in a Vietnamese AHF population previously unrepresented in this meta-analysis, and while direct comparison is limited by differences in follow-up duration and effect measure, the consistent direction of association across populations strengthens the prognostic role of serum chloride in AHF. Additionally, while the interaction between sodium and chloride levels has been extensively researched, the results remain controversial. Some studies have suggested that both chloride and sodium are independent predictors of increased mortality in patients with AHF,18 23 whereas others have highlighted a stronger influence from chloride, showing that hypochloraemia remains a predictor of poor outcomes while hyponatraemia loses its predictive value.19 20 35 Our study supports the latter hypothesis, as our results indicate that hypochloraemia had a more significant impact on patient outcomes compared with hyponatraemia.

Patients with AHF may develop hypochloraemia through two primary mechanisms. The first, and most common, is dilution due to fluid retention and volume overload. This phenomenon is driven by increased activity of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, sympathetic nervous system and elevated secretion of antidiuretic hormone, which together promote water and sodium retention and increased thirst.36 The second mechanism involves true chloride ion depletion, often seen in patients using high doses of loop diuretics, which can cause salt wasting through chloride excretion and replacement with bicarbonates as negative ions.37 Diuretics, particularly loop and thiazide types, block the reabsorption of chloride in thick ascending loop of Henle and distal convoluted tubules. This effect can be exacerbated by WNK (With-No-Lysine) kinase, a chloride-sensing kinase, which activates phosphor-NKCC2 to enhance sodium and chloride reabsorption when chloride levels drop resulting in a reduction of diuretic efficacy (urine output), necessitating higher doses of diuretics and potentially leading to diuretic resistance, explaining the poorer outcomes for patients with hypochloraemia.3840 Finally, it is important to note that loop diuretics can result in chloride excretion up to 20 times greater than that of a normal person, and 10%–20% more than sodium excretion, while sodium excretion may only increase up to 10 times.41 Additionally, sodium can be actively reabsorbed in the collecting tubules through the epithelial sodium channel, whereas chloride can only be passively absorbed paracellularly due to the electrical gradient. This phenomenon suggests that chloride levels might serve as a more sensitive marker for volume overload and overall mortality in patients with AHF compared with sodium levels.

Blood chloride levels, as reliable predictors, can be integrated into risk scores to enhance the prognosis for patients with AHF. Misumi et al incorporated hypochloraemia and BNP levels into the Get With The Guidelines-Heart Failure (GWTG-HF) risk score, creating the GWTG-BNP-Cl model to predict in-hospital mortality for patients with AHF. This model shows promising potential, achieving an AUC of 0.763 and a net reclassification improvement of 0.491 compared with the previous GWTG-HF model.42 Future studies are needed to validate and refine this risk score or develop similar ones. Additionally, understanding the predictive value of chloride levels opens opportunity for testing and deploying future strategies, including the use of medications such as acetazolamide, Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and hypertonic saline solutions.4345 These interventions could enhance the chances of successful decongestion, reduce hospital stays and potentially improve mortality rates in patients with AHF, particularly those experiencing diuretic resistance.

Limitations and strengths

When interpreting our findings, several limitations must be considered. First, as a single-centre study conducted over an 8-month period, the sample size was modest, which may limit statistical power and the generalisability of our findings. Moreover, despite our efforts to maintain contact, a substantial portion of patients—up to one-third—were lost to follow-up, including cases where family members declined to provide survival information, which may introduce systematic bias if refusal was more common among families of deceased patients. To address this, we evaluated the mechanism of missingness and found that LFTU was primarily associated with a history of CVA, supporting a MAR assumption rather than purely random missingness. Sensitivity analyses using IPW showed results consistent with the complete-case analysis, supporting the robustness of our main findings under the MAR assumption. Second, the lack of specific information regarding the exact timing of death after hospital discharge prevented us from conducting a survival analysis or estimating HRs. Consequently, the study was limited to binary outcomes at fixed timepoints, and modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was used as the most appropriate alternative to directly estimate relative risks while avoiding the overestimation inherent to ORs at event rates exceeding 10%. Third, rehospitalisation within 30 days was not captured in our dataset, as follow-up telephone interviews were designed to ascertain survival status only, and patients may have been readmitted to other hospitals in the region. Although we attempted to collect this information from patients and their families, recall bias precluded reliable ascertainment, and its potential confounding effect on postdischarge mortality could not be assessed. Finally, data on diuretic type, dose and duration were not systematically collected, precluding adjustment for diuretic exposure in the multivariable models and sensitivity analyses by diuretic class representing a potential source of unmeasured confounding. Additionally, several variables were not available for complete analysis: Body mass index data were available for only 90% of patients, limiting our ability to fully evaluate the contribution of body composition to mortality outcomes; and serum chloride was measured only at admission, preventing assessment of dynamic changes in chloride status during hospitalisation—such as resolution or progression of hypochloraemia—which prior studies have shown to carry independent prognostic significance. Finally, as a single-centre study conducted at a tertiary hospital in southern Vietnam, our findings may not be fully generalisable to other Vietnamese regions or broader Southeast Asian populations with different patient profiles and healthcare infrastructure. External validation in independent cohorts is warranted before these findings can inform routine clinical practice. Despite these limitations, our study offers several notable strengths. It is the first study in Vietnam to investigate the prevalence of chloride disorders and report their predictive value for in-hospital and short-term mortality among patients with AHF. Additionally, our prospective recruitment and follow-up approach provides a more reliable estimate of the association between chloride disorders and patient outcomes compared with retrospective enrolment methods.

Conclusion

Hypochloraemia is a relatively common occurrence among patients with AHF in the Vietnamese population. Our study effectively demonstrated that blood chloride levels, particularly hypochloraemia, serve as independent predictors of both in-hospital mortality and 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with AHF from our country. Future research is warranted to incorporate chloride levels into predictive risk models and to develop effective intervention strategies to address this disorder.
