Mutagenicity and DNA damage assessment of locally produced nano-hydroxyapatite-silica-glass ionomer cement on human periodontal ligament fibroblasts using Ames and Comet assays
Authors: Fayez Hussain Niazi, Norhayati Luddin, Abdurahman Niazy, Suharni Mohamad, Masitah Hayati Harun, Mohammed Noushad, Kannan Thirumulu Ponnuraj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis research assessed the mutagenicity and DNA damage of a novel type of nano-hydroxyapatite-silica glass ionomer cement (nano-HA-SiO2-GIC) and a conventional GIC (cGIC) using Ames and Comet assays.MethodsCell viability was tested on human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (HPLFs) using 3.125 mg/ml, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 and 200 mg/ml, on both types of GICs employing MTT assay. For the Comet assay, HPLFs were treated with IC50, IC25 and IC10 of test materials and the tail moments were measured. In the Ames test, four genotypic variants of strains of Salmonella typhimurium (TA100, TA98, TA1537 and TA1535) and a strain of Escherichia coli (WP2 uvrA) were employed. The material tested was extracted using sterile distilled water (0.2 g per ml) at 37 °C for 72 h. This was considered as 100 %, which was diluted to 50, 25, 12.5 and 6.25 % utilizing sterile distilled water. These five concentrations were incubated with the bacterial strains with and without metabolic activation (S9), along with appropriate positive controls. The number of revertant colonies was used to evaluate the outcome.ResultsThe highest cell viability (159.4 %) for nano-HA-SiO2-GIC was noticed at 3.125 mg/ml, while the lowest (24.26 %) was observed at 200 mg per ml. IC50, IC25 and IC10 values were 95.27, 51.4 and 20.1 mg/ml for cGIC, 106.9, 55.8 and 22.9 mg/ml for nano-HA-SiO2-GIC, respectively. The IC10 of both test materials showed no significant DNA damage compared to that of the negative control based on the Comet assay. The plate treated with nano-HA-SiO2-GIC showed less than double the average number of revertant colonies compared to that of negative control with regard to the Ames test.ConclusionsIt can be concluded that nano-HA-SiO2-GIC is non-mutagenic based on the Ames test and did not cause DNA damage at the lowest concentration of IC10 based on the Comet assay.Keywords: Cell viability, DNA damage, Genotoxicity, Human periodontal ligament fibroblast, Mutagenicity, Nano-hydroxyapatite-silica-glass ionomer cement

Introduction

One of the materials most frequently utilised in dental restorative procedures is glass ionomer cement, or GICs. Among its many advantages over other dental materials are their good adhesion properties in moist type of environments (Yan et al., 2007), strong chemical bond with metals and hard dental tissues (Wilson and Kent, 1971), prolonged release of fluoride, thus promoting the health of dental tissues (Sakaguchi and Powers, 2012), and lower cytotoxic effect in comparison with other dental restorative materials (European Commission, 2016). Researchers have shown that adding nano-apatitic materials, particularly hydroxyapatite, enhances the compressive strength, flexural strength, and microhardness of GICs, reduces microleakage, and increases fluoride ion release (Moheet et al., 2018). However, a series of tests, including in vitro genotoxicity testing, must be performed on any novel material before it may be used in humans.

The MTT assay displays distinctive properties that contribute to its widespread use for evaluating cytotoxicity. The MTT assay is a rapid, uncomplicated, and cost-effective technique (Freimoser et al., 1999). Another genotoxicity test is the Ames assay, which detects materials' potential to induce mutagenesis (Ames et al., 1975). The comet assay is another sensitive, quick and rapid test for assessing genotoxicity that is frequently used to study DNA damage and identify DNA strand breaks caused by chemical and physical agents (Ribeiro et al., 2005). Although several studies have been published on the physical, biological and mechanical properties of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC, there is still a dearth of information on its genotoxicity. Bearing that in mind, this study set forth to determine the genotoxic effect of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC in terms of its mutagenicity and DNA damage using Comet and Ames assays.

Materials and methods

Test material and synthesis of nano-HA-Si-O2 powder

cGIC (Fuji IX, GC Corp Tokyo, Japan. batch number 1712220) dispensed in powder and liquid form was used in the current study. Chemicals of analytical grade were employed for synthesising nano-HA-SiO2 powder. Nano-HA-SiO2 powder was synthesized by the one-pot sol–gel method as described previously (Rahman et al., 2014).

Evaluation of cell viability using MTT assay

Culturing human periodontal ligament fibroblasts

The cell line used was human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (HPLFs) which were kindly donated from Dr. Areej Mukhtar (King Saud University, Riyadh, KSA) and purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories, Catalogue No. #2360 (USA).Complete growth medium comprised 440 ml of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Life Technologies, USA), supplemented with 50 ml of 10 % foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Hyclone, Thermo Scientific, USA), 5 ml of two antibiotics (5000 IU/ml penicillin, 5000 µg/ml streptomycin) (Gibco, Life Technologies, USA), and 5 ml of non-essential amino acids.

Test material extraction

Materials used were locally produced nano-HA-SiO2-GIC and cGIC (Fuji IX, GC Corporation, Japan). A 10 % nano-HA-SiO2-GIC samples were formed following the methodology of Moheet et al. (2020). After 24 h of setting, the cement was removed from the mold and sterilized for 30 min under UV light. Then, the cements were standardised to 1000 mg by weight, put into sterile glass bottles containing 5 ml of complete growth media (200 mg/ml) and cultured for three days at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 (Ahmed et al., 2011). The material extracts were serially diluted by adding 2.0 ml of the extract to each well of a 12-well plate containing 2.0 ml of complete growth medium. The concentrations of the extracts at the end were 200, 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, and 3.125 mg/ml (Ahmed et al., 2011).The material extracts were diluted in a series and then added to a 96-well culture plate to determine the cell viability. Fifth and sixth passage sub-cultured cells were utilized. The cells were grown at 37 °C, 95 % relative humidity and 5 % CO2. The cells were trypsinized, washed, and harvested once they reached confluency. A hemocytometer was used to count the number of cells, and fresh growth media was added to the cell suspension. 100 μl of a cell suspension containing 1 × 104 cells was added in every well of the 96-well plate (Dagang et al., 2010). Likewise, two 96-well plates (one for each type of GIC group) containing cell suspensions were made. Then, the 96-well culture plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 to promote cell adhesion.

Determination of inhibitory concentrations

The MTT test was followed based on Wahab et al. (2018) to evaluate the cytotoxicity. After incubation, the culture media was carefully discarded from every well of 96-well plates and changed with 200 μl of 200, 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25 and 3.125 mg/ml of the material extracts. Cells treated with growth medium only served as the negative control. Two experimental material extracts were cultured on separate 96-well culture plates for 72 h at 37 °C and a CO2 concentration of 5 %. the percentage of cell viability was calculated. GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1) was used to produce dose–response graphs for both types of GIC groups using various concentrations. The inhibitory concentrations (IC50, IC25, and IC10) were then computed.

Comet assay

Propagation of cell line and preparation of material extract

HPLFs were propagated from the stock cultures as described previously. Comet Assay Kit (3-well slides from Abcam, UK) was used, and all lysis and alkaline solutions were prepared manually. After cell counting with a hemocytometer (Neubauer, Hawksley, Germany) and calculation of the required volume, cells were seeded in a 6-well plate (NUNC, Denmark) with 5 ml of DMEM with a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. Both cGIC and nano-HA-SiO2-GIC disc samples were UV sterilized, and their extracts were prepared so that three concentrations, IC10, IC25 and IC50 were produced.

Treatment of cells

The cells were first incubated for 24 h, and after checking confluency, the media was changed, and the cells were exposed to IC50, IC25 and IC10 for both types of GIC along with negative control (DMEM) and incubated for three days. 100 μM of the positive control hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was only exposed for 15 min.

Procedure for Comet assay

The comet assay procedure was followed based on Tice et al. (2000).

Capture of comets

Images of fifty cells were captured randomly from each slide and observed at 40× magnification under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse E600, USA), connected through a camera to an image analysis system (Comet Assay IV, Perceptive Instruments Ltd., UK). The tail moment is proportional to the amount of damage to DNA in the cell. Employing the statistical software package (SPSS), version 22.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., USA), the data of tail moments is expressed as mean ± standard error (SE) and compared using one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc analysis (Games-Howell test). The differences were considered statistically significant if p was < 0.05.

The Ames test

Test material concentrations

In order to assess the mutagenic effects of the nano-HA-SiO2-GIC, the Ames experiment was conducted in accordance with the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO 10993-33: 2015) and OECD 471 standards (OECD, 2020). The disc samples with a dimension of 10 mm (length) × 2 mm (height) of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC were made in PTEE mold, as previously explained. The test sample was extracted in a shaking water bath with sterile distilled water at an extraction ratio (0.2 g/ml) and 37 °C for seventy-two hours. To ascertain the mutagenicity, 100 % nano-HA-SiO2-GIC extract was extracted and then diluted using sterile distilled water to four additional concentrations: 50, 25, 12.5, and 6.25 %. Then, the plating was executed in triplicates for all the concentrations of the test material nano-HA-SiO2-GIC, positive and negative controls.

Tester strains

The Ames assay was conducted using the procedure outlined by Zeiger and Mortelmans (2000). The strains of bacteria were purchased from Molecular Toxicology Inc (MOLTOX, USA). Strains of Salmonella typhimurium, namely TA98, TA100, TA1535, and TA1537 and the strain of Escherichia coli (WP2 uvrA) were cultured overnight from cultures which were frozen in nutrient broth for 10–12 h. The genetic integrity of the tester strains was assessed prior to the tests.

Negative and positive controls

The negative control used in the Ames assay was sterile distilled water which was also employed as the solution to extract nano-HA-SiO2-GIC. Each strain was tested using diagnostic positive controls that acted as mutagens, both with and without S9. Appropriate positive controls were used to confirm the reversion characteristics and specificity of for the specific tester strains, both in the presence and absence of S9.

Pre-incubation assay and interpretation of results

After adding 0.1 ml of suspension of each tester strain, 0.5 ml of buffer or S9 mix was mixed with 0.1 ml of different concentrations of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC. The mixture was agitated for 20 to 30 min in a water bath kept at 37 °C. Melted top agar enriched with trace amounts of biotin, histidine, or tryptophan was applied to the surface of the minimal glucose agar. Revertant colonies on nano-HA-SiO2-GIC plates were recognised and counted 48 h after their incubation at 37 °C. Using a colony counter (aCOLyte, Synbiosis, UK), the number of revertant colonies was counted to get the mean number and standard deviation for each treatment. The average number of revertant colonies for each concentration of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC was compared to the average number of colonies in the negative control to ascertain the mutagenicity of the material. The two-fold rule was applied to analyse the Ames assay data (Mortelmans and Zeiger, 2000, Chen et al., 2022). For all the tests, MTT, Comet and Ames, they were repeated thrice and mean values were taken.

Statistical analysis

For MTT assay, the IC50, IC25 and IC10 values for cGIC and nano-HA-SiO2-GlC were determined employing GraphPad prism software (version 9.5.1). In the case of Comet Assay, tail moments are expressed as the mean ± standard error (SE) and compared using one-way ANOVA followed by the post-hoc analysis (Games-Howell test). The differences were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. For the Ames test, the mutagenicity of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC was observed by comparing the average number of revertant colonies for each concentration of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC to the average number of colonies produced in the case of negative control based on the two-fold rule (Mortelmans and Zeiger, 2000, Chen et al., 2022).

Results

Inhibitory concentrations based on MTT assay

In the cGIC group, the highest cell viability (169.5 %) was found at 3.125 mg per ml and the lowest (33.2 %) at 200 mg per ml, whereas in the nano-HA-SiO2-GIC group, the highest cell viability (159.4 %) was observed at 3.125 mg per ml and the lowest (24.26 %) at 200 mg per ml. The values of IC50, IC25 and IC10 were determined using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1), as shown in Table 1. These three concentrations of IC50, IC25 and IC10 for both types of tested materials were employed in Comet assay.

Discussion

In this study, a novel GIC was produced by incorporating nano-hydroxyapatite and nano-silica into a conventional glass ionomer cement (cGIC). According to the ISO 10993-3, (2014) guidelines, genotoxicity testing must be performed when considering potential medical device’s compositions, such as dental materials which can interact with human DNA. The MTT assay was employed in the present investigation to ascertain the test material's cell viability. It has been observed in recent research that HPLFs were used with different kinds of biomaterials owing to their close association with periodontal regenerative procedures (Bianchi et al., 2022, Nogueira et al., 2019).

Numerous investigations assessing the cytotoxicity of GICs have shown contradictory results. For instance, a study on the cytotoxic nature of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC on dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) revealed a favourable cytotoxicity response, particularly at doses less than 100 mg/ml. This results suggest that nano-HA-SiO2-GIC has similar and favourable effects to cGIC. Though the maximum concentration of 200 mg per ml of Fuji IX and HA-SiO2-GIC extract lowered the cell density (Noorani et al., 2017), the cell density showed an increase with the decreasing concentrations of the material extract for all the groups. A similar dose-dependent response was also observed in this study. A recent study on cGIC, Bio-GIC, (GIC supplemented with tricalcium phosphate, chitosan, and recombinant fortilin obtained from Fenneropenaeus merguiensis, and Biodentine using human DPSCs showed that all the test materials were non-cytotoxic, with Bio-GIC promoting cell proliferation at 72 h (Chumpraman et al., 2023). However, another study showed that the tested resin-based GICs significantly reduced the cell survival rate in a dose-dependent manner (Park and Lee, 2023). The unpolymerized or leached monomers were shown to be the cause of the test materials' cytotoxicity in an earlier investigation on the cytotoxicity of cGICs. It was suggested that 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) as well as triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) are the components contributing to most of the cytotoxicity (Stanislawski et al., 1999).

Hii et al. (2019) found that the maximum concentration of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC (200 mg/ml) exhibited a moderate level of cytotoxicity on DPSCs. The cell viability of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC was almost identical to that of cGIC in this study. Bandarra et al. (2021) used the cytokinesis-block micronucleus assay and reported that the self-adhesive resin cement (RelyX Unicem 2 Automix) was genotoxic and cytotoxic to fibroblasts. The authors presumed TEGDMA to be the main component responsible for the genotoxicity and cytotoxicity.

Researchers have widely used the Comet assay to assess DNA strand breaks, represented as tail moments caused by restorative materials (Noushad et al., 2009). For instance, while none of the five orthodontic GICs in a cytotoxicity and genotoxicity investigation by Angelieri et al. (2018) was cytotoxic, most of them displayed DNA damage at varying exposure times. The cytotoxicity and potential for DNA damage of three cGIC powders and liquids were investigated by Ribeiro et al. (2006). Although all cGIC powders exhibited significant cytotoxicity at the highest tested concentration, only Ketac Molar powder caused DNA damage, specifically at the maximum concentration tested. Additionally, liquid dilutions of all cGICs were cytotoxic at all concentrations.

It was demonstrated that none of the test materials exhibited statistically significant tail moments across four distinct cGICs, including Ketac Molar. However, slightly elevated DNA migration was induced by unpolymerized Ketac Molar and polymerized Fuji II LC (Galić et al., 2014). These findings suggest that materials might react differently depending on whether they are polymerized or unpolymerized, and that cytotoxicity does not directly correlate with DNA damage. Moreover, they also indicate that it is not just the liquid component of GIC that causes DNA damage. The Comet test findings for nano-HA-SiO2-GIC demonstrated DNA strand migration, with a degree of migration similar to that of cGIC. Our findings indicate that there was no statistically significant difference when comparing the tail moments of the identical IC concentrations between cGIC and nano-HA-SiO2-GIC. HPLFs exposed to the IC10 concentration did not exhibit any notable DNA damage. Nevertheless, the DNA damage was detected at both the IC50 and IC25 concentrations, which may be attributed to the increased concentration of the material. The leachable components of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC may be the cause of the DNA strand migration. During the setting of nano-HA-SiO2-GIC, the interaction between glass particles and polyacrylic acid (PAA) is reduced which may result in the release of unreacted free PAA into the liquid medium (Shiekh et al., 2014). Even though nano-HA-SiO2-GIC induced only slight DNA damage in the present study, future studies should focus on its individual components.

The test material, nano-HA-SiO2-GIC, did not show any mutagenic potential according to the Ames assay results. Despite the inclusion of three additional strains, a study on GICs using the Ames test revealed that just one form of GIC (Meron) generated mutagenicity, and that too only in the S. typhimurium TA98 strain. Moreover, this GIC did not show mutagenicity in the presence of S9, indicating that its mutagenic effect is eliminated as a sequel of metabolic activity (Kaplan et al., 2004). It was also shown that one type of cGIC (VitrebondTM, 3 M, USA) caused mutagenicity on only one strain of S. typhimurium (TA1537) (Stea et al., 1998). However, in the current study, nano-HA-SiO2-GIC did not induce a mutagenic effect on any of the S. typhimurium strains, TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537, or E. coli.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that there was no notable disparity between nano-HA-SiO2-GIC and cGIC when assessed for DNA damage using the Comet assay at the IC10 value on HPLFs. Furthermore, the nano-HA-SiO2-GIC did not elicit any mutagenic impact, as shown by the Ames experiment conducted on all the bacterial strains that were examined. Hence, it can be concluded that nano-HA-SiO2-GIC holds promise to be used in clinical dentistry applications.
