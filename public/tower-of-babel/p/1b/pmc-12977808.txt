Humanistic care ability and nurse turnover intention: the mediating role of caring behaviors
Authors: Burcu Nal, Mustafa Nal, İbrahim Topuz, Gülfer Bektaş, Cihan Sütlü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn many countries, shortages in the number of nurses and unfavorable working conditions have become a significant problem, leading to an increase in nurses’ intention to leave their jobs. This study aims to determine the effect of nurses’ humanistic care ability on their turnover intention and the possible mediating role of care behaviors in this relationship.MethodsThis study employed a quantitative research approach with a descriptive, cross-sectional design. The study was carried out among nurses from multiple hospitals across Turkey to ensure sample diversity and enhance the generalizability of the findings. The study included a total of 456 nurses. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 and the PROCESS 4.1 macro.ResultsResults showed that humanistic care ability had a significant negative direct effect on turnover intention (β = -0.0076, p = .039), while caring behaviors also negatively and significantly influenced turnover intention (β = -0.0076, p < .001). Furthermore, the indirect effect analysis indicated a significant mediating role of caring behaviors in the relationship between humanistic care ability and turnover intention (β = -0.0083, 95% CI [-0.0124, -0.0046]).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that nurses’ care behaviors are effective in reducing turnover intentions and that a humanistic approach plays a mediating role in the relationship between turnover intentions. Incorporating humanistic care models into nursing education and assessing students’ humanistic levels may increase job satisfaction, thereby supporting retention in the profession and enhancing the quality of care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

In recent years, inadequate nurse-to-patient ratios have led to physical and mental strain on nurses and accelerated their turnover intention from their jobs due to increasing occupational stress [1]. The turnover intention of a job refers to employees considering leaving their current work and evaluating alternative job opportunities [2]. High nurse turnover rates negatively impact job satisfaction and health levels by increasing workloads; they also lead to a shortage of experienced nurses, thereby reducing the quality of care and increasing the risks of infection, readmission, and mortality [3–5]. In addition, nurses with less experience exhibit lower levels of care behavior [6]. Therefore, it is important to ensure the retention of experienced nurses within the institution. Insufficient number of staff and increasing maintenance needs may reduce the quality of the service provided [7]. The World Health Organization projects that the global shortage of nurses will reach about 5.7 million by 2030 [8]. Hence, identifying the causes of this problem and proposing possible solutions is extremely important. Turnover intention serves as a strong predictor of actual resignation behavior and plays a crucial role in the ongoing shortage of nursing staff [9].

Researchers have suggested job satisfaction, nurse-nurse collaboration [10], organizational commitment [11], clinical belonging [12], decent work, work engagement [13], to reduce nurses’ turnover intention. This study assumes that humanistic care skills and caring behaviors can be an effective solution to reduce nurses’ intention to leave their jobs. Care behavior is the essence and core of nursing. It manifests itself as humane care and an active attitude of feeling and caring for others [14]. Watson, who proposed the Human Care Theory, defines nursing as the “science of human care” [15]. Watson defined nursing care as “an evolving art” and also viewed nursing science as a fundamental source of knowledge and starting point from an ethical, philosophical, and theoretical perspective for nursing education, patient care, research, and administrative practices [16]. Care behaviors are essential for promoting patient recovery and increasing satisfaction, which are vital indicators of healthcare quality [17]. In this context, it is important to focus on humanistic care ability that enhances nurses’ effectiveness in the patient care process. This study provides a theoretical framework from Watson’s Human Care Theory [16], which emphasizes the importance of empathic and humane care, to investigate the mediating role of caring behavior when examining the impact of nurses’ humane care ability on their turnover intentions.

Humanistic care is a fundamental approach that forms the essence of contemporary nursing and has emerged as a natural result of social development [18]. The concept of humanistic care is a new nursing approach that emphasizes the importance of patient-centered and individualized care, with the overall goal of providing comfortable nursing services [19]. Humanistic care characteristics refer to innate personal qualities reflected through nurses’ humanistic care behaviors after specific cultural training [20]. Patient-centered nursing focuses on various aspects of patients’ needs, including physical, psychological, and social aspects [21]. Nurses’ compassionate care skills are an important factor in providing high-quality patient care [22]. This ability is theoretically expected to be reflected in nurses’ caregiving behaviors, which in turn is expected to affect attitudinal and cognitive outcomes such as nurses’ intention to leave their jobs.

Previous studies have examined the effects of humanistic care in nursing on patients’ psychological well-being [23], patients’ quality of life [24], and patients’ perceived stress and self-management effectiveness [25]. In the literature, no study has been found that examines the effect of nurses’ humanistic care ability on their turnover intention and the mediating role of care behavior in this relationship.

This study aims to examine the effect of nurses’ humanistic care ability on their turnover intention and to test the possible mediating role of care behaviors in this relationship.

The following questions are addressed:

Q1. Do nurses’ humanistic care ability influence their turnover intention?

Q2. Do nurses’ humanistic care ability influence their caring behaviors?

Q3. Do nurses’ caring behaviors influence their turnover intention?

Q4. Does caring behavior mediate the effect of nurses’ humanistic care ability on turnover intention?

Methods

Study design

This research employed a quantitative, descriptive, cross-sectional design to examine the relationships among the study variables. Data were collected continuously between May and September 2025 from nurses working in different hospitals, and the study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Settings

This study was conducted on nurses working in different hospitals in Turkey. The study was conducted in various hospitals to increase sample diversity and enhance the generalizability of the findings. Non-probability convenience sampling method was used, and 600 hospital nurses were invited to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria were having at least one year of professional experience, being able to read and understand Turkish, willingness to participate voluntarily, and completion of the questionnaire without missing or erroneous responses.

Data collection procedure

The study was carried out among nurses employed in different hospitals. Data were collected via an online survey designed in Google Forms. At the beginning of the survey, participants were provided with general information about the study, including its purpose and scope. A consent statement confirming voluntary participation was also included. In addition, the inclusion criteria were clearly outlined to ensure that only eligible participants took part in the study. A total of 472 responses were obtained; however, 16 were excluded due to incomplete or inconsistent data. Thus, 456 valid and fully completed questionnaires were retained for the final analysis.

Instruments

The instruments employed for data collection are described in the following sections.

Demographic data form

The demographic data form included variables such as age, gender, marital status, education level, and sector of employment. Participants were not asked for their identity information.

Humanistic practice ability of nursing scale (HPANS)

The Humanistic Practice Ability of Nursing Scale (HAS) was used to measure the humanistic care ability of nurses. The scale was developed by Zhang et al. (2021) [26], and the scale was adapted into Turkish by Yanmış et al. (2022) [27]. The Turkish version of the scale consists of 29 items. It is a five-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The minimum and maximum scores of the scale are 29 and 145, respectively. High scores indicate that nurses have a high level of humanistic practice ability. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.94. For its subscales, 0.79 for the nursing communication ability subscale, 083 for the psychological adjustment ability subscale, 0.88 for the ethics and legal application ability subscale, 0.66 for the nursing esthetic ability subscale, and 0.88 for the caring practical ability subscale.

Caring behaviors inventory-24 scale (CBIS)

The “Caring Behaviors Inventory-24 Scale” (CBIS), developed by Wu et al. (2006) and adapted into Turkish by Kurşun and Kanan (2012), was used to measure nurses’ care behaviors [28, 29]. The scale is a 6-point Likert-type (1 = never, 6 = always) and consists of 24 items and 4 sub-dimensions (assurance, knowledge-skill, respectfulness, and connectedness). The scale score is calculated by taking the arithmetic mean of the scores for the statements; the scores that can be obtained on the scale range from 1 to 6. A high score on the scale indicates more positive nursing care behavior [28]. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficient for the scale was 0.99, while it was determined as 0.98 for assurance, 0.98 for knowledge-skill, 0.97 for respectfulness, and 0.96 for connectedness.

The CBIS demonstrated very high internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.99), which is consistent with prior validation studies [30, 31]. Although such high reliability may raise concerns about item redundancy, the scale was retained in its original validated form to preserve content validity. Item-total correlations were examined and found to be uniformly high, suggesting conceptual coherence rather than problematic redundancy.

Turnover Intention Scale

The "intention to leave scale" developed by Rosin & Korabik (1991) and adapted into Turkish by Elmas (2012) was used in the study [32, 33]. The instrument comprises four items and employs a five-point Likert response format, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A score of 3 serves as the threshold; values exceeding this point reflect a stronger intention to leave one's job. The internal consistency of the scale is high, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of .90.

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted on complete responses using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) with the PROCESS 4.1 macro [34]. Mediation analysis was performed using PROCESS Model 4 to examine the mediating role of caring behavior in the effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on turnover intention [34]. Confidence intervals for the indirect effects were estimated based on 5,000 bootstrap samples. Percentile bootstrap confidence intervals were used, and an indirect effect was considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. An a priori power analysis was not conducted for the indirect effects. However, the sample size (N = 456) exceeds commonly recommended thresholds for mediation analysis, and the use of bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples provides a robust basis for detecting indirect effects without relying on distributional assumptions. Nurses’ humanistic care skills were included in the analysis as the independent variable, turnover intention as the dependent variable, and caring behavior as the mediator variable. The relationships among the variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Normality of the data was assessed through skewness and kurtosis values, which were found to range between − 0.77 and + 1.53. Since these values were within acceptable limits, parametric tests were employed in the data analysis [35]. In this study, covariates were not included in the PROCESS models because the primary aim was to test the hypothesized mediating mechanism based on the proposed theoretical framework, rather than estimating a fully adjusted causal effect by controlling for all possible confounders. However, in additional robustness analyses performed controlling for age and tenure, the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of indirect effects did not change; this indicates that the findings are not sensitive to covariate specification.

To assess the potential impact of common method variance, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted using exploratory factor analysis. All study items were entered into an unrotated factor solution. The results indicated that a single factor accounted for 43.9% of the total variance, which is below the commonly accepted threshold of 50%, suggesting that common method variance is unlikely to be a serious concern in this study [36].

Although robustness checks with key covariates were performed, the possibility of unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Future research may employ longitudinal designs and sensitivity analyses to further assess the robustness of mediation effects.

Correlation between nurses’ humanistic care skills, caring behavior and turnover intention

The results of the Pearson correlation analysis to determine the relationship between nurses’ humanistic care skills, caring behavior, and turnover intention are shown in Table 2. The analysis revealed that nurses’ humanistic practice abilities are strongly interrelated. Nursing communication showed a strong correlation with psychological adjustment (r = .609, p < .001), ethics-legal application (r = .668, p < .001), nursing esthetic ability (r = .589, p < .001), and caring practical ability (r = .688, p < .001). These findings indicate that nurses who are effective communicators tend to demonstrate stronger ethical sensitivity, psychological adjustment, and practical caring abilities.

Within the Caring Behaviors Inventory-24, several very strong correlation were observed, particularly between connectedness and knowledge-skills (r = .633, p < .001). Respectful also demonstrated strong to very strong correlations with knowledge-skills (r = .653, p < .001) and assurance (r = .664, p < .001), emphasizing the central role of respect in the demonstration of caring behaviors.

Turnover intention was negatively associated with all dimensions of humanistic practice and caring behaviors. The relationships ranged from weak to moderate strength. The strongest negative correlations were with assurance (r = –.325, moderate, p < .001), connectedness (r = –.309, moderate, p < .001), and respect (r = –.292, weak-to-moderate, p < .001). These results suggest that higher perceptions of assurance, connectedness, and respect are linked to lower levels of turnover intention among nurses.

Overall, the findings indicate that while humanistic practice abilities and caring behaviors are strongly and mutually reinforcing, turnover intention is moderately reduced when these qualities are more prevalent. This underscores the dual importance of enhancing nurses’ interpersonal and professional competencies—not only for improving care quality but also for supporting workforce retention.

The effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on turnover intention and the mediating role of caring behavior

The study examined the direct, indirect, and total effects of nurses’ humanistic care skills on caring behavior and turnover intention. First, the effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on caring behavior was found to be significant (β = 0.0376, SE = 0.0037, t = 10.05, p < .001). The model explains 18.2% of the variance in caring behavior (R² = 0.1821). This finding indicates that nurses’ humanistic care skills are a positive and strong predictor of caring behavior.

Secondly, the regression model established on turnover intention was found to be significant (R² = 0.1018, p < .001). The direct effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on turnover intention is negative and significant (β = -0.0076, SE = 0.0037, t = -2.07, p = .0395). However, the effect of caring behavior on turnover intention is also negative and quite significant (B = -0.2214, SE = 0.0416, t = -5.32, p < .001).

Third, the total effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on turnover intention was examined. The results indicate that nurses’ humanistic care skills have a significant and negative overall effect on turnover intention (β = -0.0159, SE = 0.0034, t = -4.66, p < .001; R² = 0.0456).

Finally, according to the results of the indirect effect analysis, the indirect effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on the turnover intention path with caring behavior was found to be statistically significant (β = -0.0083, BootSE = 0.0020, 95% CI [-0.0124, -0.0046]). These findings reveal that the effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on turnover intention is partially mediated by caring behavior. In other words, nurses’ humanistic care skills affect turnover intention both directly and indirectly through caring behavior (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aims to examine the effect of nurses’ humanistic care ability on turnover intention and the mediating role of caring behavior in this relationship.

The findings of the study show that humanistic care ability of nurses reduces their turnover intentions. Zhong et al. (2021), in a study conducted on nurses working in a pediatric clinic in China, showed that the humanistic care teaching model reduced nurses’ turnover intention [37]. Humanistic care meets the needs of patients by providing a patient-centred approach, increasing the comfort of care and treatment effectiveness, and improving the quality of nursing care by reducing adverse events and nurse-patient conflicts [38]. Improved humanistic care ability of nurses may contribute to increased patient satisfaction, reduced workload pressure, and more harmonious nurse-patient interaction [39]. These findings suggest that humanistic care may increase nurses’ job satisfaction by strengthening their sense of professional meaning and value, and thus may decrease turnover intention.

The findings of the present study show that the humanistic care ability of nurses has a significant effect on caring behavior. These findings are in line with previous studies that revealed the effect of nurses’ humanistic care skills on caring behavior [40, 41]. Nurses believe that they should care for patients and help them regain their health, and they try to demonstrate their value by working hard [42]. Humanistic care characteristics form the basis of effective nurse-patient communication by combining nurses’ life experiences and personal insights; nurses with these characteristics can better understand the needs of patients and provide personalized care, and contribute to the development of nursing practice in a more human-centred way [43]. This result supports the assumption that the humanistic approach exhibited by nurses in their professional interactions will be reflected in patient care and may improve the quality of caring. Including humanistic care courses in nursing education programs and supporting them with workshops and simulation-based activities can encourage students to develop patient-centered care behaviors. By encouraging and supporting humane care behaviors, managers can create a culture of humane care in the organization.

The findings of the study show that caring behaviors of nurses reduce turnover intention, and in this process, caring behavior plays a mediating role in the relationship between humanistic care and turnover intention. Kong and Kim (2017) found that manager caring behavior and peer group caring behavior reduced turnover intention [44]. Nurses’ caring behaviors are of critical importance as they directly determine the level of quality of patient care [45]. This finding sheds light on a limitedly examined relationship in the literature and indicates that caring behaviors may be a critical mechanism in reducing nurses’ turnover intention. In this context, our current findings are in line with the general trends in the literature. The obtained result shows that the caring behaviors exhibited by nurses not only increase the quality of patient care but also have a reducing effect on turnover intention. Moreover, the findings reveal that the relationship between humanistic care and turnover intention is effective both directly and through caring behaviors. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, the mediation analysis should be interpreted as statistical mediation rather than causal mediation. Although the proposed directionality theory is based on previous research, alternative causal pathways cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal designs are necessary to determine temporal precedence among the study variables.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the first studies to examine the effect of humanistic caring ability on turnover intention and the mediating role of caring behavior.

One of the most important strengths of this study is that it examines the relationships between humanistic care, caring behaviors, and turnover intention together, which is limited in the literature. In particular, testing the mediation effect contributes to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms between variables. The findings suggest that nurses’ caring behaviors and humanistic approaches may have an impact not only on patient care but also on nurses’ tendency to stay in the organization, which has valuable implications for practice. In addition, this study provides a new perspective that can be related to theoretical frameworks such as job satisfaction, burnout, and organizational commitment. In this respect, the findings are instructive for both clinical practice and the development of people-centred management strategies in healthcare organizations.

The fact that the study was conducted only with nurses working in hospitals in Turkey limits the generalizability of the findings and constitutes a limitation that may lead to different results in different sectors or cultural contexts. The fact that the data used in the study are based on the self-reports of the participants carries the risk that the responses may be affected by subjective perceptions. This means that nurses’ personal evaluations regarding care practices and turnover intentions may lead to differences in the findings. In addition, the study only addressed the interactions between caring behaviors, humanistic care ability, and turnover intention; other forms of leadership, different models of caring, or psychosocial variables were excluded. The findings suggest that nurse managers’ focus on strategies to improve nurses’ humanistic care ability and caring behaviors can make significant contributions to reducing turnover intentions.

Since the study sample was obtained through convenience sampling, the generalizability of the results is inherently limited. Data in this study were collected using self-report measures. Therefore, participants’ responses may have varied due to personal perceptions, recall bias, or social desirability effects, thus limiting the accuracy and objectivity of the results.

In this study, since the survey was administered online and information about participants’ hospital affiliations was not collected, it was not possible to account for potential clustering effects at the hospital level. It is recommended that institution-defined or nested data structures be used in future studies to examine variance at the hospital level.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding temporal ordering among the study variables.

Nevertheless, the very high internal consistency of the CBIS may indicate some degree of item overlap, which should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of associations.

Conclusion

The findings of this study show that nurses’ caring behaviors are effective in reducing turnover intention and play a mediating role in the relationship between the humanistic care ability and turnover intention. The results reveal that strengthening the humanistic approach and caring behaviors in nursing practices can increase job satisfaction, reduce occupational stress, and support harmony in patient-patient relationships. The development of policies and practices in this direction by nurse managers will increase both the retention of employees in the profession and the quality of the service provided. Testing the findings in different organizations and cultural contexts will provide an important contribution to future studies.

It is recommended to integrate the humanistic care teaching model into vocational education programs in nursing. In addition, it may be useful to evaluate the humanistic levels of nurse candidates before education and to shape vocational education processes in this direction.

Future studies

This study was conducted only on nurses in Turkey; therefore, the generalizability of the findings should be tested by conducting similar studies in different cultural and institutional contexts. Future studies may examine the effects of caring behaviors and a humanistic approach on turnover intention in different groups of nurses. In addition to quantitative data, the use of qualitative methods will contribute to a deeper understanding of nurses’ experiences and perceptions. It is recommended that future studies consider moderator variables in addition to the mediation model to more comprehensively examine the relationship between nurses’ humanistic caring abilities and turnover intentions. For example, variables such as perceived organizational support, burnout or emotional exhaustion, workforce participation or job satisfaction, and leadership style (e.g., transformational or authentic leadership) may strengthen or weaken the relationships between caring behaviors and turnover intentions. Examining such moderators will both deepen the theoretical framework and contribute to the development of practical managerial and educational strategies.
