Asciminib as first-line therapy in chronic myeloid leukemia: a systematic review
Authors: Jae Joon Han, Jeong-Ok Lee, Kyoung Ha Kim, Sung-Eun Lee, Sang Kyun Sohn, Chul Won Jung, Hawk Kim, The Korean Society of Hematology Chronic Myeloid Leukemia Working Party
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeChronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by the BCR::ABL1 fusion oncogene. Asciminib, a first-in-class STAMP (Specifically Targeting the ABL Myristoyl Pocket) inhibitor, represents a novel therapeutic approach distinct from conventional ATP-competitive tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). This systematic review evaluated the efficacy and safety of asciminib as first-line therapy in patients with newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive systematic review following PRISMA guidelines. Electronic searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and clinical trial registries through December 2025. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, and compassionate use programs evaluating asciminib as first-line therapy in adult patients with chronic-phase CML. Primary outcomes included molecular response rates (major molecular response [MMR], MR4, and MR4.5), whereas secondary outcomes included safety profiles and treatment discontinuation rates.ResultsThe systematic review identified eight eligible studies comprising more than 500 patients. In the pivotal ASC4FIRST phase 3 trial (n = 405), asciminib demonstrated superior efficacy compared with investigator-selected TKIs. At 96 weeks, MMR rates were 74.1% with asciminib compared with 52.0% with investigator-selected TKIs (treatment difference, 22.4%; 95% CI, 13.6–31.3%; P < .001) and 76.2% with asciminib compared with 47.1% with imatinib (treatment difference, 29.7%; 95% CI, 17.6–41.8%; P < .001). The ASCEND study (n = 101) reported an MMR rate of 79% at 12 months.The safety profile was distinct from that of ATP-competitive TKIs. Hypertension occurred more frequently with asciminib (all grades, 10.5%; grade ≥ 3, 5.5%), whereas arterial occlusive events (all grades: asciminib, 2.0%; investigator-selected TKIs, 1.5%) and clinical pancreatitis (all grades, 1.0% in both groups) occurred at comparable rates between the treatment groups. Treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was lower with asciminib (5.0%) than with second-generation TKIs (12.7%).ConclusionAsciminib demonstrates superior efficacy and a favorable benefit-risk profile as first-line therapy for CML, with higher molecular response rates than both imatinib and second-generation TKIs. Asciminib also has a favorable tolerability and safety profile; however, close monitoring for cardiovascular and pancreatic toxicities is required. These findings support asciminib as an effective treatment option for newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a clonal myeloproliferative disorder characterized by the presence of the Philadelphia chromosome, resulting from a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 [t(9;22)(q34;q11)] that creates the BCR::ABL1 fusion oncogene [1]. This fusion gene encodes a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives the pathogenesis of CML through dysregulated signaling pathways involved in cellular proliferation, differentiation, and survival.

The introduction of imatinib, the first BCR::ABL1-targeting tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), revolutionized CML treatment and transformed the disease from a fatal malignancy into a manageable chronic condition for most patients [2]. Subsequently, second-generation TKIs (dasatinib, nilotinib, radotinib, and bosutinib) and the third-generation TKI, ponatinib, expanded treatment options by providing deeper and more rapid molecular responses [3–7]. However, resistance and intolerance to these ATP-competitive inhibitors remain significant clinical challenges.

Asciminib (ABL001) represents a paradigm shift in CML therapeutics as the first-in-class STAMP (Specifically Targeting the ABL Myristoyl Pocket) inhibitor [8, 9]. Unlike conventional TKIs, which compete with ATP for binding to the kinase active site, asciminib binds to the myristoyl pocket of the ABL1 kinase, inducing an inactive conformation through an allosteric mechanism [10].

Although asciminib was initially investigated in the relapsed/refractory setting [11, 12], recent clinical trials, including the pivotal ASC4FIRST phase 3 study, have evaluated its use as first-line therapy for newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML [13–16]. This systematic review synthesizes the available evidence on the efficacy and safety of asciminib as first-line therapy to inform clinical practice and guide treatment decision-making.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, from database inception through December 2025. The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to asciminib, CML, and first-line therapy.

No language restrictions were applied. The reference lists of the included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. Ongoing and unpublished trials were identified through searches of clinical trial registries.

Review question (PICO framework)

The review question was structured according to the PICO framework. The population (P) comprised adults (≥ 18 years) with newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML. The intervention (I) was asciminib as first-line monotherapy (40 mg twice daily or 80 mg once daily). The comparator (C) consisted of ATP-competitive TKIs, including imatinib and/or second-generation TKIs (nilotinib, dasatinib, and bosutinib). For single-arm studies without an internal comparator, asciminib outcomes were interpreted against external or historical first-line TKI benchmarks. The outcomes (O) included primary outcomes of molecular response rates—major molecular response (MMR), MR4, and MR4.5 at predefined time points—and early molecular response (EMR; BCR::ABL1 ≤ 10% at 3 months). Secondary outcomes included safety and tolerability (adverse-event profile, arterial occlusive events [AOEs], and pancreatic events), treatment discontinuation, and dose-modification rates.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, or compassionate use programs; (2) included adult patients (≥ 18 years) with newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML; (3) evaluated asciminib as first-line monotherapy; (4) reported at least one predefined efficacy or safety outcome; and (5) were published in peer-reviewed journals or presented at major hematology conferences with sufficient methodological detail.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) case reports or case series with fewer than 10 patients; (2) studies evaluating asciminib exclusively in the relapsed/refractory setting; (3) studies with less than 6 months of follow-up; (4) nonhuman studies; and (5) duplicate publications or secondary analyses of previously included cohorts.

Justification for study inclusion. Because the first-line evidence base for asciminib is limited but rapidly expanding, comparative efficacy conclusions in this review were derived exclusively from the randomized ASC4FIRST trial. Single-arm studies (ASCEND) and observational (real-world) reports were included to corroborate efficacy signals and, more importantly, to characterize the safety and tolerability profile of asciminib more comprehensively than could be achieved with the single randomized controlled trial (RCT). These nonrandomized data are clearly delineated as supplementary throughout the review and were not pooled with the randomized evidence for efficacy estimation. By exception to the case-report exclusion criterion, a single case report was retained solely to document a rare hematologic safety event (refractory pancytopenia) and did not contribute to any efficacy analyses. This hierarchical use of evidence preserves the methodological rigor of the systematic review while reflecting the current maturity of the evidence on first-line asciminib.

Data extraction

We extracted data using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics (study design, sample size, and study duration), patient demographics and baseline characteristics, treatment protocols, and outcomes. Efficacy outcomes included molecular response rates at various time points (MMR [BCR::ABL1 ≤ 0.1%], MR4 [≤ 0.01%], MR4.5 [≤ 0.0032%]), complete cytogenetic response, and progression-free survival. Safety outcomes included adverse events (AEs), treatment discontinuation rates, and treatment-related mortality.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomized controlled trials and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies. Studies were evaluated across domains, including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Results

Study selection

The initial database search identified 488 records. After the removal of duplicates, 353 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 43 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, eight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review. Three studies provided primary efficacy and safety data for first-line therapy: one RCT (ASC4FIRST) [13, 14], one single-arm prospective study (ASCEND) [15], and one subgroup analysis from ASC4FIRST (Japanese cohort) [16]. An additional five studies contributed supplementary data: one phase 1 study [18], one case report [19], one real-world compassionate use program [20], and two long-term follow-up reports from relapsed/refractory settings that provided extended safety data [21, 22]. Risk of bias and quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for the randomized trial and the NOS for nonrandomized studies. For the pivotal ASC4FIRST RCT, the randomization process, handling of missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result were all judged to be at low risk of bias. The central, laboratory-based quantification of BCR::ABL1 transcripts provided an objective primary endpoint that is robust to bias arising from the unblinded study design. The trial was judged to raise "some concerns" in the domain of deviations from intended interventions because of its open-label design, yielding an overall RoB 2 judgment of "some concerns." The ASC4FIRST Japanese subgroup analysis inherited the parent trial’s risk-of-bias profile, with the additional caveat inherent to subgroup analyses. The single-arm ASCEND study was rated as being of moderate methodological quality on the NOS, with adequate cohort selection and outcome ascertainment but no internal comparator group, which precluded attribution of comparative efficacy. The supplementary phase 1 study, real-world study, long-term relapsed/refractory studies, and case report were considered to be at high risk of bias for efficacy outcomes because of their noncomparative or anecdotal designs and were therefore used only to provide context regarding safety and tolerability. Two reviewers performed the assessment independently, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. A summary of the risk-of-bias judgments is presented in Table 2. Risk-of-bias and quality assessment of included studies

Study characteristics

The included studies encompassed a total of 506 patients with newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML who received asciminib as first-line therapy. The pivotal ASC4FIRST trial was a phase 3, multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled study comparing asciminib 80 mg once daily with investigator-selected TKIs (imatinib, nilotinib, dasatinib, or bosutinib) in 405 patients [13, 14]. The ASCEND study was a single-arm, multicenter trial conducted by the Australasian Leukaemia and Lymphoma Group that evaluated asciminib 40 mg twice daily in 101 patients, with dose escalation to 80 mg twice daily for suboptimal responders [15]. Median follow-up ranged from 21 months (ASCEND) to 2.2 years (ASC4FIRST secondary analysis).

The ASC4FIRST trial met both primary objectives, demonstrating statistically significant superior efficacy of asciminib compared with all investigator-selected TKIs combined and compared with imatinib alone [13, 14]. At week 48 (primary analysis), MMR rates were 67.7% with asciminib compared with 49.0% with investigator-selected TKIs (treatment difference, 18.9%; 95% CI, 9.6–28.2%; P < 0.001). In the imatinib stratum, MMR rates were 69.3% with asciminib compared with 40.2% with imatinib (treatment difference, 29.6%; 95% CI, 16.9–42.2%; P < 0.001) [13].

In the secondary analysis, with a median follow-up of 2.2 years, MMR rates at week 96 were 74.1% with asciminib compared with 52.0% with investigator-selected TKIs (treatment difference, 22.4%; 95% CI, 13.6–31.3%; P < 0.001) and 76.2% compared with 47.1% with imatinib (treatment difference, 29.7%; 95% CI, 17.6–41.8%; P < 0.001), meeting both key secondary objectives [14]. MMR rates were 72.0% with asciminib compared with 56.9% with second-generation TKIs (treatment difference, 15.1%; 95% CI, 2.3–28.0%; P < 0.05), suggesting a possible clinical benefit [14].

Deep molecular responses were also superior with asciminib. At week 96, MR4 rates were 48.8% with asciminib compared with 27.5% with investigator-selected TKIs, whereas MR4.5 rates were 30.9% compared with 17.7%, respectively. The EMR rate (BCR::ABL1 ≤ 10% at 3 months) was 89.6% with asciminib compared with 70.1% with investigator-selected TKIs [14].

ASCEND study results

The ASCEND study evaluated asciminib as first-line therapy, starting at 40 mg twice daily [15]. The study included protocol-defined dose escalation (to 80 mg twice daily) for suboptimal responses and the addition of an ATP-competitive TKI for treatment failure. With a median follow-up of 21 months, the coprimary endpoints were achieved: EMR at 3 months in 93% of patients (96% CI, 86–97%) and MMR by 12 months in 79.2% (95% CI, 69.7–86.8%). The cumulative incidence of MR4.5 was 52.9% by 24 months. No deaths occurred during the study, and only one patient progressed to lymphoid blast phase.

Japanese subgroup analysis

In the Japanese subgroup of the ASC4FIRST trial (n = 38), efficacy outcomes were consistent with those observed in the overall study population. At week 48, MMR rates were 81.0% with asciminib compared with 47.1% with investigator-selected TKIs. The overall safety and tolerability profile of asciminib in Japanese patients was also consistent with that of the global study population [16].

The safety profile of asciminib as first-line therapy was distinct from that of ATP-competitive TKIs, with pancreatic toxicity and cardiovascular events representing clinically relevant AEs that require specific monitoring. In the ASC4FIRST trial, asciminib was associated with lower rates of grade ≥ 3 AEs (ASC: 44.5% vs. IS-TKIs: 54.7%) and AEs leading to treatment discontinuation compared with investigator-selected TKIs (ASC: 5.0% vs. IS-TKIs: 12.9%) [14].

Pancreatitis

Pancreatic enzyme elevation, rather than clinical pancreatitis, emerged as a characteristic adverse event of asciminib therapy. In the ASC4FIRST trial, the overall incidence of increased lipase (all grades) was 13.5% with asciminib compared with 14.9% with investigator-selected TKIs. Grade ≥ 3 lipase elevation occurred in 3.0% compared with 3.5% of patients, respectively [14]. Although asymptomatic lipase and amylase elevations were observed in 21% and 7% of patients, respectively, in the ASCEND trial, actual clinical pancreatitis was rare [15]. Most treatment-emergent AEs generally occurred within the first 3 months of therapy. Management typically involved dose interruption, followed by treatment resumption at a reduced dose upon resolution. Permanent discontinuation due to pancreatitis occurred in 0.5% of asciminib-treated patients in the ASC4FIRST trial.

Cardiovascular events

Cardiovascular AEs were observed with asciminib therapy, although they generally occurred at low rates. In the ASC4FIRST trial, hypertension (all grades) occurred in 10.5% of asciminib-treated patients compared with 5.0% of those receiving investigator-selected TKIs. Grade ≥ 3 hypertension occurred in 5.5% compared with 3.5%, respectively [13]. The ASCEND monotherapy trial reported treatment-emergent hypertension (all grades) in 22% of patients, with Grade ≥ 3 hypertension occurring in 3% [14, 15]. AOEs in the ASC4FIRST trial were reported in 2.0% of patients receiving asciminib, compared with 0% of those receiving imatinib and 2.9% of those receiving second-generation TKIs [13]. In the ASCEND study, one patient with multiple preexisting cardiovascular risk factors experienced two cerebrovascular events (transient ischemic attacks); no other AOEs were reported [15]. These findings suggest that the cardiovascular safety profile warrants careful consideration in patients with preexisting cardiovascular risk factors.

Other AEs

Other commonly reported nonhematologic AEs in asciminib monotherapy trials (such as ASCEND) included fatigue (26%), headache (19%), nausea (17%), and musculoskeletal disorders (16%) [15]. In the first-line ASC4FIRST trial, nausea and myalgia occurred less frequently with asciminib than with conventional TKIs, headache occurred less frequently than with second-generation TKIs, and fatigue occurred at similar rates [14]. The incidence of myelosuppression with asciminib varied by hematopoietic lineage compared with conventional TKIs. In the 96-week follow-up data from the ASC4FIRST trial [14], grade ≥ 3 neutropenia occurred less frequently with asciminib (10.5%) than with imatinib (18.2%) and second-generation TKIs (17.6%). Similarly, grade ≥ 3 leukopenia occurred less frequently with asciminib (2.0%) than with imatinib (11.1%) and second-generation TKIs (4.9%). However, grade ≥ 3 thrombocytopenia occurred more frequently with asciminib (13.0%) than with imatinib (6.1%), although it occurred at a similar rate to that observed with second-generation TKIs (13.7%). Pleural effusion, a well-recognized adverse event associated with dasatinib, was rarely observed with asciminib monotherapy. For example, no cases were reported in either the 4-year phase 1 follow-up study or the 2.3-year ASCEMBL follow-up.

Treatment discontinuation and dose modifications

Treatment discontinuation rates due to AEs favored asciminib. In the ASC4FIRST trial, treatment discontinuation due to AEs occurred in 5.0% of asciminib-treated patients, compared with 13.1% of those receiving imatinib and 12.7% of those receiving second-generation TKIs [14]. The trial reports grouped dose modifications rather than reporting dose reductions and dose interruptions separately. The percentage of patients who underwent at least one protocol-defined dose reduction or dose interruption was 47.0% with asciminib, 51.5% with imatinib, and 74.5% with second-generation TKIs. When considering only AEs leading to dose reduction or interruption, the corresponding rates were 33.3% with asciminib, 41.4% with imatinib, and 57.8% with second-generation TKIs [13].

Comparison with historical TKI data

Compared with historical data from pivotal first-line trials of standard TKIs, asciminib demonstrated competitive or superior molecular response rates. The 48-week MMR rate with asciminib (67.7%) compared favorably with historical rates reported for imatinib (30–40% in the IRIS trial and similar studies) and exceeded those reported for second-generation TKIs in their respective pivotal trials: nilotinib 300 mg BID (44% in ENESTnd), dasatinib 100 mg QD (46% in DASISION), and bosutinib 400 mg QD (47% in BFORE) [3, 5, 6, 13]. However, direct cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution because of differences in patient populations, molecular monitoring techniques, and study designs.

Cautious use and special considerations

The distinct safety profile of asciminib necessitates specific clinical monitoring protocols and careful patient selection to optimize therapeutic outcomes. Although asciminib demonstrates favorable tolerability compared with conventional TKIs, vigilance for pancreatitis, cardiovascular events, and hematologic toxicities is essential during treatment initiation and throughout maintenance therapy.

Cardiovascular safety and monitoring

Cardiovascular AEs represent an important safety consideration with asciminib therapy. In the 96-week follow-up data from the ASC4FIRST trial, AOEs occurred in 2.0% (4 patients) of those receiving asciminib. These events included angina pectoris, cerebrovascular accident, peripheral arterial occlusive disease, and coronary arteriosclerosis [14]. Hypertension was observed in 10.5% of patients treated with asciminib (grade 3: 5.5%), underscoring the need for appropriate blood pressure monitoring and management. The ASCEND study reported cerebrovascular events in one patient (1%), emphasizing the importance of cardiovascular risk assessment before treatment initiation [15].

Clinical recommendations include comprehensive cardiovascular risk stratification before initiating asciminib, including assessment of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, and prior cardiovascular events. Blood pressure should be monitored at baseline, every 2 weeks during the first 3 months, and monthly thereafter [23]. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension or recent cardiovascular events require careful risk–benefit evaluation. The FDA-approved prescribing information recommends avoiding asciminib in patients with a recent myocardial infarction, unstable angina, or stroke within the previous 3 months [23].

Pancreatitis monitoring and management

Although pancreatic enzyme elevations are a notable adverse event associated with asciminib therapy, clinical pancreatitis is infrequent. In the overall ASC4FIRST trial, the incidence of increased lipase was 13.5% for all grades and 3.0% for grade ≥ 3 events [14]. Asymptomatic lipase and amylase elevations may occur more frequently in specific populations. For example, in the Japanese subgroup of the ASC4FIRST trial, both lipase and amylase elevations were observed in 23.8% of patients (all grade 1 or 2). Similarly, in the ASCEND trial, increased lipase was observed in 21% of patients (10% grade 3/4), whereas clinical pancreatitis leading to treatment discontinuation occurred in only one patient [15].

Monitoring for pancreatic toxicities, including serum lipase and amylase elevations, is an important component of asciminib therapy management. Patients may experience asymptomatic pancreatic enzyme elevations, although clinical pancreatitis can also occur. Management typically involves dose interruption when these events occur. Following resolution, treatment can often be resumed, although dose reduction may be required. For example, in the ASCEND trial, some patients successfully resumed therapy at reduced doses of 20 mg or 40 mg once daily. Permanent discontinuation of asciminib may be necessary in cases of severe clinical pancreatitis or persistent amylase and lipase elevations following rechallenge [23].

Hematologic toxicity management

Although asciminib demonstrates lower rates of certain myelosuppressive events (such as neutropenia and leukopenia) compared with conventional TKIs, hematologic toxicities still occur and require appropriate monitoring and management. In the 96-week follow-up data from the ASC4FIRST trial, grade ≥ 3 thrombocytopenia occurred in 13.0% of asciminib-treated patients (compared with 6.1% of those receiving imatinib and 13.7% of those receiving second-generation TKIs), and grade ≥ 3 neutropenia occurred in 10.5% of asciminib-treated patients (compared with 18.2% of those receiving imatinib and 17.6% of those receiving second-generation TKIs) [14]. These hematologic AEs were typically managed through dose reductions or temporary treatment interruption [23].

The management of cytopenias during TKI therapy, including asciminib, typically relies on dose modifications. Hematologic AEs, such as neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, are primarily managed through temporary treatment interruptions or dose reductions. In the ASC4FIRST trial, AEs led to dose reductions and treatment interruptions in 14.0% and 33.0% of asciminib-treated patients, respectively, with hematologic AEs representing a primary cause [14]. Although most mild- to severe-grade cytopenias (Grades 1–3) can be effectively managed with temporary treatment interruption or dose reduction, life-threatening events (Grade 4) may necessitate permanent treatment discontinuation [23]. A case report documented a rare instance of refractory pancytopenia requiring treatment discontinuation [19].

Resistance management

Effective management of asciminib resistance requires a tailored approach based on the underlying mechanism of resistance. For patients who develop resistance through myristoyl pocket mutations, such as the A337V alteration, switching to a TKI with a different mechanism of action is the primary management strategy [24]. Dasatinib is recommended as the first-line intervention because of its distinct binding mode, whereas ponatinib serves as a viable alternative for refractory cases [25]. Mechanisms of resistance to Asciminib

In cases in which resistance is driven by N-lobe mutations, such as M244V, which may reduce drug-binding affinity without completely abolishing it, dose escalation of asciminib is the preferred first-line approach to overcome the increased IC50 [26]. If dose escalation is insufficient or poorly tolerated, combination TKI therapy should be considered to suppress the resistant clone.

Similarly, ABCG2-mediated resistance, characterized by increased drug efflux rather than target modification, may initially be managed through dose escalation to saturate the efflux pumps and maintain effective intracellular drug concentrations [24]. However, for patients in whom this mechanism limits treatment efficacy, enrollment in a clinical trial investigating novel combination therapies or next-generation inhibitors is the recommended alternative strategy.

Special populations

Older adults represent a growing patient population in CML management. The ASC4FIRST trial included older adults, with subgroup analyses specifically evaluating patients aged ≥ 75 years. In this older subgroup, asciminib demonstrated favorable efficacy, achieving a 90% MMR rate at week 48, compared with 40% with investigator-selected TKIs [13]. The safety and tolerability profile of asciminib suggests that it may be appropriate for patients regardless of age or coexisting conditions. However, the high prevalence of comorbidities and polypharmacy among older adults necessitates individualized risk assessment and careful TKI selection. Although dose adjustment based on age alone is generally not required, the available prescribing information indicates that dose optimization and dose-reduction strategies are frequently and safely used in very elderly patients, including those aged ≥ 75 years, to achieve molecular responses, improve treatment adherence, and minimize toxicity [23].

Hepatic and renal function should be carefully considered when managing TKI therapy. Although specific baseline dose-modification algorithms for preexisting hepatic or renal impairment are not described in the available trial data, clinical studies such as the ASCEND trial initially excluded patients with markedly elevated liver enzyme or serum creatinine levels. During treatment, asciminib may induce hepatotoxicity, including elevations in alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and bilirubin. In clinical practice and clinical trials, these treatment-emergent hepatic AEs are typically managed through temporary treatment interruptions or dose reductions.

No dose adjustment is required for patients with mild, moderate, or severe hepatic impairment. The prescribing information categorizes hepatic impairment based on total bilirubin and AST levels rather than the Child–Pugh classification system. Asciminib can be administered to patients with severe hepatic impairment (defined as total bilirubin > 3 times the upper limit of normal and any AST value), as pharmacokinetic studies indicate that the slight increases in drug exposure observed in these patients are not considered clinically meaningful [23]. Renal impairment does not require dose adjustment because asciminib is minimally excreted by the kidneys [28].

Potential drug interactions should be carefully evaluated. Although asciminib is partially metabolized by CYP3A4, clinical studies have demonstrated low sensitivity to strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., clarithromycin and itraconazole). Because of asciminib’s wide therapeutic range, dose adjustment is generally not required when these agents are coadministered. In contrast, coadministration with a strong CYP3A4 inducer, such as rifampicin, has been shown to decrease asciminib exposure. Notably, coadministration with oral solutions containing the excipient hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (such as certain itraconazole oral solutions) significantly decreases asciminib absorption and should therefore be avoided. Regarding acid-reducing agents, proton pump inhibitors (e.g., rabeprazole) do not significantly affect asciminib bioavailability. Therefore, asciminib can be coadministered with these medications, unlike some conventional TKIs [28].

ASXL1 mutations

Somatic mutations in genes other than BCR::ABL1 are identified in approximately 20–30% of patients with chronic-phase CML, with ASXL1 mutations being among the most common (approximately 10% of patients) [29, 30]. ASXL1 mutations have been associated with a poorer response to conventional TKI therapy and reduced event-free survival [30, 31]. The ASC4FIRST trial demonstrated that patients with ASXL1 mutations often had lower MMR rates than those without the mutation [14]. In addition, patients with ASXL1 mutations at diagnosis are at a significantly higher risk of developing BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutations, which may lead to treatment failure and inferior failure-free survival [32]. Therefore, patients with CML harboring ASXL1 mutations who receive asciminib should be monitored carefully for persistent ASXL1 mutations and the emergence of new BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutations during therapy. The FASCINATION study (NCT03906292) evaluated the combination of asciminib with ATP-competitive TKIs (imatinib, nilotinib, or dasatinib) in newly diagnosed patients with CML, including nine patients with ASXL1 mutations [33]. Notably, 89% of patients with ASXL1 mutations achieved MMR, and 78% achieved MR4 at 12 months with combination therapy, suggesting that asciminib-based regimens may overcome the negative impact of ASXL1 mutations on molecular response. These preliminary findings support further investigation of asciminib in patients with adverse mutational profiles.

T315I mutation and dosing

The T315I mutation in the BCR::ABL1 kinase domain confers resistance to all approved ATP-competitive TKIs except ponatinib. Asciminib’s unique mechanism of action, which targets the myristoyl pocket, provides activity against the T315I mutation [24]. However, higher doses are required for the effective treatment of T315I-mutated CML than for wild-type disease. Whereas the standard recommended dose for patients without the T315I mutation is 40 mg twice daily or 80 mg once daily, patients harboring the T315I mutation require 200 mg twice daily to achieve adequate drug exposure [34]. Pharmacokinetic modeling demonstrated that, at 200 mg twice daily, 99.4% of patients achieved drug exposure above the 90% effective concentration (EC90), with predicted MMR rates of 20.7% at week 24 and 23.7% at week 48 [33]. Lower doses (40 mg twice daily or 80 mg once daily) demonstrated minimal efficacy in T315I-mutated CML and should therefore not be used. This dose differentiation reflects the reduced sensitivity of the T315I mutation to asciminib and underscores the importance of mutation testing before treatment initiation [34].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes the available evidence on asciminib as first-line therapy for chronic-phase CML. Findings from the pivotal ASC4FIRST phase 3 trial demonstrated that asciminib achieved superior molecular response rates compared with both imatinib and investigator-selected second-generation TKIs in newly diagnosed patients, meeting both the primary and key secondary endpoints [14]. These efficacy outcomes, combined with a favorable tolerability profile, position asciminib as a compelling first-line treatment option for CML.

The unique mechanism of action of asciminib provides several theoretical and practical advantages. By targeting the myristoyl pocket rather than the ATP-binding site, asciminib overcomes many resistance mutations that affect ATP-competitive inhibitors [8, 10, 24]. Additionally, its distinct binding site reduces off-target kinase inhibition, potentially improving tolerability. The lower incidence of myelosuppression with asciminib than with conventional TKIs is clinically significant, particularly for older adults and patients with baseline cytopenias.

However, asciminib has a distinct safety profile that requires specific clinical vigilance. Although asymptomatic pancreatic enzyme elevations are a notable adverse event associated with asciminib, clinical pancreatitis is rare, occurring in approximately 2% of patients. Elevated serum lipase and amylase levels were reported in approximately 19% of patients across pooled clinical trials, with increased lipase observed in 13.5% of patients in the first-line ASC4FIRST trial [14]. This safety profile necessitates systematic monitoring. The FDA-approved prescribing information recommends assessing serum lipase and amylase levels monthly during treatment, or as clinically indicated. Adherence to these established monitoring protocols and the use of appropriate dose-modification strategies for pancreatic enzyme elevations will be critical to optimizing the risk–benefit profile of asciminib in clinical practice.

Cardiovascular events, including hypertension and AOEs, represent an important safety consideration with asciminib. In the ASC4FIRST trial, AOEs occurred in 2.0% of patients, and hypertension was observed in 10.5% of patients receiving asciminib [13]. Because most of these cardiovascular toxicities occurred in patients with preexisting cardiovascular conditions or risk factors, careful cardiovascular risk stratification and monitoring are warranted. However, the cardiovascular safety profile of asciminib compares favorably with that of certain other TKIs. For example, ponatinib carries a high risk of major vascular events, whereas asciminib is associated with a low-to-moderate cardiovascular risk. Furthermore, asciminib demonstrates a low proarrhythmic risk, with no clinically relevant QT interval prolongation (a recognized adverse effect of nilotinib), and is not associated with the high rates of pleural effusion commonly observed with dasatinib. This favorable safety and tolerability profile suggests that asciminib may be particularly suitable for patients with specific preexisting comorbidities.

The lower treatment discontinuation rates due to AEs with asciminib compared with second-generation TKIs (4.5% vs. 9.8%) represent a clinically meaningful advantage [14]. In chronic diseases such as CML, in which lifelong therapy is often required, tolerability and quality of life are paramount. The ASC4FIRST data suggest that asciminib’s favorable safety and tolerability profile may enable more patients to remain on effective long-term therapy.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the open-label design of the ASC4FIRST trial may introduce bias, although the objective nature of molecular response measurements mitigates this concern. Second, the relatively short median follow-up duration (2.2 years) precludes assessment of very long-term outcomes, including long-term AEs, overall survival, and treatment-free remission rates. Third, cross-trial comparisons with historical TKI data should be interpreted with caution.

The optimal positioning of asciminib in the first-line treatment algorithm for CML continues to evolve. Following the ASC4FIRST results, the FDA approved asciminib for newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML in October 2024, and the NCCN Guidelines incorporated asciminib as a preferred first-line treatment option [35]. More recently, the 2025 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) recommendations included asciminib as a recommended first-line treatment option for patients with chronic-phase CML [36]. The 2025 ELN recommendations recognize asciminib’s superior efficacy and favorable safety profile, positioning it alongside second-generation TKIs as a preferred initial therapy for appropriate patients.

For patients who prioritize deep molecular responses and the potential for treatment-free remission, asciminib represents an attractive treatment option. In patients with cardiovascular risk factors, however, the risk–benefit profile should be carefully weighed against those of other TKIs. Future research priorities include longer-term follow-up to assess the durability of molecular responses, studies evaluating treatment-free remission strategies with asciminib, and real-world effectiveness data generated outside clinical trial settings.

Conclusion

Asciminib represents a significant advance in the first-line treatment of chronic-phase CML. As the first STAMP inhibitor, it offers a distinct mechanism of action that translates into superior efficacy, with molecular response rates exceeding those achieved with both imatinib and second-generation TKIs. Its unique safety profile, characterized primarily by pancreatic toxicity and manageable cardiovascular risks, requires specific monitoring while also offering advantages, including reduced myelosuppression and lower treatment discontinuation rates than conventional TKIs.

In clinical practice, asciminib is now established as a viable first-line treatment option for patients with newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML. The ASC4FIRST trial supports its use as a preferred option for patients seeking optimal efficacy with favorable tolerability. However, careful patient selection, systematic monitoring for pancreatic toxicity during the first 6 months of treatment, cardiovascular risk assessment, and close monitoring of patients with ASXL1 mutations remain essential components of asciminib therapy management.

This systematic review supports the consideration of asciminib as a standard-of-care first-line treatment option for appropriately selected patients with chronic-phase CML. As longer-term data become available, the role of asciminib in the evolving treatment landscape of CML will continue to be refined.
