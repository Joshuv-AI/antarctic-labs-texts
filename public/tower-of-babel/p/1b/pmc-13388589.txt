Marital satisfaction among women: an exploratory study of a sample from Egyptian society
Authors: Shaimaa S Abdelrheem, Talal Alburaiki, Taher M Darwish, Hasnaa Khairy, Doaa M Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the growing evidence of increasing divorce rates and marital distress among Egyptian couples, there are scarce studies that have explored marital satisfaction in Egypt. This study aimed to assess marital dissatisfaction, utilization of marital counseling services, and attitude toward seeking these services among married women in Upper Egypt.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was applied. An Interviewer-administered questionnaire was filled out by 540 formally married women attending outpatient clinics at Aswan University Hospital. It inquired about personal data, the Marital Satisfaction Scale (MSS), whether marital counseling services were utilized, and attitudes toward seeking such services.ResultsThe median and range values of total MSS and attitudes towards seeking marital counseling services were 24 (1–87) and 3 (0–7), respectively. The prevalence of utilizing marital counseling services was 8%. Poor or unknown perception of health was significantly associated with high marital dissatisfaction scores (adjusted ß = 11.959, 95% CI:7.974–15.945). The associates of a positive attitude towards using services were religion, a high education level, a professional occupation, previous consultation with a specialist, and a high marital dissatisfaction score.ConclusionsWomen in Upper Egypt have low to moderate levels of marital dissatisfaction. Poor health perception is positively linked to marital dissatisfaction. There is a low utilization of marital counseling services. High perception of marital dissatisfaction and some women’s sociodemographic features are related to the positive attitude toward utilization of marital counseling services. There is a need to integrate qualified marital counseling services with all its social and health aspects within maternal health care in Egyptian society and encourage women to use these services.Keywords: Married women, Marital satisfaction, Marital counseling, Egypt

Introduction

Marriage is considered the most fundamental relationship in human beings. It is considered the main construct in formulating the family and raising future generations [1]. Moreover, a good marital relationship is central to human well-being and provides meaning and identity sensation to the individuals’ lives [1, 2].

Marital satisfaction is defined as a subjective attitude of the person toward his or her marital relationship [3]. It is a multidimensional concept composed of different aspects of the marital relationship [4]. Ten factors were described as the top ten strengths for expecting a happy marital relationship. They originate from five areas of marital relationships: communication, couple closeness, couple flexibility, personality issues, and conflict resolution [5].

Identifying the potential factors contributing to marital satisfaction helps married couples and marital counseling specialists employ strategies that may yield a more satisfying marriage while simultaneously avoiding behaviors that contribute to marital dissatisfaction [6]. The potential correlates of marital satisfaction include sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, religious affiliation, economic status, and educational level. The other reported contributing factors include cultural considerations and marital factors, such as marriage duration, whether couples have children, and the number of children [7].

Distressed marriage has negative consequences. Individuals with dissatisfactory marriages have a higher probability of worse health perception, poor physical and mental health, and adopting unhealthy stress-coping behaviors such as smoking and alcohol drinking [8–10]. Due to the adverse effects of marital distress on the different aspects of couples’ lives, marital distress has been considered a public health problem [11].

Marital counseling provides a confidential, non-judgmental, and free environment for couples to talk openly about their conflicts and understand and solve their contributing behaviors. It assists couples to positively adjust and stabilize their distressed marital life [12]. People with a positive attitude toward marriage counseling have a greater intention to seek marriage counseling [13].

Marital stability is defined as remaining legally married without divorce, physical separation, or legal separation. A couple divorces if at least one partner believes that a better life would be lived without his/her current spouse. Marital stability is related to but distinct from marital adjustment, marital quality, marital satisfaction, and marital success [14, 15]. Judgment on satisfaction or dissatisfaction of marital life does not depend only on years spent in the couple’s relationship [16]. The Central Agency for Public Mobilization and Statistics in 2021 reported an increase in the divorce rate among Egyptian married couples by 14.7% compared to 2020 [17]. Although the growing evidence of the presence of marital distress among Egyptian couples, few Egyptian studies have explored marital satisfaction, especially among women. Moreover, they focused mainly on evaluating satisfaction among specific populations such as infertile couples, primigravida women, highly educated couples, or couples of certain religions and have another nationality [18–21].

In light of the high divorce rates in Egypt, Muslim Egypt’s Dar Al-Ifta and Christian religious institutions offer marriage counseling services. They specialize in resolving issues between family members and couples based mainly on social and religious aspects. However, such services lack the integration of the medical aspect for dealing with psychological and sexual problems in marital relationships. The current study aimed to measure marital dissatisfaction and attitudes toward seeking marital counseling services, as well as their associations among married women in Upper Egypt.

Methods

Study design and site

A cross-sectional study was conducted among 540 formally married women who sought care for themselves or their children at Aswan University Hospital in Aswan Governorate, Upper Egypt. Aswan University Hospital is the main tertiary referral hospital in the governorate and serves patients from all districts of Aswan, including urban and rural areas. Data collection was conducted from March 2022 to September 2022. The studied population was formally married women aged 18 or above, who were currently living with their husbands for at least 6 months and agreed to participate in the study.

Sample size and technique

A purposive sampling technique was applied. The sample size was estimated using the EPI Info statistical package, Version 7. The applied parameters were as follows: a hypothesized percent frequency of marital satisfaction among married women of 46.7% (the proportion of marital satisfaction from a previous Egyptian study among primigravida women) [19], a confidence level of 95%, a design effect of 1, and a margin of error of 5%. The minimum estimated sample size was 383 women. The sample size was increased to 540 women to account for possible non-response and incomplete questionnaires.

Data collection tool

An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used. It was filled in the Arabic language. It inquired about the following data:

Personal information such as basic socio-demographics including age, educational level, and occupation for both husband and wives, residence urban or rural, Muslim or Christian religion, and whether women were living in nuclear or extended families. The researchers asked the participants to report on their perceived health status.

It was developed and tested by Canel in 2013 among Turkish couples. The authors received the request to use the questionnaire from the developers. The MSS scale was designed as 101 statements with two response options: either yes or no. The scale aimed to evaluate the negative perceptions of the subjects about marital satisfaction, for example, ‘There are things disappointing me in our relationship’, ‘Sometimes I want to leave my spouse’, and ‘My spouse has never appreciated me.”

The MSS scale included some statements that were written to indicate marital satisfaction to eliminate the tendency of answering in one way, such as ‘‘We have intense love and affection in our relationship’ and ‘‘I cannot imagine life without my spouse.”

For calculating the scores for the scale, one point was given for each positively worded answer (those with a “yes” answer option). To ensure consistency of the marital dissatisfaction construct, reverse scoring was performed for the statements indicative of marital satisfaction before calculating the final scores. Scoring for the whole scale was obtained, and separate scoring was performed for each sub-dimension. The increase in the obtained total score from the scale implies a higher level of general marital dissatisfaction. Concurrently, high scores for any measured sub-dimension reveal the presence of an obvious problem in this issue. In contrast, the low scores gained from the scale indicate the presence of marital satisfaction.

A standardized translation of the English version of the MSS scale was performed. A forward–backward translation process was done by bilingual experts to ensure semantic and cultural equivalence. The translated tool was reviewed by three public health professionals for content clarity and cultural relevance. Full psychometric validation, including construct validity and factor structure, was not performed. However, the reliability of MSS scales was assessed, and the calculated Cronbach’s alpha coefficient values were satisfactory for all the subscales except the paying subscale. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient values were as follows: marital harmony (0.949), relation (0.891), conflict (0.890), closeness (0.875), anger (0.814), communication with spouse family (0.803), parenting (0.714), and paying (0.564). Although Cronbach’s alpha coefficient value for the paying subscale was less than a satisfactory level of 0.7, Taber (2018) considered a range of values (0.45–0.96) to be a sufficient value for reliability testing [23].

4.The final part of the questionnaire included the assessment of women’s attitudes toward seeking marital counseling, whether she had sought a previous marital counseling service, her barriers for not seeking marital counseling, and whether she had alternative sources of support, such as parents, brothers/sisters, and friends. The attitude toward seeking marital counseling was assessed using a valid Arabic scale composed of seven statements, and women were asked to respond to each statement with agree or disagree. Each response indicating a positive attitude toward marital counseling was given 1 point, while zero was given to the negative response. The increase in obtained scores indicates a more positive attitude toward seeking professional marital counseling [18]. The reliability of the counseling attitude scale was evaluated, and it revealed a good reliability level (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient = 0.776).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM-SPSS version 21 software. Categorical variables were expressed in the form of frequencies and percentages, while the mean ± standard deviation/ standard error or median and interquartile range/ range were used to present numerical variables.

No standardized cutoff values were provided for classifying participants into categories of dissatisfaction or positive attitude towards marital counselling. The scales’ scores were treated as continuous variables in the statistical analysis. After testing data normality, Spearman’s correlation was used to test the correlation between the different subscales of the MSS scale. Adjusted and unadjusted linear regression analyses were performed. The outcome variables were the scores of the MSS scale and its subscales, as well as the score of attitudes toward seeking marital counseling. The regression analysis encompassed the total MSS scale and subdimensions of MSS scales as separate outcome variables across different regression models. The MSS subscales were examined individually, as each subscale captured a distinct aspect of satisfaction in marital life. In addition, the total score of the MSS scale was considered as a composite construct that provides an overview of the combined different aspects. The higher scores on the total MSS scale and its subscales indicated greater marital dissatisfaction. Accordingly, positive beta coefficients in the regression models reflect higher levels of marital dissatisfaction, whereas negative coefficients indicate lower levels.

In contrast, higher scores of attitudes toward seeking marital counseling indicate a more favorable attitude. Thus, positive beta coefficients represent more favorable attitudes, while negative coefficients indicate unfavorable attitudes.

The explanatory variables in the adjusted linear regression models were the significant variables that resulted from the unadjusted screening analyses, in addition to the age variable that was added to all the adjusted models a priori. The absence of multicollinearity that might negatively affect the regression models was ensured, where all the values of variance inflation factor (VIF) in the adjusted linear regression models were less than 5. To further address potential model instability arising from conceptual overlap between subscales, each MSS subscale was entered as a separate outcome in an independent regression model, rather than simultaneously as predictors, thereby avoiding structural multicollinearity between correlated subdimensions.

All the performed analytic tests, including the total MSS and parenting subscales, were limited to women who have children (n = 484). The unstandardized coefficient and 95% confidence level were reported for the constructed models. For all statistical tests, a significant p-value was considered when it was less than 0.05. The graphic presentation was done using the Excel program.

Table 4 shows the adjusted and unadjusted linear regression models of increased marital dissatisfaction among the studied Egyptian women. Discussion

Marital satisfaction and the utilization of marital counseling services are critical components of marital stability and relationship quality. This study explores the dynamics of marital dissatisfaction and attitudes toward seeking marital counselling among Egyptian women in Aswan Governorate, shedding light on their relationship with demographic and socioeconomic factors, and barriers to seeking marital counselling.

This current study found relatively low to moderate levels of dissatisfaction across all subdimensions and the total score of the marital satisfaction scale, reflecting a general trend of marital stability with areas of dissatisfaction among the studied Egyptian women.

Caution with the generalizability of these findings to the broader population of Egyptian women in Aswan Governorate should be considered due to the recruitment of studied women from a hospital by applying a non-probability sampling technique, which may introduce selection bias and, consequently, limit the validity of the results [24]. However, similar trends have been observed among Egyptian and Jordanian married women in previous research.

In Egypt, most of the primigravida women (93.4%) were either partially or highly satisfied in their marriages [19], and a large proportion of Egyptian menopausal women (77%) experienced moderate to high marital satisfaction [25]. Relatively low dissatisfaction in marital harmony was reported among Jordanian women, suggesting that many women in traditional societies perceive their marriages as cooperative and stable [26]. Thus, the rise in divorce rates may not necessarily indicate increasing marital dissatisfaction but could instead reflect a greater ability and autonomy to leave unsatisfactory relationships [27].

Regarding the associates of marital satisfaction, the perception of poor health emerged as the sole significant factor of marital dissatisfaction in this study. Similar previous studies have consistently highlighted the deep interconnection between marital satisfaction and Egyptian women’s well-being, particularly mental health [9, 28]. However, the temporal ambiguity inherent in the applied cross-sectional design employed for the current study limits the ability to infer a causal relationship between poor health perception and marital dissatisfaction, where poor health perception could be a consequence of low marital satisfaction [29].

Additionally, the study identified interactions between subdimensions of marital satisfaction and demographic factors. For example, anger and disharmony were found to have a bidirectional relationship, where each was a significant associate in adjusted regression analysis for the other. This dynamic reflects a reinforcing cycle, where unresolved conflicts and dissatisfaction perpetuate anger, further straining the marital relationship. Similar patterns were observed among abused Palestinian wives, where they reported higher levels of negative communication, marital disharmony, and dissatisfaction with marital life [30]. In accordance, Egyptian wives exposed to emotional abuse reported low levels of marital satisfaction [31].

The current findings can be interpreted within the framework of Social Exchange Theory by Thibaut & Kelley (1959), which proposes that marital satisfaction reflects the perceived balance between relational costs and rewards [32]. Research on Arab couple relationships has confirmed that traditional power dynamics, financial conditions, and gender role expectations represent key cost factors that shape marital quality in the Arab region [27]. The significant associations observed between economic disputes, communication problems, parenting conflicts, and overall marital dissatisfaction in the current study align with this theoretical framework, suggesting that accumulation of relational costs across multiple domains is particularly detrimental to marital quality among Egyptian women.

This study also highlights the influence of demographic and socioeconomic factors on marital dynamics. Violence was found to be more prevalent among housewives and women in non-professional occupations, suggesting that financial dependency and socioeconomic inequalities contribute to marital tension. This finding is consistent with Chis’s critical review that emphasized the critical role of financial stability in marital quality, noting that economic strain is linked to marital dissatisfaction and conflict [33]. Supporting this, the professional and academic status of Portuguese women was significantly associated with marital satisfaction, likely due to increased autonomy and financial independence [34]. However, contrasting evidence was found among Egyptian housewives who reported higher marital satisfaction than employed women, possibly due to cultural norms that value traditional roles and reduce work-related stressors [19].

Gender Role Theory further contextualizes these findings within the Egyptian sociocultural environment. Traditional gender norms in Arab societies, rooted in the concept of Qiwamah (male provision and authority), assign women primary domestic responsibilities while limiting their economic autonomy and decision-making power [35]. Women in lower occupational roles in the current study may therefore experience greater role strain and financial dependence, amplifying their perception of marital costs and dissatisfaction, particularly as Egyptian society undergoes gradual but uneven changes in gender role expectations.

On the other hand, age emerged as a protective factor in our study, with increasing age significantly reducing the probability of violence in marital relationships. This finding is consistent with Yaya’s secondary analysis of the Egyptian Demographic Health Surveys (EDHS) from 2005 to 2014, which showed that married women aged 44 and below had higher odds of experiencing intimate partner violence (IPV) compared to women aged 45 to 49 years [36]. Similarly, improvement in family stability was observed with increased age among Jordanian couples [26]. These findings suggest that older women may benefit from more established marital roles, and greater conflict resolution skills [37], which contribute to reduced violence and enhanced stability in marital relationships, particularly in culturally similar contexts.

The study also revealed that poor communication with the spouse’s family was a significant associate of poor marital harmony. This finding aligns with previous studies in Egyptian society that noted significant influences of in-law relationships on marital satisfaction, as family ties are deeply embedded in marital dynamics. The challenges in communication with in-laws can lead to conflict and marital disharmony [19, 38].

Arabic married women preferred to remain in unhappy marriages rather than filing for a divorce, leading to high rates of “emotional divorce.” The lack of problem-solving and healthy communication skills puts women in a vulnerable position in their marriage. Saudi relationship education programs have been proven to effectively support healthier marriages [39].

In the current study, only 8% of the studied women in Aswan had sought marital counseling; a higher proportion of Egyptian participants (15.6%) reported having received such services in Onsy and Amer’s study (2014). This disparity may reflect regional differences in attitudes toward seeking marital counseling in Aswan, an Upper Egypt governorate, as well as differences in access or awareness of the availability of this service in this region [18]. A higher proportion of help-seeking was reported among a nationally representative Dutch sample of romantic couples, where about 36.4% of the seriously dissatisfied partners considered professional relationship help [40].

Higher education levels and professional occupations were found to be significant associates of more positive attitudes toward marital counseling, highlighting a connection between socioeconomic status and openness to seeking professional help for relationship issues. These results aligned with previous research, as higher education levels were significantly associated with more favorable attitudes toward marital counseling [18], and lower-income individuals demonstrated less favorable views of relationship treatment [41], further strengthening the connection between socioeconomic factors and help-seeking behaviors.

Our findings suggest that individuals who have not previously sought specialized counseling are more likely to hold negative attitudes toward marital counseling. This aligns with a study conducted among American married volunteers, which showed that individuals with prior counseling experience were more likely to seek marital counseling [13]. These results highlight the role of familiarity with counseling in shaping more positive attitudes toward professional help-seeking.

Furthermore, the current study indicates that increased marital dissatisfaction correlated with more positive attitudes toward seeking marital counseling. This finding is consistent with Onsy and Amer’s research, which demonstrated that Egyptian couples who perceived their marriage as being of higher quality were less likely to seek counseling [18]. Additionally, Malaysian research found that participants with negative attitudes toward seeking counseling help reported lower levels of marital satisfaction compared to those with positive attitudes [42]. This suggests that dissatisfaction may act as a motivator, driving individuals to seek external support for relationship challenges.

Our findings also reveal Muslims had significantly more negative attitudes toward marital counseling among our participants. Similarly, Emirati citizens were found to prefer seeking psychological help from religious figures, the Holy Qur’an, friends, or family members rather than professional counselors [43]. Marital counseling could be more acceptable in Arabic countries in maintaining the couples’ marital harmony when it is integrated with approaches combining Islamic knowledge and modern psychological science. An Indonesian systematic review found that counseling based on Islamic teachings not only helps couples resolve conflicts but also strengthens their spiritual bond, which is an important foundation in marriage [44].

Although the Egyptian community is considered a culturally rich society, there is a scarcity of articles exploring marital satisfaction and attitudes toward marital counseling in Egypt. Moreover, in the Arab region, there is a lack of validated culturally adapted instruments for identifying couples with mental, emotional, or behavioral disorders [45]. This limits the comprehensive discussion of some important demographic variables, such as education and employment status and their association with a favorable attitude toward marital counseling.

In the current study, the most reported barriers to seeking marital counseling were having alternative sources of support to discuss marital issues (31.6%), unawareness of the availability of marital counseling services (30%), religious beliefs about maintaining the privacy of marital matters (11.3%), and not knowing a qualified counselor (9.5%). These barriers resonate with Onsy and Amer’s findings, which identified not knowing a good mental health practitioner, lack of time, religious beliefs about keeping marital issues private, mistrust of the confidentiality of mental health practitioners, and financial limitations as common obstacles to seeking counseling [18]. A US-based study further highlighted barriers such as cost, scheduling difficulties, preferences for specific treatment approaches, lack of trust, the need for a clear therapeutic process, and relationship dynamics [46].

The low utilization of marital counseling services (8%) can be further explained through Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use, which conceptualizes utilization as a function of predisposing factors (cultural beliefs, stigma), enabling factors (awareness, accessibility), and need factors (perceived severity). In Arab populations, predisposing barriers — particularly stigma, religious beliefs about the privacy of personal matters, and cultural norms discouraging help-seeking — have been identified as the dominant determinants of low counseling service utilization [45, 47]. The finding that 30% of women were unaware of available services and 11.3% cited religious privacy beliefs as barriers is fully consistent with this model, suggesting that community-based awareness campaigns delivered through religious institutions may be the most culturally effective strategy for improving counseling uptake in Egypt.

Regarding alternative sources of support for discussing marital issues, the present study found that approximately 37% of the women consulted their parents, while about one-third (33.1%) turned to their siblings. This pattern reflects a reliance on close family members for advice and support. Similarly, a Chinese study found that individuals tended to seek advice first from family members and friends [48]. These findings highlight the fundamental role that family and social networks play in addressing marital issues, often taking precedence over professional help in many cultures.

Limitations of the study

The current study has some limitations. First, the applied cross-sectional study design has a limited ability to establish a cause-and-effect relationship, as it measured exposure and outcome simultaneously among married women at a single point in time. Consequently, the resulting temporal ambiguity makes it difficult to definitively establish whether the poor health outcome preceded marital dissatisfaction.

Second, the existence of selection bias threats in the study could limit the generalizability of the yielded results and restrict forming a complete representative picture of marital satisfaction for all Egyptian married women, as it was conducted only among women in Upper Egypt. The studied women were recruited from a single tertiary hospital, where the current study was a hospital-based study and not a community-based one. Consequently, the results might be restricted to married women who seek health care from similar health care facilities. Moreover, using a probability sampling technique was not feasible. The study applied a purposive sampling technique to capture the studied women within a restricted time frame, as the women needed to depart the hospital quickly after receiving the health care services.

Moreover, the proportion of women reporting poor or unknown health perception in the current study (22%) may be relatively higher than in community-based samples due to the clinical recruitment setting; therefore, the association between poor health perception and marital dissatisfaction should be interpreted and generalized with caution.

Third, the current study is a quantitative study design. The authors did not explore in depth the barriers to seeking marital counselling services. It is recommended to explore the topic with future qualitative studies for a deeper understanding of women’s sociocultural perspectives.

Fourth, the Arabic version of the Marital Satisfaction Scale (MSS) was prepared through a forward–backward translation process, and its reliability was assessed; however, full psychometric validation in the Egyptian context was not performed. Specifically, construct validity and factor structure of the Arabic MSS were not examined among Egyptian women. This absence of full construct validation may influence the interpretation of the findings and limit the cross-cultural applicability of the instrument. Future studies are strongly recommended to conduct comprehensive psychometric validation of the Arabic MSS, including confirmatory factor analysis, in Egyptian community-based populations.

Conclusion

Women in Upper Egypt have low to moderate levels of marital dissatisfaction. Poor health perception is linked to marital dissatisfaction. In addition, there is a low utilization of marital counseling services. High perception of marital dissatisfaction and women’s sociodemographic features are related to a positive attitude toward the utilization of marital counseling services.

There is a need to provide qualified marital counseling services with all its social and health aspects in Egyptian society. Concurrently, raising women’s awareness about these services and overcoming their perceived barriers may enhance the utilization of these services.
