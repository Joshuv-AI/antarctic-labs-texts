Endobronchial valve placement for persistent air leaks in patients with underlying lung disease: a case series from a resource-strapped county hospital
Authors: Brannen Liang, David Vasconcellos, Ching-Fei Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current guidelines recommend pleurodesis or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for the management of persistent air leaks (PALs). However, for those with underlying lung disease, the interventions may not be feasible. Endobronchial valve (EBV) placement has shown early promise as a viable option in this patient population. The aim of our study is to evaluate the time to successful resolution of PALs following EBV placement. We hypothesize that its early implementation may reduce hospital costs, particularly in the setting of a public safety-net hospital. A single-center retrospective case series study was conducted on six consecutive patients who received EBVs for PALs. Successful resolution of PAL was defined as the following: (I) complete cessation of bubbling in the atrium, (II) no pneumothorax recurrence on clamp trial, (III) removal of chest tube(s) without re-expansion of the pneumothorax. Hospitalization lengths were compared, including chest tube insertion to valve placement, valve placement to removal of the chest tube, and hospital discharge. Of the six PAL cases, five were attributed to spontaneous secondary pneumothoraces arising from various chronic lung diseases. In 83.3% (5/6) of cases, the chest tubes were successfully removed before discharge; in one case, the patient was discharged with a Heimlich valve, which was removed a week later in the clinic. On average, chest tubes were removed 4 days post-EBV insertion, compared with preceding hospital stay duration (mean: 29.4±46.9 days). Early use of EBVs for PALs may prove to be more cost-effective than standard-of-care interventions, despite higher upfront costs. Our case series demonstrating 6/6 successful closures surpasses that seen in the current literature. This may be attributed to intervening on a subset of patients with chronic lung disease and spontaneous secondary pneumothoraces who might have benefited from inadvertent lung volume reduction in the process. Our results suggest that EBV placement may be superior to prolonged chest tube suction in this subpopulation. Early intervention may translate into greater cost savings from reduced hospital days.

Introduction

Persistent air leaks (PALs) following chest tube drainage for spontaneous pneumothorax can be a source of frustration for patients and practitioners alike, leading to infectious complications, increased morbidity and mortality, a prolonged hospital course, and significant healthcare costs (1-3). Often, the wait time for spontaneous resolution of the bronchopleural fistula (BPF) via chest tube drainage can be quite protracted, especially in patients with underlying chronic lung disease due to poor healing ability (4). These patients often have one or more of the following risk factors for PAL, including a low forced expiratory volume in 1 second, preexisting lung disease, pleural adhesions, steroid use, malnutrition, diabetes, previous chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy (5).

Current guidelines from the American College of Chest Physicians and the British Thoracic Society recommend pleurodesis or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) as management for PALs (6-8). However, in more severe air leaks, apposition of the lung to the chest wall may not be physically achievable (9) and subjecting patients to problematic adhesions from chemical pleurodesis may make future thoracic interventions, such as lung transplantation, difficult (10). VATS in those with limited pulmonary reserve and concomitant comorbidities may also prove to be challenging (10). Alternatively, endobronchial valve (EBV) placement, offers a less invasive, bronchoscopic approach that does not require single-lung ventilation, and does not obliterate the pleural space. Multiple case series, review articles, and retrospective studies in the literature have proven its utility in both iatrogenic and spontaneous pneumothoraces with a PAL (11-14).

The aim of this case series was to investigate the time to discharge after successful resolution of PALs following EBV placement comparing it to the prior length of hospital days on chest tube suction. This data may support the hypothesis that a significant cost savings can be achieved if length of hospital stay could be dramatically reduced with earlier EBV intervention. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-187/rc).

Methods

We assembled a retrospective case series of 6 consecutive EBV cases performed at a single center (Los Angeles General Medical Center, a county hospital in Los Angeles, CA, USA) from December 1, 2020 to December 1, 2022. All valves were from Spiration (Olympus, Redmond, WA, USA) and all cases were performed by the same operator (C.F.C.) using a standardized protocol (15). Successful resolution of PAL was defined as the following: (I) complete cessation of bubbling in the atrium, (II) no pneumothorax recurrence on clamp trial, (III) eventual removal of chest tube(s) without re-expansion of the pneumothorax. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Southern California (No. HS-22-00466) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. The study included 6 patients who compassionately received EBVs (Spiration Inc.) for PALs under the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Humanitarian Device Exemption (16). These patients were >18 years old who had EBVs placed for PALs at the request of the inpatient pulmonary consult team and financially approved by the chief medical officer of the hospital. The timing of valve placement could not be standardized due to multiple factors explained later in the discussion. Demographics (age, sex, comorbidities, underlying lung disease), number of valves placed, and outcomes are recorded below in Tables 1,2. Calculated endpoints included length of hospitalization days from: (I) chest tube insertion to valve placement, (II) valve placement to removal of chest tube, and (III) valve placement to hospital discharge. Endpoints were analyzed using descriptive statistics.

Results

In our case series of 6 EBV cases performed for PALs over a two-year time span, we found that 5 of the 6 patients in our study experienced PALs due to secondary spontaneous pneumothorax with an underlying chronic lung disease (Table 1). The remaining patient had a complicated hydropneumothorax from adenocarcinoma of Mullerian origin. The five patients with an underlying chronic lung disease were male. The median age of the six patients was 52.5 years (interquartile range, 46–58 years). In 83.3% (5/6) of cases, chest tubes were successfully removed prior to hospital discharge (Table 2). The remaining patient had their chest tube converted to a Heimlich valve, which was later removed as an outpatient. Mean days from chest tube insertion to EBV placement was 29.4 days [standard deviation (SD) 46.9; range, 16–138 days]. Mean days from EBV placement to chest tube removal was 4.5 days (SD 2.2; range, 1–7 days). Mean days from EBV placement to hospital discharge was 8.2 days (SD 4.4; range, 5–17 days). Discussion

In a resource-strapped capitated public hospital, we investigated the durations of time from chest tube insertion to EBV placement, EBV placement to chest tube removal, and EBV placement to hospital discharge. Among the 6 patients with PALs included in our case series, all were safely discharged home from the hospital within approximately one week of EBV placement. Notably, 5/6 patients had their chest tubes removed on average 4.5 days after EBV insertion, despite having remained on chest tube suction for an average of 29.4 days prior. This raises the question of whether EBV placement could have facilitated an earlier hospital discharge.

Management of PALs in patients with chronic lung disease is often challenging due several factors: (I) impaired healing ability of the diseased lung, (II) difficulty achieving chest wall apposition, and (III) poor surgical candidacy for VATS due to reduced pulmonary reserve. In our study, 5/6 of our patients had an underlying chronic lung disease and tolerated bronchoscopic insertion of EBVs well with few complications. As previously mentioned, patient one who had bronchiectasis was discharged with a Heimlich valve because the PAL did not fully resolve in the days following EBV placement. Another complication was observed in patient four who had fibrosis secondary to acute respiratory distress syndrome. Following EBV placement, this patient would desaturate requiring recovery with inhaled nitric oxide. As a result, serial valve insertions were performed at different time intervals. The patient’s course was complex, requiring transfers between the bronchoscopy suite, a long-term acute care hospital (with bilateral chest tubes), and the intensive care unit. Ultimately, the patient received a total of 3 valves and was able to be discharged from the hospital. Similarly, Donatelli et al. reported a case series of patients who had experienced PALs due to acute respiratory distress syndrome from coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pneumonia requiring mechanical ventilation (3). The patients also had severe parenchymal disease, but resolution was achieved with EBVs. The authors concluded that EBV placement in this patient population is safe, feasible, and effective (3).

In the many of the cases, size 9 valves were placed in more proximal locations to reduce the total number of valves used and lower costs. This led to complete lobar closure in many cases, similar to bronchoscopic lung volume reduction, but without causing severe lobar collapse or a separate pneumothorax afterwards. This may be due to collateral ventilation as fissural integrity was not assessed beforehand, but would be difficult to prove in retrospect. Future investigation on the mechanistic impact of lobar closure during EBV for PALs should be explored.

In addition to efficacy and tolerability in patients with chronic lung disease, we hypothesized that other benefits of early EBV insertion might include: (I) cost-savings by reducing number of hospital days, (II) reduction in complications (infections, deep vein thrombosis, pressure ulcers, etc.) related to an unnecessarily prolonged hospital stay, and (III) improved patient comfort and quality of life. Previous studies have suggested a potential cost-saving benefit of using EBVs for PALs by the reduction of hospitalization days (17-19). However, in these earlier studies, the patient population and etiologies of the PALs varied.

Cattran et al. recently published a study evaluating the economic cost of inpatient management of PALs in patients also with chronic lung disease from lymphangioleiomyomatosis (18). They found that $8,194,680 was spent over a total of 645 days for the inpatient management of PALs in patients with lymphangioleiomyomatosis (18). In a separate cost analysis, Podgaetz et al. found that the use of EBVs was financially justified if the PALs lasted more than 8 days (19). In our study, the true economic impact was difficult to quantify due to the use of differing diagnosis reimbursement rates per patient and the constraints of a capitated public hospital system, but the reduction in hospital days alone suggests the potential cost savings despite higher upfront procedural and valve costs.

There are several limitations. First, this is a retrospective descriptive case series that included a small number of patients, which limits the generalizability of our findings. The small sample size limited the standardization of patients’ backgrounds which resulted in the heterogeneity of comorbidities and underlying lung disease. We also acknowledge that our patient selection and length of stay may be inadvertently biased. Multiple factors influenced patient selection, timing of EBV placement, and discharge. First, the physician who decided when the EBV was required was the inpatient pulmonary consult attending. After discussing the plan with the advanced bronchoscopist who performed all of the cases, financial approval was necessary from the chief medical officer of the county hospital for both the purchase of the valves and the additional costs of inserting them under general anesthesia. Thus, this critical limiting step determined which patients could undergo the intervention—essentially narrowing the pool to only those who were considered extreme cases. Finally, the timing of chest tube removal and discharge could not be standardized because the decision fell upon the inpatient pulmonary consult attending and primary teams, not the advanced bronchoscopist. Because the former rotated every two weeks, and their level of comfort in managing PALs varied, some of the chest tubes were left in longer than necessary. Discharge was also at the discretion of the primary medicine team. However, despite this, the average number of hospital days after valve insertion was only four days, so the above factors mostly confounded the number of days before valve insertion. We still feel that our study demonstrates cost-savings in regards to a faster discharge after EBV placement.

Of note, because of complexities in Medicare diagnosis-related group coding at the county hospital, it was impossible to do a true cost-effectiveness analysis, so length of stay was used as a surrogate for cost. The presumption is that, if hospital days for managing air leaks could be shortened by use of EBV, the upfront costs may be mitigated as well as overall cost-savings achieved. This is particularly impactful in capitated systems such as government hospitals (county, VA) as well as health maintenance organizations systems such as Kaiser Permanente.

Some experts may criticize that our patients were not considered for alternative interventions (i.e., blood patches, chemical pleurodesis, Watanabe spigots) before resorting to EBVs. Again, while these approaches are less costly and reasonable to consider, most of the patients in our case series had unusually long and protracted hospital stays prior to consideration of EBV. Given the number of rotating physicians involved in the care, the management plan was not within our control. However, at the time of the referral for EBV, the most urgent goal was to definitively stop the air leak and discharge the patient as soon as possible. Trying these other various methods with inconsistent success rates reported in the literature could potentially prolong hospital stay, whereas, in our limited experience, EBVs have never failed to solve the problem. In addition, for certain patients who may need lung transplantation or thoracic surgery in the future, blood patch or chemical pleurodesis could potentially make the process more difficult.

Despite the limitations in this case series, we feel that the overall findings are still helpful because it provides a “real-world” perspective on how PALs are managed outside of a controlled study. Certainly, the surprisingly high rate of success in our experience provides an exploratory, hypothesis-generating pilot study that deserves further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, the use of EBVs may serve as a viable option for the management of PALs in selected patients, particularly when conventional approaches are not feasible or fail. Our study suggests that as salvage intervention in patients with chronic lung disease and PALs, EBVs may facilitate rapid and safe resolution. Earlier implementation may also offer potential cost savings through reduced hospital length of stay. We also observed an unusually high success rate (6/6) in patients with chronic lung disease, in contrast to prior studies in the literature (11–14). There may be a mechanistic explanation for this that deserves additional investigation. Further studies are needed to elucidate the optimal timing of valve placement for cost-effectiveness, as well as patient characteristics that might increase the chances of success.
