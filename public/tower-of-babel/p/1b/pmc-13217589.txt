The Efficacy and Safety of Intranasal Dexmedetomidine Spray Followed by Nitrous Oxide for Paediatric Oral Procedures
Authors: Yu-Jie Xiao, Hong-Yu Zhang, Meng-Di Nie, Guo-Sheng Liu, Wei Peng, Ji-Hong Zhao, Zhen-Hao Hu, Huan Sun, Yang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsThe management of uncooperative children requiring complex outpatient oral procedures presents a significant clinical challenge. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of intranasal dexmedetomidine (DEX) spray combined with nitrous oxide/oxygen (N₂O/O₂) inhalation sedation for the extraction of impacted supernumerary teeth in paediatric patients.MethodsThis retrospective clinical study enrolled uncooperative children aged 6 to 12 years (Frankl Scale score 2-3). Preoperative sedation was achieved using 2 µg/kg intranasal DEX spray. After sedation onset, heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) were monitored. Throughout the procedure, N₂O/O₂ inhalation was administered to maintain a moderate sedation level, with HR, BP, SpO₂, and sedation depth recorded at 5-minute intervals. Postoperatively, patients were observed in the recovery room until all discharge criteria were met.ResultsAll 63 patients (34 boys and 29 girls) cooperated with nasal hood placement and successfully underwent parental separation. Sixty children (95.24%) completed the procedure successfully, with 56 (88.89%) maintaining moderate sedation depth throughout. There were significant decreases in HR and BP (P < .001), while SpO₂ (P = .120) remained stable during procedures. Adverse events included drowsiness (5 cases, 7.94%), dizziness (2 cases, 3.17%), and hypotension (8 cases, 12.70%). Only one (1.59%) case experienced hypoxemia concurrently presenting with bradycardia and hypotension, which required brief clinical intervention.ConclusionThe combination of intranasal DEX spray and N₂O/O₂ appears to be a promising sedation technique for impacted supernumerary tooth extraction in paediatric patients, with minimal adverse effects rarely requiring clinical intervention.Clinical relevanceThis protocol shows promise in addressing the challenge of non-cooperation prior to N₂O administration, and may offer an effective, convenient, and relatively safe approach for managing uncooperative paediatric patients undergoing complex oral surgery.Keywords: Intranasal dexmedetomidine, Nitrous oxide, Procedural sedation, Impacted supernumerary teeth, Paediatric dental treatment

Introduction

Impacted supernumerary teeth are a common condition in paediatric patients. Early removal is important to prevent complications such as crowding and damage to neighbouring teeth.1 However, the surgery itself is challenging: it can be invasive, takes considerable time, and is often highly stressful for the paediatric patients. This creates a major problem, particularly for children experiencing dental fear and parental separation anxiety, who frequently cannot tolerate the procedure with non-pharmacological behaviour guidance techniques alone.2,3 At the same time, many parents are hesitant about general anaesthesia due to its perceived risks. Furthermore, the additional time commitment and financial burden associated with general anaesthesia are also significant considerations for many families. Therefore, procedural sedation has emerged as a safe, effective, and well-accepted alternative for managing paediatric oral surgical procedures under local anaesthesia.

Currently, nitrous oxide/oxygen (N2O/O2) inhalation is the preferred sedation technique in paediatric dentistry owing to its quick onset/offset, high safety margin, and low incidence of side effects.4 However, when used for oral surgical procedures in paediatric patients with preoperative anxiety, N₂O/O₂ inhalation is often associated with induction failure and inadequate sedation. This may subsequently lead to frequent intraoperative movement, potentially compromising both the safety and efficiency of the procedure.

Dexmedetomidine (DEX) has been established as a safe and effective sedative for minor paediatric procedures. Its intranasal route has emerged as a prevalent non-intravenous administration method in clinical practice, suitable for preoperative sedation in paediatric patients undergoing invasive procedures.5,6 However, as intranasal DEX produces a sedation state resembling natural sleep that is susceptible to arousal by external stimuli, it is often insufficient as a sole agent for complex surgical procedures involving strong stimulation.

To date, no study has explored the combined use of intranasal DEX spray and N₂O/O₂ inhalation for sedation in complex paediatric oral surgery. Theoretically, this sedation protocol holds significant promise. Intranasal DEX spray, a well-tolerated administration route in paediatric patients, served as an effective premedication to reduce anxiety and improve cooperation, after which N2O can be titrated during the procedure to maintain adequate sedation. This two-step strategy may thus provide effective moderate sedation3,4,7 while supporting respiratory safety. This study is the first to propose and retrospectively evaluate the safety and efficacy of a novel sedation protocol using intranasal DEX spray for preoperative sedation followed by N₂O/O₂ inhalation for maintenance during the extraction of impacted supernumerary teeth in paediatric patients. The aim is to provide an innovative, reliable, and readily generalisable sedation solution for complex paediatric oral procedures in the outpatient setting.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted in the outpatient clinic of the Department of Oral Surgery of the Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University, from January to October 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of School & Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (Approval Number: [WDKQ2025]LUNSHEN(B48)) and adhered to the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its amendments. All data were obtained from the Department of Oral Surgery’s clinical case database for the period from January to October 2025. All treatment decisions, including sedation regimens, were part of routine clinical practice, with no active intervention or allocation by the researchers.

Participants

Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) aged 6 to 12 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I to II; (3) clinically diagnosed with one or more impacted supernumerary teeth requiring extraction; (4) unresponsive to non-pharmacological behaviour guidance techniques with Frankl Scale3 (Table 1) score of 2 or 3 and inability to tolerate treatment with N2O alone; (5) treatment time ≤40 minutes; (6) the sedation regimen was 2 µg/kg intranasal DEX spray combined with N₂O/O₂ inhalation. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) history of allergy to anaesthetics such as DEX or N2O etc.; (2) severe adenoid hypertrophy (radiographic evidence of nasopharyngeal obstruction ≥70%) or severe tonsillar hypertrophy (third-degree); (3) presence of difficult airway (restricted mouth opening, small jaw, restricted neck movement, pathological obesity, etc.); (4) gastroesophageal reflux disease, active upper respiratory infection or uncontrolled asthma; (5) known or suspected congenital heart disease, neuromuscular disorders, or metabolic diseases affecting respiratory drive;8 (6) intellectual disability or cognitive impairment limiting communication; (7) incomplete medical records regarding sedation or vital signs.

Sedation process and perioperative managements

All procedures were performed in a fully equipped outpatient clinic at the Department of Oral Surgery of the Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University. Prior to the surgical procedure, each patient’s body weight, baseline heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and oxygen saturation (SpO2) were measured. The patient was then placed in a seated position, and the nasal passages were cleared. Based on the patient’s weight, the nurse calculated the required dose of dexmedetomidine (2 µg/kg, with a single dose not exceeding 100 μg). The DEX solution (2 mL: 0.2 mg, Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Lianyungang, China) was diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 1.5 mL. Using a 5 mL atomised device (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Lianyungang, China), the mixture was evenly administered into both nostrils. The patient was instructed to tilt the head back slightly and inhale gently to facilitate absorption. The patient was continuously observed, and Ramsay sedation score7, 8, 9 (Table 2) was recorded every 5 minutes.

Approximately 20 minutes after administration, once a Ramsay sedation score of 2 or higher was achieved, the patient typically exhibited calm and cooperative behaviour. The patient was then assisted into a supine position in the dental chair, and monitoring of HR, SpO₂, BP was initiated. Following confirmation of vital signs, a nasal hood was placed to initiate N₂O/O₂ inhalation via a standard titration protocol: introduction of 100% oxygen for 1 to 2 minutes followed by titration of N2O in 10% intervals every 2 minutes.10 After about 5 minutes, when the child reached a deeper level of sedation (Ramsay sedation score ≥3), the surgical procedure was initiated.

The surgery began with the administration of local anaesthesia. This process involved 2 steps: first, compound chamomile and lidocaine hydrochloride gel (Kamistad, STADA Arzneimittel AG, Bad Vilbel, Germany) was applied to the surgical site for superficial anaesthesia to minimise needle insertion pain. Second, after superficial anaesthesia had taken effect, local infiltration anaesthesia was delivered using a computer-controlled local anaesthetic delivery system (Single Tooth Anesthesia System, Milestone Scientific Inc., Roseland, USA) via the system’s handpiece (The Wand, model STA-5050-301, Milestone Scientific Inc., Roseland, USA) with articaine hydrochloride and epinephrine tartrate injection (Primacaine Adrenaline, PRODUITS DENTAIRES PIERRE ROLLAND, Zone Industrielle du Phare, France). Each cartridge was injected at a rate not exceeding 1 mL/min, with a total of 1 to 2 cartridges administered per procedure to ensure adequate analgesia. Following confirmation of adequate anaesthetic effect, parental separation was performed, and the surgical extraction proceeded. The surgical protocol included incision, flap elevation, bone removal using an ultrasonic bone scalpel (US-III LED), and tooth sectioning when necessary with an electric handpiece (ES5). Both instruments (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guilin, China) were used with continuous 4 °C normal saline cooling. Subsequently, the supernumerary tooth was extracted, followed by socket debridement, collagen sponge placement, suture, and pressure haemostasis. We used gauze instead of cotton pellets for site disinfection and haemostasis due to its lower aspiration risk and superior absorbency.

All surgical procedures were performed by an experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeon and a specialised dental nurse. Throughout each procedure, a dedicated attending clinician was responsible for continuous monitoring of the patient’s vital signs, behavioural responses, and sedation depth. This clinician recorded HR, BP, SpO₂, Ramsay sedation score, and N₂O concentration at 5-minute intervals, and dynamically adjusted the N₂O concentration (not exceeding 50%) based on the patient’s real-time sedation depth and behavioural responses to maintain an optimal level of moderate sedation. If oversedation occurred, the patient was aroused by gentle tactile stimulation (e.g., light tapping) and the N₂O concentration was reduced accordingly. Spontaneous breathing was maintained throughout the procedure. Should signs of airway obstruction or respiratory depression occur, immediate interventions were implemented as needed, including temporarily suspending the dental procedure, performing a jaw-thrust manoeuvre, adjusting sedation depth, or providing assisted ventilation via mask.8

Near the end of the surgical procedure, N2O inhalation was discontinued and replaced with 100% oxygen for at least 5 minutes to facilitate recovery. The nasal hood was then removed, and the patient was gently awakened and transferred to the recovery room. Vital signs (HR, SpO₂, BP) continued to be monitored every 5 minutes until discharge criteria7 were met, after which the patient was discharged in the care of the guardians. The time from the end of the surgery to discharge from the hospital was recorded for each patient. The overall procedural sedation workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. A follow-up telephone call was made to the guardian 24 hours postoperatively to record any adverse events.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was overall treatment completion evaluated upon procedure completion using the Houpt Scale3,11 (Table 3). Scores were categorised as: Success (score 3-6) or Failure (score 1-2). Successful cases were further classified as good (score 5-6) or fair (score 3-4).

Houpt rating scale for overall behaviour.

The secondary outcomes included nasal hood acceptance success rate (Mask Acceptance Scale score (Table 4) of 3 or 4), parental separation success rate (Parental Separation Anxiety Scale score (Table 4) of 3 or 4), onset time, recovery time, operator satisfaction assessed using a 4-point scale (Table 4), sedation depth and the incidence of adverse events. Sedation depth was evaluated every 5 minutes using the Ramsay sedation score. Scores were categorised as: successful (scores remained 2-4 at all recorded time points), inadequate (score <2 at 2 consecutive recorded time points, or a level of sedation insufficient to allow the procedure to continue), over (score >4 at 2 consecutive recorded time points, or the occurrence of complications requiring clinical intervention, such as respiratory depression or hypoxemia). Adverse events included hypoxemia, bradycardia, hypotension, drowsiness, dizziness, vomiting, and postoperative agitation. According to Paediatric Advanced Life Support guidelines,12 bradycardia was defined as a heart rate <60 bpm, hypotension as systolic blood pressure (SBP) < (70 + [age in years × 2]) mmHg for children under 10 years old or <90 mmHg for those aged 10 and above, and hypoxemia as oxygen saturation <94%.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were described according to their distribution: normally distributed data as mean ± SD, and non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as a number (percentage).

To investigate the extreme effect of the surgical intervention on vital signs, preoperative baseline values were compared with the intraoperative nadir (lowest recorded values) for HR, BP and SpO2. As these measurements were paired, the difference (preoperative baseline − intraoperative nadir) was calculated for each patient. The normality of these difference scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the differences were normally distributed, a paired samples t-test was applied; if not, the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P-value of <.05.

Results

Baseline patient demographic and perioperative characteristics

A total of 86 records were identified from the clinical database of the Department of Oral Surgery, Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University, for the period from January to October 2025. Excluded patients (n = 23) had records that did not meet the eligibility criteria, including age outside the specified range (n = 5), incomplete medical records regarding sedation or vital signs (n = 10), or procedure time exceeding 40 minutes (n = 8). A final cohort of 63 patients was included in the analysis (Figure 2). The median (IQR) number of tooth extractions in the group of patients was 1.0 (1.0, 2.0). Median (IQR) age was 8.0 (7.0, 9.0) years and weight 24.3 (22.5, 26.3) kg. Mean (SD) onset time was 21.14 ± 3.01 minutes, and median (IQR) recovery time was 61.0 (58.0, 63.0) minutes. The median (IQR) maintenance N₂O concentration was 30.0% (30.0%, 40.0%), with all patients remaining within the protocol-specified maximum of 50% (Table 5).

Treatment compliance and sedation results

Following preoperative intranasal DEX spray, all patients (100%) successfully accepted the nasal hood and commenced N₂O/O₂ inhalation. After the administration of local anaesthesia, successful parental separation was achieved in all cases (100%).

Sixty (95.24%) patients successfully completed the procedure. Among these, 52 (82.54%) demonstrated good cooperation, allowing the surgery to proceed smoothly without interruption, while 8 (12.70%) exhibited fair cooperation, characterised by procedural difficulties or brief interruptions that were ultimately overcome. Three (4.76%) patients failed to complete the procedure owing to insufficient cooperation. Operator satisfaction was rated as “excellent” in 51 (80.95%) cases, “good” in 7 (11.11%) cases, “fair” in 2 (3.17%) cases, and “poor” in 3 (4.76%) cases.

With respect to intraoperative sedation depth, the successful sedation rate was 88.89% (56/63). Inadequate sedation occurred in 1 (1.59%) case, which led to procedural failure. Oversedation was observed in 6 (9.52%) cases (Table 6).

Safety results

Compared to the pre-sedation baseline, there were significant decreases in HR (from 94.35 ± 5.23 to 80.35 ± 6.74 bpm, P < .001), SBP (from 105.10 ± 5.23 to 93.27 ± 6.52 mmHg, P < .001), and DBP (from 67.48 ± 4.55 to 58.30 ± 4.35 mmHg, P < 0.001) following sedation. In contrast, oxygen saturation remained stable (from 98.0 (98.0, 99.0) to 98.0 (97.0, 99.0) %, P = .120) (Table 7). These hemodynamic changes are consistent with the known sympatholytic effects6,8 of DEX. Hypotension occurred in 8 (12.70%) patients. Five (7.94%) patients experienced drowsiness, and 2 (3.17%) patients reported dizziness. No cases of vomiting or postoperative agitation occurred. Furthermore, no post‑discharge complications were reported during follow‑up (Table 8).

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated the efficacy and safety of a procedural sedation protocol: utilising intranasal DEX spray for premedication followed by N₂O/O₂ inhalation for maintenance during the extraction of impacted supernumerary teeth in uncooperative paediatric patients. The results demonstrated a high level of treatment cooperation and sedation success, with a reduction in preoperative anxiety and a low incidence of adverse events.

The surgical procedure of impacted supernumerary teeth often involves gingival incision, flap elevation, bone removal, tooth sectioning, and luxation with elevators. Compared to routine restorative or endodontic treatments, the extraction of impacted supernumerary teeth is more invasive, time-consuming, and involves intense stimuli such as surgical forces, noise, and aerosolised water. Consequently, the procedure demands a high level of cooperation from the patient when performed under local anaesthesia. Children in the mixed dentition stage often exhibit dental anxiety and barriers to separate from parents, which are often amplified during more invasive and traumatic surgical procedures. In such cases, non-pharmacological behaviour guidance techniques alone are frequently insufficient.

In paediatric oral surgery practice, general anaesthesia under an office-based setting is a common choice to ensure complete patient cooperation. However, parental attitudes toward general anaesthesia vary across different cultural backgrounds.13 Many parents express reservations regarding general anaesthesia, primarily due to its substantial cost, prolonged procedure time, and perceived risks. In addition, general anaesthesia imposes strict preoperative fasting requirements, necessitates specialised anaesthesiologists and dedicated equipment, results in an extended recovery period, and carries the risk of postoperative complications such as nausea and emergence agitation. In such cases, moderate sedation offers an alternative clinical strategy. Moderate sedation has been proven effective for delivering comfortable, efficient, and high-quality dental care, while also helping to foster a positive attitude in patients toward future dental treatment.14 In this state, the patient remains capable of responding purposefully to verbal commands or after light tactile sensation. The airway maintains independently, and spontaneous ventilation is adequate.3,7 Compared to deep sedation, moderate sedation allows for better preservation of protective reflexes, thereby reducing the risk of aspiration of water spray and debris generated during the procedure. Furthermore, in the event of oversedation, simple interventions such as calling the patient’s name, gentle stimulation, or chin-lift manoeuvre can usually achieve prompt recovery.

N₂O is one of the most commonly used agents for moderate sedation owing to its short-acting and titratable pharmacokinetic profile. Transported as a free gas, N2O exhibits low tissue solubility and is quickly eliminated via the lungs, thus delivering a rapid onset and recovery (2-3 minutes). And it has little effect on the respiratory system.10 However, in paediatric patients with dental fear, compliance often remains poor even after basic behaviour guidance. These patients may refuse to lie down, resist wearing the nasal hood, or even actively resist through crying, breath-holding, or other behaviours, preventing effective inhalation of N2O and making it difficult to establish an adequate baseline level of sedation. This challenge is especially pronounced during highly stimulating oral procedures, where anxiety-driven movements can severely disrupt surgical progress. In such cases, the use of protective stabilisation,3 while sometimes necessary for safety, may carry the risk of inducing psychological distress or trauma in the child. Although some researchers have proposed rapid induction with high concentrations of N2O,15 the sedation depth achieved may still be insufficient to match the strong stimuli of complex surgical procedures. Children may still exhibit movement and struggle during local anaesthesia injection or surgical manipulation, leading to sedation failure. Moreover, this approach may increase the risk of nausea and vomiting.16

Therefore, to address the challenge of non-cooperation prior to N2O inhalation sedation, premedication is commonly employed to reduce preoperative anxiety. Under ideal conditions, premedication should manifest as a rapid onset of action, easy to administer, alongside minimal adverse effects with good patient tolerance.17 Among the various agents available for preoperative sedation, DEX has emerged as a particularly favourable option for paediatric patients due to its distinctive pharmacological profile, and has been widely used in diagnostic settings requiring complete stillness, such as imaging and electroencephalography.18

DEX is an α2-adrenoceptor agonist with sedative, anxiolytic, sympatholytic, and analgesic-sparing effects. It can induce a state of unconsciousness similar to natural sleep, with the unique aspect that patients remain easily rousable and cooperative. Unlike conventional preoperative sedatives such as midazolam, which act via GABA or opioid receptors, DEX exerts minimal depression on the respiratory function owing to its unique mechanism of action,5,6,18, 19, 20 constituting a significant advantage for its use in outpatient oral surgical sedation. A randomised controlled trial demonstrated that the adjunctive use of DEX in orthognathic surgery significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss, suggesting that DEX may contribute to hemodynamic stability.20 DEX can be administered through various routes, including intravenous, intranasal, oral, or intramuscular administration. Among these, the intranasal spray route offers significant advantages. This entirely non-invasive approach eliminates needle-related pain and anticipatory anxiety while avoiding the discomfort or swallowing reflex associated with intranasal drops, significantly improving paediatric acceptance. The nasal mucosa’s rich vascularity enables rapid drug absorption and onset of action, with a bioavailability of approximately 82%.6 Its proximity to the subarachnoid space and olfactory pathways further facilitates rapid central nervous system delivery, accelerating therapeutic effects.21 Compared to traditional intranasal drops, intranasal spray does not require a supine position, offering greater comfort, higher acceptance among children, and shorter onset and recovery time.22

The Dutch Paediatric Formulary made a recommendation for intranasal DEX used as premedication in paediatric patients aged between 6 months and 18 years of 1 to 2 µg/kg once prior to induction of anaesthesia.23 Similarly, a systematic review and network meta-analysis of 32 RCTs on intranasal DEX for reducing paediatric preoperative anxiety found a nonlinear dose-response relationship, with an optimal dose range of 1 to 2 µg/kg.17 A randomised controlled trial by Yuen et al. found that in children aged 5 to 8 years, a 2 µg/kg dose of intranasal DEX resulted in a significantly higher rate of satisfactory sedation compared to 1 µg/kg, with no adverse hemodynamic events reported.24 Also, in a meta-analysis including 14 randomised controlled trials and 1809 paediatric subjects with ages from 1 month to 14 years to determine the efficacy of intranasal DEX for sedation in children, it was found that the dose of 2 µg/kg seemed to be the optimal choice in light of the benefit-risk assessment across the 1 to 4 µg/kg dose range.25 Furthermore, a meta-analysis found that age and body weight were not significantly associated with the incidence of DEX-induced bradycardia.26 Therefore, a dose of 2 µg/kg intranasal DEX was selected as the standard regimen for this study based on the above evidence, balancing efficacy and safety for this age group.

Consistent with observations in multiple previous studies, our findings suggest that 2 µg/kg intranasal DEX spray appears effective in reducing preoperative anxiety. The mean (SD) onset time was 21.14 ± 3.01 minutes, which is similar to previous studies.14,27 All patients remained calm and cooperative during nasal hood placement, effectively addressing the issue of initial non-cooperation prior to N₂O administration. Unkel et al.18 demonstrated the advantages of intranasal DEX combined with N₂O for sedation during posterior paediatric crown restorations. In the present study, we further explored the potential of intranasal DEX spray combined with N₂O in more complex oral procedures. Except for one (1.59%) case of inadequate sedation, most patients presented with drooping eyelids and a blank stare, remaining calm and permitting local anaesthetic injection. Although needle sensation was perceived, significant body movement or behavioural resistance was rare, possibly owing to DEX in attenuating stress responses.6 After the administration of local anaesthesia, all patients achieved successful parental separation. The surgical procedures were completed in the majority of patients (95.24%) with the combined sedation regimen. Fifty-six (88.89%) patients maintained a moderate sedation level throughout the procedure. Operator satisfaction was rated as excellent or good in 92.06% of cases (58/63).

Pain control is an essential component for maintaining an adequate level of sedation. Although Dawson et al.28 found that a DEX/N2O combination in rats can provide enhanced and more durable analgesia than when N2O is used alone, the effectiveness of local anaesthesia remains the determining factor for the quality of analgesia. Among the 60 successful treatments, 8 (12.70%) patients exhibited fair cooperation primarily due to painful stimulation. The procedures were completed smoothly after effective pain relief. Therefore, we recommend refined and painless local anaesthesia techniques, including superficial anaesthesia and adequate as well as evenly administered local infiltration anaesthesia.

Among the 3 failed cases, all were attributed to uncooperative behaviour. Initially, all 3 patients accepted the procedure following preoperative communicative guidance, and N₂O inhalation was commenced after a Ramsay sedation score of 2 was achieved following intranasal DEX administration. In one case, the patient showed marked resistance during local anaesthetic injection, with the Ramsay sedation score dropping to 1, indicating inadequate sedation depth. The remaining 2 patients achieved the target moderate sedation depth, presenting with calm demeanour and drooping eyelids, but refused to open their mouths when local anaesthesia was about to be administered. All 3 procedures were ultimately discontinued, and the patients were rescheduled for treatment under general anaesthesia. These cases suggest that actual patient behaviour during sedation is more complex than preoperative expectations, and that achieving the target sedation depth does not guarantee procedural cooperation in paediatric dental patients. Under moderate sedation, mouth opening requires active cooperation from the patient, which is influenced by multiple factors, including temperament, previous dental experiences, and the child’s cognitive and psychological maturity.3 Research on paediatric temperament has identified effortful control, which refers to the voluntary and wilful regulation of attention and behaviour, as an important predictor of child behaviour and treatment success during N₂O sedation.29, 30, 31 Although baseline Frankl Scale scores were significantly associated with all behavioural outcomes, children with low effortful control were 3.5 times more likely to experience adverse behavioural outcomes than those with high effortful control.31 In particular, inhibitory control, which is a key component of effortful control and refers to the capacity to suppress inappropriate approach or avoidance responses in novel or uncertain situations,30 may be especially relevant here. Children with poor inhibitory control may be unable to suppress escape responses when faced with unfamiliar and stimulating environments such as the surgical setting. This may help explain the apparent contradiction observed in these cases, where preoperative verbal agreement was followed by intraoperative refusal to cooperate.

As uncooperative behaviour is not always closely linked to dental fear,3 procedural failure may still occur despite effective anxiolysis achieved by DEX. Furthermore, no single assessment tool is completely accurate in predicting patient behaviour.3 Beyond the Frankl Scale, additional information regarding the child’s temperament, personality characteristics, and cognitive and psychological maturity can be obtained through tools such as the CBQ-SF,29 review of previous dental experiences, and communication with parents. The clinician should then assess the child’s cooperative potential through direct observation and interaction. A guidable willingness to cooperate is a key behavioural marker. Typically, these patients show initial anxiety or mild withdrawal upon arrival, but after behavioural guidance and a clear procedural explanation (e.g., “We can give you a nasal spray first to help you feel less scared. During the treatment, you will only feel the injection briefly, and then it will be sensation without pain. We need you to cooperate by lying down and opening your mouth. Is that okay?”), they can provide a definite, though occasionally hesitant, affirmative response to accept the procedure.

Six (9.52%) patients reached a deeper sedation level during the procedure. However, all maintained stable vital signs and patent airways. As recognised in sedation guidelines, patients under moderate sedation may inadvertently progress to a deeper level. The median (IQR) maintenance N₂O concentration was 30.0% (30.0%, 40.0%), well within the protocol-specified maximum of 50%, indicating that oversedation was unlikely to result from excessive N₂O administration. Instead, as Uusalo et al. reported that the maximal sedative effect of intranasal DEX was achieved at a median (IQR) of 45 (40-48) minutes after dosing,32 suggesting that the cumulative sedative effect of DEX during the intraoperative period may have contributed to the progression beyond the target sedation level. Furthermore, assessment of sedation depth based solely on observer evaluation without bidirectional communication33 may not fully capture rapid transitions in sedation depth. Current practice guidelines for moderate procedural sedation recommend periodic assessment of the patient’s response to verbal commands at approximately 5-minute intervals.33 In the context of oral procedures, consciousness can be assessed by asking the patient to provide a purposeful response, such as a “thumbs up” to verbal stimulation.33 If unintended deep sedation is identified, the procedure should be paused, and the patient aroused with gentle tactile stimulation. N2O concentration should then be reduced and airway patency maintained until the patient returns to the target sedation level. Among these 6 patients, 5 (7.94%) exhibited drowsiness, and 2 (3.17%) reported dizziness. Median (IQR) recovery time was 61.0 (58.0, 63.0) min, consistent with the observations reported by Surendar et al.14 Although most patients could be awakened immediately after the procedure, the postoperative recovery time was relatively prolonged,34 which may be associated with DEX’s elimination half-life of 2.1 to 3.1 hours.6

DEX’s main adverse effects are bradycardia and hypotension, although most evidence suggests that these seldom require clinical intervention.5,6,18,19,35 As noted by Mason et al., DEX produces electroencephalogram activity similar to natural non-rapid eye movement (non-REM) sleep in children, a physiological state inherently associated with a relative decrease in HR.35 Current literature does not support an association between this HR response and impending hemodynamic compromise. To date, no valid link has been established between DEX‑induced bradycardia and hemodynamic deterioration in otherwise healthy, non‑cardiac adults or children. Our study also observed significant decreases in HR (P < .001) and BP (P < .001) following sedation. But oxygen saturation remained stable (P = .120), reflecting the minimal respiratory depression associated with DEX. Hypotension was observed in 8 (12.70%) patients, and bradycardia occurred in 1 (1.59%) case, which were similar to the reports of Unkel et al.36

Only one patient required clinical interventions, who developed hypoxemia accompanied by concurrent hypotension and bradycardia. This case, which occurred in the initial stage of the study period, involved the administration of an additional 15 µg of DEX 30 minutes after the scheduled dose due to inadequate sedation onset. Although the surgical procedure was ultimately completed successfully, the patient exhibited drowsiness postoperatively and subsequently developed bradycardia, hypotension, and hypoxemia during observation. Following timely intervention with a jaw-thrust manoeuvre and mask oxygen delivery, SpO2 returned to normal within 2 to 3 minutes. The patient was then closely monitored with a patent airway secured. HR and BP gradually returned to normal in the following hours. No complications were reported during postoperative follow-up. Regarding the underlying causes, since SpO2 recovered promptly after airway opening and supplemental oxygen administration, the hypoxemia in this case was more likely attributable to upper airway obstruction secondary to oversedation rather than to direct respiratory depression. Although a retrospective observational study indicated that the incidence of bradycardia induced by intranasal DEX in procedural sedation was not correlated with the use of an additional dose,37 the bradycardia and hypotension observed in this patient suggest that concomitant N2O administration may have potentiated DEX’s dose-dependent hemodynamic effects. Furthermore, the nonlinear dose-response relationship identified by Zhenyu et al.17 implies that an additional dose may heighten the risks of hemodynamic instability. Regarding clinical management, intervention is warranted when HR falls below 60 bpm.12 As clinically significant bradycardia following intranasal DEX is rare, there is no standardised management protocol. According to Paediatric Advanced Life Support guidelines, drug‑induced bradycardia should first be managed by discontinuing the medication, followed by supportive measures including airway maintenance, oxygenation, and, if necessary, CPR and epinephrine.12 However, caution is warranted when using anticholinergic agents to manage DEX-induced bradycardia, as the anticholinergic-induced increase in HR and cardiac output acts synergistically with DEX’s persistent peripheral vasoconstriction, resulting in a marked elevation in BP.38

Preoperative fasting was not required in the present study. Although AAPD guidelines state that fasting may not be necessary when N₂O is used alone,10 the present protocol involved combination sedation. To mitigate aspiration risk, strict patient selection criteria were applied, including ASA class I/II status, absence of obesity or gastroesophageal reflux disease, and treatment time limited to less than 40 minutes. All patients were advised to avoid alcoholic beverages and large meals on the day of the procedure. Furthermore, N₂O concentration was titrated conservatively to avoid prolonged deep sedation. Notably, no cases of vomiting, aspiration, or airway-related complications were observed in this cohort, which provides preliminary support for the safety of this approach under the described patient selection criteria and sedation protocol.

First, as a single-centre, retrospective, observational study without a control group, it is susceptible to selection bias, and definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy cannot be drawn. Multicenter randomised controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings. Second, the predominance of children aged 6 to 8 years may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader target population aged 6 to 12 years, and the relatively small sample size precluded multivariable analysis of predictors of sedation outcomes. Future studies with larger cohorts should investigate whether baseline characteristics such as age, weight, sex, or vital signs are independently associated with treatment and sedation outcomes. Third, preoperative fasting was not required in this study based on clinical reasoning. However, aspiration risk and fasting-related outcomes were not prospectively monitored, and the safety of this approach warrants further evaluation in future studies.

Despite these limitations, the DEX/N₂O combination shows considerable promise for managing uncooperative paediatric patients undergoing complex oral procedures. Future multicenter randomised controlled trials, ideally comparing this protocol against other non-invasive premedication strategies combined with N₂O, are warranted to establish comparative efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, future studies should investigate the pharmacokinetic interactions between DEX and N₂O to elucidate the mechanistic basis for their synergistic sedative effect. Furthermore, prospective studies incorporating comprehensive preoperative patient assessment should go beyond behavioural rating scales such as the Frankl Scale to include multiple dimensions of child characteristics. Such multidimensional assessment frameworks would help identify predictors of procedural cooperation and enable more individualised patient selection for this sedation protocol.

Conclusions

Our study is the first to investigate the efficacy and safety of a combined sedation protocol using intranasal DEX spray and N₂O in paediatric complex oral surgery. Intranasal DEX spray as a premedication appeared effective in reducing preoperative anxiety, enabling patient cooperation with nasal hood placement and thereby addressing the challenge of initial non-cooperation prior to N₂O administration. During the procedure, DEX and N₂O showed notable synergistic sedative effects, facilitating successful parental separation and maintaining a moderate sedation level throughout most procedures, which contributed to the successful completion of the vast majority of cases. Adverse events were infrequent and typically resolved spontaneously without clinical intervention. These findings are preliminary, and multicenter randomised controlled trials are warranted to confirm the efficacy and safety of this combined sedation protocol relative to existing alternatives.
