Validation and Refinement of the Self-Regulatory HIV/AIDS Symptom Management Model Among People With HIV in China Using Path Analysis: A Secondary Data Analysis
Authors: Meilian Xie, Aiping Wang, Zhiyun Zhang, Kerong Wang, Yanping Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored the path affecting the quality of life (QOL) of people with HIV in China and clarified the applicability and interpretability of the Self-Regulatory HIV/AIDS Symptom Management Model. We conducted a cross-sectional survey in nine regions of China and collected information about demographics, symptom experience, social support, perceived symptom manageability, self-efficacy, and QOL. A total of 711 patients participated in this survey. After four rounds of path analysis were conducted, the most fit indices met the standards (χ2/df = 2.633, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation = 0.081, Goodness-of-Fit Index/Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index/Comparative Fit Index/Incremental Fit Index/Tucker–Lewis Coefficient > 0.9), and the relationships between variables were statistically significant (p < .05). Among all the pathways, social support had the most significant impact on the QOL, with a total path coefficient of 0.665. Symptom experience, as a negative factor, had a total effect value of −0.361. This study showed how all the factors influencing QOL were interconnected, serving as a crucial theoretical foundation for intervention strategies in future clinical practice.

With the rapid advancement of antiretroviral therapy (ART) medication, the overall mortality of people with HIV (PWH) has significantly decreased. Their longevity is almost the same as that of the general population (Kehler et al., 2022; Marcus et al., 2020). The aging of the global PWH population is intensifying. According to a cohort study from the United States, it is estimated that by 2030, PWH over 50 years will account for more than 73% of this population (O'Keefe et al., 2013). However, living longer does not necessarily mean living a healthy and happy life. A previous study revealed that PWH face a number of factors that affect their quality of life (QOL), including increasing physical disabilities and morbidities, psychiatric illnesses, losses of partners and friends, social isolation, and stigma (Wing, 2016).

In 2005, the Self-Regulatory HIV/AIDS Symptom Management Model (SSMM-HIV; Spirig et al., 2005), developed by the University of Basel's Institute of Nursing Science in Switzerland, positioned as a critical starting point and provides the necessary basis for clinical nursing practice. This model interconnected the concepts of symptom experience, perceived symptom manageability (PSM), symptom management, social support, adherence to medication, clinical outcomes, and QOL into a recursive model. From a theoretical perspective, it comprehensively elucidates the impact of various related variables on the QOL of PWH, particularly analyzing the factors influencing the QOL of the PWH from both a medical and a social perspective, and their causal pathways. This model provides a novel perspective and direction for future nursing practices.

The SSMM-HIV model is based on a synthesis of relevant theories, literature analysis, and common human knowledge (Fierz et al., 2013; Nicca et al., 2012; Spirig et al., 2005). It serves as a theoretical model to guide clinical nursing professionals and investigators in understanding how PWH experience and control their symptoms, adhere to their behaviors, and enhance their health status and QOL. It integrates social, human behavior, and psychological variables, such as social support, treatment adherence, and PSM, providing a diverse and comprehensive depiction of PWH's health status and social functioning within their social networks. It serves as a critical theoretical foundation for the management of PWH.

According to the framework of SSMM-HIV, Han et al. developed a symptom management intervention based on symptom management group sessions combined with a mobile health (mHealth) application and validated its efficacy through a randomized controlled trial in 61 PWH (Han, Pei, Wang, et al., 2021. Han, Pei, Zhao, et al., 2021). Schnall et al. developed the Video Information Provider-HANA application to help PWH self-manage their symptoms based on SSMM-HIV (Schnall et al., 2019, 2021). Their findings suggest that PWH are interested in monitoring their symptoms, which could have implications for the wider use of digital health for patient surveillance. SSMM-HIV was also used to improve ART adherence (Schnall et al., 2022) and smoking cessation (Wood et al., 2023) among PWH. As Spirig et al. (2005) explained, SSMM-HIV is a pathway that can provide clear guidance for nurses and clinical researchers to understand how PWH experience and manage their symptoms and how this affects their treatment adherence in today's health care environment. It offers various methods to help health care professionals assess and assist patients' understanding of their disease and manage symptoms more effectively. Health care providers can assess their patients' symptom management needs at various levels, involving both the patients themselves and their social support networks. They can teach patients how to monitor symptoms and change their lifestyle to reduce symptom burden and enhance treatment adherence through education. SSMM-HIV also underscores the crucial value of effective collaboration among patients, primary caregivers, and health care professionals in health management.

Researchers established SSMM-HIV based on theoretical analysis. Although the SSMM-HIV model is directly applied in the care and management of PWH, its framework or structure has not been further validated in target populations, and whether its understanding and mechanisms may change in different cultural contexts has not been effectively explored. Given the rapid advancement in medical care over the past few decades and the inherent differences among cultures in different countries, it is still necessary to explore and validate how SSMM-HIV exhibits recursive relationships among PWH in China. Therefore, this study will further investigate the applicability and interpretability of the SSMM-HIV with data from Chinese PWH.

Methods

Design and Population

Our study is a secondary analysis of cross-sectional research data collected from nine regions in China between 2020 and 2021 (Xie et al., 2023). Based on the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in mainland China, our study ultimately used a convenience sampling and quota sampling approach to select nine provinces or directly administered municipalities for investigation. These include three high-prevalence areas (Xinjiang, Guangxi, and Sichuan); three regions with relatively higher incidence (Beijing, Hunan, and Guangdong); two moderately affected areas (Qinghai and Henan); and one area with lower incidence (Liaoning). After continuing to collect some data, we ultimately formed the dataset for our study. This quota sampling is based on the distribution structure of factors such as gender, age, and urban–rural classification. Our study inclusion criteria were PWH who (a) had an HIV infection diagnosis, (b) were older than 18 years, (c) were on ART, and (d) provided informed consent to participate in our study. Excluded criteria were PWH who could not complete the survey because of severe comorbidities or cognitive impairment.

Measurement

Demographic questionnaire

A sociodemographic questionnaire was used to collect demographic and clinical information, including age, gender, ethnicity, education, marital status, employment status, economic income, residential district, HIV-positive duration, ART use, latest CD4+ T cell count, disease staging, and comorbidities (Xie et al., 2022, 2022a, 2023).

Self-report symptom scale

The self-report symptom scale (SRSS) was developed by combining the self-report symptom checklist (Zhu et al., 2019) with the dimensions of the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (Cheng et al., 2009). The Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale, a previously reported reliable and valid instrument (Vincenzi et al., 2009), has been used in our analysis of older individuals with HIV infection (Xie et al., 2022a). Twenty-seven core symptoms are included in SRSS. The participant is asked to report their particular symptom experience across the following three dimensions of 27 symptoms: frequency, severity, and distress. The frequency and severity of these 27 symptoms are evaluated with a 4-point Likert scale, and a 5-point Likert scale rates the distress of symptoms. A higher score indicates a higher level of symptom experience. The tool's content validity index is 0.918, and the Cronbach's α coefficient is 0.916. In addition, in this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient is 0.981.

Perceived social support

Perceived social support (PSS) was measured by a Chinese version of the Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (Sherbourne & Stewart, 1991; Yu et al., 2015). Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey is a self-report tool, containing 20 items, with Cronbach's α = 0.97 and test–retest reliability = 0.84, and it includes four subscales measuring the perceived adequacy of tangible support, informational and emotional support, positive social interaction, and affectionate support (Sherbourne & Stewart, 1991). Each item is responded to on a 5-point Likert-type scale to indicate how often the respondent receives the support. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better PSS. The Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale is 0.980, and the intraclass correlation coefficient for test–retest reliability is 0.840. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient is also 0.980, indicating high internal consistency and test–retest reliability.

Perceived symptom manageability scale

Our study developed and validated the 15-item self-report PSM scale with high reliability and validity with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.924 and an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.877 (Xie et al., 2024). It can evaluate patients' perceived ability to bring social and personal resources to deal with successfully or control symptoms despite difficulties. Each item is responded to on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral [neither agree nor disagree], 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree). A higher score indicates better symptom manageability.

Self-efficacy

The Chinese version of the Self-Efficacy for Managing Chronic Disease 6-Item Scale (Zhang & Zhao, 2008) was used to measure PWH's self-efficacy. The scale consists of six questions, which were scored on a Likert scale from 1 (not at all confident) to 10 (totally confident). This scale's total scores range from 6 to 60, with higher scores representing higher perceived self-efficacy for managing chronic diseases. Some researchers translated and applied it to a preinvestigation in a population of patients with chronic disease, and the Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale was 0.938 (Wang-Lili et al., 2011). In our study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the scale is 0.963.

Quality of life

The QOL of PWH was rated by a Chinese version of the World Health Organization Quality Of Life Questionnaire For HIV Brief Version (Yao et al., 2002; Zhu et al., 2017), including 31 items covering six domains. The domain scores were calculated by multiplying the mean of all items within the domain by 4. All domain scores ranged from 4 to 20. Higher scores in each domain indicated higher QOL for that domain. A previous study (Zhu et al., 2017) had demonstrated Cronbach's α coefficients ranging from 0.91 to 0.93. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient is 0.944, indicating a high level of internal consistency.

Data Collection

Our survey was carried out through an online survey platform (https://www.wjx.cn/; Xie et al., 2022a, 2023). Clinical characteristics were extracted from the medical records after completing the survey by designated investigators in every medical institution. To ensure survey quality, all survey processes were conducted under the supervision of on-site interviewers through face-to-face interviews.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using FreeStatisticsV1.7. Depending on the data's continuous and categorical variables, the count data were presented in the form of frequencies and percentages, and the normally distributed continuous data were provided in the form of mean ± SD. By contrast, descriptions of nonnormally distributed continuous data were presented as median and interquartile ranges. The statistical analysis for the path analysis was conducted using analysis of moment structures (AMOS) software, and χ2, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation, Comparative Fit Index, Goodness-of-Fit Index, Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index, Incremental Fit Index, and Tucker–Lewis Coefficient were used to assess the fit between the model and observed data. The above statistical tests were all performed with a significance level of p < .05 (two-tailed), indicating that the differences were statistically significant.

Participants

A total of 711 patients participated in our survey, including 513 men (72.15%) and 198 women (27.85%). The mean age of all surveyed individuals was 39.22 years (SD = 11.60, range 18–74 years). A total of 24.05% of the surveyed individuals had been diagnosed with HIV for less than a year, and 87.62% were currently receiving ART. Among the participants, 43.32% had a CD4+ T cell count of ≥ 500 cells/mm3, and only 18.71% had comorbidities (Table 1).

Assumption of Model Construction

Based on the theoretical framework of our study (Figure 1), incorporating existing literature evidence, professional knowledge, and the results of preliminary research analysis, we propose path assumptions for the impact of symptom experience, PSM, self-efficacy, and social support as core variables on the QOL of HIV-infected individuals. We designed an initial path diagram (Figure 2) to explore the interaction mechanisms among the influencing factors of the QOL of PWH. The independent variables (explanatory variables) are symptom experience, PSM, self-efficacy, and social support. The dependent variable (response variable) is the QOL. Here, λi (i = 1, …) represents path coefficients, indicating the degree of influence of each latent variable on the corresponding observed variable. In other words, when a latent variable (explanatory variable) changes by one unit, the corresponding observed variable (response variable) will change by how many units, depending on the direction of influence, whether it is an increase or decrease. ei (i = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) represents the random influencing factors of the respective observed variables.

Common Method Bias Examination

In the data collection process, our study implemented various methods, such as anonymous filling and reverse item formulation, to control for common method bias. However, because all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires or scales, conducting Harman's single-factor test was necessary to further examine common method bias (Zhou & Long, 2004). Exploratory factor analysis without rotation was performed on all items of the SRSS, PSS, self-efficacy, and QOL. The results showed nine factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first extracted factor could explain 37.71% of the total variance, which was below the recommended 40% critical threshold by Podsakoff et al. (2003). This suggests that there is no serious common method bias in the data of our study.

Normality Testing of Variables in the Model

Normality testing was conducted on the variables of symptom experience, PSM, self-efficacy, social support, and QOL in HIV-infected individuals. AMOS normality testing primarily involves estimating skewness and kurtosis. Generally, it is considered that when the absolute value of skewness is < 3, and the absolute value of kurtosis is < 8, the variables are considered to have no significant skewness and are suitable for model fitting (Wu, 2010). In our study, all variables met the requirements for normality testing, with skewness coefficients ranging from −0.830 to 1.906 and kurtosis coefficients ranging from −0.514 to 3.787. Specific results of the normality testing are presented in Table 2.

Initial Path Model Examination and Revision

The initial path model (Figure 2) was examined using AMOS 27.0. In this initial model, PSM and self-efficacy were set as mediating variables, assuming parallel mediating roles for symptom experience and social support while also positing direct effects of symptom experience and social support on QOL. In addition, reciprocal relationships were hypothesized between symptom experience and social support and between PSM and self-efficacy. However, the model did not yield satisfactory results, suggesting that empirical data did not fit the assumed model. Therefore, the model needed to be revised in light of established theories.

After reviewing previous research, considering that social support might primarily influence the experience of PWH's symptoms, but the reverse pathway might not hold in real situations, the path “symptom experience → social support” was removed (Figure 3). However, the model still failed to fit the data. The study's focus on the exploratory variable, PSM, raised questions about whether it was related to self-efficacy and whether it would act as a mediating variable in the pathway from symptom experience to QOL. These uncertainties necessitated the creation of various potential pathways in the subsequent model construction.

The revised path diagram illustrating the evolution of the impact mechanism on the QOL of people with HIV. PSM = perceived symptom manageability; QOL = quality of life.

Through two rounds of alternative testing of the paths “PSM → self-efficacy” and “self-efficacy → PSM,” although both models could fit the data according to fit indices, none of the fit indices met the fitting standards. This indicated a poor model fit with the empirical data, necessitating further revision (Figure 3). In both rounds of model fitting, it was revealed that the direct impact of “symptom experience → PSM” was very weak. It was the weakest among all pathway relationships and could not pass significance testing.

Therefore, considering the model revision indices, nonsignificant path coefficients, a reexamination of the original theory, and the potential differences between PSM and self-efficacy, and the deeper conceptualization and theoretical explanation of PSM itself, the path “symptom experience → PSM” was removed to obtain the final acceptable pathway model based on the self-regulation theory of symptom management in the impact mechanism of QOL in PWH (Figure 4). Most fit indices met the standards, and the coefficients of the relationships between variables were statistically significant (p < .05), indicating a good fit for the revised model. The results indicated that in the revised model, 63.0% of the variance in the QOL of PWH could be explained by exogenous variables. Discussion

Our study validated and explored the relationships among critical variables in the SSMM-HIV theoretical model for the first time using structural equation modeling in a real-life context. It also introduced the value of mediating variables such as self-efficacy and PSM into the model, expanding our understanding of the mechanisms and intrinsic connection about SSMM-HIV in Chinese PWH and providing new perspectives for future disease management.

In the initial stages of structural equation modeling, the original model (Figure 2) was established based on the SSMM-HIV theory. Both the relationships between PSS and symptom experience, and self-efficacy and PSM, were initially set as reversible model structures with bidirectional arrows. However, the model could not be established through our experiment, whether both relationships existed simultaneously or if only one was present, indicating that the previously understood reversible model structure did not fit the population data collected in our study. After multiple validations and adjustments, the final model construction ensured the reasonable range of various fit indices and provided continuity and new insights into the original theory.

Through constructing the path model, we found the causal mechanisms by which symptom experience, PSM, self-efficacy, and social support affect the QOL. First, social support not only directly influences the QOL, but it also affects the symptoms experience or the internal emotional responses within the body (self-efficacy, PSM). This result indicates that social support promotes the QOL by improving psychological well-being, enhancing a sense of belonging, and stimulating internal agency. This is consistent with the results obtained from previous regression and correlation (Xie et al., 2023). Second, through path analysis, a deeper understanding of the distinction between PSM and self-efficacy was achieved. PSM is more significantly influenced by social support, although it has not yet shown any connection with symptom experience. The concept of PSM mainly deals with the degree to which individuals perceive external resources can help them overcome difficulties, thereby being more influenced by external support. By contrast, self-efficacy primarily reflects internal motivation, representing an individual's confidence, perception, or belief in their abilities and focusing on the internal perceptual level of the body. It is a common mediating variable in the path to influence the QOL, indicating that the QOL is an experiential and subjective feeling related to the individual's goals, expectations, standards, and concerns. The differences and similarities between these two variables differ slightly from the descriptions in the original theory.

Furthermore, this study found that the impact of symptom experience on the QOL is moderated by self-efficacy. Symptom experience negatively affects an individual's self-efficacy, which, in turn, affects the QOL. Symptom experience does not improve the QOL by affecting an individual's PSM, correcting our initial understanding and insights into the relationship between these two variables.

Although this study incorporated numerous research variables that influence the QOL in PWH across various levels, it is acknowledged that it may only cover some influencing factors, such as individual sociodemographic factors and others not included in the path model. In addition, there should be a more comprehensive understanding of the definition of social support in the original model. By contrast, this study focused only on the patient's perspective of PSS. As a result, the final model constructed may deviate slightly from the original model. In the future, further exploration in these areas will be pursued to enhance the holistic research findings on the QOL improvement process for PWH.

Conclusion

A path model constructed based on previous theories, concepts, and clinical experience was validated in this study. We have reshaped the original model by applying real data from Chinese PWH, a real-life case of moving closer from theory to reality. The new path model shows how all the factors influencing QOL are interconnected and interact, serving as a crucial theoretical foundation for intervention strategies in the future clinical practice. Social support plays the most significant role as the initiating facilitating factor for the entire process. Among these, PSM and self-efficacy have connections and distinctions as mediating factors. Future research can focus on developing best practices for moderating the effects of these mediating variables.
