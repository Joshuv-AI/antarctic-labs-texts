PCB 37 (3,4, 4’-trichlorobiphenyl) increased apoptosis and modulated neuronal morphogenesis in primary rat cortical neuron-glia cocultures in a concentration-, sex-, age-, and CREB-dependent manner
Authors: Jessie R. Badley, Peter M. Andrew, Hans-Joachim Lehmler, Pamela J. Lein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Higher-chlorinated (HC) polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are known developmental neurotoxicants. In contrast, there are limited data regarding the developmental neurotoxicity of lower-chlorinated (LC) PCBs despite the increasing environmental prevalence and detection of LC-PCBs in contemporary human tissues, including the perinatal brain. This study characterized the neurotoxic effects of the LC-PCB congener PCB 37 in primary male and female rat cortical neuron-glia co-cultures. Cultures were exposed to varying concentrations of PCB 37 for 48 h beginning on day in vitro 0 or 7. Cell viability was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase release into the culture medium and the percentage of live cells identified using Calcein-AM and Hoechst staining. Apoptosis was measured using fluorometric assays of caspase 3/7 activity and Annexin V binding. Axonal and dendritic growth were quantified in neurons immunostained for Tau-1 or transfected with MAP2B-red fluorescent protein, respectively, using automated image analysis protocols. At environmentally relevant concentrations (0.0001, 0.1, and 100 nM) that did not affect cell viability, PCB 37 caused sex-, age-, and concentration-dependent increases in apoptosis, axonal, and dendritic growth. Pretreatment with the CREB inhibitor 666–15 (500 nM) blocked the effects of PCB 37 on apoptosis and dendritic morphology but not axonal growth. These findings suggest that the pro-apoptotic and dendrite-promoting effects of PCB 37 are mediated by CREB signaling, but that CREB-independent mechanisms underlie PCB 37 effects on axonal growth. Overall, these findings identify PCB 37 as a potential developmental neurotoxicant and further support increasing evidence identifying CREB as a convergent mechanism of developmental neurotoxicity.

Introduction

Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) remain a significant risk to human health, and the developing brain has been identified as a vulnerable target (Berghuis et al., 2015, Boucher et al., 2009, Klocke and Lein, 2020, Pessah et al., 2019, Schantz et al., 2003). Prior studies in both rodent models (Carlson et al., 2023, Klocke et al., 2020) and humans (Balalian et al., 2024, Schantz et al., 2003) have reported positive associations between developmental PCB exposures and impaired cognitive function. More recent epidemiological literature suggests that developmental exposure to PCBs may also increase risk for neurodevelopmental disorders (Keil-Stietz and Lein, 2023). This epidemiological association is supported by observations from experimental animal studies demonstrating that developmental exposure to PCBs causes adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes that have been implicated in the etiopathogenesis of many neurodevelopmental disorders (Keil-Stietz and Lein, 2023).

The mechanism(s) underlying PCB developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) remain speculative. It is known that developmental exposure to PCBs does not cause overt structural damage, but rather induces subtle organizational defects that alter the patterning of synaptic connections in the developing brain (Klocke and Lein, 2020, Pessah et al., 2019). Two critical determinants of synaptic connectivity are neuronal apoptosis and neuronal morphogenesis, the latter encompassing axonal outgrowth and dendritic arborization. Spatiotemporal changes in apoptosis, axonal outgrowth, or dendritic arborization are thought to underlie cognitive deficits and many of the behavioral abnormalities associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (Guang et al., 2018, Panesar et al., 2020, Stamou et al., 2013).

PCBs have been shown to interfere with apoptosis (Howard et al., 2003, Yang and Lein, 2010), axonal outgrowth (Pessah et al., 2019, Sethi et al., 2017), and dendritic arborization (Sethi et al., 2017, Wayman et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2009). However, most studies of PCB developmental neurotoxicity have focused on Aroclors, the legacy PCB mixtures produced and commercially marketed in the United States from 1920s-1970s, or the higher-chlorinated (HC)-PCBs found in the legacy PCB mixtures (Klocke and Lein, 2020). In contrast, there are limited data available regarding the DNT potential of lower-chlorinated (LC)-PCBs, which are defined as congeners with ≤ 4 chlorine substitutions. This is a substantial data gap because, in contrast to HC-PCBs, environmental levels of LC-PCBs, many of which are inadvertent industrial byproducts (Hu, 2009), are increasing worldwide (Grimm et al., 2015, Melymuk et al., 2022). LC-PCBs are regularly detected in samples of indoor and outdoor air, water, food, and in human tissues, including breast milk and brain (Klocke and Lein, 2020, Li et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2020) and have been found to comprise more than 70 % of the total PCBs in the serum of pregnant women who are at increased risk of having a child with a neurodevelopmental disorder (Sethi et al., 2017, Sethi et al., 2019).

In prior studies, we found that the LC congener PCB 11 and its major human metabolites significantly increased axonal and dendritic growth in primary cortical rat neurons grown in neuron-glia co-cultures (Sethi et al., 2017) and that the dendrite-promoting activity of PCB 11 was mediated by CREB signaling (Sethi et al., 2018). However, whether these observations extend to all LC-PCBs is unknown. A recent post-mortem analysis of neonatal and adult brains detected the LC-PCB, PCB 37, in 31–40 % of all tissue analyzed (Li et al., 2022). PCB 37 has also been found in indoor air samples (Kraft et al., 2018). Thus, in this study, we evaluated the developmental neurotoxicity potential of PCB 37 using sex-segregated primary rat cortical neuron-glia cocultures.

Materials and methods

Materials

PCB 37 (3,4,4′-trichlorobiphenyl; catalog: C-37N, lot#11230–02) was purchased from AccuStandard Inc. (New Haven, CT). Stock solutions of PCB 37 (1000X) were made in dry sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and stored at room temperature in the dark. Plating and growth media were prepared as previously described (Sethi et al., 2017). Briefly, plating medium consisted of Neurobasal Plus medium (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 2 % B27 (Gibco™), 1x GlutaMAX supplement (Gibco™), 10 % heat-inactivated horse serum (Gibco™), and 1 M HEPES buffer (pH 7.5). Growth medium consisted of Neurobasal Plus medium supplemented with 2 % B27 and 1x GlutaMAX. Feeding medium consisted of growth media supplemented with 5 μM arabinose-C (Thermo Fisher Scientific), an anti-mitotic to halt glial overgrowth. Stock concentrations of PCB 37 were diluted 1:1000 directly into growth medium at 1–2X the final concentration in the well; vehicle controls were were exposed to 0.001 % DMSO in growth medium.

Cell culture

All experiments involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and protocols approved by the University of California, Davis, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). To account for variability, experiments were repeated using independent cell isolations, with both male and female cultures plated on the same plate to minimize plate effects. Plates included untreated controls, and image analysis was automated with the analyst blinded to treatment conditions until data visualization. Timed-pregnant Sprague Dawley rat dams were purchased from Charles River Laboratory (Hollister, CA) and individually housed in transparent plastic cages with corn cob bedding in a temperature—and humidity controlled vivarium maintained at 22 ± 2°C on a 12-h light-dark cycle. Animals were provided Teklad 2018 Rodent Feed (Inotiv, Lafayette, IN) and water ad libitum. Dams were imported 5 days before giving birth and allowed time for acclimatation.

Primary cortical neuron-glia co-cultures were dissociated from the neocortices of sex-segregated postnatal day 0 Sprague Dawley rat pups (Fig. 1) as previously described (Sethi et al., 2017). Cell suspensions were plated in plating medium onto glass bottom 96-well plates (CellVis, Sunnyvale, CA) or German glass coverslips (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) in plastic Corning 24-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) precoated with 0.5 mg/mL poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich) in B buffer (3.1 mg/mL boric acid and 4.75 mg/mL borax, pH 7.5, Sigma-Aldrich). To analyze the length of the axonal plexus of an individual neuron (Morgan, 2020), cells were plated at 33,000 cells/cm2; to quantify dendritic arbors, cells were plated at 83,000 cells/cm2. Cell viability and caspase 3/7 activity were assessed at both cell densities. Cells were allowed to adhere to coverslips in plating medium for 4 h at which time the plating medium was replaced with growth medium. Cultures were maintained at 37°C under 5 % CO2. In cultures used to assess dendritic growth, half the medium in each well was exchanged at day in vitro (DIV) 4 with feeding medium.

PCB 37 exposure

Cortical neurons in culture develop structurally according to a stereotypic morphogenetic program (Fig. 1). Within the first 24 h, neurites begin to project from the cell body. By 48 h, a single neurite begins to elongate more rapidly than the others and this differentiates into an axon. On days 3–7, the remaining neurites begin to differentiate into dendrites, and as the culture ages, the dendritic arbor becomes more complex as indicated by increasing length and branching (Flynn, 2013, Wilson et al., 2024a). To analyze axonal and dendritic morphology, two different exposure paradigms were used (Fig. 1). Axonal outgrowth, cell viability, and caspase 3/7 activity were measured on DIV 2. For these experiments, cultures were exposed to vehicle (0.001 % DMSO) or varying concentrations of PCB 37 for 48 h starting 4 h after plating cells. Dendritic arborization, cell viability, and caspase 3/7 activity were measured on DIV 9, in cultures exposed to vehicle (0.001 % DMSO) or varying concentrations of PCB 37 for 48 h starting on DIV 7.

Cell viability

Two different assays were used to assess cell viability: (1) the live/dead assay and (2) the quantification of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released from cells into culture media. In the live/dead assay, calcein AM is used to label live cells. This membrane permeable dye is converted by intracellular esterases in live cells from the nonfluorescent parent compound into the fluorescent metabolite; dead cells do not have this metabolic capability. Hoechst 33342, which stains the nuclei of live and dead cells, was used to measure total cell number. To determine the percentage of live cells, cultures were co-stained with 0.5 μM calcein AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 μg/mL Hoechst-33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 1x Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline with calcium and magnesium (DPBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After addition of these reagents, cultures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min covered from light. Cultures were then immediately imaged using an ImageXpress Micro XLS high content imaging system (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). The percentage of live cells was determined from the overlay of calcein AM and Hoechst-33342 labeling analyzed with MetaXpress 6.2 software (MetaXpress High Content Image Acquisition and Analysis Software, Live Dead Application Module, Molecular Devices) and was calculated as #calcein AM-labeled cells / #Hoechst-labeled nuclei X 100.

The CytoTox-ONE™ Homogeneous Membrane Integrity Assay (Promega, Madison, WI) a fluorometric assay, was used to measure LDH in the culture medium; this enzyme is released from cells with damaged membranes, which is a canonical indicator of cytotoxicity. Per the manufacturer’s instructions, 100 μL of conditioned medium from each well was mixed with 100 μL CytoTox-ONE™ reagent and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Stop solution was added, and the fluorescent signal was measured at 560/590 nm using a BioTek Gen5 plate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). The level of fluorescence is proportional to the number of lysed, or dead, cells. Data were normalized as a percent of the fluorescent signal measured in vehicle-treated cells.

Apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed by measuring two biomarkers of apoptosis: (1) caspase 3/7 activity and (2) annexin V binding. The Caspase-Glo® 3/7 assay (Promega) is a luminescence assay for quantifying caspase-3/7 activity. Per manufacturer directions, 100 μL Caspase-Glo® 3/7 reagent was added to each well followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min. Staurosporine (10 μM; Millipore Sigma), a known apoptosis inducer, was used as a positive control. The amount of luminescence is directly proportional to caspase 3/7 activity. The RealTime-Glo™ Annexin V Apoptosis and Necrosis Assay (Promega) measures phosphatidylserine (PS), which is expressed on the outer leaflet of cell membranes during the apoptotic process (Schutte et al., 1998). The assay also includes a membrane-impermeant DNA-binding dye to label dead cells as a measure of membrane integrity. The amount of luminescence is proportional to PS and annexin V binding (early apoptosis), and the fluorescent signal is proportional to the loss of membrane integrity (necrosis). For all apoptotic assays, luminescence and fluorescence were quantified using a BioTek Gen5 plate reader (Agilent). Data were normalized as a percent of the fluorescent signal measured in vehicle-treated cells.

Visualization of axons

On DIV 2, all culture media were removed, and cells were fixed with 4 % (w/v) paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher Scientific) in sodium phosphate buffer, 0.2 M for 1 h. After washing 3 times with 1X DPBS, cells were then permeabilized with 0.5 % (v/v) Triton X-100 (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA) in 1x DPBS for 5 min. Cells were washed 3 times with 1X DPBS, and then incubated at room temperature with a blocking buffer consisting of 5 % (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1X DPBS for 1 h, followed by an overnight incubation with mouse anti-tau-1 antibody, clone PC1C6 (Sigma-Aldrich # MAB3420, RRID:AB_3068606) diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer at 4°C with gentle rocking. After a 24 h incubation, the primary antibody was removed, cells were washed 3 times with 1X DPBS, then incubated with a goat anti-mouse IgG2a Alexa fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific # A-21131, RRID:AB_2535771) diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer on the rocker at 4°C covered from light for 1 h. Cells were washed 3 times in 1X DPBS, then coverslips were mounted cell-side down on SuperFrost glass slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Prolong Gold Antifade Mounting media with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and allowed to dry overnight at room temperature while from light before imaging. Brefeldin A (1 μg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific), an inhibitor of vesicular traffic, was used as a positive control (data not shown).

Visualization of dendrites

The plasmid encoding the MAP2B-pCAG-fusRED cDNA construct (Wayman et al., 2006), generously provided by Dr. Gary Wayman (Washington University, Pullman, WA), was transformed using One Shot TOP10 chemically competent E. coli (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Bacteria were plated on Luria-Bertani broth (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and ampicillin (AMP,100 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) agar plates (MilliporeSigma) and incubated overnight at 37°C. After 24 h, a single colony was chosen and expanded in sterile Luria-Bertani broth + AMP overnight on the shaker at 220 rpm and at 37°C. On the following day, a Qiagen plasmid Maxiprep (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed per the manufacturer’s instructions to isolate and purify MAP2B-pCAG-fusRED cDNA. Purified MAP2B-fusRED cDNA concentration was then measured on a Nanodrop 1000 spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific) using an absorbance value of 260 nm.

On DIV 6, 24 h before exposure to PCB 37, cultures were transfected with 0.8 μg of MAP2B-pCAG-fusRED cDNA in 2 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) per well. The MAP2B fusion protein is selectively localized to the somatodendritic compartment of neurons and does not alter their fundamental dendritic growth patterns in culture (Wayman, 2006). To achieve a low-efficiency (1–10 %) transfection, the incubation time was reduced to only 2 h, followed by total replacement of the transfection medium with the conditioned medium. On DIV 7, cultures were exposed to vehicle or PCB 37 at varying concentrations. On DIV 9, media were removed, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 1 h, washed 3 times with 1X PBS, and then mounted cell-side down on glass slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Prolong Gold Antifade Mounting media with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and allowed to dry for 24 h at room temperature in the dark before imaging.

Morphometric analyses of axon outgrowth and dendritic arborization

The Automated ImageXpress high-content imaging system (Molecular Devices), which is interfaced to MetaXpress software (Molecular Devices), was used to acquire images from cell cultures immunostained for tau or transfected with MAP2B-FusRed (Morgan, 2020). A total of 25 images per well or coverslip were acquired at 10X magnification, with at least 3 technical replicates per dissection, per experimental group, for a total of 75 images per group per independent dissection. For axon outgrowth, DAPI and FITC channels were used; for dendritic arborization, DAPI and Texas Red channels were used.

To measure axon outgrowth, images of tau-1 immunofluorescence were overlaid on fluorescent images of Hoechst-33342 staining from the same field. Axonal lengths were quantified in tau-1 immunopositive cells using the Neurite Outgrowth application module in the MetaXpress high-content image acquisition and analysis software.

Sholl analysis (Wilson et al., 2017) was used to quantify dendritic arborization in cells expressing the MAP2B-FusRed protein. After images of MAP2BFusRed-transfected neurons were acquired on the high-content imaging system, a series of custom ImageJ macros (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.15749929) (Badley et al., 2025); see supplemental files) were used for automated analysis of dendritic arborization. These macros automatically identify fluorescently labeled neurons associated with a nucleus, extract image data from the background, select center points for the Sholl rings, and subsequently automatically count the number of dendritic processes of individual neurons that intersect with each of the concentric Sholl rings. Criteria used to include labeled neurons in the automated analysis included: (1) the fluorescent image had only one associated nucleus (e.g., clumps of cells were not included in the analysis); (2) the neuron was isolated (e.g., its processes did not intermingle with processes of another labeled neuron); and (3) the image was not located on the border of the field or cut off by the field edges.

CREB inhibition

To investigate the role of CREB signaling in mediating PCB 37 effects, CREB was inhibited by pretreating cells with 666–15 (Millipore sigma, 5383410001), a selective CREB inhibitor, at a final concentration of 500 nM. The inhibitor was added to the culture medium 30 min prior to PCB 37 exposure.

Statistical analysis

Power analysis based on prior data (Sethi et al., 2018) indicated that a sample size of 4 independent dissections (3 wells/group in each dissection) would provide sufficient statistical power (≥80 %) to detect a significant effect size. All statistics were done in GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798) version 10.0.0. All data, except the Sholl analysis, were analyzed using an ordinary one-way ANOVA with a post-hoc Dunnet’s test to identify significant differences (p-value <.05) between vehicle and PCB-exposed cultures from both males and females. A mixed-effect analysis with post-hoc Dunnet’s test was used to identify significant differences in the number of intersections at each set distance between vehicle and PCB-exposed cultures from both males and females (Wilson et al., 2017). A one-way ANOVA with a post-hoc Šídák’s multiple comparisons test was used to identify significant differences between PCB-exposed cultures and cultures co-exposed to 500 nM 666–15.

Results

Cell viability

To identify concentration-dependent effects of PCB 37 on cell viability, dissociated primary rat cortical neuron-glia co-cultures, sex segregated at the time of plating, were grown under the same culture conditions used for apoptosis, axonal growth, and dendritic arborization assays, and exposed to varying concentrations of PCB 37 ranging from 0.0001 nM to 100 μM. Cell viability was determined by quantifying LDH release into the culture media and by determining the percentage of cells stained with calcein-AM, which labels live cells on DIV 2 and DIV 9. Relative to age- and sex-matched vehicle controls, cultures from the same dissections exhibited no significant change in either LDH release (Fig. 2B, C, E, F) or percentage of live cells (Fig. 2B, C, E, F) following a 48-h exposure to PCB 37 at concentrations ≤ 1 μM. At concentrations > 1 μM, there was a significant decrease in cell viability in cultures of both sex (Fig. 2B, C, E, F). Based on this observation, all remaining experiments were conducted using PCB 37 at concentrations < 1 μM.

PCB 37 induced apoptosis in a timeand sex-dependent manner

Caspases, considered to be the executioners of apoptosis, are known for their critical role in shaping the development of the central nervous system (Lein et al., 2018). To determine whether PCB 37 influences apoptosis, we first measured caspase 3/7 activity in cultures exposed to PCB 37 at concentrations ranging from 0.0001 to 100 nM (Fig. 3). Caspase 3/7 activity was significantly increased in female DIV 2 cultures exposed to 100 nM PCB 37 (Fig. 3A). In contrast, significant changes in caspase 3/7 activity were not observed in any of the DIV 2 male cultures exposed to PCB 37 (Fig. 3C). By DIV 9, both female (Fig. 3B) and male (Fig. 3D) cultures exposed to PCB 37 at 100 nM exhibited significant caspase 3/7 activation.

To confirm that PCB 37 effects on caspase 3/7 activity reflect PCB 37-induced apoptosis, we also assessed phosphatidylserine (PS)-annexin V binding in DIV 9 neuron-glia co-cultures exposed to PCB 37 (Fig. 4). PS is normally restricted to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane; however, during early apoptosis, PS becomes exposed on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. When PS is exposed, annexin V can bind to it freely. Since annexin V can also bind to necrotic cells with ruptured membranes, a DNA-binding dye is used to distinguish between annexin V-binding cells that are apoptotic vs. necrotic. PS externalization was significantly increased in both female and male cultures exposed to 100 nM PCB 37 (Fig. 4 A,C); however, membrane integrity was not altered in any of the cultures exposed to PCB 37 at any of the tested concentrations (Fig. 4 B,D), suggesting that PCB 37 at 100 nM induces apoptosis.

PCB 37 increased axon length in a sex-dependent manner

Axonal growth is an essential determinant of neuronal connectivity in the developing brain (Desai-Chowdhry et al., 2022). Our previous studies demonstrated that the LC-PCB, PCB 11, significantly altered normal patterns of axonal growth in primary cortical neurons (Sethi et al., 2017). Therefore, we tested whether PCB 37 influenced axonal outgrowth in primary cortical neuron-glia co-cultures. Automated image acquisition and analysis of cultures immunostained for tau-1, a biomarker of axons (Probst et al., 1996), revealed sex-specific effects of PCB 37 on axon length (Fig. 5). In DIV 2 female cortical neurons, PCB 37 had no significant effect on axon length after a 48-h exposure to concentrations ranging from 0.0001 to 100 nM (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the average axon length of DIV 2 male cortical neurons was significantly increased following exposure to 0.0001 nM PCB 37 (Fig. 5C). PCB 37 had no significant effect on average cell body area in female (Fig. 5B) or male (Fig. 5D) cortical neurons cultures at any of the tested concentrations.

PCB 37 altered dendritic arborization in both male and female cortical neurons

Legacy HC-PCBs and Aroclor mixtures have been shown to increase dendritic growth of rat cortical neurons both in vitro and in vivo (Wayman et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2009) and we previously demonstrated that the LC-PCB, PCB 11, similarly enhanced dendritic arborization in cultured cortical neurons (Sethi et al., 2018). Sholl analysis was used to quantify the effects of PCB 37 on dendritic arborization in primary cortical neurons (Fig. 6A). In DIV 9 female cortical neurons, exposure to PCB 37 at 0.0001 or 100 nM significantly decreased the number of Sholl lintersections within 50–100 μm from the soma compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 6C). This decrease was confirmed by a significantly reduced area under the curve (AUC) of the Sholl plot in cultures exposed to PCB 37 at 0.0001 or 100 nM (Fig. 6D). In DIV 9 male cortical neurons, exposure to PCB 37 at 0.0001 or 0.1 nM PCB 37 exposure significantly decreased the number of Sholl intersections in proximal dendritic regions (Fig. 6E), which was reflected in decreased AUC values (Fig. 6F). In contrast, exposure to PCB 37 at 100 nM significantly increased dendritic complexity in DIV 9 male cortical neurons, as confirmed by significantly increased AUC values in this exposure group (Fig. 6F).

CREB inhibition blocked some but not all endpoints of PCB 37 developmental neurotoxicity

Given that PCB 11-induced dendritic growth was unaffected by RyR, AhR, or THR antagonism but was completely blocked by the selective CREB inhibitor 666–15 (Sethi et al., 2018), we investigated whether CREB signaling also mediated the DNT effects of PCB 37. Pretreatment with the CREB inhibitor 666–15 (500 nM) for 30 min prior to PCB 37 exposure effectively blocked PCB 37 effects on apoptosis and dendritic morphology (Fig. 7). As previously shown in Fig. 3, PCB 37 significantly increased caspase 3/7 activity in DIV 9 female and male cultures in the absence of 666–15; however, in cultures pretreated with 666–15, a 48-h exposure to PCB 37 at 100 nM had no significant effect on caspase 3/7 activity (Fig. 7A,B). Pretreatment with 666–15 similarly blocked the dendrite-promoting activity of PCB 37 previously observed in DIV 9 female and male cortical cultures (Fig. 7D–G). In contrast, pretreatment with 666–15 failed to block PCB 37-enhanced axonal growth in DIV 2 male cortical neurons (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

The data from these studies expands our previous work characterizing the effects of the LC-PCB, PCB 11, on neuronal morphogenesis in vitro (Sethi et al., 2017). The findings described here suggest that PCB 37 is also a potential developmental neurotoxicant that may interfere with normal neurodevelopment. Specifically we found that PCB 37 induced significant sex- and concentration-dependent effects on neuronal morphology and apoptosis independent of cytotoxicity. Caspase 3/7 activity increased in DIV 2 female, but not male neurons exposed to PCB 37 at 100 nM, while neurons from both sexes showed increased caspase 3/7 activity and phosphatidylserine externalization without loss of membrane integrity by DIV 9 when exposed to PCB 37 at 100 nM. Dendritic complexity decreased at DIV 9 in neurons of both sexes at lower concentrations (0.0001–0.1 nM) but increased in DIV 9 males at 100 nM. Axonal growth was enhanced in DIV 2 male neurons exposed to PCB 37 at 0.0001 nM, with no significant effects in female neurons or on cell body area in either sex. CREB inhibition blocked PCB 37 effects on dendritic complexity and apoptosis but not axonal growth.

Developmental exposure to PCBs, including the commercial Aroclor mixtures and HC-PCBs (Bell et al., 2018, Keil Stietz et al., 2021, Lein et al., 2007, Liberman et al., 2020, Wayman et al., 2012, Wilson et al., 2024b, Yang et al., 2009) and the LC-PCB 11 (Sethi et al., 2017, Sethi et al., 2018), have previously been shown to disrupt axonal and dendritic morphology in vitro and in vivo. Our findings extend these previous observations and suggest that changes in neuronal morphogenesis may be a generalized outcome of PCB neurotoxicity. The functional consequences of these changes were not investigated in this study; however, axonal and dendritic morphology are considered major determinants of synaptic connectivity, which in turn, determines neurological function. Deviations in axonal and dendritic number, length, and/or branching patterns are thought to be an underlying cause of human neurodevelopmental disorders (Guang et al., 2018, Stamou et al., 2013), and even subtle changes in the spatiotemporal development of axons and dendrites are associated with altered behaviors in animal models (Berger-Sweeney, 1997, Gozzi and Zerbi, 2023).

Apoptosis refines and sculpts synaptic networks (Su, 2020). Inappropriate modulation of apoptosis can alter neurodevelopmental trajectories, resulting in persistent cognitive and behavioral deficits (Levenson, 2005, Yochum et al., 2008). In vitro studies have demonstrated that Aroclor mixtures and HC-PCBs induce apoptosis in primary rodent neurons at concentrations that do not cause overt cytotoxicity (Howard et al., 2003, Sanchez-Alonso et al., 2004, Yang and Lein, 2010). Studies of individual congeners indicate that the pro-apoptotic activity of these Aroclor mixtures is largely mediated by the non-coplanar non-dioxin-like (NDL) PCBs, which are thought to be predominantly responsible for PCB DNT (Klocke and Lein, 2020), though not all NDL congeners exhibit this property (Howard et al., 2003). The mechanism (s) by which NDL PCBs trigger apoptosis vary between congeners. PCB 52 has been shown to induce apoptosis in human neuronal SK-N-MC cells through a p53-independent mechanism (Hwang, 2001), while PCB 47 significantly increased caspase-dependent apoptosis in primary rat hippocampal neurons, but not cortical neurons (Howard et al., 2003). Our findings extend these previous studies by demonstrating that the LC-PCB 37 promotes apoptosis via CREB-dependent activation of caspase 3/7 activity.

The concentration-effect relationships observed in this study varied across neurodevelopmental endpoints. Dendritic arborization was the most sensitive endpoint, demonstrating a nonmonotonic dose-response relationship. In contrast, apoptosis exhibited a monotonic response, while axonal outgrowth was affected at low concentrations in males. In the prior research of PCBs, two frequently identified findings are the nonmonotonicity and low-dose effects of PCBs on dendritic arborization (Wayman et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2009). The biological explanation for the non-monotonic dose-related effects of PCBs remains unknown; however, one of the mechanisms that has been implicated in nonmonotonicity is receptor selectivity, where lower doses of the chemical bind with high affinity to one receptor, but at higher doses, the same chemical can bind to multiple receptors, including receptors for which it has relatively low affinity (Mu et al., 2009). The divergence in concentration-effect relationships suggests distinct underlying mechanisms. These findings highlight the complexity of PCB neurotoxicity and the importance of examining multiple endpoints to fully characterize developmental neurotoxic effects.

We also noted sex differences in response to PCB 37. Major sex differences were observed with respect to PCB 37 effects on apoptosis, as evidenced by increased caspase 3/7 activity in DIV 2 female cultures at 100 nM PCB 37, with no corresponding increase in males. Additionally, PCB 37 enhanced axonal growth in DIV 2 male neurons at 0.0001 nM but had effect on axonal growth in female neurons at this concentration. Exposure to PCB 37 at 100 nM increased dendritic complexity in DIV 9 male but not female neurons. There are known sex differences in the developmental trajectory of the brain (Brenhouse and Andersen, 2011) that underlie sex differences in response to developmental neurotoxicants (Bell et al., 2018, Sethi et al., 2021), and this may be responsible for the sex differences observed in this study. The distinct maturation processes in male and female brains may result in varying susceptibility to PCB-induced neurotoxic effects (Hilz and Gore, 2022).

CREB is essential for nervous system development and plasticity, particularly in cortical regions, where it predominantly affects pyramidal neurons (Imaki et al., 1994, Pham et al., 1999, Sakamoto et al., 2011,). Here, we found that pharmacological inhibition of CREB blocked PCB 37 effects on apoptosis and dendritic arborization but did not alter the effects of PCB 37 on axon growth. Whether PCB 37 effects on apoptosis and dendritic arborization are linked or mediated via independent mechanisms is unclear. Certainly, caspase 3/7 activation can lead not only to apoptosis but also to subtle changes in neuronal morphology (Su, 2020). This could be due in part to disrupted caspase-mediated cleavage of cytoskeletal or microtubule-associated proteins, such as tau (Chung et al., 2001, Fasulo et al., 2000), or because loss of synapses and retraction of neurites may precede cell death (Luo and O’Leary, 2005, Wang et al., 2012). Disruption in these processes can lead to impaired axonal growth and dendritic branching (Bendiske et al., 2002, Chang et al., 2018, Marchisella et al., 2016). This suggests that the effects of PCB 37 on apoptosis and dendritic growth are independent of its effects on axonal outgrowth. Prior research demonstrated that the HC-PCB 95 enhanced dendritic arborization via CREB activation downstream of ryanodine receptor (RyR) sensitization (Wayman et al., 2006, Wayman et al., 2012). Subsequent studies found that PCB 11 also promoted dendritic growth via CREB-dependent mechanisms; however, the upstream signaling pathway did not involve RyR activation or signaling via the aryl hydrocarbon receptor or thyroid hormone receptor (Sethi et al., 2018). The upstream signaling pathways modulated by PCB 37 to activate CREB remain to be determined. Nonetheless, the findings of the current study contribute to the growing body of evidence implicating CREB signaling as a convergent mechanism in the developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) caused by PCBs (Klocke et al., 2020).

Epidemiological studies have consistently shown that early-life exposure to PCBs negatively impacts neuropsychological function (Grandjean and Landrigan, 2014, Hertz-Picciotto et al., 2018, Schantz et al., 2003, Stolevik et al., 2013), and the predominant theory in the field is that they elicit these neurobehavioral deficits by interfering with the establishment of synaptic connectivity (Panesar et al., 2020, Panesar et al., 2022). Human neurodevelopmental disorders are thought to arise, in part, from disruptions in synaptic connectivity (Copf, 2016, Hoersting and Schmucker, 2021, Paus, 2023). Additionally, studies in animal models have demonstrated that subtle shifts in the timing and spatial patterns of brain development can lead to behavioral alterations (Berger-Sweeney, 1997, Sethi et al., 2021, Wilson et al., 2024b). While much of the research has focused on the DNT of legacy PCB mixtures or HC-PCBs, recently, studies have begun investigating the neurodevelopmental impacts of individual PCB congeners or mixtures based on profiles seen in human tissues (Li et al., 2022). Our results suggest that LC-PCBs modulate synaptic connectivity via modulation of axonal growth, dendritic arborization, and apoptosis, and thus, may influence individual risk for neurodevelopmental disorders.

Conclusions

The developmental neurotoxicity profile of PCB 37 does not directly mirror that of PCB 11 (Sethi et al., 2017, Sethi et al., 2018). PCB 11 and PCB 37 both modulate dendritic arborization via CREB signaling but differ in their effects and specificity. PCB 11 promotes axonal and dendritic growth across concentrations. In contrast, PCB 37 exhibits variable effects depending on concentration and sex, decreasing dendritic complexity in DIV 9 cortical neurons at lower doses while increasing complexity in male neurons at higher doses (100 nM). PCB 37 also selectively increases axon length in DIV 2 male, but not female, cortical neurons. These findings underscore PCB-specific and context-dependent neurodevelopmental effects, adding to the literature that suggests there are overlapping but also unique toxic effects associated with individual PCB congeners. This underscores the challenges of conducting risk assessments based on individual PCB congeners, as they may not fully capture the developmental neurotoxic potential of other human-relevant PCBs. Further evaluation of human-relevant contemporary PCBs and PCB mixtures in appropriate animal models will be essential to understanding how PCBs interact to influence adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. Additionally, the development of more complex quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models will be instrumental in predicting DNT outcomes and improving risk assessment strategies of human exposures to PCB mixtures.

These data also suggests that, despite structural differences, CREB activation may represent a convergent mechanism of PCB developmental neurotoxicity (Klocke et al., 2020). Identifying the specific upstream mechanisms by which PCB 37 influences CREB activity will be crucial for understanding the full scope of PCB-induced neurotoxicity and for developing targeted interventions to mitigate these adverse effects.
