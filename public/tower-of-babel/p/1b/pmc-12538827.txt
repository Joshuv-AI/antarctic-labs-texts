Comparative transcriptomics reveals differential carbohydrate and lipid metabolism in roots and nodules of Rhizobia-Inoculated alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.)
Authors: Bao-Fu Lu, Wen-Juan Kang, Shang-Li Shi, Fang Jing, Jian Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe symbiotic nitrogen-fixing system formed between alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) and rhizobia requires precise regulation of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism to sustain their high-energy-demand system. However, metabolic divergence between roots and nodules remains poorly characterized.ResultsUsing comparative transcriptomics, we analyzed gene expression profiles in pink nodules (PN), white nodules (WN), Pink nodule roots (PNR), white nodule roots (WNR), non-nodule roots (NNR) and control roots (CKR) from rhizobia-inoculated plants at 35 days post-inoculation. Key findings revealed metabolic specialization between tissues: PN exhibited elevated expression of lipid catabolism genes (MsECHIA, MsACX) and key genes of the TCA cycle regulators, driving direct energy supply for nitrogenase activity. PNR, WNR preferentially expressed glycolysis (MsPKP2) and pentose phosphate pathway (MsG6PD5) genes to convert photoassimilates into dicarboxylic acids via a directional transport system to nodules. WN showed enriched fatty acid elongation genes (MsKCR1, MsHACD2), suggesting compensatory synthesis of structural lipid to maintain symbiotic interfaces under carbon limitation. NNR, CKR retained starch metabolism dominance. Weighted geneco-expression network analysis revealed that symbiotic signaling synchronizes nodule lipid degradation with root carbon repartitioning to prioritize photoassimilate allocation to nodules. Nodulated roots may supplement nodule energetics through lipid precursor synthesis or storage lipid hydrolysis, thereby forming a “root-nodule metabolic relay” mechanism. Our results demonstrate that the alfalfa-rhizobia symbiosis establishes a hierarchical energy distribution network through tissue-specific regulation of metabolic genes, coordinating nitrogen fixation efficiency with energy supply homeostasis.ConclusionsThis study elucidates metabolic coordination mechanisms underlying legume-rhizobial symbiosis, providing a theoretical framework for optimizing symbiotic energy economics through targeted gene editing approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07290-3.

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a widely cultivated leguminous forage, establishes symbiotic relationships with rhizobia to form nitrogen-fixing nodules—specialized “factories” for symbiotic nitrogen fixation (SNF) [1–3]. This symbiotic system efficiently converts atmospheric nitrogen into bioavailable ammonium (NH₄⁺), significantly enhancing nitrogen utilization efficiency. The nitrogen-fixing capacity of alfalfa and rhizobia symbiosis is fundamentally determined by their genetic compatibility. Highly compatible rhizobial strains recognize flavonoid based signaling molecules secreted by host roots, thereby inducing the formation of pink nodules (PN). These functional nodules contain leghemoglobins that regulate oxygen tension, maintaining optimal activity of nitrogenase complexes to catalyze the reduction of atmospheric dinitrogen (N₂) to ammonium. In contrast, suboptimal compatibility or environmental stressors (e.g., pH extremes, aluminum toxicity) may drive rhizobia to establish “pseudo-symbiosis” through molecular mimicry of nodulation factors (Nod factors). This results in white nodules (WN) characterized by leghemoglobin deficiency and nitrogenase inactivation, representing metabolically immature structures devoid of nitrogen-fixing capacity. Complete genetic incompatibility or severe environmental constraints disrupt rhizobial penetration through root epidermal barriers, ultimately leading to symbiotic failure and non-nodulation phenotypes [4–6]. The alfalfa-rhizobia symbiosis operates as a tightly regulated process, and our prior studies have indicated peak SNF activity at 35 days post inoculation (dpi) [7]. SNF is energy-intensive, requiring 16 ATP molecules per fixed nitrogen molecule. Plants fuel this process by catabolizing sucrose into dicarboxylates (mainly malate and succinate), which serve as carbon skeletons and electron donors for bacteroids [8, 9]. Crucially, plants employ a merit-based resource allocation strategy, preferentially supporting high-efficiency PN while restricting energy supply to low-performance WN [4, 10, 11]. Recent studies suggest evolutionary links between legume signaling pathways for rhizobial symbiosis and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis. While traditional models posit carbohydrates as the primary energy currency for microbial partners, emerging evidence reveals that AM fungi rely on host-derived lipids [12], a mechanism conserved in other plant-microbe interactions [13–17]. However, the role of lipids in the alfalfa-rhizobia system remains unresolved.

This study investigates energy-partitioning mechanisms across symbiotic organs in alfalfa. Preliminary metabolomic analyses identified significant lipid and carbohydrate enrichment in PN and PNR, yet the underlying genetic regulation remains uncharacterized [18]. We performed transcriptomic profiling of rhizobia-inoculated plants at 35 dpi, comparing PN, WN, PNR, WNR, NNR and CKR. Our objectives are to dissect differential expression patterns of lipid and carbohydrate metabolism genes, elucidate host strategies for energy allocation during SNF, and provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing energy flux in the alfalfa-rhizobia symbiosis through targeted metabolic engineering.

Results

Transcriptome analysis

To investigate the molecular basis of differentiation between root nodules and root systems, we performed transcriptome profiling on two nodule types (PN, WN) and four root types (PNR, WNR, NNR, CKR) with sampling positions illustrated in Fig. 1A. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between nodules and roots, with the first two principal components explaining 62.57% of total variance. Biological replicates clustered tightly within groups (P < 0.05), confirming experimental reproducibility (Fig. 1B). Cluster analysis revealed high intra-group sample correlations (indicating similar gene expression patterns within groups), with PN and WN nodules showing significant positive correlations to their corresponding roots PNR and WNR, a moderate correlation between PN and WN, whereas NNR and CKR exhibited low correlations with other samples (Fig. 1 C). Gene expression quantification revealed that WN nodules exhibited 23.6% lower mean TPM values compared to PN, indicating transcriptional suppression under host-mediated resource toward these fixation-deficient units. while other groups showed comparable expression levels (Fig. 1D). Differential expression analysis identified distinct gene sets across comparisons: WN-specific genes constituted 60.63% (730/1,205) of PN-associated DEGs; PN vs. PNR displayed balanced unique gene numbers (PN: 326 vs. PNR: 298); (3) WNR contained 2.12-fold more unique genes than WN. Notably, CKR harbored the largest repertoire of unique genes (931), significantly exceeding other root types (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1E).

Analysis of TFs regulation differences

Among different roots and nodules, the abundance and functional roles of TF families were characterized as follows: The bHLH family exhibited the highest gene count, with over 1,500 members in PN, WN, and PNR, and exceeding 1,200 in other roots, regulating growth, development, cell differentiation, and signal transduction. The MYB family, second in abundance, contained more than 1,000 genes in PN, WN, and PNR, and surpassed 800 in other roots. Together with the MYB-related family (containing over 500 genes), these families were implicated in secondary metabolism, hormone response, and stress adaptation. The NAC family comprised nearly 1,000 genes, primarily regulating growth, development, and stress responses. The bZIP, C2H2, and WRKY families ranged between 500 and 1,000 genes, with WRKY specifically associated with immunity and stress. The AP2, ARF, and ARR-B families were less abundant but widely distributed (Fig. S1).

Differential expression analysis revealed that the PN vs. PNR comparison displayed the highest number of DEGs (10,042), whereas PNR vs. NNR had the fewest (4,077). Enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs across all comparison groups revealed significant enrichment in three major functional categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). Further KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in the number of enriched pathways among groups. Specifically, the PN vs. WN and NNR vs. CKR groups exhibited the highest number of enriched pathways (25 each), while the PNR vs. CKR and WNR vs. CKR groups showed the fewest (12 and 11 pathways, respectively; Fig. S4A), indicating distinct activation levels of metabolic and regulatory networks in response to root and nodule variations.

DEG enrichment in nodules

In the PN vs. WN comparison, DEGs were significantly enriched in 32 GO terms and 25 KEGG pathways. GO analysis highlighted BP terms predominantly associated with morphogenesis, including “anatomical structure morphogenesis” and “nodule morphogenesis” (16 terms total), with upregulated DEGs consistently outnumbering downregulated genes. CC terms were enriched only in “extracellular region” (17 DEGs). MF terms were linked to metal ion binding and oxidoreductase activity, such as “heme binding” and “iron ion binding” (15 terms; Fig. S2A). KEGG analysis identified five pathways critical to carbon and lipid metabolism: “starch and sucrose metabolism”, “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”, “linoleic acid metabolism”, “fatty acid degradation”, and “pyruvate metabolism”, suggesting this group plays a central role in energy metabolism regulation.

Comparative analysis of root and nodule DEGs

In the PN vs. PNR group, DEGs were enriched in three BP terms (“defense response”, “movement of cellular or subcellular component”, and “microtubule-based movement”), dominated by downregulated genes. CC terms were enriched in “extracellular region” (23 DEGs), while MF terms spanned “nutrient reservoir activity”, “oxidoreductase activity”, and “microtubule motor activity” (21 terms; Fig. S2B). For the WN vs. WNR group, DEGs were enriched in 61 GO terms, predominantly MF categories (50 terms), including “peroxidase activity” and “antioxidant activity”. BP terms involved morphogenesis pathways (e.g., “nodule morphogenesis”), and CC terms were enriched in “extracellular region” (21 DEGs; Fig. S2C). KEGG analysis revealed that PN vs. PNR DEGs participated in “ABC transporters”, “flavonoid biosynthesis”, and “plant hormone signal transduction” (24 pathways; Fig. S4C). In contrast, WN vs. WNR DEGs shared pathways such as “ABC transporters” and “plant hormone signal transduction” (9 pathways) but uniquely enriched “linoleic acid metabolism” and “α-linolenic acid metabolism” (Fig. S4D). Notably, both groups shared 14 pathways, but exhibited divergent DEG numbers in “starch and sucrose metabolism” (Fig. S3E), implying that nodule types may differentially regulate carbohydrate and fatty acid metabolism to influence energy allocation and material transport.

Enrichment analysis of root DEGs

In GO enrichment analyses of DEGs across root comparison groups, the PNR vs. WNR group showed significant enrichment in symbiotic-related functions such as “nodulation” and “terpene synthase activity” (Fig. S3A). The PNR vs. NNR group exhibited a preference for “cellular carbohydrate metabolic process” in BP terms (Fig. S3B), while PNR vs. CKR was linked to “defense response” (35 DEGs) and “hormone metabolism process” (7 DEGs), suggesting a synergistic interplay between symbiosis and immune regulation (Fig. S3C). In contrast, the WNR vs. NNR group had fewer enriched terms (42) but higher DEG counts, with MF terms dominating (28 entries) (Fig. S3D). The WNR vs. CKR group displayed significantly more BP-associated DEGs than MF-associated ones (Fig. S3E), whereas NNR vs. CKR showed the highest number of enriched terms (103), primarily related to basal metabolism and stress responses (Fig. S3F). These differences imply that rhizobia may reprogram gene expression to prioritize symbiosis-associated processes (e.g., energy supply, redox balance) while coordinating immune and hormonal pathways to optimize resource allocation, thereby sustaining nitrogen fixation efficiency and host adaptation.

KEGG pathway analysis further revealed that the PNR vs. WNR group was enriched in 14 pathways, with 21 downregulated DEGs in “linoleic acid metabolism”, indicating suppressed fatty acid metabolism during symbiosis (Fig. S5A). The PNR vs. NNR group exhibited 16 pathways, including “α-linolenic acid metabolism” and “pyruvate metabolism”, with 120 DEGs enriched in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism (Fig. S5B). In PNR vs. CKR, coordinated enrichment of “starch and sucrose metabolism” (59 genes) and “α-linolenic acid metabolism” suggested enhanced energy metabolism in rhizobia-inoculated alfalfa to support symbiotic nitrogen fixation (Fig. S5C). Additionally, the WNR vs. NNR and WNR vs. CKR groups were enriched in 16 and 11 pathways, respectively (Fig. S5D-5E), while NNR vs. CKR highlighted 25 stress-responsive pathways (Fig. S5F).

Shared pathways across comparisons, such as “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” and “α-linolenic acid metabolism”, may regulate secondary metabolites and lipid signaling. Unique pathway divergences were evident: PNR vs. WNR implicated “plant-pathogen interaction”, reflecting crosstalk between symbiosis and immunity; PNR vs. NNR emphasized “tyrosine metabolism” and “carbon fixation in photosymthetic organisms”, indicating NNR reliance on photoassimilates; and NNR vs. CKR highlighted “amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism”, suggesting carbon skeleton remodeling in uninoculated roots under stress (Fig. S5G). Collectively, rhizobial symbiosis reprograms fatty acid metabolism, energy transport, and secondary metabolic pathways to balance carbohydrate-lipid metabolism and optimize resource allocation in alfalfa, ensuring nitrogen fixation efficiency and environmental adaptability.

WGCNA analysis of differentially expressed genes

Construction of weighted gene co-expression networks

A weighted gene co-expression regulatory network was constructed using the WGCNA algorithm. The gene expression matrix was normalized to FPKM values, and a soft threshold power (β) of 8 was selected (R² >0.8) to satisfy scale free network topology (Fig. 3 A and B). Hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting partitioned genes into 26 color-coded modules, with the turquoise module containing the most genes (5,182) and the orange module the fewest (88) (Fig. 3 C), reflecting coordinated expression patterns across functional modules. Notably, modules exhibited strong interconnectivity (Fig. 3D), and significant correlations were observed between specific modules and distinct roots/nodules (Fig. 3E).

Analysis of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism related DEGs

We focused on carbohydrate and lipid metabolism across different root systems/root nodules. KEGG tertiary metabolic pathway analysis revealed significant differential expression patterns in carbohydrate metabolism (253 DEGs) and lipid metabolism (287 DEGs) between root systems and root nodules. The number of DEGs enriched in these two metabolic pathways was significantly higher in root nodules (PN, WN) than in root systems (PNR, WNR, NNR, CKR), with PNR showing the highest enrichment among root systems (Fig. 7A). Distinct distribution patterns of highly expressed genes were observed: In PN, lipid degradation genes dominated (122 genes), directly fueling nitrogen fixation, whereas WN prioritized fatty acid elongation (121 genes) to sustain structural integrity under carbon limitation, in root nodules, lipid metabolism related genes (PN: 122; WN: 121) outnumbered carbohydrate metabolism genes by 1.67 and 1.23-fold, respectively. Conversely, root systems exhibited a predominance of carbohydrate metabolism genes, with NNR showing the highest ratio (1.57) and WNR the lowest (1.12) (Fig. 7B and D), indicating functional divergence in energy metabolism regulation between root nodules and root systems.

Further analysis of genes enriched in tertiary carbohydrate and lipid metabolic pathways demonstrated: Carbohydrate metabolism: PN and WN root nodules were dominated by “starch and sucrose metabolism” (17.39% in PN vs. 13.91% in WN), whereas PNR, WNR, and NNR root systems primarily enriched “amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism” with comparable proportions. CKR resembled root nodules in prioritizing “starch and sucrose metabolism” (13.7%) (Fig. 7E). Lipid metabolism: All organs showed enrichment in pathways such as “glycerophospholipid/glycerolipid metabolism”. WN contained only 78.57% of PN’s gene count in “glycerophospholipid metabolism”. Root systems exhibited marked variations: PNR dominated in “α-linolenic acid metabolism” but was weaker in “glycerophospholipid metabolism”; WNR showed parity with other root systems in “fatty acid degradation” and “fatty acid biosynthesis”; NNR specialized in “etherl ipid metabolism”; CKR displayed significantly fewer lipid pathway genes than other root systems (Fig. 7F).

Disscusion

The symbiotic nitrogen fixation system of alfalfa and rhizobia has garnered significant attention in agricultural and ecological research due to its high nitrogen-use efficiency. As an energy-intensive process, symbiotic nitrogen fixation relies on precise regulation of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Previous studies revealed organ-specific metabolic profiles in high-efficiency nitrogen-fixing alfalfa: carbohydrate content in PNR was 2.1-fold higher than in WNR, likely to support nodule development, while fatty acid levels in PN were 3.2-fold higher than in WN, suggesting prioritized energy allocation for nitrogen fixation [18]. Although transcriptomic studies have elucidated molecular mechanisms of symbiotic nitrogen fixation and stress responses [21, 22], systematic insights into carbon and lipid metabolic differences between roots and nodules post-rhizobial inoculation remain limited. This study employs transcriptomics to dissect organ-specific carbohydrate and lipid metabolic pathways in symbiotic alfalfa during peak nitrogen fixation, unveiling regulatory networks that underpin metabolic reprogramming. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing energy allocation and interaction efficiency in the Medicago-rhizobia symbiosis.

DEG enrichment analysis

Organ specific DEG distribution and metabolic functional divergence

DEGs exhibited significant quantitative and expressional divergence across roots and nodules, reflecting distinct energy allocation demands in the symbiotic system. PN showed markedly more DEGs than WN (1.41-fold higher upregulated genes), predominantly enriched in lipid degradation (MsECHIA, MsACX) and the TCA cycle (Citrate synthase), directly supporting the high energy demands of nitrogen fixation. Aligns with host sanctions that redirect photoassimilates toward high-efficiency nodules while restricting investment in fixation-deficient units (WN) [10]. This aligns with prior findings of elevated fatty acid metabolites and carbohydrates in PN [18]. In contrast, WN upregulated fatty acid elongation genes (MsKCR1, MsHACD2) under carbon limitation to synthesize structural lipids. PNR, WNR exhibited high expression of glycolysis (MsPKP2) and pentose phosphate pathway (MsG6PD5) genes, driving photosynthetic product conversion to dicarboxylates for targeted transport. NNR, CKR favored starch and sucrose metabolism (MsBGLU12), emphasizing carbon storage and basal energy supply.

Enrichment disparities

Integrated GO and KEGG analyses systematically revealed multi-dimensional regulatory features of DEGs in the symbiotic system, spanning functional modules to metabolic networks. In PN vs. WN, PN exhibited significant enrichment in linoleic acid metabolism and glycolysis genes, while WN dominated phytohormone signaling pathways. PN also showed upregulated genes associated with root nodule morphogenesis, and shared oxidoreductase activity terms with WN, suggesting synergistic roles in fatty acid/sugar metabolism. These findings imply that PN may modulate oxygen levels within nodules via fatty acid degradation and oxidoreductase activity, establishing a low-oxygen microenvironment to balance nitrogenase functionality (oxygen-sensitive) and energy production. This hypoxic niche protects nitrogenase from oxidative damage while sustaining basal respiration, achieving dynamic equilibrium between nitrogen fixation and energy metabolism [23–25]. In contrast, WN’s carbohydrate metabolic advantage was counteracted by dysregulated phytohormone signaling and the absence of leghemoglobin, rendering its metabolic activity ineffective for nitrogen fixation [26].

PN vs. PNR and WN vs. WNR comparisons revealed nodulation-specific metabolic reprogramming. Both PN and WN downregulated immune response genes (e.g., phenylpropanoid biosynthesis) and established carbon-prioritized allocation networks mediated by ABC transporters and ATP-binding cassette transporters, stabilizing the symbiotic microenvironment [27]. However, adaptive divergence emerged: PN relied on fatty acid metabolism (linoleic acid pathway) and oxidoreductase activity to sustain a low-oxygen state, whereas WN employed α-linolenic acid metabolism and antioxidant systems to mitigate oxidative stress. Aberrant cytokinin signaling in WN likely disrupted symbiotic coordination, contributing to low nitrogen fixation efficiency. Collectively, carbon directional carbon transport and immune suppression are prerequisites for symbiosis establishment, with PN prioritizing energy metabolism and WN relying on oxidative stress regulation.

KEGG enrichment highlighted plant–pathogen interaction and stilbenoid biosynthesis pathways in PNR and WNR. GO analysis further showed exclusive activation of symbiotic interaction development in PNR and terpene synthase activity in WNR. PNR may repurpose pathogen defense pathways to establish rhizobial interaction interfaces via shared signaling molecules [28], enhancing both symbiosis efficiency and rhizobial survival. WNR, conversely, leveraged terpenoid synthesis to improve symbiotic specificity. Terpenoids, known for mediating microbial recognition [29], likely attract specific rhizobial strains, fostering stable symbiotic partnerships.

Differential expression of TFs

TFs play pivotal roles in mediating gene expression divergence across roots and nodules in the symbiotic nitrogen fixation system. Several TFs have been identified as critical regulators in legume-rhizobia symbiosis [30–33]. In this study, the distribution and functional specialization of TFs exhibited significant organ-specific patterns, with the NAC and bHLH families being particularly prominent. The NAC family, unique to plants, displayed the highest gene abundance across organs. Notably, GmNAC039 and GmNAC018 homologs regulate nodule senescence in soybean symbiosis [34]. The elevated NAC family abundance in WN may reflect reduced demand for environmental adaptation linked to nitrogen fixation. Conversely, the bHLH family, the second most abundant TF group, was predominantly enriched in PNR and PN. bHLH TFs act as cytokinin signaling targets in nodule cells [35], with MtbHLH1 implicated in modulating nodule vasculature patterning and nutrient exchange between roots and nodules [36]. Intriguingly, bHLH gene numbers followed a gradient of roots surpassing nodules in quantity, with PNR showing the highest root-specific abundance and PN exceeding WN in nodules. This suggests that bHLH TFs may coordinate complex metabolic adaptations in PNR (e.g., energy metabolism) and PN (e.g., nitrogen fixation), where dynamic environmental cues demand robust regulatory networks.

Multi layered carbon allocation strategies in symbiotic nitrogen fixation

Starch and sucrose metabolism drives carbon source allocation divergence

Starch and sucrose metabolism (regulated in the cytoplasmic matrix of roots and nodules) [37, 38] exhibited distinct organ-specific patterns. A total of 48 highly expressed genes (log2FC > 0.5) were identified in starch and sucrose metabolism, with 26 showing organ-specific expression. PN high expressed genes for up to14, including MsSS1[MsG0380016531_01] and 1,4-α-glucan branching enzyme MsGBE1[MsG0780041091_01], suggesting PN as an active site for starch synthesis. WN showed high expression of 9 genes (α-amylase3 and lysosomal β-glucosidase MsgluA [MsG0680031685_01]), indicating a preference for starch degradation. Conversely, PNR and WNR showed suppressed MsSS1 expression (consistent with reduced starch synthesis demands in absorption-specialized roots) but elevated expression of endoglucanase MsCEL1 [MsG0480023913_01]and β-glucosidase MsBGLU12[MsG0680034978_01], which degrade cell wall polysaccharides to release glucose for rhizobial colonization. NNR, CKR prioritized β-fructosidase MsBFRUCT1[MsG0280011203_01]-mediated sucrose hydrolysis into glucose and fructose, the latter polymerizing into functional oligosaccharides. These findings demonstrate organ-specific sugar metabolic strategies for carbon partitioning (Fig. 9).

Glycolysis/gluconeogenesis adaptation to carbon-energy demands

Glycolysis (glucose-to-pyruvate breakdown yielding 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose [39]) and gluconeogenesis (non-carbohydrate-to-glucose conversion [40]) were dynamically regulated. Among 38 DEGs in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways, 23 genes were significantly upregulated (log2FC > 0.5) in at least one organ. PN overexpressed acetyl-CoA synthesis genes (MsACS [MsG0780040123_01], MsCCL3 [MsG0880047141_01], MsLPD2 [MsG0780037292_01]), channeling substrates into the TCA cycle or fatty acid synthesis [41, 42], while WN uniquely upregulated pyrophosphate-dependent fructose-6-phosphate kinase (MsPFP-β [MsG0880047679_01]) to balance glycolytic/gluconeogenic flux. In roots, PNR highly expressed pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH-E1β) and fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase) genes to synchronize carbon metabolism with PN’s energy demands. WNR upregulated plastid pyruvate kinase (MsPKP2 [MsG0680031874_01]) and enolase (MsENO1 [MsG0280009241_01]), accelerating glycolysis via rate-limiting steps (e.g., PEP-to-pyruvate conversion) to fuel non nitrogen fixing functions [43–45]. These organ specific enzymatic adjustments underscore dynamic carbon-energy trade-offs in symbiosis (Fig. 10).

Pyruvate metabolism, a pivotal carbon metabolic node [48], displayed functional divergence. Among 42 highly expressed pyruvate metabolism genes, 27 showed differential distribution. Division of labor in fatty acid elongation metabolism

The fatty acid elongation process converts short/medium-chain fatty acids into long-chain forms by enzymatic reactions [57, 58]. Eleven highly expressed genes were identified in fatty acid elongation pathways. Fatty acid elongation diverged between PN and WN. Organ specific differentiation in fatty acid degradation

There were 32 genes with high expression related to fatty acid degradation, with 21 showing differential expression. Fatty acid degradation via β-oxidation dominated in nodules and nodulated roots. PN overexpressed Enoyl-CoA hydratase (MsECHIA [MsG0880047170_01]) to generate Acetyl-CoA for nitrogenase activity [63, 64], while PNR and WNR upregulated Acyl-CoA oxidase (MsACOX [MsG0580024543_01]) to meet developmental energy demands [65]. NNR downregulated MsALDH3F1 [MsG0480021060_01] (Aldehyde dehydrogenase), suggesting rhizobia suppress host lipid stress responses to prioritize symbiosis [66], [28]. These findings highlight nodule-root metabolic division: nodules and nodulated roots prioritize lipid catabolism for energy, while NNR adjust basal metabolism in response to symbiotic cues (Fig. 16).

Fig. 16Fatty acid degradationIn conclusion, these expression differences reflect alfalfa’s precise regulatory mechanisms in balancing root and nodule functional demands to optimize resource allocation and maintain organ-specific physiological activities. Specifically, alfalfa employs a multi layered energy partitioning strategy through tissue specific metabolic gene expression: In PN and WN, elevated expression of lipid degradation pathway genes (MsECHIA, MsACX) and TCA cycle enzymes (Citrate synthase [MsG0380013811_01]) prioritizes energy metabolism to fuel symbiotic nitrogen fixation via ATP and reducing equivalents. In contrast, PNR and WNR activate Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (MsPKP2) and pentose phosphate pathway genes (MsG6PD5), channeling photoassimilates toward dicarboxylate synthesis for targeted delivery to nodules to meet their elevated energy demands. At the same time, the co-expression of fatty acid synthesis gene (MsKAS2) in nodules and structural lipid metabolism genes in roots further reflected the fine division of metabolic pathways in alfalfa, which not only ensured the efficient operation of nitrogen fixation function, but also maintained the basic needs of root absorption and transportation. This dynamic regulatory network achieves the optimal allocation of resources by accurately balancing the spatial and temporal distribution of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, and ensures the global coordination of the symbiotic system between energy supply, nitrogen fixation efficiency and organ development.

However, this study has certain limitations: (1) The single sampling time point (35 days post-inoculation) does not comprehensively cover the entire symbiotic continuum, including symbiosis establishment, functional nitrogen fixation maintenance, and nodule senescence. (2) White nodules (WN) represent a heterogeneous cohort of “resource-restricted fixation-deficient units” rather than purely non-fixing entities. This population potentially includes developmentally arrested or ineffective nodules, and bulk transcriptomics integrates signals across diverse cell types, potentially obscuring metabolically distinct subpopulations. Future investigations will expand the temporal dimension to multiple symbiotic stages, comparing high-efficiency nitrogen-fixing systems (plants harboring pink nitrogen-fixing nodules, PN) with non-fixing reference systems (plants exhibiting exclusively white nodules, WN). This will be achieved through spatiotemporal multi-omics approaches to systematically resolve tissue- and time-specific distributions of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism in nodules and root systems, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for enhancing symbiotic nitrogen fixation efficiency and advancing agricultural sustainability.

Conclusion

This study systematically deciphers the transcriptional networks and metabolic division of labor strategies in distinct root and nodule organs of the alfalfa-rhizobia symbiotic system. In PN, lipid degradation and the TCA cycle dominate energy production to drive nitrogen fixation. In contrast, PNR and WNR prioritize glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway (MsG6PD5) to efficiently convert photosynthates into dicarboxylates for targeted delivery to nodules. Intriguingly, these organs retain the capacity to synthesize lipid precursors and activate lipid metabolism genes (MsKAS2), suggesting a complementary mechanism to sustain nodule energy demands. The elevated expression of fatty acid elongation genes in WN, alongside the starch metabolism preference in NNR and CKR, highlights organ-specific metabolic specialization: nodules prioritize lipid catabolism for energy, while roots focus on carbon conversion and transport (Fig. 17). Collectively, this work unveils a spatiotemporally resolved regulatory framework that optimizes resource allocation by balancing carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Materials and methods

Plant and rhizobial materials

The tested rhizobial strain was Sinorhizobium meliloti LL11, and the alfalfa cultivar was Medicago sativa ‘Gannong No. 9’. Both materials were provided by the Key Laboratory of Grassland Ecosystem of the Ministry of Education, Gansu Agricultural University.

Experimental methods

Seedling cultivation

Healthy seeds of M. sativa ‘Gannong No. 9’ were surface-sterilized using iodophor and ST solution (i.e., a solution of 0.9% aseptic sodium chloride and 0.5% Tween-80), then placed on moist filter paper in Petri dishes for dark cultivation at 28 °C for 2–3 days to induce germination. Germinated seeds were transferred to a hydroponic system filled with Hoagland’s nitrogen-free nutrient solution (Chinese Utility Patent, patent number: ZL-2022-2-2289363.4) [67]. After 7 days, seedlings without root nodules were selected and transplanted into sterilized fine-sand nursery trays for individual cultivation. Periodic irrigation was performed (initial nitrogen supplemented solution, followed by alternating nitrogen free nutrient solution and distilled water at 5 d intervals). Subsequently, seedlings were placed in an artificial climate chamber (i.e., light 16 h·d−1, the lighting photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) 400 µmol·m−2·s−1) with day and night temperatures of 25 °C ± 1 °C and 20 °C ± 1 °C, respectively. The humidity was fixed at 50% relative humidity.

Rhizobial strain activation and inoculation

The S. meliloti LL11 strain, stored at − 80 °C and then resuscitated on yeast mannitol agar solid mediumat28°C. After culturing for 24 h, single colonies were inoculated into flasks containing Tryptone yeast (TY) liquid medium. This product was then incubated for 18–20 h at 28 °C on a rotary shaker at 180 rpm and centrifuged at 4 °C and 10,000 r·min−1 for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, the same amount of sterile water was added to wash the bacteria, and the bacterial suspen sion was configured using the same volume (i.e., OD600mm = 0.5 (concentration of 109 CFU·mL−1)). Using a pipette, 5 mL of bacterial suspension was then inoculated onto the root of each seedling, which ensured that no part of the seedling other than the root was inoculated. Sterile water was used to create a control condition; here, water was inoculated alone in CK (nonsymbiotic) plants using the procedure described above. Each experiment was repeated three times. Finally, after inoculation, 300 mL of Hoagland nutrient solution was irrigated. All samples were taken on the 35th day after inoculation.

RNA extraction and illumina sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from roots and nodules using an RNA extraction kit. RNA integrity and concentration were verified, followed by enrichment of polyA-tailed mRNA. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized, and libraries were constructed with size selection for fragments of 370–420 bp. Library quality was assessed using Qubit and Agilent Bioanalyzer, and quantification was performed via qRT-PCR. Qualified libraries were subjected to Illumina sequencing. Raw reads were filtered to remove low-quality data, yielding clean reads. HISAT2 software was used for alignment to the reference genome. Raw sequencing data are available in the NCBI SRA database under accession number PRJNA1248660 (Table S1).

Reference genome alignment

The reference genome of Medicago sativa ‘Zhongmu No.1’ was downloaded from the designated genome database. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5 for rapid and precise mapping.

Differential gene screening and functional annotation

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between root and nodule tissues were identified using DESeq2 (v1.20.0) with thresholds of |log₂ (fold change) | ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (padj) ≤ 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed via GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathways using ClusterProfiler (v3.8.1), with significance set at padj ≤ 0.05. Significantly enriched terms and pathways were selected for further interpretation.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) is a system biology method. It constructs a co-expression network by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between genes, clusters genes with similar expression patterns into modules, and screens key modules related to sample characteristics for functional enrichment analysis.

Gene expression analysis using qRT-PCR

RNA simple Total RNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH, Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) was used to extract RNA from different roots and nodules, and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA by FastKing RT Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Primer-BLAST was used to design specific primers for differential genes on NCBI (Table S2). The primer sequence was synthesized by Shanghai Shenggong, and alfalfa MsACTIN was used as an internal reference gene. SYBr Green qPCR Master Mix (Wuhan Xavier Biotechnology Co., LTD) was used for qPCR amplification on LightCycler 96 (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Three biological replicates were set, and three technical replicates were set for each biological replicate. The relative expression of each differential gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Data statistics and analysis methods

The data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2010 and visualized with GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Gene read counts were quantified by Feature Counts (v1.5.0-p3), followed by FPKM normalization for gene expression levels. Differential expression analysis between experimental groups was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.20.0). Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway assessments using cluster Profiler (v3.8.1).
