Additive genotoxic effects in cord blood cells upon indirect exposure to chemotherapeutic compounds crossing an in vitro placental barrier
Authors: Carolina Velazquez, Lien Loier, Ilana Struys, Eline Verscheure, Leentje Persoons, Lode Godderis, Liesbeth Lenaerts, Frédéric Amant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal exposure to toxins can adversely affect long-term health outcomes of the offspring. Though chemotherapeutics are now standard of care for treating cancer patients during pregnancy, certain compounds are known to cross the placenta and harm placental tissue. The consequences for the fetus are largely unexplored. Here we examined the responses of newborn cord blood mononuclear cells in tissue culture to two chemotherapeutic drugs, cyclophosphamide and epirubicin, when either directly exposed to these drugs, or indirectly after crossing a placenta trophoblast bilayer barrier. Cord blood mononuclear cells exposed to the conditioned media obtained from cyclophosphamide-exposed trophoblast barriers showed a significant 2.4-fold increase of nuclear ROS levels compared to direct exposure to cyclophosphamide. Indirect exposure to epirubicine-exposed trophoblast barriers not only enhanced nuclear ROS levels but also significantly increased the fraction of cord blood cells with double strand breaks, relative to directly exposed cells. Neither apoptosis nor proliferation markers were affected in cord mononuclear blood cells upon direct or indirect exposure to cyclophosphamide or epirubicin. Our data suggests that trophoblast cells exposed to cyclophosphamide or epirubicine may induce an indirect ‘bystander’ effect and can aggravate genotoxicity in the fetal compartment.Keywords: In vitro, Placental barrier, Chemotherapeutic compounds, Prenatal toxicity, Pregnancy, Bystander effectSubject terms: Cancer therapy, Paediatric research

Introduction

Harmful exposures during pregnancy have been linked to adverse effects in the offspring, increasing the risk of diseases later in life, a phenomenon known as the developmental origins of health and disease1. The placenta represents a crucial interface controlling the flow of harmful molecules reaching the developing fetus and consists of several types of cells, i.e. trophoblast, fibroblasts, mesenchymal and endothelial cells, as well as connective tissue structures2. Trophoblasts in particular provide a structural and biochemical barrier between the maternal and fetal compartments during pregnancy. Although the placenta acts as a barrier, a fraction of potentially harmful molecules can pass and directly reach the fetus3–6.

Apart from direct fetal toxicity of molecules that cross the placental, evidence for a placental-induced bystander effect has emerged in recent years. A number of studies have shown that the placenta itself may mediate toxicity to fetal cells upon exposure to different types of toxic agents, ranging from metals, benzene derivatives, to pesticides and radiation7–10. The exact mechanism underlying such a bystander effect are largely unknown. In vitro, ex vivo and in vivo models using placental barriers pointed to the release of signalling molecules like mitochondrial free radicals, TNF-α and cytokines secreted by the barrier cells upon exposure to metals or nanoparticles which subsequently caused DNA damage to indirectly exposed embryonic stem cells7,9.

Encompassed within the category of genotoxic molecules are chemotherapeutic compounds, which are currently part of the standard treatment schedules for pregnant cancer patients. Administration of chemotherapy in the first trimester is contraindicated due to the risk of congenital malformations11. Yet, treatment in the second and third trimester of the pregnancy with a variety range of anticancer compounds, such as anthracyclines (like epirubicin) and alkylating agents (like cyclophosphamide), has been considered safe based on the neurocognitive, cardiologic, behavioural and general health assessment in children that were prenatally exposed to chemotherapy12,13. Nonetheless, administration of chemotherapy containing cocktails of anthracyclines, alkylating agents and/or taxanes was associated with fetal growth restriction in about 21% of the cases14 and small for gestational age in the particular case of platinum-based treatments15. Unfortunately, literature on the long-term health outcomes of these children is scant and hence it is unknown whether these children are at increased risk for cardiovascular diseases, cancer or compromised fertility at adult age. In fact, the potential toxic effects of prenatal exposure to chemotherapy on the fetal genome are only now beginning to be investigated16.

Chemotherapeutics are known to cross the placental barrier, penetrating the fetal compartment at rates depending on the class of compounds. For instance, transplacental passage of two of the most applied chemotherapeutics during pregnancy, i.e. cyclophosphamide (alkylating agent) and epirubicin (anthracycline), is about 25% and less than 5%, respectively17,18. At the same time, chemotherapeutic compounds have been shown to affect placental biology, perturbing trophoblast differentiation and placental angiogenesis19–21. The evidence on how chemotherapy affects placenta is very limited. Human placentas exposed to chemotherapy presented a disrupted vascular perfusion and inflammatory lesions, suspected for contributing to a suboptimal exchange of nutrients, oxygen and waste products. In particular, placentas exposed to chemotherapy regimens containing cyclophosphamide and epirubicin exhibited elevated levels of apoptosis and increased oxidative damage22. The extent to which this placental toxicity contributes to potential adverse fetal outcomes is largely underexplored.

Given the known toxic effect of chemotherapy on placental tissue, we hypothesized that the exposure of placenta cells to chemotherapeutic compounds could elicit a bystander toxic effect to fetal cells. To date, this has not been the subject of prior scientific investigation. To this end, we developed an in vitro placental trophoblast barrier model and compared the genotoxic effects of a direct exposure of human cord blood cells to chemotherapy, with that of an indirect exposure across the trophoblast barrier. In brief, BeWo cells were cultured on semi-permeable membranes (transwell inserts), producing a polarized monolayer that decently recapitulates the barrier function of the placenta, despite not showing the fused, multi-nucleated cells23,24. This model is extensively used for studying the transport and bystander effect of other agents in the placental barrier7,9,10. While this physical cellular barrier hampers the passage of chemotherapy compounds, it would be susceptible for the toxic effects of those compounds. Therefore, chemotherapeutic agents might cause the placental barrier to release factors that reach and potentially damage the fetal haematopoietic cells, represented in our study by the mononuclear blood cells isolated from cord blood. This model represents a simple and reproductible way to explore the hypothesised bystander effect originated in the placental barrier.

Material and methods

Primary cells and cell cultures

The human choriocarcinoma-derived trophoblast placental cell line, BeWo cells (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures Catalog Number 86082803), passage 3 to 7, was cultured in Ham’s F-12 K medium, supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin under a humidified 5% CO2/95% air atmosphere at 37 °C. Cord blood samples from healthy pregnant woman were collected at delivery, at University Hospitals Leuven and cord blood mononuclear cells were isolated via density gradient centrifugation over Lymphoprep (StemCell Technology) and aliquots were directly cryopreserved at − 80 °C in 90% Human AB Serum and 10% DMSO. Ethical approval for the collection, storage and use of cord blood mononuclear cells was obtained from the Ethics Committee Research of University hospital (Universitaire Ziekenhuizen, UZ) and the Catholic university (Katholieke Universiteit, KU) of Leuven (study number: S/62388). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The primary blood mononuclear cells used in the experiments were isolated cord blood sampled from three different healthy pregnancies. These three different samples were used in each different experiment where, for each treatment condition, 3 technical replicates for each sample were performed. The Supplementary Table 1 compiles the main information for the three samples used in the study.

Chemotherapy compounds and chemicals

Cyclophosphamide (Endoxan) and epirubicine (Farmorubicine), dissolved in water at a concentration of 20 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL respectively, were kindly provided by the UZ hospital pharmacy. Solutions were stored for a maximum of 4 weeks at 4 °C.

Deuterated cyclophosphamide was from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. (Germany); cyclophosphamide monohydrate and epirubicin hydrochloride were from Merck (Belgium). Hydrochloric acid (HCl), used for adjusting the pH,was from VWR (Belgium), newborn calf-serum (heat inactived) was used as economic alternative for human plasma and (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Belgium).

Set up of the in vitro placenta BeWo barrier model for assessing chemotherapy exposure

The in vitro placenta BeWo barrier model was set up as previously described7,25. Briefly, BeWo cells were seeded on a semipermeable membrane (transwell) in a multiwell plate (12 wells, Corning Costar) at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2 and cultured for 7 days to create bilayer barriers. To monitor the resistance of the barrier due to the tightness of junctions between cells, BeWo barriers were daily subjected to transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements with the Millicell® ERS-2 Electrical Resistance System (Merck). In parallel, BeWo cell growth and confluency was continuously assessed an Incucyte® Live-Cell Analysis System (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

Upon confluency, the apical ‘maternal’ side (AP) of the trophoblast bilayer was exposed to the clinical reference dose of cyclophosphamide (CP) or epirubicin (EPI)26. For direct and indirect exposure experiments, the chemotherapy formulations were dissolved in the cell culture medium to final concentrations of 128 µM and 38.4 µM of Endoxan and 16.6 µM and 231 nM of Farmorubicine (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

To evaluate the confluency of the BeWo barrier after exposure to these drugs, transwell inserts with BeWo bilayers were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co.) in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. The membrane with the fixed cells was then cut with a scalpel and washed with PBS before being incubated with 1 µg/mL DAPI solution (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co.) to stain the nuclei for 10 min in the dark. The cells were washed again with PBS and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The medium collected on the other side of the membrane, i.e. the basolateral ‘fetal’ side (BL), was referred to as ‘conditioned media’.

Quantification of cyclophosphamide and epirubicin

After exposure of the AP side of the BeWo barrier to the clinical reference dose of CP or EPI26 registered in patients after the administration of the formulations, the fraction of these drugs crossing the barrier and reaching the basolateral ‘fetal’ (BL) chamber, pre-filled with serum, was measured. As a reference, for each chemotherapy compound a sample was prepared which was spiked with the concentration applied to the AP chamber. For each condition there were 3 replicates and each experiment was repeated 3 times independently. The samples were analyzed on Acquity ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) H-class PLUS system (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) with an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm column (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA), coupled to a Xevo TQ-XS Mass Spectrometer (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) with an electrospray ionisation source. Calibrators and experimental samples were prepared based on a modified protocol of Di Francesco et al.27. The calibration curve was prepared with cyclophosphamide monohydrate and epirubicin hydrochloride and used later to calculate the concentrations in the experimental samples.

Direct versus indirect exposure of cord blood mononuclear cells to chemotherapeutic drugs

Cord blood mononuclear cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) containing either ‘conditioned media’ (i.e. the medium from the BL side of the BeWo barrier, collected after exposing it to CP or EPI) or cell culture medium spiked with CP and EPI or vehicle (culture medium). Cells were incubated for 24 h, harvested and used immediately in assays to measure DNA damage, proliferation or apoptosis markers. Each condition was tested in triplicate (3 wells/condition). Each experiment was repeated three times independently.

Analysis of Ki67, Caspase-3 and γ-H2AX expression by flow cytometry

Upon direct or indirect exposure of cord blood mononuclear cells, cells were washed with PBS and the centrifugated pellet was re-suspended in the supplied eBioscience™ Fixation Solution (Invitrogen) and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Cells were washed with the eBioscience™ Permeabilization Concentrate and Diluent following the manufacturer instructions (Invitrogen) and stained with 1) (1:20) mouse anti-H2A.X Phospho (Ser139) AF488 (AB_2248011); 2) (1:20) rabbit anti-Caspase-3 , AF 647, Clone: C92-605 (BD) and 3) (1:20) mouse anti-Ki-67 APC-eFluor 780, Clone: 20Raj1 (Invitrogen™) for 1 h at room temperature in the darkness. Cells were then washed again, pelleted and the cell pellet was resuspended in buffer consisting of 0.5% BSA in PBS and 1 µg/mL of 4',6'-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride, 98% (Thermo Scientific™). Samples were analyzed on a Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) CantoI II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Instrument performance QC was performed using Cytometer Setup & Tracking Beads Kit (BD Biosciences). Data were acquired and analyzed using BD FACSDiva™ software v 6.x (BD Biosciences).

Nuclear ROS measurement

To determine the ROS at nuclear level in the live cells after directly and indirectly exposing cord blood mononuclear cells, CellROX™ Green Reagent (Invitrogen™) was used. The cell-permeant CellROX dye exhibits bright green photostable fluorescence upon oxidation and subsequent binding to DNA. Once the cord blood mononuclear cells were exposed to either conditioned media or directly to the chemotherapeutic compounds, CellROX Green Reagent was added at the final concentration of 2 μM to each well of the 96-well plate and incubated for 1 h. Then, after 3 consecutive washes with PBS, then the cord blood mononuclear cells were resuspended in cell medium and incubated in an IncuCyte device for Live Cell image recording. Average green object mean intensity data for each well were analyzed with the IncuCyte ZOOM version 2016B (Essen BioScience).

Data and statistical analyses

After assessing whether our data set was normally distributed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, Student’s t tests were used where only two experimental groups were compared. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Tukey HSD Test was used where three or more experimental groups were compared. When a p value of > 0.05 was found post hoc tests were performed. Data are described as mean ± SD. The mean and SD were calculated with the values of the three technical replicates for each independent experiment.

Results

Establishment of a placental trophoblast barrier model for evaluating indirect exposure to chemotherapeutic compounds

To explore whether human cord blood cells experience a bystander effect, i.e. increased levels of genotoxicity when they are in proximity to placental cells that are exposed to chemotherapeutic compounds, we resorted to a widely used in vitro placental barrier model. Previously, this model, in which human trophoblast choriocarcinoma-derived BeWo cells are grown as a bilayer on a semipermeable membrane, was applied to evaluate the indirect effects of compounds like iron, silver nanoparticles, benzene derivates and pesticides on cells that were shielded from direct exposure by the barrier8,24,25,28. Given that this model was never used to explore any potential bystander effect upon indirect exposure to chemotherapy, we first determined the optimal chemotherapy exposure conditions to guarantee an intact trophoblast cell bilayer. We focused on cyclophosphamide (CP) and epirubicin (EPI) which are the main constituents of the first line chemotherapy regimens used to treat the most frequently diagnosed cancer during pregnancy, namely breast cancer14,29,30.

First, BeWo cells, mimicking the trophoblast layer, were grown on the apical side of a microporous membrane, following previously published protocols7,25. As demonstrated previously, a throphoblast bilayer barrier is required for a bystander signalling cascade9. The IncuCyte live cell analysis system enabled the real-time morphological and phenotypic tracking of the BeWo monolayer (established at day 4) as it progressed to a bilayer barrier at day 7 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). TEER measurements increased steadily in an approximately linear fashion over these 7 days (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Second, at day 7, the BeWo bilayer barrier was exposed to either CP or EPI. We applied chemotherapy concentrations that were equivalent to reported maximum peaks in the blood of treated cancer patients (i.e. 128 µM for CP and 16.6 µM for EPI), further referred to as ‘clinical reference doses’. These concentrations were detected in blood from 12 to 33 h after administration, therefore, an exposure time of 24 h was set up as initial parameter26. To inspect the integrity of the barriers upon chemotherapy exposure, BeWo barriers were evaluated by TEER measurements and DAPI immunofluorescence imaging. Exposure to the clinical reference dose of CP did not affect the BeWo barrier integrity compared to the vehicle-treated condition (Supplementary Fig. 2C and D). In contrast, upon exposure to the clinical reference dose of EPI during 24 h, confluency of the BeWo barrier was decreased up to 80% (Supplementary Fig. 2C, panel 1). When evaluating various exposure times and EPI-concentration (Supplementary Fig. 2, panel 1–7), a 3 h-exposure to the clinical reference dose of EPI (16.6 µM) was found to preserve the integrity of the BeWo bilayer barrier comparable to the vehicle-treated condition (Supplementary Fig. 2D panel 7). According to these results, downstream analyses were performed using the optimized exposure conditions, i.e. 24 h and 3 h exposure times to clinical refence doses of CP (128 µM) and EPI (16.6 µM), respectively.

Third, given their lipophilic nature, relatively low molecular weight and different protein binding, we pursued to evaluate whether and to which extent CP and EPI applied in the apical side were passing through the BeWo barrier. When the upper surface of the barrier was exposed to CP and EPI under the carefully optimized exposure conditions, the concentrations of the compounds measured in the serum underneath the barrier, referred to as the “conditioned media,” were found to be 30% and 1.4% of the concentrations initially applied (Fig. 1). As these values are in line with reported transplacental passage rates of CP and EPI, measured in in vivo and ex vivo models18,31–33, the customized BeWo barrier model was considered adequate to start evaluating a potential bystander effect on cord blood cells upon indirect exposure to these chemotherapeutic drugs.

Determination of the fractions of cyclophosphamide (CP, panel A) and epirubicin (EPI, panel B) crossing the bilayer BeWo barrier. After 24 h and 3 h-exposure of bilayer BeWo barriers to clinical reference doses of CP (128 µM) and EPI (16.6 µM) respectively, serum from the basolateral chambers was collected and analysed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC). As a reference, a serum sample was spiked with the concentration applied to apical chamber. Three independent experiments (EXP) were performed with 3 replicates per condition per experiment. The bars (x-axis values) indicate the measured concentrations of each drug in the apical chamber (EXP1-3) and in the spiked sample (spike). The orange line (with value 1 on the y-axes) represents the fraction of each drug crossing the BeWo barrier.

Cord blood mononuclear cells indirectly exposed to CP and EPI show increased levels of γ-H2AX and nuclear ROS

Considering that CP and EPI are genotoxic agents, with CP generating DNA alkyl cross-links and EPI interfering with DNA replication through intercalation34, our subsequent objective was to determine whether the BeWo bilayer, when exposed to these chemotherapeutic compounds, would lead to an additional DNA-damaging effect (referred to as bystander effect) on cord blood cells compared to any direct genotoxic impact. Therefore, human cord blood mononuclear cells were exposed to either the conditioned media (obtained after exposing the BeWo barrier to the clinical reference doses of either CP or EPI) or to culture medium spiked with the fractions of these drugs that were shown to cross the barrier (i.e. 30% and 1% for CP and EPI, respectively; referred to as ‘direct exposure’). After a 24 h-exposure time, we characterized the levels of DNA damage in the cord blood mononuclear cells by measuring the γ-H2AX and nuclear ROS levels (Fig. 2).

Characterization of a chemotherapy-induced bystander effect by an exposed BeWo bilayer to human cord blood cells. Cord blood mononuclear cells were exposed to either ‘conditioned media’ obtained from BeWo barriers that were treated either with cyclophosphamide (CP, 128 µM) or epirubicin (EPI, 16.6 µM), referred to as ‘indirect exposure’, or to culture medium spiked with the concentrations of CP (38.4 µM, i.e. 30% of 128 µM) or EPI (231 nM, i.e. 1% of 16.6 µM) that were shown to cross the BeWo barrier, named as ‘direct exposure’. Vehicle-treated condition consisted of culture medium supplemented with water at the equivalent volume of the chemotherapeutic compounds. After a 24 h-exposure, DNA damage was measured in the exposed cord blood mononuclear cells. All the values were normalized to the vehicle exposure (reference orange line) and expressed as a fold change. (A) The fraction of cells with double strand breaks (γ-H2AX) was determined using FACS, showing that the γ-H2AX-positive fraction increased in all conditions compared to the vehicle-treated reference (reference orange line). The difference was only statistically significant for the ‘indirect exposure’ conditions. Furthermore, for EPI, the increase associated with the ‘indirect exposure’ was statistically significantly higher than seen with the ‘direct exposure’. (B) Measuring of nuclear ROS levels, expressed as the mean green intensity that represents the signal emitted by the fluorogenic probe upon oxidation and consequent binding to DNA. Direct exposure to both CP and EPI did not change the levels of ROS in the nuclei of cord blood mononuclear cells compared to the vehicle. In contrast, indirect exposure to CP or EPI increased the levels more than 50% (> 1.5-fold) compared to the vehicle. Plots represent the mean ± SD of 3 independent experiments with 3-technical replicates per experiment. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 as determined by the unpaired t-test, both relative to vehicle and direct exposure. Orange asterisks refer to significant differences when comparing to the vehicle condition. Black asterisks indicate significant differences between direct and indirect exposure conditions.

First, using FACS, we evaluated the number of cord blood mononuclear cells showing a positive signal for γ-H2AX, as a marker of DNA double strand breaks. Compared to the vehicle-treated condition, only exposure to the conditioned media obtained from BeWo barriers exposed to CP or EPI significantly increased the fraction of γ-H2AX positive cord blood cells (p-value < 0.01, Fig. 2A). Although cord blood cells directly exposed to either CP or EPI were also more frequently γ-H2AX positive than the vehicle reference, this difference was not statistically significant. Only for EPI, exposure to the conditioned media caused a significantly higher level of γ-H2AX-positive cord blood mononuclear cells than in directly exposed cord blood cells (p-value 0.019).

Complementarily, we measured the level of nuclear ROS in cord blood mononuclear cells exposed to the different conditions. Exposure to the conditioned media, obtained from both CP and EPI-exposed BeWo barriers, resulted in a statistically significant increase in nuclear oxidative damage levels compared to the vehicle reference. Intriguingly, exposure to the conditioned media resulted in 1.5 to 2-times higher nuclear ROS levels in cord blood mononuclear cells than direct exposure of these cells to CP and EPI, respectively (p-value < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

These data suggest that one or more factors are present in the conditioned media of the BeWo barriers treated with CP or EPI, which are causing an additive ‘bystander’ effect thereby significantly escalating oxidative DNA damage (for both CP and EPI) and double strand breaks (for EPI) in exposed cord blood cells.

Indirect exposure to CP and EPI through the BeWo barrier does not alter proliferation or apoptosis in cord blood mononuclear cells

In parallel to evaluating a DNA damage bystander effect, we assessed whether exposure to the conditioned media also affected cell proliferation or apoptosis in subsequently exposed cord blood cells. As shown in Fig. 3 neither proliferation, measured as the fraction of Ki67-positive cells (Fig. 3A), nor apoptosis, measured as the fraction of caspase-3-positive cells (Fig. 3B), was significantly altered in any of the tested conditions. In general, caspase-3 levels were increased relatively to the vehicle reference, with comparable effects seen in the directly and indirectly exposed conditions of each chemotherapeutic-compound which may suggest that the mild cytotoxic effect observed in the cord blood mononuclear cells could be mainly caused by the direct effect of the chemotherapeutic compounds. However, high intra-group variabilities diminished the probability of identifying significant differences.

(B) The caspase-3 positive fraction was increased in all the conditions, although the difference was not statistically significant. Together, the presented data demonstrate that exposing cord blood mononuclear cells to the conditioned media from the basolateral ‘fetal’ side of the BeWo barrier, which contains only fractions of the initial concentrations of CP and EPI, results in significantly elevated levels of DNA damage compared to direct exposure of cord blood cells to chemotherapy levels of CP and EPI. This increase in DNA damage is observed without a concomitant significant increase in cell death. This suggests the presence of DNA damaging factors which are released by the placental barrier in vitro in response to CP and EPI exposure, causing additive DNA damage to cord blood mononuclear cells.

Discussion

Studying to what extent prenatal exposure to chemotherapeutic compounds affects placenta and, subsequently, fetal cells, is essential to characterize the safety of these drugs used for treating cancer patients in pregnancy. Though cyclophosphamide and epirubicin are part of standard cancer treatment regimens for pregnant cancer patients14,29,30, placenta toxicity following prenatal administration to these drugs is largely unexplored. The sparse studies investigating this topic showed that placentas exposed to cyclophosphamide and epirubicine presented higher levels of apoptosis and inflammatory lesions20,35,36. Likewise, it is also largely unknown whether and how such placental toxicity would adversely affect the fetus. To address this latter question, we set up a simplified in vitro placental barrier model using clinical references doses of cyclophosphamide and epirubicin. Given the known genotoxicity profile of these chemotherapeutic drugs we investigated whether cord blood cells experienced altered DNA damage levels when exposed to these drugs indirectly following their passage through the bilayer BeWo trophoblast barrier, compared to when exposed to the drugs directly.

Whereas direct exposure to either cyclophosphamide or epirubicin induced DNA double strand breaks in cord blood mononuclear cells, exposure to conditioned media obtained from BeWo bilayers treated with these drugs resulted in a significant increase of both DNA double strand breaks and the presence of ROS within the cellular nucleus. In contrast, exposure to the conditioned media of the treated BeWo barriers did not alter apoptosis or proliferation in cord blood mononuclear cells when compared to direct exposure to the drugs. Similarly, previous exposure of a BeWO cell barrier to cobalt and chromium nanoparticles resulted in increased γ-H2AX levels in progenitor neurons and astrocytes without eliciting changes in cell death or proliferation rate37.

These findings strongly indicate the presence of an additive ‘bystander’ effect originating from the exposed trophoblast bilayer, leading to a significant increase of DNA damage in the fetal compartment. So far, assessment of fetal genotoxicity after in utero exposure to chemotherapy has exclusively been done in rodent models, pointing to increased levels of chromosomal aberrations and DNA strand breaks in fetal and newborn tissues38–40. However, any contribution of a potential bystander effect coming from affected placental tissue cannot be deduced from these studies.

Given that chemotherapy-exposed placentas were previously shown to present with increases in oxidative DNA damage in trophoblast cells20, it could be speculated that the elevated DNA damage seen in cord blood mononuclear cells after exposure to the conditioned media may originate from chemotherapy-induced oxidative damage to the BeWo barrier cells. Previous in vitro models using trophoblast barriers to assess the effect of indirect exposure to toxins, including nanoparticles and heavy metals, have found that the bystander effect was mediated by signalling molecules like TNF-α and other cytokines secreted by the barrier cells7,9. On the other hand, both CP and EPI can be metabolised by cytochrome 450 (CYP) enzymes, giving rise to metabolites. BeWo cells express one of the CYP isoforms, CYP2C18, implicated on the biotransformation of CP into phosphoramide mustard and acrolein41. Interestingly, higher amount of ROS are generated with acrolein exposure as compared to CP42 meaning that the potential presence of acrolein in the conditioned medium could be responsible for part of the observed oxidative damage43. A logical next step would be exploring which factors underly the exacerbated DNA damaging effect seen in cord blood mononuclear cells that were indirectly exposed to cyclophosphamide and epirubicin in our placental barrier model, with special emphasis on assessing the concentration of acrolein in the conditioned media.

In the here presented in vitro placental barrier model, we applied single-agent treatments at clinical reference doses. This strategy allowed us to properly optimize the exposure conditions for each of the compounds. Likewise, to maximize the reproducibility of our model, we avoided inducing syncytialization with forskolin. Whereas syncytialization of BeWo cells allows to obtain the multinucleated syncytia barrier, forskolin stops proliferation and this would affect the toxic effect of the chemotherapeutic compounds in the barrier19. Moreover, the syncytialization of BeWo cells could imply changes in the permeability of the monolayer due to the widening of the intercellular spaces, increasing the transferring of the compounds44. While syncytialization of the BeWo cells is essential when investigating the maturation, differentiation or hormonal function of trophoblasts, we consider it may not be determinant to study the cytotoxic and cytostatic effect of chemotherapeutic compounds19. Taken together, this choriocarcinoma cell line, BeWo cells, is useful but not perfect for modelling the placental tissue; On the one hand, the induction of differentiation of trophoblasts to synctiotrophoblast may compromised the toxicity exerted by the chemotherapeutic compounds due to the proliferation decline. On the other hand, the fact that BeWo cells are a tumor cell line means that its proliferation rate may be higher comparing other placenta cell types, therefore cytotoxicity may be more likely to occur.

Given that patients usually receive a cocktail of different chemotherapeutic drugs, future assessments could characterize the synergistic effects of combinatory cyclophosphamide and epirubicin exposures. Furthermore, the analyses could be broadened to evaluate whether similar bystander effects can also be observed with other chemotherapeutic compounds given during pregnancy and with a different mode of action. Future studies can rely on more complex co-culture, microfluidic, and organoid models to continue advancing on the evaluation of compound transfer and toxicity on the placenta45,46. Only recently, human trophoblast organoids haven been developed as innovative tool to study trophoblast function and barrier integrity47.

Although we tried to simplify the conditions of the model favouring reproducibility, the variability of the assessed toxicity markers among the different samples was high. Probably the fact that our clinical samples are representing blood from different individuals and composed by different proportions of blood cell types, may determine differences in the response to the DNA damage48,49. Future analyses could consider stratifying by specific blood cell subsets or alternatively, using a cell line for a first more accurate assessment of cellular responses to toxic effects. In an attempt to reduce the sex-specific variability, we used only male fetuses cord blood in our experiments but, future studies should include clinical samples from both sexes to better represent the intrinsic variability associated with variable responses to environmental damage.

Taken together, the results shown here indicate that the fetal compartment can be susceptible to genotoxic bystander effects originating from placental cells exposed to chemotherapeutic compounds. These data may open new avenues to further investigate the toxicity profile of chemotherapeutic drugs administered during pregnancy.
