Response to letter re: “Artificial intelligence-powered real-time multimodal model for predicting recurrence and survival in head and neck cancer: a multicenter, multinational study”
Authors: HA Jung, R Merkin, AL Feng, D Lee, K Lee, S Park, J-M Sun, S-H Lee, JS Ahn, M-J Ahn, LJ Wirth, JC Park
Published: 2026

We thank Li et al.1 for their interest in our study and for their thoughtful comments regarding the development of an artificial intelligence (AI)-powered multimodal model for predicting recurrence and survival in head and neck squamous-cell carcinoma.2

Their observations provide an opportunity to clarify several methodological considerations and outline future directions for the Recurrence And Death AI-based Risk (RADAR) framework.

External validation

We agree that independent external validation is essential for establishing clinical generalizability. Although our model was developed using datasets from two geographically and ethnically distinct institutions to enhance robustness, this does not fully replace validation in independent cohorts. As acknowledged in our discussion, prospective studies across diverse health care settings are needed to determine the real-world clinical impact of the model.

Efforts are currently under way to expand multi-institutional collaborations for this purpose.

Clinical heterogeneity

We appreciate the emphasis on clinical heterogeneity across stages and tumor subsites. Our cohort included patients with American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage I-IVB disease undergoing curative-intent surgery, reflecting contemporary practice at tertiary centers where selected locally advanced cases may remain surgical candidates within a multidisciplinary setting.

While we conducted subgroup analyses by human papillomavirus status, further stratification by stage and subsite may improve risk discrimination and will be explored in future work alongside integration of additional modalities such as radiomic and molecular data.

Missing data and model deployment

The XGBoost algorithm was intentionally selected for its ability to accommodate missing values commonly encountered in longitudinal clinical datasets. Importantly, the laboratory variables and pathological features used in this study are routinely collected as part of standard clinical practice, allowing the model to be applied without requiring additional tests or specialized data acquisition. This pragmatic feature reduces the risk of systematic missingness and represents a key advantage for real-world implementation.

Nonetheless, we agree that data completeness remains important for reliable prediction at the individual patient level. Future deployment strategies will incorporate data-quality checks and safeguards, including automated alerts for critical missing variables, to support safe and appropriate clinical use.

Laboratory data and reproducibility

Laboratory variables were extracted from electronic medical records at multiple post-operative surveillance time points as part of routine care.

We acknowledge that additional detail regarding sampling patterns would further support reproducibility and will aim to provide expanded methodological transparency in subsequent reports.

Future perspectives

We share the authors’ view that continued refinement—including external validation, deeper subgroup modeling, and deployment-ready safeguards—will be necessary for successful clinical translation. Our goal remains to enable dynamic, individualized risk assessment that can inform surveillance strategies while reducing unnecessary interventions.

We thank Li et al. again for their constructive comments and for contributing to the ongoing advancement of AI-driven prognostic tools in oncology.
