Demoralization, employment status, and early symptom reduction as correlates of treatment discontinuation in outpatient alcohol use disorder
Authors: Chung-Han Lee, Ting-Ting Yen, I-Chun Chen, Chiann-Yi Hsu, Ting-Gang Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment discontinuation is common in outpatient alcohol use disorder (AUD) care, and attendance-related missingness can complicate interpretation of early symptom change in naturalistic cohorts.MethodsWe followed 72 adults newly entering outpatient care for moderate-to-severe AUD at a tertiary hospital in Taiwan (baseline and 1, 3, and 6 months). Discontinuation was defined as ≥30 consecutive days without documented clinic contact. Symptom trajectories were modeled using generalized estimating equations with and without baseline severity adjustment; baseline correlates of 1-month discontinuation were estimated using Firth bias-reduced logistic regression, and early symptom change was evaluated in a landmark analysis restricted to participants retained to the 1-month visit.ResultsThe demoralization-by-time interaction for Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) reduction was significant in unadjusted models but attenuated after adjusting for baseline AUDIT (P = .385). Regular employment was independently associated with higher odds of 1-month discontinuation [adjusted Firth odds ratio (OR) 7.67, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.19–49.24]. In the landmark cohort (n = 50), early symptom improvement at 1 month was not significantly associated with subsequent discontinuation by 3 months (Firth OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.16–2.04).ConclusionsAfter accounting for baseline severity, demoralization was not a robust determinant of symptom trajectories. Regular employment showed a consistent association with early discontinuation, suggesting potentially modifiable time- and access-related barriers; early symptom change should be interpreted as an attendance-conditioned correlate rather than a standalone prognostic marker.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, treatment discontinuation, demoralization, early symptom reduction, employment barriers, outpatient addiction services

Introduction

Essential background and unresolved problems

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) remains highly prevalent, affecting ~10%–11% of adults, yet treatment retention continues to be low. Only a small proportion of individuals meeting treatment need receive specialty addiction care, and early treatment discontinuation remains common (Kranzler 2023). Structural and psychological barriers—including stigma, moral-failure attributions, underdiagnosis, and ambivalence toward reducing alcohol use—continue to limit initiation and sustained attendance in outpatient treatment (Kranzler 2023, Haber 2025). Because treatment retention is strongly associated with improved alcohol treatment outcomes, identifying early predictors of discontinuation is an urgent clinical priority.

Disorder-specific basis and brief literature review

Demoralization has gained attention as a clinically significant construct in early addiction treatment. Characterized by hopelessness, helplessness, and diminished self-efficacy, elevated demoralization has been linked to early treatment discontinuation from care (Rabinowitz et al. 2023). Chronic alcohol use has been associated with alterations in motivational and regulatory systems, which may heighten stress sensitivity and contribute to affective difficulties (Wood et al. 2024). Interpersonal factors, such as stigma and metadehumanization, may additionally threaten psychological needs and reinforce demoralization among individuals with high-risk drinking patterns (Fontesse et al. 2024).

Executive functioning difficulties are frequently reported in AUD, but their relevance to treatment participation remains uncertain. Deficits in cognitive flexibility, planning, or inhibitory control may pose practical challenges for some patients, particularly in settings with limited scheduling flexibility (Brion et al. 2017, Russell et al. 2025).

Employment is often considered a component of recovery capital; however, emerging evidence indicates that regular, full-time employment may also be associated with scheduling constraints that reduce opportunities for treatment attendance in routine outpatient settings. Findings on employment at treatment entry are mixed, with some studies suggesting that baseline employment is not consistently protective, whereas improvements in employment stability over time—such as increases in months worked—more reliably relate to reductions in substance use (Sahker et al. 2019, Bormann et al. 2025).

Early symptom reduction has commonly been associated with lower treatment discontinuation across addiction treatment modalities (Charney et al. 2010). However, because early change is often only observable among those who remain engaged long enough to be assessed, such associations may be influenced by attendance-related selection in naturalistic cohorts. Early decreases in alcohol use or psychological distress [e.g. AUDIT, Brief Symptom Rating Scale (BSRS-5)] may therefore be best viewed as a tentative marker of early engagement rather than a guaranteed indicator of longer-term retention.

Research gap

Existing research tends to examine demoralization, executive functioning, employment constraints, or early symptom change independently. As a result, little is known about how these psychological, cognitive, and structural factors jointly relate to early treatment discontinuation in outpatient AUD care (Morgenstern and Bates 1999, Litt et al. 2018, Kline et al. 2021, Chang, Yen et al. 2022). No prior studies have examined these domains simultaneously within the same analytic approach. This gap may constrain efforts to identify more targeted, evidence-informed approaches to address short-term treatment discontinuation.

Study aims and applicable methods overview

To address these limitations, this prospective cohort study examined demoralization, executive functioning, employment status, and early symptom change in relation to early and longitudinal treatment discontinuation among adults newly entering outpatient care for moderate-to-severe AUD. To test these hypotheses, we assessed demoralization, executive functioning, early symptom change, and employment status; evaluated 1-month discontinuation using Firth bias-reduced logistic regression (including a prespecified parsimonious adjusted model for employment); and modeled longitudinal outcomes using generalized estimating equations (GEE) in a naturalistic outpatient setting.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, naturalistic cohort study was conducted at the outpatient alcohol treatment program of Taichung Veterans General Hospital, Taiwan, between August 2022 and July 2024 (IRB CE22036B-1). Adults newly seeking care for alcohol-related problems were assessed at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months. As no randomization or assigned intervention occurred, clinical trial registration was not required.

Participants

Participants were aged 20 years or older and met Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision criteria for moderate-to-severe AUD. Board-certified psychiatrists conducted diagnostic interviews. Exclusion criteria were: current psychotic disorders, bipolar I disorder, severe neurocognitive disorders, active suicidal intent, or unstable medical illness. Of 77 individuals screened, 5 did not complete baseline assessments (primarily due to insufficient time or questionnaire refusal); the final cohort included 72 participants. Sampling was consecutive to enhance representativeness.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables

Sociodemographic and clinical variables

Baseline sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, and employment status, were recorded via self-report. Employment status was categorized into regular employment, irregular employment, or unemployment. To ensure statistical stability in adjusted models and visualizations, employment was operationalized a priori as a binary indicator: regular employment versus non-regular/unemployed. Clinical history and baseline medical conditions were verified through clinical interviews and electronic medical records.

Clinical and neuropsychological measures

Clinical and neuropsychological measures

All assessments were administered by psychiatrists or trained clinical psychologists blinded to the study hypotheses. Standardized training was conducted to ensure inter-rater reliability.

Demoralization Scale – Mandarin Version Demoralization was assessed using the demoralization scale—Mandarin Version (DS-MV), a validated 24-item measure of helplessness, hopelessness, and reduced coping capacity. Total scores range from 0 to 96, with higher scores indicating greater demoralization. Elevated demoralization was defined as a total score >30, consistent with a prior validation study(Kissane et al. 2004), and with its use in Taiwanese studies involving cancer and heroin-use populations (Chang, Huang et al. 2022, Chang, Hung et al. 2022, Chang, Yen et al. 2022).

Biological markers

Biological markers

Fasting blood samples were collected to measure hepatic enzyme activity [glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase (GOT), glutamic pyruvic transaminase, gamma-glutamyl transferase], cholesterol levels, and triglycerides, providing physiological indices of alcohol-related organ stress.

Procedures and treatment

Baseline clinician interviews, standardized questionnaires, and neuropsychological assessments were administered by trained staff, with follow-ups conducted at 1, 3, and 6 months. Participants received standard Treatment as Usual (TAU) at a tertiary referral hospital, reflecting real-world AUD management in Taiwan. This regimen included physician-led consultations (every 1–4 weeks), pharmacotherapy (e.g. naltrexone or acamprosate), and case management. While individual psychotherapy was available via referral, no structured group therapy was provided.

Outcome definitions

Primary outcome: treatment discontinuation

Primary outcome: treatment discontinuation

Treatment discontinuation was defined as 30 or more consecutive days without an attended face-to-face outpatient consultation with a psychiatrist. This 30-day window, which excludes telehealth and case management contacts, effectively captures missed scheduled appointments given the standard 1–4 week follow-up interval. This definition aligns with established substance-use research (Samples et al. 2018) for operationalizing early dropout.

Early symptom reduction

Early symptom reduction

Early symptom reduction was defined as any decrease in AUDIT or BSRS-5 scores at the 1-month assessment compared to baseline. Because this measure is only observable among individuals who attended the 1-month visit, it was treated as an attendance-conditioned correlate rather than an independent prognostic factor for initial 1-month discontinuation.

Landmark approach and improvement

Landmark approach and improvement

To ensure proper temporal ordering, the association between 1-month symptom change and subsequent discontinuation was evaluated using a landmark approach. This analysis was restricted to participants retained through the 1-month visit (Y1 = 0). Additionally, “improvement before discontinuation” was strictly recorded only when score reductions were documented at a scheduled assessment occurring prior to the patient meeting the discontinuation criteria.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and group comparisons between high and low demoralization groups (DS-MV >30 vs. ≤30) were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (version 24) and Python (version 3.11.2), with a two-tailed alpha of 0.05.

Longitudinal symptom trajectories GEE with an exchangeable working correlation structure were employed to model changes in AUDIT and BSRS-5 scores across 1, 3, and 6 months. We employed a tiered modeling approach to assess the demoralization–time interaction: (i) an unadjusted model using the DS-MV cutoff, (ii) a baseline-adjusted model treating DS-MV as a continuous predictor and controlling for baseline AUDIT, and (iii) a sensitivity analysis further adjusting for BSRS-5. These adjustments were implemented to isolate the association from potential baseline severity effects and regression-to-the-mean.

Treatment discontinuation analyses

Treatment discontinuation analyses

To address potential sparse-data bias and quasi-complete separation in logistic models, Firth bias-reduced logistic regression (Jeffreys prior penalization) was implemented to identify predictors of treatment discontinuation. First, we estimated univariate and parsimonious adjusted models (controlling for age, sex, and baseline AUDIT) for 1-month discontinuation. Second, a landmark approach was implemented, restricted to participants retained at the 1-month visit (Y1 = 0), to evaluate how early symptom change (1-month change in AUDIT and BSRS-5) predicts subsequent 3-month discontinuation (Y2) while preserving temporal ordering.

Longitudinal discontinuation patterns across all time points were further summarized using binary GEE models. To ensure robustness, sensitivity analyses were conducted using varying improvement thresholds, continuous change scores (Δ AUDIT, Δ BSRS-5), and a delta-adjusted pattern-mixture model to account for informative missingness (i.e. disengagement from care). Detailed scripts and sensitivity results are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Missing data

Missing data

Analyses used an available-case approach for primary analyses. Because missingness largely reflected disengagement from care (i.e. informative missingness), we did not perform imputation for the primary models. To partially address attendance-related selection for early symptom change, we implemented a landmark analysis restricted to participants retained to 1 month (Y1 = 0). For longitudinal symptom trajectories, we additionally conducted a simple delta-adjusted pattern-mixture sensitivity analysis (last observation carried forward plus delta adjustment [LOCF+Δ]) to assess robustness to departures from missing at random (Supplementary Table S2f). We interpret symptom trajectories as conditional on observed follow-up and emphasize this limitation in the Discussion.

Baseline clinical and biological profiles

Baseline alcohol-related severity was higher in the High Demoralization group, reflected by elevated AUDIT scores (29.1 ± 6.0 vs. 21.2 ± 8.3; P < .001) and a greater proportion exceeding clinically severe AUDIT cutoffs (>29: 65.1% vs. 17.2%, P < .001; >31: 34.9% vs. 6.9%, P = .006; Table 2). BSRS-5 and TFAB scores did not differ significantly between groups (P > .05). Suicide-related ideation scores were higher in the High Demoralization group (BSS: 2.2 ± 5.4 vs. 0.0 ± 0.2; P = .035). Among biological markers, triglyceride levels were higher in the high demoralization group (216.9 ± 254.1 mg/dl vs. 112.0 ± 56.3 mg/dl; P = .016), while other liver enzyme and cholesterol values showed no group differences.

Longitudinal symptom trajectories

AUDIT scores showed time-related change across follow-ups. In an unadjusted group-based model, the demoralization × time interaction was statistically significant (Fig. 1A). However, when DS-MV was modeled as a continuous predictor and baseline AUDIT was fixed (baseline-adjusted GEE), the time × DS interaction was not significant (P = .385; Fig. 1B), suggesting that the apparent differential improvement may be partly explained by baseline severity and regression-to-the-mean effects. This inference was unchanged after further adjustment for baseline BSRS-5 in a sensitivity model (time × DS P = .312) (Supplementary Table S2g).

Early treatment discontinuation and predictors at 1-month follow-up

By 1 month, 22 of 72 participants (30.6%) met criteria for treatment discontinuation, increasing to 36 by 3 months and 53 by 6 months. Employment status was associated with early attrition: most early discontinuations occurred among participants with regular employment (Tables 3 and 4). In an adjusted Firth logistic regression controlling for age, sex, and baseline AUDIT, regular employment remained associated with higher odds of 1-month discontinuation [odds ratio (OR) = 7.67, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.19–49.24; Supplementary Table S2c]. Because 1-month symptom change is only observed among attendees, we did not interpret naïve associations between early improvement and 1-month discontinuation as prognostic. In the landmark cohort restricted to participants retained to the 1-month visit (Y1 = 0; n = 50), early symptom improvement at 1 month was not significantly associated with subsequent discontinuation by 3 months (Firth OR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.16–2.04, P = .394; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2b). Findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses using stricter improvement thresholds and continuous change scores (Supplementary Tables S2d and e).

Employment status and treatment discontinuation trajectories

Treatment discontinuation patterns differed by employment status (Fig. 3). Because the unemployed subgroup had no discontinuation at 1 month (sparse cell), non-regular employment and unemployment were combined as the reference category for longitudinal summaries and the binary GEE model. Participants with regular employment showed higher discontinuation rates at each follow-up point (1 month: 36%; 3 months: 57%; 6 months: 80%) compared with those with non-regular/unemployed status (1 month: 6%; 3 months: 33%; 6 months: 61%). Binary GEE analyses indicated that regular employment was associated with a greater likelihood of treatment discontinuation (OR = 2.98, 95% CI 1.21–7.34; P = .018). The likelihood of discontinuation increased across time relative to 1 month (3 months: OR = 2.38, 95% CI 1.59–3.56; 6 months: OR = 6.91, 95% CI 3.88–12.31; Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Expanded summary and clinical integration

In this prospective cohort of adults beginning outpatient treatment for AUD, three main observations emerged. First, patients with higher baseline demoralization presented with more severe alcohol-related problems. In unadjusted longitudinal models, larger numeric reductions in AUDIT were observed in the higher-demoralization group; however, this pattern attenuated after baseline adjustment when DS-MV was modeled continuously, consistent with regression-to-the-mean and baseline severity effects (Fig. 1). Second, regular employment was associated with a higher likelihood of treatment discontinuation across follow-up, including in a prespecified parsimonious adjusted Firth model (Supplementary Table S2c). Third, early symptom improvement at 1 month should be interpreted as an engagement-related correlate among attendees rather than a stand-alone prognostic factor; in the landmark cohort, early improvement was not significantly associated with subsequent discontinuation by 3 months (Fig. 2).

Regular employment was associated with a higher likelihood of treatment discontinuation across follow-up. Discontinuation also became more common at later time points, underscoring how structural constraints—such as limited scheduling flexibility or competing occupational demands—may contribute to disengagement in routine outpatient care (Zhang and Wu 2025).

Early symptom reduction at 1 month should be interpreted as an engagement-related marker among attendees rather than a stand-alone predictor of discontinuation. After restricting to participants retained to the 1-month visit (landmark cohort), early improvement was not significantly associated with subsequent discontinuation by 3 months.

Demoralization and early symptom change

Higher demoralization at intake was associated with greater alcohol-use severity and higher rates of suicidal ideation, consistent with prior work documenting the clinical relevance of demoralization across psychiatric and medical populations (Chang, Huang et al. 2022, Chang, Hung et al. 2022, Chang, Yen et al. 2022). In unadjusted analyses, participants with higher demoralization showed larger numeric reductions in AUDIT over follow-up; however, when DS-MV was treated as a continuous predictor and baseline AUDIT was adjusted, the time × DS interaction was not significant (P = .385). These findings suggest that baseline severity and regression-to-the-mean effects may explain a substantial portion of the apparent differential improvement, and demoralization should not be interpreted as an independent driver of symptom change in this dataset.

This pattern did not extend to treatment discontinuation outcomes. Although early discontinuation rates were numerically lower among individuals with higher demoralization, the difference was not statistically significant. Overall, these findings suggest that demoralization may be informative for understanding early symptom trajectories in AUD, whereas its relevance for short-term treatment discontinuation remains inconclusive and warrants further investigation.

Employment and structural determinants of treatment discontinuation

Although employment is often considered an aspect of recovery capital, participants with regular employment in this cohort showed higher treatment discontinuation rates than those with irregular employment or unemployment. This association remained in a prespecified parsimonious adjusted model (age, sex, baseline AUDIT) estimated using Firth penalization to reduce sparse-data bias (Supplementary Table S2c). We interpret employment as a marker of structural constraints (e.g. limited scheduling flexibility) rather than a direct mechanism, and residual confounding by unmeasured contextual factors remains likely.

These findings may reflect structural rather than patient-level factors, as individuals with inflexible work hours could face practical barriers to accessing care within conventional clinic schedules. Service adaptations—such as extended clinic hours, flexible appointment windows, or telehealth options—may help support continuity of care among employed patients.

Notably, some studies in substance-use treatment have reported higher dropout among unemployed or socioeconomically disadvantaged patients, likely reflecting reduced resources, greater psychosocial instability, and competing survival needs (Zhang and Wu 2025). Our opposite directionality may therefore be context-dependent and may reflect scheduling incompatibility with standard clinic hours among regularly employed patients, selection into care among those able to access services, and residual confounding by unmeasured structural factors (e.g. workplace flexibility, commuting time, paid leave, stigma, and social support). Accordingly, we frame regular employment as a marker of access barriers in this setting rather than a causal mechanism.

Early symptom change and treatment engagement

Early symptom change at the 1-month assessment co-occurred with continued engagement in care, but this association is intrinsically conditioned on attendance at that visit. Accordingly, we reframed early symptom reduction as a correlate among attendees and evaluated its association with later discontinuation using a landmark approach. In this landmark analysis, early improvement was not independently associated with subsequent discontinuation by 3 months, underscoring that short-term symptom change does not necessarily guarantee longer-term retention.

A nominal association between elevated baseline GOT and 1-month treatment discontinuation was observed, although this did not persist beyond univariate testing. This pattern may reflect underlying liver-related clinical burden rather than behavioral factors and should be interpreted cautiously (O'Shea et al. 2010).

Overall, these findings reinforce the potential value of structured early-phase monitoring. Identifying early non-response may help clinicians recognize individuals at higher risk of treatment discontinuation who could benefit from additional support or timely treatment adjustments.

Executive functioning

Executive functioning, as measured by the TFAB total score, was not associated with symptom change or treatment discontinuation in our analyses. However, this null finding should be interpreted cautiously because the TFAB is a brief screening instrument and the total score may have limited sensitivity for the specific executive processes most relevant to outpatient attendance (e.g. planning/organization, prospective memory, and set-shifting under real-world time and access constraints), as well as potential restricted range/ceiling effects in relatively high-functioning outpatients. Executive dysfunction is well documented in AUD (Brion et al. 2017), and prior work suggests that associations between executive functioning and treatment engagement/outcomes may vary by treatment context and cognitive demands (Morgenstern and Bates 1999, Russell et al. 2025). More targeted and ecologically valid executive-function assessments may therefore be required to clarify cognition-related barriers to retention in outpatient AUD care.

Strengths

This study contributes by examining demoralization, executive function, employment status, and early symptom change within the same naturalistic outpatient AUD cohort. The use of brief, validated assessment tools (DS-MV, TFAB, AUDIT, BSRS-5) allowed for efficient data collection while maintaining psychometric rigor. The treatment discontinuation outcome—defined as ≥30 consecutive days without clinic contact—is consistent with prior work underscoring the prognostic importance of treatment duration in addiction care (Zhang et al. 2003). In addition, the prospective design and repeated assessments over 6 months enabled evaluation of temporal patterns in symptom change and treatment discontinuation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, this single-site study with a modest sample size limits generalizability and yields imprecise estimates (wide confidence intervals). Second, sparse cells led to quasi-complete separation in conventional logistic regression; we therefore used Firth bias-reduced logistic regression and collapsed sparse categories to obtain stable estimates, but residual instability may persist for rare predictors. Third, missingness was informative because follow-up assessments depend on clinic attendance; symptom trajectories and early-change metrics are therefore conditional on observed follow-up. Thus, symptom improvement may be overestimated if early discontinuers had poorer unobserved outcomes. We mitigated temporal-ordering and attendance-related selection for early symptom change using a landmark analysis, but we did not implement full joint MNAR selection models; however, we performed a simple delta-adjusted pattern-mixture sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S2f), and residual selection bias remains possible. Notably, BSRS-5–based improvement thresholds yielded small cell sizes (e.g. ≥2-point reduction), resulting in imprecise estimates despite Firth penalization. Fourth, TAU treatment exposure (pharmacotherapy type/dose and psychosocial contact intensity) was not prospectively captured in the IRB-approved protocol and could not be retrospectively abstracted, limiting our ability to adjust for treatment heterogeneity. Finally, structural and social determinants (e.g. workplace flexibility, transportation, stigma, support) were not measured and may confound associations, particularly for employment. Therefore, employment should be interpreted as an observational marker, not a causal factor.

Conclusion

In adults newly entering outpatient AUD treatment, higher baseline demoralization co-occurred with greater initial severity; apparent demoralization-related differences in AUDIT change attenuated after accounting for baseline severity. Regular employment was associated with elevated risk of treatment discontinuation, supporting the interpretation that occupational constraints may serve as a marker of structural barriers to continued attendance in routine outpatient care. Early symptom improvement at 1 month should be interpreted as an engagement-related correlate among attendees rather than a stand-alone prognostic factor for subsequent discontinuation. These findings underscore the importance of early-phase monitoring and service models that accommodate both psychological burden and practical access constraints. Larger, multisite studies are needed to validate these results and clarify mechanisms.
