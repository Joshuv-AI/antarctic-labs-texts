Sodium and potassium consumption in Jamaica: National estimates and associated factors from the Jamaica Health and Lifestyle Survey 2016–2017
Authors: Trevor S. Ferguson, Novie O. M. Younger-Coleman, Karen Webster-Kerr, Marshall K. Tulloch-Reid, Nadia R. Bennett, Tamu Davidson, Andriene S. Grant, Kelly-Ann M. Gordon-Johnson, Ishtar Govia, Suzanne Soares-Wynter, Joette A. McKenzie, Evelyn Walker, Colette A. Cunningham-Myrie, Simon G. Anderson, Alphanso L. Blake, James Ho, Robyn Stephenson, Sharmaine E. Edwards, Shelly R. McFarlane, Simone Spence, Rainford J. Wilks
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to estimate dietary sodium and potassium consumption among Jamaicans and evaluate associations with sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. A cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the Jamaica Health and Lifestyle Survey 2016–2017. Participants were noninstitutionalized Jamaicans aged ≥15 years. Trained staff collected sociodemographic and health data via interviewer-administered questionnaires and spot urine samples. The Pan American Health Organization formula was used to estimate 24-hour urine sodium and potassium excretion. High sodium level was defined as ≥2000 mg/day, and low potassium levels as <3510 mg/day (World Health Organization criteria). Associations between these outcomes and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were explored using multivariable ANOVA models using log-transformed 24-hour urine sodium and potassium as outcome variables. Analyses included 1009 participants (368 males, 641 females; mean age 48.5 years). The mean sodium excretion was 3582 mg/day (males 3943 mg/day, females 3245 mg/day, P < .001). The mean potassium excretion was 2052 mg/day (males, 2210 mg/day; females, 1904 mg/day; P = .001). The prevalence of high sodium consumption was 66.6% (males 72.8%, females 60.7%, P < .001) and that of low potassium intake was 88.8% (85.1% males, 92.3% females, P < .001). Sodium consumption was inversely associated with older age, higher education, and low glomerular filtration rate but was directly associated with being male, current smoking, and obesity. Overall, males had higher sodium consumption than women, with the effect being larger among hypertensive men. Women with hypertension had lower sodium consumption than nonhypertensive women; however, hypertensive men had higher sodium consumption than nonhypertensive men. Potassium consumption was higher among men, persons with obesity, and those with high total cholesterol but was lower among men with “more than high school” education compared to men with “less than high school” education. We conclude that most Jamaican adults have diets high in sodium and low in potassium. In this study, sodium consumption was directly associated with male sex, obesity, and current smoking but was inversely associated with older age and higher education. High potassium consumption was associated with obesity and high cholesterol levels. These associations should be further explored in longitudinal studies and population-based strategies should be developed to address these cardiovascular risk factors.

1. Introduction

Excess sodium consumption is a major risk factor for hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD) and has been estimated to cause 3 to 5 million deaths globally each year.[1–3] Although the human body requires small amounts of sodium to maintain homeostasis, excess sodium has been associated with several adverse health outcomes, including high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease, stomach cancer, and obesity.[1] The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that sodium intake should be limited to <2.0 g per day, but the global average for sodium intake far exceeds that, at approximately 3.95 g per day.[4,5] Given this high sodium intake, the WHO has published recommendations for member states to institute measures to reduce dietary sodium intake at both individual and population levels.[5] The population approach is also recommended by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) and the Caribbean Public Health Agency (CARPHA).[6,7]

Strong evidence supports the potential impact of dietary sodium reduction in the general population as a strategy for reducing hypertension and CVD.[8–14] An example of the successful implementation of population-wide sodium reduction was observed in the United Kingdom, where a voluntary salt reduction program was developed and implemented in collaboration with the food industry from 2003 to 2004.[11,15] This resulted in a 15% reduction in mean population salt intake, from 9.5 g daily (3.8 g sodium) in 2003 to 8.1 g daily (3.2 g sodium) in 2011.[10,11] A subsequent study demonstrated a 2.9 mm Hg reduction in the population mean SBP after accounting for potential confounding factors.[10,11] They also found that stroke and ischemic heart disease mortality decreased by 36% over the same period.[10] Successful population-wide sodium reduction programs have been demonstrated in Finland, Japan, and Portugal.[10]

Potassium intake is associated with several health outcomes including hypertension and cardiovascular diseases.[16,17] Most studies have found that a higher potassium intake is associated with lower blood pressure.[16,17] It has also been found that the sodium-to-potassium intake ratio is strongly associated with hypertension and CVD.[18] The WHO recommends an increase in potassium intake from food to reduce blood pressure and the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, and coronary heart disease in adults.[19] The WHO suggests that adults have a potassium intake of at least 90 mmol/day (3510 mg/day).[19]

Cardiovascular disease and hypertension are major health problems in Jamaica, with hypertension consistently being among the top 5 leading causes of death.[20,21] Data from the most recent Jamaica Health and Lifestyle Survey conducted in 2016–2017 (JHLS-III) showed that two-thirds of the population had an abnormally high blood pressure; approximately one-third (33.8%) had hypertension (blood pressure > 140/90 mm Hg) and 34.0% had prehypertension (SBP 120–139 mm Hg or DBP 80–89 mm Hg) using the JNC-7 classification.[22]

There are very little data on sodium consumption patterns among Jamaicans, although sodium consumption is generally believed to be high. The Spanish Cohort Study reported an estimated sodium excretion of 3.3 g/24 hours in the 1990s[23]; we do not have any recent or national estimates for Jamaica. The development and implementation of programs, including sodium reduction strategies, are critical for mitigating the impact of elevated blood pressure on population health. However, adequate baseline data are needed to inform policies and assess their impacts. In this study, we estimated dietary sodium and potassium consumption among Jamaicans using spot urinary analyses, and conducted exploratory analyses to evaluate the associations between sodium and potassium consumption and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and data sources

A cross-sectional analysis of data collected as part of the Jamaica Health and Lifestyle Survey 2016–2017 (JHLS-III), a national health examination survey conducted between September 2016 and February 2017, was conducted. Details of the procedures used in the JHLS-III and the protocol for this study have been previously published.[24,25] Trained personnel collected data on a wide cross-section of health-related indices using interviewer-administered questionnaires, physical measurements, point-of-care, laboratory measurements, and geographic information system mapping. A multiple-stage sampling design was used to recruit participants aged ≥15 years. A total of 171 enumeration districts were selected based on a probability proportional to the size of the ED. Within each ED, twenty households (dwellings) were systematically selected, beginning at a random starting point. Within each household, 1 individual who met the inclusion criteria was selected using the Kish method.[26] Both the JHLS-III and this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committees of the University of the West Indies (UWI)—UWI—ECP 25, 15/16 and the Ministry of Health and Wellness (MOHW)—CREC-MN.153 2020/2021; MOHW—2015/51, 2021/05. All participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Questionnaire data

An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic data on medical history, including diagnosis and medication use for conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, and information on health behaviors, including dietary practices, physical activity, and tobacco smoking.

Educational level was categorized based on participants’ responses to a question on the highest level of education reached. Categories were as follows: “less than high school” for persons reporting no education, elementary/primary school education, or junior secondary school education (up to grade 8), “high school” for participants reporting at least some secondary level education (grades 9–13) and “more than high school” for those reporting at least some postsecondary school education, whether through college, university, or vocational training institutions.

Household socioeconomic status was assessed based on the participants’ responses to whether they had a list of 22 household assets (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J989, which provides a full list of items included in household assets). Tertiles were created for the number of household assets used in the analyses. Tertile 1 included persons with 9 or fewer household assets, tertile 2 included those with 10–12 items, and tertile 3 persons with 13–22 items.

Physical activity was estimated using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form, and physical activity levels were classified as low, moderate, or high using the cutoff recommended by International Physical Activity Questionnaire developers.[27]

Tobacco smoking was assessed using the question “Do you currently smoke any form of tobacco (cigarettes, cigars, beady etc)?” Participants who answered yes to this question were categorized as current smokers.

2.3. Blood pressure and anthropometric measurements

Blood pressure was measured using an oscillometric device (Omron 5 series blood pressure monitor; Omron Healthcare, Lake Forest, IL, USA). Three measurements, at 1-minute intervals, were obtained from the right arm of seated participants after ensuring at least 5 minutes of rest. These procedures were developed and standardized for the International Collaborative Study of Hypertension in Blacks.[28] To ensure the reliability of the measurements, the field staff were trained and certified prior to starting the fieldwork and at approximately 3-month intervals thereafter. The means of the second and third systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) measurements were used in the analyses. Blood pressure was categorized using the criteria from the seventh report of the joint national committee on prevention, detection, evaluation, and treatment of high blood pressure[29] consistent with the most recent Jamaica Ministry of Health Guidelines.[30] Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥140 mm Hg, DBP ≥90 mm Hg, or a participant report of previously being on medication for hypertension.

Weight was measured using a portable digital scale (Tanita HD-351 Digital Weight Scale, Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg. Height was measured using a portable stadiometer (Seca 213 Mobile Stadiometer; Seca GmbH & Co., Hamburg, Germany) and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. BMI was categorized according to WHO criteria.[31] Persons with BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 were classified as underweight; normal was defined as BMI 18.5–24.99 kg/m2; overweight defined as BMI 25.0–29.99 kg/m2 and obese, BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2.

2.4. Point of care and laboratory measurements

Fasting glucose and total cholesterol levels were measured from a capillary blood sample using a point-of-care device (SD LipidoCare, Suwon, South Korea). Diabetes cutoff points were based on the American Diabetes Association criteria,[32] with diabetes defined as fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/l or participants’ report of previously being on medications for diabetes. Cholesterol cutoff points were based on the Adult Treatment Panel III,[33] with high cholesterol defined as fasting total cholesterol ≥5.2 mmol/l or participants reported being on medications for high cholesterol.

Serum creatinine was measured using a Cobas c111 Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) and reported in µmol/l. Estimated glomerular filtration rate was obtained from serum creatinine using the formula from the chronic kidney disease epidemiology (CKD-EPI) collaboration[34] and reduced GFR was defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2.[35]

2.5. Estimated 24-hour urine sodium and potassium excretion

An early morning spot urine sample was collected from the study participants, and urine sodium and potassium concentrations were measured using a Roche 9180 Electrolyte Analyzer (Mannheim, Germany). Urine creatinine levels were measured using a Cobas c111 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Spot urine sodium and potassium concentrations were used to estimate 24-hour urine sodium (Na) and potassium (K) levels using the formulae recommended by PAHO, as shown in Equation 1 below.[36] Estimated 24-hour urine creatinine was calculated using the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula (Equation 2) as follows:[37,38]: Equation 1:[Estimated 24−hour value =(measured spot urine Na or K ÷ measured spot urine creatinine [Cr])) × estimated 24−hour urinary Cr]. Equation 2:[Estimated 24−hour urine Cr = 879.89 + 12.51 × weight (kg) − 6.19 × age + (34.51 if black) −(379.42 if female)]

We then used the 24-hour urine sodium and potassium levels to categorize the participants’ sodium and potassium consumption categories and to estimate the sodium-to-potassium ratio. High sodium consumption was defined as ≥2000 mg/day, while low potassium consumption was defined as <3510 mg/d, according to the WHO criteria.[5,19,39] Normal sodium consumption was defined as <2000 mg/d, whereas adequate potassium consumption was defined as ≥3510 mg/d. A high sodium-to-potassium ratio was defined as ≥1.0, as recommended by the INTERSALT investigators.[18]

2.6. Sample size and power

Details of the sample size and power calculations are reported in the study protocol.[25] We obtained urine samples from 1091 participants, which translated to an effective sample size of 563 participants after applying a design effect of 1.94 to account for the survey design. The hypothesized mean sodium excretion of 2565 mg/d was obtained from the Health of the Nations Study in Barbados.[40] Using the “power onemean” command in Stata (RRID:SCR_012763) and 10% margin of error (i.e., 2656 vs 2390), the effective sample size of 563 gave a power of 97.5% for this study. We also assessed the power of the study to estimate sex-specific means again using the Barbados study. Given the smaller available sample size of 641 women and 368 men (with effective sample sizes of 190 and 330, respectively), we used a 15% margin of error with a mean sodium level of 2876 mg/d for men and 2453 mg/d for women. The estimated power was 98% and 81% for men and women, respectively.

2.7. Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using Stata version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). We estimated means and proportions for 24-hour urine sodium, 24-hour urine potassium, and related variables and assessed associations with covariates including age, sex, socioeconomic status, health behavior, and clinical characteristics. Differences in proportions were estimated using Pearson chi-square test or Fisher exact test, whereas differences in means were estimated using Student t test or analysis of variance, as appropriate. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analyses for population mean sodium and potassium consumption, estimates for the prevalence of high sodium and low potassium, bivariate models, and multivariable models were performed using survey weights to account for the multistage survey design and the age and sex distribution of the population. Descriptive statistics showing the sample means and proportions are unweighted. The prevalence of high sodium and low potassium consumption was estimated within and across the sex and age categories. The distributions for both 24-hour urine sodium and 24-hour urine potassium were right-skewed and therefore log-transformed for use in bivariate and multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) models. There was evidence for sex interaction in the relationship between sodium and potassium consumption and some explanatory variables; therefore, we included interaction terms in the final multivariable models.

Covariates included in the multivariable analyses were age category (in 20-year bands), sex, socioeconomic status (education level and number of household possessions), adding salt at the table, cigarette smoking and physical activity, hypertension, diabetes status, obesity, high cholesterol, and low eGFR. Those selected for inclusion in the final models had at least 1 category with a P value of <.2 for association with the outcome in the bivariate models, except for “adding salt at the table,” which was included a priori, given that we wanted evaluate whether this practice was associated with sodium consumption.

Analyses were restricted to participants who had nonmissing data 24-hour urine sodium data, age, sex, and survey weights, resulting in an analytic sample of 1009 participants. Of these 1009 included participants, 510 (50.5%) had no missing values for any of the variables selected for use in the analyses and 499 participants (49.5%) had one or more missing values. The missing data were primarily due to smoking, with 34% of participants having missing data, 8% having missing data for fasting cholesterol, and 6% having missing data for fasting glucose. The proportion of missing data was <5% for all other variables with missing data. Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J991, shows the number and proportion of missing values for the variables included in the analyses. There were no statistically significant differences between participants with and without missing data when the means and proportions of the variables used in the analyses were compared. Given the proportion of participants with missing data, complete case analysis (list-wise deletion of missing data) would result in a loss of power; therefore, we chose to use multiple imputations to deal with missing data in this study.[41,42] We generated 50 imputed datasets such that the number of imputations was at least equal to the proportion of incomplete cases, representing an adaptation of the rule of thumb suggested by White et al.[42] The imputation models included all variables for inclusion in the multivariable models. Bivariate and multivariable models were created using Stata mi estimate command using the 50 multiply imputed datasets, and estimates were combined using Rubin rules.[43,44] We also performed complete case analyses (i.e., models with no imputed values) to assess whether the inferences were similar to those obtained from multiple imputation analyses.

3. Results

The final sample included 1009 participants (368 males and 641 females). Summary statistics for participant characteristics are shown in Table 1; the mean age of participants was 48.5 ± 18.2 years, with no sex difference. Males were taller than females, but females had higher mean weight and BMI. Males also had a higher mean SBP, but women had higher fasting glucose levels. The mean DBP and eGFR were similar between men and women. The mean spot urine sodium concentration was 2973 mg/l which was higher in men than in women (3151 mg/l vs 2870 mg/l [P = .001]). The mean spot urine potassium concentration was 2319 mg/l, which was not significantly different between sexes. We also obtained survey-weighted estimates for mean summary statistics to reflect population-based estimates (see Table S3, http://links.lww.com/MD/J992, which provides these survey-weighted summary statistics).

Associations between sodium and potassium consumption and biomedical characteristics were also explored using bivariate ANOVA models with log-transformed 24-hour urine sodium and potassium levels. These findings are summarized in Tables S6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J995, and S7, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J996. The tables also include P values for sex interaction of each variable considered in the bivariate models. For sodium consumption, significant associations were observed for age (both continuous and categorical) and sex. The association between hypertension and sodium consumption varies by sex, with higher sodium consumption among hypertensive versus normotensive males and lower sodium consumption among hypertensive females. Sodium consumption was higher among obese individuals, both males and females, and among current smokers (males and combined male + female groups). Low glomerular filtration rate is inversely associated with sodium consumption. There was evidence of a significant sex interaction (P < .001) for hypertension. Regarding potassium consumption, significant positive associations were observed with age, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol levels. Inverse associations were observed for high school education among males only. Significant sex interactions were observed in the “high school education” category. Given the significant sex interactions identified in the bivariate analysis, sex-interaction terms were included in the multivariable models, and category-specific estimates were presented where appropriate.

Table 4 shows the results of multivariable ANOVA models evaluating the association between sodium consumption and covariates, including age category, hypertension, obesity, low GFR, education level, household possession category, current smoking, and salt added at the table, using log-transformed 24-hour urine sodium as the outcome variable. We report β-coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), as well as the percentage difference in 24-hour urine sodium compared to the reference category. The percentage difference was calculated as (exponential of the β-coefficient − 1 × 100). Older age was associated with lower urine sodium; the age group 55–74 years was associated with a statistically significant 18% decrease in 24-hour urine sodium compared to the age 15–34 years (P = .02). Men had higher sodium consumption than women, with a larger sex effect (66% higher than women, P < .001) among hypertensive men than among nonhypertensive men (16% higher than women, P = .03). The association with hypertension also varied by sex; hypertensive women had 15% lower sodium consumption (P = .03) than nonhypertensive women, whereas hypertensive men had 22% higher sodium consumption (P = .002) than nonhypertensive men. Obesity and current smoking were also associated with higher sodium consumption (43%, P < .001 and 18%, P = .03, respectively). A low GFR was associated with 22% lower sodium consumption (P = .01). There was no association between salt added at the table and sodium consumption in this model.

4. Discussion

In this study, we found that the estimated mean sodium consumption was high among Jamaican adults, whereas the mean potassium consumption was low. Sodium consumption was directly associated with male sex, obesity, and current smoking but was inversely associated with older age and higher education. High potassium consumption was associated with obesity and high cholesterol levels.

The estimated mean sodium consumption in this study was higher than that reported among persons 25–64 years old in Barbados (2656 mg/d), but lower than the estimated global mean (3950 mg/d), and similar to estimates from the United States of America (3608 mg/d).[4,40,45] The estimates in this study were similar to those reported in the Spanish Town Cohort in the 1990s.[23] As seen in our study, the mean sodium consumption estimates were higher among men in Barbados, USA, and in the global estimates. The estimated prevalence of high sodium consumption was similar in Jamaica and Barbados, 67% in both, but lower than that in the USA (75%), even though the USA cutoff point was 2300 mg/d, whereas the WHO cutoff point of 2000 mg/d was used for Jamaica and Barbados. The estimated mean potassium consumption was higher in Jamaica than in Barbados (1469 mg/d), and similar to that reported in the USA (2155 mg/d).[40,45] The findings in Jamaica were also similar to those reported in a recent systematic review of sodium and potassium excretion in the Americas, where the mean sodium excretion was 157 mmol/d (3617 mg/d) and the mean potassium excretion was 58 mmol/d (2256 mg/d).[46] However, sodium consumption in our study was lower than that reported in Moldova and Northern China, with estimates of 3972 mg/d and 5336 mg/d, respectively.[47,48] In the latter 2 studies, potassium excretion was 2842 mg/d and 1595 mg/d in Moldova and Northern China, respectively.

The analytical models in this study were primarily exploratory, as we sought to identify factors associated with sodium and potassium consumption, particularly those that could be targeted in future public health interventions. The strongest association was found for obesity, which was associated with 43% higher sodium and 43% higher potassium consumption in multivariable models. Similar findings have been reported in studies in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Korea.[49–52] An association between high-salt intake and increased caloric consumption may be a possible mechanism contributing to these effects. However, it should be noted that the studies cited above assessed the effect of caloric intake and found that the association persisted after adjusting for caloric intake. This suggests that a higher caloric intake only partially explains the association between salt intake and obesity. Other hypothesized mechanisms include direct effects on appetite through the stimulation of ghrelin[53] or addictive properties through effects on opiate and dopamine receptors, resulting in overeating associated with “salt withdrawal.”[54]

In this study, sodium consumption was inversely associated with hypertension among women but was directly associated with hypertension among men. No significant association was observed between hypertension and potassium intake in this study. These findings differed from the expected direct association between sodium intake and inverse association with potassium intake reported in other studies.[55,56] The inverse association among women may be due to reverse causality, in which women with hypertension are advised to reduce their salt intake.

We found that age category was inversely associated with sodium consumption but was statistically significant only in the 55–74 age group. Barbados[40] did not report a significant association between age and sodium consumption in multivariable models. In the USA study, bivariate analyses suggested similar sodium consumption in age categories and higher potassium consumption among older individuals.[45] Our study found that persons with more than high school education had lower sodium consumption, but this was not corroborated in studies from Barbados or the USA. With regard to cigarette smoking, neither the USA nor the Barbadian study reported an association; however, a preliminary Korean study reported that current smokers had a higher mean sodium intake and preference for salty foods.[57] The finding of an inverse association between low GFR and sodium intake may also be related to reverse causality resulting from reduced sodium consumption in patients with CKD as well as sodium retention associated with CKD itself.

This study has some limitations. First, sodium and potassium levels were measured using spot urine samples rather than the preferred 24-hour urine samples. However, recent studies have shown that spot urine sample estimates can provide good indications for mean population salt intake and the need for population-based action.[58] The consistency of our findings with those from studies based on 24-hour urine collections also adds to the plausibility of our estimates. Second, spot urine samples were only available for a subset of the survey participants, and some participants had missing data. We used multiple imputations to fill in the missing data, thus minimizing the potential bias and reducing power.

The strengths of this study include its population-based nature and use of survey weights to ensure that the estimates are nationally representative. These data represent the first report of national estimates of sodium consumption. Other strengths include the use of standardized measurement procedures to ensure data quality and the use of multiple imputation to account for missing data.

These findings have important public health implications in Jamaica. Given the high prevalence of hypertension and the more recent findings of a high prevalence of chronic kidney disease,[20,22,59–61] there is an urgent need to increase awareness of the dangers of high-salt diets and the implementation of public health interventions that take a population-based approach to reduce sodium intake and increase potassium intake.

Potential public health interventions may include strategies that combine efforts to reduce caloric intake with efforts to reduce sodium consumption in public education programs. Nutritional labels with warnings for high-salt content and other unhealthy components in food products are another possible population-level intervention that has been recently shown to be effective.[62–64] In addition, public health experts should seek to improve dietary potassium intake through the consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables. The high prevalence, even in the younger age group, suggests that interventions should start early and include strategies to engage younger persons, civil society, and industry regulation. Given the association between cigarette smoking and being male, individual-level interventions targeting men and people who smoke should also be considered.

The sustainability of low sodium and increased potassium consumption can be improved by emphasizing the population-based approach rather than the individual-level approach only and ensuring that the food industry reduces the sodium content of processed foods. This approach was used in the UK, where a 15% reduction in sodium intake (9.5 g to 8.1 g) was achieved between 2003 and 2011,[11] and this has been maintained at 8.4 g/d in 2019.[65] While not achieving the targeted 6.0 g/d, this reduction translated into a significant reduction in heart disease and stroke and is therefore a useful approach.

Further research should identify the specific dietary sources of sodium and potassium, implement appropriate interventions, and evaluate their effectiveness in the Jamaican context.
