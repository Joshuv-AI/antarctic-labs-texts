High-flow nasal-cannula oxygen therapy in intensive-care-unit patients: a prospective multicenter observational cohort study (OHE-REA)
Authors: Paul Compagne, Stephan Ehrmann, Maud Jonas, Jean Morin, Julien Cadiet, Christophe Guitton, Damien Du Cheyron, Jerome Devaquet, Bertrand Sauneuf, Hadrien Winiszewski, Toufik Kamel, Agathe Delbove, Karim Lakhal, Jean Reignier, Aurélie Le Thuaut, Jean-Baptiste Lascarrou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-flow oxygen therapy (HFOT) has become the most common oxygen-supplementation modality in patients with acute respiratory failure, based chiefly on data from tightly selected patients included in randomized trials. Our objective was to obtain real-life data on HFOT failure. This prospective observational multicenter French study done in 13 intensive care units in 2019–2020 was designed to determine the proportion of patients with failed HFOT, defined as intubation, noninvasive ventilation, standard oxygen therapy with an estimated FiO2>50%. We included 257 patients before prematurely ending the study due to the COVID-19 pandemic. HFOT failed in 79 patients (32%), including 42 (17%) who required intubation. Mean HFOT duration was 2.4 ± 2.2 days. By multivariate analysis, a low ROX index (ratio of pulse-oximeter saturation (SpO2) over FiO2, over the respiratory rate (RR) was associated with HFOT failure (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.83; 95%CI, 0.77–0.90, P < 0.0001), as were elevated mean arterial pressure (aHR, 1.03; 95%CI, 1.02–1.05), vasopressor therapy (aHR, 3.35; 95%CI, 1.44–7.79), and worse Glasgow Coma Scale score (aHR, 0.69; 95%CI, 0.57–0.80, P < 0.001). The identification of factors independently associated with HFOT failure should help to determine which patients are most likely to benefit from HFOT, thereby preventing delayed intubation. Introduction

High-flow oxygen therapy (HFOT) is increasingly used instead of standard oxygen therapy in patients with acute respiratory failure (ARF). HFOT uses a nasal cannula to deliver high flows of up to 70 L/min of a warm and humidified gas mixture1,2. Advantages of HFOT include a high, controlled inspired oxygen fraction (FiO2); generation of a low level of positive end-expiratory pressure, depending on the flow rate; decreased nasopharyngeal resistance; and washout of the anatomical dead space by the high nasal flow3–6. Also, patient comfort is better than with noninvasive ventilation (NIV)7.

The FLORALI randomized controlled trial (RCT) in patients with hypoxemic ARF compared standard oxygen, NIV, and HFOT8. Although the intubation rate by day 28 (primary outcome) was similar across the three groups in the overall population, HFOT was associated with fewer intubations in the subgroup with severe hypoxemia. Also, overall, HFOT was associated with a lower day-90 mortality rate. A meta-analysis of RCTs, however, concluded that the evidence was insufficient to recommend HFOT for hypoxemic ARF and that using HFOT might delay intubation9,10. Nonetheless, HFOT was widely used during the COVID-19 pandemic based on its pathophysiological advantages, comfort, and ease of use. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in patients with severe ARF due to COVID-19, compared to standard oxygen therapy, HFOT decreased the need for intubation but not the day-28 mortality rate, which was the primary outcome11. Moreover, compared to HFOT, intubation with protective ventilation may decrease the risk of self-inflicted lung injury12.

HFOT may therefore be best reserved for patients who are unlikely to require intubation. Predicting intubation needs, i.e., the risk of HFOT failure, would therefore be valuable. In two observational cohort studies, the ROX index was shown to predict HFOT failure in patients with ARF due to COVID-1913,14. The ROX index is computed as the ratio of pulse-oximeter saturation (SpO2) over FiO2, over the respiratory rate (RR).

The primary objective of this prospective, observational, multicenter study designed before the COVID-19 pandemic was to determine the incidence of HFOT failure in patients admitted to the ICU with ARF. The secondary objectives included the identification of clinical or laboratory parameters that predicted HFOT failure and to describe weaning off HFOT.

Results

Subgroup analysis of immunocompromised patients

Factors significantly associated with HFOT failure by univariate analysis were worsening FiO2 over time, low SaO2, dyspnea without respiratory support, a low ROX index/flow, MAP or diastolic pressure elevation, and a worse GCS score (eTable 2).

Subgroup analysis of patients weaned off high-flow oxygen therapy due to improved respiratory status

Of the 247 patients, 181 were weaned off HFOT due to an improvement in their respiratory status. In this subgroup, the mean flow rate was 54 ± 8 L/min on the first HFOT day and 48 ± 11 L/min 1 h before HFOT discontinuation. The corresponding mean FiO2 values were 57.4 ± 18.1% and 37.1 ± 10.1%, respectively (eTable 3). By univariate analysis, only male sex and FiO2 level before attempted weaning were significantly associated with weaning failure (eTable 4).

Discussion

In our prospective multicenter cohort of 247 patients admitted to medical or surgical ICUs and given HFOT to manage ARF, this treatment failed in 79 (32%) patients including 42 (42/247, 17%) who required intubation. The factors independently associated with HFOT failure were a lower ROX index (which reflects SpO2, FiO2, and RR), worse consciousness impairment, greater MAP elevation, and need for vasopressor therapy.

In the FLORALI RCT comparing HFOT, NIV, and standard facemask oxygen therapy, 38% of patients in the HFOT group required intubation8. In a Cochrane database review, the proportion was 33%9. These intubation rates are consistent with ours. The HFOT failure rate was lower, at 12%, in a retrospective single-center study13. Studies are difficult to compare, given the differences in case-mix. Thus, the initial required FiO2 during HFOT was 57 ± 18% in our cohort compared to 80 ± 21% in the FLORALI RCT. Corresponding values for HFOT duration were 2.4 ± 2.2 days and 24 ± 8 days, respectively. Moreover, the FLORALI RCT and most of the trials included in the Cochrane review used a narrower definition of ARF compared to our study, notably a PaO2/FiO2 ratio < 300.The baseline features of our patients were similar to those in previous studies, with a predominance of overweight men aged 60 to 65 years. However, the proportion of patients with an immunocompromised status or a history of chronic respiratory disease was higher in our cohort.

We are aware of a single previous study that investigated factors associated with HFOT failure13. Low FiO2 and a ROX index ≥ 9.2 predicted successful weaning off HFOT. The ROX index was developed in patients who received HFOT to treat ARF related to infectious pneumonia and was subsequently evaluated in cohorts of patients with Covid-1914, as a potential predictor of intubation. The three components of the ROX index reflect the severity of ARF. In previous studies, the ROX index was determined at specific timepoints after HFOT initiation, e.g., at2, 6, and 12 h. An advantage of our study is the evaluation of the ROX index as a continuous variable.

In our cohort, neither a history of chronic respiratory disease nor an immunocompromised status was associated with HFOT failure. However, most patients had non-hypercapnic hypoxemic ARF. In previous studies, HFOT was as effective and more comfortable compared to NIV in patients with mildly hypercapnic ARF, possibly due to the dead-space washout effect15,16. A history of chronic respiratory disease was not far from being independently associated with a lower risk of HFOT failure (HR, 0.58; 95%CI, 0.33–1.01). However, monitoring bias may have occurred in patients with chronic respiratory disease, who often also received aerosol therapy and respiratory physiotherapy. Previous results in immunocompromised patients are difficult to interpret: the risk of intubation may be lower, notably compared to NIV, but HFOT was not associated with mortality and produced similar outcomes to standard oxygen therapy17,18. Routine use of HFOT after extubation has provided discordant results18,19. Interestingly, we found that predictors of HFOT failure were not different in the two subgroups of interest vs. the overall population20.

A survey in 34 French ICUs demonstrated considerable variability in weaning practices, notably regarding the decrease in HFOT settings and the timing of the switch to standard oxygen therapy21. In our study, decisions to wean patients off HFOT were nearly always made by physicians. The usual strategy was an FiO2 decrease to 30–40% with a fresh gas flow rate of 50–60 mL/min, in accordance with the scarce literature13,22.

MAP elevation was independently associated with HFOT failure in our cohort. Few data on this parameter are available. In an observational study of 40 patients with COVID-19, worsening of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio was associated with both blood-pressure elevation and a decrease in serum potassium, suggesting activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)23. A pathophysiological study found a decrease in the expression of the angiotensin-converting enzyme type 2 during COVID-1924. This enzyme cleaves angiotensin 2, and a decrease in expression therefore activates the RAAS, inducing systemic vasoconstriction with blood-pressure elevation, as well as pulmonary vasoconstriction with ventilation/perfusion ratio changes responsible for impairments in alveolar gas exchanges. Another expected effect is salt and water retention with hypokalemia and activation of fibrosis in the lung parenchyma. In our cohort, the main cause of ARF was infectious pneumonia. Although the nature of the infection was not collected, patient recruitment occurred chiefly during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting that many patients had COVID-19, with the above-described effects on blood-pressure variables including MAP. Studies of hemodynamic parameters and RAAS activity in ARF due to other causes might identify serum predictors of a poor response to HFOT, knowledge of which might help to avoid delayed intubation.

That HFOT might delay intubation is a major concern. A study suggests such an effect, with an increase in mortality as a result, but the methodology used does not allow a firm conclusion10. A post-hoc analysis of data from the FLORALI RCT found no association between HFOT and delayed intubation25, in keeping with a study done in the Covid-19 era26. In a prospective cohort study of patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, delaying intubation and protective mechanical ventilation for any reason was associated with higher mortality compared to early intubation, although mortality was higher in patients with vs. without intubation27.

One of the main limitations of our study is the selection bias caused by the recruitment during the COVID-19 pandemic. COVID-19 was probably the main cause of ARF in our cohort. This bias may limit the general applicability of our findings, as HFOT outcomes may vary according to the cause of ARF. A second limitation is that the number of patients with HFOT weaning failure was too small to provide sufficient statistical power for reliable subgroup analyses. Third, no data were collected between 1 h and 48 h after HFOT discontinuation. Fourth, at the time of our study, only symmetrical HFOT cannulas were available. Asymmetrical cannulas may be associated with better ventilation and comfort parameters28,29. Fifth, we did not record the time from symptom onset to treatment initiation, which may have influenced the treatment outcomes. Finally, our study design did not allow us to evaluate a potential association between HFOT duration before intubation and mortality.

In conclusion, we identified several predictors of HFOT failure, i.e., the ROX index, MAP elevation, need for vasopressor therapy, and consciousness impairment. Knowledge of these predictors should improve decisions about when to switch to invasive MV. Our study provides information on HFOT use and outcomes in clinical practice. Further work is needed to define optimal HFOT indications, duration, and weaning modalities.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective, observational, multicenter study was done in 13 ICUs in France, in ten hospitals. Consecutive adults admitted between November 20, 2019, and October 30, 2020 and given HFOT to manage ARF related to hypoxemia were included (hypercapnic ARFs were not targeted). Of note, the COVID pandemic started in France in March 2020. Decisions about using HFOT were left to the attending physicians. The exclusion criteria were age ≤ 18 years, person under guardianship, pregnancy, and HFOT given routinely to prevent extubation failure. HFOT weaning was supervised by the attending physician, and discontinuation was usually considered when SpO₂ was ≥ 92% with an FiO₂ ≤ 60%. Flow and fraction of inspired oxygen were gradually reduced in parallel, without a formal protocol, across all participating ICUs.

Data collection

For each patient, the clinical and laboratory data for the study were entered into a paper case-report form by the attending physician, without altering the usual practices of each participating ICU. The following were recorded: age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index30, history of chronic respiratory disease, cause of ARF (infectious pneumonia, non-infectious pneumonia, heart failure, alveolar ventilation disorder, pulmonary embolism, or exacerbation of obstructive respiratory disease), and whether the patient had an immune deficiency. Three times a day from baseline to day 10 in the ICU, we recorded the clinical variables, HFOT parameters, and HFOT tolerance assessed using a visual analog scale. Laboratory variables obtained as part of standard care, the ROX index31, and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio were recorded once a day. Data collection points were choose to allow continuous monitoring by each nurse shift during the stay. Data were also recorded 1 h and 48 h after HFOT discontinuation. Two collection points were choose: one early and one late after HFOT discontinuation similarly to invasive mechanical ventilation weaning32. For each patient, the paper case-report form was the only source of data for the analyses. These data were recorded and coded in an Excel database (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) using a computer questionnaire created with Sphinx Online (Chavanod, France).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with HFOT failure, defined as a need for endotracheal mechanical ventilation (MV), de-novo NIV, a switch to standard oxygen therapy with an FiO2 equivalent > 50%33related to poor tolerance of HFOT, or death while receiving HFOT.

The secondary outcomes included the proportion of patients with HFOT weaning failure defined as the resumption of HFOT or of an oxygen flow with an FiO2 equivalent > 50% within 48 h after HFOT discontinuation prompted by an improvement in the respiratory status. Additional secondary endpoints were clinical parameters likely to be risk factors for HFOT failure and HFOT weaning failure, namely, FiO2, RR, SpO2, mean arterial pressure (MAP), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, need for vasopressors, immunocompromised status, history of chronic respiratory disease, and a high Charlson Comorbidity Index. Patient tolerance to HFOT and adverse effects of HFOT were also secondary endpoints.

Sample size

The target sample size was 600 patients. The proportion of patients in ARF who experience failure of HFOT varies widely, from 5% to 50%8,34, according to the cause of the ARF. We chose to base our calculations on a value of 33%, a conservative approach regarding proportion from8. Given that we planned to perform a multivariate analysis of risk factors for HFOT the required sample size was at least 10 patients per risk factor35. Assuming that twenty risk factors were to be included in the multivariate analysis, 200 patients with HFOT failure were required, resulting in a sample size of 600 patients.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were described as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range]. Distribution normality was checked by visual inspection. The worst daily value was kept for variables recorded several times a day. When HFOT was discontinued, the value recorded 1 h before discontinuation was considered.

Factors associated to HFOT failure was assessed by time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models. Variables associated by univariate analysis with P values < 0.2 were introduced into the multivariate model. These variables were sex, past history of chronic respiratory disease, immunocompromised status, Charlson Comorbidity Index, dyspnea, ROX index, MAP, and the GCS score. The adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with their 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were computed. Univariate analysis was also performed in the subset of patients immunocompromised. A similar analysis was planned to identify variables associated with failed HFOT weaning. We also conducted a subgroup analysis of patients in whom HFOT was discontinued due to an improvement in their respiratory status. Values of P < 0.05 were taken to indicate statistically significant differences. Missing data were counted and reported in the tables. SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used to perform the analyses.

Ethics

The patients were included on an emergency basis at a time when they were too ill to provide informed consent. In compliance with French law, the next of kin was asked as soon as possible about informed consent to continued participation in the study. Patients were asked for informed consent as soon as they regained decision-making capacity, if they then refused, their data were eliminated.

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the French Intensive Care Society (CE19-23) on June 16, 2019. The study was registered on the ClinicalTrials.gov registry on February 11, 2019 (NCT04141956).

All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations. Research were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki36.
