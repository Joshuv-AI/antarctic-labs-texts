Selective Remediation and Reduction of Cr(VI) to Cr(II) Using Carbon-Supported Co, Ni, and Zn Single-Atom Catalysts: A First-Principles Investigation
Authors: Liang Ye, Xianfei Chen, Yi Huang, JinShu Zhang, YuBin Li, Chengye Yang, Beibei Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hexavalent chromium (Cr­(VI)) is a prevalent heavy metal contaminant in soil, exhibiting high biotoxicity and posing significant threats to both ecosystems and public health. Reducing Cr­(VI) to lower oxidation states without introducing an additional reducing agent is an effective strategy to mitigate its toxicity. However, the selective remediation and reduction of highly hazardous Cr­(VI) have been hampered by the absence of an appropriate catalyst with desired activity and a comprehensive molecular-level understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Herein, a two-dimensional N-doped graphene (N4G) with a 3d transition metal as an active site was systemically investigated for reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(II) using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The results show that Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G catalysts exhibit pronounced selective adsorption toward the Cr­(VI) and humic acid (HA) in comparison to H2O, O2, and N2 under soil environments. The reaction pathways and associated dynamic barriers for each reduction step of Cr reduction were explored in detail, and the involved mechanisms of Cr­(VI) reduction to Cr­(IV) and Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) was elucidated. Notably, the Co–N4G and Ni–N4G catalysts exhibit low E bar of 0.59 and 0.49 eV during the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(VI) over other single-atom catalysts. In contrast, the Zn–N4G catalyst exhibits higher catalytic activity toward the Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) with an E bar of 1.35 eV. These findings offer an atom-scale insight for the rational design of advanced catalysts to enhance the selective remediation of heavy metal contamination in soil.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of industrial and agricultural activities has undeniably elevated living standards; however, it has also precipitated significant environmental degradation, with heavy metal pollution emerging as a critical concern. − Chromium, primarily introduced through mining, metallurgy, leather tanning, dyeing, and industrial cooling processes, stands out as one of the most deleterious heavy metals. − The toxicity of chromium ions varies markedly with their oxidation states, with hexavalent chromium [Cr­(VI)] exhibiting pronounced immunotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, neurotoxicity, and carcinogenicity. − Cr­(VI) can infiltrate the human body via air, water, and food, affecting the lungs, liver, kidneys, and endocrine system through dermal, respiratory, and gastrointestinal pathways. − According to World Health Organization guidelines, the maximum permissible limit for the discharge of Cr­(VI) species into surface water bodies is 0.05 mg·L–1. , In contrast, Cr­(III) demonstrates significantly lower toxicity and solubility, with approximately 100 times less toxic than Cr­(VI) and functions as one essential trace element in human body. − Similarly, Cr­(II) also exhibit lower toxicity compared to Cr­(VI), and it should be noted that Cr­(II) is unstable under natural conditions and will rapidly oxidizes to Cr­(III). Consequently, the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(III) or other lower oxidation states (e.g., Cr­(II), Cr­(IV), Cr­(V)) is recognized as the most effective method for mitigating the hazards of Cr­(VI) contamination and ensuring long-term environmental sustainability. −

In cultivated soil, Cr­(VI) predominantly exists as Cr2O7 2– and CrO4 2– anions, with CrO4 2– being the dominant form under all conditions due to the facile conversion of Cr2O7 2– to CrO4 2–. Considerable attention has been directed toward the reduction of Cr­(VI) with the addition of various electron donors (e.g., glucose, acetate, propionate, glycerol, and glycine) to enhance reaction efficacy under natural conditions. For instance, Hu et al. found that calcium polysulfide (CPS) could effectively reduce Cr­(VI) under alkaline conditions without significantly altering soil pH, thereby enhancing the stability of reduction products. Nguyen et al. showed that H2S exhibits superior efficacy for Cr­(VI) removal in cold regions. Liu et al. demonstrated that Pseudomonas aeruginosa can utilize various electron donors (e.g., glucose, succinate, and malate) to reduce Cr­(VI). Similarly, Wani et al. reported that Burkholderia cepacia can effectively reduce Cr­(VI) concentrations in the presence of electron donors such as lactose, glucose, sucrose, and starch. Zhang et al. employed rice husk, an organic matter-rich amendment, to remediate Cr­(VI)-contaminated soil, while Di-Palma et al. used FeSO4 to remediate Cr­(VI)-contaminated soil. These studies provide a pivotal advancement in our understanding of the reduction mechanism of highly toxic Cr­(VI) to less harmful species, providing a promising solution to this critical environmental challenge. Nevertheless, the introduction of external electron donors or reductants may pose new risks or contamination to the soil microbial ecosystem, potentially leading to additional environmental issues.

Humic acid (HA), a heterogeneous and complex mixture of organic molecules, is predominantly composed of aromatic structures, phenolic hydroxyl groups, and carboxyl functional groups. , It is primarily formed through the microbial decomposition of plant and animal residues, which constitute the major organic matter in soil. These substances play a critical role in soil chemistry, influencing nutrient availability, metal ion binding, and microbial activity. − Also, HA is extensively utilized across agriculture, forestry, healthcare, and environmental protection due to its abundance and versatility, often serving as an organic reductant in environmental remediation projects. − Development of promising green catalyst capable of reduce Cr­(VI) to Cr­(II) in the presence of HA is highly desired to address the Cr­(VI) contamination issues and ensuring agricultural land security. − Zhu et al. investigate the Cr­(VI) reduction mechanism by HA, using ethanol and ethanediol as analogs for HA on the surface of montmorillonite, providing molecular-level insights into the adsorption and reduction processes. However, the reaction rate on pristine montmorillonite remains to be improved, in light of the low activity of natural montmorillonite.

The selection of catalysts plays a crucial role in environmental remediation. Carbon-based materials, owing to their ecological compatibility and environmental friendliness, have been widely applied in this field. For instance, Jiang et al. utilized graphene-based single-atom catalysts for HCHO oxidation and reported a rate-limiting step energy barrier of only 0.92 eV for Cr-SG, significantly lower than that of commercial Ag catalysts. Wang et al. investigated O-ligand graphene-based single-atom catalysts for ethyl acetate oxidation and demonstrated that the O-ligand Ni doped single-atom catalyst exhibits a d-band center closer to the Fermi level compared to conventional N-ligand, facilitating electron transfer and enhancing catalytic activity. Zhang et al. constructed a heterojunction by coupling single-atom catalysts (SACs) with metal oxides, as exemplified by the Co3O4@Co1/C3N5 system. This heterostructure significantly enhanced the peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation performance and further improved the degradation kinetics of diclofenac sodium. Furthermore, Sun et al. combined machine learning and density functional theory to explore the intrinsic factors governing PMS activation by single-atom M–N-C catalysts. In this context, we investigated nitrogen-doped graphene (N4-G) supported single-atom catalysts (SACs) as a novel and promising system for Cr­(VI) reduction in the presence of HA, given the ecological compatibility and environmental friendliness of carbon-based materials. − A system screening of the promising catalytic activity center (Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) was conducted in light of their thermodynamic and electrochemical stability in soil. , The adsorption and reduction mechanisms of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) and Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) by HA on TM-N4G have been elucidated. The results indicated that Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G exhibited high structural and electrochemical stability, as well as unique selective adsorption and catalytic effects on Cr­(VI) in a complex environment of soil. Our findings provide molecular-level insights into the Cr­(VI) reduction mechanism in the presence of HA in soil and offer important guidance for the design of efficient catalyst systems capable of selectively adsorbing Cr­(VI) and reducing its high toxicity to Cr­(II).

Computational Methods

All calculations in this work were performed using the DMol3 software package based on density functional theory (DFT). , The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional were employed to describe the electron exchange and correlation. , A polarized double numerical orbital (DNP) basis set was used, with core electrons described by an all-electron method, including the relativistic effects. To prevent interlayer interactions, vacuum layers exceeding 20 Å were included in the simulation. Spin polarization was considered in all calculations to assess the intrinsic magnetic properties of the transition metal atoms. For geometric optimization, the convergence criteria for energy, force, and maximum displacement were set to 1.0 × 10–5 Ha, 0.002 Ha Å–1 and 0.005 Å, respectively. The minimum energy path (MEP) was determined using the LST/QST method with a RMS value of 0.002 Ha/Å, and further confirmed by the NEB method. The semiempirical dispersion-corrected, proposed by Grimme, was used to model van der Waals interactions. Brillouin zone integration was carried out using 3 × 3 × 1 k-points for geometry optimizations, and a global cutoff radius of 5.4 Å was chosen for high-quality results. A conductor-like screening model (COSMO) was used to simulate the H2O solvent environment, whose dielectric constant was set to be 78.54. A smearing value of 0.005 Ha is applied to accelerate structure convergence.

Due to the large molecular size of HA (average molecular weight >10,000), direct simulation using density functional theory (DFT) techniques remains a significant challenge. Given the structural similarities between formic acid (HCOOH) and HA, particularly the presence of carboxyl groups and the computational feasibility of formic acid, it is commonly employed as a surrogate for HA in the reduction studies of Cr­(VI). This approach is supported by prior literature. For instance, Nakayama et al. also used HCOOH as a surrogate for HA in their first-principles study on the removal of humic acid by hydro-garnet compounds, demonstrating its acceptability for simulating the key interactions of HA.

The cohesion energy (E coh) of metal bulk is given by 1 Ecohesive=(Ebulk−nEatom)/n where E bulk, E atom, and n are the total energy of a bulk metal, a single metal atom, and the number of metal atoms in the bulk, respectively. To estimate the stability of the metal atoms on the N4G substrate, the binding energy (E b) was defined as 2 Eb=Esubstrate+atom−Esubstrate−Efree−atom where E substrate+atom, E substrate, and E free‑atom are the total energy of metal-atom-doped N4G system, that of pristine N4G, and that of an isolated metal atom, respectively. Furthermore, the adsorption strength between Cr­(VI) and the catalyst is described by the adsorption energy (E ad), which is given by the formular 3 Ead=Esubstrate+adsorbent−Esubstrate−Eadsorbent where E substrate+adsorbent, E substrate, and E adsorbent are the total energy of adsorption system, isolated substrate, and adsorbent, respectively. According to this definition, negative values correspond to exothermic processes. In addition, the dissolution potentials (U diss) are used to evaluate whether the catalysts can exist stably in the electrochemical environment, , which is defined as 4 Udiss=Udiss°(metal,bulk)−(Eb−Ecohesive)/ne where E b and E cohesive are the bonding energy of metal atoms in substrate and cohesion energy in bulk metal, respectively. U diss °(metal, bulk) represent the standard dissolution potential of the bulk metal and n is the number of electrons involved in the dissolution. According to our definition, U diss > 0 V is considered to be electrochemically stable.

Results and Discussion

Thermodynamic and Electrochemical Stability of the TM-N4G Catalyst

Due to their distinctive structural and electronic properties, carbon-supported single-atom catalysts (SACs) have attracted significant attention in the field of molecule catalysis. , As shown in Figure a, the vacancy defect in the carbon monolayer provides an optimal central cavity for accommodating the transition metal (TM) atoms. To identify the most appropriate catalyst for Cr­(VI) reduction, we systematically explored the potential of all 3d transition metal atoms as active centers on the TM-N4G substrate. The optimized configurations of the SAC systems and the corresponding structural parameters are provided in Figure S1 and Table S1. The vacancies in the N-doped carbon substrate offer sufficient space for accommodating the metal atoms, allowing the anchored TM atoms to integrate seamlessly into the graphene surface without significant protrusions. Upon loading the metal atoms, the average bond lengths for the TM-N bonds, N–C bonds, and C–C bonds in the carbon layer are found to be L TM‑N = 1.89–2.00 Å, L N–C = 1.36–1.38 Å, and L C–C = 1.44–1.46 Å, respectively. Although the TM-N bond length varies significantly due to the different radii of the TM atoms, the choice of different TM atoms has a minimal impact on the N–C and C–C bond lengths in the carbon layer. Meanwhile, Mulliken charge analysis reveals that the charge transfer from the TM atoms to the N-doped graphene substrate ranges from 0.48 to 1.46 |e| (Table S1), indicating that the TM atoms adopt a highly oxidized state and form strongly polarized and hybridized TM-N bonds. Given the high tendency for isolated metal atoms to aggregate, it is crucial for the catalytic substrate to provide robust bonding with highly mobile metal atoms. This is important because the aggregation of single metal atoms could lead to reduced catalytic activity or even complete deactivation. From a thermodynamic perspective, the binding energy (E b) between the substrate and the metal atom must exceed the cohesive energy (E coh) of the corresponding bulk phase to prevent metal atom aggregation. A comparison between the calculated E b and their corresponding E coh in the bulk state are shown in Figure b, where the calculated E b values for all 3d transition metal atoms anchored on the N-doped graphene substrate are negative, indicating that the formation of SAC system is exothermic and thermodynamically favorable compared to the aggregation state. Moreover, the E b values are significantly higher than the E coh values, demonstrating a strong preference for the TM atoms to exist in a single-atom form. The formation of metal clusters would require additional energy input, highlighting the stability of the single-atom configuration.

(b) Bonding energy (E b) between N-doped graphene and 3d transition metal atoms, and cohesive energy (E coh) between metal atoms in their respective bulk phases. In addition to thermodynamic stability, the electrochemical stability of the TM-N4G systems was evaluated by calculating their dissolution potentials (U diss). Given the natural potential differences between various minerals, which can form primary batteries, especially in the soil environment, SACs are also subject to electrochemical dissolution. The dissolution potential represents the minimum potential required to initiate the dissolution reaction of the catalyst, reflecting its stability under electrochemical conditions. A positive dissolution potential indicates favorable resistance to dissolution and high electrochemical stability, while a negative dissolution potential suggests increased susceptibility to dissolution. As shown in Figure c and Table S2, the U diss values for all single-atom catalysts, except for Sc, Ti, and V atoms, are positive, indicating their resistance to dissolution in soil environments.

Competition Adsorptions between CrO4 2– and Other Typical Molecules on TM-N4G

Due to the complex composition of the soil environment, strong adsorption of nontarget molecules on the active sites of the catalyst can lead to irreversible catalyst poisoning, making regeneration highly challenging. Furthermore, if the catalyst exhibits a stronger affinity for other key soil components than that for CrO4 2–, a series of side reactions may occur, further hindering the catalytic process. Both catalyst poisoning and competitive side reactions can significantly reduce the catalytic reduction performance of the catalysts for CrO4 2–. Therefore, an effective catalyst must demonstrate appropriate selectivity for CrO4 2–, ensuring that other soil components do not competitively occupy its active sites. Similarly, during the reduction of CrO4 2– by HA, the active sites must remain preferentially accessible to CrO4 2–, preventing competition with other soil constituents.

The inorganic components of soil primarily consist of minerals, water, and air, with air typically occupying approximately 50% of the total soil volume. Organic matter in soil predominantly exists in the form of HA. To investigate potential side reactions and catalyst poisoning issues, the E ad of CrO4 2– as well as other typical inorganic substances (O2, N2, and H2O) and organic substances (HA) were considered. Figure S2 illustrates the most stable adsorption configurations for CrO4 2– and other typical soil components. All of the CrO4 2–, O2, N2, H2O, and HA incline to adsorb above the metal atoms of the TM-N4G catalyst, with the metal atoms slightly protruding due to strong interactions with the adsorbates. This suggests that the incorporated single metal atom, serving as the active center, exhibits a high affinity for the target molecule. Specifically, CrO4 2– binds to the transition metal atoms in TM-N4G via oxygen atoms (Oa) in CrO4 2–, with bond lengths (L TM‑Oa) ranging from 1.81 Å to 2.23 Å (Figure S2). Additionally, the Cr–Oa bond length (1.69 Å to 1.78 Å) is notably longer than the Cr–O bond length (1.63 Å) in the original CrO4 2– species, indicating the activation of CrO4 2– after adsorbing on the SAC. Importantly, as depicted in Figure , the E ad of CrO4 2– on TM-N4G is substantially higher than that of other typical soil components, which is probably due to the electrostatic attraction between the positively charged metal atoms and the negatively charged CrO4 2–. These results demonstrate that the TM-N4G systems (TM = Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn) exhibit high selectivity for CrO4 2–.

Comparison of E ad of CrO4 2– and HCOOH, O2, N2, and H2O on TM-N4G.

To reveal the strong interactions between CrO4 2– and the substrate, Figure illustrates the charge density difference diagrams for the adsorption of CrO4 2– on TM-N4G catalysts. The regions of electron depletion and accumulation are represented by yellow and blue regions, respectively. Notably, the electron depletion region is predominantly localized on the O atoms of CrO4 2– that are not bonded to the TM atom, while the electron accumulation region is mainly localized on the O atom of CrO4 2– that is close to the TM atom. Mulliken charge analysis indicates that 0.48, 0.52, 0.41, and 0.46 e are transferred from CrO4 2– to Cr–N4G, Mn–N4G, Fe–N4G, and Co–N4G, which are significantly larger than those for Ni–N4G, Cu–N4G, and Zn–N4G catalysts (0.23, 0.26, and 0.31 e). Accordingly, the former exhibit stronger affinity toward CrO4 2– compared to the latter, which is consistent with the TM-O bond lengths (1.85, 1.81, 1.88, 1.89, 2.23, 2.08, and 1.95 Å for Cr–N4G, Mn–N4G, Fe–N4G, Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, Cu–N4G, and Zn–N4G).

Consistent with the requirement for catalytically active sites to selectively target Cr­(VI) in complex soil environments, the resulting TM-N4G/Cr­(VI) system must also exhibit a high selectivity toward HCOOH in subsequent catalytic reactions. To investigate potential side reactions and catalyst poisoning issues, the E ad values of HCOOH as well as other typical inorganic substances (O2, N2, H2O) were considered. Figure S3 illustrates the most energetically favorable adsorption configuration for subsequent HA adsorption on TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn). Upon adsorption, the hydrogen atoms (H) from the −OH and −CH groups of HA are positioned close to two oxygen atoms (O) of CrO4 2–. In Cr–N4G/CrO4 2–, Mn–N4G/CrO4 2–, Co–N4G/CrO4 2–, and Zn–N4G/CrO4 2– systems, the distance between the H of −OH and the O of CrO4 2– (1.46, 1.47, 1.41, 1.44 Å) is significantly shorter than that between the H of −CH and the O of CrO4 2– (2.47, 2.36, 2.54, 2.49 Å), indicating that the −OH group of HA serves as the primary active site. Notably, the H of −OH would spontaneously transfer from HA to the O of CrO4 2– when it was adsorbed on the surface of Fe–N4G/CrO4 2–, Ni–N4G/CrO4 2–, and Cu–N4G/CrO4 2– catalysts. Figure presents the E ad of HCOOH, O2, N2, and H2O on TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn). The E ad of HA molecule on TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn) are −0.24 eV and −0.40 eV, respectively, which are close or even smaller than the E ad for O2 (−0.43 eV, −0.40 eV). These results suggest that in TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn), the active center may be covered by the O2 molecule in the soil, leading to degenerated activity for CrO4 2– reduction. In contrast, in the SAC system of Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn, the catalysts exhibit significant selectivity for the HA molecule, thereby promoting interactions between HA and CrO4 2– and facilitating their subsequent reduction process.

The standard DFT calculations are typically performed under idealized conditions at zero-point temperature, which limits their ability to accurately describe the adsorption behavior of molecules in soil under realistic conditions. To account for the influence of actual temperature on adsorption performance, the relative energy (E r) was calculated to evaluate the adsorption capacity of soil molecules at room temperature 5 Er=Esubstrate+adsorbent−Esubstrate−n(Eadsorbent+μadsorbent(T,P)) Here, E substrate+adsorbent, E substrate, and E adsorbent represent the total energy of the systems for adsorbing soil molecules, the adsorption substrate, and the total energy of soil molecules, respectively. μadsorbent (T, P) represents the chemical potential of different soil molecules at specific temperatures and pressure. μadsorbent (T, P) is defined as 6 μadsorbent(T,P)=ΔH−TΔS+kBTln(PP0) where, k B represents the Boltzmann constant, ΔH and ΔS denote the changes in enthalpy and entropy, respectively, calculated from the thermodynamic parameter table and first-principles calculation, and P 0 is the standard pressure of 0.1 MPa. According to the formula, when the E r value becomes positive, the soil molecules will spontaneously desorb from the substrate. Figure illustrates the relative energy (E r) of adsorption for HCOOH, O2, N2, and H2O molecules on TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) under standard pressure (P = 0.1 MPa) and elevated temperatures. As shown in Figure a,b, for TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn), HCOOH desorbs prior to O2, occurring at 129 and 191 K, respectively. This indicates that under ambient temperature and pressure, HCOOH cannot be stably adsorbed on the surface of TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Cr, Mn). In contrast, Figure c–g reveals that for TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn), O2, N2, and H2O desorb completely at 300 K, while HCOOH desorbs at 375 K, 346 K, 443 K, 402, and 361 K, respectively. These results demonstrate that TM-N4G/CrO4 2– (TM = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, or Zn) exhibits high selectivity and excellent adsorption performance for HCOOH at room temperature. Consequently, the following section focuses on the catalytic reduction of CrO4 2– by HA using Fe–N4G, Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, Cu–N4G, and Zn–N4G catalysts.

Electronic Property of TM-N4G (TM = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn)

The incorporation of transition metals into carbon systems may lead to the emergence of potential magnetism due to the presence of unpaired electrons. Emergence of magnetism in the catalyst system is beneficial for the recyclability of the catalyst in soil environments, as it allows for effective recovery using an external magnet. In Figure , the asymmetric partial density of states (PDOS) for Fe–N4G, Co–N4G, and Cu–N4G indicate that the considered catalyst exhibits certain magnetic properties. Specifically, the calculated magnetic moments for Fe–N4G, Co–N4G, and Cu–N4G are 1.82 μB, 0.74 μB, and 0.93 μB, respectively. In contrast, the spin-up and spin-down electron states for the Ni–N4G and Zn–N4G systems are completely symmetric, indicating that these systems are nonmagnetic. Moreover, the total density of states (TDOS) of all TM-N4G systems spans the Fermi energy level, confirming their metallic behavior and favorable electrical conductivity, which is essential for ensuring efficient electron transfer during the Cr­(VI) reduction reaction process. The electron localization function (ELF) of TM-N4G (TM = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) catalysts are depicted in Figure . An ELF value between 0 and 0.5 indicates ionic bonding; a value of 0.5 signifies metallic bonding; and values ranging from 0.5 to 1 suggest covalent bonding. As shown in Figure , the ELF values for both the TM-N and N–C bonds in TM-N4G exceed 0.5, demonstrating that the anchoring of the TM atoms to the carbon substrate primarily involves covalent interactions.

Reduction Mechanism of Cr(VI) to Cr(II)

The complete reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(II) proceeds via a two-step process. In the first step, Cr­(VI) is reduced to Cr­(IV), as described in eq : Cr­(VI) + HCOOH→Cr­(IV) + CO2; In the second step, Cr­(IV) is further reduced to Cr­(II), following eq : Cr­(IV) + HCOOH→Cr­(II) + CO2. The potential reaction pathways for Cr­(VI) reduction on TM-N4G catalysts (TM = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) are illustrated in Figure . This reduction process is driven by the transfer of hydrogen (H) atoms from the hydroxyl (−OH) and methylene (−CH) groups in humic acid (HA) to CrO4 2–. Previous studies have demonstrated that the reduction of Cr­(VI) by various organic reducing agentssuch as organic acids, alcohols, and tartaric acidinvolves not only sequential single-electron transfers but also occurrences of simultaneous two-electron transfer. Accordingly, in this study, two distinct mechanisms are proposed to govern H atom transfer during the process. The first one is a stepwise mechanism, where hydrogen atoms are sequentially transferred from the −OH and −CH groups in HA to the oxygen (O) atoms coordinated with Cr. The other mechanism is the synergistic mechanism, where two hydrogen atoms are simultaneously transferred from the −OH and −CH groups in HA to the O atoms coordinated with Cr.

Reduction Mechanism of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) by the First HA

Figure illustrates the potential reaction energy profiles for CrO4 2– to H2CrO4 2– on the surface of TM-N4G (TM = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn), along with the corresponding intermediate configurations. For the sake of clarity, the hydrogen and oxygen atoms in the hydroxyl (−OH) group are designated as H1 and O1, respectively, as illustrated in Figure a. The remaining oxygen and hydrogen atoms in Figure a are labeled as O2 and H2. In the reaction pathways depicted in Figure , the transition from state A to state B indicates the adsorption of the first HA molecule. Notably, upon adsorption on Fe–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Cu–N4G surfaces (Figures b–d), the H1 atom of HA spontaneously transfers to the Oa site of CrO4 2–, forming HCrO4 2–, with adsorption energies (E ad) of −0.85 eV, −1.11 eV, and −0.98 eV, respectively. For Fe–N4G, the subsequent reduction of HCrO4 2– to H2CrO4 2– follows pathway B → TS1BC → C, wherein the H2 atom is transferred to the Ob atom of HCrO4 2–. At the transition state (TS1BC), the H2···Ob distance contracts from 2.64 to 1.14 Å, overcoming an E bar of 1.40 eV. Then, the H2···Ob distance further decreases to 0.98 Å, leading to the formation of a stable H–O bond, which comes to the final states (C). The subsequent desorption of the first HA molecule is an endothermic process (C → D), with an energy cost of 0.25 eV. In contrast, in the Ni–N4G and Cu–N4G systems, the H2 atom is initially positioned too far from the O atom of HCrO4 2– to enable immediate transfer. Therefore, a reorientation step (B → C) is required, which requires an energy cost of 0.09 and 0.01 eV for Ni–N4G and Cu–N4G, respectively. The small energy penalties indicate that this reconfiguration occurs with remarkable ease. As a result, the H2 atom gets close to the Ob atom, and the distance turns out to be 2.52 Å for Ni–N4G/HCrO4 2– and 2.62 Å for Cu–N4G/HCrO4 2–. For Ni–N4G, the reaction of HCrO4 2– to H2CrO4 2– follows pathway C → TS1CD → D, where the H2 atom was transferred to the Ob atom of HCrO4 2–. At the transition state (TS1CD), the H2···Ob distance decreases from 2.52 Å to 1.15 Å, which need to conquer an E bar of 0.49 eV. This distance subsequently contracts to 0.98 Å, forming a stable H–O bond (state D). The final step, the desorption of the first HA molecule (D → E), has a relatively low energy cost of 0.14 eV. Similarly, for Cu–N4G, the reaction proceeds via C → TS1CD → D, where the H2 atom is transferred to the Ob atom of HCrO4 2–. At the transition state (TS1CD), the H2···Ob distance decreases from 2.62 Å to 1.66 Å, requiring an E bar of 2.21 eV. Then, the H2···Ob distance further contracts to 0.98 Å, leading to a stable H–O bond in state D. The desorption barrier of the first HA molecule (D → E) in Cu–N4G exhibit a barrier of 0.12 eV, comparable to that of Ni–N4G (0.14 eV).

In contrast to Fe–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Cu–N4G, the adsorption of HA on Zn–N4G and Co–N4G does not involve direct H atom transfer (Figure e,f). Instead, the H1 atom of the −OH group and the H2 atom of the −CH group in HA merely approach two O atoms of CrO4 2– without the formation of distinct new bonds. Specifically, after adsorption, the H1···Oa distance is 1.44 Å for Zn–N4G and 1.41 Å for Co–N4G, whereas the H2···Ob distances are 2.49 and 2.54 Å, respectively. Consequently, the adsorption of the HA molecule occurs primarily via interactions between its −OH group and the oxygen atom of CrO4 2–. Moreover, the E ad of the first HA on the Zn–N4G/CrO4 2– (−0.82 eV) and Co–N4G/CrO4 2– (−0.84 eV) are slightly lower than those for Ni–N4G/CrO4 2– (−1.11 eV), Cu–N4G/CrO4 2– (−0.98 eV), and Fe–N4G/CrO4 2– (−0.85 eV) system. Unlike the step-by-step hydrogen transfer mechanism observed in Fe–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Cu–N4G, a synergistic mechanism dominates the reduction process in the Zn–N4G and Co–N4G catalyst. For Zn–N4G, the hydrogen transfer follows the pathway B → TS1BC → C, during which the H1 atom and H2 atom are simultaneously transferred to the Oa and Ob atom of CrO4 2–. At the transition state (TS1BC), the H1···Oa distance decreases from 1.44 Å (state B) to 1.00 Å, and the H2···Ob distance decreases from 2.49 to 1.11 Å, which need to conquer an E bar of 1.22 eV. Thereafter, both distances further shorten to 0.98 Å, forming two stable H–O bonds in final states (C). The subsequent desorption of the first HA molecule, expressed as C → D, require an energy input of 0.28 eV. In contrast, the reduction of CrO4 2– to H2CrO4 2– on Co–N4G can occur via either a synergistic mechanism (B → TS1BC → C) or step-by-step mechanism (B → TS2BE → E → TS3EC → C). In the synergistic mechanism, the H1···Oa distance decreases from 1.41 to 1.11 Å and the H2···Ob distance decreases from 2.54 to 1.23 Å, with a corresponding E bar of 0.59 eV at TS1BC. Alternatively, the step-by-step pathway involves an initial hydrogen transfer (TS2BE) from H1 to Oa with a minimal barrier of 0.02 eV, resulting in an intermediate states (E) characterized by the formation of HCrO4 2– with a H1–Oa bond length of 1.11 Å. The subsequent transfer of H2 to Ob (TS3EC) requires an E bar of 1.50 eV, with the resulting H2···Ob decreases from 2.66 to 1.14 Å. The lower barrier for the initial hydrogen transfer relative to the second indicates that the −CH group is less reactive than the −OH group during Cr­(VI) reduction. This finding is consistent with the reports on Cr­(VI) reduction by ethanol and ethanediol on the surface of montmorillonite. Thereafter, both distances further shorten to 0.98 Å, forming two stable H–O bonds in final states (C). C → D is the desorption of the first HA molecule in this system, with an energy change of 0.19 eV. Overall, the synergistic mechanism (with an E bar of 0.59 eV) is more favorable for CrO4 2– reduction than the step-by-step mechanism (E bar is 1.50 eV) in the Co–N4G system.

Competition Adsorptions between the Second HA and other Soil Molecules on TM-N4G/H2CrO4 2–

Prior to investigating the reduction of H2CrO4 2– by the second HA, we have examined the competitive adsorption behavior of HA and typical inorganic soil constituents (O2, N2, H2O) on the TM-N4G/H2CrO4 2– system. As shown in Figure S4, the E ad values of the second HA on Fe–N4G/H2CrO4 2–(−0.81 eV) and Cu–N4G/H2CrO4 2–(−0.85 eV) were higher than those for N2 (−0.21 and −0.21 eV, respectively) and H2O (−0.45 and −0.39 eV, respectively) but lower than that for O2 (−1.01 and −0.97 eV, respectively). These findings suggest that an oxygen-rich environment could saturate the catalyst surface and significantly hinder the adsorption of the second HA, thereby inhibiting the reduction of H2CrO4 2– to H4CrO4 2– in the Fe–N4G/H2CrO4 2– and Cu–N4G/H2CrO4 2– systems. In contrast, the adsorption energies for the second HA on TM-N4G/H2CrO4 2– (TM = Co, Ni, Zn) are −0.79, −0.95, and −0.86 eV, respectively, which exceed those for O2 (−0.67, −0.84, and −0.61 eV), N2 (−0.04, −0.19, and −0.18 eV), and H2O (−0.40, −0.43, and −0.44 eV). Thus, no significant competitive adsorption is observed in the Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G systems, which exhibit high selectivity for the second HA molecules.

Reduction Mechanism of Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) by the Second HA

Figure illustrates the reaction pathways for the reduction of H2CrO4 2– to H4CrO4 2– by the second HA molecule on Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G systems, with the corresponding transition state and intermediate configurations provided in the inset. The reaction starts with the adsorption of the second HA molecules (A → B), during which the H3 atom of the −OH group and the H4 atom of the −CH group approach two O atoms on H2CrO4 2–. Upon adsorption, the H3···Oc distance become 1.47, 1.48, and 1.44 Å, while the H4···Od distances are 2.79, 2.49, and 2.43 Å for Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G, respectively. The calculated E ad of the second HA adsorption on Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G are −0.79, −0.95, and −0.86 eV, respectively, indicating that van der Waals interactions predominate in the adsorption process. In contrast to the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV), the synergistic mechanism operates during the reaction of Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) in the Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G systems. For Co–N4G, the reaction pathway is denoted as B → TS1BC → C, wherein the H3 atom is transferred to the Oc atom and the H4 atom to the Od atom of H2CrO4 2– simultaneously. At the transition state (TS1BC), the H3···Oc distance decreases from 1.47 Å to 1.13 Å and the H4···Od distance from 2.79 Å to 1.15 Å, corresponding to an E bar of 1.80 eV. Subsequently, both distance contract to 0.98 Å, forming two stable H–O bonds in the final states (C). The desorption of the second HA molecule is also energy favorable, requiring an energy input of 0.26 eV. The reaction pathway for the reduction of H2CrO4 2– to H4CrO4 2– in the Ni–N4G system is analogous: during TS1BC, the H3···Oc distance diminishes from 1.48 Å to 1.00 Å and the H4···Od distance from 2.49 Å to 1.01 Å, which need to overcome an E bar of 1.73 eV. Then, the H3–Oc and H4–Od bonds forms with a bond length of 0.98 Å in final states (C). The desorption of the second HA molecule in Ni–N4G (0.26 eV) is also the same with that in Co–N4G. For the Zn–N4G, the reaction pathway is comparable, although the E bar for reduction of Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) is 1.35 eV, significantly lower than those observed for Co–N4G and Ni–N4G.

During the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) by the first HA molecule, the Co–N4G and Ni–N4G catalyst systems exhibit notably lower E bar (0.59 and 0.49 eV, respectively) than those reported for alcohols on the surface of montmorillonite (0.78–0.91 eV). Consequently, when employed as catalyst, Co–N4G and Ni–N4G (with E bar is 0.59 and 0.49 eV, respectively) can enable the rapid conversion of CrO4 2– to H2CrO4 2– by the HA in the soil. In contrast, during the reduction of Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) by the second HA molecule, the Zn–N4G catalyst displays the lowest E bar (1.35 eV), which is significantly smaller than those of the Co–N4G (1.80 eV) and Ni–N4G (1.73 eV) systems. Nevertheless, the overall conversion from Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) proceeds much more slowly than the initial reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) owing to its higher barrier. Furthermore, the reduction efficiency of this carbon-based catalyst appears to temperature dependent, with enhanced activity at elevated temperatures (e.g., summer) compared to lower temperatures (e.g., winter). The diminished activity of the Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) step compared with Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) may be ascribed to variations in the TM-substrate interactions or electron transfer therein, as evidenced by the gradual elongation of the TM-OCr bond length throughout the reduction of Cr­(VI) → Cr­(IV) → Cr­(II).

Partial Density of States (PDOS) Analysis

To further elucidate the mechanism involved in the reduced activity observed during the Cr­(IV) → Cr­(II) transition, the partial density of states (PDOS) for the adsorption of the first HA molecule (in the Cr­(VI) → Cr­(IV) step) and that of the second HA (the Cr­(IV) → Cr­(II) step) were analyzed, given that HA adsorption and activation constitute the initial stages of chromium reduction. As shown in Figure , the red dotted regions indicate the orbital hybridizations among TM-N4G/CrO4 2– or TM-N4G/H2CrO4 2– (TM = Co, Ni, Zn), and the adsorbed HA molecule. Notably, the orbital hybridization regions for the adsorption of the second HA molecule (Cr­(IV) → Cr­(II)) shift away from the Fermi level compared to those of the first one (Cr­(VI) → Cr­(IV)). For instance, in the Co–N4G/CrO4 2– system (Figure a), the orbital hybridizations occur in the ranges of −6.43 eV ∼ −4.59 eV, and −3.14 eV ∼ −0.30 eV during the adsorption of the first HA molecule, whereas for the second HA, the orbital hybridizations appears in the ranges of −7.04 eV ∼ −6.25 eV and −3.70 eV ∼ −1.92 eV (Figure d). Similar observations are also noted in the Ni–N4G and Zn–N4G systems. These observations indicate that the second HA molecule is less activated during the Cr­(IV) → Cr­(II) transition, which leads to the higher energy barrier observed above. This conclusion is further supported by the notably lower electron transfer values observed during the adsorption of the second HA molecule compared to the first one. Specifically, the electron transfer from H atoms of the −OH group decreased from 0.39 and 0.41 e (the first HA) to 0.08 and 0.39 e (the second HA) for Co–N4G and Zn–N4G, respectively. Similarly, the electron transfer from H atoms of the −CH group decreased from 0.06 e (for both catalysts) to 0.02 and 0.06 e for Co–N4G and Zn–N4G, respectively.

Conclusions

In this study, the feasibility of employing TM-N4Gs as catalysts for Cr­(VI) reduction was systematically investigated through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Our findings reveal that the nitrogen-doped vacancy in graphene plays a crucial role in anchoring 3d transition metal (TM) atoms (Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn), which serve as the active sites for Cr­(VI) reduction. Among the considered systems, Co–N4G, Ni–N4G, and Zn–N4G catalysts exhibit favorable structural and electrochemical stability. Furthermore, these catalysts demonstrate distinct selective adsorption toward Cr­(VI), as evidenced by their significantly higher E ad values relative to other common soil constituents. During the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) by the first HA, the Co–N4G and Ni–N4G catalyst systems exhibit lower E bar of 0.59 and 0.49 eV, respectively, compared to the Zn–N4G system (E bar = 1.22 eV). These results indicate that when Co–N4G and Ni–N4G are employed as catalysts, CrO4 2– can be rapidly reduced to H2CrO4 2– by the first HA. In contrast, during the subsequent reduction of Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) by the second HA, the E bar for TM-N4G (TM = Co, Ni, Zn) increase to 1.80, 1.73, and 1.35 eV, respectively. These values are notably higher than those observed for the reduction of the Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) process, and Zn–N4G exhibits the highest activity toward the Cr­(IV) to Cr­(II) reduction. Partial density of states (PDOS) analyses indicate that the elevated energy barrier associated with the Cr­(VI) to Cr­(IV) transition arises from a diminished activation of the second HA molecules on the catalyst. These results demonstrate that the combined use of TM-N4G (TM = Co, Ni, Zn) can serve as an efficient catalyst for the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(II) with high activity.
