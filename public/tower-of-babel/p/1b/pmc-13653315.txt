Culture, power and practice: Critical discourse analysis of workplace‐based assessments in hierarchical postgraduate medical education
Authors: Qamar Riaz, Dominique Waterval, Carolin Sehlbach, A. T. G. (Aggie) Paulus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWorkplace‐based assessments (WPBAs) are widely promoted as formative tools to promote learning in postgraduate medical education. However, their meaning and effectiveness are deeply influenced by sociocultural and institutional contexts, particularly hierarchy and power relations. Understanding how these dynamics shape WPBA discourses remains underexplored. This study examines how residents' discourse about WPBAs reflects and reproduces hierarchical relations in Pakistan.MethodsThis qualitative study employed Fairclough's three‐dimensional Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) framework to examine WPBA discourses as reflected through residents' language‐in‐use. Data were collected through semi‐structured interviews and focus group discussions with senior medical residents from diverse specialties and institutions across Pakistan. Analysis focussed on textual features (micro level), discursive practices (meso level) and broader sociocultural structures (macro level).ResultsThree themes were identified: (1) WPBAs for Surveillance and Control, where assessments were linked to reputational risk and gatekeeping rather than learning; (2) Hierarchy and Fear of Judgement, highlighted how norms of deference, silence and emotional restraint shaped assessment interactions; and (3) Strategic Compliance to Survive WPBAs, where residents employed adaptive behaviours and self‐regulation to minimise risk of reputational damage, loss of clinical opportunities or strained faculty relationships. These findings illustrate how WPBAs are not neutral tools but culturally situated practices that reflect and reinforce institutional power dynamics.DiscussionOur findings indicate that culturally sensitive adaptation of WPBAs requires more than faculty training or checklist refinement; it should consider the communicative norms and power dynamics of everyday clinical education. When assessments are shaped by fear, reputational risk or hierarchical control, WPBAs can undermine their intended developmental purpose. This cautions against uncritical adoption of WPBA frameworks across contexts and emphasises culturally responsive approaches that foster psychological safety, dialogue and genuine learner development. Otherwise, in hierarchical contexts, WPBAs risk functioning as mechanisms of control rather than as tools for feedback and growth.

INTRODUCTION

Workplace‐based assessments (WPBAs) are widely promoted as formative tools intended to support competency development through direct observation, feedback and reflection in postgraduate medical education. 1 However, WPBAs are often reported to fall short of their formative intent, perceived less as opportunities for learning and more as bureaucratic or high‐stakes requirements. 2 , 3 Such challenges extend beyond procedural design and instead reflect the complex sociocultural and relational dynamics of the contexts in which WPBAs are enacted. 4 , 5

A growing body of literature positions WPBAs as social practices embedded within institutional cultures, shaped by professional hierarchies, learner–supervisor relationships and communication expectations. 6 , 7 , 8 Central to these dynamics are hierarchy and power. Hierarchy in postgraduate medical education refers to the formal structuring of authority within clinical training, whereas power distance reflects how such authority and inequalities are normalised and reproduced in interaction. 9 , 10 However, hierarchies within academic and professional settings do not necessarily mirror broader societal structures; rather, institutional culture shapes how authority and deference are enacted. 5 From this perspective, hierarchy and power distance are not fixed cultural traits, as suggested in culture‐as‐dimension models, but are dynamically produced through culturally and institutionally patterned ways of speaking and interacting within specific training environments. 11 , 12 , 13 Although professional hierarchies exist globally, their expression in authority, supervision and feedback is shaped by institutional design, regulatory frameworks, and professional norms, and therefore varies across institutions and training systems, within and across countries, influencing whether WPBAs enable dialogue and learning or become compliance‐driven exercises constrained by caution, emotional restraint, and impression management. 14 , 15 , 16

Studies across contexts demonstrate that institutional structures and professional norms emphasising deference to seniority limit feedback exchange and learner agency during WPBAs. 13 , 17 , 18 Even where hierarchies are less explicit, entrenched assessment processes and assessor practices may reproduce subtle power asymmetries, influencing how learners engage with WPBAs. 4 , 7 , 19 , 20 This may inadvertently undermine their developmental intent and instead reinforce silence, compliance and risk‐avoidant behaviours. 4 , 15 , 16 Institutional priorities, such as performance signalling, politeness and documentation compliance, may further constrain the formative intent of WPBAs. 8 , 21 These findings position WPBAs as culturally and institutionally situated social practices, whose meanings and uses are shaped by prevailing norms of authority and communication in the context in which they are enacted. Without examining how these dynamics shape WPBA interactions, reform efforts risk focussing on tools and documentation while leaving underlying power relations intact, limiting WPBAs' developmental intent.

This is particularly important in the context of Pakistani postgraduate medical education, where WPBAs have recently been introduced under competency‐based reforms by the national accrediting body College of Physicians and Surgeons Pakistan (CPSP). Residency training in Pakistan typically spans 4 to 6 years following a 5‐year undergraduate medical degree. It is largely apprenticeship‐based and embedded within close, prolonged supervisory relationships with senior consultants who control access to clinical exposure, evaluation and professional advancement. Within such arrangements, assessment and feedback encounters are inseparable from hierarchical power‐laden relationships that influence how residents seek, interpret, and act upon feedback. 20 Understanding how power operates within these interactions is therefore essential to understand how and why the developmental and formative intent of WPBAs may be reshaped in practice. 22

Prior studies have shown that context influences learner experiences around WPBAs, 8 , 12 , 13 and learners navigate WPBA interactions through strategies, such as selective assessment‐seeking or impressions management, shaped by relational and contextual factors. 4 , 23 However, these experiences have rarely been examined as discourses, that is, ways of speaking and interacting, that both reflects and shapes hierarchy, power and ideology within WPBA encounters. 24

Because hierarchy and power are enacted in everyday interaction, discourse serves as the mechanism through which these are normalised, negotiated and contested. Residents' talk, therefore, offers insights into how WPBAs are enacted in practice, how authority is positioned and managed, and how institutional culture shapes formative intent.

While culture‐as‐dimension models (e.g. power distance frameworks) offer useful heuristics, they risk oversimplifying complex institutional realities by portraying hierarchy as a stable cultural attribute rather than a contextually enacted practice. 11 , 25 Instead, we conceptualise hierarchy as culturally and institutionally produced through professional norms, institutional traditions, and communicative practices, and employ Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) to examine how such hierarchies are reflected and reproduced in residents' talks. CDA attends not only to what is said but also to how it is said, revealing implicit meanings, hierarchies, power relations and institutional values embedded in WPBA practices. 24 , 26 CDA has previously been used in medical education to study clinical teachers' power in curricula, 27 discursive construction of CBME 28 and the discourse surrounding mental health. 29 Guided by this perspective, we ask: How do residents in Pakistan construct and navigate WPBAs through their discourse?

METHODS

Study design

This qualitative study was informed by a critical realist stance, which recognises that hierarchy and power structures are materially real in their effects yet discursively constructed and sustained through language and social interaction. 30 We adopted Fairclough's three‐dimensional model of CDA for its ability to link language‐in‐use with institutional practice and sociocultural structures. 28 Unlike Foucauldian discourse analysis that focusses on the historical discursive formation, Fairclough's framework enables examination of how lived (hierarchical) communicative practices shape and are enacted through WPBA practices and institutional norms. 31 , 32

Study setting and participants

Discourses may be examined through both written texts and spoken narratives, including interview transcripts, whichever provides the most analytically rich evidence. 31 In this study, we focused on residents' narratives as primary texts of analysis, examining how language reflects individual experiences and the institutional and sociocultural contexts shaping WPBA practices. 31

At the time of data collection, WPBAs were not yet mandated by CPSP and were implemented in only a few programmes as institutional or programmatic initiative. 33 , 34 In most settings, a specified number of WPBAs were required within defined time periods; however, quantity, frequency, documentation and feedback practices, and their use in progression decisions varied across programmes. 35 Consequently, a snowball sampling strategy was employed. Program directors (PDs) at QR's institution were initially approached and asked to connect with PDs from other institutions conducting WPBAs, who then shared contact details of eligible residents. We purposively recruited senior residents with at least 1 year of WPBA experience to ensure extended exposure to WPBA encounters and the ability to provide information‐rich accounts. Such experiences enabled reflection on hierarchies, power relations and institutional practices surrounding WPBAs—aspects central to a critical discourse perspective. 24 , 36 Participants were selected from multiple disciplines and institutes across Pakistan to ensure diversity in specialty, gender and institute type (public versus private), enabling a broad exploration of WPBA experiences. Eligible residents were contacted via email or text message; all except one, who was on clinical rotation, provided written informed consent for voluntary participation and were included in the study.

Data collection

We used a combination of semi‐structured interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) using a literature‐informed interview guide to explore how residents discursively constructed their experiences with WPBAs. 37 First, QR conducted in‐person or Zoom‐based interviews with residents from diverse training institutions across Pakistan. Lasting 35–60 min, the one‐on‐one format encouraged participants to share personal experiences freely. 23 Subsequently, QR facilitated FGDs with residents from the Aga Khan University (AKU). Each discussion lasting 60–90 min enabled residents to collectively reflect on shared experiences, surfacing commonly held beliefs, language patterns and emotional responses that shaped group‐level discourses around WPBA practices in a shared context. 38 This sequencing, from individual interviews to FGDs, enabled a layered understanding of both private and collective constructions of meaning.

All sessions were conducted in a mix of English and Urdu based on participant comfort in expressing their thoughts and emotions. As English is the primary language of medical education and formal communication in Pakistani postgraduate training, most interviews were conducted predominantly in English, with occasional shifts into Urdu. Urdu excerpts were translated into English by QR, who is fluent in both languages, with attention to preserving contextual meaning.

All interviews and FGDs were audio‐recorded with informed consent, transcribed verbatim and checked for accuracy. Identifiers were replaced with pseudonyms prior to analysis. All audio files and transcripts were stored on encrypted institutional data servers accessible only to the research team. Data collection and discourse‐oriented analysis proceeded concurrently in keeping with CDA's iterative, hermeneutic logic. 24 Data collection ceased when additional interviews no longer generated new discursive patterns or subject positions relevant to the research question, consistent with CDA's commitment to analytical depth over breadth. 39 , 40 , 41

Data analysis

We applied Fairclough's three‐dimensional CDA model as the analytic framework, moving iteratively across micro (text), meso (discursive practice) and macro (social practice) levels rather than treating them as sequential or independent stages. This approach enabled us to connect residents' language‐in‐use with broader institutional structures and norms shaping WPBA practices. 24 , 42

Analysis was inductive and cyclical, involving repeated movement between the three levels. Initial coding was conducted independently by QR and DW on a subset of transcripts, followed by regular discussions to refine codes, ensuring interpretations remained grounded in participants' narratives and aligned with a critical discourse approach. Codes were then developed into broader discursive themes through iterative comparison across interviews and focus groups, prioritising explanatory sufficiency rather than frequency. Regular analytic discussions refined categories and ensured coherence across micro, meso and macro levels of analysis. Final themes were refined after several rounds of analytic deliberation with the full research team.

Analysis began at the micro level, with transcript familiarisation and line‐by‐line coding to identify recurrent linguistic features such as metaphors, pronoun shifts (e.g. shifts between ‘I,’ ‘we’ and ‘they’) as indicators of how residents positioned themselves in relation to assessor, WPBA tools, and institutional authority, without attributing causal or cultural explanations.

At the meso level, we examined how these linguistic patterns functioned within routine WPBA interactions and institutional communication practices. Here, the focus shifted from individual experiences to shared ways of talking about WPBAs, such as how WPBA encounters were organised; how feedback was delivered and responded to; and how norms of deference, silence, or impression management reflected and sustained interactional norms.

Analysis was then extended to the macro level, where discursive practices identified at the micro and meso levels were interpreted in relation to broader institutional and sociocultural structures through which WPBA practices are sustained. This included postgraduate training arrangements, WPBA requirements, and professional and cultural expectations surrounding seniority and authority.

Rigour and ethical considerations

Rigour was achieved through a transparent and systematic application of Fairclough's framework, incorporating iterative interpretation, reflexivity, and explicit linkage between data and interpretation using textual excerpts, in line with guidance for discourse analytic research. 43 , 44 Member checking was conducted by sharing a summary of preliminary findings with participants via email; several affirmed that interpretations reflected their views, while others did not respond.

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki: participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. The participants' anonymity was ensured by assigning codes, and informed written consent was obtained. Approval for the study was granted by the institutional Ethics Review Committee (ERC# 2023‐7623‐25738).

ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2025) was used to refine the language of this manuscript, including rephrasing for clarity, coherence and flow. All intellectual content, analysis and interpretations remain entirely the author's original work, and all AI‐assisted edits were critically reviewed for accuracy and integrity.

Reflexivity

The research team brought complementary perspectives that strengthened analytical rigour. QR, a medical graduate and health professions educationist involved in WPBA implementation at AKU, contributed contextual insight into local assessment practices. She does not hold a supervisory or evaluative role with the participants, minimising the risk of coercion or power imbalance. However, her professional familiarity with some participants may have restricted openness during interviews or FGDs. To mitigate this, QR emphasised confidentiality and voluntary participation.

QR's positionality as principal investigator, institutional insider, and translator shaped both data generation and analysis. Her familiarity with the clinical culture and fluency in English and Urdu facilitated rapport and nuanced interpretation of culturally embedded meanings, tone and emotion. At the same time, this carried the risk of bias, including normalising hierarchical power relations or selective interpretation. These risks were addressed through iterative team reflection and cross checking of interpretations.

DW and CS, both PhD‐trained experts in qualitative research and health professions education, provided external perspectives that challenged QR's interpretations. AP, a senior professor with multidisciplinary expertise, provided independent review of data and interpretations.

We also acknowledge that prior exposure to the culture‐as‐dimension model and Global North/Global South framings of hierarchy could have shaped early analytic assumptions. Through iterative team discussion, we deliberately grounded interpretations in interactional dynamics evident in the data, thus representing hierarchy as an emergent and relational feature enacted within specific institutional practices, rather than as a predefined cultural attribute.

RESULTS

Nine semi‐structured interviews and two FGDs (with seven and six participants in FGD 1 and 2, respectively) were conducted to capture a range of discourses related to WPBAs. The demographic details of the participants are provided as Supporting Information.

The analysis of resident narratives revealed that WPBAs are deeply embedded within institutional hierarchies and power structures, shaping how trainees engage with and experience the assessment process. The findings highlight three key themes: (1) WPBAs for surveillance and control, (2) hierarchy and fear of judgement, and (3) strategic compliance to survive WPBAs; each of them is discussed in detail hereafter, illustrated by resident quotes. The supporting quotes are labelled as I (Interview) or FGD (Focus Group Discussion) to denote the method of data collection, while ‘R’ refers to ‘resident’.

Although analysis proceeded inductively from micro‐level linguistic features to meso‐level discursive practices and macro‐level social practices, findings are presented in a top‐down manner. This structure foregrounds the broader social functions and power relations surrounding WPBAs, before demonstrating how these are enacted through everyday interaction and language use. Presenting findings in this way reflects the critical orientation of Fairclough's CDA, in which discourse is examined for how it reproduces institutional power relations, while maintaining analytic grounding in textual evidence.

WPBAs for surveillance and control (macro‐level: social practice)

At the macro level, the analysis examined how broader institutional structures, hierarchical norms and assessment policies shape how WPBAs are practiced and understood.

Residents described WPBAs as mechanisms of surveillance and institutional control, rather than tools for learning and feedback, with consequences extending beyond the assessment encounter. As one participant shared.

‘Sometimes a faculty with whom the WPBA did not go so well will say that until you improve your skills, I will not let you enter my OR; or will not give you cases (hands‐on). The faculty might be doing this with good intentions … but then residents think that I better had not done this WPBA with him’ (FGD 1; R#2). This reflects how, in this context, faculty authority links assessments to access and gatekeeping, where underperformance results in exclusion from clinical opportunity rather than support.

‘Sometimes a faculty with whom the WPBA did not go so well will say that until you improve your skills, I will not let you enter my OR; or will not give you cases (hands‐on). The faculty might be doing this with good intentions … but then residents think that I better had not done this WPBA with him’ (FGD 1; R#2).

Another participant noted:

‘Some consultants don't sign it [the WPBA form], saying you don't know anything about it … go prepare and then come again’ (I; R#2). This exemplifies the asymmetry in control over assessment completion where feedback is not experienced as formative, but as a judgement to be earned. The formative intent of WPBAs is further displaced by their function as institutional formalities:

‘Some consultants don't sign it [the WPBA form], saying you don't know anything about it … go prepare and then come again’ (I; R#2).

‘The Mini‐CEX and DOPS are being done only as a formality. Residents get them done yearly just to meet the requirements’ (FGD 2; R#6). The term ‘formality’ captures undermining the developmental intent of the WPBAs, where compliance with institutional checklists takes precedence over learning. This was reinforced by another resident's description:

‘The Mini‐CEX and DOPS are being done only as a formality. Residents get them done yearly just to meet the requirements’ (FGD 2; R#6).

‘It is only a programmatic requirement but is not used for learning. It has to be done for survival’ (I; R#4). The language of ‘survival’ reveals the emotional weight of WPBAs, where compliance ensures safety, feedback carries reputational risk and observation heightens assessment anxiety, making WPBAs high‐stakes encounters.

‘It is only a programmatic requirement but is not used for learning. It has to be done for survival’ (I; R#4).

‘It is the fear of being observed and fear of poor performance in the Mini‐CEX. So, it is the fear of the examination, and not the fear of abdominal examination.’ (I; R#6) This distinction suggests that fear is not merely an individual emotional reaction but reflects an institutionalised assessment logic in which WPBAs function as mechanisms of surveillance and symbolic control, shaping how residents experience and navigate evaluation.

‘It is the fear of being observed and fear of poor performance in the Mini‐CEX. So, it is the fear of the examination, and not the fear of abdominal examination.’ (I; R#6)

Collectively, these narratives reflect the broader social practice where WPBAs, embedded within institutional structures, function as tools of surveillance and symbolic control. Residents' participation in WPBAs is mainly to avoid risk and protect their standing, potentially masking the lack of developmental intent.

Hierarchy and fear of judgement (meso level: discursive practice)

At the meso level, the focus was on how everyday communication, feedback exchanges and interactional norms within the clinical learning context reproduce authority, influencing resident engagement with assessment discourse.

Residents described WPBAs as shaped by hierarchy, deference to authority and emotional restraint, turning assessments into power‐laden encounters where the expectation of respect for seniors and fear of reputational damage often limited engagement with feedback.

‘We have some very, very senior faculty; you will be a little scared to get yourself assessed by them and … be more prepared’ (I; R#1). Resident's language here reveals how they, as a group, position themselves within hierarchical WPBA encounters, capturing both the emotional weight of interacting with authority figures and the anticipatory self‐monitoring such interactions require.

‘We have some very, very senior faculty; you will be a little scared to get yourself assessed by them and … be more prepared’ (I; R#1).

The tone and delivery of feedback were also sources of distress. Residents distinguished between acceptable critique and emotionally harmful interactions:

‘There is a difference between scolding and insulting … consultants also need to maintain the decorum of being seniors’ (I; R#4). At the meso level of discursive practice, this shows how feedback can slip into humiliation, violating respect and dignity, while also reflecting cultural norms of hierarchy that reinforce fear of judgement.

‘There is a difference between scolding and insulting … consultants also need to maintain the decorum of being seniors’ (I; R#4).

The participants also described how feedback often focussed disproportionately on deficiencies, limiting its developmental value.

‘Focus is more on areas of improvement. No one talks about our strengths’ (FGD 2; R#5). This feedback style, experienced as one‐directional and judgmental, contributed to a broader perception of WPBAs as punitive rather than supportive. The consequences of negative feedback were perceived as far‐reaching and reputationally damaging. Referring to the positional authority of the program director, one resident shared:

‘Focus is more on areas of improvement. No one talks about our strengths’ (FGD 2; R#5).

‘If I get negative feedback, it goes to the PD … I then get in their bad books’ (FGD 2; R#3) Another described the pervasive influence of senior faculty within the institutional hierarchy:

‘If I get negative feedback, it goes to the PD … I then get in their bad books’ (FGD 2; R#3)

‘In a hierarchical setup, just one person can destroy your reputation, and it will be affected through and through’ (I; R#8). These quotes demonstrate how the threat of institutional memory and social labelling shapes the discourse of assessment, not just as feedback, but as a moment of personal and professional risk.

‘In a hierarchical setup, just one person can destroy your reputation, and it will be affected through and through’ (I; R#8).

Discourses of silence and deference were prominent, particularly in how residents navigated disagreement:

‘We are not taught in our undergraduate or postgraduate training to disagree. Even if we disagree with something, we do not say it because they are our teachers, our seniors. Because if we disagree, then it would be disrespectful; and, the senior will get upset with you, and then you will be labelled as a very argumentative person’ (I; R#2). This extended reflection illustrates how cultural norms around obedience and hierarchy shape the boundaries of what can be said, suppressing feedback dialogue and reinforcing passive compliance.

‘We are not taught in our undergraduate or postgraduate training to disagree. Even if we disagree with something, we do not say it because they are our teachers, our seniors. Because if we disagree, then it would be disrespectful; and, the senior will get upset with you, and then you will be labelled as a very argumentative person’ (I; R#2).

Together, these narratives reflect a discursive culture of caution, fear and reputational management, where hierarchy structures both what can be said and how it is received. WPBAs are embedded within these norms, and residents learn to perform within them by guarding their words, managing impressions and prioritising relational safety over pedagogical growth.

Strategic compliance to survive WPBAs (micro level: text)

At the micro level, attention was given to residents' language use, revealing how subtle expressions, metaphors, pronoun shifts and word choices operate. These linguistic features show how residents discursively position themselves and employ adaptive strategies to navigate risk and maintain professional credibility within hierarchical assessment settings.

Residents described strategically engaging with WPBAs to preserve psychological safety and professional standing. Rather than resisting the institutional structures around them, they adapted silently, employing calculated forms of avoidance, impression management and linguistic restraint to survive in an assessment culture they perceived as evaluative and unpredictable.

One of the most common strategies was assessor selection. Residents admitted to choosing faculty not based on pedagogical value but on perceived safety.

‘We don't choose those who are like lethal faculty… those who are straightforward, blunt on the face’ (FGD 2; R#3). Here, the metaphor ‘lethal’ constructs certain assessors as emotionally risky, positioning residents as vulnerable within these interactions, while the use of pronoun ‘we’ indicates a collective adaptive strategy. Similarly, another resident reflected:

‘We don't choose those who are like lethal faculty… those who are straightforward, blunt on the face’ (FGD 2; R#3).

‘There is definitely bias … You'd like to go to a faculty who's easy going and lenient’ (I; R#1). Here, generic pronoun ‘you’ normalises assessor selection as a shared practice and distances personal accountability, reflecting caution in describing power‐laden interactions, whereas the evaluative terms ‘safe’ versus ‘risky’ assessors indicate assessment choices.

‘There is definitely bias … You'd like to go to a faculty who's easy going and lenient’ (I; R#1).

Residents withheld participation in WPBAs until they felt their performance would meet unspoken standards.

‘Unless I have gone through the whole procedure and am confident in doing each step, I will not ask for an assessment’ (FGD 2; R#2) This quote illustrates that residents experienced WPBAs as evaluative and reputationally consequential; conditional phrasing ‘unless’ positions participation as contingent on readiness, making delay a strategy for managing hierarchical vulnerability. Such fear of judgement and perceived high stakes shifted WPBA focus from learning opportunities to carefully staged performances.

‘Unless I have gone through the whole procedure and am confident in doing each step, I will not ask for an assessment’ (FGD 2; R#2)

‘I even changed my writing style depending on the faculty assessing me’ (I: R#4). These choices reflect a self‐monitoring discourse rooted in fear of exposure and judgement. Rather than viewing WPBAs as spaces for formative learning, residents internalised the expectation of performative competence before engaging. This subtle self‐regulation demonstrates how power operates not only in explicit ways but also through implicit control over how residents present themselves in assessments.

‘I even changed my writing style depending on the faculty assessing me’ (I: R#4).

Even when residents disagreed with feedback, they often chose silence to avoid conflict. One participant admitted,

‘I may disagree [with the feedback], but I would not say it … I don't want to make things complicated’ (I; R#5). The mitigation ‘may’ and avoidance language ‘I would not say it’ exemplifies discursive submission, that is, intentional withholding of dissent to maintain harmony and protect one's image, rendering feedback a transactional exchange rather than a dialogic learning opportunity.

‘I may disagree [with the feedback], but I would not say it … I don't want to make things complicated’ (I; R#5).

Together, these accounts illustrate how residents engage in strategic compliance, using silence, delay and assessor selection to navigate power safely within WPBAs. This discourse of restraint, adaptation and self‐censorship reflects adaptation rather than resistance. While such performative alignment allows residents to meet institutional requirements and manage emotional and reputational risk, it undermines the developmental intent of WPBAs.

DISCUSSION

This study examined how residents in Pakistan discursively construct their experiences of WPBAs, revealing how institutional norm and authority relations shape WPBA encounters. Rather than treating hierarchy and power distance as fixed cultural traits, we conceptualise them as relational and institutional discourses, enacted through everyday assessment interactions within specific training contexts. 11

Using Fairclough's framework, analysis moved across three interconnected levels: micro (linguistic features of talk), meso (interactional and institutional routines) and macro (broader sociocultural and professional patterns). 45 At the micro level, residents' language signalled deference and authority in feedback talk. At the meso level, these patterns formed routinised assessment practices characterised by limited dialogue and reputational sensitivity. At the macro level, WPBAs were positioned as supervisory and consequential rather than developmental.

At the macro level, WPBAs were frequently experienced as high‐stakes mechanisms of surveillance and gatekeeping, where underperformance risked clinical opportunities or faculty support. Similar shifts from formative intent to bureaucratic compliance are reported internationally. 7 , 18 , 46 , 47 Our findings suggest that in Pakistan, these dynamics are intensified by the entrenched hierarchies and rapid policy adoption without parallel shifts in institutional culture or robust faculty development, positioning WPBAs as sites where institutional power relations are reproduced.

At the meso level of discursive practice, norms of deference, respect for seniority and emotional restraint constrained feedback interactions. Feedback was unidirectional, deficiency‐focussed and reputationally consequential, limiting open dialogue and acceptance, consistent with literature linking hierarchical authority to limited feedback exchange and disagreement to disrespect. 10 , 13 , 48 Expressions such as fear of ‘getting in the bad books’ or being ‘labelled as argumentative’ illustrate how institutional discourse reinforces authority, discourages dialogue and prioritises obedience over inquiry, fostering silence and impression management rather than collaborative meaning‐making and growth. 13

Micro‐level analysis demonstrated how residents navigated this terrain through strategies such as selecting lenient assessors, delaying assessments until confident, adapting performance to faculty expectations or withholding disagreement. While similar self‐regulatory and impression management behaviours have been reported internationally, 4 , 8 , 16 here they functioned as survival strategies, reframing assessment‐seeking as negotiation of institutional power rather than learning optimisation. WPBAs thus risk rewarding strategic compliance over developmental vulnerability. 4 , 47

These findings extend Gauthier et al.'s model by demonstrating that assessment‐seeking strategies are not merely individual choices based on perceived relational safety and individual risk calculations, but are collectively normalised within hierarchical discourse. 23 Assessment‐seeking thus becomes less a matter of optimising learning opportunities and more a strategy for managing surveillance and preserving professional identity.

Importantly, this pattern reflecting bureaucratic WPBAs is not unique to Pakistan; fear of judgement, performative completion of forms and constrained feedback dialogue are well documented globally. 4 , 13 , 23 , 46 , 49 , 50 Our study illustrates how institutionalised hierarchy can magnify these challenges, transforming formative WPBA encounters into spaces where power is enacted and identities are managed. 49

Faculty perspectives further contextualise these dynamics. Studies from diverse contexts report that supervisors often view WPBAs as administrative tasks, feel pressured by time and workload, default to checklist completion rather than engaging in dialogic feedback or hold honest feedback to avoid reputational risk. 6 , 8 , 21 This suggests faculty are not merely agents of hierarchy but are themselves situated within institutional logics that prioritise documentation, defensibility and efficiency over developmental dialogue. Residents' accounts in our study suggest that such practices may reflect faculty's own professional socialisation within hierarchical norms, where expertise is enacted as authority rather than dialogue. These insights underscore that WPBAs are socially embedded practices co‐constructed by both learners and assessors within broader assessment cultures.

These insights highlight that hierarchy itself is not inherently detrimental; in some contexts, it can provide structure, accountability and support professional identity formation. 9 The challenge, therefore, is not to eliminate hierarchy altogether but to calibrate how power is enacted, whether through surveillance and reputational control or through mentorship, trust, and psychologically safe dialogue.

These findings may also reflect the recent implementation of WPBAs in Pakistan, paralleling early experiences elsewhere where unclear purpose and insufficient assessor training constrained formative use. 50 , 51 , 52

For both local and international audiences, this study underscores the need for contextually grounded educational models that consider culture, discourse and institutional realities. Improving WPBAs requires more than faculty training or checklist refinement; it demands addressing the communicative norms, hierarchical dynamics and institutional logics that shape how WPBAs are enacted and experienced. Faculty development alone is therefore insufficient if institutional structures continue to reward compliance over learning. Designing WPBA systems in hierarchical contexts may benefit from structural safeguards that normalise dialogue and shared sense‐making, such as protected feedback time, reflective prompts or peer‐assisted assessment discussions.

Future research should explore WPBAs across diverse stakeholders and settings, including faculty perspectives and institutional texts, to better understand how assessment cultures are co‐constructed in hierarchical settings. Comparative studies across contexts may further inform culturally responsive approaches to assessment.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its use of CDA to highlight how language reproduces and constructs power, hierarchy and compliance, uncovering the sociocultural and institutional dynamics underpinning WPBAs. Including residents from diverse disciplines and institutions enhances transferability. However, dynamics might unfold differently in other disciplines or institutions, and it would be worth exploring to increase insight into contextual and cultural differences.

A limitation is the exclusive reliance on resident narratives. Incorporating additional data sources, such as policy documents, WPBA forms, observations, and faculty perspectives, could offer a more comprehensive understanding of how WPBA practices are shaped.

CONCLUSION

WPBAs in Pakistan are embedded in context‐specific discourses that often reinforce hierarchy and control, undermining their formative intent. These findings challenge assumptions of neutrality and universal applicability of pedagogical interventions, highlighting the need for culturally responsive, contextually grounded assessment practices that foster psychological safety, dialogue and learner agency. While not unique to Pakistan, these patterns illustrate how contextual hierarchies can amplify global challenges in formative assessment. Recognising hierarchy as relational and discursively enacted invites educators to examine how institutional assessment practices may reinforce inequitable learning relations. Without context‐sensitive adaptation, WPBAs risk fostering silence, anxiety and compliance rather than developmental growth.
