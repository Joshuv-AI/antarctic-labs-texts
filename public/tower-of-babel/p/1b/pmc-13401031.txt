Rapid and non-destructive estimation of chemical properties of date syrup using visible-near infrared hyperspectral imaging combined with machine learning predictive models
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Nargesi, Kamran Kheiralipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, an innovative hybrid approach combining hyperspectral imaging and artificial intelligence was developed to replace costly and time-consuming laboratory methods of determining chemical properties of date syrup. Hyperspectral data related to acidity, Brix, fructose, HMF, pH, and viscosity were analyzed using three machine learning models: Artificial neural network, partial least squares regression, and support vector regression. These models were evaluated under two strategies, with and without feature selection step. Among the models, the Artificial Neural Network demonstrated superior performance compared to the other methods. The results revealed that the optimal performance was achieved with four neurons in the hidden layer and the use of the Levenberg-Marquardt activation function. The prediction accuracies for the mentioned properties were 100%, 99.99%, 100%, 99.99%, 100%, and 99.99%, respectively. In the strategy without feature selection, the prediction accuracy for all chemical parameters reached 100%. These findings highlight the capability of hyperspectral imaging and artificial neural networks in estimating chemical properties of date syrup.Keywords: Hyperspectral imaging, Image analysis, Machine learning, Quality, Date syrup

Introduction

The date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) is considered one of the most important agricultural crops in many Middle Eastern countries, with its fruit-dates-being a staple in the diet of millions of people (Alkhoori et al., 2022). Dates are an excellent source of energy, containing approximately 78% carbohydrates, 2–3% protein, and less than 1% fat. In addition to being a natural energy source, they play a key nutritional role due to their significant content of vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber (El-Nagga & Abd El–Tawab, 2012). Dates are considered a highly nutritious fruit due to their rich content of carbohydrates, proteins, and essential minerals. They are an excellent source of thermal energy for the human body, with sugars making up approximately 80% of their total weight (Shehzad et al., 2021; Siddeeg et al., 2019; Siddiq et al., 2013). The high potassium content and extremely low sodium levels make dates an ideal dietary choice for individuals with high blood pressure (Keshtkaran & Mohammadifar, 2013). Epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that regular and high consumption of dates is associated with a reduced risk of chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, various types of cancer, aging, atherosclerosis, neurodegenerative disorders, tumors, and mutagenic agents (Al-Farsi et al., 2007; El-Beltagy et al., 2009; Gad et al., 2010). Furthermore, research indicates that consuming date extracts during the postpartum period can enhance the immune system in women (Gad et al., 2010). In addition, a polysaccharide isolated from dates has shown notable antitumor properties (Ishurd & Kennedy, 2005). Furthermore, dates are rich in a wide range of bioactive compounds that exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and even anti-infertility properties (Alhamdan et al., 2021). Industrially, dates are used to produce a variety of products such as syrup, juice, jam, jelly, candy, processed dates, pickles, and vinegar. Among these, date syrup is one of the most widely consumed derivatives. It is highly valued for its rich content of glucose, fructose, minerals, and organic acids. Due to the presence of polyphenols and similar compounds, date syrup also exhibits notable antioxidant activity (Alhamdan et al., 2021; El-Nagga & Abd El–Tawab, 2012). It is widely used in the preparation of both traditional and industrial foods, such as beverages, confectionery, ice cream, and dairy-based desserts like yogurt (Gholamhosseinpour & Hashemi, 2022; Keshtkaran & Mohammadifar, 2013).

Due to their nutritional composition, low-quality dates have also been used to produce value-added products. Date syrup, known locally in some countries as “Rub Al Tamr,” is prepared from specific local varieties of dates in nations such as Libya, Iraq, and Tunisia (Barreveld, 1993). This syrup is either consumed directly or used as an ingredient in the formulation of food products such as ice cream, beverages, sweets, bakery items, sesame paste/date syrup blends, jams, and date butter (Barreveld, 1993; Razavi et al., 2007; Roukas & Kotzekidou, 1997). Date syrup is a processed product with high nutritional value, obtained as a thick liquid from dates through thermal extraction. This syrup is free from fibers, sediments, impurities, and foreign matter, making it suitable for direct consumption or use in the production of confectionery and bakery products (Yahmed et al., 2021). It consists of 86.6% total sugars, 81.7% reducing sugars, and 4.9% sucrose. Additionally, it contains 24.8% moisture, 0.02% acidity, 2.1% protein, and 6.6% ash on a dry weight basis. The syrup is also a good source of vitamins A and B (Ashraf & Hamidi-Esfahani, 2011) and is rich in minerals (Al Hilfi et al., 2019). Date syrup is a valuable food source, rich in sugars, minerals, vitamins, and antioxidants (Al-Farsi et al., 2018). Research has shown that dates have a low glycemic index, making them a beneficial option whether consumed alone or in combination with plain yogurt (Yousif et al., 1996). Date fruit exhibits tumor-fighting properties (Ishurd & Kennedy, 2005), as well as antioxidant, anticancer, and antimutagenic effects (Maqsood et al., 2020). However, date syrup extracted through traditional methods often appears darker in color due to the prolonged application of high heat, which leads to the formation of colored compounds such as caramel, resulting from reactions between sugars and amino acids. Another common issue is crystallization, caused by the separation and accumulation of sugars, which can be managed by adding citric acid. Reduced fluidity is also a concern, as the presence of pectic substances gives the syrup a jelly-like consistency. Furthermore, if the concentration of the syrup drops below 75%, there is an increased risk of fermentation and microbial contamination due to insufficient hygiene (Ashraf & Hamidi-Esfahani, 2011). Quality assessment of materials is a fundamental component in achieving sustainable production (Kheiralipour, 2022). The evaluation of physicochemical properties of date syrup such as hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) content, Brix value, fructose concentration, moisture content, pH, acidity, and viscosity are typically conducted using advanced laboratory instruments under the supervision of skilled operators (Caliskan & Polat, 2011; Cepeda & Villaran, 1999; Farahnaky et al., 2016). However, these procedures are time-consuming, costly, and require access to specialized laboratory facilities. Therefore, the development and application of rapid, non-destructive, and accurate methods and also combined with machine learning algorithms can play a crucial role in quality monitoring of food and optimization of production processes (Nargesi et al., 2026; Deng et al., 2026). Different imaging techniques are avilable to record area data of products with high speed and accuracy and low cost (Kheiralipour, 2012). Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) is an advanced technique that integrates spectroscopy with imaging sensors to simultaneously capture high-resolution spatial and spectral information (Kheiralipour, 2024). This technique serves as a powerful tool for identifying and analyzing the chemical and physical properties of food products and related materials (He & Sun, 2015; Wang et al., 2021; Kheiralipour, Singh, & Jayas, 2023; Sun et al., 2024; Ahmed et al., 2024). This technique generates images containing comprehensive spectral data for each pixel, allowing detailed spectral characterization of samples (Kheiralipour, Chelladurai, & Jayas, 2023). Unlike conventional imaging systems that rely solely on three broad color bands (RGB), HSI acquires data across hundreds of narrows, contiguous spectral bands over a wide wavelength range (Kamruzzaman, 2023; Kheiralipour & Jayas, 2024). This broad spectral coverage facilitates the extraction of specific information regarding chemical composition, structural features, and various physical attributes of food samples (Elmasry et al., 2012; Jia et al., 2024; Kheiralipour, Sajadipour, & Nadimi, 2025; Nargesi, Kheiralipour, & Khodaei, 2025). The quality and utility of this hyperspectral data are inherently influenced by sensor characteristics such as signal-to-noise ratio, spectral resolution, and spatial resolution; recent research has explored how evolving artificial intelligence techniques can adapt to and leverage these variable data qualities for improved analysis (Li, Liu, Cheng, et al., 2026; Li, Liu, Huang, et al., 2026). One of the key advantages of HSI is its non-destructive and non-invasive nature, which allows for in-depth analysis without altering or damaging the sample (Kheiralipour, Sajadipour, & Nargesi, 2025). This feature is particularly important for food assessment, as it preserves product integrity, avoids sample preparation steps that may affect its physicochemical properties, and enables real-time monitoring during industrial processing and quality control operations (Kamruzzaman et al., 2015). In addition to its applications in the food industry, HSI is widely employed in environmental monitoring tasks such as detecting vegetation changes, assessing forest health, and tracking land-use transformations (Lee et al., 2024). Furthermore, recent advancements, such as the multi-scale autoencoder suppression strategy for hyperspectral image anomaly detection, have significantly enhanced the capability to identify spectral irregularities (Tu et al., 2025). Within the food sector, HSI is used for quality control purposes, including the identification of defects or contaminants in food products (Abbasi et al., 2025; Kheiralipour et al., 2014; Ordoudi et al., 2014; Sunil et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Analysis using advanced machine learning models demonstrate the cross-sector potential of these techniques, which are equally applicable to food quality assessment (Xu et al., 2025; Nargesi and Kheiralipour, 2025). Altogether, these capabilities highlight HSI as a versatile and valuable tool across diverse agricultural and food-related applications (Esmaeily et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2020; Mahesh et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2023). The continuous evolution of deep learning architectures for spectral image analysis and the detection of small targets in complex spectral data (Li, Liu, Cheng, et al., 2026; Li, Liu, Huang, et al., 2026) demonstrates the growing sophistication of techniques for extracting subtle features from hyperspectral imagery.

The aim of this research is to evaluate the effectiveness of HIS systems in developing predictive models for the rapid and non-destructive estimation of chemical properties of date syrup, which are key indicators of its quality. This technology enables product authentication in terms of ingredient composition. Although several studies have investigated the physicochemical properties of date syrup using conventional analytical methods, comprehensive, rapid, and non-destructive approaches for quality assessment are still limited. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the first systematic investigations employing HIS to characterize the quality attributes of date syrup. HIS, by enabling precise analysis of date syrup, holds great potential for the development of portable and on-site systems that can serve as effective tools for quality control and determination of chemical parameters in the food industry.

Materials and methods

The research steps from preparing the test samples to analyzing the data are shown in Fig. 1.

Research process steps.

Sample preparation and handling chemical tests

Three different grades of date syrup under the brand name Mardasang were purchased from a local market in Bandar Abbas, Hormozgan, Iran. Chemical analyses confirmed that these samples represented distinct grades, as they differed in key parameters including acidity, Brix, fructose content, hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), pH, and viscosity. The collected samples were stored at room temperature until analysis (Fig. 1).

In this study, the properties of acidity, Brix, fructose, Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), pH, and viscosity of date syrup were measured using standard laboratory methods and specialized instruments at the Central Laboratory No. 2, Lorestan University, Khoramabad, Lorestan, Iran.

All chemical properties of date syrup samples were determined following the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 1997). Acidity was measured in terms of malic acid. The Brix test was conducted to measure the total soluble solids (mainly sugars) in the date syrup. This is one of the most critical quality control tests for sugary products, as it directly relates to the sweetness, concentration, and overall quality of the product. Brix values were measured using a refractometer. The results were used to assess the natural sugar content, detect possible adulteration (such as the addition of sugar or syrups), and ensure compliance with packaging and export quality standards. Fructose, one of the main sugars in date syrup (alongside glucose), is a key indicator in quality control, nutritional evaluation, and fraud detection. In this study, fructose content was measured using an enzymatic method. HMF is a compound formed due to excessive heating or prolonged storage of sugary products. Elevated HMF levels may indicate poor processing, improper storage, or thermal adulteration. Measurement of pH is important for assessing the microbial and chemical stability of the product. The pH level of the date syrup was measured using a pH meter (Model: pH 7110 model, WTW company inolab) at 20 °C.

Viscosity is a key physical property of date syrup, directly related to its concentration, Brix value, and sensory characteristics. In this study, viscosity was measured using a Brookfield viscometer, in accordance with international standards such as ASTM D2196 or ISO 2555. Higher viscosity indicates better consistency and product quality, making it an important factor in the formulation of food products.

Hyperspectral scanning

Hyperspectral images of the date syrup samples were captured using a hyperspectral camera (Model: Specam, Parto Sanat Company, Zanjan, Iran) in the wavelength range of 400–950 nm. As shown in Fig. 2, each sample was placed on a motorized platform, and its hyperspectral image was captured individually. This process was carried out through linear scanning at a constant speed using four 25-watt LED lamps. A white reference plate was used for the white reference, and a dark reference was automatically applied during image capturing when the lamps were turned on. The raw hyperspectral images (with 666 bands) were calibrated using the white and dark references before data analysis.

Hyperspectral imaging camera.

Processing of hyperspectral data

Hyperspectral images were processed using MATLAB software version 2016. A region of interest with dimensions 50 × 80 pixels from the center of the raw hyperspectral image was selected to isolate the data corresponding from the container's boundaries to the samples and extract spectral information.

To reduce data complexity, improve analysis accuracy, and minimize data volume, the selection of relevant features was essential. For this purpose, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed, which reduces the resolution by averaging pixel values within each block. The selection of effective channels was based on the average of the first (PC1) and second (PC2) principal components, with key image channels being determined from the peak points in the PC1 and PC2 curves (Fernandez et al., 2019; Kheiralipour et al., 2021; Hasan & Mohieldein, 2019; Nargesi et al., 2026). Then the corresponding wavelengths were found. After selecting appropriate wavelengths, various features were extracted from the selected channels including mean, minimum, maximum, median, variance, and standard deviation (Kheiralipour et al., 2014; Nargesi, 2024). From each hyperspectral image, 7 effective wavelengths were chosen, and 6 features (Mean, Minimum, Maximum, Median, Variance, and Standard deviation) were extracted from each wavelength. In total, 42 effective features were extracted from the hyperspectral images. In cases of high-dimensional data, dimensionality reduction techniques were employed to enhance model performance. In this study, efficient features were selected from the extracted features using sequential feature selection (SFS) method (Nargesi, Kheiralipour, & Jayas, 2024). This method operates based on the model fit criterion, which is the generalized residual sum of squares (Lee et al., 2025; Nargesi & Kheiralipour, 2024).

Model development for predicting the syrup's properties

In this study, HIS data were used as input features, and laboratory-measured chemical pproperties of date syrup including acidity, Brix, fructose, hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), pH, and viscosity were used as labeled output variables. Three predictive algorithms were designed and trained: artificial neural network (ANN), partial least squares regression (PLSR), and support vector regression (SVR). Model performance was evaluated based on the accuracy of their predictions. In the ANN model, the activation functions used for the hidden and output layers were tansig and purelin, respectively. The Levenberg-Marquardt training algorithm was employed due to its high convergence speed and its ability to reduce the risk of overfitting (Nargesi, Amiriparian, et al., 2024; Nargesi, Heidarbeigi, et al., 2024). For the SVR model, the hyperparameters C, lambda, epsilon, and kernel width were initially chosen based on commonly recommended values in the literature and then fine-tuned through a grid search to optimize prediction accuracy. The final values were set to C = 100, lambda = 1, epsilon = 0.001, and Gaussian kernel with a width of 0.01, as these provided the best balance between bias and variance. For the PLSR and SVR models, 80% of the data were allocated for training and 20% for testing. In contrast, for the ANN model, the dataset was randomly divided into three subsets: 60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing. This difference was due to the need for an independent validation set in ANN training to monitor convergence and prevent overfitting, while PLSR and SVR do not require a separate validation set under the cross-validation framework employed (Nargesi & Kheiralipour, 2024; Nargesi, Kheiralipour, & Jayas, 2024).

The features were used for developing predictive models considering two strategies, with and without feature selection step.

Conventional methods for determining ash content in coal, based on proximate analysis protocols, are time-consuming, costly, and require destructive procedures such as direct combustion of samples. This study introduces an innovative and non-destructive approach utilizing mid-infrared Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy in the range of 1450 to 350 cm−1 to identify spectrally sensitive regions (comprising fourteen selective absorption bands) for accurate prediction of ash yield in coal samples. A set of advanced machines learning algorithms, including Piecewise Linear Regression (PLR), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), PLSR, SVR, and Random Forest (RF), were employed for predictive modeling. Additionally, a multi-model estimation (MME) framework was proposed, combining the outputs of the top three performing models (PLR, PLSR, and ANN) to enhance prediction accuracy and model robustness. The resulting hybrid model demonstrated superior performance, achieving a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.883, root mean square error (RMSE) of 3.059 wt%, RMSE percentage (RMSE%) of 30.080, mean bias error percentage (MBE%) of 3.694, and mean absolute error (MAE) of 2.249 wt%. These findings highlight the high potential of FTIR spectroscopy as a rapid and reliable tool for non-destructive ash yield prediction in coal, underscoring its applicability as an efficient analytical solution for quality monitoring in industrial coal processing (Mishra et al., 2025).

In the analysis of hyperspectral data for predicting the chemical properties of date syrup, the performance of three machine learning models, namely Artificial Neural Network (ANN), PLSR, and SVR, was compared. The results indicated that ANN consistently outperformed the other two models across all chemical parameters, achieving prediction accuracies close to or at 100%. In contrast, PLSR and SVR provided acceptable predictions but exhibited slightly lower accuracies, particularly for parameters with complex nonlinear relationships with the hyperspectral data, such as viscosity and HMF content.

The superior performance of ANN can be attributed to several factors. First, hyperspectral data often exhibit complex nonlinear relationships with chemical properties, and ANN is inherently capable of modeling these nonlinearities through multiple layers and nonlinear activation functions, whereas PLSR assumes linear relationships and SVR requires careful kernel selection. Second, ANN can efficiently process high-dimensional data without extensive dimensionality reduction, while PLSR relies on latent variable extraction and SVR may suffer from overfitting in the presence of irrelevant features. Third, many hyperspectral bands are highly correlated; ANN, through its network structure and learning algorithms, can handle multicollinearity more effectively, whereas PLSR is more sensitive to highly correlated predictors.

Although the inclusion of a feature selection step slightly improved the performance of PLSR and SVR by reducing input dimensionality and noise, ANN achieved near-optimal results even without this step. This highlights the model's intrinsic ability to directly extract the most informative spectral patterns from the full hyperspectral dataset. Overall, these results indicate that ANN is particularly well-suited for hyperspectral applications in food analysis, providing high prediction accuracy, robustness to complex nonlinearities, and adaptability to high-dimensional spectral data.

Conclusions

In this study, hyperspectral imaging was employed to estimate six chemical properties of date syrup, including acidity, Brix, fructose, HMF, pH, and viscosity. Three prediction algorithms Artificial Neural Networks, Partial Least Squares Regression, and Support Vector Regression were implemented and evaluated using two strategies: with and without feature selection. Initially, the effective spectral channels were identified, and six prominent features were extracted from each of the seven selected wavelengths, yielding 42 initial features. Feature selection reduced this set to eight key features, significantly simplifying the models while maintaining predictive power. The results demonstrated that Artificial Neural Networks models using the selected features outperformed other algorithms, achieving correlation coefficients (R2) of 99.99–100% across all properties and the lowest validation errors. Partial Least Squares Regression and Support Vector Regression also showed strong predictive performance, particularly when feature selection was applied, with R2 values exceeding 91% for most properties. Quantifying the practical benefits, the Artificial Neural Networks based system reduced the number of input features by 81% (from 42 to 8), which can substantially decrease computation time during real-time monitoring. The high prediction accuracy and reduced model complexity indicate that such systems could shorten detection time by approximately 30–40% while maintaining near-perfect prediction of chemical properties. These results highlight the potential of hyperspectral imaging combined with ANN for developing intelligent, real-time quality control systems in the food processing industry, capable of both optimizing operational efficiency and enhancing the reliability of chemical property monitoring in date syrup.
