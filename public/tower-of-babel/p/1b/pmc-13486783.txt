A novel ex vivo platform for functional evaluation of treatment responses in metastatic ovarian cancer
Authors: Alain Valdivia, Adebimpe Adefolaju, Morrent Thang, Caroline Stockwell, Luz Andrea Cuaboy, Catherine John, Breanna Mann, Andrew Satterlee, Victoria L. Bae-Jump, Shawn Hingtgen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The lack of functional precision models that recapitulate the structural and microenvironmental features of advanced ovarian cancer remains a major barrier to improving therapeutic selection. Here, we present an organotypic mesentery membrane culture (OMMC) model, an ex vivo platform that supports rapid engraftment of freshly resected human ovarian cancer tissue and established cell lines onto intact rat mesenteric membranes. This system enables functional assessment of responses to standard-of-care therapies within five days. We demonstrate sustained viability of mesenteric tissue, successful engraftment and expansion of tumor cells and patient-derived tumor tissue, and measurable tumor–mesentery interactions. Using a multi-parametric drug sensitivity score (DSS), we quantified tumor response relative to off-target mesenteric toxicity across a panel of FDA-approved therapies. Application of the platform to patient-derived tumor samples revealed inter-patient variability in drug sensitivity profiles. Comparison of OMMC-derived responses with matched clinical outcomes suggests that this approach may help inform treatment response in a subset of cases. Together, these findings support the potential of the OMMC platform as a functional assay to complement existing preclinical and molecular approaches for evaluating therapeutic responses in ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is one of the most common gynecological cancers around the world, with a dismal 5-year overall survival rate of 50% in the US alone1,2. Although most ovarian cancer patients respond to initial treatment with a combination of surgery and platinum/taxane-based chemotherapy, the majority will eventually recur and subsequently develop platinum-resistant disease, with no available tools to determine the next best therapeutic option to give. Thus, functional assays (conducted in real-time) are needed to define the most efficacious therapy for each patient’s cancer and guide true precision oncology, optimizing the use of the most effective treatments and simultaneously diminishing the use of ineffective therapies.

There is an urgent need to include functional testing of live tumor tissue in the current clinical diagnostic landscape. Direct measurements of tumor response to drugs ex vivo are an ongoing alternative under study. In recent years, 3D models, Spheroids3,4, patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models5–8, Organoids9–14, tumor explants15,16, and organotypic models17–21 of OC have increasingly attracted the attention of scientists. Although a great part of these 3D models has advanced ovarian cancer research beyond traditional 2D systems, they remain imperfect representations of in vivo disease. Patient-derived spheroids and organoids often fail to fully capture the tumor microenvironment, lacking critical stromal, immune, and extracellular matrix interactions22,23. Spheroids commonly are prone to oxygen and nutrient diffusion gradients that generate central necrotic cores and variable proliferation, heterogeneous proliferation, and limited long-term stability24. Although organoids better preserve genetic and morphological characteristics, they may lose intratumoral heterogeneity over time, and the absence of standardized culture protocols increases inter-laboratory variability25. Moreover, the lack of vasculature, systemic influences, scalability, and cost-efficiency limits reproducibility and translational predictive accuracy.

In the case of tumor explants and organotypic models, freshly resected tumors, if maintained alive in the research laboratory at least for a short period, can maintain tissue architecture, spatial organization, tumor microenvironment, and even inherent intra-tumoral heterogeneity26,27. This allows the evaluation of tumor cell behavior within their extracellular matrix and surrounding microenvironment, which may suggest fidelity in the drug tumor response27.

In vitro and in vivo models have become the gold standard for preclinical research and often do not offer enough relevant, reliable, or rapid information to directly guide clinical care28–30. These deficiencies are most manifested in an inability to maintain resected patient tissue in a manner that allows functional analysis ex vivo, such as quantifying tumor cell kill after treatment. An ideal ovarian cancer model would bridge preclinical research and clinical care by 1. enabling engraftment of resected patient tumor tissue within its native anatomic environment ex vivo, 2. quantifying drug response of such tissue against experimental and approved therapies, 3. analyzing relative response rates of tumor and drug panels to provide insight on the most effective treatment for each patient, and 4. providing summarized readouts within a rapid timeframe to help guide treatment decisions. Such a model could improve the entire drug development pipeline, among many cancer types, including ovarian cancer.

In this study, we introduce the organotypic mesentery membrane culture (OMMC) model. This ex vivo platform provides a living tissue substrate on which freshly resected, uncultured ovarian tumor tissue can be rapidly engrafted, treated, and analyzed to assess therapeutic responses. We first evaluated and optimized the OMMC platform by seeding established human ovarian cancer cell lines and assessing tumor growth, mesentery-tumor interactions, tumor response to treatment with conventional FDA-approved standard-of-care treatments for OC, and off-target toxicity to the OMMC substrate itself. Later, using live, surgically dissected OC tissue from patients at UNC Hospitals, we show tumor engraftment, survival, and response to therapy on OMMCs. We also use a previously validated multipoint algorithm31 to calculate a normalized, summarized DSS and compare the drug-induced killing of established tumor cell lines and patient tumor tissues. Finally, we compare the drug sensitivity results of resected patient tumor tissues on OMMCs to the actual clinical response of matched patients.

Results

OMMCs generation, characterization, and optimization

In the OMMC system, living mesenteric tissue is used as a living tissue substrate to capture fresh OC tissue and established OC cell lines. To first establish living rat mesenteric membranes, mesenteric tissue from 8-week-old rats was aseptically removed, carefully transferred into transwell membranes, and seeded in plates with Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Avantor®Seradigm, Premium Grade) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S) (GibcoTM) that created an air-tissue interface (Fig. 1A). The region of interest within the mesentery cultures, where tumor cells are subsequently seeded, is shown in Fig. 1B. This thin membranous area is circumscribed by surrounding adipose tissue, which is excluded from all analyses. The membrane consists of a delicate network of collagen and elastin fibers interspersed with resident cells, as confirmed by bright-field microscopy and hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. 1B, right). It is well established that the rat mesentery contains blood and lymphatic vessels, resident immune populations including macrophages and mast cells, as well as fibroblasts, all embedded within a structured extracellular matrix composed primarily of collagen and elastin fibers32–36. We confirmed the viability of mesenteric tissue using two different approaches. First, we transduced the mesenteries with lentiviral vectors encoding mCherry and luciferase. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) of the thin inner membranes at 4, 6, 8, and 10 days post-seeding showed robust signals with no significant differences between time points (Fig. 1C). To confirm these imaging results, we used our established nuclear permeability assay using propidium iodide (PI) as a fluorescent marker of cell death31. High-resolution imaging of the PI-treated mesentery again showed robust and stable viability of the mesentery through 10 days. Interestingly, the mesenteric viability decreased slightly at later time points, reducing by ~20% at 17 days post-seeding in our OMMC system (Fig. 1D). To assess the influence of donor age, mesenteric membranes were isolated from rats aged 3–16 weeks, including adult dams. Within each age group, mesenteries maintained approximately 80% cellular viability, with no significant decline observed over 17 days, although a gradual decrease was observed (Fig. 2B). However, few differences between groups were noted, suggesting potential age-dependent variability. In contrast, mesenteric tissues from 3–4-week-old rats were structurally unstable despite initially high viability; compaction around day 5 led to membrane collapse and loss of the region of interest (Fig. 2C). Increasing donor age was associated with a progressive increase in adipose content within the mesentery, resulting in smaller usable regions of interest and, in some cases, increased bleeding during isolation (Fig. 2D).

We also examined whether the anatomical source of the mesentery influenced tissue characteristics by isolating membranes from the ileum and jejunum (Fig. 2E). Quantitative confocal imaging with PI staining revealed no significant difference in cell density between regions ( ~ 600 cells/mm²). However, the ileum exhibited a modest but statistically significant increase in thickness compared to the jejunum ( ~ 28 µm vs. ~22 µm, respectively) (Fig. 2F).

Based on these findings, we standardized the OMMC platform using ileal mesenteric membranes isolated from 8-week-old rats throughout this study. This decision was guided by both technical considerations and empirical observations obtained during platform optimization. Mechanical stability, relatively uniform adipose distribution, and consistent optical clarity were critical factors for optimal OMMC performance. These features enabled reproducible imaging and provided a stable, well-defined surface for tumor seeding, while preserving a distinct adipose ring surrounding the region of interest (Fig. 1B). Although a moderate adipose component remains within the region of interest in our current OMMC configuration, future studies may intentionally incorporate adipose-enriched membranes to investigate the specific contribution of adipose tissue to tumor progression.

Establishing tumors in the OMMC system

The ultimate goal of the OMMC platform is to engraft, treat, and analyze established cell lines and human patient tumor tissues. Having established the mesenteric component of the platform, we next focused on the OC tumor component. To simplify development, we began our testing with two of the most well-established human OC cell lines: ES-2 and SKOV3. These two cell lines represent distinct tumor types: SKOV3 is a non-serous carcinoma cell line, while ES-2 is a clear cell ovarian carcinoma (CCOC). They exemplify different forms of ovarian cancer, characterized by variations in histology, genetics, and clinical presentation. The cell lines were transduced with the reporter genes mCherry and Firefly Luciferase. On Day 0, 5 to 7 tumor spots, each containing approximately 2 ×104 cells, were implanted onto the established mesenteric tissue (Fig. 3A). Brightfield imaging was employed to visualize the tumor spots, followed by serial fluorescence and BLI to monitor the growth, expansion, and viability of each focus (Fig. 3B). Each ovarian cancer foci developed into a rounded, uniform tumor spot on the rat mesentery, mirroring the metastatic tumor morphology seen in the human omentum and mesentery of ovarian cancer patients at advanced stages37. Longitudinal cell viability tracking showed that both cell lines exhibited consistent growth and expansion on the mesentery, expanding 4-fold over 10 days (Fig. 3C). To confirm consistency in the seeding process, we calculated an inter-well variability of the initial OC seeding (Day 0) of 216 tumor foci and distributed on 36 individual mesenteric tissues. Results showed only 2.7% of variability on tumor seeding in between wells was observed (Fig. 3D). The mesentery and omentum are known to contain blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, resident macrophages, and fibroblasts all embedded within extracellular material such as collagen and elastin fibers32–36. We next investigated tumor–mesentery interactions by assessing the potential activation of macrophages residing in mesenteric tissue in the presence of ovarian cancer (OC) foci. Previous studies have reported the presence of activated subsets of CD11b⁺ myeloid cells, including macrophages, in response to ovarian cancer cells within the omentum and mesentery35,38. Consistent with this possibility, mesenteric tissue containing OC foci was stained with anti-CD11b antibodies 72 h after tumor seeding. Immunofluorescence imaging revealed accumulation of CD11b⁺ myeloid cells at the tumor–mesentery interface, with signal co-localizing with OC foci (Fig. 3F, G). This pattern was not observed in mesentery lacking tumor cells, suggesting redistribution and activation potential of macrophage-lineage myeloid cells in response to OC foci. High-magnification imaging revealed that CD11b⁺ cells formed a population distinct from mCherry⁺ tumor cells, producing a peripheral “halo-like” distribution (Fig. 3F) surrounding the tumor cell footprint on OMMCs

Developing OMMCs as a drug-testing platform

To build towards the development of a new drug-testing platform, we next investigated drug responses within the OMMC system. Using the PI assay, we first measured the off-target toxicity of a panel of therapeutics by exposing OMMCs to a panel of FDA-approved agents for the treatment of OC. The treatment panel consisted of olaparib, gemcitabine, carboplatin (carbo), paclitaxel (pac), and carboplatin–paclitaxel combination therapy (carbo–pac). Single-agent treatments were administered at doses of 0, 10, 50, 100, 250, 500, 750 and 1000 µM. For combination therapies, carboplatin was titrated across the same concentration range, whereas paclitaxel was maintained at fixed concentrations of 10, 50, or 100 µM (carbo–pac 10, carbo–pac 50, and carbo–pac 100). This broad concentration range (up to 1000 µM) was used to capture full dose–response behavior in the OMMC platform, where tissue complexity may require higher nominal concentrations than 2D systems. This enabled assessment of efficacy, toxicity, and later DSS across treatments. While some doses exceed clinical levels, the goal was comparative functional profiling rather than pharmacokinetic modeling. Our PI assay showed that the single-agent treatment induced no significant decreases in the viability of the mesenteric tissue. Next, we focused on the tumor response and seeded the ES-2 and SKOV3 cell lines directly atop the mesenteric tissue. We assessed drug activity against the OC foci by quantitative BLI (Fig. 4B). Dose-response curves and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were then calculated for each therapeutic agent, and the best-fit dose-response curves for ES-2 and SKOV3 killing and off-target OMMC toxicity were generated (Fig. 4C). See also supplemental figures for individual tumor response curves per cell line (Supplementary Fig. S1).

We then utilized our previously developed normalized scoring system to calculate “Drug Sensitivity Scores” (DSSs) for each drug-tumor-OMMC interactions31. This summarized scoring system compares the dose-response curves for tumor kill and OMMC toxicity for each therapeutic to calculate “therapeutic windows” across 11 different characteristics of curves, such as the IC50 and the Area Under the Curve (AUC). The therapeutic window ratio of tumor kill to OMMC toxicity for each parameter can range from −1 (low tumor kill but high OMMC toxicity) to +1 (high tumor kill and low OMMC toxicity), and the final DSS value is calculated from a weighted, multipoint mathematical algorithm that collapses all 11 parameters into a single score. DSS values can range from −100 to +100, where +100 describes the maximal performing agents (greatest tumor kill, lowest toxicity) and −100 describes the lowest performing agents (poorest tumor kill, highest toxicity). These unique values represent an overall metric that captures the drug response across tumor foci detected by BLI and the changes in the viability of normal mesentery tissue measured by our predefined PI assay, allowing drugs with different potencies to be compared with less bias toward more potent compounds.

ES-2 and SKOV3 cell lines showed similar sensitivity to single-agent carboplatin and the carboplatin–paclitaxel (100 µM) combination, yielding comparable DSS values. In contrast, DSS values varied for lower-dose combination therapy (carbo–pac 10) and for single-agent treatments with gemcitabine, paclitaxel, and olaparib. For example, gemcitabine produced a DSS of 62 in ES-2 and 53 in SKOV3 (Fig. 4E), indicating greater tumor killing in ES-2. This difference was driven by variations in AUC and the inability of gemcitabine to achieve ≥75% killing in SKOV3 (Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 4E). Additional therapeutic window analyses are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Advancing the OMMC platform to incorporate fresh OC patient tissue

A patient’s own viable tumor tissue most accurately represents their disease; however, models that functionally assess drug responses using uncultured tumor tissue have proven challenging. Such systems often require prolonged lead times and culture conditions that induce phenotypic alterations, clonal selection, and genetic drift, ultimately resulting in loss of the cellular and genetic heterogeneity characteristic of clinical ovarian cancer specimens.

To develop OMMCs into a model that addresses these limitations, we established a method to prepare and engraft a diverse panel of living, uncultured patient-derived OC tumor tissues onto OMMCs for rapid functional drug screening and potential therapeutic guidance. Fresh surgically resected OC tumor tissues were obtained from patients undergoing standard-of-care debulking procedures at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC-CH) hospitals following informed consent under the UNC-CH MASCOT trial (LCCC 1855 MASCOT: Manufacturing and Analysis of Stem Cells from Skin Cells for Ovarian Cancer Treatment).

Following cryopreservation and subsequent thawing using our optimized protocol, tumor tissues were mechanically dissociated into a homogeneous, minced and filtered suspension. The dissociated tissue was rapidly transduced with LV-mCh-FLuc and seeded onto OMMCs as concentrated tumor foci, each containing approximately 0.5 mg of representative tumor tissue.

To evaluate the capacity of OMMCs to support engraftment of patient-derived OC tissue, tumor survival and growth were assessed by normalized bioluminescence signals from Day 3 to Day 6 across tumor samples from nine patients (Fig. 5A). Day 3 was selected as the baseline for BLI quantification after comparing Day 2 and Day 3 measurements (Supplementary Fig. S6), which indicated that tumor foci at Day 2 were still adapting to the mesenteric environment and initiating expression of the optical reporter genes. We further compared six of these tumor specimens cultured under three conditions: 1. on OMMCs, 2. in transwell culture without OMMCs, and 3. in standard in vitro culture, as assessed by BLI (Fig. 5B-C). Tumor tissues cultured in vitro or ex vivo without mesenteric support exhibited minimal or undetectable viability, whereas those seeded onto OMMCs demonstrated significantly enhanced survival.

To assess the reproducibility of tumor spot placement, inter-well variability at Day 0 was evaluated using tissue from Mascot Patient #62. Across 30 mesenteric membranes (five tumor foci each), variability was minimal (3.68%), indicating high reproducibility of the seeding process (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

We next examined the cellular composition of an ovarian tumor specimen from one patient (Mascot 82). Flow cytometric analysis revealed a heterogeneous cellular composition (Fig. 5D; see Supplementary Fig. S4 for the gating strategy). CD45⁺ leukocytes represented a minor fraction of the total viable single-cell population, whereas most cells were CD45⁻ tumor-associated populations, including a substantial proportion of B7H3⁺ cells with variable Ki67 expression. This distribution is consistent with the predominance of tumor cells relative to immune infiltrates commonly observed in ovarian cancer and with previous reports showing B7-H3 (CD276) expression in both tumor epithelial cells and stromal compartments of the ovarian tumor microenvironment39.

Within the CD45⁺ compartment, myeloid-lineage populations predominated, particularly M2 tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and other myeloid subsets, whereas T cells and NK cells were present at lower frequencies. This myeloid-enriched profile suggests an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that may limit effective anti-tumor immune responses and reduce responsiveness to immunotherapeutic strategies40.

We subsequently evaluated drug responses of patient-derived tumor tissues exposed to 500 µM olaparib, gemcitabine, carboplatin, and carbo–pac combinations (50 and 100 µM) (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Distinct differences in drug sensitivity were observed across patients at this fixed dose. Reproducibility was evaluated by performing repeated dose–response experiments (0–1000 µM) with the PARP inhibitor olaparib in a single patient-derived tumor sample (Fig. 5E). Non-linear regression analysis yielded comparable R² values across curves, indicating consistent model fit. Mixed-effects modeling of tumor responses to olaparib across multiple doses (0, 100, 250, 500, 750, and 1000 µM) revealed no statistically significant differences among replicate measurements, except at 500 µM in one replicate (P = 0.03), further supporting assay stability. IC50 values were calculated and plotted (Fig. 5F), with lower IC50 values indicating greater tumor sensitivity across tumor patients. IC50 values are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Although IC50 provides an informative measure of drug efficacy, it does not fully capture drug sensitivity in the OMMC platform. Patient tumor response curves were grouped by treatment and therapeutic window ratios to facilitate comparison across patients and parameters (Fig. 6A). DSSs were then calculated using a multi-parametric algorithm, integrating normalized parameters into a single comparative metric for each drug–tumor–OMMC interaction (Fig. 6B). OMMC-derived patient sensitivities and clinical data

The above data suggest that our OMMC functional precision medicine platform might be a promising strategy to determine differences in treatment response among agents. We next explored the relationship between treatment responses observed in the OMMC assay and clinical outcomes from five patients with ovarian cancer. Clinical data, including tumor type, genetic mutations, treatments received, and recurrence status, were collected and evaluated alongside DSSs derived from patient tumor tissues following treatment on OMMCs (Table 1). Clinically, responses of ovarian cancer (OC) tumors to therapy are assessed using clinical symptoms, imaging, and serum CA125 levels. In the OMMC assay, DSSs provide a quantitative measure of tumor response to each treatment, allowing comparisons across treatments within individual patients and across patients for a given treatment. In this study, we collected all individual tumor biopsies on the day of surgery; later, the processed tumors were seeded on the OMMC and exposed to the single therapies olaparib, gemcitabine, and carbo, in addition to the combination carbo-pac. Discussion

The OMMC platform introduces a physiologically relevant ex vivo system that enables functional assessment of human tumor responses within a living tissue microenvironment by incorporating an intact living mesothelial tissue from rats with preserved stromal architecture, extracellular matrix, and vascular structures that are central to peritoneal metastatic colonization, a key feature of ovarian cancer progression. Although the mesentery is of rodent origin, the fundamental cellular and structural components involved in tumor–mesothelium interactions, including mesothelial cells, fibroblasts, extracellular matrix proteins, and vascular elements, are conserved across species41–45.

The primary distinction of our OMMC model lies in its ability to support human tumor survival and evaluate drug response in the context of direct interactions with viable host tissue. This feature is particularly relevant for modeling peritoneal metastatic environments, such as those observed in ovarian cancer. Although existing systems effectively preserve tumor viability and architecture, they generally do not incorporate living, non-tumor tissue, limiting assessment of tumor–tissue interactions within a defined anatomical setting.

Importantly, OMMC is intended to complement, rather than replace, existing preclinical models by providing a rapid functional readout of tumor response. Compared with organoids and cell line–based systems, which may lack key stromal and immune components, and in vivo models, which are time- and resource-intensive, OMMC offers a balance between biological fidelity and experimental throughput. This positioning may be particularly valuable in precision oncology, where timely, patient-specific functional data are needed to inform therapeutic decisions.

Although OC treatment decisions are mainly based on histologic classifications, in combination with specific tumor genetic mutations, there is a clear percentage of patients who do not show a clinical response to standard treatments, despite having histology and molecular alterations that are biomarkers for response (i.e., high-grade serous and high response to carbo-pac or BRCA mutations and PARPi)46–52. Our study describes a platform that may enable functional assessment of tumor responses to therapy and could help inform treatment decisions.

The optimized OMMC platform supports consistent mesenteric viability ( ~ 80%) for 10–11 days across experiments and independent of animal age; however, 8-week-old rats were selected as the optimal model for this study. Reproducibility studies confirmed that established human ovarian cancer (OC) cell lines survived and expanded on the mesenteric membrane over this period, while surgically resected human tumor tissues exhibited sustained viability within the OMMC, contrasting with their limited survival under standard in vitro or ex vivo (mesentery-free) conditions. Notably, tumor engraftment was accompanied by activation of tissue-resident mesenteric macrophages, suggesting that the presence of a living stromal substrate may be required to establish key biological conditions that support tumor survival and interaction.

Peritoneal macrophages play essential roles in tissue homeostasis, repair, immune surveillance, and tumor progression53. These cells can polarize into classically activated (M1) or alternatively activated (M2) phenotypes, each exhibiting context-dependent pro- and anti-tumor functions35,38,53,54. Emerging evidence indicates that specific CD11b⁺ macrophage subsets may promote ovarian cancer metastasis in vivo through secretion of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby facilitating metastatic niche formation54,55. The potential macrophage activation observed within our system suggests that OMMC might recapitulate key aspects of tumor–host interactions, providing a dynamic microenvironment that preserves intact tumor–stromal crosstalk. Together, these findings support the utility of OMMC as a biologically responsive platform for studying human tumor tissues and investigating microenvironment-directed therapeutic strategies.

Given the critical role of the tumor microenvironment in modulating tumor progression and therapeutic response, we were able to characterize the initial immune composition of an ovarian tumor specimen (Mascot 82) within the OMMC platform. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a heterogeneous cellular architecture, with CD45⁺ leukocytes comprising a minor fraction of the total viable population and the majority consisting of CD45⁻ tumor-associated cells, including a substantial B7H3⁺ subset with variable Ki67 expression. Within the immune compartment, myeloid-lineage cells—particularly M2 tumor-associated macrophages—predominated, whereas T cells and NK cells were comparatively less abundant, consistent with an immunosuppressive microenvironment. The presence of proliferative B7H3⁺ tumor cells supports a potential dual role for B7H3 as both a tumor-associated antigen and an immunomodulatory factor39. Although derived from a single patient sample, these findings establish an initial framework for defining the immune landscape within OMMC and underscore the importance of incorporating micro-environmental context into functional drug testing strategies.

Our OMMC system enabled us to test tumor responses of established human OC cell lines and surgically dissected tumor tissues from patients to FDA-approved standard-of-care treatments for OC patients. Reproducibility of tumor response was proven, showing the functional reliability of our OMMC. Simultaneously, because we could also measure the impact of drug toxicity on the healthy mesenteries, we were able to calculate DSS for each treatment within each cell line or tumor tissue, in which a series of 11 kinetic parameters were taken into account to reflect a more complete measure of the treatment efficacy and potentially facilitate a tool to guide precision medicine in the future. The DSS values per cell line and treatment suggested similarities in tumor cell killing efficacy for single carbo and its combination (carbo-pac 100) (Fig. 4D). Lower and variable DSS values from Gemcitabine treatment (ES-2 DSS: 62, SKOV3 DSS: 53) were seen when compared with taxane-platin-based therapies. In addition, ES-2 was more sensitive to the PARPi olaparib than SKOV3 (ES-2:77, SKOV3:44). These results from our OMMC are aligned with in vitro data generated by the Genomic of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer Project56. These two cell lines are classified as distinct tumor types: SKOV3 is a non-serous carcinoma cell line, while ES-2 is an atypical CCOC that resembles high-grade serous carcinoma more closely57. However, there are some similarities in the genetic profiles of these two well-known OC cell lines, including TP53 mutation, BRCA wild-type status, and HRD negativity. Given this, we might have expected both cell lines to exhibit less sensitivity to PARPi treatment. Thus, these results suggest that we should not just consider BRCA- and TP53-mutations or HRD status58 to delineate the sensitivity of different drugs to PARPi and other chemotherapeutics, but the entire mutational profile of the potential oncogenes/tumor suppressor genes for each cell line.

In our small cohort study with 5 patients, the OMMC allowed us to elucidate preliminary associations between the ex vivo tumor responses from patients reflected in the DSSs and the results from the clinic after being exposed to similar treatments. Since we collected tumor biopsies on the day of surgery, some patients had already received NACT carbo-pac; nonetheless, residual tumors after NACT, as well as tumors at primary debulking, were all effectively tested on the OMMC. To better discuss potential associations, we focused on clinical outcomes, such as complete or partial remission, the use of maintenance therapy (PARPi and/or bevacizumab) and the recurrence rate, in relation to DSSs of these treatments revealed from OMMC.

All MASCOT patients went into complete remission after frontline treatment with surgery and chemotherapy. On our OMMC, these tumors responded well to carbo and carbo-pac treatment when we established relative comparisons of their DSSs to the other treatments (rows Table 1) and also showed homogenous tumor responses among patients (columns Table 1). These results aligned with the patient’s clinical outcomes, showing a positive association between these two separate results. It is well-known that the majority of OC patients (up to 85%) can achieve remission after front-line carbo-pac chemotherapy in combination with cytoreductive surgery, regardless of whether chemotherapy is given in a neoadjuvant or adjuvant manner. MASCOT Patients #57 and 67 both underwent primary debulking surgery as opposed to interval surgical debulking (Patients # 43, 50 and 62); however, tumors from all patients had similarly strong responses to platin/taxane-based treatment on the OMMC.

In the OMMC, we included gemcitabine, as a nucleoside metabolic inhibitor, a common second-line agent for OC treatment. Since none of these patients were treated with gem in the clinic during the period of our study, we could not explore associations with our OMMC. Interestingly, if we focus on the DSSs of gem, carbo, and carbo-pac of all patients, our ex vivo platform found heterogeneity in tumor responses to gem among different patients (DSSs columns Table 1), in contrast to relatively homogeneous responses from carbo-pac-based therapies. This aligns with the usual greater sensitivity of OC to frontline carbo-pac treatment as opposed to second-line gemcitabine therapy, as seen in the clinic.

After cytoreductive surgery, for patients with biomarkers for response to PARPi (either somatic or germline BRCA mutations or HRD + ), maintenance therapy with PARPi +/- bevacizumab is recommended after 6 cycles of ACT. In our OMMC, we included olaparib as the only PARPi; however, in the clinic, either olaparib or niraparib was used. If we compare the DSSs of olaparib with the DSSs of platin-based treatment (OMMC section, rows 1–6 Table 1) of the same patient and for all patients, they were lower for the four patients classified as HGSC (Mascot Patients #43, 50, 62 and 67), suggesting less relative efficacy than carbo and the combination of carbo-pac. If we look closely at the DSS values for olaparib across patients (OMMC section, column 1 Table 1—53, 36, 51, and 56 for Mascot Patients #43, 50, 67, and 62, respectively), we noticed Mascot Patient #50 had the lowest DSS of 36, while the other ones were similar. In the clinic, Mascot Patient #50 had an HRD + tumor that should be predictive of response to PARPi treatment; however, this patient did recur within four months. Thus, these findings suggest the importance of the functional drug testing capabilities of OMMC, given that the OMMC reflected the lowest DSS of this treatment among all patients and was linked to the only recurrence within 6 months of frontline therapy.

The tumor from Mascot Patient #57 was classified as stage 1C, CCOC. In the OMMC, the tumor tissue of this patient showed a strong response to all drug therapies used and the highest DSS values for olaparib and gemcitabine among the rest of the patients, meaning potentially greater sensitivities for these drugs. Notably, the CCOC cell line ES-2 exhibited significantly higher DSS for these two drugs compared to SKOV3, indicating similarities in tumor responses between the ES-2 cell line and the patient tumor, both classified as CCOC. Importantly, this patient remained recurrence-free for a six-month period. Studies have reported that CCOC tumors can be sensitive to PARPi even in the absence of BRCA/HDR mutations59, however, CCOC is known to be resistant to platinum-based chemotherapy, while a small subset shows a positive response60,61. This patient’s tumor tissue responded well to carbo and carbo-pac-based therapy in the clinic and on our platform. CCOC is characterized by genetic alterations distinct from those found in HGSC, and the therapeutic options with PARPi are limited due to the low frequency of BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations in these cancers62. Consequently, a great deal of attention in OC cell type has been paid to synthetic lethal therapies that target vulnerabilities conferred by ARID1A deficiency. Like the BRCA1 and BRCA2 proteins, BAF250A/ARID1A promotes homologous recombination-mediated repair of DNA double-strand breaks, suggesting that PARP inhibition might be therapeutically effective63. Multiple ongoing and completed clinical trials are assessing the efficacy of the PARP inhibitors olaparib and niraparib in ovarian and other malignancies, incorporating ARID1A deficiency as a predictive biomarker (NCT04065269, registered 1 August 2019; NCT04042831, registered 29 July 2019; NCT03207347, registered 7 July 2017). Mascot Patient #57 did have a tumor with ARID1A deficiency, which may explain sensitivity to PARPi treatment on the OMMC platform. Here again, we have demonstrated that results from OMMC aligned with the clinical outcomes in this case, as well as suggesting our OMMC could be a valuable tool to carry out functional drug testing for precision oncology.

It is important to interpret the objectives and scope of the OMMC platform within the context of its inherent limitations. The OMMC model provides a physiologically relevant ex vivo system to study tumor–tissue interactions, capturing conserved biological processes such as local immune responses and therapeutic effects, while preserving key cellular and extracellular components that are broadly conserved between rodents and humans41–44. Nevertheless, species-specific differences in genetics, signaling pathways, immune regulation, and tissue architecture may influence experimental outcomes. The OMMC platform is intended at present as a short-term, functional ex vivo platform that complements, rather than replaces, human-based models.

In addition, the OMMC platform may not yet achieve the throughput of fully in vitro systems (e.g., organoids or conventional cell lines), particularly for large-scale drug library screening. However, such simplified in vitro systems do not preserve native tissue architecture, extracellular matrix composition, or tumor–host interactions. In contrast, OMMC maintains these critical microenvironmental features, making it especially valuable in contexts where biological relevance and tissue fidelity are prioritized over maximal assay scale. In its current configuration, the system supports moderate-throughput drug testing. Ongoing optimization, including standardized tissue preparation, partial automation of tumor deposition, and integration of AI-based image analysis (currently under development), is expected to enhance throughput and support progression toward translational and clinical applications.

Viability and cell density measurements alone do not capture changes in mesenteric and tumor cell composition or underlying microenvironmental dynamics. Future studies will incorporate complementary approaches, including flow cytometry and immunohistochemical analyses, to assess cellular heterogeneity, extracellular matrix remodeling, and paracrine signaling, thereby enabling a more comprehensive characterization of tumor–host interactions within the platform. Consistent with our previous work31, the current analytical framework emphasizes relative comparisons: evaluating the effectiveness of multiple drugs against an individual patient’s tumor, as well as assessing the performance of specific agents across diverse tumor types. As additional tumors are analyzed, the expanding dataset will enhance the platform’s potential to inform clinicians regarding which agents may be more or less effective for a given tumor profile. Importantly, however, the system is not intended to define optimal clinical dosing regimens or to predict the magnitude of residual tumor response at a given dose.

In summary, we present an ex vivo platform that enables functional assessment of tumor responses within a living tissue microenvironment. The OMMC system supports short-term survival and drug response evaluation of patient-derived ovarian tumor tissue, while incorporating key features of tumor–host interactions. Although further validation in larger cohorts is required, this approach may complement existing molecular and preclinical models and contribute to improving personalized treatment strategies in ovarian cancer.

Methods

Animals

All experiments were conducted on deceased animals in accordance with protocols approved by the University of North Carolina Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Randomization was not applicable, as experiments were performed using postmortem tissues and sample allocation was based on tissue availability. Adult dams and 3-, 4-, 8-, and 16-week-old female Sprague–Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories) were used. Animals were acclimatized for 48 h and housed in an AAALAC-accredited facility at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill under controlled conditions (20–24 °C; 40–70% humidity). Euthanasia was performed using 5% isoflurane for 10 min followed by cervical dislocation

Surgical procedure and mesentery collection

All mesenteric dissections were performed immediately following euthanasia. The rats were anesthetized and euthanized using an overdose of 5% isoflurane for a period of 10 min followed by a second method of euthanasia: cervical dislocation on rats weighing less than 250 g and thoracotomy on dams. The rats were placed in a prone position, and the skin on the abdominal area was disinfected with 7.5% iodine followed by 70% ethyl alcohol. A longitudinal incision of the skin and the abdominal muscle was made along the midline, starting right above the urinary aperture up to the xiphoidal process of the sternum in the thoracic region. Subsequently, the intestines were exposed, and the mesentery’s regions of interest were identified and dissected using sterile microscissors and forceps. Ten to twelve mesenteric membranes were harvested per animal and used as independent organotypic culture substrates. Each tissue piece (one per well) supported 5–7 tumor spots exposed to a single drug concentration. Drug testing was performed across eight concentrations (0–1000 μM), requiring eight tissue pieces per replicate. Given that each animal yields sufficient tissue to complete a full concentration range, testing a single drug for one patient-derived tumor required approximately one rat per replicate, with additional animals used as needed for multiple treatments or replicates.

Cell lines and culture

Human ovarian cancer well-established cell lines SKOV3 (non-high-grade serous carcinoma cell line) and ES-2 (unusual clear cell adenocarcinoma but appears more like high-grade serous carcinoma) were obtained from the Lineberger Cancer Center Tissue Culture Facility of the University of North Carolina. Transduction of these cell lines with lentiviral vectors encoding optical reporters mCherry-firefly-luciferase (LV-mCherry-FLuc) was carried out in our laboratory. SKOV3 and ES-2 cell lines were cultured and propagated in McCoy’s 5 A media (GibcoTM) supplemented with 10% FBS (Avantor®Seradigm, Premium Grade) and 1% P/S (GibcoTM) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. The cells were used at 80% to 90% confluency for all the experiments, with cell viability ranging from 95 to 98% (Countess II FL, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Chemotherapies

Paclitaxel from Selleck Chemicals (S1150 | CAS: 159634-47-6), olaparib from Selleck Chemicals (S1060 | CAS: 890090-75-2), and gemcitabine from ApexBio (A8437 | CAS: 95058-81-4) were reconstituted in 100% DMSO to make solutions of 50 mM. (Fisher BioReagents). Carboplatin from Sigma-Aldrich (C2538-100MG | CAS: 41575-94-4) was reconstituted in PBS only and used immediately after. Either ovarian cancer cell lines or patient-derived tumors were exposed to monotherapies with carboplatin, paclitaxel, olaparib, or gemcitabine, as well as combination therapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel, for 3 days. For single-agent treatments, drugs were added to the culture medium beneath the transwell inserts at final concentrations of 0,10, 50, 100, 250, 500, 750, and 1000 µM. For combination therapy, carboplatin was administered at the same concentrations as in single-agent experiments, while paclitaxel was maintained at fixed concentrations of 10, 50, or 100 µM. These combination conditions were designated as Carbo–Pac 10, Carbo–Pac 50, and Carbo–Pac 100, respectively. A fixed final DMSO concentration of 3% was maintained across all treatment groups.

Ovarian cancer cell lines on OMMC

SKOV3–mCherry-FLuc and ES-2–mCherry-FLuc ovarian cancer cells were seeded onto OMMCs as discrete tumor spots 2 h after mesentery isolation. Cultures were maintained in MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Avantor Seradigm) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) beneath the transwell inserts. Each mesenteric membrane accommodated 5–7 tumor spots consisting of approximately 2.0 × 10⁴ cells suspended in 1 μL.

Ovarian cell lines' viability and drug responses on OMMC

Tumor cell viability was evaluated using longitudinal BLI on Days 1, 4, 7, and 10, or on Days 1 and 5 in independent experiments, with all values normalized to Day 1. For drug-response studies, imaging was conducted at baseline (Day 2, prior to drug exposure) and repeated on Day 5. Immediately following each imaging session, wells and transwell inserts were washed three times with PBS to remove residual luciferin, and fresh MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin was added beneath each insert. Each tumor-bearing mesenteric membrane (one membrane per well) was exposed to a single drug concentration or combination therapy. The single-agent and combination treatments were applied as previously described. Evaluation of six treatments across this concentration range required 48 membranes per tumor model, corresponding to a minimum of 4–5 rats.

Following treatment, cultures were maintained for 3 days at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Total flux values were normalized to the Day 2 baseline measurement for each individual membrane and expressed as a percentage of the baseline signal. Dose–response curves were generated, and treatment groups were compared to untreated controls.

Preparing human tumor tissue for in vitro, ex vivo culture and survival

After consent, 1 to 4 g of metastatic ovarian tumor tissues surgically resected from patients at UNC hospitals were placed in sterile Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) from GibcoTM and kept at 4 °C while transferred to the UNC Tissue Procurement Core Facility (TPF). After approval for release, the tumor sample was processed in the Hingtgen laboratory within 1 h and minced into pieces of about 0.5 mm diameter using a disposable scalpel and PBS. Tumor pieces were stored in a cryogenic vial and frozen in tissue freezing medium (CryoStor CS10) in a FreezeCell™ at −80 °C overnight before transfer into liquid nitrogen. When tumor tissue was required for experimentation, frozen samples were thawed at 37 °C for 5 min, washed with PBS, centrifuged, and resuspended in MEM supplemented with 20% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and recombinant human insulin–zinc (Gibco). The tumor tissue suspension was subsequently filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer using a sterile syringe plunger to apply controlled mechanical dissociation. Gentle pressure was applied, with intermittent addition of culture medium to facilitate passage, until the entire sample had been processed through the strainer. The homogeneous minced and filtered suspension was then divided into 4 equal portions, and then each 50 mg tissue sample was transduced by incubation with 1 mL mCherry-FLuc lentivirus (1.5 × 10⁷ vg/mL) and 2 μL polybrene for 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the tumor tissues were washed with PBS three times and centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed. Pellets were reconstituted with 4 different growth mediums: 1. DMEM with 20% FBS and 1% PS), 2. MEM with 20% FBS, 1% PS, and insulin human recombinant Zinc (GibcoTM), 3. DMEM/F-12 with 20% FBS and 1% PS and 4. McCoy’s 5 A (modified) Medium, all from GibcoTM with 20% FBS and 1% PS. All cultures were initiated in a volume of 1 mL per well using 12-well plates (Corning Life Sciences) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The minced/filtered tissues were allowed to settle and attach to the bottom of the plates for 2-3 days. The floating cellular debris was then carefully aspirated, wells were washed with PBS, and 2 mL of fresh medium was added. A potential tumor cell survival was visually checked every two days and also by BLI on Day 6. The culture medium was routinely changed every 3 ~ 5 days.

Patient ovarian tumor preparation for engraftment onto OMMCs

Following tumor processing as described above, and after the transduction step, the finely minced and filtered tissue suspension was resuspended and distributed into ~200 tumor spots, corresponding to ~0.25 mg of tissue in 1 µL of PBS per spot on OMMCs. After tumor spot placement, plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. The culture media was changed after 24 h and was subsequently changed every 3 days. The initial viability of the patient tissue was measured by BLI on Day 3 for all the bearing-tumor mesenteries before any chemotherapy was administered, and a second BLI was read on Day 6. Data values were normalized to Day 3 for all groups and transformed into survival percentages. Each group was compared to an untreated control group.

Bioluminescence and Fluorescence Imaging Analysis

Optical imaging was performed using an AMI HTX optical imaging system (Spectral Instruments Imaging), and images were analyzed with Aura Imaging Software (version 3.2). For BLI, IVISbrite™ D-luciferin Potassium Salt (PerkinElmer) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 0.375 mg/mL prior to image acquisition in all experiments. Fluorescence images of mesenteric tissues were acquired using 535 nm excitation and a 630 nm emission filter, with an exposure time of 1s, a field of view (FOV) of 10, and an f-stop of 2. For BLI acquisition, mesenteric tissues and ovarian cancer cell lines were incubated with D-luciferin for 5 min prior to imaging. Patient-derived tumor-bearing mesenteric tissues were incubated for 10 min to ensure adequate substrate equilibration. All BLI images were acquired with an exposure time of 60 s, f-stop 2, and FOV 10. Radiance (photons s⁻¹ cm⁻² sr⁻¹) and/or total flux (photons s⁻¹) were quantified for each individual sample. Values were normalized to the corresponding baseline measurement for each sample and expressed as a percentage of the baseline signal or fold change, as indicated. Treatment groups were compared to untreated controls.

Mesentery viability measured by bioluminescence

Six freshly prepared mesenteric membranes with an approximate diameter of 1.5 cm were incubated with 1 µL polybrene and 2 mL of a lentivirus with the mCherry fluorescence reporter protein fused to firefly luciferase (LV–mCh-FL) at 7.5e6 vg/mL at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the mesenteric tissue was washed three times with 1X Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove the residual virus. The mesenteric tissue was then replated in fresh MEM culture media supplemented with 10% FBS (Avantor®Seradigm, Premium Grade)1% P/S (GibcoTM). On Days 4, 6, 8, and 10 following initial mesenteric membrane plating, bioluminescence intensity was measured for each transduced membrane. After each imaging time point, residual luciferin was removed by washing with PBS, and fresh culture medium was added.

Mesentery viability and tissue health condition measured by fluorescence using the propidium iodide (PI) assay

By measuring the fluorescence emitted by dead cells in the mesentery, the PI (Sigma-Aldrich ®) assay was used to assess the tissue health condition across experiments, the viability over time of fresh tissue isolated right after dissection, and the potential local toxicity of each treatment the membrane was exposed to. Mesentery viability of various rat ages (3, 4, 8, and 16 weeks old) and dams was compared to select the optimal animal age to be used for the OMMCs in this study. Toxicity was assessed at the conclusion of each therapy assay (after 3 days of treatment). PI was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL and incubated for 1 h prior to measurement of fluorescence intensity. Positive control was generated by killing the OMMCs via incubation with 100% DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) for 3 days. A killing curve was generated after the tissue was exposed to increasing concentrations of DMSO (0.5, 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50, 75, and 100%) in the culture media.

Mesentery cell count per region, and membrane thickness

Five mesenteries of 8-week-old rats with an approximate surface area of 1.76 cm2 were selected, and 5 regions in each mesenteric sample were randomly studied to estimate the thickness and the number of cells in the membranes belonging to the Jejunum and Ileum separately. A total of 10 mesenteries were killed by exposition of 100% DMSO for 3 days, and the PI assay was applied to stain all dead cells. A confocal laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 780) at the UNC-CH Neuroscience Microscopy Core was used to measure the thickness and cell number of mesenteric membranes. For thickness measurements, Z-stacks were acquired as a series of optical sections using the following parameters: Z-step size: 4 µm; number of slices/total depth: 10; Pixel size/XY resolution: 0.2–0.3 µm; Laser/detector gain: 594 nm laser, Master Gain 700 (photon counting mode). Imaging was performed with Zeiss Zen software, and membrane thickness was determined by measuring the distance between the top and bottom optical sections.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed using the primary antibody for a cluster of differentiation molecule 11B (CD11B [Abcam, ab133357], 1:250. Day 1 cultured mesenteries without tumor spots and Day 3 cultured mesenteries with SKOV3 mCherry-FLuc tumor spots were fixed in the same culture plates, with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stored at 4 °C. After 48 h of fixation, the mesenteric membranes were carefully washed three times with PBS and then stored at 4 °C until IHC was conducted. Samples were then washed for 10 min in 0.1% triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature, then blocked in 5% fetal bovine serum (Avantor®Seradigm, Premium Grade) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated for 24 h at room temperature in a primary antibody solution (CD11B), and a blocking buffer with a rotating motion, then washed three times in PBS for 10 min. Next, the mesenteries were incubated with a secondary antibody consisting of a blocking buffer solution, and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11008, 1:1000) for 1 h in a darkroom, then washed three times with PBS for 10min and mounted on microscopic slides. Liquid mounting (ProLongTM Gold Antifade Mountant, Cat: 10144) was applied, coverslips were added, and slides were allowed to cure overnight. Z-stack images were acquired using a Zeiss 780 confocal microscope at UNC-CH Neuroscience Microscopy Core. Z-stack images are processed by converting them into maximum intensity projection (Max IP) images. The brightness of Max IP images was then further adjusted to accurately assess and present the overexpression of CD11B of activated myeloid cells.

Flow cytometry analysis of patient-derived ovarian tumor samples

Cryopreserved tumor specimens were thawed at 37 °C with gentle agitation and enzymatically dissociated using the Human Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-095-929) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm strainer to obtain a single-cell preparation. Cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Near-IR viability dye (Invitrogen, L34980) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. Antibody concentrations were titrated in control cell lines and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) prior to application in patient samples.

Following viability staining, cells were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15710) for 15 min and blocked using Human Fc Block (BioLegend, 422301), Monocyte Blocker (BioLegend, 426101), and Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, 563794) before surface staining. Surface staining was performed for 30 min in PBS using anti-CD45 PE-Cy7 (BD, 560915), anti-CD11b BUV805 (BD, 568345), anti-B7-H3 (CD276) RB705 (BD, 756996), anti-CD206 RY703 (BD, 770722), anti-CD86 RB780 (BD, 755466), and anti-CD56 BV711 (BioLegend, 318335). Cells were then permeabilized using the FlowX FoxP3/Transcription Factor Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (R&D Systems, FC012-100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and stained intracellularly overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD3 BV421 (BioLegend, 300434) and anti–Ki-67 BUV395 (BD, 564071). After washing, cells were resuspended in PBS and stained with Helix NP Blue nuclear dye (BioLegend, 425305) prior to acquisition.

Single-color controls and full-stained positive controls (human PBMCs, U251 cells, or compensation beads; Invitrogen, 01-3333-42) were used for spectral unmixing and compensation. Positive and negative populations were defined using unstained controls and fully stained reference samples. Data were acquired on a Cytek Aurora 5 L (Cytek Biosciences) at the UNC–Chapel Hill Flow Cytometry Core and analyzed using SpectroFlo v3.0.3 (Cytek Biosciences).

Calculating drug sensitivity scores (DSSs)

To calculate the DSSs, we followed our well-detailed method recently published52, which compares the tumor cell survival, measured via BLI, to the health of the OMMCs, measured via PIA. An overall drug sensitivity value resulted from the integrated and meticulous calculations involving eleven combined and weighted parameters: 1. killing at maximum dose (Max Kill), 2. dose required to kill 10% of the tumor (EC10), 3. dose required to kill 25% of the tumor (EC25), 4. dose required to kill 50% of the tumor (EC50), 5. dose required to kill 75% of the tumor (EC75), 6. dose required to kill 90% of the tumor (EC90), 7. slope through the EC50, 8. the area under the curve (AUC), 9. tumor growth acceleration, 10. biphasic killing (rapid killing at low doses and limited additional killing at higher doses), and 11. incomplete kill at the highest dose. Overall DSS values from 0 to 100 indicate increasing efficacy in tumor cell killing relative to OMMC toxicity, whereas values from 0 to −100 indicate conditions in which tumor cells exhibit greater survival than OMMCs under a given treatment. Dose–response values were calculated by linear interpolation of raw data rather than from best-fit curve equations.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests and sample sizes are included in figure legends. All data are shown as mean ± Std. In all cases, the p-values are represented as follows: ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, and not statistically significant when P > 0.05. In all cases, the stated “n” value is either the number of mesenteric tissues or the number of tumor spots placed on the OMMCs to obtain data points for each. Mean values between two groups were compared using t-tests with Sidak’s correction for multiple comparisons. Mean values between three or more groups were compared to the control by using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test or Tukey test to compare all groups to each other. For the analysis of reproducibility and variability, non-linear regression curves were generated to identify the best fit, and a comparison of mean values across multiple doses of experimental repeats was performed using a mixed-effects model. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.1.0). For all quantifications of BLI or FL, the samples being compared were processed in parallel and imaged using the same settings, scale, and laser power.
