Highlight: How the clock ticks—species-specific mutation rate informs koala conservation
Authors: Shady A Kuster
Published: 2026

The mutation rate of a genome is one of the fundamental evolutionary properties that determine the amount of genetic variation available in a population (Kardos et al. 2021). Because the mutation rate itself is a trait that evolves and varies by species (Lynch et al. 2016), demographic inferences that rely on mutation rate estimates (e.g. reconstructing changes in effective population size through time) can be skewed if an incorrect rate is used. Mutation rates can be difficult to estimate in natural populations where genomic resources are typically scarce, because they require accurate genomic comparisons across generations or between species. This difficulty often precludes evolutionary biologists from associating past population changes with specific environmental shifts, which has implications for the utility of genomic data to inform conservation measures.

As the only extant species of Phascolarctidae, the iconic yet vulnerable Australian koala typifies this challenge. Previous estimates of the koala's demographic history identified an early population decline approximately 30 to 40 thousand years ago (kya). It is estimated that humans first arrived to Australia shortly before this time, implicating our species in the historical population decline of this marsupial. Crucially, human and mouse mutation rates, not koala mutation rates, were used to deduce this demographic history.

When demographic histories are estimated for a species with scant genomic resources, a few approaches can be used to estimate their mutation rate. A phylogenetic estimate can be made using neutral substitutions that arose during divergence of two species. Since the koala's closest living relative is the distantly-related wombat (family Vombatidae), an alternative technique that sequences the genomes of parents and their offspring may produce a more accurate estimate. “Phylogenetic approaches to mutation rate estimation compare genomes between species to estimate the average evolutionary rate over millions of years, while the parent-offspring trio approach compares genomes between an individual and its parents to identify new mutations,” explains Toby Kovacs, a PhD candidate at the University of Sydney working on how mutation rate estimates impact demographic reconstruction. “Trios give a direct estimate of the number of mutations occurring within the span of one generation, which we expect will be more relevant to the short timescales that are involved in analyses of populations.”

Under the supervision of Drs. Carolyn Hogg and Simon Ho, Kovacs used four parent-offspring trios to estimate koala mutation rates and present their findings in a new Molecular Biology and Evolution article (Kovacs et al. 2026). According to Kovacs, “We aimed to refine our estimate of the demographic history of the koala and test whether its population decline was tied to the arrival of humans in Australia.”

Kovacs’ parent-offspring trios revealed a mutation rate of 6.12 × 10−9 mutations/bp/generation, a value nearly half of the estimated human per-generation mutation rate of 11.6 × 10−9 mutations/bp/generation (Bergeron et al. 2023). Therefore, the previous demographic estimates that relied on human mutation rates and that pointed to human involvement in early koala population declines may have been incorrect. To put their main hypothesis to the test, Kovacs et al. (2026) reconstructed demographic trajectories of koalas using whole genomes from 457 individuals from five populations. Their results pushed the early population decline of koalas to around 100,000 years ago, tens of thousands of years prior to the first arrival of humans to the continent (Fig. 1). Around this time, the local environment was undergoing aridification at the onset of the last glacial period, which had lasting impacts on Australian fauna. For koalas, effective population size was reduced significantly, likely as eastern and western koala ranges became fragmented.

The reconstruction of the koala's past effective population size using a newly estimated, species-specific mutation rate (Kovacs et al. 2026) suggests that koala populations declined rapidly after the onset of the last glacial period. During this time, koala habitat was fragmentated during aridification of the Australian continent. The first humans to arrive in Australia did so well after this decline, implying that environmental changes are the major cause of the large reduction in koala population size during the Pleistocene. Kovacs et al. (2026) further estimated recombination rates for each of five koala populations and found low rates compared with eutherian mammals, with significant variation among the populations. These recombination rate estimates permitted the inference of more recent demographic history and revealed population size recovery in northern populations that previous studies, using a general recombination rate, did not observe. In recent times, koala populations have experienced varied threats of extinction, and a lower recombination rate is likely to challenge the species’ recovery due to decreased selection efficacy. However, one of the studied koala populations was found to have a higher recombination rate which could have contributed to their substantial recovery from a recent severe bottleneck.

By using koala-specific mutation rate estimates to reconstruct the species’ past, Kovacs et al. (2026) uncovered a new story: koala populations struggled to survive the environmental changes during the last big ice age, and only some contemporary populations have recovered since. The decline continued through the arrival of humans and was eventually exacerbated through the fur trade. Throughout the time since, and particularly in contemporary times, populations have experienced bushfires, habitat fragmentation and encroachment, feral dog attacks, and collisions with vehicles, all of which are affected by human interventions in their habitat. Determining how conservation strategies will be most effective depends on leveraging information on genetic variation of specific populations. As Kovacs states, “Accurate mutation and recombination rate estimates allow for more reliable reconstructions of population history, providing a stronger foundation for conservation strategies that aim to preserve genetic diversity and evolutionary resilience.”
