Distribution of antibiotic resistance genes in rodent droppings in a hospital environment
Authors: Chidinma O Akanazu, Uzochukwu G Ekeleme, Amarachi B Nwokoro, Ezejindu Cosmas Nnadozie, Onuigbo Martin Chinemerem, Nwokeleme Ogechi Chikaodi, Ijeoma G Chukwuemeka, Queeneth C Onuoha, Christopher Chike A Okereke, Chiamaka C Ogujiuba, Chigozie C Ukachukwu, Juliana C Omire, MaryRose Ogechi Echeta, Ugonma Winnie Dozie, Chimezie Christian Iwuala, Chinwendu L Opara, Ikwuagwu Vivian Ogochukwu, Uchechukwu M Chukwuocha

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interaction of rodents with humans in the hospital environment may facilitate transmission of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria. This study investigated the presence of genes harboured by the antibiotic-resistant bacteria in rodent droppings from nine locations in the Federal University Teaching Hospital (FUTH), Owerri, by using a purposive sampling method. Standard microbiological techniques were used to isolate and identify the bacteria present, followed by antibiotic sensitivity via the broth dilution method and antibiotic resistance genes by conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR).ResultsFive species of bacteria were isolated and identified as Staphylococcus sciuri subsp.lentus, Staphylococcus gallinarum, Raoultella ornithinolytica, Leuconostoc lactis, and Burkholderia cepacia complex. The bacteria demonstrated low MIC values below their respective resistance thresholds for gentamicin and intermediate susceptibility to ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin. Three (3) isolates (12.5%) were defined as MDR, with resistance to three (3) antimicrobial classes, while the remaining isolates were resistant to one or two classes and were thus defined as non-MDR. MDR was detected only in Raoultella ornithinolytica isolates from MW10. Isolates from FW7, MW6, HH, KA, SS, OR, HR, and MS were resistant particularly to β-lactams, as well as tetracyclines or macrolides, but it was not sufficient for MDR classification. SHV and TEM antibiotic resistance genes were seen in Raoultella ornithinolytica and Burkholderia cepacia complex, respectively, while MsrA was seen in Staphylococcus spp.ConclusionThe findings show that rodent droppings in the hospital environment contained antibiotic-resistant bacteria and selected resistance gene markers. These findings provide preliminary, context-specific evidence of environmental contamination and highlight the need for improved sanitation, rodent control, and further studies using larger and more representative samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05219-w.Keywords: Rodent, Droppings, Bacteria, Antimicrobial resistance

Background

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), which are among the most widespread in the microbiome, are a significant public health concern on a global scale [9]. The environment is facing a threat to many habitats due to the overuse of antibiotics and evolutionary processes in the resistome. ARGs and ARBs of farms, cities, hospitals, and wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) or as water runoffs can be deposited in water, soil, and air [43].

The studies of Rajendra and Keugta [30] reported widespread prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes and bacteria in both soil and water across various climates. Human activity on the environment may lead to increased transmission of ARB and ARGs in the environment. Such activities encompass excessive use of antibiotics in clinics, hospitals and nursing homes [14]. The other activities encompass the use of antibiotics to prevent diseases and achieve growth in farm animals and aquaculture [43].

Recent studies of Karnwal et al. [20] have demonstrated that rodent populations can harbour a diverse array of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and ARGs, including genes conferring resistance to β-lactams, macrolides, and other commonly used antimicrobials. These resistance determinants are often located on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, facilitating horizontal gene transfer between bacterial species. Consequently, rodent droppings may serve not only as indicators of environmental contamination but also as reservoirs of transferable resistance genes capable of entering clinical transmission pathways [38].

Rodents are the most diverse of the order of mammals that contains over 2,200 species; rodents comprise close to forty two percent of all biodiversity of mammals worldwide [3]. They serve as great reservoirs of various zoonotic pathogens such as different viruses, bacteria, protozoa and fungi and are especially competent in disease transmission, particularly in species that are synanthropic and which are in close contact with people [6]. As of the start of April 2025, the scientific community recognises about fifteen thousand two hundred and five rodent-related viruses that are spread over thirty-two viral families [17]. The most significant ones among them are representatives of the Arenaviridae, Hantaviridae, Picornaviridae, Coronaviridae, and Poxviridae families. Rodents, both in urban and rural areas, are highly effective vectors spreading pathogens in an environment with a diversity of ecological settings [34].

Rodent droppings are a common indication of rodent presence, and one cannot tell if a rodent is carrying a sickness just by looking at it because many diseases do not manifest as any visible illness in rodents [26]. Rodents, in fact, play a significant role in the transmission and development of a variety of infectious diseases, including leptospirosis, leishmaniasis, salmonellosis, plague, lassa fever, viral hemorrhagic fevers, and others, for which they are well-known reservoirs and hosts [1]. These diseases can be transmitted to people directly through handling of rodents, contact with rodent faeces (poop), urine or saliva (such as through breathing in air or eating food that is contaminated with rodent waste), or rodent bites [26]. Rodents can also carry ticks, mites or fleas that can act as vectors to transmit diseases between rodents and man. Additionally, rodents that were inadvertently consumed by animals could act as a medium for the spread of disease to people if the livestock's products were improperly handled before consumption [1].

Rodents pose a significant health risk when found in hospital environments due to their potential role as carriers of various pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi [32]. The presence of rodents and their droppings in hospitals raises concerns about the transmission of infectious diseases to vulnerable patients, healthcare workers and visitors [34]. Furthermore, the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant strains of pathogens is a growing global health threat, and hospitals must be vigilant in monitoring and controlling such resistance [34]. Despite the potential risks associated with rodent infestations and their droppings in hospitals, there is a lack of comprehensive research and assessment regarding the specific bacterial pathogens present and the extent of antimicrobial resistance in these bacterial pathogens within hospital settings.

A global public health concern for both clinical and veterinary medicine is antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and one of the main infectious sources of zoonotic diseases, including AMR bacteria, is rodent droppings [22]. Despite the known and emerging threats from rodent infestation, the role of rodents as reservoirs and vectors of pathogens with anti-microbial resistance (AMR), as well as the extent of their resistance, is still poorly understood.

This knowledge gap hinders the development of effective control measures and infection prevention strategies. Therefore, the primary problem to be addressed in this research is: "Assessing the Presence, Diversity, and Antimicrobial-Resistance Profiles of Bacterial Isolates in Rodent Droppings Found in Hospital Environments." This problem statement underscores the need to investigate the types of bacterial pathogens carried by rodents in hospitals, evaluate their resistance to antimicrobial agents, and develop evidence-based strategies to mitigate the potential transmission of diseases and the emergence of antimicrobial resistance in healthcare settings. The general objective is to determine the bacterial pathogens and their antibiotic-resistant strains in rodent droppings.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was used for this study. This design allowed the simultaneous assessment of a diverse dataset that potentially reveals patterns or variations in rodent infestations, bacterial contamination, and the distribution of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in rodent droppings across different locations within the hospital environment.

Study setting

The setting of the study was Federal University Teaching Hospital (FUTH), Owerri, Imo State, Nigeria. It is a public health care centre located in Owerri city in Imo State, southeastern Nigeria. It was founded as a colonial dispensary in 1903, promoted to a District Hospital, then a General Hospital, before becoming a Federal Medical Centre in 1995 [28], and finally it became a Federal University Teaching Hospital on 10th May, 2023. It is a 700-bed capacity hospital.

Study population

The study population of 115 rodent droppings seen within the premises of Federal University Teaching Hospital (FUTH), Owerri, was used in this study.

Sample size and sampling methods

Sample size

The 115 rodent droppings used for this study were collected from hospital-selected locations where rodent activities had previously been observed. The sample size was guided by sampling adequacy and spatial coverage. This ensured adequate representation of sampling sites and improved the reliability of the findings. This approach is consistent with approaches used in environmental field studies where sampling is often constrained by logistical and operational factors [13]. A formal probability-based sample size was not used given the exploratory nature of the study and not a population-level inference. The final sample size reflects the maximum number of samples obtainable within the study period.

Sampling method

A purposive sampling method was adopted in choosing the sampling sites (locations), as previous evidence of rodent presence was used, namely, places where rodent droppings were observed frequently. This was deemed suitable since the aim of the study was to examine the distribution of the antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the environments that had a high probability of contamination, as opposed to estimating the prevalence in the entire population.

Collection of rodent droppings

A total of 115 rodent droppings were collected from nine (9) different parts of the Federal University Teaching Hospital (FUTH), Owerri, namely; Main store (25), Male ward 6 surgical (8), Open Registry (12), Heart to Heart area (5), Head Record Office (10), Super Store (15), Kitchen Area (20), Male ward 10 Medical (10), and Female ward 7 Surgical (10). Of these, 48 (41.7%) yielded bacterial growth. The necessary precautions were taken by using the appropriate protective gear, such as gloves made of latex, protective googles, and a half mask air-purifying respirator. Other materials used to enhance the collection of samples were: a sterile forceps, a sterile specimen bottle with a cap, a bottle sterilised by 70 per cent ethanol, a pack of cotton wool for making swabs and Ziploc bags. The droppings were collected using sterile forceps and transferred/stored in a sterile specimen container with a cap. Prior to this, the specimen bottles and forceps were disinfected by swabbing with cotton-wool soaked with 70% ethanol. With the help of the forceps, the faecal pellets were picked and deposited into the sterile specimen bottles and the cap was covered. The specimen bottles were transported in a Ziploc bag to the Infectious Disease and Molecular Epidemiology laboratory of the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research (NIMR), Yaba, Lagos State.

Identification of the rodent droppings

The rodent droppings varied in colour. The rats were brown and black in colour, which was identified based on images of Guiry et al. [18] on the dropping of UK invasive rodents. The brown rat dropping was 13–19 mm long, 3–4 mm thick and had rounded ends. The black rat dropping was 7–14 mm long, 3–4 mm thick and had tapered ends.

Laboratory analysis of rodent dropping

Culture of rodent dropping samples on media

The rodent dropping pellets were homogenised thoroughly by vortexing for 30 s at the maximum speed. Then 900 μl of 1 × PBS was added into four 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes, and a 1:10 serial dilution from the faecal samples was performed using the wide-bore tips. Using the micropipette, 100 μl of each dilution of samples was aseptically inoculated into Selenite F broth and Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth. The inoculated broths were incubated for 16 to 24 h to allow for selective enrichment of target organisms, before subculturing in different culture media: Salmonella-Shigella (SS) Agar (Himedia Laboratories), Nutrient agar (Himedia Laboratories), Mannitol Salt Agar (Himedia Laboratories), Blood agar (Himedia Laboratories) and Eosin-Methylene Blue (EMB) Agar (Oxoid). The plates were incubated at 37 °C in an air incubator and incubated up to 48 h prior to being discarded if no growth [37]. In the negative control, the media plates in each batch were incubated uninoculated to ensure sterility (no growth). The positive controls were reference organisms obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA, whose growth characteristics were known to confirm performance: Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (MacConkey agar with cefotaxime), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 (mannitol salt agar and blood agar), Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 (cetrimide agar), and Salmonella enterica serovar typhimurium ATCC 14028 and Shigella flexneri ATCC 12022 (Salmonella–Shigella agar). All Isolates recovered were stored at − 80 °C in TSB with glycerol.

Identification of bacterial isolates

The pure cultures were characterized and identified based on their motility, microscopic and colonial morphologies, gram staining reaction, biochemical tests including catalase test, methyl red test voges proskauer (MR-VP) test, nitrate reduction test, starch hydrolysis, gelatin liquefaction test, indole test, oxidase test, urease test, triple sugar iron agar (TSI) and sugar fermentation tests as described in medical laboratory manual for tropical countries with reference to the Bergey's manual of systemic bacteriology [11]. Pure isolates were stored in nutrient broth with 20% glycerol at –80 °C for further analysis [16]. The bacteria isolated were subjected to genomic DNA extraction, and purification was done using ZR DNA MiniPrep™ kit (50 Preps, Model D6005) from Zymo Research, California, USA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA sequencing was performed by Sanger (dideoxy) Sequencing Technique to determine the nucleotide sequence of the specific bacteria isolated using automated PCR cycle-Sanger Sequencer™ 3730/3730XL DNA Analyzers from Applied Biosystems [24, 33]. The amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was performed using universal bacterial primers: 27 F (Forward Primer): 5′ AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG–3′ and 1492R (Reverse Primer): 5′ TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT–3′.

Susceptibility testing for isolated bacteria

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of bacterial isolates were determined using the broth microdilution method in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [12].

Serial two-fold dilutions of each antibiotic (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, gentamicin, erythromycin, cefotaxime, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, tetracycline, amikacin, ampicillin, and ampicillin–sulbactam) were prepared in Mueller–Hinton broth over defined concentration ranges (µg/mL).

Bacterial suspensions were standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity (1 × 108 CFU/mL) and further diluted to achieve a final inoculum of 5 × 105 CFU/mL per well [42]. Microtiter plates were incubated at 35–370C for 18–24 h under aerobic conditions.

The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of antibiotic that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. Interpretation of susceptibility was performed using CLSI-established breakpoints (Tables 1, 2 and 3), applied according to the Gram classification and organism group (Gram-positive vs Gram-negative) to account for differences in intrinsic resistance profiles. All tests were performed in duplicate.

Extraction of genomic DNA

The DNA of the resistant bacteria from the antibiotic sensitivity testing were extracted using the ZR DNA MiniPrep™ kit (Model D6005, Zymo Research, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, bacterial cells were lysed using the provided lysis buffer and incubated at 56 °C for 10 min to ensure efficient cell disruption. DNA was purified using silica-based spin column technology, involving sequential washing steps to remove contaminants and inhibitors.

Purified DNA was eluted in 50 μL of nuclease-free water and stored at -20 °C until further analysis. The concentration and purity of extracted DNA were assessed spectrophotometrically (A260/A280 ratio), and DNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis [15]. DNA samples were considered acceptable for downstream PCR analysis when A260/A280 ratios ranged between 1.8–2.0, indicating minimal protein contamination. Negative extraction controls (no template) were included to monitor contamination during the extraction process. All procedures were carried out under aseptic conditions. The DNA was then stored at −200C.

Detection of antibiotic resistance genes by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Genomic DNA extracted from bacterial isolates was used as a template for the detection of antibiotic resistance genes by conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [21]. Specific primers targeting known resistance genes (Table 4) used for the amplification during the current study are available in the Science Data Bank repository link of https://www.scidb.cn/en/anonymous/akVqYTYz.

Primers for detection of antibiotic resistance genes

The primer stocks were diluted according to the manufacturer's instructions to yield working solutions for the PCR [23]. The PCR reaction mixture (10 µL) consisted of 5 µL of Master Mix (Promega, USA), 1 µL nuclease-free water, 3 µL DNA template and 0.5 µL of each primer mix. The negative control was also set up containing all the above, but without the DNA template.

The PCR amplification protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, 36 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s; annealing at 56.0, 56.5, 64.5, 60.4, 55.0, 55.0, 55.0 and 55.0 °C for ermF, ermX, ereA, MsrA, VEB, CTX-M, SHV and blaTEM, respectively for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. This was followed by the final extension at 72 °C for 5 minutes and a hold temperature at 4°C. The PCR reactions were performed as described [2]. Three (3) µl of the PCR product was run on a 2% agarose gel (Bioline) with 5 µl of 10 mg/ml ethidium bromide at 100 V for 45 minutes. A 1 kb plus DNA marker (Invitrogen) was used as a size marker. The DNA amplicons were viewed under an ultraviolet (UV) transilluminator and the results were recorded [4, 35].

Method of data analysis

All the data obtained were coded and entered into the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 21) for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means) were used to summarise bacterial isolates, sampling locations, and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values. Antibiotic susceptibility was interpreted using established resistance breakpoints, and multidrug resistance was defined as resistance to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes.

An exploratory multivariate logistic regression was performed at the isolate level to examine potential predictors of antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) presence.

Results

A total of 115 rodent droppings were cultured, 48 (41.7%) yielded bacterial growth. From these, 24 morphologically distinct isolates were selected for further analysis, representing unique colony morphology discovered on the culture of the 48 positive samples of rodent dropping. The characteristics of size, shape, colour, margin, elevation, texture and haemolytic appearance on culture media were compared in the colonies. If more than one isolate with the same morphology was obtained at the same sample location, only one was analysed, as this would mean analysing the same type of bacteria again. These representative isolates from each sample location with different colony morphology were kept for further molecular characterization, after excluding duplicates from the same sample location (Table 5).

The result revealed that most isolates demonstrated susceptibility to gentamicin and tetracycline, as indicated by the predominance of green color coding. The bacterial isolates (except Raoultella ornithinolytica) demonstrated intermediate susceptibility to ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and amikacin, with consistently low MIC values using the organism-appropriate CLSI breakpoints in Table 2. Likewise, the MIC values for cefotaxime (CTX) and erythromycin (ERY) were low, but with some isolates showing high values, suggesting resistance.

On the other hand, ampicillin (AMP) yielded high MIC values above the CLSI resistance breakpoint for all isolates, which showed high resistance. This was also the case for ampicillin-sulbactam (SAM), where a number of strains, including Staphylococcus sciuri subsp. lentus and Staphylococcus gallinarum, were resistant. Resistance to the combination of sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SXT) was also observed, with some isolates above the resistance cut-off.

For Leuconostoc lactis, MIC values are presented descriptively, as standardised CLSI interpretive criteria are limited for this genus, and therefore, categorical susceptibility assignments were tentative MIC breakpoint values (Table 3) commonly adapted for Leuconostoc and other lactic acid bacteria derived from a combination of CLSI M45 guidance, Nøhr-Meldgaard et al. [27], and Stefańska et al. [36].

The multidrug resistance (MDR) pattern of the bacterial isolates is shown in Table 7. Twenty-four isolates from nine locations were tested. Three (3) isolates (12.5%) were defined as MDR, with resistance to three (3) antimicrobial classes, while the remaining isolates were resistant to one or two classes, and were thus defined as non-MDR.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) classification of bacterial isolates

MDR was detected only in Raoultella ornithinolytica isolates from MW10, which were resistant to the following classes: fluoroquinolones, aminoglycoside and β-lactams. Isolates from FW7, MW6, HH, KA, SS, OR, HR, and MS were resistant particularly to β-lactams, as well as tetracyclines or macrolides, but this was not sufficient for MDR classification.

Resistance to β-lactams was the most common among all locations, affecting all isolates. It is important to note that the MDR classification was based on the profiles of isolates grouped by location. The classification for Leuconostoc lactis was treated with caution because of the tentative (Table 3) MIC breakpoints used for interpretation were adapted from published literature and related susceptibility studies of lactic acid bacteria, which might influence the comparability and clinical interpretation of the antimicrobial susceptibility data.

The prevalence of antibiotic resistance gene markers showed that TEM was the most frequently detected single gene (62.5%), followed by SHV (31.3%), while MsrA was detected at a low frequency (6.3%). No single-gene detection was observed for ermF, ermX, ereA, VEB, or CTX-M. No instances of three-gene co-detection were identified (Table 9).

Discussion

The detection of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes in the rodent droppings within the Federal University Teaching Hospital (FUTH), Owerri was studied. The identified microbial resistance patterns may indicate a dynamic interface of environmental, clinical, and zoonotic routes of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). This adds to mounting evidence that hospital-related environments, such as non-clinical spaces, may be important reservoirs of resistant organisms and genes.

The Staphylococcus sciuri subsp. lentus was detected in several locations within the hospital environment. The bacteria have even been viewed as commensals, but are increasingly being suspected as reservoirs of transferable resistance genes, especially in environmental niches [7, 29, 40]. In this study, the high sequence similarity of the isolates validates the accuracy of the identification, while the moderate relatedness of some of the isolates may indicate the presence of intraspecies genetic diversity, which may increase adaptability and ability to transfer genes horizontally. This variability may be attributed to the development of resistance characteristics and survival in non-homogeneous conditions [4, 5].

Of particular importance is the finding of Gram-negative organisms whose relatedness values are very high, like Raoultella ornithinolytica and Burkholderia cepacia complex. These organisms are characterised by inherent resistance mechanisms and the capability to survive in the selective pressure environment at hospitals [41]. Their high correlation with the presence of ARG in the regression model also suggests a possible association with ARG presence; however, this should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size, as previous studies of Zhuang et al. [43] have shown that Gram-negative bacteria are the main contributors to resistance dissemination to the environment.

The isolates demonstrated intermediate susceptibility to fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), aminoglycosides (amikacin), whereas they are resistant to β-lactam antibiotics (ampicillin and ampicillin-sulbactam). This may reflect patterns consistent with previously reported β-lactam resistance studies that indicate widespread β-lactam resistance in both environmental and clinical isolates [10, 29]. The identified resistance is also closely associated with the presence of TEM and SHV genes that encode 2-lactamases that may hydrolyse penicillin and cephalosporins [42]. The high percentage of TEM genes (62.5) in this study is consistent with the presence of β-lactamase-associated resistance genes.

It is noteworthy that there were both single genes (MsrA, SHV, TEM) and double resistance genes co-detected (SHV and TEM; MsrA and TEM). Although this may be an indication of the development of resistance in the environment under study, it does not reduce the risk to the population's health. The horizontal gene transfer may reflect intraspecies diversity [20, 38], however, its role in gene transfer was not assessed in this study. The lack of erm and CTX-M genes may reflect the absence of these genes in the sampled isolates.

The patterns of ARGs at hospital locations provide preliminary insights into the distribution of ARGs across locations. The increased presence in high-contact zones and clinically adjacent zones like the Heart to Heart Area, wards, and kitchen units may be associated with environmental and operational factors, although these were not directly assessed. The study of Sukhum et al. [37] reports that the environments constructed by hospitals quickly become congested with resistant organisms and human-related genes. Conversely, the lack of ARGs in the Super Store suggests that the distribution of resistance is not homogenous, may be associated with environmental and operational factors, although these were not directly assessed

Mechanistically, the identified organisms are known to possess resistance mechanisms such that may be attributed to resistance. Gram-negative bacteria have three mechanisms of resistance: the production of β-lactamase (SHV, TEM), efflux pump, and lower permeability of their membranes [8, 19, 31]. The target site modification (e.g., MsrA-mediated macrolide resistance) and biofilm formation are examples of resistance mechanisms that improve the survival of Gram-positive organisms in the presence of antimicrobial pressure [25]. The fact that these mechanisms are consistent with previously described resistance in environmental isolates, but do not establish the presence or activity of specific mechanisms [39].

The results of this study suggest that rodent droppings may contain antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes in hospital environments. The report of Basiouni et al. [6] noted the spread of resistant bacteria through rodents as mobile carriers between the hospital units. This suggests the importance of combined infection control measures, which concern not only areas of patient care but also the environment and zoonotic sources of contamination. However, given the purposive sampling design and limited number of isolates, findings should be interpreted as exploratory and not indicative of transmission dynamics or causal relationships.

Limitations

There were a number of limitations that need to be taken into account when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the sample size of only 115 rodent droppings collected and detailed analysis of only 24 bacterial isolates may make the results difficult to generalize. Secondly, the samples were collected by purposive sampling and were only from one hospital, which means the results might not be applicable to other hospitals or other geographical areas. Third, species identification of rodents was not done, which prevented identification of whether any species-specific differences in bacterial carriage existed. Furthermore, isolates were selected according to their ability to grow in the form of colonies, which may have led to a selection bias because morphologically similar colonies could have been different strains of bacteria, and less dominant organisms may have been missed. Lastly, in case of lactic acid bacteria (e.g. Leuconostoc spp.), the standardized clinical MIC breakpoints are not yet available for some of the antibiotics tested. Therefore, tentative MIC breakpoints used for interpretation were adapted from published literature and related susceptibility studies of lactic acid bacteria, which might influence the comparability and clinical interpretation of the antimicrobial susceptibility data.

Conclusions

The detection of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes in the rodent droppings within the Federal University Teaching Hospital (FUTH), Owerri, suggests that these samples may contribute to the local microbial and resistance landscape. The identification of organisms, such as Staphylococcus sciuri subsp. lentus, Raoultella ornithinolytica, and Burkholderia cepacia complex, may indicate the possibility of interaction between environmental and hospital-associated microorganisms.

The occurrence of β-lactam resistance, based on the presence of SHV and TEM genes indicate the presence of resistance determinants within the sampled environment, but their epidemiological significance remains uncertain. While the observed intermediate susceptibility to fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides suggests the potential continued effectiveness of these agents. The uneven distribution of resistance genes across locations within the hospital indicates possible localised environmental influences, although this should be interpreted cautiously given the study design. Overall, these findings underscore preliminary, context-specific evidence and highlight the need for further, larger-scale studies to better understand environmental contributions to antimicrobial resistance.
