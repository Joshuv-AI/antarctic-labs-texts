Restoring self-esteem and quality of life through multidisciplinary care: a cross-sectional evaluation of the Panzi One-Stop Centre model among rape survivors in Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Samuel Kasereka Musisiva, Géant Chuma Basimine, Juvenal Bazilashe Mukungu Balegamire, Ida Andersen, Adelaide Blavier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexual violence in conflict settings is a major public health and social justice issue with long-lasting psychological consequences. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the Panzi One-Stop Centre model provides integrated medical, psychosocial, legal, and socio-economic support to rape survivors. However, evidence on how different combinations of services are associated with psychosocial well-being and equity in health outcomes remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in July 2025 among 314 rape survivors who had received care between 2022 and 2024 in Eastern DRC. Self-esteem and life satisfaction were assessed using validated scales. Sociodemographic data and care pathways were collected through structured interviews. Standardised scores were analyzed using correlation and regression analyses, while analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare mean standardised scores between pillars. General linear models (GLMs) were used to examine associations between sociodemographic factors, care configuration, self-esteem, and life satisfaction. Standardized regression coefficients (β) are reported throughout.ResultsAverage life satisfaction was low, whereas self-esteem was moderately low, with substantial variability across participants. Survivors who were classified in care configurations including a combination of three care pillars (medical, psychosocial and socio-economic support), trend to show the slightly higher observed psychosocial well-being scores, although confidence intervals (CI) overlapped across several subgroups. Medical-only care was associated with comparatively lower observed scores. Employment was positively associated with self-esteem, whereas perceived social support appeared to be more consistently associated with life satisfaction than with self-esteem. A positive association was observed between the number of care pillars utilised and both self-esteem and life satisfaction. Care configurations including the socio-economic pillar tended to display a more pronounced positive relationship between self-esteem and life satisfaction. However, subgroup comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because several groups had small sample sizes and overlapping confidence intervals.ConclusionsIntegrated multidisciplinary care was positively associated with psychosocial well-being among survivors. Variations observed across different combinations of care pillars suggest that equitable access to holistic services may be related to differences in recovery indicators. These findings highlight the potential relevance of ensuring the continuity and availability of multidisciplinary services for survivors of sexual violence in conflict-affected settings. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than evidence of causal effects and warrant confirmation in longitudinal studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02936-6.

Introduction

Sexual violence in conflict situations is one of the most severe forms of trauma and appears to be related high rates of post-traumatic stress disorder and significant psychosocial consequences [22]. According to the World Health Organisation ‘WHO’, sexual violence is alarmingly widespread: at least one in three women worldwide has experienced this phenomenon [42]. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), UN Women [36] states that more than one million women have been raped. These figures vary greatly from one source to another and estimates likely represent only a fraction of the actual number of survivors.

The Panzi General Referral Hospital, located in the Ibanda health zone in Bukavu, South Kivu, in eastern DRC reports that it has treated more than 83,000 survivors of sexual violence since its establishment in 1999 [28]. This figure does not reflect the full extent of the phenomenon, as it accounts for only one organisation in a single location in the eastern part of the country. Sexual violence has multifaceted consequences affecting physical health [7], as well as mental and social health [19].

In response to this phenomenon, the World Health Organisation [41] recommends a holistic approach to care. This approach was adopted in 2006 at the regional level by the International Conference on the Great Lakes Region (ICGLR), particularly in the context of responding to conflict-related sexual violence [1]. From this perspective, the “One Stop Centre” model developed by Panzi illustrates an integrated care approach combining medical care, psychosocial support, legal assistance and socio-economic support [25]. This model is based on a comprehensive care pathway, ranging from the identification and referral of survivors from the community to Panzi Hospital, to post-hospital follow up. As part of the expansion of its healthcare model, Panzi Hospital has established ‘One Stop Centre’ (or ‘guichet unique’) facilities, including the Bulenga Hospital Centre, located in northern South Kivu, in the east of the DRC. Survivors of sexual violence are referred through a network of local associations and health facilities and are then admitted for medical and psychosocial assessment [23]. Upon admission, the medical team conducts a systematic screening to identify any comorbidity and provide emergency care. Psychological support provides psychoeducation and appropriate therapies for mental health conditions. Psychosocial activities include occupational therapy, music therapy, recreational excursions, family mediation, and follow-up services to promote recovery. Socio-economic reintegration is supported through vocational training and literacy programmes. Legal support is also provided to assist with legal juridical proceedings. This survivor-centred model emphasises patient autonomy and the availability of services [25]. Survivors can choose to use all four pillars of care (pending availability) or only some of them. Access to the care model can occur through any of the pillars, although the medical pillar is the main entry point. However, the legal pillar remains the least utilised, due to perceptions of inaccessibility and inefficiency of the judicial system in the DRC, which is often associated with feelings of resignation and discouragement among survivors [17]. In contrast, in other contexts, particularly in the Central African Republic, the NENGO project highlights that a significant proportion of beneficiaries access the model through the legal pillar [12].

The unavailability of certain services can limit access to comprehensive care, resulting in partial reliance on specific pillars. While medical and psychosocial services are generally accessible due to their integration into Panzi Hospital’s primary healthcare system, access to legal and socioeconomic assistance remains dependent on external funding, associated with intermittent availability and selective eligibility. This dependence on funding creates inequalities in access to care, raising questions about the sustainability of the holistic, equitable, and rights-based approach advocated by the WHO and the ICGLR. This variability explains the diversity of care combinations reported by survivors, the most common include: (1) medical care only; (2) medical and psychosocial care; (3) medical, psychosocial, and legal care; (4) medical, psychosocial, legal and socio-economic care; as well as combinations involving only the (5) legal, (6) psychosocial or socio-economic pillars.

Although there is substantial literature on the clinical and logistical aspects of the model [25], there is limited information on beneficiaries’ satisfaction with the services received [23], as well as on comprehensive statistics regarding survivors identified and treated by the hospital [28]. There is also limited research on the long-term psychosocial effects of the different combinations of care received by survivors. In particular, overall life satisfaction—a subjective dimension of quality of life—remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, no study has examined the associations between combinations of care pillars utilised during the care pathway. Life satisfaction, understood as an individual’s overall evaluation of their life in relation to their aspirations [37], is a bidirectional indicator of resilience and overall health [31]. Beyond biological functioning or morbidity alone, life satisfaction is an observable measure of long-term resilience [30]. When life is perceived as satisfactory, it enhances self-esteem and the ability to cope with future challenges. From this perspective, it is important to examine the extent to which the Panzi model contributes, not only to medical recovery, but also to improving survivors’ life satisfaction, in line with the WHO’s definition of health as a state of complete physical, psychological and social well-being (World Health Organization, [39]).

The objective of this study is therefore to analyse the association between care pillars and rape survivors’ self-esteem and life satisfaction following treatment at Panzi Hospital.

More specifically, it seeks to:

By examining these influences on post-traumatic subjective well-being, this study contributes to a better understanding of the processes of identity and emotional reconstruction among survivors of sexual violence. It is grounded in the theoretical framework of positive psychology [13] and an integrative perspective on health aligned with the WHO definition (World Health Organization, [39]). From a practical perspective, identifying the pillars or combinations of pillars that have the most significant impact on self-esteem and life satisfaction will help redefine intervention priorities and strengthen support mechanisms for survivors.

Methods

Sample

A sample of 314 survivors of conflict-related rape was included in this study. Participants were recruited in collaboration with local associations partnering with the Panzi Foundation and community representatives, taking into account criteria such as geographical accessibility, safety, and availability in the target areas. The sample consisted primarily of adult women who had benefited from the “One Stop Centre” care model in Bulenga or at Panzi Hospital, either as inpatients or outpatients.

Study design an setting

A quantitative study based on cross-sectional data was conducted in the territories of Kalehe (60%), Uvira (23%) and Kabare (17%) in South-Kivu, DRC, a region affected by armed conflict, population displacement, and socio-economic insecurity. The study sites were selected due to the presence of one of Panzi’s One Stop Centres and the high number of survivors of sexual violence treated between 2022 and 2024. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, this project was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Psychology, Speech Therapy and Educational Sciences of the University of Liège in Belgium, under reference number 2223-024.

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

The majority of participants, 59% were engaged in agriculture, 12% in small businesses, and 23% were unemployed. 30% had separated from their partners following the rape, 30% were still living with their partners (often without having disclosed the assault), and 23% were single. Half of the participants had never attended school, 34% had dropped out of primary school, and 15% had dropped out of secondary school. These profiles reflect a significant socioeconomic and educational vulnerability, exacerbating precarious living conditions and the psychological burden, which may negatively influence survivors’ self-esteem and life satisfaction.

Data collection

Local partner organisations of Panzi and health facilities served as access points to survivors, particularly those involved in community solidarity groups. These community-based organisations are responsible for raising awareness about sexual violence, as well as for the identification and referral of survivors to local health facilities. These facilities provide initial emergency care and refer survivors to Panzi Hospital or the Bulenga One Stop Centre for specialised treatment. After receiving holistic care, survivors are reintegrated into their communities, where partner organisations ensure the continuity of social support. Due to the unstable security context and population movements, only survivors available at the time of data collection were included. After providing general information about the study and guarantees regarding participation (confidentiality, security, safety, and the freedom to participate or withdraw without prejudice), secure spaces were set up for two weeks within healthcare facilities to receive volunteer participants at four listening points, depending on their availability in each area.

Participants who provided informed consent then individually completed two psychometric instruments: the Self-Esteem Scale (ESTIM) and the Life Satisfaction Scale (ESV). The decision to use these instruments was based on recent studies that have confirmed their reliability and validity in African contexts, particularly in South Africa [29]. Furthermore, large-scale cross-cultural research confirms their robustness across diverse populations [35].

The Social Self-Esteem Inventory [5] (consists of 30 descriptive items, half of which are positively worded and half negatively worded. Participants respond using a six-point Likert scale ranging from completely unlike me (1) to exactly like me (6). The normative is 132, with a standard deviation of 21. The Life Satisfaction Scale (ESV) is designed to assess an individual’s overall satisfaction with life. It consists of five statements to which respondents indicate their degree of agreement on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 7 (“Strongly agree”). The total score ranges from 5 to 35, obtained by summing the responses to the five items. High scores indicate greater life satisfaction. A score of 20 represents the neutral point (neither satisfied nor dissatisfied), while scores between 5 and 9 indicate extreme dissatisfaction, and scores above 30 indicate very high life satisfaction. Data collection was carried out by four locally recruited interviewers in each territory, who were trained and supervised by the principal investigator. Given the generally low level of education among participants, the questionnaires were administered as interviews: questions were read aloud and explained in the local language before allowing participants time to respond. Each assessment session lasted approximately 20 min, and each participant took part only once. Data were anonymised using identification codes, to ensure confidentiality.

The formula made it possible to standardise the units of measurement and performed normalisation to ensure comparability and interpretability based on a common basis. This transformation rescales values to a range between 0 and 1, with a theoretical mean of 0.5 (50%), while preserving the internal structure of the data. In this context, a score greater than or equal to 0.5 reflects average to high levels of life satisfaction or self-esteem. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio and R 4.2.2. To analyse the relationships among variables, correlation analyses, regressions, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted, and a correlation matrix was constructed. To visualise all the relations between variables integrated in the correlation matrix, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used solely as an exploratory visualisation tool. The first component accounted for 13.09% of variance and the second for 10.98%. Given the relatively modest proportion of explained variance, these components should be interpreted as distinct latent dimensions but rather as a descriptive representation of relationships among variables. The suitability of the data for PCA was assessed and confirmed using Bartlett’s test of sphericity and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) (0.70) and Bartlett’s sphericity test (χ² = 297.14; df = 10; p < 0.001) measure of sampling adequacy. To assess differences in self-esteem and life satisfaction across care models, a one-way ANOVA within the General Linear Model (GLM) framework was conducted, followed by Games–Howell post hoc tests. The type of care received depended both on participant’s choice and on the availability of services across the different pillars. Six care models were defined based on reported care pathway: medical care only (M), medical and psychosocial care (MP), medical, psychosocial and legal care (MPL), medical, psychosocial, legal and socio-economic care (MPLS); medical, psychosocial and socio-economic care (MPS), and psychosocial care only (P). A multivariate General Linear Model (GLM) was used to examine the effects of the number of care pillars, types of care received, and sociodemographic variables (marital status, social networks, occupation, education level, and social support) on the outcome variable. All regression coefficients reported in this study are standardised beta coefficient (β). Predictor variables included mariatal status, occupation, education level, perceived social support, and number of care pillars utilised, and care configurations. Results are presented with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals and p-values. The packages ggplot2, ggpubr, and dplyr were used for analysis and visualisation.

Participants receiving medical care alone showed a mean standardized self-esteem score of 0.43. Although the distribution was moderately dispersed, this configuration remained positively associated with self-esteem within the study sample. In contrast, psychosocial support alone tended to show slightly higher observed scores; however, the very small subgroup size (n = 6) means that this observation should be considered as exploratory. The combination of two care pillars, specifically medical and psychosocial (0.44), shows the widest distribution and high variability. Although this combination is the most frequent (n = 172), it was not consistently associated with the highest observed self-esteem scores.

Configurations including three pillars specifically MPL (0.468) or MPS (0.439), or on average (0.45), tended to display slightly higher observed scores; however, these descriptive differences should be interpreted cautiously because of the small subgroup sizes, particularly for MPL. On the other hand, the most comprehensive combination, medical, psychosocial, legal and socio-economic (0.41), does not seem to be associated with the best results.

The standardized β coefficients showed a similar descriptive pattern. Indeed, a progressive positive trend is observed between the use of several pillars and self-esteem scores, indicating a gradual increase in standardized coefficients across care configurations. Participants receiving a single care pillar showed β estimates close to zero (β ≈ 0.01); two-pillars configurations were associated with moderately higher standardized coefficients (β ≈ 0.06). The highest standardised coefficients were observed among participants receiving three pillars of care; however, overlapping confidence intervals suggest that these differences should be interpreted cautiously. In addition to the pillars of care, social support showed a weak and statistically unstable association with self-esteem (β = 0.6, and occupation r = 0.165**).

The standardized life satisfaction scores (ESV Z-score) showed modest variations across the different care configurations. Slightly lower mean scores were observed for the four-pillar configuration (medical, psychosocial, legal and socio-economic support; 0.28) and for the medical–psychosocial combination (0.28). The moderate variation observed across care configurations suggests that life satisfaction may also be associated with additional individual and social characteristics beyond the observed combinations of care. In particular, perceived social support was positively associated with life satisfaction (β = 0.178*), indicating that participants reporting higher levels of perceived social support also tended to report higher life satisfaction within this study population.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest a positive association between the number of care pillars utilised and psychosocial well-being indicators, which is consistent with the rationale underlying the One-Stop Centre model designed to provide comprehensive care for victims of sexual violence in the DRC [25].

However, this situation continues to be shaped by significant socio-economic vulnerability, a lack of education and the burden of stigma, all of which limit the overall well-being of survivors [10, 16, 24, 33, 38]. Despite that, the high variability in life satisfaction suggests that outcomes differ among survivors, with higher levels observed among those who accessed at least three pillars of care, which may be related to differences in care pathways and contextual support. This observed association, already documented in other contexts, generally psychotherapeutic [15, 32], is consistent with previous studies reporting positive associations between integrated multidimensional care and resilience indicators, with relatively higher observed scores among participants receiving three pillars of care. However, the observed mean scores did not continue to increase beyond three pillars, indicating a non-linear relationship between care and outcomes [34]. In this case, Bunger, [6] suggests the need for coordination and quality personal relationships between providers, while Andersen and Buttigieg [2] emphasise that the continuity and consistency of interventions are more important than their absolute number.

Unlike the work of Andersen and Buttigieg [2], our results show that survivors who only received medical care, although this was the most frequently sought form of support (74%), display comparatively lower observed life satisfaction scores, suggesting that purely medical interventions, which are essential for treating physical injuries and preventing sexually transmitted infections, appear to be associated with comparatively lower life satisfaction scores than multi-pillar configurations. The psychosocial pillar showed comparatively stronger associations with life satisfaction, despite the small sample size making this observation exploratory. One survivor who received only psychosocial support reported a high life satisfaction score, consistent with the hypothesis that a robust psychosocial pillar may explain self-esteem more than medical care alone [3, 27]. These findings are consistent with WHO guidelines, which recommend holistic, personalised care to restore survivors’ quality of life [40]. Recent systematic reviews have reported beneficial effects of psychosocial interventions in reducing PTSD symptoms and improving well-being [27]. These data reinforce the need to systematically integrate these approaches into medical care, while highlighting the potential relevance of including socio-economic support. Indeed, our results show that combinations of care including socio-economic support show the strongest associations, suggesting that socio-economic may be associated with conditions facilitating that allow restored self-esteem to translate into life satisfaction. This additional integrated pillar may be related to the translation of medical and psychological progress into tangible well-being, thus highlighting the importance of a comprehensive and integrated approach [4, 26], rather than a fragmented one, for a truly harmonious reconstruction.

Furthermore, although legal assistance has been documented as a potentially promising intervention for the restoration of dignity through recognition and reparations [21], the finding that higher quality-of-life outcomes were observed among participants who did not engage with this pillar raises questions regarding the accessibility and perceived quality of judicial support provided in this context. Several studies conducted in the same context as this research report persistent dissatisfaction among survivors with regard to legal proceedings, mainly due to the inaccessibility of the alleged perpetrators, who are often members of armed groups active in the region [18]. This perception of inaccessible and ineffective justice fosters feelings of resignation and discouragement, especially since the majority of rape complaints do not result in convictions and reparations are rarely enforced [17].

Despite that, our results show that these different combinations of pillars are associated with variations in self-esteem and life satisfaction and with the observed relationship between these two dimensions, suggesting that these findings are consistent with frameworks emphasising that takes trauma into account [20] requires not only improving each dimension separately, but above all creating the conditions for their synchronised evolution and mutual reinforcement. These approaches are commonly associated with resilience processes by restoring social ties and the ability to envisage a future [9]. Nevertheless, the inaccessibility of the legal and socio-economic pillars often linked to funding, contrasts with their importance in the integrated model. This discontinuity in the availability of services when survivors need them could contribute to the persistence of unmet needs and the maintenance of socio-economic vulnerability. Although the model recognises the interdependence of survivors’ needs, its dependence on external funding hinders the continuity of comprehensive care and the sustainability of the model [4]. However, the results indicate that higher life satisfaction scores tended to be observed among participants who consulted a greater number of care pillars, highlighting the benefit of interdisciplinary synergy in the recovery process.

Furthermore, apart from the pillars of care, the results identified positive associations with contextual and socio-demographic factors, particularly occupation and social support. This is in line with the work of Fayaz [11] indicating that restoring self-esteem and rebuilding the social fabric are essential elements of post-traumatic well-being. The fact that participants in employment have higher self-esteem suggests that resuming a productive role has been described as being associated with identity reconstruction and autonomy (Frazier, 2003) and that social support acts as a buffer against shame and isolation [8]. However, the analysis reports relatively weak associations, indicating the potential existence of other factors influencing life satisfaction. For example, persistent insecurity contributes to the emergence of systemic poverty in the region and possibly to this low level of life satisfaction. These results corroborate those of Hossain et al. [14], according to which post-rape recovery is a multidimensional process, dependent on individual, social and institutional variables.

Our findings are consistent with the biopsychosocial paradigm by showing a positive association between the number of pillars consulted and well-being scores. They highlight that resilience relies on the consistent synchronisation of the psychological and socio-economic dimensions of recovery, in a context marked by structural vulnerabilities that have a lasting impact on resilience. On a practical level, the study highlights the relevance of interdisciplinary collaboration, where medical care, although essential, must be complemented by psychosocial support, which showed comparatively stronger associations, and socio-economic support, which was associated with comparatively higher psychosocial scores. The latter may be associated with more favourable psychosocial profiles, based on the continuity of interventions and the personalisation of care pathways. At the programmatic level, our results support the potential relevance of institutionalising of the integrated model and the strengthening of underutilised pillars to ensure the effectiveness of the approach. The dependence on external funding highlights the urgent need for greater commitment from public authorities and community partners. This commitment would empower the model not only to treat physical sequelae, but also to restore personal value, dignity and hope, which are the foundations of true healing.

Overall, the association between social support and life satisfaction appears positive and relatively consistent, whereas the association with self-esteem appears weak and statistically unstable. The perceived social support appeared to be more consistently associated with life satisfaction than with self-esteem but statistically less stable across models.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has certain limitations, notably the small sample size and the imbalance in numbers between the subgroups of the different pillars, which reduces its statistical robustness. The cross-sectional nature of the study does not allow conclusions to be drawn regarding causal links between types of care and life satisfaction. The influence of the context on the maintenance of psychosocial balance and on the sampling process meant that the sample was not representative of the population under study, limiting access to certain groups. Furthermore, the use of convenience sampling, based on participant availability, as well as recruitment through partner organisations, may have led to an over-representation of those who remained in contact with support services and the exclusion of those who are more vulnerable or difficult to reach. Finally, the lack of information on the needs expressed by survivors upon admission limits the assessment of whether the services provided met their expectations, thereby reducing the ability to evaluate their overall satisfaction with their lives. These results should therefore be interpreted with caution, whilst acknowledging their contribution to the development of context-appropriate interventions in humanitarian situations. Further research, incorporating longitudinal follow-up and qualitative data into its methodological design, would be necessary to explore in depth the trajectories of psychological recovery and community resilience over time.

Conclusion

This study suggests that psychosocial well-being of rape survivors may be associated not only with access to care, but also with the combination of services received within and integrated care pathway. Configurations combining medical, psychosocial, and socio-economic support tended to show higher observed self-esteem and life satisfaction scores. However, because of the cross-sectional design and the small size of some subgroups, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than casual effects. Future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand how different care configurations related to long-term psychological recovery among survivors of conflict-related sexual violence.
