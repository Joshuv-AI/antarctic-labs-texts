Root development progression involves RALF peptide-mediated ROS signaling and pectin dynamics in rice
Authors: Meiru Jia, Hanlei Yang, Bingyue Li, Zhao Xie, Shaoxue Cao, Leiting Pan, Baocai Zhang, Yihua Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoot development involves the maintenance of longitudinal cell division, cell expansion, and maturation, which is coordinated by signaling networks composed of peptide ligand-receptor complexes and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, and is coupled with dynamic cell-wall remodeling. However, these regulatory modules remain disconnected, and the systematic mechanisms that efficiently orchestrate root development remain elusive. Here, we uncover an intrinsic regulatory hub in rice that coordinates root development by integrating ROS transmembrane signaling with pectin structural organization. THESEUS3, a receptor-like kinase, interacts and phosphorylates RbohB to promote superoxide anion (O2−) generation, negatively regulating pectin demethylesterification in the meristem. Rice RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR4 (OsRALF4), functioning as a ligand, balances O2− homeostasis through inhibiting THESEUS3-RbohB signaling in the meristem and modulates pectin demethylesterification. Demethylesterified pectin subsequently binds with OsRALF4 to mediate pectin bundling into a condensed structure, marking the onset of cell expansion. Our study establishes the integrated mechanistic framework underlying root development.Subject terms: Root apical meristem, Cell wall, Extracellular signalling molecules

Introduction

Root development is a tightly regulated process that is essential for anchoring plants, absorbing water and nutrients, and facilitating interactions with soil environment1.Root growth arises from cell division in the root apical meristem (RAM), known as the meristematic zone (MZ) and stem cell niche2. Most cells begin to expand and differentiate once they leave the MZ, thereby entering the elongation zone (EZ) and mature zone. Root developmental progression is processed along the timeline in longitudinal axis, reflecting a dynamic interplay of genetic pathways, hormonal signals, and environmental responses3,4. Elucidating the mechanism that integrates major regulatory networks during root development will advance our knowledge and ultimately enable the rational design of root systems5.

Among the three developmental zones, MZ size makes the main contribution to root growth, which is highly associated with the maintenance of cell stemness. Root meristem growth factor 1–3 (RGF1-3) are crucial peptide signals controlling the size of MZ6. Five leucine-rich repeat RLKs (LRR-RLKs), including RGF1 INSENSITIVES (RGIs) and RGF1 RECEPTORS (RGFRs), have been found to act as receptors of RGF17–9. The RGF1-RGIs complexes regulate meristem homeostasis through stabilizing the transcription factor PLETHORA 1/2 (PLT1/2) that controls stem cell activity10,11, or activating PLT1/2 via a MAPK cascade12. Another notable signal hub of RGF1 pathway is reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis along the root developmental zones, which is known controlled by a transcription factor RGF1-INDUCIBLE TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 1 (RITF1)11. However, how cell surface ligand-receptor complexes are directly connected to ROS homeostasis in roots is an unsolved issue.

ROS that encompass singlet oxygen (O2), superoxide anion (O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radical (OH•) are ubiquitous second messengers involved in myriad basic processes of plant growth and stress adaptations13. ROS are the intrinsic signal for establishing MZ size11,14,15. O2− primarily accumulates in the MZ to maintain stemness, whereas H2O2 is abundant in the EZ to promote stem cell differentiation, which is coordinated by counteracting the activities of ROS-producing and scavenging enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 1a)16,17. The plasma membrane NADPH oxidases/respiratory burst oxidase homologs (Rbohs) are the well-studied proteins for O2− production18. The transcription factor UPBEAT1 (UPB1) balances the ROS signals in the MZ and EZ through modulating the expression of a set of peroxidase genes. Mutation in UPB1 alters this balance and delays the onset of cell expansion16. Disruption of ROOT MERISTEMLESS1, a gene encoding a glutathione biosynthesis enzyme, causes disability in the formation of root meristem19. Redox thus plays a crucial role in sustaining root meristem homeostasis. How this confined O2− distribution is maintained, as well as the implications, remains elusive.

The transition of meristematic cells from quiescence to expansion is usually accompanied by cell wall dynamics. Pectin, characterized by α-1,4-linked d-galacturonic acid, is the main cell wall component in growing roots. In general, newly synthesized pectins are highly methylesterified, and are demethylated by pectin methylesterases (PMEs) while inserting into cell walls20. Pectin demethylesterification, a key process that modulates the physicochemical properties of pectin, represents a major form of cell-wall remodeling during plant development21–23. Thus, modulating the degree and pattern of methyl esters on pectin can balance cell wall rigidity and elasticity required for plant cell growth24–27. However, whether pectin remodeling is incorporated into ROS-mediated root development, and how these two processes are integrated, are unknown.

RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTORs (RALFs) are important peptide signals. The recognition of a RALF by the cell surface receptor FERONIA, a Catharanthus roseus receptor-like kinase1 like protein (CrRLK1L), triggers the phosphorylation of the plasma membrane H+–adenosine triphosphatase, regulating extracellular alkalinization and cell expansion28. Over the past decade, several pairs of RALFs and CrRLK1L members have been reported, identifying RALFs as crucial signals for plant growth and development29–35. Recently, Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana, At) RALF1 can bind extracellular pectin and serves as the cell surface sensor in response to environmental stressors36. Furthermore, AtRALF4 and AtRALF22 can interact with cell wall–anchored protein LEUCINE-RICH REPEAT EXTENSINs, facilitating wall assembly in pollen tube and root hair37,38. Thus, clarifying biological functions of RALFs is crucial to deciphering the mechanisms of plant growth and development.

Here, we identify a RALF-mediated intrinsic regulatory hub in rice (Oryza Sativa, Os) that maintains meristem homeostasis and drives the transition from division to expansion. THESEUS3 (THE3), a CrRLK1L member homologous to THE1, interacts and phosphorylates RbohB to promote O2− generation in the RAM. OsRALF4 controls O2− homeostasis in root meristem through inhibiting THE3-RbohB signaling. O2− level, in turn, negatively regulates the stability of PMEs, suppressing pectin demethylesterification. With the decrease of O2−, demethylesterified pectin are generated and bound directly to OsRALF4, facilitating pectin condensation and cell elongation. Hence, our study elucidates the systematic mechanism that governs the progression of root development from MZ maintenance to the onset of cell expansion, shedding lights in breeding crops with elite root architecture.

Results

RbohB interacts with THE3 to sustain the profile of O2– in root meristem

Root meristem homeostasis depends on the O2– accumulation in the RAM17. To test this in rice roots, we examined O2– in the root apex of a japonica variety, Nipponbare (NP), using nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), a reagent that specifically stains O2–. Meanwhile, we defined the RAM using 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU), a chemical that indicates de novo DNA synthesis. The signal profile of NBT almost overlapped with that of EdU (Fig. 1a, b), indicating that O2– accumulates in the MZ of rice roots. O2– is usually produced by Rbohs39, and rice has nine Rboh genes (RbohA-I)40. To determine which Rbohs is responsible for O2– production in the MZ, we analyzed the expression level of Rbohs in the NP root tips. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) revealed that RbohB and RbohC are highly expressed among the nine Rbohs (Fig. 1c). We then generated a rbohb and rbohc mutants using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing in a japonica variety Zhonghua11 (ZH11), and examined root growth and O2– level in these mutants (Supplementary Fig. 1b,c). Compared with rbohc, rbohb-2 exhibited more significant decline in the root growth and O2– content (Supplementary Fig. 1d–g), indicating RbohB as a dominant Rboh functioning in rice roots. To confirm this, we obtained the rbohb-1 mutant and RbohB overexpressing (RBox) lines in the NP background18 (Supplementary Fig. 1h). Consistent with their performance in root growth (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j), rbohb-1 also showed a reduction in O2–, whereas RBox lines had increased O2– in the root tips (Fig. 1d, e). Hence, RbohB serves as the major gene driving O2– production in the rice RAM.

To dissect the signaling pathway associated with RbohB, we performed TurboID-mediated proximity labeling coupled with mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis in the rice protoplasts expressing RbohB-TurboID-cMyc fusion protein. Among the identified proteins (Supplementary Dataset 1), CrRLK1L family member THE3 was identified as an interacting protein (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). To verify this, we used THE3 as prey to perform reciprocal TurboID labeling assay. In contrast to the controls, the specific antibody for RhobB probed RbohB in the streptavidin-pulled proteins extracted from the protoplasts expressing THE3-TurboID-cMyc (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2c). RhobB interacts with THE3.

THE3 belongs to CrRLK1L subfamily and encodes an 860-aa protein harboring a malectin-like domain and a protein kinase domain (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Subcellular localization assay showed that THE3 is co-localized with mRFP-tagged HIR1, a plasma membrane marker (Supplementary Fig. 3c). The N terminus of Rbohs, critical for ROS production, often serves as the interaction site with protein kinases41. As revealed by split-luciferase complementary assay in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, we found that the N-terminus of RbohB (RbohBN) mediates the interaction with THE3 (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 3d). This interaction was further confirmed by the pull-down assay and Co-IP analysis in the rice protoplasts (Fig. 1h, i).

To determine whether THE3 affects O2– production, we generated a rice mutant in ZH11 using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. We obtained the the3 mutant, which carries an 1-bp deletion in the first exon, resulting in a frameshift and premature translational termination (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). For genetic validation, we prepared the complementary transgenic plants by introducing 6977 bp genomic fragment of THE3 in the3 (Comthe3) (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). The transformation fully rescued the defects of the3 in root growth and O2– level (Fig. 1j, k and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). We further overexpressed THE3 in ZH11 (THE3ox) (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). The overexpression elevated the root length and O2– level compared to the wild type (Fig. 1j, k and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f), indicating that THE3 regulates root growth. To validate the genetic relationship between THE3 and RbohB, we overexpressed RbohB in the3 mutant (RBoxthe3) (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). The O2– level in the RAM of RBoxthe3 was restored to the wild-type level; the comparable root growth suggests that RbohB functions downstream of THE3 (Fig. 1j, k and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). THE3 is involved in RbohB-mediated O2– production in the RAM.

THE3-RbohB interaction is crucial for meristem homeostasis

The accumulation of O2− in the MZ maintains MZ stemness16. We thus detected the DNA synthesis activity in the RAM of THE3 and RbohB relevant genetic materials using EdU staining. The rbohb and the3 mutants exhibited shorter EdU-staining region than the wild type, whereas the complementary and overexpressing plants displayed slightly extended EdU-staining (Fig. 2a, b), largely consistent with the alterations in MZ size (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4g). The THE3-RbohB module orchestrates root growth through sustaining meristem homeostasis.

To explore the underlying mechanism, we performed in vitro phosphorylation assay using the purified THE3 proteins. The phosphorylation of THE3 and a universal substrate confirmed it as an authentic protein kinase (Supplementary Fig. 5a). By separating the reaction products of THE3 and RbohBN in the phos-tag gel, we detected the phosphorylated RbohBN, and this signal was abolished by the addition of calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP) (Fig. 2d). We further mutated RbohB at Ser140, Ser191 and Thr278 to alanine to mimic the non-phosphorylated forms according to the report41 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In the presence of THE3, in vitro phosphorylation assay confirmed that these variants mediated lower phosphorylation levels than the wild-type RbohB (Supplementary Fig. 5c). To determine whether THE3 can phosphorylate RbohB in vivo, we co-expressed RbohBN-NanoLUC and THE3-GFP in the rice protoplasts. Phosphorylation of RbohB was detected in the protoplasts expressing THE3, while it was eliminated by treating the proteins with CIP (Fig. 2e). Thus, THE3 can phosphorylate RbohB.

As phosphorylation usually affects protein stability, we compared RbohB level in the wild-type, the3, and THE3ox plants. Indeed, RbohB level showed a decrease in the3 mutant and an increase in the THE3ox plants (Fig. 2f). We further performed a cell-free degradation assay by incubating the purified RbohB-His proteins with the total proteins extracted from the wild-type, the3, and THE3ox roots, separately. RbohB-His was degraded more rapidly while adding the the3 extracts than the wild-type and THE3ox extracts (Fig. 2g). The stability of RbohB showed a dependence on the level of THE3 (Fig. 2h). THE3-mediated phosphorylation augments RbohB stability.

We further found that the phosphorylation level of RbohB was reduced in the3 root tips, but was enhanced in THE3ox root tips (Fig. 2i). To explore the effect of RbohB phosphorylation on enzyme activity, we analyzed NADPH oxidase activity using the proteins extracted from the root tips of these plants. By applying equal amount of isolated RbohB, we detected lower oxidase activity in the3 and higher activity in THE3ox (Fig. 2j), consistent with their alterations in O2– level (Fig. 1k). Thus, THE3-mediated phosphorylation enhances the activity of RbohB. To obtain the evidence in O2– generation, we transfected N. benthamiana leaves with THE3, RbohB and the combinations of THE3 with either wild-type RbohB or its variant with a mutation at the phosphorylation site (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). After ensuring that all the transgenes were expressed in N. benthamiana leaf discs (Supplementary Fig. 5d), NBT staining revealed that co-infiltration of THE3 with RbohB variants generated significantly less O2– level than that cotransfecting THE3 with wild-type RbohB (Fig. 2k, l). Despite the comparable protein abundance, the changed RbohB phosphorylation levels led us to conclude that phosphorylation is crucial for driving O2– production (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 5c).

Therefore, THE3 mediated phosphorylation regulates the activity and stability of RbohB, influencing O2– production and meristem homeostasis in the RAM.

Methylesterified pectin is rich in the RAM accumulating O2–

Pectin demethylesterification is a crucial step in cell growth22. To investigate the methylesterification state of pectin in the RAM, we immunolabeled the wild-type root tips using LM20 and 2F4, the antibodies preferentially recognizing methylesterified pectin (mPectin) and demethylesterified pectin (dmPectin), respectively. LM20 generated obvious signal for mPectin in the MZ (Fig. 3a), whereas dmPectin recognized by 2F4, was mainly present in the EZ (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Furthermore, the distribution of the LM20 signal coincides with the MZ in the root tips (Fig. 3b), suggesting that the RAM is rich in mPectin.

As root meristem also accumulates O2– (Fig. 1a), to ascertain the relationship between O2– and pectin demethylesterification, we treated the wild-type roots with Tiron, a scavenger of O2–, and analyzed the pectin in the root tips using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI–TOF MS) (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Tiron treatment significantly reduced the levels of methyl esters on pectin (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 6c), in agreement with the effects of Tiron on root growth, MZ size, and O2– content (Supplementary Fig. 6d–h). O2– accumulation may sustain pectin in a methylesterified state in root tips.

To test this, we examined the methylesterification degree of pectin in the root tips of the3 and rbohb mutants and the relevant transgenic plants. By immunolabeling their RAMs using LM20, the fluorescent signal for mPectin almost disappeared in the rbohb and the3 mutants, but was restored to the wild-type level in the complementary plants (Fig. 3d, e). The RbohB overexpression plants exhibited the extended mPectin region along the longitudinal axis (Fig. 3d, e). We further chemically analyzed the methylesterified degree of pectin in the root tips using MALDI–TOF MS (Supplementary Fig. 7). Consistently, the methyl esters on pectin were dramatically reduced in the rbohb and the3 mutant root tips, whereas those in the complementary and overexpression plants were comparable to the wild-type plants (Fig. 3f). Therefore, O2– level maintained by the THE3-RbohB module locally affects pectin methylesterification degree in the RAM.

The activity of PMEs is suppressed by O2–

To dissect how the accumulated O2– regulates pectin demethylesterification, we extracted total proteins from NP root tips treated with Tiron and analyzed the PME activity. The pectoplate assay revealed that the PME activity of the Tiron-treated root tips was higher than the control (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Thus, O2– accumulation may suppress PME activity. To test this, we incubated a commercial PME with an increasing gradient of O2–, which was released from the xanthine (XON)-xanthine oxidase (XOD) reaction in the pectoplate (Supplementary Fig. 8c). The PME activity decreased with the increase of O2– release (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Consistently, the total PME activity was elevated in rbohb and the3 root-tip extracts compared to the wild-type, whereas it was significantly reduced in the overexpression lines (Fig. 4a). Thus, O2– can block PME activity. Since O2– usually affects protein stability, we measured the protein level of the commercial PME in the presence of O2–. The continuous drop of protein abundance with the increase of O2– indicates that O2– impairs PME stability (Supplementary Fig. 8e).

This finding prompted us to identify the endogenous PMEs in rice RAM. LC-MS analysis revealed that OsPME30 abundance, as indicated by Peptide Spectrum Match (PSM) value, increased by 75-fold after Tiron treatment (Supplementary Fig. 8f and Supplementary Dateset 2), suggesting it as a major PME functioning in rice roots. Cell-free protein degradation assay and in vitro enzyme activity analysis showed that the stability and the activity of the recombinant PME30 proteins were reduced with the release of O2– (Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 8g,h). We further transfected the N. benthamiana leaves with OsPME30-Myc and performed protein blotting. Tiron treatment stabilized OsPME30 (Fig. 4c). All these demonstrate that O2– disables OsPME30 through disrupting the protein stability.

Rice PME30 was grouped into a subclade containing Arabidopsis PME30 (Supplementary Fig. 9). To obtain the genetic support, we isolated two allelic mutants, both containing frameshifts that introduce premature stop codons (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). The proteins extracted from the root tips of the ospme30 mutants displayed significantly lower PME activity than those extracted from the wild type (Fig. 4d). Similarly, immunolabeling using LM20 revealed the extended mPectin area in ospme30 roots (Fig. 4e), consistent with the augmented level of methyl esters on the pectin (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 10c). Notably, the promoted root growth in ospme30 mutants was accompanied by the increased pectin methylesterification, enhanced cell division, and an enlarged MZ (Fig. 4g–j and Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). Hence, OsPME30, as a major PME in rice RAM, is disabled by O2– produced by the THE3-RbohB module.

OsRALF4 is a ligand of THE3, suppressing THE3-RbohB signaling

THE3 homologs often serve as the receptors of RALF peptides28. To determine the ligand of THE3, we analyzed the transcriptomes of rice root developmental zones, and found four RALF peptides highly expressed in the rice RAM (Supplementary Fig. 11a and Table 1). We chemically synthesized these RALF peptides, and measured their binding affinity with the ectodomain of THE3 (THE3ECD) using micro-scale thermophoresis (MST). OsRALF4 specifically bound THE3ECD with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 6.1 µM (Fig. 5a), whereas the other OsRALFs exhibited a higher Kd value or did not bind to THE3ECD (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Split-luciferase complementation assay verified the interaction between OsRALF4 and THE3 (Fig. 5b). OsRALF4, clustered in the subclade with AtRALF4 and AtRALF2237,38 (Supplementary Fig. 11c), is a potential ligand of THE3.

Next, we explored the impact of this binding on the THE3-RbohB signaling pathway. In the presence of OsRALF4, split-luciferase complementation assay detected the obvious attenuation in the interaction between THE3 and RbohBN (Fig. 5c). This finding is strengthened by in vitro phosphorylation analysis, in which the addition of OsRALF4 significantly decreased the level of phosphorylated RbohB (Fig. 5d). To determine its impact on O2– production, we applied different concentrations of OsRALF4 onto N. benthamiana leaves (Supplementary Fig. 12a). NBT staining of the treated N. benthamiana leaf discs revealed that OsRALF4 treatment resulted in a dose-dependent inhibition of O2– levels (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, by transiently transfecting the N. benthamiana leaves with the constructs containing THE3 or/and RbohB, the addition of OsRALF4 weakened the NBT signal enhanced by coexpressing THE3 and RbohB (Fig. 5f, g). OsRALF4 exerts an inhibitory effect on the THE3-RbohB pathway.

Next, we generated OsRALF4 mutants by CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. Two allelic mutants resulted from 1-bp deletion or insertion are loss-of-function mutations and displayed increased root length and MZ size as revealed by EdU labeling (Fig. 5h–k and Supplementary Fig. 12b). OsRALF4 thus restrains root growth. To verify its inhibitory role in the THE3-RbohB signaling pathway, we treated the relevant genetic materials using the synthesized OsRALF4 polypeptides. Consistent with the compromised root growth (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d), OsRALF4 treatment attenuated NBT intensity in the root tips except the3 (Fig. 5l,m); meanwhile, the MZ size of the wild-type and the overexpression plants was shortened, but was almost unchanged in the3 and rbohb mutants (Fig. 5n and Supplementary Fig. 12e). Moreover, the polypeptide treatment restored the promoted root growth of osralf4 to the wild-type level, as indicated by NBT staining and MZ size measurement (Fig. 5l–n and Supplementary Fig. 12c–e). Hence, OsRALF4 attenuates the THE3-RbohB signaling pathway, reducing O2– production.

OsRALF4 binds dmPectin, facilitating pectin condensation and cell expansion

RALF peptides can play structural roles in the expandable cell wall of pollen tubes and root hairs37,38. To determine whether OsRALF4 has structural role in root development, we prepared a fluorescent tag Alexa Fluor 647-labeled OsRALF4 and Flag-tagged OsRALF4. MST assay and physiological treatment demonstrated that the addition of these tags did not affect OsRALF4 function (Supplementary Fig. 13a–e). After incubating the root longitudinal sections with LM20 and 2F4 antibodies, super-resolution 3D-stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy detected that OsRALF4 is colocalized with the 2F4-labeled dmPectin in the walls of the EZ cells (Fig. 6a, b), but failed to overlap with the LM20-targeted mPectin in the MZ cell walls (Supplementary Fig. 13f). Taken together with the cell-wall localization of OsRALF4-GFP in the N. benthamiana cells (Supplementary Fig. 13g), OsRALF4 can integrate into the expanding cell walls. We further prepared mPectin and dmPectin (Supplementary Fig. 13h), and examined their binding capacity to OsRALF4 by MST assay. OsRALF4 bound to dmPectin with a Kd of 6.7 µM and mPectin with a Kd of 201.5 µM (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 13i). Since a high Kd value indicates weak binding, OsRALF4 preferentially binds to dmPectin.

To determine its role in cell wall organization, we analyzed pectin structure in the EZ cell walls of wild-type and osralf4-1 roots using 3D direct stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (3D-dSTORM). By imaging the 2F4-labeled walls of macerated cortex cells at XZ plane, we observed a bimodal distribution of dmPectin (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b and Supplementary Movie 1). In opposite to the condensed dmPectin in the wild-type walls, we observed more fine particles in that of osralf4-1 (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 14c). Notably, dmPectin formed random bundles in wild-type cell walls, whereas in the mutant, it appeared less aggregated (Fig. 6d). OsRALF4 facilitates pectin condensation in the EZ.

Given its dual roles in signaling and structure, a key question arises: how do this functional duality modulates root development? We thus explored the effect of dmPectin on the formation of OsRALF4-THE3 complex. Co-IP assay using the rice protoplasts coexpressing OsRALF4-Flag and THE3ECD showed that applying dmPectin weakened the interaction between OsRALF4 and THE3ECD; however, adding mPectin did not (Fig. 6f). The presence of dmPectin likely disturbs the perception of OsRALF4 by THE3. qPCR analysis further revealed an ascending expression gradient of OsRALF4 along the root developmental axis, spatially opposed to O2– distribution (Figs. 6g and 1b). Notably, immunolabeling of the root tips using LM20 exhibited the extended mPectin pattern in osralf4 mutants (Fig. 6h). OsRALF4 peptide may facilitate pectin demethylesterification. To verify this, we treated the osralf4, the3, rbohb and the overexpression plants with exogenous OsRALF4 polypeptides. The treatment significantly repressed mPectin pattern in the RAM, as probed by LM20 (Fig. 6i), demonstrating that OsRALF4 promotes dmPectin generation, boosting OsRALF4-dmPectin interaction and cell expansion.

Therefore, we propose that the increase of OsRALF4 promotes the production of dmPectin, which anchor free OsRALF4 into the cell wall. Thus, the location of pectin demethylesterification would mark the acquisition of a structural role for OsRALF4, in addition to its signaling function, thereby promoting the onset of cell elongation during root development.

Discussion

The systematic progression of development is crucial for plants to acquire their specific functional traits. Plant root, an important organ essential for robustness, is established through the tight orchestration of multiple regulatory modules. In the past decade, although several cascades regulating root development have been identified based on its trackable cellular status6,7,10,11,16, the molecular signaling pathways, O2− distribution, and cell-wall dynamics are disconnected. Here, we report an intrinsic hub in root development that integrates the OsRALF4-THE3-RbohB pathway with OsRALF4-dmPectin interaction. This hub not only modulates meristem homeostasis by balancing O2− levels in the RAM, but also promotes the onset of cell expansion by facilitating pectin demethylesterification.

ROS dynamics are central to numerous fundamental biological processes19,42. Amongst, O2− accumulation in the RAM is indispensable for MZ maintenance11. However, the mechanistic link between the ligand-receptor complexes and O2− distribution remains unclear. In this study, oxidase RbohB has been identified as a key player of O2– production in the rice RAM. TurboID proximity labeling and a series of experiments verified that THE3 can phosphorylate RbohB, which regulates its activity and stability, thereby facilitating O2− generation. Based on a series of experiments, we propose that RbohB phosphorylation mediated by THE3 may be more crucial for O2− production. This aligns with the studies indicating that phosphorylation serves as the main regulatory mechanism for Rboh activity41,43–46. The discovery of THE3-RbohB module, which contributes to O2− generation in the RAM, reveals a previously unrecognized direct link between a cell-surface receptor and ROS homeostasis that orchestrates root development.

During the transition from cell proliferation to expansion, the volume increase of meristematic cells is accompanied by cell wall remodeling. Pectin, a polysaccharide rich in growing cells, plays a key role in this cell-state transition24,26. However, whether pectin remodeling involves in root development progression is still elusive. In this work, mPectin accumulation in the RAM showed a similar spatial pattern to O2– distribution, suggesting a link between O2– and pectin methylesterification modification. Multiple lines of evidence demonstrate that O2– facilitates PME degradation, thereby restraining PME activity and keeping pectin in methylesterified state in the RAM. Moreover, lesions in OsPME30 enhance MZ stemness and promote root growth, providing genetic proofs for this mechanism. Interestingly, mPectin profile in the root tips was significantly attenuated by Tiron treatment and by mutations in THE3, RbohB or OsPME30, yet the reduction in their MZ size was modest, suggesting a nonlinear correlation between the degree of pectic methylesterification and the MZ size. Considering the significance of MZ homeostasis, it is plausible that additional layers of regulation exist. Herein, this study reveals an unexplored link between ROS signaling and cell wall remodeling, specifically via pectin methylesterification modification, highlighting a pivotal, coordinated mechanism in root development.

RALF peptides not only act as ligands, participating in myriad plant growth processes and stress responses35,36,47, but also exert structural role in cell wall integrity37,38. However, elucidating the dual function of RALFs within one developmental context is very limited. Here, we identify OsRALF4 as a ligand of THE3, which represses the THE3-RbohB signaling and O2– production. The root phenotypes of the mutants in the OsRALF4-THE3-RbohB signaling axis, following the treatment with synthesized OsRALF4, further corroborate the function of this pathway. Given that OsRALF4 functions as an inhibitory signal, we propose that the unique distribution of O2− in the RAM is likely maintained in an equilibrium through the competitive perception of ligands by THE3. In fact, the CrRLK1L signaling pathway, which regulates pollen tube penetration, can be equilibrated by competitive ligands48. Here, the antagonistic signal of OsRALF4 could be a peptide, possibly RGF1 homologs6, but this hypothesis needs further experimental validation (Fig. 7). In addition to its signaling role, by employing cutting-edge super-resolution imaging techniques STED and 3D-dSTORM, we demonstrate that OsRALF4 incorporates into the cell wall by binding to dmPectin, promoting pectin condensation in expanding root cells. Our study thus elucidates an integral mechanistic framework of root development.

A proposed working model of OsRALF4-THE3-RbohB cascade in root development.

The OsRALF4-THE3-RbohB signaling pathway controls root developmental progression. In the meristem zone, the presence of unknown signals, such as RGF1 homologs, activates the interaction between THE3 and RbohB, which phosphorylates RbohB and promotes O2– production. This signal pathway is competed by OsRALF4. The low level of OsRALF4 in the MZ mildly inhibits THE3-RBohB signaling, balancing the distribution of O2– in the RAM. The accumulation of O2– disables PMEs through degrading PMEs, sustaining pectin in methylesterified state and maintaining meristem homeostasis. As root develops, OsRALF4 level increases in the transition region, which enhances the inhibitory effect on the THE3-RbohB signaling pathway and decreases O2– production, thereby stabilizing PMEs and promoting pectin demethylesterification. The meristematic cells thus convert to expansion. In the elongation zone, the further enhanced OsRALF4 inhibits O2– generation, which activates pectin demethylesterification through promoting PME stability. The resulting dmPectin binds to OsRALF4 to form the condensed pectin pattern in the EZ cell walls, facilitating cell wall integrity during cell elongation. In this figure, the thickness of arrows and lines indicates the degree of promotion and inhibition. The question marker indicates the signals need to be confirmed. CW, cell wall; mPectin, methylesterified pectin; dmPectin, demethylesterified pectin; PM, plasma membrane.

Remarkably, O2– production and OsRALF4 expression exhibit mutually antagonistic distribution patterns along root developmental axis. The functional duality of OsRALF4 is likely dictated by the availability of OsRALF4 and its binding partners. In the proposed working model (Fig. 7), OsRALF4 constitutes a pivotal integrated hub, whose dual roles as a signaling ligand and structural component likely modulate the progression of root development in a dose-dependent manner. In the MZ, OsRALF4 abundance remains low, which mildly inhibits THE3-RBohB signaling to equilibrate O2– distribution, suppressing pectin demethylesterification. As OsRALF4 level rises, the inhibitory effect is strengthened, and pectin undergo demethylesterification. As the increases of dmPectin, OsRALF4 peptides promote cell expansion by binding to dmPectin and condensing pectin matrix. Despite several uncertain steps, this mechanistic framework outlines an intricate scenario for root development, laying the foundation for the intelligent design of root systems with innovation.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All the rice plants used in this study were planted during the growing seasons in the experimental fields at the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology in Beijing (China) and Lingshui (Hainan Province, China). The rice seedlings were cultivated in hydroponic medium or grown in air-permeable germination pouches under long-day conditions (16 h of light and 8 h of dark).

The the3 and osralf4 mutants were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. The ospme30 mutants was purchased from BIOGLE GeneTech (China). To generate the THE3 overexpression lines, the coding sequence (CDS) of THE3 was cloned into pCAMBIA1300 vector between the 35S promoter and NOS terminator. For generation of RbohB overexpression lines, the CDS of RbohB was cloned into pJL1460 vector between ubiquitin promoter and NOS terminator. These resulting constructs were introduced into japonica variety ZH11 or the3 mutant via the transformation of Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain EHA105). For genetic complementation assays, the genomic fragment containing a 2158 bp promoter, the full coding region, and a 1,239 bp terminator was introduced into the the3 mutant. After confirming the genotyping by sequencing, the homozygous T2 plants were used for the experiments. The primers and gRNAs used in this study were listed in Supplementary Dataset 3.

qPCR analysis

For gene expression analysis, total RNAs were extracted from rice root tips using Plant RNA Reagent (Invitrogen, 12322012) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 2 μg of the total RNA were treated with DNase I (Takara, 2270 A) and used to generate cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, RR037A). qPCR was performed using a real-time PCR system (CFX96, Bio-Rad) with FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (Roche, 4913914001). Three biological replicates were included. The primers were listed in Supplementary Dataset 3.

NBT staining

For detecting O2–, 3 or 5-day-old rice seedlings and infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves were immersed in the NBT solution (0.05%) freshly prepared in 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0) and 10 mM NaN3. After infiltrating under vacuum at 0.8 psi for 2 min (root tips) or 30 min (N. benthamiana leaves), the stained samples were bleached and stored in 95% (v/v) ethanol for photographing. For quantifying NBT length, we measured the distance from the root tip to the onset of diminished NBT staining (indicated by a white triangle). For quantifying formazan, approximately 20 mg of stained tissues was employed to extract the resultant formazan using 1 mL of 2 M potassium hydroxide: chloroform (1:1, v/v) at 4 °C. The resulting extracts were dried under N2 at 4 °C, and resuspended by dissolving in 350 μL of DMSO and 300 μL of 2 M potassium hydroxide at room temperature. Formazan amount was measured according to absorbance at 630 nm using a spectrophotometer (Enspire, PerkinElmer). Three biological replicates were included.

EdU staining

For measuring cell proliferation using EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) staining, three to seven 3 or 5-day-old rice seedlings were incubated in PBS solution containing 20 μM EdU (Beyotime, C0085S) for 4 h. The resulting seedlings were fixed in freshly prepared fixative solution (3.7% [v/v] paraformaldehyde and 1% [v/v] Triton-X 100 in 1× PBS) for 1 h. After rinsing with 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× PBS twice, the seedlings were treated with 100 μL Click-iT reaction mix for 30 min to label the incorporated EdU with Azide Alexa Fluor 488, and then applied to visualize DNA synthesis using a confocal microscope (LSM980, Zeiss). We recorded the length of the root axis exhibiting EdU fluorescence as the EdU length.

TurboID proximity labeling

To identify the interacting proteins of RbohB and THE3, the CDS of RbohB and THE3 were fused in frame with the CDS of TurboID between Ubiquitin promoter and NOS terminator in Raichu vector. The resulting plasmids were transformed into rice protoplasts prepared according to the previous report49. After incubating for 12 h, the protoplasts were treated with 50 µM biotin for 3 h and then subjected to total protein extraction using the extraction buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM DTT, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1% Triton X-100, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). After centrifuging at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min, the protein extract supernatants were incubated with 30 µL of Dynabeads C1 Streptavidin (Invitrogen) at 4 °C for 4 h. The biotinylated proteins bound to streptavidin beads were separated by SDS-PAGE. After destaining, reduction with dithiothreitol (DTT), and alkylation with iodoacetamide, Coomassie-stained gel bands were digested overnight with trypsin, followed by stepwise extraction of peptides with acetonitrile at different concentrations. The peptide mixture obtained from enzymatic digestion was subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis, and then used for database search and protein identification via the SEQUEST HT search engine in Thermo Proteome Discoverer (version 1.4.0.288) against the rice annotation project database (RAP-DB, https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/). Search parameters include trypsin digestion with 2 allowed missed cleavage sites, precursor ion mass tolerance < 10 ppm, and fragment ion mass tolerance < 0.02 mDa. Spectrum filtering was performed using Percolator with Delta Cn < 0.1, false discovery rate (FDR) set to 1%, and peptide filtering parameter selected as “High” peptide confidence.

To detect RbohB in the TurboID-labeled samples, we prepared polyclonal antibodies against RbohB using a synthetic peptide (amino acids 30–43, CPNSGNLGSSNRSTK) in rabbit (HUABIO, China). For protein blotting analysis, the biotinylated proteins were extracted from the rice protoplasts expressing THE3-TurboID-cMyc fusion protein, and separated on 10% (w/v) SDS-PAGE gel. By transferring to nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad), the blot was blocked in TBST buffer containing 5% (w/v) milk for at least 2 h, and incubated with anti-RbohB antibody at 1:1000 dilution, followed by incubating with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at 1:10,000 dilution (Dingguo). After developing with chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Cytiva, RPN2235), the blot was imaged by Tanon 5200 Imaging System (Tanon). The relative amount of proteins was quantified using ImageJ software. The experiments were repeated three times with similar results.

Protein interaction assays

For split-luciferase assay, the CDSs of THE3, RbohB and OsRALF4 were cloned into the pCMABIA1300-cLUC vector and the pCAMBIA1300-nLUC vector, respectively. The resulting constructs were pairwise infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After cultivation for 48 h, the infiltrated leaves were sprayed with 1 mM D-luciferin and then kept in dark for 5 min. The luciferase signal was captured using IndiGO software (Berthold). GFP-nLUC and cLUC-GFP were served as negative controls.

For Co-IP assay, the CDS of THE3 and RbohB was fused with cMyc in the pCAMBIA1300 vector or fused with GFP in the pBI221 vector to generate 35S:THE3KD-cMyc/35S:GFP or THE3KD-cMyc/35S:RbohBN-GFP constructs. The resulting constructs were transformed into rice protoplasts. After overnight cultivation, these protoplasts were applied to total protein extraction as described above. The protein samples were incubated with 20 µL of GFP-trap agarose at 4 °C for 4 h. The co-precipitated proteins were eluted and separated by SDS-PAGE. Protein blotting was performed using anti-GFP antibody (Abcam) and anti-cMyc antibody (Abclonal) as described above.

For pull-down assay, the coding sequence of THE3 kinase domain was fused with GST (Glutathione-S-Transferase) in pGEX-6p-1 vector. The N-terminal RbohB was cloned in pET28a vector. The resulting constructs were transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3), separately. The recombinant proteins of THE3KD-GST and RbohBN-His were purified following the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 3 µg purified RbohBN-His were incubated with 9 µg immobilized GST or GST-THE3KD at 4 °C for 2 h. The eluants were separated by SDS-PAGE and then subjected to protein blotting using anti-GST (Abclonal) or anti-His (Biodragon) antibody. The above experiments were repeated three times each, and the results were similar.

Subcellular localization

To determine the subcellular localization of THE3, the CDS of THE3 was amplified and fused to GFP in the vector pBI221 (HonorGene) using the primers (Supplementary Dataset 3). The THE3-GFP construct, together with the construct harboring an mRFP-HIR1 (a plasma membrane marker), was transformed into the protoplasts prepared from 2-week-old NP seedlings as described above. By incubating in the dark overnight, the transfected rice protoplasts were photographed under a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM980). 35S:GFP was used as a control.

For the subcellular localization of OsRALF4, the CDS of OsRALF4 was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector using the primers (Supplementary Dataset 3). OsRALF4-GFP was transformed into the 3-week-old Nicotiana tabacum leaves. The N. benthamiana leaves were soaked in 1 M mannitol for 10 minutes for plasmolysis. Fluorescence was oberved using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss, LSM710). The above experiments were repeated three times with similar results.

Protein phosphorylation assay

For in vitro phosphorylation assay, the purified THE3KD-GST was incubated with a universal substrate, myelin basic protein (Sigma) or MBP in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], 0.1 mM ATP, and 5 μCi [γ-32P]ATP) for 1 h, and then terminated by the addition of stop buffer (0.25 M Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% [w/v] SDS, 50% [v/v] glycerol, 0.5% [w/v] bromophenol blue, 5% [v/v] β-mercaptoethanol). The labeled proteins were separated in 10% SDS-PAGE gel and subjected to autoradiography using a Typhoon 9410 imager (GE Healthcare). The proteins in parallel reactions without [γ-32P]ATP were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

For the phosphoprotein mobility-shift assay, the purified RbohBN-His were incubated with GST-THE3KD in a kinase buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM ATP) at 30 °C for 30 min. Additional parallel samples were incubated with CIP (NEB) at 37 °C for 30 min. By separating in a 12% (w/v) SDS-PAGE gel containing 50 μM Phos-tag and 100 μM MnCl2, the phosphorylated proteins were detected using anti-His antibody (Biodragon) as described50.

To validate the in vivo phosphorylation of RbohB, the CDS of RbohB was fused to NanoLUC in Raichu vector. The resulting construct was transfected into the rice protoplasts together with THE3-GFP or GFP. After overnight incubation, the total proteins were extracted. The aliquots of the samples were treated with 1 U/μL CIP (NEB) or 2 μM OsRALF4 peptide for 30 min. After trichloroacetic acid precipitation, the samples were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gel containing 50 μM Phos-tag. By washing the gel in an SDS-removing buffer (50 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA) for 1 h, the gel was developed using Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay Substrate (Promega, N1110) and was imaged using a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescent imager. The relative protein level was determined by normalized densitometry. The experiments were repeated three times with similar results.

NADPH oxidase activity analysis

RbohB proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti-RbohB antibody, and the NADPH oxidase activity was detected following the manufacturer’s instructions (AIDISHENG, ADS-F-NA001). NADPH oxidase activity was measured as Δ450 per mg protein per min. Three biological replicates were included.

Protein degradation assays

Cell-free protein degradation was performed as described previously with some modifications50. In brief, the total proteins were extracted from 2-week-old wild-type, the3, and THE3ox seedlings using extraction buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 20% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1 × inhibitor cocktail). The purified RbohB-His proteins (10 μg) were incubated with an equal amount of the total extract proteins with 10 mM ATP. The protein mixtures were separated by SDS-PAGE and probed using anti-His monoclonal antibody. The loading control was indicated by the proteins probed by anti-actin (EASYBIO). The relative protein level was determined by normalized densitometry.

To examine the stability of OsPME30 protein, the purified MBP-OsPME30 or MBP proteins were treated with O2– generated from the reaction containing 3 mM xanthine (XON) and 0.5 U or 1 U xanthine oxidase (XOD). The residues were subjected to protein blotting using the anti-MBP monoclonal antibody (NEB). To obtain in vivo support, the cMyc-OsPME30 construct was infected into the 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves along with the RNAi inhibitor vector P19 (OD600 = 1). After cultivation for 48 h, the leaves were treated with 200 µM Tiron for 1 h. Total proteins were extracted from the infected leaves and subjected to western blotting. The content of OsPME30 was determined by probing the blot with anti-cMyc antibody. The experiments were repeated three times with similar results.

Pectin analysis

By germinating wild-type and mutant seeds in a wet petri dish for 3 d, we further treated the wild-type seedlings in 200 μM Tiron for 12 h. The root tips from 20 individual plants of each sample were harvested and ball-milled into powder. The resulting samples were extracted with alcohol and treated with 1% α-amylase (w/v) to obtain destarched alcohol insoluble residues (AIRs). The AIRs were treated with endo-polygalacturonanase M2 (Megazyme) in 50 mM sodium acetate solution at 30 °C for 16 h. The released oligogalacturonides were derivatized in 100 mM procainamide (Sigma) containing 250 mM sodium cyanoborohydride at 37 °C overnight. For MALDI–TOF MS assay, the derivates were mixed with a matrix solution (25 mg/mL 2,5-dihydroxbenzoic acid) and spotted on the target plate with 0.5 μL of 5-chloro-2-mercaptobenothiazole (10 mg/mL). The mass spectra were collected within m/z range of 200–2,000 in the reflection positive ion mode (laser intensity 95%, ion source 1 voltage 20.00 kV, ion source 2 voltage 17.75 kV, lens voltage 7.60 kV, detector voltage 2127 V, and pulsed ion extraction 180 ns). Each spectrum corresponded to an ion accumulation of 2,000–5,000 laser shots randomly distributed on the spot. The spectra were analyzed using FlexAnalysis v.3.4 software (Bruker Daltonics). The relative intensity of methyl esters on oligogalacturonides was determined by dividing the methyl-esterified signal by the total detected oligmers. At least three replicates were included, and the replicates without Tiron treatment were set as controls.

PME activity assay

Total PME activity was determined according to a previous study51. Five-day-old root tips were ground in liquid nitrogen and subjected to the extraction of total proteins using 200 μL extraction buffer (0.1 M citrate acid, 0.2 M Na2HPO4, and 1 M NaCl, pH 5.0). The samples were incubated on ice for 1 h and centrifuged at 4 °C at 13,000 × g for 15 min. Protein extracts were added in the reaction containing 0.5% citrus pectin (0.2 M NaCl and 0.002% methyl red, pH 6.8) at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 525 nm using EnSpire Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer). Calibration curve was obtained using a gradient of 0 to 200 nM HCl solution. Total PME activity was calculated according to the established standard curve.

PME activity was also measured using pectoplate method52. In brief, pectoplate was prepared as 1% (w/v) low-temperature agarose (USB) medium containing 0.1% (w/v) citrus pectin (Sigma), 12.5 mM citric acid, and 50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 6.5. Equal amounts (30 μL) of the eluent fractions or protein extracts were loaded into each punched well and incubated at 30 °C for 16 h. The plates were stained with 0.02% (w/v) ruthenium red (Sigma) for 30 min. By rinsing with distilled water, the size of stained circle was measured to indicate PME activity. To examine PME activity with the increase of O2–, a gradient amount (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 10 U) of xanthine oxidase (XOD) were added in the pectoplate containing 3 mM xanthine (XON). PME activity was measured as described above.

The CDS of OsPME30 was fused with His-tag to its C-terminus in pPICZα-C vector (Invitrogen) and introduced into Pichia pastoris strain X-33 cells by electroporation following the manufacturer’s instructions. OsPME30-His recombinant proteins were purified using affinity chromatography53. The activity of OsPME30 was measured using pectoplate as described above. The experiments were repeated three times with similar results.

RNA-seq analysis

The total RNA was extracted from ZH11 roots using Trizol reagent. The samples were subjected to deep sequencing using Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina) after library construction (Novogene). The obtained paired-end clean reads were aligned to the rice reference genome, Release 7 of the MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.uga.edu/), using HISAT2. Read counting and normalization (FPKM) were performed using StringTie version 2.1.7 (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/) and the package Ballgown (https://github.com/alyssafrazee/ballgown). Three biological replicates were included in this experiment for each sample.

MST assay

The mature peptides of OsRALF1, OsRALF4, OsRALF6 and OsRALF15 were synthesized with high purity (> 95%) using the services of Sangon and HUABIO. To test their binding affinity to THE3 or pectin, OsRALF4 peptides, BSA or the purified GST-THE3ECD recombinant proteins were labeled using RED-NHS (Monolith, MOL011) in the buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 6.8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20) for 30 min, separately. The resulting samples were purified by affinity chromatography using the B-Column to wash off free fluorescent chemicals. Followed by collecting the conjugated peptides, a gradient of the labeled RALFs was incubated with GST-THE3 pectin or peptides in 1 mg/mL BSA, respectively. The reactions were loaded into the silica capillaries (Monolith NT.115 Standard Treated Capillaries, MO-K002; Monolith NT.115 MST Premium Coated Capillaries, MOK005) and measured by using Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper) at 20% or 40% LED power. Dissociation constant (Kd) value was calculated using Mo Affinity Analysis (V2.2.4) based on the fit curve. To examine the binding affinity between OsRALF4 and dmPectin or mPectin, the operation was the same as described above, using BSA as a negative control. Three biological replicates were included in this experiment.

Confocal microscopy

For pectin immunolabeling, the rice seedlings were germinated in petri dish for 2 d, and then transferred to ½ MS medium containing 200 µM Tiron for an additional 1 d. The roots were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in 4% agarose. Longitudinal sections with 15-µm thickness were prepared using a vibratome (Leica, VT1200) and probed with the LM20 and 2F4 antibody (Kerafast) at a 1:200 dilution. Alexa Fluor 488-coupled anti-rat IgG (Abcam) and Alexa Fluor 568-coupled anti-mouse IgG (Abcam) were used as the secondary antibody at a 1:500 dilution. Fluorescent signals of 10 images from three individual plants were recorded using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM980). For OsRALF4 treatment, the germinated rice seeds were cultivated on ½ MS medium containing 2 µM OsRALF4. The immunolabeling assay was performed as described above. The replicates without Tiron and OsRALF4 treatment were set as controls.

To determine the meristem length, root tips of at least eight 3-day-old seedlings were used to prepare root longitudinal sections as described above. By staining with Calcofluor White (CFW), the images were taken by a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM980) and analyzed using ZEN software. In these images, the distance from the root tip to the onset of cell expansion was determined as MZ size. At least 10 images from at least eight individual plants were analyzed.

Super-resolution microscopy

For STED microscopy, OsRALF4 peptides were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The root longitudinal sections were prepared and incubated in the T/Ca/S buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.2) containing 0.2 μM labeled OsRALF4 for 1 h. The sections were further probed with LM20 and 2F4 antibodies, followed by incubating with Alexa Fluor 488-coupled anti-rat IgG and STAR ORANGE conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Aberior, NC1933863) at a 1:500 dilution. The images were acquired using STEDYCON (Aberrior microscope system) with excitation wavelengths of 640 nm for Alexa Fluor 647, 550–610 nm for STAR ORANGE and 495 nm for Alexa Fluor 488. At least 10 images from three individual plants were analyzed.

For dSTORM microscopy, at least three 3-day-old rice roots were pretreated in 10% peracetic acid solution (v/v) for 30 min. The cells in the root elongation zone were isolated and transferred onto the 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (Cellvis, D35-14-1.5-N) coated with poly-L-lysine (Merck, P4707). By incubating with primary and secondary antibodies sequentially, root cells were post-fixed for 15 min in 3.5% paraformaldehyde to prevent thermal drift of antibodies. For imaging, T/Ca/S buffer was replaced with the dSTORM imaging buffer (Nano-Microimaging, NWIB#1). Images were acquired by a commercial dSTORM system (Nano-Microimaging, STORM Ultra300) based on an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Ti2-E) equipped with a sCMOS camera (TUCSEN, Dhyana 95V2) and a 100 × oil-immersion objective (Nikon CFI Plan Apochromat λ, NA 1.49). The 642 nm laser (~2 kW/cm2) was used to excite the sample to photoswitch with most of the labeled Alexa Fluor 647 dye into a dark state. The images were collected at ~100 frames/sec. Typically, dSTORM images were reconstructed from ~50,000 frames based on single-molecule localization algorithm as described previously54,55. For data analysis, peak fitting was performed using a Gaussian function in OriginPro 2021. A custom MATLAB program was used for cluster analysis of the cell wall. Clustering was performed using the DBSCAN function with the arguments (epsilon, minpts). The polyarea function was used to calculate the area of each cluster, which was used for distribution statistics. At least 5 images from three individual plants were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Two-tailed Student’s t test was performed using Excel, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with Holm-Sidak method using GraphPad Prism (v9.1.1).
