Marked Increase in Zinc Concentration of Ascitic Fluid After Cell-free and Concentrated Ascites Reinfusion Therapy in Patients With Cirrhotic Refractory Ascites: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: TADASHI NAMISAKI, AKIHIKO SHIBAMOTO, SATOSHI IWAI, MASAYOSHI TAKAMI, HIROYUKI MASUDA, YUKI TSUJI, YUKIHISA FUJINAGA, HIROAKI TAKAYA, TAKASHI INOUE, SHINYA SATO, KOH KITAGAWA, NORIHISA NISHIMURA, KOSUKE KAJI, AKIRA MITORO, KIYOSHI ASADA, HITOSHI YOSHIJI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimZinc (Zn) deficiency is common among patients with cirrhosis and is associated with hepatic encephalopathy, immune dysfunction, and sarcopenia. In Japan, cell-free and concentrated ascites reinfusion therapy (CART) is widely employed for refractory ascites, effectively restoring albumin (ALB) and coagulation factors. However, its effect on trace elements has not been investigated. This retrospective study aimed to assess Zn concentration changes in ascitic fluid before and after CART in patients with cirrhotic refractory ascites.Patients and MethodsData from 24 patients with decompensated cirrhosis who underwent CART at Nara Medical University Hospital between June 2022 and May 2024 were analyzed. The concentrations of Zn and ALB in original and processed ascitic fluid were measured.ResultsAmong the cohort (mean age, 66.3±13.7 years, 69.6% men), 75.0% had Child-Pugh class C cirrhosis. The mean volume of drained ascitic fluid was 5,947.2±2,345.9 ml, whereas that of processed ascitic fluid was 350.6±167.6 ml. Positive correlations were observed between serum Zn and serum albumin (ALB) levels as well as ascitic Zn and ascitic ALB levels. The Zn concentration significantly increased from 12 µg/dl in the original ascitic fluid to 119 µg/dl in the processed ascitic fluid (p<0.001). The calculated recovery rates for Zn and ALB, based on the ratio of total Zn and ALB content in processed versus original fluid, were approximately 70% and 80%, respectively.ConclusionCART markedly increases Zn concentration in ascitic fluid, which may contribute to the restoration of micronutrient levels in patients with cirrhosis. Further studies should determine whether reinfusion of Zn-rich processed ascitic fluid increases serum Zn levels and enhances clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Refractory ascites is a serious complication of advanced liver cirrhosis, affecting approximately 10% of patients annually despite optimal diuretic therapy and sodium restriction (1). It is associated with poor prognosis, with a 2-year survival rate of approximately 30%, and poses a high risk for complications, such as hepatorenal syndrome and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (2). Apart from the physical burden, refractory ascites limits mobility, appetite, and independence, markedly impairing quality of life. The Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines for Liver Cirrhosis in Japan recommend two major interventional approaches: large-volume paracentesis (LVP) with ALB infusion and cell-free and concentrated ascites reinfusion therapy (CART) (3,4). Although LVP rapidly relieves tense ascites, it leads to the loss of substantial amounts of ALB and other plasma proteins, requiring replacement to avoid paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction (5). CART, which is domestically developed, has the advantage of concentrating and reinfusing autologous ascitic fluid following filtration to remove bacteria and malignant cells, thereby restoring valuable proteins and other components to the systemic circulation (6).

Previous studies have demonstrated that CART effectively increases the concentrations of total protein, ALB, immunoglobulin, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and coagulation factors, such as antithrombin-III, factor VII, and factor X. However, its effect on micronutrients, particularly trace elements, has received little attention (7).

Zinc (Zn) is an essential trace element involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions, including those associated with ammonia metabolism, protein synthesis, antioxidant defense, and immune regulation (8). Zn deficiency is highly prevalent in cirrhosis owing to several factors, including reduced dietary intake, impaired intestinal absorption, increased urinary excretion, and ascitic fluid sequestration (8). Clinically, Zn deficiency contributes to hepatic encephalopathy (HE), altered taste, impaired wound healing, and sarcopenia. Supplementation has been demonstrated to improve neuropsychiatric symptoms and nutritional status in some patients.

Considering the role of Zn in cirrhosis and the known protein-concentrating capacity of CART, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the concentration of Zn, similar to ALB, may increase during the procedure. To our knowledge, no published study has evaluated the change in ascitic Zn concentration before and after CART (9).

Therefore, this retrospective observational study was conducted to evaluate Zn concentration changes in ascitic fluid during CART in patients with cirrhotic refractory ascites using data from a previously characterized clinical cohort. The study aimed to provide preliminary evidence on whether CART may be used as a protein restoration therapy and a potential adjunct for trace element replenishment.

Patients and Methods

Study design and ethical approval. This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Gastroenterology, Nara Medical University Hospital. The study was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committee of Nara Medical University Hospital (Nara-Portal, 20-001) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for CART and the use of clinical data for research was obtained from all the patients.

Patient population. The study enrolled 24 consecutive patients with decompensated cirrhosis and refractory ascites who underwent CART between June 1, 2020, and May 31, 2022. According to international criteria, refractory ascites was defined as ascites unresponsive to maximal diuretic therapy or rapidly recurring following therapeutic paracentesis. Patients were excluded if they had spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (polymorphonuclear leukocyte count ≥250/mm3 in ascitic fluid), active gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal wall hematoma, gastrointestinal perforation, or clinical signs of peritonitis.

The patients’ baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), etiology of cirrhosis, Child-Pugh score and class, presence of hepatocellular carcinoma, and presence of portal vein thrombosis.

CART procedure. CART was performed using an internal pressure-type system (KPS-8800Ce; Asahi Kasei Kuraray Medical, Tokyo, Japan) and a dedicated circuit (KMT-8601; Kawasumi Laboratories, Tokyo, Japan). Following complete drainage of ascitic fluid via abdominal paracentesis at a rate of 50-100 ml/min, the fluid was passed through an AHF-MO filtration filter for the removal of cellular components and bacteria (Asahi Kasei Kuraray Medical) and then concentrated using an AHF-UP concentration filter (Asahi Kasei Kuraray Medical).

A rate of 1.0 ml/min was initially applied in the intravenous infusion of the final processed ascitic fluid and gradually increased to 5.0 ml/min, depending on patient tolerance. The reinfusion was typically completed within 1-2 h.

Zinc measurement. The Zn concentrations in original and processed ascitic fluid were measured via atomic absorption spectrophotometry in the hospital’s central clinical laboratory. To avoid contamination, samples were collected in trace element-free tubes, centrifuged to remove residual cells or debris, and promptly analyzed to prevent precipitation or degradation.

Statistical analysis. Continuous variables are expressed as mean±standard deviation (10). Paired t-tests were employed to compare the Zn concentrations before and after CART. A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS for Windows, version 20.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics. The study cohort included 10 men (41.7%) and 14 women (58.3%), with a mean age of 66.3±13.7 years and mean BMI of 22.0±5.6 kg/m2 (Table I). The etiologies of cirrhosis were alcohol-related liver disease (n=10, 41.7%), hepatitis C virus infection (n=6, 25.0%), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (n=5, 20.8%), and hepatitis B virus infection (n=3, 12.5%). A total of six patients (25.0%) had Child-Pugh class B and 18 (75.0%) had class C. Furthermore, eight patients (33.3%) had hepatocellular carcinoma and five (20.8%) had portal vein thrombosis. The mean volume of drained ascitic fluid per session was 5,947.2±2,345.9 ml, the mean volume of processed ascitic fluid was 350.6±167.6 ml, and the mean reinfusion rate was 2.2±1.2 ml/min.

Zinc concentration changes. The mean concentration of Zn in the original ascitic fluid was 12 µg/dl, whereas that in the processed ascitic fluid was 119 µg/dl (Figure 1A). Meanwhile, the mean ALB concentration in the original ascitic fluid was 0.83 g/dl, whereas that in the processed ascitic fluid was 8.9 g/dl. These values represent a nearly tenfold increase, and the difference in ascitic Zn and ALB was statistically significant (p<0.001) (Figure 1B). The calculated recovery rates for Zn and ALB, based on the ratio of total Zn and ALB content in the processed versus original fluid, were approximately 70% and 80%, respectively (Table II).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that CART markedly increases the Zn concentration of processed ascitic fluid compared with the original fluid in patients with cirrhotic refractory ascites (11). The nearly tenfold increase from 12 to 119 µg/dl reflects the concentrating effect previously documented for ALB and other high-molecular-weight proteins during CART. This finding suggests that CART may play a role in micronutrient restoration in cirrhosis, in addition to ALB and coagulation factor restoration.

In plasma, approximately 60%-80% of Zn is bound to ALB, with most of the remainder bound to α2-macroglobulin and transferrin. Free Zn accounts for less than 1% of total plasma Zn. Considering that ALB is well retained by the filtration and concentration system used in CART, it can be inferred that protein-bound Zn in ascitic fluid is similarly concentrated during processing. The 80% Zn recovery rate observed for CART in the present study is consistent with previously reported protein recovery rates (7), indicating that Zn retention is mainly dependent on the preservation of ALB and other Zn-binding proteins during filtration and ultrafiltration.

Furthermore, ascitic Zn may exist in a dynamic equilibrium with plasma Zn. Although the extent to which reinfused Zn redistributes into systemic circulation remains unclear, it is plausible that CART-mediated Zn restoration could transiently elevate plasma Zn levels. This would be particularly relevant in patients with severe baseline Zn deficiency, where even a modest increase could have clinical consequences.

Zn deficiency is highly prevalent in patients with cirrhosis, with some studies reporting prevalence rates exceeding 80% (12,13). The deficiency arises from multiple factors, including reduced dietary intake due to anorexia and altered taste sensation (14), malabsorption from portal hypertensive enteropathy (14), increased urinary incontinence related to diuretic therapy (15), and Zn sequestration in ascitic fluid (16), particularly in patients with large-volume ascites (17).

Clinically, Zn deficiency aggravates hyperammonemia by impairing the activity of ornithine transcarbamylase and other urea cycle enzymes (18). Moreover, it impairs protein synthesis, compromises immune defenses, delays wound healing, and contributes to hypogeusia and anorexia. Several clinical trials have demonstrated that oral or intravenous Zn supplementation can enhance ammonia metabolism, neuropsychiatric performance, and nutritional status in patients with cirrhosis (18,19).

HE is a common complication of advanced cirrhosis and is characterized by neuropsychiatric symptoms and impaired psychomotor function (20). Some studies have shown that Zn supplementation reduces blood ammonia levels and increases HE scores, particularly in patients with documented deficiency (21). If CART-induced Zn restoration can transiently increase serum Zn concentration, it can support ammonia detoxification pathways and enhance HE control, although this remains speculative (22).

Considering these associations, the restoration of Zn-rich processed ascitic fluid through CART could theoretically provide a supplemental source of Zn, particularly in patients undergoing repeated sessions. Although the total Zn concentration reinfused during CART is modest compared with pharmacological supplementation, it is autologous, bioavailable, and reintroduced along with ALB and other supportive proteins (23).

To date, no other interventional therapies for refractory ascites are known to restore micronutrients. LVP with ALB infusion helps maintain oncotic balance but does not restore patient-derived trace elements. Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt may reduce ascites accumulation but does not directly impact micronutrient status. Thus, the potential of CART to restore trace elements, such as Zn, could represent a unique and underrecognized benefit, particularly in settings where nutritional deficiencies are common.

Sarcopenia is another major concern in decompensated cirrhosis, which negatively affects patient survival and quality of life (24). Zn plays a pivotal role in protein synthesis and muscle regeneration; thus, Zn restoration may indirectly contribute to muscle maintenance (25). Although the concentration of restored Zn during CART may be insufficient to reverse sarcopenia alone, it could complement targeted nutritional interventions.

The main limitations of the present study include its small sample size, retrospective design, and lack of postoperative measurements of serum Zn concentration. The lack of paired serum data makes it impossible to determine the extent to which reinfused Zn affects systemic Zn status. In addition, clinical outcomes, such as HE episodes, infection rates, and muscle mass changes associated with Zn restoration, have not been evaluated. Future research should fill these gaps through prospective studies that include measurement of serum Zn before and after CART over multiple sessions and assessment of functional endpoints, including neuropsychiatric testing, ammonia levels, and muscle strength.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that CART is not only an effective therapy for refractory ascites and a means of protein restoration but also a potential vehicle for restoring Zn, an essential micronutrient with far-reaching metabolic and clinical roles. Further investigation of this novel aspect of CART is warranted to determine its contribution to patient outcomes in advanced liver disease.
