Introduction

Background

Undernutrition is a condition in which an individual’s bodily functions are impaired due to insufficient intake or absorption of nutrients, resulting in the inability to maintain normal physiological performance [1]. It encompasses both acute malnutrition characterized by bilateral pitting edema and/or wasting and deficiencies in essential micronutrients [2, 3]. A strong bidirectional relationship exists between undernutrition and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, wherein each condition exacerbates the other, creating a vicious cycle of declining health [4].

Nutritional status is commonly assessed using Body Mass Index (BMI), with undernutrition defined as a BMI below 18.5 kg/m² [5]. It is further classified by severity as severe (BMI < 16 kg/m²), moderate (BMI 16 to 17.00 kg/m²), or mild (BMI ≥ 17 to < 18.5 kg/m²) [6].

Globally, 40.8 million people were living with HIV as of 2024 [7]. Undernutrition both contributes to and results from HIV infection, as malnutrition weakens the immune system and HIV increases nutritional needs while impairing nutrient absorption and appetite [8, 9]. In 2018, Africa reported 256.1 million cases of undernutrition, ranking third globally after Asia [10]. Of the 37.9 million people living with HIV at that time, approximately 70% resided in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), making it the region most burdened by the dual challenges of HIV and malnutrition [11].

Numerous studies across SSA have reported varying rates of undernutrition among people living with HIV (PLHIV): 43.3% in Nigeria [12], 19.4% in Tanzania [13], 19.2% in Senegal [14], 13.8% in Ghana [15], 10.28% in Uganda [16], and 10% in Zimbabwe [17]. In Ethiopia, one of the SSA countries most severely affected, the prevalence of undernutrition among PLHIV remains high: 25.2% in Dilla [18], 18.3% in Asella [19], 34% and 30% in the Oromia region [20, 21], 60% in the Benishangul Gumuz Regional State [22], 29.2% Bench_Sheko zone, Southwest Ethiopia [23].

Several individual and contextual factors have been associated with undernutrition among PLHIV. These include advanced stages of HIV disease [17] and recent exposure to opportunistic infections (OIs) [24]. Nutritional factors such as low dietary diversity [25] and eating difficulties [26], household food insecurity [27, 28] and socio-demographic and economic challenges, have also been identified as key contributors to undernutrition [13, 15, 28, 29].

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the pooled odds ratios of associated factors of undernutrition among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia. A previous systematic review by Alebel et al. [30] reported the pooled prevalence of undernutrition, but did not address the associated factors quantitatively. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively assess the pooled prevalence of undernutrition among PLHIV in Ethiopia and identify its key associated factors.

Understanding these associated factors is critical for the design and implementation of effective nutrition and health interventions. By identifying the most influential factors, stakeholders and policymakers can develop tailored strategies to improve the nutritional status and overall health of PLHIV. Such insights are also essential for informing long-term, sustainable responses to the dual burden of HIV and undernutrition in Ethiopia.

Methods

Reporting and protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (S1 Table 1) [31]. The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42023403023. The registered protocol is accessible at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?ID=CRD42023403023.

Ethical approval

was not required, as this review synthesized data from previously published, publicly available studies. All original studies included in the analysis had obtained informed consent from their respective participants. The review followed a systematic and sequential process, beginning with a comprehensive literature search and concluding with data extraction, analysis, and synthesis of findings.

Search strategies

A comprehensive search was conducted to identify studies on the prevalence and associated factors of undernutrition among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia. Peer-reviewed articles and grey literature were retrieved from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, AJOL, and Google Scholar, supplemented by manual searches of references and organizational websites. Search terms used keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) for nutritional status, HIV, adult populations, and Ethiopia, using Boolean operators. The PubMed search syntax was: (“undernutrition” OR “malnutrition” OR “nutritional status” OR “underweight” OR “wasting” OR “stunting”) AND (“HIV” OR “AIDS” OR “HIV-positive” OR “ART”) AND (“adults” OR “adolescents” OR “young adults”) NOT “children” AND “Ethiopia.” Studies published between January 2020 and May 2025 were included.

All identified records were imported into a reference management software, and duplicates were removed. The titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers, followed by full-text screening of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Screening

Three researchers (A.A, AA, and EW) individually selected studies that matched the inclusion criteria. The titles and abstracts were examined for inclusion, and duplicate citations were removed. After reviewing titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria outlined above, the researchers collected the complete texts of potentially eligible papers. To ensure that qualifying research was included, the researchers ran the entire text through a systematic and pre-tested approach. In the event of conflicts, they were resolved through consensus among the researchers or by consulting a third author (TT). The researchers used EndNote software (version X7) to import papers from electronic databases and manage citations.

Data extraction

Three reviewers (A.A, AA, and EW) worked individually to gather data from the included studies. A predefined data extraction format on a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was used to collect information on the name of the author or authors, the year of publication, the study design, the place of the study, the region of the study, the sample size, the age range of study participants, the sex of study participants, the prevalence of undernutrition, and pertinent risk factors or associated factors of undernutrition.

Outcome measurement

Anthropometric indicators, namely stunting, underweight, wasting, or thinness, were used based on WHO growth standards. Undernutrition (underweight) refers to an individual with a BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2. The severity of undernutrition is further classified as severe (BMI 16 kg/m2), moderate (BMI 16–16.99 kg/m2), or mild (BMI 17–18.48 kg/m2) [34].

Assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) [33]. Key domains assessed included study objectives, sample size, and sampling technique. Studies scoring more than 6 points were deemed of sufficient quality and included in the meta-analysis (see Table S2). Quality assessment was conducted independently by three author (A.A., T.T., A.A., and E.W.), with disagreements resolved through discussion with a fourth author. For studies reporting associated factors, each variable’s association with undernutrition was critically assessed using the same quality threshold.

Data analysis and assessment of publication bias

Data analysis was performed using STATA software, focusing on both prevalence estimates and associated factors of undernutrition. The methodological quality of included studies was assessed by three reviewers (A.A., A.A., and E.W.) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data [35]. which consists of nine criteria evaluating study validity. Each item was rated as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not Applicable.

To assess potential publication bias, a funnel plot was visually inspected, and Egger’s regression test was conducted. A p-value of less than 0.05 in the Egger test was considered indicative of significant publication bias.

Data processing and analysis

Data were extracted into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using STATA version 14.0. A random-effects model was applied to estimate the pooled prevalence of undernutrition and the pooled odds ratios (ORs) of associated factors, with 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and I² statistic, with I² values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [35].

Publication bias was evaluated using a funnel plot and Egger’s test, with p < 0.05 indicating bias. Subgroup analyses were conducted by region, study setting, publication year, sample size, number of cases, and gender. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding each study one at a time to test result stability.

Heterogeneity across the studies

Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q-statistic and the I² statistic, which quantify variability across studies that is not due to chance alone [36]. The Q-statistic tests the null hypothesis that all studies share a common effect size, while the I² statistic describes the percentage of total variation across studies attributable to heterogeneity rather than sampling error.

In this review, the results revealed a high level of heterogeneity among the studies reporting undernutrition prevalence and its associated factors. The calculated I² value was 95.7%, with a corresponding p-value < 0.001 from the Q-test, indicating statistically significant and substantial heterogeneity across the studies included in the meta-analysis.

Due to this high heterogeneity, a random-effects meta-analysis model was employed using the DerSimonian and Laird method to estimate the pooled effect sizes. This model assumes that the included studies are not functionally identical but instead estimate different, yet related, true effects. It accounts for both within-study and between-study variability, providing more conservative and generalizable estimates, particularly when heterogeneity is considerable.

The substantial heterogeneity observed may stem from differences in study populations, settings, geographic regions, time of data collection, methodological quality, or variations in operational definitions and measurement tools. To further explore the sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses were conducted, as detailed in earlier sections.

Results

Study selection

The search and selection process for eligible studies is summarized in Fig. 1. Searches across multiple databases yielded the following results: PubMed (5,817 articles), ScienceDirect (1,721 articles), EMBASE (Elsevier) (10 articles), African Journals Online (AJOL) (3,550 articles), Cochrane Library (27 articles), and Google Scholar (5,930 articles), for a total of 17,055 records.

After removing 986 duplicates using EndNote software, 16,069 articles remained. These were screened by title and abstract, resulting in the exclusion of 16,005 articles that were irrelevant to the review objectives. The remaining 64 articles underwent full-text review. Of these, 12 were excluded because they were conducted outside Ethiopia, 19 were unrelated to the study scope, 4 involved non–HIV-positive populations, and 1 focused solely on elderly individuals.

Ultimately, 28 studies met the initial inclusion criteria; however, 17 were excluded at a later stage due to issues such as inappropriate study population, setting, outcome measures, design, or duplication. This process resulted in 11 studies being included in the final meta-analysis, providing comprehensive evidence on the prevalence and associated factors of undernutrition among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia (Fig. 1).

Pooled prevalence of undernutrition among PLHIV in Ethiopia

Across the included studies, the reported prevalence of undernutrition among people living with HIV (PLHIV) varied substantially from 42.9% in the Tigray region [43] to 16% in the Bench Maji Zone of the Southern Ethiopia region [39].

Using a random-effects meta-analysis model, the pooled prevalence of undernutrition was estimated at 26.70% (95% CI: 21.31–32.10) (Fig. 3). The analysis revealed a high level of heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 95.7%, p < 0.001), indicating substantial variability in prevalence estimates, likely due to differences in study settings, populations, and measurement methods (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis results are summarized in and illustrate variations in the pooled prevalence of undernutrition among PLHIV across different categories.

By Region: The Tigray region reported the highest prevalence at 42.9% (95% CI: 38.01–47.79), whereas the Harari region had the lowest at 19.10% (95% CI: 15.66–22.54) ( Table 4).

By Sample Size: Among the seven studies with ≤ 500 participants, the pooled prevalence was 29.58% (95% CI: 22.80–36.37), higher than in studies with larger sample sizes.

By Data Collection Method: Studies using interviewer-administered questionnaires showed a higher prevalence 37.15% (95% CI: 32.37–41.93) compared to those using dichotomous approaches 24.69% (95% CI: 19.39–29.98). This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.002).

Regarding regional representation, four studies were from Oromia [19, 20, 37, 40], three from the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR) [18, 23, 42], two from Addis Ababa [39, 43], and one each from Harari [41] and Tigray [38]. This distribution reflects a broad geographical coverage, enhancing the representativeness of the pooled findings (Fig. 3).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using both a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test. The funnel plot illustrated a relatively symmetrical distribution of study estimates, suggesting no substantial bias in the published literature. This visual finding was supported by the Egger test result (p = 0.062), indicating no statistically significant evidence of publication bias. Overall, the analysis suggests that the pooled prevalence estimates are unlikely to be distorted by selective publication ( Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the stability of the pooled prevalence estimates for undernutrition among PLHIV. Sequentially removing each study did not produce a notable change in the overall pooled prevalence, which consistently remained within the 95% confidence interval of the total estimate. This indicates that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall findings. The pooled prevalence values observed during exclusions ranged from 26.70% (95% CI: 7.66–68.51), confirming the robustness and reliability of the meta-analysis results (Table 2).

Meta-regression

A random-effects meta-regression was conducted to explore possible sources of heterogeneity across the included studies. Study-level covariates assessed included region, year of publication, sample size, quality score, and data collection tool. The analysis revealed no statistically significant associations between these covariates and the prevalence of undernutrition (all p-values > 0.05) (Table 3). This suggests that the variability among studies could not be explained by the examined parameters. The absence of significant moderators may reflect the limited number of included studies, reducing statistical power, as well as the influence of unmeasured factors—such as socioeconomic disparities, cultural practices, or health service accessibility—that were not captured in the analysis.

Associated factors of undernutrition among adults (PLHIV)

This meta-analysis quantitatively synthesized selected variables of undernutrition in people living with HIV (PLHIV), focusing on factors consistently defined and reported across multiple primary studies. Other potential predictors were excluded from pooling due to variability in definitions and outcome measures.

Opportunistic Infections (OIs): Five of the eleven included studies identified OIs as a strong predictor of undernutrition in PLHIV [19, 20, 38, 41, 44]. The pooled analysis yielded an odds ratio (OR) of 3.50 (95% CI: 1. 78–5.22), indicating that PLHIV with one or more OIs were nearly four times more likely to be undernourished than those without OIs.

WHO Clinical Stage: Four studies reported a significant association between WHO clinical stage II or higher and undernutrition, compared to stage I [19, 20, 38, 44]. The combined OR was 2.92 (95% CI: 1.09–4.74), suggesting that advanced disease stage nearly tripled the risk of undernutrition among PLHIV in Ethiopia.

CD4 Count: Five studies found that a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm³ significantly increased the risk of undernutrition compared to counts ≥ 500 cells/mm³ [18, 20, 23, 38, 41]. The pooled OR was 3.10 (95% CI: 1.42–4.78; I² = 0.0%, p = 0.619), indicating a threefold higher risk in individuals with severe immunosuppression.

Food Insecurity: Five studies highlighted food insecurity as a key contributor to undernutrition in PLHIV [23, 39, 40, 45]. The pooled OR was 3.62 (95% CI: 1.46–5.79; I² = 0.0%, p = 0.983), showing that individuals experiencing food insecurity were over three times more likely to be undernourished than food-secure counterparts (Table 4).

These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of undernutrition among PLHIV, with both biomedical (OIs, clinical stage, CD4 count) and socio-economic (food insecurity) factors playing significant roles (S3 Figure A to D).

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to provide updated evidence on the prevalence and associated factors of undernutrition among people living with HIV (PLHIV) in Ethiopia through a systematic review and meta-analysis. By synthesizing recent literature, this work builds on earlier findings and offers a more comprehensive understanding of the current situation.

The pooled prevalence of undernutrition among Ethiopian PLHIV was 26.70% (95% CI: 21.31–32.10%). This figure is slightly lower than the 32.6% reported in India [46] but higher than the 23.72% reported in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [47]. Such differences may be due to variations in socio-cultural contexts, economic conditions, food security, and healthcare access. In Ethiopia, widespread poverty, high population density, and recurrent food insecurity may explain the relatively high prevalence. Our finding is consistent with earlier Ethiopian meta-analyses reporting prevalence rates around 26–27% [30, 48]. However, prevalence comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, as differences in study design and population characteristics may limit direct comparability. Future research should explore the specific drivers of undernutrition in PLHIV to guide targeted, context-sensitive interventions.

Subgroup analysis revealed notable regional differences, with prevalence ranging from 16.0% in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR) to 42.90% in Tigray. These differences likely reflect disparities in socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, food security, and access to healthcare [49, 50]. In SNNPR, lower prevalence could also be influenced by cultural perceptions that associate higher body weight with prosperity, potentially masking underlying undernutrition. Nonetheless, the predominance of cross-sectional studies limits the ability to assess long-term patterns and causal relationships.

Our analysis showed that PLHIV with one or more opportunistic infections were 3.50 times more likely to be undernourished (95% CI: 1.78–5.22) compared to those without OIs. This association is well-documented in other settings, including Brazil and SSA, where OIs exacerbate nutritional decline by increasing metabolic demands and impairing nutrient absorption [51–53]. Undernutrition, in turn, weakens immune function, creating a cycle of infection and nutritional deterioration. This bidirectional relationship underscores the need for integrated management of nutrition and infection among PLHIV receiving ART.

Advanced WHO clinical stage (II or above) was also significantly associated with undernutrition, with a pooled odds ratio of 2.92 (95% CI: 1.09–4.74). This aligns with findings from SSA and Zimbabwe [17, 30, 48], where advanced disease stages are linked to weight loss, wasting, and higher mortality risk [54]. Early ART initiation, routine nutritional assessment, and targeted interventions for individuals in advanced stages could help reduce these risks.

A CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ was strongly associated with undernutrition (OR: 3.10, 95% CI: 1.42–4.78), consistent with prior SSA and Ethiopian meta-analyses [30, 48]. As HIV progresses, recurrent and severe OIs further compromise nutritional status, while undernutrition itself impairs immune recovery, creating a self-reinforcing decline [55]. These findings emphasize the importance of incorporating nutritional support into routine HIV care, particularly for immunocompromised patients.

Food insecurity emerged as another strong association with food-insecure PLHIV being 3.63 times more likely to be undernourished (95% CI: 1.46–5.79) compared to food-secure counterparts. Limited access to adequate and nutritious food not only affects body weight but also undermines ART adherence and treatment outcomes. Addressing food insecurity through targeted programs such as food assistance, livelihood support, and community-based agricultural initiatives could significantly reduce undernutrition and improve overall health outcomes in Ethiopian PLHIV.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Like any systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA), this study has both strengths and limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Strengths of this review include a comprehensive search strategy that combined manual and electronic searches across multiple databases, a rigorous and clearly defined methodology for data abstraction and analysis, and the use of predefined eligibility criteria for population, exposure, control, and outcomes. The broad search strategy increased the likelihood of capturing all relevant studies, thereby enhancing the robustness of the evidence base.

This systematic review and meta-analysis has some limitations. First, restricting the search to studies published in English may have excluded relevant non-English research, though this is unlikely to affect findings given that Ethiopian research is predominantly published in English. Second, variations in definitions of undernutrition and associated factors across studies may have limited comparability and hindered data pooling. Third, inconsistent reporting of BMI categories meant that some eligible studies could not be included in the meta-analysis. Finally, reliance on cross-sectional studies limits the ability to infer causal relationships, as these designs capture associations at a single point in time.

Implications of the findings

Our findings highlight the multifactorial nature of undernutrition in PLHIV, influenced by biomedical, socioeconomic, and structural factors. Addressing it requires integrated interventions combining nutritional assessment and support with ART, infection prevention, and socioeconomic assistance. Regional disparities call for tailored, context-specific strategies. By providing updated, nationally representative data and pooled estimates of key risk factors, this study offers evidence to guide policymakers, health officials, and stakeholders in prioritizing resources and designing comprehensive interventions to reduce undernutrition among PLHIV in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

Undernutrition among people living with HIV (PLHIV) remains a significant public health concern in Ethiopia. Major associated factors identified include exposure to opportunistic infections, advanced WHO clinical stages (II–V), and CD4 counts below 200 cells/mm³. These factors require particular attention, as they contribute substantially to the country’s growing burden of undernutrition. Beyond medical conditions, structural issues like food insecurity further exacerbate vulnerability.

Addressing this challenge demands integrated policy and programmatic interventions. National HIV programs should embed routine nutritional assessment, counseling, and support into standard HIV care, with targeted attention to high-risk individuals. Strengthening food and nutrition assistance programs, particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged households, can help mitigate underlying causes.

At the program level, training healthcare providers to identify and manage nutritional risks, integrating nutrition counseling into ART follow-up, and linking patients to community-based support services are essential. Coordinated action across health, agriculture, and social protection sectors is vital for sustainable improvements. Implementing these measures can reduce undernutrition, improve treatment outcomes, and enhance the quality of life for PLHIV in Ethiopia.
