Integrative BSA-seq and time-course RNA-seq prioritize candidate loci and genes associated with whole-plant cadmium (Cd) accumulation and tolerance in dark jute (Corchorus olitorius L.)
Authors: Xu Jian-tang, Zhu Shixin, Victoria Oluwapelumi Akintayo, Zhao Yaqi, Li Lijun, Li Han, Zhang Wei-qun, Tao Aifen, Fang Pingping, Qi Jianmin, Zhang Shu-biao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDark jute (Corchorus olitorius L.) is a high-biomass fiber crop with potential value for phytoremediation, but the genetic basis of whole-plant cadmium (Cd) accumulation and tolerance remains poorly understood. Identifying loci and candidate genes associated with Cd-related traits can support molecular improvement of dark jute for cultivation in Cd-affected environments.ResultsIn this study, we integrated bulked segregant analysis sequencing (BSA-seq) with time-course RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), Cd phenotyping, and qRT-PCR validation to investigate Cd accumulation and tolerance in dark jute. A Cd-tolerant, low-accumulating accession (CO57) and a Cd-sensitive, high-accumulating accession (CO83) were used to generate an F₂ population for extreme-phenotype bulking. Time-course RNA-seq of the tolerant parent revealed substantial Cd-responsive transcriptional reprogramming, with 3,784-4,167 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) detected across root control-versus-Cd comparisons and 2,216-3,532 DEGs detected across aboveground tissue comparisons. Overlap analysis showed 1,207 shared DEGs between root and aboveground DEG sets. GO classification and KEGG pathway annotation provided functional context for the Cd-responsive gene set, indicating the involvement of cellular regulation, metabolic responses, stress response, transport, detoxification, sulfur metabolism, glutathione-related metabolism, and protein degradation pathways. For BSA-seq, 50 extremely low-accumulating and 50 extremely high-accumulating F₂ individuals were pooled, yielding 625,354 high-quality SNPs. ΔSNP-index analysis detected five candidate intervals spanning 7.13 Mb and containing 662 annotated genes. By integrating positional evidence, Cd-responsive expression patterns, and functional annotations, seven candidate genes were prioritized, including genes encoding ABC transporter-like proteins, a NAC-domain transcription factor, and two PLAC8-domain proteins annotated as Cd-resistance-related proteins. qRT-PCR analysis supported the expression patterns of the prioritized candidates.ConclusionsThese results identify candidate genomic intervals and prioritized genes associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation and tolerance in dark jute. The study provides a genetic and transcriptomic framework for future functional validation, marker development, and phytoremediation-oriented improvement of dark jute.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09553-z.Keywords: Dark jute, Whole-plant cadmium accumulation, Cadmium tolerance, Time-course RNA-seq, BSA-seq, Candidate genes

Introduction

Elevated Cadmium (Cd) in agricultural environments arises from multiple anthropogenic and geogenic sources, including mining and smelting activities, atmospheric deposition, and the long-term use of Cd-bearing phosphate fertilizers, which can increase Cd inputs to arable soils over time [1, 2]. Because dietary exposure to Cd is largely driven by plant-derived foods, reducing Cd accumulation in crops and crop-based products is a priority for both sustainable agriculture and public health [3].

In plants, Cd disrupts growth and development by impairing photosynthesis, disturbing nutrient homeostasis, and triggering oxidative damage and cellular dysfunction [4]. Cd-induced oxidative and transcriptomic stress responses have also been reported in other organisms exposed to Cd, suggesting that oxidative damage is a conserved consequence of Cd toxicity across biological systems [5]. Cd-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) can damage nucleic acids and proteins [6], and Cd stress has been associated with reductions in photosynthetic indices, including photosynthetic rate and intracellular CO2 concentration [7]. Consistent with these effects, Cd can impede plant growth and development even at low concentrations [8].

At the molecular level, Cd enters root cells largely via transport system for essential divalent cations (e.g., Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Ca²⁺), and is subsequently regulated through chelation, sequestration, and transport processes [4]. Natural variation for Cd accumulation and tolerance is widespread, but these traits are typically quantitative, environmentally sensitive, and controlled by multiple loci [3, 4, 9].

High-biomass crops are attractive candidates for reducing heavy metal (HM) bioavailability through phytoremediation and phytoextraction, while aided phytostabilization using organic amendments has also been shown to improve remediation outcomes in metal-contaminated sites such as Pb-Zn mine tailings [10–12]. In phytoextraction-oriented systems, whole-plant metal accumulation is a useful integrated indicator because it combines tissue metal concentration with biomass production. Therefore, exploiting plant diversity remains important for improving HM accumulation capacity and identifying effective hyperaccumulating or remediation-suitable crops [13, 14].

Dark jute (Corchorus olitorius L.) is a major bast-fiber crop with substantial biomass potential and expanded genomic resources that facilitate locus discovery and molecular breeding [15, 16]. Recent studies support the capacity of jute to tolerate HM stress and accumulate metals [17], and integrative genetic dissection has demonstrated that mapped loci can be connected to candidate genes using combined genomic and transcriptomic evidence [18].

From a crop improvement perspective, breeding for Cd-related traits requires resolving two partially independent components: whole-plant Cd accumulation which reflects uptake, internal transport, and retention across organs; and Cd tolerance, reflecting the ability to maintain growth and limit toxicity under Cd exposure [3, 4]. Bulked Segregant Analysis (BSA), and sequencing-based BSA approaches (BSA-seq) enable efficient mapping of loci by comparing pooled DNA from phenotypic extremes [19, 20], but candidate intervals often contain many genes, requiring additional evidence to prioritize candidate genes. Transcriptome profiling provides complementary support by identifying stress-responsive pathways and genotype-specific programs, and integrating mapping-by-sequencing with RNA-seq can improve candidate resolution by intersecting mapped regions with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and enriched pathways [3, 4, 18, 21, 22]. Such integrative strategies have successfully identified candidate loci and genes for Cd accumulation in other crops, including rapeseed [9].

Despite these advances, the genetic architecture underlying whole-plant Cd accumulation and tolerance in dark jute remains insufficiently characterized, and integrative datasets linking mapped loci to time-resolved transcriptional responses are limited. Addressing this gap is important for developing molecular markers and candidate genes that support Cd-resilient dark jute improvement and broader deployment of high-biomass crops in Cd-impacted production systems [1, 23, 24].

Here, we combined BSA-seq of F₂ bulks defined by whole-plant Cd accumulation with time-course RNA-seq in the tolerant parent to map Cd accumulation-associated loci, characterize tolerance-associated transcriptional responses and prioritize candidate genes, including those related to metal transport. The objectives of this study were to: (i) evaluate Cd tolerance and accumulation differences between contrasting parental lines, (ii) identify candidate genomic intervals associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation using BSA-seq; (iii) characterize tissue-specific and time-dependent transcriptional responses to Cd stress; and (iv) prioritize candidate genes associated with Cd accumulation and tolerance for future functional validation and marker development.

Materials and methods

Plant material, Cd growing conditions, and experimental design

Seeds of dark jute accessions, CO57 (P1) and CO83 (P2), which were used as parental lines, were selected from the germplasm collection maintained by the Key Laboratory of Genetics, Breeding and Multiple Utilization of Bast Fiber Crops, Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University (FAFU), Fuzhou, China based on preliminary Cd-response screening. The accession names CO57 and CO83 were assigned by our laboratory for germplasm identification and management. These accessions were cultivated germplasm materials and were not collected from the wild; therefore, no specific permission or licence was required for sample collection. The plant materials were authenticated as Corchorus olitorius L. by Dr. Xu Jian-tang, FAFU, based on institutional germplasm records and morphological characteristics. No voucher specimen was deposited because the materials used in this study were cultivated accessions from the institutional germplasm collection. The experimental research on plants complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Seeds were sown in 72-cell trays and grown in the greenhouse of the Field Laboratory of Genetics, Breeding and Multiple Utilization of Bast Fiber Crops, FAFU, under a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod at 25 °C. After two weeks, uniform seedlings at the four-leaf stage were selected and transferred to a 100 mL conical flask containing half-strength of Hoagland solution to allow root recovery from handling stress. The half-strength Hoagland solution was prepared using a modified formulation to avoid Cd2+ precipitation, following the hydroponic protocol described previously [25].

To characterize parental responses and determine an appropriate working Cd concentration, seedlings of both parents were exposed to a Cd concentration gradient (30, 50, 70, and 100 µmol/L) supplied as CdCl2.2.5H2O in the modified half-strength Hoagland solution. Control (CK) plants were maintained in the same nutrient solution without Cd addition. Treatments were arranged in a completely randomized design with three biological replicates. The nutrient solution was renewed every three days to avoid microbial growth [26]. Cd tolerance was evaluated using biomass-derived indices, including the tolerance index (TI) and growth inhibition (GGI), calculated from CK and Cd-treated dry weight at the replicate levels. Visible toxicity symptoms (e.g., chlorosis and leaf discolouration) were recorded photographically (Fig. S1A-B). Whole-plant Cd accumulation was assessed as Cd concentration on a dry-weight basis (roots and shoots combined) and quantified by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Sect. "Cd quantification using ICP-OES").

Based on the parental dose-response results, 70 µmol/L Cd²⁺ was selected as the working concentration for population screening because it produced clear phenotypic differentiation between P1 and P2 while avoiding the severe growth inhibition observed at 100 µmol/L. The two parental lines were crossed to generate F₁ plants. Successful F₁ hybridization was verified using an SSR marker that distinguished the two parental accessions; F₁ plants showing the expected hybrid banding pattern were retained for selfing to generate the F₂ population (Fig. S2A-B; Table S1). A total of 300 F₂ seedlings were screened under 70 µmol/L Cd²⁺ using the same hydroponic system. After 7 days of Cd exposure, surviving plants with sufficient biomass were harvested for phenotypic evaluation, and whole-plant Cd concentration was determined by ICP-OES (Sect. "Cd quantification using ICP-OES"). A total of 296 F₂ individuals yielded valid Cd concentration data and were used for downstream analyses.

Fresh weight (FW) and dry weight (DW) were measured for each accession under CK and Cd treatments at 30, 50, 70, and 100 µmol/L. Cd quantification using ICP-OES

To quantify whole-plant Cd accumulation, roots of stressed seedlings were immersed in 20 mM Na2 EDTA for 15–20 min to remove Cd adhered to the surface of the root. After which the roots were washed thrice with distilled water and finally with de-ionized water [28]. Whole plants (shoot and root combined) were weighed and dried in a 65 ◦C oven for 3 days until reaching a constant weight. The samples were ground into powder using a hammer cyclone mill at 60 Hz frequency for 90 s. For acid digestion, 0.1 g of each powdered sample were weighed in three replicates and put in a 50 mL conical flask. 5 mL of HNO3 were added to each conical flask, and the digestion was performed on a hot block digester. A glass funnel was placed on each of the 50 mL conical flasks, the samples were heated as follows, 120 °C for 60 min, 140 °C for 60 min, and 160 °C for 60 min and the whole digestion process was done in a ventilator.

After the digestion was completed (a clear colourless liquid), the sample was diluted with distilled water to make 50 mL and 10 mL of the diluent was later used to detect the Cd concentration [29]. Cd concentration was measured using an ICP-OES (Avio 220 Max, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at an analytical wavelength of 228.802 nm with four technical readings per sample. A standard curve was generated using Cd standard solution containing 5% HNO3 at 0 mg/L, 5 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 20 mg/L, 30 mg/L, 40 mg/L and 50 mg/L (Fig. S3). Sample analysis followed the Chinese national standard GB 5009.268–2016, issued by National Standard Substances Center of China.

Time-course RNA-seq design and analysis

The tolerant parental line P1 was grown for three weeks and subjected to Cd stress as described in Sect. "Plant material, Cd growing conditions, and experimental design". To characterize Cd-responsive transcriptional dynamics, root (R) and aboveground tissue (L) samples were collected at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. The 0 h CK samples were used as baseline controls, and Cd-treated samples were collected at 24, 48, and 72 h after exposure to 70 µmol/L Cd²⁺. Tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. For each time-course × tissue × treatment combination, three biological replicates were collected and sequenced. RNA-seq libraries were prepared and sequenced by Jisi Huiyuan Biotechnology (Nanjing, China) following standard protocols. Sequencing yield and base-quality metrics for all libraries are summarized in Table S2.

Raw reads were quality-controlled using fastp v0.20.0 with adapter removal, filtering of reads containing more than 5 ambiguous bases (N), and removal of reads in which bases with quality < 5 accounted for more than 50% of the read length (main parameters: -q 5 -n 5). Clean reads were aligned to the dark jute reference genome TC [16] using HISAT2 v2.1.0 with the option --novel-splicesite-outfile, and the resulting SAM files were converted, sorted and indexed as BAM files using samtools v1.9.

Alignment quality, mapping efficiency and coverage of exons and transcripts were assessed using Qualimap v2.2.1 in RNA-seq mode. Transcript assembly and expression quantification were performed with StringTie v2.1.3. For each sample, transcripts were assembled with parameters -f 0.2 -a 30 -c 5 -j 5, and assemblies were merged using the --merge function to obtain an optimized annotation. StringTie was then rerun in quantification mode (-e -p 4) using this annotation to generate gene-level abundance estimates. Read counts were organized using Ballgown (R), and FPKM/TPM values were calculated using in-house Python scripts. Differential expression analysis was performed on raw counts using DESeq2 v1.42.0, with DEGs defined as those with |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and FDR ≤ 0.05. The DEGs were annotated by sequence alignment to the Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Non-Redundant Protein Database (NR), Swiss-Prot (swissprot), and InterPro databases using BLAST (v 2.15.0) [30].

DNA isolation and sequencing

Leaf samples were collected from 5 plants of each parent, P1 and P2. From the F2 population, 50 plants with high whole-plant Cd concentration were combined to construct the high-Cd bulk, and 50 plants with low whole-plant Cd concentration were combined to construct the low-Cd bulk. Genomic DNA was extracted from each bulk using CTAB-based protocol [31]. The concentration and quality of the total genomic DNA were determined using a NanoDrop2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA libraries (350 bp) for Illumina/BGI sequencing were constructed for the bulks and parent. After DNA library construction, sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq XTen/NovaSeq/BGI platform by a commercial service Jisi Huiyuan Biotechnology (Nanjing, China), with 150 bp read lengths. Raw reads (FASTQ) were processed on BMK Cloud platform (www.biocloud.net) to remove adapter contamination and low-quality reads; Q20, Q30, GC content and duplication levels were calculated, and only high-quality clean reads were retained (Table S3).

Read mapping, variant calling, and BSA-seq

Clean reads were aligned to the dark jute reference genome TC [16] using bwa-mem2 v2.2 (mem -t 4 -M), and the alignments were sorted and indexed with samtools v1.9. Mapping statistics, average sequencing depth and genome coverage at ≥ 1×, ≥ 5× and ≥ 10× were calculated from the sorted BAM files; most samples showed high mapping rates, with mapping rates ranging from 87.28% to 99.20%. Genome coverage at ≥ 10× was higher in the F₂ bulks than in the parental samples (Table S4). SNPs and small InDels were called using GATK v3.8 HaplotypeCaller. Variants within 5 bp of an InDel or within 10 bp of adjacent InDels (bcftools vcfutils.pl varFilter -w 5 -W 10), clustered variants (clusterSize = 2, clusterWindowSize = 5), and those failing QUAL < 30, QD < 2.0, MQ < 40 or FS > 60.0 were removed, with other parameters kept at GATK defaults (Table S5).

Based on the mapping results, structural variants (insertions, deletions, inversions and ectopic rearrangements) were detected using Manta with default parameters. For BSA-seq analysis, SNP-index values were calculated for the high- and low-Cd accumulation bulks. The ΔSNP-index was calculated by subtracting the SNP-index of the low-accumulation bulk from that of the high-accumulation bulk. Genome-wide ΔSNP-index values were examined using a sliding-window approach, and candidate regions were defined as intervals exceeding the 99th percentile threshold. The coding genes in the candidate region were functionally annotated using multiple databases: NR (NCBI non-redundant protein sequences) [32], SwissProt (A manually annotated and reviewed protein sequence database) [32], GO (Gene Ontology) [33], KO (KEGG Ortholog database) [34], COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups of protein) [35] using BLAST [36] software.

Integration of BSA-seq and RNA-seq data for candidate-gene prioritization

BSA-seq was used to define genomic intervals associated with segregation for whole-plant Cd accumulation, and time-course RNA-seq in the tolerant parent was used as supportive functional evidence for candidate gene prioritization. Genes located within BSA-seq candidate intervals were extracted from the reference genome annotation. Candidate genes prioritization was based on combined evidence, including genomic position within candidate intervals, proximity to ΔSNP-index peaks, Cd-responsive expression patterns in P1 time-course RNA-seq dataset, and functional annotations related to stress response, transport, detoxification, transcriptional regulation, or metal-associated processes. GO and KEGG annotations were used as supportive functional information rather than as mandatory filtering criteria. Expression patterns consistent with Cd response in the tolerant parent were defined as genes showing significant differential expression in at least one Cd-treatment comparison and/or clear time-dependent expression changes after Cd exposure, particularly in tissues relevant to Cd uptake and stress response. Therefore, the final candidates were prioritized from combined positional, expression, and annotation evidence rather than from a single pathway criterion.

Next, RNA-seq expression data (Sect. "Time-course RNA-seq design and analysis") were then used to refine candidates based on Cd-responsive transcriptional regulation. Differential expression between Cd-treated and CK samples was assessed using DESeq2 (|log₂FC| ≥ 1, FDR ≤ 0.05). Genes were retained as integrated candidates if they were located within the BSA-seq intervals and were significantly differentially expressed in at least one relevant comparison, with expression patterns consistent with Cd response in the tolerant parent. FPKM values were used to summarize expression levels and visualize temporal expression profiles.

qRT-PCR analysis

Gene-specific qPCR primers for the seven candidate genes and the reference gene were designed using PrimerQuest (Integrated DNA Technologies) based on the C. olitorius genome and transcript sequences (Table S6). The C. olitorius PP2A catalytic subunit (CoPP2A/PP2Ac) was used as the internal reference gene because of its stable expression reported in jute [37]. For each time-course × treatment × tissue combination, three biological replicates were analyzed, and each biological sample was run with two technical qPCR replicates.

The RNA samples used for qRT-PCR were the same as those used for transcriptome sequencing and cDNA was synthesized using the full-strand gold reverse transcription kit (AT311, China). For each reaction, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase (Cat. no. 18064-014, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and oligo (dT)₁₂–₁₈ primers (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturers’ instructions. qRT-PCR was performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using SYBR Green chemistry. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method [38].

Statistical analysis

All phenotypic measurements were obtained from three biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.1.1. Differences between treatments or accessions were assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with significance defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***) and p < 0.0001 (****). The distribution of whole-plant Cd concentration in the F₂ population was evaluated using histogram visualization and the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Leaf-colour changes in Fig. S1 were descriptively quantified using ImageJ by measuring mean green-channel intensity from representative leaf regions and normalizing values to the CK value of each accession. Graphs were generated using GraphPad prism, Tbtools, ImageJ, and R.

Results

Parental responses to Cd concentration gradient

Both parental lines exhibited dose-dependent growth suppression under Cd stress, reflected by decreasing TI and increasing GGI as Cd concentration increased. At 30 µmol/L, inhibition was mild and no significant difference was observed between accessions. With increasing Cd concentration, P2 showed a stronger loss of tolerance, with a pronounced decline in TI (Table S7) and increased GGI (Fig. 1). In contrast, P1 maintained higher tolerance across moderate to high Cd levels, showing consistently higher TI and lower GGI. Because 70 µmol/L produced clear phenotypic differentiation between the parents while avoiding the most severe inhibition observed at 100 µmol/L, it was selected as the working concentration for subsequent stress treatment and F₂ population screening. Cd exposure also induced distinct visual symptoms in the two parents (Fig. S1A-B). At 30 µmol/L, both lines displayed leaf chlorosis relative to CK. In P1, chlorosis progressively intensified with increasing Cd and was accompanied by gradual senescence and abscission of older leaves. In P2, red venation in mature leaves was evident and intensified with Cd concentration, together with stronger chlorosis and more extensive leaf loss. At 70 and 100 µmol/L, symptoms severity increased in both lines, but P2 consistently showed more pronounced discolouration and defoliation than P1.

GGI were calculated per biological replicate using CK biomass (dry weight) (Bc) and Cd-treated biomass (dry weight) (Bt). Bars represent mean ± SEM. Asterisks indicate significant differences between accessions at each concentration by two-tailed Student’s t-test: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***) and p < 0.0001 (****)

To provide quantitative support for the visible leaf-color changes, mean green-channel intensity was measured from representative leaf regions in Fig. S1 and normalized to the CK value of each accession. P1 maintained relatively stable green-channel intensity across Cd treatments, whereas P2 showed a marked reduction at higher Cd concentrations, especially at 100 µmol/L. This result supports the visual observation that P2 experienced stronger Cd-induced leaf discoloration than P1(Fig. S4).

Cd accumulation differed between parental lines

Following 7 d Cd exposure, whole-plant Cd concentration (mg/kg DW) increased markedly in both parental lines compared with CK (Fig. 2). In P1, Cd concentration increased from 4.04 mg/kg DW to 328.05 mg/kg DW in Cd treatment, while in P2 it increased from 5.30 mg/kg DW in CK to 584.20 mg/kg DW in Cd treatment. Thus, Cd exposure resulted in ≈ 81-fold and 110-fold increases in P1 and P2, respectively. Under Cd treatment, P2 accumulated significantly more Cd than P1 (1.78-fold higher), confirming clear parental divergence in whole-plant Cd accumulation capacity.

Segregation in the F2 population under Cd stress

At 70 µmol/L Cd, a total of 300 F₂ seedlings were screened for whole-plant Cd accumulation, and valid ICP-OES measurements were obtained for 296 individuals (Table S8). Whole-plant Cd concentration (mg/kg DW) showed a continuous, unimodal distribution spanning a broad range (approximately 25.5-853.9 mg/kg DW), with a central tendency of µ = 338 mg/kg DW and dispersion of σ = 149 mg/kg DW (coefficient of variation ≈ 44%). The fitted normal density curve closely tracked the histogram across the central bins, indicating that Cd accumulation behaves as a quantitative trait rather than a discrete, single-gene class. Notably, the distribution exhibited a slight right tail, with a small number of individuals showing markedly elevated Cd concentrations (> 600 mg/kg DW), suggesting transgressive segregation for high accumulation and providing a clear basis for selecting extreme phenotypes.

Normality was formally assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (W = 0.9812, p = 0.007095). Although the p-value indicates a statistically detectable deviation from perfect normality (which is common for large sample sizes), the W statistic close to 1 and the overall histogram shape support an approximately normal, quantitative inheritance pattern for whole-plant Cd accumulation. The two parental lines differed in their positions within the F₂ distribution, with P1 close to the population mean and P2 shifted toward the higher-accumulation side (Fig. 3), consistent with segregating alleles contributing to variation in Cd accumulation.

Differentially expressed genes in response to Cd stress

To characterize Cd-responsive transcriptional dynamics, time-course RNA-seq was performed in P1 using root (R) and aboveground tissue (L) samples collected at 24, 48, and 72 h after Cd exposure. The 0 h CK samples served as baseline controls for differential expression comparisons with Cd-treated samples at each time point. Pearson correlation analysis showed strong consistency among biological replicates and clear separation by tissue and treatment/time (Fig. 4). Principal component analysis (PCA) further supported the global separation of RNA-seq samples by tissue and treatment/time (Fig. S5A-B). PC1 explained 43.09% of the total expression variation and mainly separated aboveground tissue samples from root samples, indicating that tissue identity was the dominant source of transcriptomic variation. PC2 and PC3 explained 10.50% and 8.27% of the variation, respectively, and captured additional time-dependent transcriptional differences within tissues.

Pearson correlation heatmap of RNA-seq samples across tissues and time points under CK and Cd treatments

Differential expression was assessed using DESeq2 with padj ≤ 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1. In roots, 3,784 DEGs were detected in RCK_vs_R24Cd, 4,167 in RCK_vs_R48Cd, and 4,102 in RCK_vs_R72Cd. In aboveground tissues, 3,465 DEGs were detected in LCK_vs_L24Cd, 2,216 in LCK_vs_L48Cd, and 3,532 in LCK_vs_L72Cd (Table S9). These results indicate that Cd exposure induced substantial transcriptional reprogramming in both roots and aboveground tissues.

Pairwise time-course comparisons further showed that transcriptional responses changed dynamically during Cd exposure. In roots, 1,607 DEGs were detected between 24 h and 48 h, whereas only 64 DEGs were detected between 48 h and 72 h. In aboveground tissues, 579 DEGs were detected between 24 h and 48 h, and 1,160 DEGs were detected between 48 h and 72 h (Table S9). These patterns suggest that the strongest transcriptional changes occurred early in roots, whereas aboveground tissues showed more sustained time-dependent changes.

Overlap analysis indicated both shared and tissue-specific Cd responses. A total of 1,207 DEGs were shared between root and aboveground DEG sets, while 2,623 and 3,318 DEGs were specific to roots and aboveground tissues, respectively (Fig. 5A). Time-course Venn analysis further showed that only a small core set of DEGs was shared across consecutive time transitions under Cd stress, with 10 shared DEGs in roots and 34 shared DEGs in aboveground tissues (Fig. 5B–C). Together, these results suggest that Cd-induced transcriptional responses in dark jute were tissue-specific and strongly dependent on exposure time.

Functional classification and pathway annotation of Cd-responsive DEGs

To summarize the biological functions associated with the Cd-responsive gene set, GO classification and KEGG pathway annotation were performed using the DIFF gene set, with all annotated genes used as the background reference (Figs. 6 and 7A-B). Because the GO results are presented as classification summaries and the KEGG pathway results showed q-values above the conventional FDR significance threshold, these analyses were interpreted as functional classification and pathway-level annotation rather than definitive enrichment evidence. GO classification assigned the DIFF genes to three major GO categories: cellular component, molecular function, and biological process (Fig. 6). In the cellular component category, many genes were assigned to cell, cell part, organelle, membrane, membrane part, and organelle part. In the molecular function category, binding and catalytic activity represented the dominant functional classes, followed by transcription regulator activity, transporter activity, molecular function regulator, and molecular transducer activity. In the biological process category, cellular process, metabolic process, biological regulation, response to stimulus, developmental process, localization, and cellular component organization or biogenesis were the major represented terms. Notably, stress-related categories, including response to stimulus, detoxification, transporter activity, antioxidant activity, and oxidoreductase-related functions, were also represented, suggesting that Cd-responsive genes may participate in stress perception, transport, detoxification, and redox regulation.

KEGG pathway classification further showed that the DIFF genes were mainly associated with metabolism, genetic information processing, environmental information processing, and cellular processes (Fig. 7A). The most represented KEGG categories included translation, carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, folding, sorting and degradation, signal transduction, nucleotide metabolism, and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. Pathway-level annotation also identified genes associated with sulfur metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, glutathione-related metabolic processes, ABC transporter-related pathways, RNA transport, proteasome, carbon fixation, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (Fig. 7B). These pathways are relevant to Cd-stress responses because sulfur-containing compounds, amino acid metabolism, transport processes, protein turnover, and redox-related pathways are commonly involved in heavy-metal detoxification and stress adaptation. Overall, the GO and KEGG results provide functional context for the Cd-responsive gene set and support the involvement of transport, detoxification, metabolism, and stress-response processes in dark jute under Cd exposure.

Identification of candidate intervals associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation by BSA-seq

To map genomic regions associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation, BSA-seq was performed on the two parental lines (P1 and P2) and two F₂ DNA bulks representing extreme phenotypes, namely the high-Cd-accumulation bulk and low-Cd-accumulation bulk. Pairwise SNP differences among parents and the bulks showed clear genetic divergence between the extreme pools (189,967 SNPs) and between each bulk and its corresponding parent (Table S10). After filtering, 625,354 high-quality SNPs were retained for downstream SNP-index analysis (Table S11). Genome-wide ΔSNP-index analysis identified discrete peaks exceeding the 99th percentile threshold (ΔSNP-index cutoff = 0.02). These peaks defined five candidate intervals across three chromosomes (Fig. 8; Table S11). In Fig. 8, the red dashed line indicates the 99th percentile threshold used to define candidate regions, and the five candidate intervals are indicated by red arrows. The candidate intervals included three regions on Chromosome 3 (GWHBQTL00000003), one region on Chromosome 4 (GWHBQTL00000004), and one region on Chromosome 7 (GWHBQTL00000007), spanning a total of 7.13 Mb and containing 662 annotated genes in the reference genome (Table S11). These candidate intervals were used for subsequent candidate gene prioritization and integration with transcriptomic data.

These intervals were considered candidate genomic regions associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation in dark jute

Integrative prioritization of candidate genes using BSA-seq and time-course RNA-seq

BSA-seq based on ΔSNP-index analysis identified five candidate intervals associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation across Chromosomes 3, 4, and 7. These intervals spanned 7.13 Mb and contained 662 annotated genes in the reference genome (Table S11; Fig. 8). To prioritize candidate genes within these intervals, we integrated positional evidence from BSA-seq with Cd-responsive expression profiles from the time-course RNA-seq dataset. All annotated genes located within the candidate intervals were first considered. Candidate-gene prioritization was then performed using combined evidence, including genomic position within the BSA-seq intervals, proximity to ΔSNP-index peaks, functional annotation, and expression response under Cd treatment. Functional annotation was used as supportive information rather than as a strict filtering criterion, so genes were not selected solely because they belonged to predefined HM-related pathways.

RNA-seq data from the tolerant parent were then used to evaluate whether interval genes showed Cd-responsive expression across 0, 24, 48, and 72 h in roots (R) and aboveground tissues (L). Genes were prioritized when they showed significant differential expression in at least one Cd-treatment comparison and/or clear time-dependent expression changes after Cd exposure, particularly in tissues relevant to Cd uptake and stress response. Using these combined criteria, seven genes were prioritized as candidates associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation and Cd-responsive adaptation: COS03g_00658, COS03g_00659, COS03g_03287, COS03g_03316, COS03g_03200, COS03g_03180, and COS07g_05235 (Table S12; Fig. 9). These genes included annotations related to transport, transcriptional regulation, stress response, and metal-associated processes.

Time-course RNA-seq expression profiles (FPKM) of prioritized candidate genes in aboveground tissues (L) and roots (R) under Cd stress. Expression levels (FPKM) are shown for Cd-treated samples at 24, 48, and 72 h, with 0 h CK samples used as baseline controls. Points represent mean values across biological replicates and error bars indicate ± SE

Time-course expression profiles provided additional support for tissue-dependent Cd responses among the prioritized candidates (Fig. 9). COS03g_00658 and COS03g_00659 showed consistently higher expression in roots than in aboveground tissues during the Cd exposure period, suggesting a root-associated response. COS03g_03316 also showed higher root expression and dynamic changes after Cd exposure. COS03g_03200, annotated as a NAC-domain transcription factor-like gene, showed Cd-responsive expression patterns consistent with a possible regulatory role. In contrast, COS07g_05235 showed comparatively modest temporal variation between tissues. These expression patterns supported the prioritization of the seven genes, although functional validation is still required to confirm their roles. To provide additional functional context, conserved protein features were examined using domain-based annotations. Domain architecture analysis supported the presence of protein features consistent with stress-response, regulatory, and transport-related functions, including PLAC8 domains in COS03g_00658 and COS03g_00659, ABC transporter-associated features in COS03g_03287 and COS03g_03316, and a NAM/NAC-related domain in COS03g_03200 (Fig. S6). Overall, the combined BSA-seq, RNA-seq, annotation, and domain evidence identified these seven genes as prioritized candidates for further validation and functional characterization.

qRT-PCR validation of candidate gene expression

qRT-PCR was used to validate expression patterns for the prioritized candidate genes across the Cd exposure time course. Overall, qRT-PCR trends were consistent with the RNA-seq expression directionality for the tested genes, supporting the reliability of the transcriptomic dataset (Fig. 10A-G). Notably, COS03g_00658 showed stronger induction in P1 than in P2 at 24 h and 72 h, whereas COS03g_00659 displayed higher expression in P2 across Cd-stress time points. The ABC transporter candidate COS03g_03316 showed consistently higher expression in P1 than in P2 across the time course. Other candidates showed weaker or more variable differences between accessions.

Discussion

This study used an integrative genomic and transcriptomic approach to investigate whole-plant Cd accumulation and Cd tolerance-associated responses in dark jute. The continuous distribution of Cd concentration in the F₂ population (Fig. 3) suggests that Cd accumulation is a quantitative trait governed by multiple loci rather than a single major gene, consistent with reports in other crop species [9, 39]. The identification of five candidate intervals spanning 7.13 Mb across Chromosomes 3, 4, and 7 and containing 662 annotated genes (Table S11) underscores the complex architecture of Cd accumulation and provides a defined genomic framework for downstream functional analysis.

Previous studies have begun to reveal the genetic and physiological basis of HM responses in jute. A machine-learning phenotyping and GWAS study of Cd tolerance and absorption in jute showed that Cd-related traits are genetically complex and influenced by multiple loci [17]. Consistent with this, the continuous F₂ distribution and the five candidate intervals detected in the present study support the quantitative nature of whole-plant Cd accumulation in dark jute. Compared with previous GWAS-based work, our study used an F₂ extreme-bulk BSA-seq strategy combined with time-course RNA-seq, enabling candidate-region mapping and expression-supported candidate-gene prioritization. Pb accumulation and tolerance in dark jute have also been investigated, with candidate mechanisms related to HM stress responses reported [18]. Together, these studies suggest that dark jute may possess a broader HM-response framework involving transport, redox regulation, detoxification, and stress signaling. However, the genes prioritized here should be interpreted as Cd-associated candidates because Pb and Cd differ in mobility, transport behaviour and detoxification mechanisms.

The GO classification and KEGG pathway annotation results provided functional context for the Cd-responsive gene set. The GO classification assigned Cd-responsive genes to major cellular component, molecular function, and biological process categories, including cell part, organelle, membrane, binding, catalytic activity, transcription regulator activity, transporter activity, cellular process, metabolic process, biological regulation, response to stimulus, localization, and detoxification-related processes (Fig. 6). These classifications suggest that Cd-responsive transcriptional changes in dark jute involve broad cellular regulation, metabolic adjustment, stress response, transport, and detoxification processes.

KEGG pathway annotation further showed that the Cd-responsive gene set was mainly associated with metabolism, genetic information processing, environmental information processing, and cellular processes (Fig. 7A-B). Several annotated pathways were biologically relevant to Cd-stress responses, including sulfur metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, glutathione-related metabolism, transporter-associated pathways, protein turnover, and amino acid metabolism. These pathways may contribute to Cd-stress adaptation because sulfur-containing compounds and glutathione are involved in Cd chelation, ROS buffering, and the formation of Cd-phytochelatin complexes that facilitate vacuolar sequestration in plants [40, 41]. Cd-responsive glutathione S-transferase expression has also been reported under Cd exposure, further supporting the role of glutathione-associated detoxification systems in metal stress responses [42].

Transporter-related annotations also provided useful biological context for the prioritized candidate genes. Membrane transporters, particularly members of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) family, are key regulators of HM efflux and compartmentalization [39, 43, 44]. In rice, ABCG36 mediates Cd extrusion from root cells [45], while ABCC-type transporters contribute to vacuolar sequestration of Cd-glutathione complexes in crop species [46]. Within our candidate intervals, COS03g_03287 and COS03g_03316 encode ABC transporter–like proteins. The higher root expression of COS03g_03316 (Fig. 10B) suggests that it may be involved in root Cd transport, redistribution, or sequestration, although functional validation will be required to confirm its role.

Two additional candidates, COS03g_00658 and COS03g_00659, contain PLAC8 motifs characteristic of PCR family proteins (Fig. S6). PCR/PLAC8 proteins have been shown to regulate Cd tolerance and accumulation in several species [47]. Overexpression of OsPCR1 and OsPCR3 enhances Cd tolerance and reduces shoot Cd accumulation in rice [48], while AtPCR2 contributes to Cd resistance in Arabidopsis thaliana [49]. In the present study, COS03g_00658 and COS03g_00659 showed root-associated expression patterns under Cd stress. qRT-PCR analysis further indicated different expression patterns between the two parental accessions, with COS03g_00658 showing stronger induction in P1 and COS03g_00659 showing relatively higher expression in P2 (Fig. 10F-G). These results suggest that the two PLAC8-domain candidates may have distinct roles in Cd-tolerance and support their prioritization for future functional analysis.

The NAC transcription factor-like gene COS03g_03200 showed relatively high expression under Cd stress. NAC family proteins are well-established regulators of abiotic stress responses, coordinating antioxidant defenses, cell wall modification, and downstream detoxification pathways [50, 51]. The reported role of OsNAC25 in regulating potassium homeostasis in rice further supports the involvement of NAC proteins in stress-related ion-balance regulation [52]. Thus, COS03g_03200 may act as an upstream regulator of Cd-stress adaptation in dark jute, although functional validation is required to confirm its role.

COS03g_03180, predicted to function extracellularly, displayed higher expression in roots (Fig. 10E). Extracellular or cell wall-associated proteins can contribute to Cd immobilization in the apoplast, thereby limiting symplastic entry [53]. This suggests that COS03g_03180 may contribute to Cd immobilization at the root interface and may influence whole-plant Cd accumulation. In contrast, COS07g_05235 showed no strong tissue bias (Fig. 10D), suggesting that it may function in a broader stress-response process rather than Cd-specific detoxification pathway.

Root qRT-PCR validation supported several RNA-seq trends, particularly for COS03g_00658, COS03g_00659, and COS03g_03316. The concordance between transcriptome data and targeted validation strengthens confidence in these genes as prioritized candidates. However, qRT-PCR provides expression validation rather than functional validation. Expression changes indicate that these genes respond to Cd stress, but they do not prove that the genes are causal determinants of Cd accumulation or tolerance. In addition, not all genes within the BSA intervals showed strong expression differences, which is expected for quantitative traits such as Cd accumulation. Candidate intervals often contain genes with modest, tissue-specific, or context-dependent effects that may not exhibit pronounced transcriptional variation under a single experimental condition.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting this study. First, Cd accumulation was evaluated as whole-plant Cd concentration, whereas root- and shoot-specific Cd accumulation, translocation factor, bioconcentration factor, and total Cd uptake were not separately assessed. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as an integrated estimate of whole-plant Cd accumulation rather than a complete description of Cd uptake, transport, and partitioning among organs.

Second, the population screening was conducted under hydroponic conditions using a defined Cd concentration. This approach enabled controlled phenotyping and clear differentiation among accessions, but it may not fully represent Cd availability, soil chemistry, rhizosphere interactions, or environmental variation under field conditions. Validation under Cd-contaminated soil and multi-environment conditions will therefore be necessary before these findings can be applied to phytoremediation-oriented improvement.

Third, time-course RNA-seq was performed in the tolerant, low-accumulating parent, whereas transcriptome profiling of the sensitive, high-accumulating parent was not included. As a result, the RNA-seq data provide evidence for Cd-responsive expression in the tolerant parent but cannot fully resolve accession-specific transcriptional differences between the two parents. Future comparative transcriptome analysis of P1 and P2 under the same Cd-treatment conditions would help identify tolerance-associated and sensitivity-associated regulatory differences.

Fourth, the candidate intervals were identified using ΔSNP-index analysis. Although the use of extreme bulks and high-quality SNPs supports the reliability of the mapped intervals, additional BSA-seq approaches such as Euclidean distance or G′ analysis would further improve confidence in candidate-region detection and help reduce potential false-positive intervals. Therefore, the present intervals should be interpreted as candidate genomic regions requiring further validation.

Finally, the seven prioritized genes were identified based on positional evidence, Cd-responsive expression patterns, functional annotation, and qRT-PCR validation, but their biological functions remain to be experimentally confirmed. Detailed causal variant information, including exact functional SNP positions, amino acid changes, promoter variants, or haplotypes, was also not available for all candidate genes. Future studies using fine mapping, allelic variation analysis, gene editing, overexpression or complementation analysis, subcellular localization, transporter assays, yeast Cd-tolerance assays, and field-based phenotyping will be important to verify the roles of these candidate genes in Cd accumulation and tolerance.

Taken together, the integration of BSA-seq and time-course RNA-seq identified candidate genomic regions and prioritized genes associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation and Cd-tolerance in dark jute. Among the prioritized genes, COS03g_00658, COS03g_00659, and COS03g_03316 represent particularly promising candidates for further study because they combine positional evidence, Cd-responsive expression, and functional annotations related to HM tolerance or transport. These findings provide a useful genetic and transcriptomic framework for future functional validation, marker development, and improvement of Cd-related traits in dark jute.

Conclusion

This study integrated BSA-seq, time-course RNA-seq, Cd phenotyping, and qRT-PCR validation to investigate whole-plant Cd accumulation and tolerance-associated responses in dark jute. Using two contrasting parental accessions and an F₂ population screened under Cd stress, BSA-seq identified five candidate genomic intervals on Chromosomes 3, 4, and 7, spanning 7.13 Mb and containing 662 annotated genes.

Time-course RNA-seq of the tolerant parent revealed tissue- and time-dependent transcriptional responses to Cd stress. By combining mapped intervals with Cd-responsive expression patterns, functional annotations, and domain information, seven candidate genes were prioritized, including PLAC8-domain/PCR-family genes, ABC transporter-like genes, and a NAC-domain-related gene.

Overall, these findings provide candidate genomic regions and prioritized genes potentially associated with whole-plant Cd accumulation and tolerance in dark jute. This study establishes a useful genetic and transcriptomic foundation for future fine mapping, functional validation, marker development, and improvement of Cd-related traits in this high-biomass fiber crop.
