Phenomenology in Primary Care: Integrating Phenomenological Insight Into Clinical Practice and Research
Authors: Yuya Yokota, Daisuke Son, Tetsuya Sakakibara, Yumi Nishimura, Shin‐ichi Taniguchi
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

In primary care, the importance of holistic medicine is widely acknowledged. Clinicians strive for a comprehensive understanding that extends beyond a patient's disease to encompass their personal and social contexts [1]. However, a fundamental question remains: how can we more deeply understand the patient's illness experience, which cannot be fully captured by objective data and frameworks alone?

Phenomenology is a philosophy that seeks to clarify the structure of meaningful experience by returning to phenomena themselves—things as they are lived and imbued with meaning [2]. While this approach has yielded significant insights within fields such as nursing, its principles have the potential to deepen our understanding of patients' subjective experiences and enhance the quality of holistic medicine in primary care. This is because primary care clinicians frequently encounter undifferentiated and complex health problems, requiring them to provide tailored care that accounts for the patient's subjective experience and context [3].

To explore this potential, we convened an online symposium at the 16th Annual Conference of the Japan Primary Care Association. The symposium brought together experts in phenomenology and primary care clinicians to integrate phenomenological insight into clinical practice and research.

The philosopher (author T.S.), a specialist in phenomenology, initiated the discussion by outlining its fundamental concepts. A key concept presented was epoché, the deliberate “bracketing” of the natural scientific attitude—the objective, quantitative viewpoint that clinicians often unconsciously adopt—to return to the patient's “natural attitude” within their everyday life [4]. The presentation also demonstrated how concepts such as care, embodiment, and intersubjectivity offer rich implications in a medical context.

Subsequently, the nursing scholar (author Y.N.) provided a concrete example of a phenomenological approach in nursing practice and research. A narrative analysis of an experienced nurse revealed that care is not a unidirectional intervention but a relational process in which the patient and nurse mutually influence each other.

Next, two primary care physicians reported on their clinical applications. One physician (author Y.Y.) presented a case in which he bracketed the conventional medical goal of identifying a cause and normalizing values for a patient with unexplained laboratory abnormalities [5]. Instead, he reframed the approach by asking, “What constitutes a good life for this patient?” Another physician (author D.S.) proposed a model for managing medically unexplained symptoms by understanding the patient as a “being‐in‐the‐world” and facilitating the co‐creation of new meaning through dialogue [6].

Finally, the symposium culminated in a lively discussion with the audience, which explored practical questions such as how epoché can be enacted in a clinical setting.

This symposium underscored the significant potential of phenomenology for both practice and research in primary care. First, it can foster qualitative improvements in clinical practice by prompting clinicians to re‐examine their assumptions. This stance provides new perspectives on complex cases where biomedical models are insufficient. Second, phenomenology provides a sophisticated methodology for clinical research, enabling the articulation of the tacit knowledge and rich, non‐quantifiable experiences inherent in primary care.

However, a significant challenge remains: the barrier posed by complex philosophical terminology. A concerted effort is required to translate key concepts such as “epoché” and “being‐in‐the‐world” into a common language accessible to clinicians. This symposium represents a crucial first step, and continued dialogue between clinicians and philosophy scholars is essential for progress.
