Opioid-Sparing Effect of Celiac Plexus Neurolysis in Palliative Care Patients with Upper Abdominal Cancer: A Single-Center Retrospective Case Series of Thirteen Patients
Authors: Dong Hyuck Kim, Yun-A Kim, Tae Hyun Ha, Sang Gyu Kwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWhether celiac plexus neurolysis (CPN) retains opioid-sparing efficacy in palliative care patients receiving modern extended-release opioids remains uncertain after a recent randomized trial that reported no benefit. We describe fluoroscopy-guided CPN in such patients.MethodsThirteen consecutive palliative care patients with unresectable or metastatic upper abdominal cancer (seven pancreatic, five hepatobiliary, one gastric) on strong opioid therapy underwent posterior fluoroscopy-guided CPN with absolute ethanol after a diagnostic lidocaine block. Daily oral morphine equivalent (OME) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores were recorded at baseline and 1, 2, and 4 weeks. Pain response was defined a priori as ≥30% NRS reduction.ResultsMedian age was 67 years, baseline median OME 86.5 mg/day (range, 15~215), and median NRS 6. Median OME decreased to 72, 60, and 57.5 mg/day at 1, 2, and 4 weeks (median reductions 46.5%, 26.7%, 43.5%; all P≤0.002), and median NRS improved to 3, 4, and 4 (all P≤0.001). A pain response was achieved in 8/13 (61.5%) at 1 week, 7/13 (53.8%) at 2 weeks, and 5/12 (41.7%) at 4 weeks. Opioid-related adverse events declined numerically (nausea, 7/13→3/12; constipation, 4→2; somnolence, 2→0; all McNemar P>0.10). No procedural complication occurred (0/13).ConclusionCPN was followed by reduction in opioid use and pain scores, with resolution of opioid-related symptoms and no complications. Although limited by the small sample size, these findings suggest a potential role for CPN in palliative care patients receiving extended-release opioids and warrant prospective investigation.

INTRODUCTION

Visceral pain due to neoplastic invasion of the celiac plexus is one of the most debilitating symptoms of advanced upper abdominal malignancy. More than 80% of patients with pancreatic cancer experience moderate to severe pain, and a substantial proportion of patients with hepatobiliary or gastric cancer share similar pain trajectory [1,2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) analgesic ladder remains foundational [3]; however, escalating systemic opioid therapy is frequently complicated by constipation, nausea, sedation, and opioid-induced hyperalgesia, which are adverse effects that constrain dose escalation precisely when the analgesic demand is greatest [4].

A celiac plexus block (CPB) interrupts visceral nociceptive transmission in the celiac ganglion, providing targeted analgesia independent of the systemic opioid dose [5]. Multiple randomized trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated that celiac plexus neurolysis (CPN) reduces pain scores and opioid consumption compared to analgesic therapy alone in pancreatic cancer [6-9]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 controlled trials by Pacheco-Feijoo et al. [10] similarly concluded that CPN is safe, with only mild and transient adverse effects, and that—although pooled pain reduction did not consistently exceed pharmacotherapy alone—individual trials repeatedly documented opioid-sparing in upper abdominal cancers. The Korean tertiary center experience paralleled these findings. Byeon et al. [11] reported that fluoroscopy-guided CPN in 51 terminal cancer inpatients produced a significant reduction in Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) scores at one week with only mild and self-limited adverse events. Kwon et al. [12] in the largest Korean cohort to date (n=513), identified celiac plexus metastasis, absence of diabetes mellitus, and absence of prior upper abdominal surgery as independent predictors of a successful response to neurolytic celiac plexus block. However, this evidence base has been challenged by Kanno et al. [13], who reported that endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided CPN did not significantly reduce opioid consumption or pain scores compared to medication alone in patients on extended-release oxycodone or transdermal fentanyl. Although influential, the Kanno trial tested only one technique (EUS-guided), enrolled a single tumor type (pancreatic cancer) under oncology rather than palliative care management, and did not assess opioid-related symptom burden as an outcome. These limitations constrain how broadly its negative finding should be applied.

These three gaps require attention. First, to our knowledge, no published series has reported outcomes after fluoroscopy-guided posterior CPN, the technique most accessible to non-endoscopic pain physicians and most appropriate for frail inpatients enrolled in a formal palliative care service. Second, opioid-related adverse effects, which are often the dominant determinants of quality of life and drivers of opioid de-escalation in palliative practice, have rarely been assessed as outcomes. Third, contemporary evidence on CPN performed before opioid escalation with fully exhausted tolerability is essentially absent despite repeated calls in the palliative care literature to reposition interventional analgesia as an early option rather than a salvage procedure.

We report a single-center retrospective case series of 13 consecutive palliative care patients with advanced upper abdominal cancer who underwent fluoroscopy-guided CPN while receiving strong opioid therapy. We characterized opioid consumption, pain trajectories, opioid-related adverse events, and procedural safety over a four-week window.

METHODS

This retrospective single-center case series included 13 consecutive adult palliative care patients with histologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic upper abdominal cancer (pancreatic adenocarcinoma, hepatobiliary, or gastric) who underwent celiac plexus neurolysis (CPN) at our tertiary center between March 2025 and April 2026. All had been referred to palliative care for symptom management, had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 1 to 3, and were receiving strong opioid therapy (extended-release oxycodone, transdermal or transmucosal fentanyl) with persistent pain (NRS ≥4). During the same period, four additional patients underwent diagnostic celiac plexus block (CPB) but did not proceed to CPN (two had sufficient analgesia from CPB alone, one died before scheduled CPN, and one had no analgesic response) and were excluded. Retrospective collection of de-identified data was approved with a waiver of informed consent by our Institutional Review Board (approval number 2026-05-037).

All 13 patients underwent a two-stage fluoroscopy-guided procedure: diagnostic CPB followed by definitive CPN on a separate day. Via a posterior percutaneous approach, the needle tip was advanced to the anterior aspect of the upper third of the L1 vertebral body; position was confirmed on anteroposterior and lateral views, and iodinated contrast verified prevertebral spread and excluded intravascular placement. Blocks were performed unilaterally on the side of predominant pain. Diagnostic CPB used 15~20 mL of lidocaine; responders without intolerable side effects proceeded to CPN at the same site using 15~20 mL of 99% absolute ethanol. Procedures were performed by board-certified pain physicians under standard monitoring with intravenous preloading to mitigate postblock hypotension.

Daily opioid consumption was recorded as oral morphine equivalents (OME, mg/day) at baseline (day of CPN) and 1, 2, and 4 weeks. Standard equianalgesic ratios were used (oral oxycodone-to-morphine 1:1.5; transdermal fentanyl 25 µg/h ≈ oral morphine 60 mg/day; transmucosal fentanyl via parenteral fentanyl, 100 µg ≈ oral morphine 4 mg). Co-analgesics (gabapentinoids, corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acetaminophen) and rescue doses were not systematically recorded and were excluded from the OME calculation, a limitation revisited in the Discussion. Patient-reported NRS (0~10) was collected at the same time points, with a clinically meaningful response defined a priori as ≥30% NRS reduction [14]. Opioid adjustments were at the palliative care physicians’ discretion. Adverse events were graded by Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0, and opioid-related events (constipation, nausea/vomiting, somnolence) were tabulated as the proportion of patients with grade ≥2 at each time point. The 4-week endpoint matched the primary endpoint of the Kanno et al. trial [13] and the time points most commonly reported in CPN trials [7,9].

This was a descriptive case series, and all inferential analyses were exploratory. Continuous variables were reported as medians with ranges and means with standard deviations. Paired comparisons of the OME and NRS scores at each follow-up were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Paired changes in the proportion of patients with grade ≥2 opioid-related adverse events between baseline and 4 weeks were assessed with the exact McNemar test. Categorical proportions are reported with 95% Wilson score confidence intervals (CIs). Two-sided P-values were descriptive, no primary endpoint was prespecified, and no multiple-comparison adjustment was applied. One patient who was transferred to another hospital before the 4-week assessment was included in the available case analysis at each completed time point. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Individual patient data are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 67 years (range, 49~83); 9 patients (69%) were male. Primary diagnoses were pancreatic adenocarcinoma (7), hepatobiliary cancer (5), and gastric cancer (1), with metastatic disease in 10 (77%). The baseline median OME was 86.5 mg/day (range, 15~215) and the baseline median NRS was 6 (range, 4~8).

The outcomes at each time point are summarized in Table 2. Median daily OME decreased from 86.5 mg/day at baseline to 72, 60, and 57.5 mg/day at 1, 2, and 4 weeks, with median per-patient reductions of 46.5%, 26.7%, and 43.5%, respectively. The median NRS score improved from 6 at baseline to 3, 4, and 4, respectively. In exploratory Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against baseline, two-sided P-values were 0.001 (1 week, N=13), 0.002 (2 weeks, N=13), and 0.001 (4 weeks, N=12) for OME, and 0.001, 0.001, and <0.001 for NRS; given the absence of a control group, these P-values describe within-patient change rather than causal effect. Pain response (≥30% NRS reduction) was achieved by 8 of 13 patients at 1 week (61.5%; 95% CI 35.5~82.3%), 7 of 13 at 2 weeks (53.8%; 95% CI 29.1~76.8%), and 5 of 12 at 4 weeks (41.7%; 95% CI 19.3~68.0%); the wide intervals reflect the small sample size.

Response patterns varied. A sustained 4-week response was most consistent in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma with ECOG ≤2 (Patients 1, 3, 10, and 12). Patient 5 (metastatic hepatobiliary cancer) showed paradoxical worsening (NRS 6 to 7; OME 15 to 30 mg/day at 2 weeks) and was transferred before the 4-week assessment. Two patients with high baseline opioid burden (Patients 9 and 13) showed an early opioid-sparing response that attenuated by 4 weeks, whereas Patients 2, 6, and 11 achieved substantial OME reduction without meeting the 30% NRS threshold, indicating that CPN may enable opioid de-escalation even when the pain-response criterion is unmet.

Opioid-related adverse events (CTCAE grade ≥2) at baseline were nausea/vomiting in 7 of 13 patients (54%), constipation in 4 (31%), and somnolence in 2 (15%). At 4 weeks, these values had decreased numerically to 3 of 12 evaluable patients (25%), 2 of 12 patients (17%), and 0 of 12 patients (0%). Exact McNemar tests comparing the baseline with the 4-week status did not reach significance for any event (constipation, P=0.69; nausea, P=0.29; somnolence, P=0.50), reflecting both the limited sample size and improvement in some patients being partially offset by new-onset events in others. No procedure-related complications were observed (0/13; 95% CI 0~24.7%); specifically, no postprocedural hypotension requiring intervention, transient diarrhea, persistent injection-site back pain, or major neurovascular events were recorded.

DISCUSSION

In this single-center retrospective case series of 13 palliative care patients on strong opioid therapy for advanced upper abdominal cancer pain, fluoroscopy-guided CPN was followed by within-patient reductions in opioid consumption and pain scores from baseline through 4 weeks (exploratory Wilcoxon P≤0.002 at all time points), a pain response in approximately half of evaluable patients at 4 weeks, numerical declines in opioid-related nausea, constipation, and somnolence, and no procedural complications. The magnitude and trajectory of these effects are consistent with classical descriptions of celiac plexus blockade duration [5] and with pooled estimates of the opioid-sparing effect from earlier randomized trials [6-9]. These findings support the hypothesis—rather than establish—that CPN deserves consideration as part of a quality-of-life-preserving strategy in palliative care, rather than being reserved as a last-resort option for analgesic-refractory pain.

These observations are most relevant to the question raised by Kanno et al. [13]: Does CPN retain its clinical value when patients are managed with modern extended-release opioids? Our findings differ in three respects, which may help interpret the discrepancy. First, our procedure was a posterior fluoroscopy-guided CPN preceded by a diagnostic lidocaine block, an approach that may help identify likely responders before neurolysis, in contrast to EUS-guided neurolysis without the pre-selection used by Kanno et al. Second, our cohort was already on strong opioid therapy at the time of CPN (median baseline OME 86.5 mg/day, range 15~215), reflecting an opioid-tolerant population in which escalation had often reached a side effect-limited ceiling rather than an analgesic ceiling alone. Third, our outcome battery explicitly captured opioid-related adverse events. The parallel decline of these events with opioid dose suggests that the clinical benefit in this population is best assessed by a composite of opioid burden, pain, and tolerability, rather than pain scores alone. Although observational data have raised concerns about the association between CPN and decreased survival in pancreatic cancer [15], confounding by indication cannot be excluded; nonetheless, this consideration warrants attention when weighing neurolysis in patients with limited life expectancy. Taken together, the negative Kanno findings should not be generalized to blanket retreat from CPN in palliative care, although confirmation in an adequately powered prospective trial is required.

This study addresses three underexplored areas. First, evidence from patients in formal palliative care services—rather than the oncology and gastroenterology cohorts that dominate published trials—is sparse. Second, outcome data on the posterior fluoroscopy-guided approach used here, accessible to non-endoscopic pain physicians, remain limited relative to EUS-guided CPN [16,17]. Third, opioid-dose and pain-score reductions did not always co-vary: several patients achieved substantial OME reduction without meeting the 30% NRS threshold (Patients 2, 6, and 11) [18], suggesting that pain scores alone may underestimate CPN benefit and supporting composite outcomes. The hepatobiliary cases (Patients 2, 5, and 11) point to pain afferents beyond the celiac plexus [19], so pre-procedural assessment of disease extent and pain phenotype may help identify likely responders.

Anatomical and host factors also modulate CPN response. In the largest Korean NCPB analysis (n=513), Kwon et al. identified celiac plexus metastasis, absence of diabetes, and absence of prior upper abdominal surgery as independent predictors of success [12]; although our sample precludes formal regression, these factors plausibly explain part of the heterogeneity observed, including the paradoxical worsening in Patient 5 and the attenuated 4-week response in high-opioid-burden patients (Patients 9 and 13). Consistent with our patient-level findings, the systematic review of 13 RCTs by Pacheco-Feijoo et al. [10] reported that pooled pain-score reduction did not consistently exceed pharmacotherapy despite opioid-sparing and overall safety, again favoring composite endpoints.

Clinically, these findings reframe CPN as a quality-of-life-preserving option to consider once opioid therapy begins to compromise tolerability or function, rather than as a last resort; the parallel decline in opioid-related symptoms is arguably as relevant as pain reduction near the end of life, and the absence of complications in this frail cohort (median age 67; ECOG 1~3) supports its feasibility in palliative inpatients. Consistent with our experience, Byeon et al. [11] reported a significant NRS reduction without a significant fall in morphine-equivalent daily dose in 51 terminal cancer patients, attributing the dissociation to side effect-limited titration ceilings, and noted that referral is frequently delayed by interdepartmental gaps and patient-level contraindications such as coagulopathy and performance deterioration. Given the prolonged analgesia of neurolysis (partial-to-complete relief beyond 12 weeks in approximately 90% [7]), early rather than salvage CPN—performed before the side-effect ceiling and before disease progression disrupts injectate spread—is biologically coherent and warrants testing in an adequately powered prospective trial.

This study has clear limitations. The sample size was small (N=13), the design was retrospective and uncontrolled, and outcomes were assessed by treating clinicians who were not blinded to the CPN status; therefore, opioid dose decisions were not independent of the intervention. Coanalgesic use and rescue doses were not systematically captured, which is an additional source of imprecision in attributing the OME trajectory to the CPN. Quality of life and functional outcomes were not assessed using validated patient-reported outcome measures (e.g., EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL, ESAS, and IPOS); future studies should incorporate such instruments. The absence of a contemporaneous control group means that the observed within-patient changes cannot be attributed to CPN with confidence. Secular trends, regression to the mean, and concurrent palliative care interventions could each contribute. Multiple paired comparisons were performed without adjustment, and McNemar comparisons of opioid-related adverse events were severely underpowered; a non-significant P-value reflects the inability of an N=13 sample to detect a plausible effect rather than its absence.

A further limitation was the short follow-up. The 4-week window is shorter than the typical analgesic duration of CPN: Eisenberg et al. reported partial-to-complete relief in approximately 90% of patients beyond 12 weeks [7], Mercadante’s trial described an effect persisting until death [6], and Kanno et al. found the strongest between-group separation at 3 months [13]. With a mean postprocedural survival of 66 days in Korean palliative care [11], most recipients have a life expectancy compatible with 12-week benefit. A 4-week window therefore cannot establish whether the observed opioid-sparing and adverse-event resolution are sustained or whether re-escalation occurs. Future studies should pre-specify follow-up of at least 12 weeks, use validated patient-reported outcomes, and quantify time-to-opioid re-escalation as a clinically meaningful endpoint.

Within these limitations, this series should be read as hypothesis-generating: the consistent within-patient trajectory of opioid-sparing, pain reduction, and adverse-event resolution in palliative care patients on extended-release strong opioids is sufficient to justify, but not substitute for, a prospective controlled evaluation. A randomized trial comparing CPN plus standard pharmacotherapy with pharmacotherapy alone in palliative care patients receiving extended-release strong opioids, using a composite primary endpoint of pain response, opioid reduction, and resolution of opioid-related symptoms, supplemented by validated patient-reported outcome measures, is most likely to clarify the modern role of the celiac plexus block in cancer pain management.
