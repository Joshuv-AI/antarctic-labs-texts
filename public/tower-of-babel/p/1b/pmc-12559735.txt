LAMP using distance based paper device for quantitative detection of ESBL producing Escherichia coli in UTI patients
Authors: Natkrittaya Saengsawang, Panthita Ruang-areerate, Saovanee Leelayoova, Mathirut Mungthin, Piraporn Juntanawiwat, Patomroek Hanyanunt, Patsanun Potisuwan, Worarachanee Imjaijitt, Piyanate Kesakomol, Wijitar Dungchai, Toon Ruang-areerate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) producing E. coli are the most critical cause of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and multidrug resistance (MDR) producing prolonged hospitalization and burden in treating nosocomial infection, in which the conventional culture regularly constitutes the gold standard diagnosis. In this study, we developed and validated a simple and inexpensive distance-based paper device (dPAD) LAMP assay for simultaneous screening and semi-quantifying the resistant bacterial load of ESBL-producing E. coli in 427 urine samples of patients with urinary tract infection (UTI). The device could measure the LAMP amplicons of blaTEM gene and semi-quantify the degree of ESBL-producing E. coli in heavy (≥ 102 CFU/mL), light (101–102 CFU/mL) and none (< 101 CFU/mL) using the fluorescent measurement of the migratory distance. The sensitivity and specificity exhibited reliable performances, achieving as high as 98.9 and 96.5%, respectively. The assay could be performed within 1 hour, which was comparatively faster than the culture method (> 48 h) and cheaper than qPCR. To empower early AMR diagnosis and fast treatment of MDR, this inexpensive dPAD LAMP assay is simple, reliably fast and practically portable for point-of-care settings, particularly in resource-limited areas that can be set up in all levels of healthcare facilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21371-1.Keywords: Escherichia coli, ESBL, LAMP, Quantitative, Paper device, SYBR safe, UTI, UrineSubject terms: Applied microbiology, Bacterial techniques and applications, Urinary tract infection

Introduction

Escherichia coli, a gram-negative bacterium1,2, is one of the most common bacterial urinary tract infections (UTIs) in hospitals globally and prevalent between sexes and among all age groups3. Females are more affected by UTIs and have recurrent bladder infection within six months in 20 to 30% of women3–5. Patients with symptomatic UTI typically have a high concentration of bacterial infection; usually, ≥105 colony-forming units (CFU) per mL in a urine specimen6–8. Frequently, successful treatment of UTI is difficult and can cause parenchymal damage leading to renal insufficiency and further complications9–11. Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) producing E. coli are categorized as the most critical cause of antimicrobial resistance (AMR)12 and multidrug-resistance (MDR) causing prolonged hospitalization and burden in treating nosocomial infection resulting in high morbidity and mortality13,14. ESBLs are mostly plasmid mediated beta-lactamases and may also carry additional beta-lactamase genes as well as genes conferring resistance to other antimicrobial classes15–17. This can limit the chemotherapeutic options and facilitate the inter- and intraspecies dissemination of ESBLs such as E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae and other Enterobacteriaceae16,18–20. In addition, ESBLs were associated with various multilocus regions and genes21,22 in which 62.9% corresponded to blaCTX−M, blaTEM and blaSHV genes in Tanzania23. Therefore, ESBLs have become a public health concern worldwide24.

The conventional culture method using specific culture agar media constitutes the gold standard diagnosis isolating and counting the colonies of E. coli presenting in infected urine6–8. At least 24 h is required to identify the colonies and 5 to 7 days to confirm using biochemical and serological methods25–29. In contrast, molecular-based methods, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), generally exhibit more sensitivity and specificity, albeit at comparatively higher costs due to the requirement of expensive instruments30. Unlike PCR, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) can specifically generate a mixture of various lengths of stem-loop DNA amplicons under isothermal condition (60–65 °C) and synthesize large amounts of DNA target in a short time31,32. Consequently, LAMP requires simple procedures and common thermal control instrument, empowering user-friendly, low cost, and simple approach33. According to its simplicity, LAMP could be further combined with various methods and devices to increase the sensitivity and simplify the accessibility, e.g., AuNP colorimetric precipitates34,35, magnetic separation36, microchips37, microfluidic impedimetric sensing36 and distance-based paper devices (dPADs)30,38,39. LAMP was effectively useful for rapidly detecting MDR bacterial infection with high sensitivity and specificity21,40. Recently, rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) methods have been developed to monitor the susceptibility of pathogens to antibiotics including digital LAMP quantification41, metabolic enzyme-based AST42,43, and single-cell respiratory AST44 which can determine the phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility directly from clinical samples in 0.5 to 4.5 h, less than a conventional method that can take at least three days.

In this study, we have developed and clinically validated a semi-quantitative distance-based paper device (dPAD) using the fluorescent LAMP assay to screen and evaluate resistant bacterial load of E. coli with ESBL in the urine of patients in 42 min without the requirement of the postreaction staining process. A schematic experimental principle to visualize LAMP products of the blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene using semi-quantitative dPAD is shown in Fig. 1. A single drop could quantify E. coli with ESBL as low as 10 CFU/mL and evaluated light and heavy resistant bacterial loads by 102 CFU/mL cutoff value. The assay is an inexpensive alternative method, faster than gold standard diagnosis, that is practically portable, cost effective, and easily delivered to end-users, particularly in low levels of hospital-to-point-of-care settings in resource-limited areas.

Schematic procedure of ESBL-producing E. coli screening and quantitative evaluation in urine using distance-based paper device (dPAD) LAMP assay. Results

Optimization of LAMP reaction and quantitative dPAD LAMP assay

Amplification of LAMP amplicons specific to the blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene was successfully achieved and confirmed by DNA sequencing. The LAMP products could be amplified under 63 and 65 °C consistently (Fig. 2a). The limit of incubation time was 30 min (Fig. 2b). Finally, the selected optimal condition was 63 °C for 30 min.

The dPAD fabrication process is shown in Fig. 3a. The LAMP reaction was dropped on the sample channel and could flow in the detection channel zone after the hydrophobic wax ink penetration was inactivated at 120 °C for 1.5 min. Then transparent adhesive tape was installed to limit the boundary channel of sample and detection zones of the dPAD (Fig. 3a). The significant differences in migratory distance (Δdistance) between the positive (106 CFU/mL) and negative results of circular (4 mm × 3.5 mm), triangle and hexagonal (n = 3) were 4.83, 4.83 and 4.17 mm, respectively, (t-test, P-values = 0.0027, 0.0022 and 0.0023, respectively) (Fig. 3b) in which Pattern1 showed the most appropriate length in Δdistance with high sufficient percentage of relative standard deviations (%RSD); 5.88% in negative and 8.66% in positive results, respectively. The width difference of three circular patterns (Pattern1, 4 and 5) was significantly observed in six replicates (t-test, P-values = 0.0043, 0.003 and 0.0108, respectively) (Fig. 3c). Pattern4 (4 mm × 4 mm) gained the finest Δdistance, 4.08 mm, with ≤10%RSD (7.02 to 10.27%). The evaluation of Δdistance at 4.0, 5.0 and 6.0 µL (n = 6) indicated 4.00, 4.42 and 4.83 mm, respectively (t-test, P-values < 0.0001, < 0.0001 and < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 3d). The six-microliter load of sample volume was chosen due to the highest difference in Δdistance (4.83 mm) and ≤10%RSD (8.75 to 10.16%) (Fig. 3d).

Semi-quantitative analysis and specificity

Regarding to naked eye evaluation, the LoD was 102 CFU/mL in closed tube LAMP screening and semi-quantitative dPAD (Fig. 4a). Non-linearity of the untreated dPADs was found in which R-square was 0.7299 (Fig. 4b). A calibration of the device showed linearity between the distance of fluorescent migration and blaTEM LAMP amplicons in the range of 102 to 106 CFU/mL of polyethyleneimine (PEI)-treated dPAD with a linear equation as y = 1.1167x + 6.9 and R2 as 0.987 (Fig. 4c). For ΔdPAD, the linear equation was y = 1.1167x + 1.567 and R2 as 0.987. Based on ICH Q2(R2) guidelines (LoDdPAD = 3.3 × SE/slope; xdPAD and xΔdPAD = 0.9267) that SE and slope of linear regression of both evaluations were 0.3136 and 1.1167, the lowest limitations of the fluorescent length of migratory distance were 7.93 and 2.60 mm (100.987 or 101 CFU/mL), respectively. According to the linearity range of 102 to 106 CFU/mL (LoD), the amounts of the blaTEM LAMP amplicons correlated to the fluorescent length of migratory distance on dPAD. However, the device could not quantify but detected the amplicons from 101 to 102 CFU/mL (LODdPAD). Therefore, the lowest limitations and LODdPAD were used as the cutoff values of dPAD and ΔdPAD for the resistant bacterial loads of ESBL-producing E. coli that could be grouped into none, light and heavy levels.

Using closed tube screening and dPAD evaluation, cross amplifications were undetected except Shigella flexneri (Fig. 5). Application of dPAD to quantify resistant bacterial loads

Regarding to the lowest limitations and LODdPAD (Fig. 4c), the resistant bacterial loads of ESBL-producing E. coli could be grouped at three levels: none (concentration; <101 CFU/mL, length of dPAD; <7.93 mm, length of ΔdPAD; 2.60 mm), light (101–102 CFU/mL, 7.93–9.13 mm, 2.60–3.80 mm) and heavy (≥102 CFU/mL, ≥ 9.13 mm, ≥ 3.80 mm). Thus, the cutoff values of dPAD and ΔdPAD are 101 CFU/mL (7.93 and 2.60 mm, respectively) between none and light infection and 102 CFU/mL (9.13 and 3.80 mm, respectively) between light and heavy infection. Based on the cutoff values, the resistant bacterial loads of the dPAD, ΔdPAD and dPAD combined with the fluorescent closed tube screening (fluorescent closed tube + dPAD) could be classified in three levels: heavy (mean ± SD; 10.23 ± 1.06 mm, 5.44 ± 1.23 mm, 10.23 ± 1.06 mm; n = 63, 78, 63, respectively), light (8.58 ± 0.32 mm, 3.28 ± 0.36 mm, 8.10 ± 0.87 mm; n = 24, 19, 36, respectively) and none (5.18 ± 0.75 mm, 0.57 ± 0.99 mm, 5.11 ± 0.65 mm; n = 340, 330, 328, respectively) (Fig. 6a and c). Significant differences were observed in migratory distance among three levels at a 95% confidence level for these three detecting approaches (F-test, P-value < 0.0001, < 0.0001 and < 0.0001, respectively). Additionally, the resistant bacterial loads between heavy, light and no levels significantly differed in dPAD, ΔdPAD and dPAD combined with fluorescent closed tube screening (heavy vs. light, Bonferroni test, P-value < 0.001, < 0.001 and < 0.001, respectively; light vs. none, < 0.001, < 0.001 and < 0.001, respectively) as well as qPCR (Fig. 6a and d). According to simplified measurement and procedure, dPAD combined with the fluorescent closed tube approach was chosen to gain the highest sensitivity and specificity. The approach could semi-quantify and evaluate the resistant bacterial loads of ESBL-producing E. coli among patients with UTI in heavy (n = 63), light (n = 36) and none (n = 328) (Fig. 6c).

Evaluation of sensitivity and specificity

Four hundred twenty-seven DNA samples were extracted from urine including patients with ESBL-producing E. coli (nculture = 40; nqPCR = 88) and patients without (nculture = 387; nqPCR = 339) to evaluate and validate the dPAD LAMP assay. Using the CLSI culture method as a gold standard, the sensitivity and specificity of the dPAD combined with the closed tube LAMP (fluorescent closed tube + dPAD) were 72.5 (n = 29; 95% CI, 56.1 to 85.4%) and 81.9% (n = 317; 95% CI, 77.7 to 85.6%), respectively (Table 1). According to our CLSI culture criteria that selectively isolated and cultured only MDR E. coli in UTI patients, it could exclude other ESBL-producing bacteria resulting in false positive detection. In contrast, our LAMP primers are specific to the blaTEM gene. Thus, the CLSI culture may be unsuitable as a gold standard for observing the sensitivity and specificity of the ESBL-detecting LAMP assay. Despite CLSI, qPCR was used as a reference standard. The sensitivity of the fluorescent closed tube approach was the same as that of dPAD combined with the closed tube LAMP, 98.9% (n = 87; 95% CI, 93.8 to 100.0%). However, the sensitivity differed in dPAD and ΔdPAD; 88.6 and 94.3% (n = 78, 83; 95% CI, 80.1 to 94.4%, 87.2 to 98.1%, respectively). The specificity was high in the fluorescent closed tube approach, dPAD, ΔdPAD and dPAD combined with the closed tube LAMP: 96.5, 97.3, 95.9 and 96.5% (n = 327, 330, 325 and 327; 95% CI, 93.9 to 98.2%, 95.0 to 98.8%, 93.2 to 97.7 and 93.9 to 98.2%, respectively). The dPAD combined with the fluorescent closed tube approach and qPCR showed significant results that were fair agreement when compared with the gold standard, conventional culture (P-value < 0.0001 and < 0.0001; Kappa = 0.328 and 0.336, respectively). However, the fluorescent closed tube approach, dPAD and dPAD combined with the fluorescent closed tube approach showed significance and were in almost perfect agreement when compared with qPCR (P-values < 0.0001, < 0.0001 and < 0.0001; Kappa = 0.911, 0.863 and 0.911, respectively).

Discussion

The successful quantitative detection of ESBL-producing E. coli was achieved using the dPAD LAMP assay. The device could detect the amount of the blaTEM gene of the ESBL-producing E. coli in three resistant bacterial loads: heavy (≥ 102 CFU/mL), light (101–102 CFU/mL) and none (< 101 CFU/mL) using the fluorescent measurement of the migratory distance on the dPAD30,38. The heavy level of the blaTEM gene of the ESBL-producing E. coli has correlated with CLSI culture and potentially represented a high risk of antibiotic resistance development, whereas the none and light levels are essential for predictive susceptible treatment of the patients during the screening by the device. However, clinical symptoms and infection severity do not always directly correlate with bacterial load density. The assay could selectively perform simultaneous visualization using a direct closed tube mixture for rapid screening as well as quantitative dPAD based on DNA immobilized cellulose Whatman No. 1 in 42 min without requiring the postreaction staining process30. According to the stability of the dye45, the remaining LAMP reaction could be kept and preserved for further re-analysis such as AuNP-probe hybridization35. A mixture of stem-loop DNA amplicons depended on the amount of DNA precursor in each reaction sample. It could be immobilized on the cellulose paper regarding the physical adsorption and covalent bonding between the amine-terminated nucleic acid and the polysaccharide38. Therefore, the length of migratory distance directly correlated to the number of LAMP products and reflected the initial concentration of the blaTEM gene on the ESBL plasmid of E. coli. In addition, PEI generates a positive charge in an acidic environment that can bind to the negative charge of LAMP amplicons. Hence, large amounts of LAMP products could interact with PEI treated on dPAD in which electrostatic reaction between PEI and the negative charge could delay the fluorescent flow distance of the LAMP amplicons in detection channel resulting in improvement and stabilization of the migratory distance in the device.

Unlike the detection of E. coli in urine46, the assay is not specific to E. coli but directly detects the blaTEM gene on the ESBL plasmid that is one of two predominant groups (blaTEM and blaCTX−M) in Thailand47. Other bacteria could also be detected if they have the same transfer plasmid containing the blaTEM gene such as S. flexneri48,49 as same as our study that demonstrated the existence of the plasmid in both ESBL-producing E. coli and S. flexneri (Figs. 5 and S5). Otherwise, the other ESBL-producing bacteria were excluded from the CLSI test because E. coli isolates were solely selective and cultured based on ≥106 CFU/mL and MIC values. Consequently, incongruent results could be possible between dPAD LAMP and CLSI culture, such as false positives. Low sensitivity and specificity were expected regarding the LAMP primers specific to the blaTEM gene (72.5 and 81.9%, respectively) because the ESBL gene related to multi-loci and transfer plasmid17. To evaluate reliable validation of the assay, qPCR could serve as an alternative option better than CLSI culture50. Then the sensitivity and specificity were improved to 88.6 to 98.9 and 95.9 to 97.3%, respectively, depending on LAMP approaches with an almost perfect agreement (P-values < 0.0001; Kappa range = 0.863–0.911) in which no cross-amplification was observed, except closely related gram-negative S. flexneri in which the ESBL transfer plasmid could be presented38,48,49,51,52. In addition, the resistant bacterial load was considered consistently in all resistant bacterial loads with no error readout of the migratory distance using dPAD, ΔdPAD and dPAD combined with the fluorescent closed tube approach, when compared with qPCR.

In spite of ΔdPAD, the dPAD combined with the closed tube approach was feasibly inexpensive and practically convenient because of unnecessary negative quantification in a prereaction mixture46. Likewise, combined methods could rapidly determine the effective detection of E. coli46. The dPAD LAMP assays could specifically amplify specific gene regions of the microorganisms faster46 but is limited and unsuitable for detecting all main types of ESBL variants as effectively as the CLSI culture guidelines53 at a single detection time. Despite conventional methods, multiplex gene detection of multi-loci of ESBL-producing E. coli could be possible by a specific colorimetric LAMP microfluidic chip36,54 or different fluorescent dye labeling LAMP primers55.

Due to the requirement of noncostly instruments such as simple heating devices, the dPAD LAMP assay is convenient and affordable for local health service centers30. Eventually, the dPAD LAMP assay could evaluate the level of ESBL-producing E. coli based on the blaTEM gene within 42 min faster than the conventional CLSI culture method (> 48 h) and ~ US$1.4 cheaper than qPCR46. However, the processing time could be extended depending on the extraction protocol46,56,57. In Thailand, ESBL-producing E. coli has been reported to contain multiple variants of the ESBL gene47,58, whereas the coexistence of ESBL genes mostly comprised blaTEM and blaCTX−M47. Therefore, incorporating other major variants of the ESBL gene, e.g., blaCTX−M, could concrete the dPAD LAMP to detect multiloci ESBL-producing E. coli in a single test and be as effective as CLSI culture in the future.

In this study, we developed and validated the dPAD LAMP assay to detect and quantify the blaTEM gene of ESBL-producing E. coli in urine with high sensitivity (98.9%) and specificity (96.5%). Moreover, it could evaluate the degree of ESBL-producing E. coli in heavy, light and none infection using the one-step LAMP assay without requiring advanced instruments. The assay remains limited to detect all major variants of ESBL gene in a single test. However, it remains sufficient for fast screening ESBL-producing E. coli because blaTEM is one of the major variant of the ESBL gene predominantly in Thailand, ranging from 30 to 70% depending on the location47,58. According to ESBLs associated with various multilocus regions and genes21,22, the assay could be standardized by incorporating a major multilocus ESBL panel for early AMR diagnosis in the future. Regarding a reliably fast, simple, inexpensive and practical technique, the dPAD LAMP assay is a promising rapid approach to facilitate early AMR diagnosis and promote suitable fast treatment of MDR that can reduce severity and prevent disease progression, particularly in low levels of hospital to point-of-care settings in resource-limited areas. Hence, the LAMP assay can be integrated in global MDR molecular surveillance networks21.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Leftover urine samples, routinely collected for UTI diagnostic purposes, were stored in a microbiological laboratory of Phramongkutklao Hospital. The samples were coded and anonymized before specimen processing and analysis. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board, Royal Thai Army Medical Department (IRBRTA 1427/2564, IRBRTA 1214/2565 and IRBRTA 1356/2566). The informed consent statement was waived by the Institutional Review Board, Royal Thai Army Medical Department.

Materials and reagents

The reagents and enzyme comprised 1× Thermopol buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), betaine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), dNTP mix solution (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA), magnesium sulfate (New England Biolabs), acetic acid glacial (AR grade) (QRëC™, Auckland, New Zealand), Millipore Milli Q water system (R ≥ 18 MΩ cm), SYBR™ Safe DNA Gel Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), Bst DNA Polymerase Large Fragment (New England Biolabs) and KAPA SYBR® FAST (Sigma-Aldrich). The chemical solutions were prepared and made according to Saengsawang et al. 38. The tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Mini Kit was purchased from Favorgen® Biotech Corp. (Ping Tung, Taiwan). The dPADs were fabricated from Whatman No. 1 (Vernon Hills, IL, USA).

Sample Preparation

ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were cultured and confirmed using CHRMID®ESBL media (bioMérieux, Marcy-I’Etoile, France) at 37 °C for 24 h, and the CFUs were calculated per mL using a 2100Q portable turbidimeter (HACH, Düsseldorf, Germany) and the ESBL-producing 0.5 McFarland Equivalence Turbidity Standard (Remel, Lenexa, KS, USA) to adjust an equivalent concentration to 108 CFU/mL. The ESBL-producing E. coli culture solutions were centrifuged at 2300g for 10 min to collect pellets and stored at − 20 °C.

The urine samples were spontaneously mixed and inoculated using a calibrated loop on McConkey or blood agar for E. coli screening. The agar plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then CFU per mL of bacteria was measured based on colony counts in which the cutoff value for the antimicrobial susceptibility test was typically ≥106 CFU/mL. ESBL-producing E. coli was screened and evaluated using minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) values by broth microdilution method with two antimicrobial agents including cefpodoxime and aztreonam53. MIC is the lowest concentration of an antibacterial agent which completely prevents visible growth of the isolates. E. coli isolates were diluted with normal saline to 0.5 McFarland (approximately 108 CFU/mL) and then diluted with Mueller Hinton broth before pipetting in 96-well plates containing antibiotics. The working stock concentrations of antibiotics were based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines53. Eventually, the plate was incubated at 37 °C, 18 to 24 h. The results were evaluated using MIC values according to CLSI guidelines.

On the other hand, 10 mL of urine sample was centrifuged and washed three times with phosphate buffer saline to precipitate ESBL-producing E. coli at 2300g for 10 min. The pellets were kept at − 20 °C before DNA extraction. Of 427 uroculture, 40 (9.4%) were ESBL-producing E. coli.

DNA extraction

DNA of the pellets of ESBL-producing E. coli culture and urine sample was extracted using the FavorPrep™ Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Favorgen® Biotech Corp.) according to manufacturer protocol. The purified genomic DNA was eluted to 100 µL final volume and subsequently preserved at − 20 °C until further use.

Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP)

A LAMP primer set specific to ESBL-producing E. coli was designed based on the blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene. To avoid potential cross amplification, a consensus sequence specific to blaTEM plasmid of E. coli was constructed using BioEdit Software, Version 7.0.5.3 (Ibis Biosciences, Carlsbad, CA, USA) from multiple sequence alignment of 20 ESBL-producing E. coli strains under Genbank accession numbers including AP019762, AP023198, AP023236, AY425988, CP034956, CP040398, CP049300, CP049612, CP054237, CP052879, CP071442, KY271103, LC577768, LC636041, LC712755, MK962306, MN891682, MT387456, MT387478, MT636819, MW390534, NG_052650 and NG_068216. Then the consensus sequence was imported to PrimerExplorer Software, Version 5 (http://primerexplorer.jp/lampv5e/index.html). The primers were designed and selected based on specific regions to avoid cross amplification. The specificity of the primers was preliminarily verified using Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) analysis. The six selective LAMP primers are shown in Table 2. The primers were synthesized by Bionics Co. Ltd. (Seoul, South Korea).

To amplify the blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene, the LAMP reaction was prepared in a total volume of 25 µL of reaction mixture. Each reaction mixture consisted of 1× Isothermal Amplification Buffer (New England Biolabs), 6 mM MgSO4, 0.8 M Betaine (Sigma-Aldrich), 1.4 mM dNTP (Promega Corporation), 1.6 µM of each inner primer (FIP and BIP, Table 2), 4 µM of each outer primer (F3 and B3, Table 2), 0.4 µM of each loop primer (LF and LB, Table 2), 8 U of Bst DNA Polymerase Large Fragment (New England Biolabs) and 2 µL of template DNA. Then 1× SYBR™ Safe DNA Gel Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each reaction mixture as the nonspecific fluorescent dye indicator of LAMP products. To optimize the temperature of LAMP amplification, the reaction mixture was parallel-incubated at different temperatures including 60, 63, and 65 °C for 105 min, and inactivated the reaction at 80 °C for 2 min using a FlexCycler2 Thermocycler (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). Then amplification was performed at 63 °C; the optimal temperature of LAMP amplification, for 20, 25 and 30 min to minimize incubation time range. Each reaction mixture was performed in triplicate, and Milli Q water was used for no template control (NTC).

After incubating, each LAMP reaction was divided in two portions: one for qualitative fluorescent closed tube screening and one for further semi-quantitative dPAD evaluation. For closed tube screening, the LAMP amplicons were examined based on direct visual inspection of the reaction tubes using either a blue light (BL) or an ultraviolet light (UV) transilluminator. A positive amplification involved vivid fluorescent emissions, whereas no fluorescent emission was found without amplification. Five microliters of the reaction mixture were electrophoresed on 2% agarose gel stained with SYBR™ Safe (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using Molecular Imager® Gel Doc™ XR + System with Imager Lab™ 3.0 (Bio-rad) to determine a mixture of various lengths of the stem-loop DNA of LAMP products. Then target LAMP amplicons were purified and sent to Bionics Co. Ltd. (Seoul, South Korea) for sequencing and deposited under NCBI accession numbers: PV268677–PV268679. The sequencing chromatograms were validated using BioEdit (Ibis Biosciences) and multiple-aligned with the reference blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene retrieved from GenBank.

qPCR amplification

The qPCR reactions were performed under a final volume of 10 µL consisting of a 1 µL DNA template, 2 pmol of each primer (F3 and B3, Table 2) and 5 µL of KAPA SYBR® FAST (Sigma-Aldrich). The blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene was amplified using a CFX96™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with ESBL-producing E. coli isolates’ DNA as a positive control, whereas the negative control comprised Milli Q water. The amplification profile was one cycle of initial amplification at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification: 10 s denaturation at 95 °C and 30 s extension at 60 °C. Melting curve analysis was generated by increasing the temperature of the sample reaction from 65 to 95 °C in increments of 0.5 °C continuously at 0.05 s. Each reaction was performed in triplicate.

The positive qPCR products were electrophoresed on 2% agarose gel stained with SYBR safe (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using Molecular Imager® Gel Doc™ XR + System with Imager Lab™ 3.0 (Bio-rad). Then target qPCR amplicons were purified and sent to Bionics Co. Ltd. (Seoul, South Korea) for sequencing and deposited under NCBI accession numbers: PV268680–PV268681. The sequencing chromatograms were validated and multiple-aligned with the reference blaTEM plasmid region of the ESBL gene, as described.

Device fabrication and quantitative LAMP

The dPADs were fabricated using Whatman filter paper No. 1 (Cole-Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA). Five different patterns of dPADs were designed to semi-quantify LAMP target amplicons and divided in sample and detection zones using Adobe Illustrator, Version 26.4.1 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The sample zones included three circulars (height 4 mm and width 3.5, 4.0, 5.0 mm, respectively), one triangle (height 4 mm and width 4 mm) and one hexagonal (height 3.5 mm and width 4.0 mm). The detection zones were fixed with a channel length of 30 mm and a width of 1.5 mm. All designs were printed using hydrophobic wax ink and a wax printer (Xerox ColorQube 8570, Japan) on filter paper with a width of 1 mm to control and limit the territory flow of the LAMP reaction sample. To induce the wax to penetrate the filter paper, the dPAD was baked at 120 °C for 1.5 min and then cooled at room temperature to limit the wax boundary of the sample and detection zones. The bottom side of the device was sealed with transparent adhesive tape to prevent any leakage of the flow sample reaction38, and the millimeter scale was printed on the left side of the device for the readout measurement.

The LAMP reaction mixture was dropped on the sample zone of the dPAD in which the sample flowed in the detection channel and dried within 5 to 7 min at room temperature. The migratory distance of the device was measured using a millimeter scale by visual inspection under the BL transilluminator. Initially, the migratory distances of five patterns of dPADs were compared to select the dPAD pattern obtaining the clearest fluorescent distances at 4 µL. To optimize the sample volume for the selective pattern of dPAD, the loading volume of the LAMP reaction was evaluated at 4, 5 and 6 µL in which the highest fluorescent length of reaction flow distance was chosen.

To enhance the constant migratory distance of the LAMP reaction in the dPAD, 4 µL of PEI was applied and treated on sample and detection zones of dPAD at 0.02% weight per volume (%w/v) for 5 min. To determine the detection limits (LoD) of the LAMP primers and calibrate semi-quantitative measurement of the dPAD, purified genomic DNA of ESBL-producing E. coli culture was tenfold serially diluted from 106 to 101 CFU/mL using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (DeNovix Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA), and were equivalent to 2.3 ng/µL to 23 fg/µL, respectively. Linear regression was performed to assess the independent association of fluorescent distance and loading volumes, 102 to 106 CFU/mL (230 fg/µL to 2.3 ng/µL), between untreated and PEI-treated dPADs. Then the mean distances of each dilution were substituted in the linear equation of the calibration curve to calculate the correlation coefficient (R2) of the visual readout obtained using dPAD. The fluorescent distance of each loading volume was undertaken in triplicate, and the negative control comprised Milli Q water.

To ensure that the LAMP primers were specific to the blaTEM plasmid of E. coli and cross-amplification with other pathogens was unlikely, the LAMP reactions were examined against human genomic DNA as negative control (GC) and bacteria’ DNA including Bacillus cereus, Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella enterica, Shigella flexneri, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Vibrio cholera and Vibrio parahaemolyticus. Extracted DNA of ESBL-producing E. coli was used as positive control.

Evaluation of sensitivity and specificity of semi-quantitative LAMP assay using dPAD

The sensitivity and specificity of the molecular method were determined using a total of 427 genomic DNA samples from urines, consisting of 40 confirmed ESBL-producing E. coli infections and 387 uninfected cases. Diagnosis of ESBL-producing E. coli was typically ≥106 CFU/mL cutoff value and broth microdilution screening method was positive as recommended by CLSI guidelines. Instead of ESBL-producing E. coli selective agar and broth media as a gold standard, qPCR data were additionally used to validate and evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of different LAMP approaches to detect the blaTEM gene for ESBL as a reference standard. The strength of the agreement of the culture gold standard was determined among molecular methods including qPCR and LAMP (dPAD combined with fluorescent closed tube screening). The strength of the agreement of molecular reference standard was determined between qualitative detection including fluorescent closed tube screening (SYBR safe) and quantitative detections using dPAD LAMP detection (dPAD and ΔdPAD) and dPAD combined with fluorescent closed tube screening. The methods were assessed using the kappa statistical test at 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant using STATA, Version SE14 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

For dPAD combined with the fluorescent closed tube screening (fluorescent closed tube + dPAD), the semi-quantitative result was examined and evaluated after the pre-reaction mixture of the fluorescent closed tube LAMP showed a positive result. When the fluorescent closed tube screening results were negative, the migratory distance of the reaction mixture would not be further measured by a semi-quantitative dPAD30,46.
