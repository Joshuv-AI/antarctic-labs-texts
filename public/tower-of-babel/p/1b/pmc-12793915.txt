The Beta-blocker chronicle: From past principles to present practice – Implications for the Indian subcontinent
Authors: Saumitra Ray, Banshi Saboo, Debabrata Roy, Dhurjati Prasad Sinha, Dhiman Kahali, Mrinal Kanti Das, Sanjay Tyagi, Satyendra Tewari, Hriday Kumar Chopra, Jyotirmoy Pal, Rakesh Yadav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstractβ-blockers have evolved from being foundational cardiovascular agents to precision therapies across diverse clinical scenarios. Their journey from early non-selective to β1-selective and vasodilatory molecules reflects advances in understanding adrenergic modulation in cardiovascular continuum. β-blockers remain integral in managing hypertension, chronic coronary syndromes, arrhythmias, and heart failure. Their pharmacodynamic attributes such as β1-selectivity, inverse agonism, sympathoinhibition, and vasodilation allow personalized use in patients with comorbidities like diabetes, asthma or chronic kidney disease. Among contemporary agents, bisoprolol and nebivolol exemplify next generation β-blockers with high β1-selectivity, favourable tolerability, and cardioprotective effects beyond blood pressure and heart rate control. Global and national guidelines recommend them for angina relief, secondary prevention post–MI, and rate control in tachyarrhythmias. In Indian context, prescribing trends highlight bisoprolol among the most prescribed β-blockers, reflecting its clinical trust and relevance. Introduction

For over five decades, β-adrenergic receptor antagonists or β-blockers have stood as pillars in the management of cardiovascular (CV) diseases. These agents exert their therapeutic effects primarily through inhibition of catecholamine action at β-adrenergic receptors, resulting in reduced heart rate (HR), myocardial contractility, and renin secretion. This multifaceted mechanism underpins their clinical utility in ischemic heart disease (IHD), heart failure (HF), hypertension (HTN), and tachyarrhythmias.1,2

The conceptual foundation for β-blockade was laid in the early 20th century with Sir Henry Dale's initial description of adrenergic receptors and Raymond Ahlquist's pivotal classification of α- and β-receptors in 1948. The clinical revolution began in 1958 when Sir James Black developed propranolol, the first effective β-blocker, designed to lower myocardial oxygen demand in angina. This breakthrough transformed the treatment paradigm for IHD and earned Black the Nobel Prize. The identification of receptor subtypes: β1, β2, and β3 enabled more targeted therapeutic strategies. Further advances, such as the crystallization of the β2-receptor by Lefkowitz and Kobilka in 2007, deepened our understanding of receptor biology and earned another Nobel recognition in 2012.1, 2, 3

Despite these landmark achievements, the role of β-blockers has been periodically reassessed. Meta-analyses over the past two decades have questioned their efficacy as first-line antihypertensives, particularly older agents like atenolol and propranolol, citing suboptimal outcomes in stroke prevention and overall CV mortality. These criticisms have informed recent hypertension guidelines, which now often position β-blockers as second-line options. However, this broad generalization may not hold true for newer agents especially cardioselective and vasodilatory β-blockers which demonstrate more favourable hemodynamic, metabolic, and vascular profiles.1, 2, 3

Today, β-blockers retain a central role across the CV continuum. Their benefits extend beyond traditional uses to encompass cardiomyopathies, HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), and complex comorbid populations, including those with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Their evolving utility reflects an expanding evidence base and an increasing emphasis on individualized, guideline-driven care.1, 2, 3

This review is undertaken to synthesize updated pharmacological concepts, evidence from landmark trials, and regional consensus, with a special focus on the relevance and integration of β-blockers in the Indian clinical landscape. By bridging theory, evidence, and practice, this review aims to offer clinicians a nuanced, evidence-informed perspective on the optimal use of β-blockers in contemporary CV care.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) in India: A Unique Burden

India faces a disproportionately high burden of CVD, characterized by earlier onset, elevated case fatality, and a greater proportion of premature deaths compared to global averages. Indian populations experience CVD nearly a decade earlier than Western counterparts, with approximately 62 % of CVD-related deaths classified as premature. The disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rate for IHD is 1.6 times the global average.4

A 2025 meta-analysis estimated the overall prevalence of CVD among Indian adults to be about 11 %, with a higher burden in urban populations (12 %) compared to rural areas (6 %). Gender-specific data suggest slightly higher prevalence in women (14 %) than in men (12 %). Notably, CVD in India disproportionately affects older adults, but also shows concerning trends among younger cohorts.5

The multifactorial drivers of India's CVD epidemic include poorly controlled traditional risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, both of which have low awareness and treatment rates. Socioeconomic disparities, early life programming, and limited health literacy further compound the risk. Indians also display a distinct biological susceptibility, including higher insulin resistance and greater metabolic risk from an early age. Migrant studies reinforce this observation, revealing a heightened risk of coronary heart disease and earlier mortality among individuals of Indian origin.4

Data from the PURE study underscore that even with a relatively lower prevalence of classical risk factors, Indian populations exhibit significantly higher rates of major CV events and death compared to high-income countries. This paradox points to a complex interplay of biological vulnerability and six major societal transitions; epidemiological, demographic, nutritional, environmental, sociocultural, and economic that are reshaping health trajectories in India.4

Historical evolution of β-Blockers

The introduction of β-blockers revolutionized CV pharmacotherapy. In 1958, Sir James Black transformed therapeutic strategy by shifting focus from increasing myocardial oxygen supply to reducing oxygen demand through sympathetic inhibition, an approach inspired by Ahlquist's β-receptor classification. Early compounds like dichloroisoproterenol showed β-antagonism but limited efficacy, paving the way for Black's development of propranolol, the first clinically successful non-selective β-blocker for angina and other cardiac conditions.3

To overcome bronchospasm, a major limitation of non-selective agents, practolol, the first β1-selective (cardioselective) β-blocker was developed in 1966, initiating the era of safer, more targeted therapy. Although later withdrawn due to adverse effects, it paved the path for second-generation agents like atenolol and metoprolol. Subsequently, third-generation β-blockers such as carvedilol and nebivolol, introduced vasodilatory effects via α-blockade or nitric oxide release, improving vascular compliance and metabolic profiles.3 Pharmacokinetic advancements further yielded long-acting and ultra-short-acting formulations, enhancing adherence and use in both acute and chronic settings. Over six decades later, β-blockers remain integral to CV therapy and have expanded utility beyond cardiology, in anxiety, migraine prophylaxis, and portal hypertension.3

Despite this enduring legacy, their modern role is marked by active debate. The classification of β-blockers as first-line antihypertensive agents has been re-examined, with recent outcome data favouring alternatives for primary stroke prevention. Yet, β-blockers remain preferred in patients with concomitant coronary artery disease (CAD), arrhythmias, or heart failure. In heart failure, their mortality benefit is well established in HFrEF, while their role in HFpEF continues to evolve, with ongoing trials exploring potential benefit in symptom reduction and hospitalization rates. These controversies exemplify how foundational pharmacological principles continue to shape dynamic, evidence-based practice.

In the Indian context, prescription trends mirror this global evolution. Registry and real-world data from India indicate that β-blockers, including bisoprolol remain among the most commonly prescribed antihypertensive agents, occupying a substantial class share.6 Their widespread use reflects clinician familiarity, favourable tolerability, and enduring therapeutic confidence across diverse cardiovascular presentations. This sustained real-world relevance reinforces the continuing importance of β-blockers in optimizing outcomes within the Indian cardiovascular landscape.

Pharmacological properties of β-Blockers and their clinical relevance

Receptor selectivity and cardioselectivity

One of the key pharmacological distinctions among β-blockers is their relative affinity for β1-versus β2-adrenoceptors. Cardioselective β-blockers preferentially inhibit β1-adrenoceptors, which are primarily located in cardiac tissue, while non-selective agents block both β1-and β2-receptors. This selectivity carries important clinical implications. For instance, bisoprolol demonstrates a high degree of β1-selectivity approximately 14-fold resulting in negligible β2-blockade even at therapeutic doses. In contrast, atenolol and metoprolol exhibit lower β1-selectivity.

High β1-selectivity translates into enhanced safety and tolerability, particularly in patients with coexisting pulmonary or metabolic conditions. Cardioselective agents like bisoprolol are better tolerated in patients with asthma or COPD and are not absolutely contraindicated. Moreover, they are less likely to adversely affect glycaemic control or contribute to erectile dysfunction. Despite these advantages, β-blockers remain underutilized in such populations, potentially depriving patients of their proven CV benefits.

Differences in receptor binding kinetics also play a role in therapeutic performance. Bisoprolol binds slowly and dissociates even more slowly from β1-adrenoceptors, prolonging receptor occupancy and enhancing duration of action. In contrast, metoprolol and atenolol exhibit faster binding and dissociation, leading to more fluctuating receptor coverage and potentially variable efficacy.7,8

Intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA)

Certain β-blockers possess intrinsic sympathomimetic activity due to partial agonism at β-adrenoceptors. While ISA reduces the extent of β1-mediated reductions in HR and cardiac output, and attenuates β2-mediated vasoconstriction, its clinical utility remains limited. In fact, ISA appears to attenuate antihypertensive efficacy and offers no added advantage in ischemic heart disease. Clinical trials suggest that ISA may even impair outcomes in patients with heart failure. Consequently, current treatment guidelines recommend β-blockers without ISA such as bisoprolol for optimal CV protection.7,8

Vasodilatory properties

Some β-blockers exhibit additional vasodilatory effects, either through α1-blockade or stimulation of β3-adrenoceptors. Carvedilol and labetalol achieve vasodilation via α1-receptor antagonism, while nebivolol exerts a distinct endothelial-dependent vasodilatory action mediated by enhanced nitric oxide (NO) availability. This action is primarily attributed to β3-adrenoceptor stimulation and activation of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS), leading to improved endothelial function and arterial compliance.

Although these properties contribute to blood pressure (BP) lowering and may support endothelial health, evidence from comparative trials and meta-analyses has not demonstrated superior CV outcomes for vasodilatory β-blockers over non-vasodilatory ones. Bisoprolol, though lacking vasodilatory mechanisms, provides efficacy in hypertension and heart failure management. On the other hand, nebivolol has been shown to preserve or even improve erectile function, an important consideration given that sexual dysfunction is reported in up to 20 % of patients receiving traditional β-blockers.7,8

Lipophilicity

The lipophilicity of β-blockers significantly influences their ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and their primary route of elimination. Hydrophilic β-blockers such as atenolol and sotalol, exhibit poor BBB penetration and are predominantly excreted via the kidneys, potentially lowering the risk of CNS adverse effects. lipophilic agents like propranolol and metoprolol readily cross the BBB and may be associated with CNS-related side effects, including sleep disturbances, depression, and hallucinations. Bisoprolol and betaxolol display moderate lipophilicity, placing them in an intermediate category with balanced solubility in both lipids and water, resulting in a partial but controlled ability to cross the BBB.7,8

Inverse agonism

Most β-blockers act as inverse agonists, stabilizing β-adrenoceptors in an inactive conformation and suppressing their baseline activity. This mechanism may reduce receptor desensitization and help preserve receptor density over time, theoretically enhancing long-term efficacy. However, clinical evidence linking inverse agonism to superior CV outcomes remains inconclusive, and its relevance appears largely theoretical.7

Biased signal transduction

Emerging data suggest that some β-blockers engage in biased agonism, preferentially activating non-classical intracellular signalling pathways. Carvedilol and nebivolol, for instance, activate the β-arrestin signalling cascade while concurrently blocking G-protein mediated signalling. This dual activity may underlie their anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory properties.

While this mechanism might help explain variations in clinical effects between different agents, biased signalling is not yet a clinically actionable concept. Its potential benefits remain speculative pending more robust translational and outcomes-based research.7

Sympathoinhibition and baroreflex modulation

Beyond their classic postsynaptic effects, β-blockers exert presynaptic actions by inhibiting catecholamine release through β1-receptor blockade on sympathetic nerve terminals. This reduces sympathetic drive, lowers cardiac workload, and contributes to BP control. Lipophilic agents are more likely to suppress central sympathetic outflow due to better CNS penetration.

There are also differences in how β-blockers influence baroreceptor reflex sensitivity. Carvedilol, for example, has demonstrated superior reduction in myocardial norepinephrine levels and greater downregulation of β1-receptors in failing hearts compared to metoprolol, suggesting a more pronounced sympathoinhibitory profile.7

Dialyzability

Pharmacokinetic characteristics such as dialyzability become crucial in patients on haemodialysis. The extent to which a β-blocker is removed during dialysis can influence both its efficacy and safety in patients with end-stage renal disease. Careful selection based on dialyzability is therefore essential when managing hypertension in this subgroup.7

Clinical Implications and Representative β-Blockers.

Hypertension

A pivotal update in the 2023 European Society of Hypertension (ESH) guidelines is the reclassification of β-blockers as first-line therapy for hypertension management, placing them on par with other major antihypertensive drug classes. This marks a shift from the 2018 ESH/ESC guidelines, which acknowledged β-blockers primarily in specific comorbid conditions such as heart failure, post-myocardial infarction (Post–MI), angina, atrial fibrillation, and in younger women limiting their use to selective cases.9

The 2023 guidelines now recommend β-blockers as foundational options for both monotherapy and combination therapy, extending their role to a broader range of cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular comorbidities. This expansion is underpinned by robust clinical evidence showing that β-blockers reduce office, ambulatory, and home BP measurements comparable to other drug classes.9 Fig. 1 illustrates the percentage reduction in CV events, including CV mortality, and all-cause mortality associated with various antihypertensive drug classes. Meta-analyses further confirm that the extent of BP reduction, regardless of drug class, correlates with proportional CV risk reduction.9

The ASCOT Legacy Study (15.7-year follow-up) showed no significant difference in all-cause mortality between atenolol- and amlodipine-based regimens, supporting the long-term efficacy of β-blockers.10 Additionally, meta-analytic data highlight β-blockers benefits in younger patients and those with elevated BP or left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), though they are less effective in stroke prevention in the elderly underscoring the need for individualized therapy.11

Spotlight on β-blockers in Indian hypertension management

In the Indian context, emerging expert consensus supports the revitalized role of β-blockers in clinical practice. A 2023 expert opinion survey involving 828 cardiologists across India recommended a BP target of 130/80 mmHg in hypertensive patients with angina and emphasized the use of cardioselective β-blockers particularly in fixed-dose combination with telmisartan for improved BP control. This combination was especially favoured in patients with LVH and atrial fibrillation.17

Given the persistently low hypertension control rates in India (reported to be under 10 % when aligned with ACC or ISH guidelines), there is a growing consensus that β-blockers, especially bisoprolol, nebivolol, and metoprolol should be considered early in the treatment algorithm. Younger hypertensive patients (<60 years), or those presenting with elevated resting heart rates or sympathetic overactivity, stand to benefit significantly. The updated 2023 ESH guidelines have reinforced this view, providing a rationale for the broader use of β-blockers in India's high-burden CVD population.18

Heart Failure (HF)

β-blockers are firmly established as a cornerstone therapy in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and are increasingly being considered for patients with mildly reduced (HFmrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Their therapeutic role is underpinned by extensive clinical evidence supporting reductions in mortality, symptom burden, and cardiac remodelling. However, their use in HFpEF and HF with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF) remains more selective and requires further validation through randomized data.

HFrEF

Robust randomized controlled trials have consistently shown that β-blockers particularly bisoprolol, carvedilol, and metoprolol succinate reduce mortality and hospitalization rates in HFrEF patients. These agents have demonstrated a reduction of over 30 % in sudden cardiac death (SCD) and significant improvements in both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.19 Seminal trials such as CIBIS II, MERIT-HF, and COPERNICUS provide the foundational evidence for the use of β-blockers in HFrEF, even when added to optimized background therapy.20, 21, 22 Beyond survival benefits, β-blockers also improve quality of life and promote reverse left ventricular remodelling, with some of the lowest number-needed-to-treat (NNT) values in heart failure management.19 Table 3 presents key trial data highlighting the efficacy of β-blockers in HFrEF.

Evidence of β-Blockers in HFrEF Management.

HFmrEF

Emerging evidence from large-scale registries such as the Swedish Heart Failure Registry and analyses from trials like DELIVER suggest that β-blockers may confer benefits in patients with HFmrEF. These benefits include reductions in all-cause mortality and HF-related hospitalizations, though this has not yet been fully integrated into earlier clinical guidelines.19

HFimpEF

Patients who experience improvement in LVEF after previously meeting criteria for HFrEF may also benefit from continued β-blocker therapy. Observational data indicate that maintaining β-blocker therapy at moderate-to-high doses in this population supports sustained remodelling and better long-term outcomes, although no prospective randomized trials have specifically addressed initiation in HFimpEF.19

HFpEF

Although the role of β-blockers in HFpEF remains contentious, a 2023 meta-analysis indicated potential benefit in reducing all-cause mortality and hospitalizations when compared to calcium channel blockers.28 however, these findings are largely hypothesis-generating and not yet incorporated into guideline-directed therapy. In clinical practice, β-blockers are used in HFpEF patients for comorbid conditions such as hypertension and atrial fibrillation. Their anti-adrenergic and possible anti-fibrotic effects may provide benefit in select subgroups, though the overall data are mixed. Importantly, β-blockers may be less effective in HFpEF patients with chronotropic incompetence, underscoring the need for individualized treatment decisions and further randomized trials.19

Spotlight on role of β-blockers in heart failure management in India

In India, the clinical adoption of β-blockers in HF management aligns with global practice, especially in patients with HFrEF due to their proven cardioprotective benefits.29 The CIBIS-I trial first demonstrated a significant survival benefit with bisoprolol in patients with chronic HF, particularly those without a history of myocardial infarction and those with dilated cardiomyopathy.23 This evidence was reinforced by the landmark CIBIS-II study, which showed a 44 % reduction in SCD compared with placebo.20 Further, the CIBIS-III trial revealed that initiating treatment with bisoprolol was associated with a 46 % reduction in SCD during the first year compared to other treatment strategies.24

Clinical outcomes observed in Indian patients are consistent with international data, showing notable improvements in mortality and HF symptom control. However, despite their proven efficacy, β-blockers remain underutilized or underdosed in many Indian settings due to factors such as limited physician awareness, monitoring challenges, and polypharmacy in patients with multiple comorbidities.29

Addressing these barriers is essential. Increasing physician awareness and reinforcing guideline-directed medical therapy could significantly enhance the use of β-blockers in routine care. Their incorporation into standard HF treatment algorithms has been shown to deliver meaningful clinical improvements, emphasizing the need for a more systematic, evidence-based approach to HF management in the Indian context.29

The Indian consensus by Chopra et al. highlights sympathetic overdrive as a key therapeutic target across heart failure phenotypes, reinforcing the role of β-blockers, specifically bisoprolol.30,31

Coronary artery disease (CAD)

β-blockers are a cornerstone in the management of CAD due to their well-documented clinical benefits. A substantial body of evidence supports their ability to reduce the risk of adverse CV outcomes following acute MI, including myocardial ischemia, reinfarction, and all-cause mortality. Large-scale cohort studies evaluating the timing of oral β-blocker initiation have demonstrated a clear advantage for early administration. Compared to delayed initiation, early oral β-blocker therapy is associated with significantly lower in-hospital mortality (odds ratio [OR] = 0.41; 95 % CI: 0.21–0.80) and reduced incidence of severe LV dysfunction (OR = 0.57; 95 % CI: 0.42–0.78). These findings are corroborated by results from multiple clinical trials.

Based on this robust evidence base, the ESC recommends initiating oral β-blockers within the first 24 h of symptom onset in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), unless contraindications such as heart failure, a low-output state, or cardiogenic shock are present. For patients with non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI), oral β-blocker therapy is similarly recommended in the absence of contraindications. Although the long-term prognostic benefit of β-blockers instable angina remains uncertain, they continue to be widely prescribed for symptom relief and secondary prevention.

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)

Among β-blockers, bisoprolol is frequently prescribed in the post-ACS setting, as highlighted by an intervention review from the National Guideline Centre at the Royal College of Physicians. In a study by Maclean et al, early administration of low dose bisoprolol, initiated within 4 h of hospital presentation was associated with a markedly lower incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) during hospitalization in patients with NSTEMI. Specifically, patients receiving early bisoprolol experienced significantly fewer events, including ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac death, and recurrent MI (1 vs 27 events; p = 0.005) (Table 4).1,32

The 2019 ESC guidelines recommend β-blockers as first-line agents for relieving angina, particularly in patients with prior MI or elevated resting HR. Spotlight on β-blockers in CAD management in Indian clinical practice

The 2023 Asia–Pacific Delphi consensus emphasized the critical role of β-blockers across the CVD continuum, especially in CAD management.1 In ACS, early initiation of β-blockers, ideally within 24 h of symptom onset is strongly recommended in patients with ST-elevation or non-ST-elevation MI, provided there are no contraindications such as acute heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or low cardiac output.

Evidence for early β-blocker use has evolved significantly. Older trials such as COMMIT (2005)34 showed that while intravenous metoprolol reduced reinfarction and arrhythmic deaths, it increased cardiogenic shock risk in unstable patients, yielding no mortality benefit. Later reperfusion-era studies, including METOCARD-CNIC (2013)35 and EARLY-BAMI (2016),36 demonstrated that early pre-reperfusion metoprolol in hemodynamically stable anterior STEMI safely reduced infarct size and improved left ventricular function. These data support selective, rather than routine, early β-blocker use.

Recent NEJM44 and ESC data have further clarified post–MI β-blocker roles in patients with preserved or mildly reduced ejection fraction. The ABYSS45 and REBOOT/BETAMI/DANBLOCK trials46, 47, 48 suggest that discontinuing long-term β-blockers in stable post–MI patients (LVEF ≥40 %) offers no advantage and may slightly worsen outcomes, whereas benefit in preserved EF (>50 %) remains uncertain and largely hypothesis-generating.

Although the ideal duration of therapy beyond the acute phase remains uncertain for patients without heart failure, current evidence supports continued β-blocker use for up to three years post–MI.49 In chronic stable ischemic heart disease, long-term β-blocker therapy improves ischemic control, reduces reinfarction risk, and enhances survival outcomes. For those with stable angina but no prior MI, β-blockers remain first-line agents for symptom relief and reduction of ischemic burden.

Among available options, bisoprolol is widely used in real-world Indian practice, both in post-ACS and stable CAD populations, especially in primary care settings. Of additional relevance to the Indian subcontinent, a large population-based study from Asia highlighted that β1-selective blockers significantly reduced the risk of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events in patients with cirrhosis who experienced an acute MI.1

Tachyarrhythmia

Beyond their well-established role in BP control and myocardial protection, β-blockers are pivotal in managing cardiac arrhythmias, particularly in high-risk CV populations. Their ability to modulate autonomic tone and suppress arrhythmic triggers has made them a cornerstone of rhythm and rate control strategies.54

The foundational CORIDS study highlighted resting HR as an independent predictor of all-cause and CV mortality, emphasizing HR reduction as a modifiable prognostic target effectively addressed through β-blockade.55 This was further supported by findings from Perk et al (2003), which showed that elevated resting HR was associated with increased mortality in elderly women but not in men, underscoring potential sex-specific prognostic relevance and therapeutic implications for β-blocker use in elderly females.56

The use of β-blockers in both supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias is firmly endorsed in contemporary European and American clinical guidelines.54 Their efficacy has been substantiated across a spectrum of large, randomized trials and post-hoc analyses, which have demonstrated consistent benefits in ventricular rate control, reduction in arrhythmic episodes, and improvements in clinical outcomes, including mortality (Table 6).

Indian perspective and practical insights

Although global evidence firmly establishes β-blockers as foundational in cardiovascular care, their application in India warrants a nuanced view. Indian patients often present with early-onset hypertension, high sympathetic tone, and multiple comorbidities, favoring the use of β1-selective agents such as bisoprolol and nebivolol. National expert surveys and consensus statements continue to endorse their role, particularly in hyperadrenergic or post-ischemic states.

In heart failure, Indian real-world data mirror international trials, yet suboptimal dosing and therapeutic inertia remain challenges. Strengthening awareness, follow-up systems, and guideline adherence could enhance outcomes. In post–MI and ACS, β-blocker initiation is sometimes delayed due to late presentation and limited PCI access, underscoring the need for hemodynamically guided but timely therapy.

From a health-system standpoint, cost and availability remain decisive. Generic β-blockers, especially bisoprolol offer excellent affordability, making them an accessible and sustainable option across diverse socioeconomic settings.

Taken together, Indian clinicians balance strong global evidence with practical realities of cost, access, and comorbidity. This interplay between science and pragmatism defines contemporary β-blocker practice in India and underlines the need for more structured real-world outcome research.

Conclusion

β-blockers continue to serve as cornerstone agents in cardiovascular medicine, offering robust clinical benefits across a wide range of conditions, from hypertension and ischemic heart disease to arrhythmias and heart failure. Advances in β-blocker pharmacology, particularly the development of cardioselective agents like bisoprolol, have enabled more individualized and better-tolerated therapeutic approaches. Contemporary evidence supports their early but selective use in acute coronary syndromes and confirms strong prognostic benefit in HFrEF, while findings in HFpEF remain hypothesis-generating and not yet guideline-directed. In the Indian context, the adoption of β-blockers continues to expand, driven by growing awareness, international guideline support, consensus recommendations, accumulating real-world evidence and cost-effectiveness. As treatment paradigms evolve, β-blockers are poised to remain integral to the comprehensive management of cardiovascular risk and disease across global and Indian practice settings.
