Effect of genetic polymorphism of rosuvastatin transporter gene rs2231137 on its clinical efficacy and safety
Authors: Mahjabeen Sharif, Kulsoom Farhat, Mudassar Noor, Muhammad Bilal Siddique, Ayesha Tariq, Raja Kamran Afzal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rosuvastatin is considered as the cornerstone therapy for primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases. Although clinical efficacy of rosuvastatin is well established, but wide inter-individual variations have been observed in its plasma levels, lipid lowering efficacy and adverse effects. So the current study was undertaken to evaluate the impact of genetic polymorphism of rosuvastatin efflux transporters on variable bioavailability, clinical efficacy and adverse effects. For this study, three hundred and eightyfour hyperlipidemic patients with low density lipoprotein more than 130 mg/dl and serum creatine phosphokinase level within normal range > 190 U/L were enrolled. Study design was quasi experimental and non-probability purposive sampling was done for recruitment of patients. It was conducted from June 2022 to December 2023 in two tertiary care hospitals of Pakistan. All the enrolled patients were treated with rosuvastatin (10 mg) once daily for 12 weeks. Fasting lipid profile, serum creatine phosphokinase, liver and renal function tests were measured at the start of study. After 12 weeks of intervention with rosuvastatin, biochemical tests, genotyping and plasma rosuvastatin levels were determined. Frequency of ABCG2 34 C > T polymorphism for wild type CC, heterozygous mutant CT and homozygous mutant TT genotypes were 55.2, 35.9 and 8.9% respectively. Patients with TT and CT have significantly higher plasma levels of rosuvastatin with mean value of 30.37 ± 6.4 ng/mL and 22.27 ± 4.68 ng/mL respectively as compared to CC genotypes 15.57 ± 8.94 ng/mL (p = < 0.001) which ultimately has improved lipid lowering efficacy. After 12 weeks of intervention, rosuvastatin significantly reduced total cholesterol (mean % decrease: CC 23.04 ± 10.05%, CT 37.43 ± 7.64%, TT 41.32 ± 6.97%, p = < 0.001), low density lipoprotein (mean % decrease: CC 31.11 ± 11.6% CT 47.27 ± 8.22% TT 53.29 ± 8.03%, p = < 0.001), very low density lipoprotein (mean% decrease: CC 36.23 ± 13.27%, CT 44.41 ± 10.29%, TT 54.5 ± 9.63%, p = < 0.001), triglycerides (mean% decrease: CC 24.34 ± 12.66%, TC 35.2 ± 10.47%, TT35.94 ± 8.6%, p = < 0.001) and high density lipoproteins (mean% increase: CC 9.19 ± 10.29%, CT 16.51 ± 9.33%, TT 23.10 ± 8.32%, p = < 0.001). Patients with TT and CT genotype has greater improvement in lipid profile compared with CC genotypes. Frequency of myopathy and hepatotoxicity in study population was 5.2 and 3.13% respectively. We conclude that rosuvastatin 10 mg is highly efficacious and well tolerated by most of the patients, however genotype profiling should be done before initiating rosuvastatin therapy to reduce the risk of adverse effects and to improve the compliance.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are the foremost cause of mortality and morbidity around the globe. Hyperlipidemia is one of the main risk factors that contributes to the development of ischemic heart disease. Main cause of ischemic heart disease is atherosclerosis leading to narrowing of vessel lumen which ultimately enhances the ischemic vascular events. Timely diagnosis and treatment of atherosclerosis can reduce serious outcomes of cardiovascular diseases1,2.

Worldwide prevalence of hyperlipidemia in males is 37% and 40% in females and in South Asia, it is about 30.3%3. Statins are considered as the first line therapy for reducing low density lipoprotein (LDL-c) which ultimately decreases the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke4,5. Amongst all statins, rosuvastatin has the longest half life and better efficacy. It is highly effective hydroxyl methyl glutaryl coenzyme A (HMGCO-A) reductase inhibitor, so it inhibits cholesterol biosynthesis in liver. Decrease in intracellular cholesterol ultimately increases the cell surface LDL-c receptors leading to increased internalization and degradation of circulating LDL-c. Studies have shown that rosuvastatin has greater LDL-c lowering effect as compared to other statins. It also decreases total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TGs), very low density lipoproteins(VLDL-c) and elevates high density lipoproteins (HDL-c)2,6. Rosuvastatin also has shown a wide range of pleiotropic effects including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory and anti-oxidant effects. It also improves endothelial function & plaque stability and retards platelet aggregation and progression of coronary atherosclerosis7.

It is administered orally and once daily dose is recommended. Its oral absorption is 50% and has elimination half life of 19 h8,9. Studies have shown that Asian population taking rosuvastatin have higher plasma levels with 70% bioavailability as compared to European population with 20% bioavailability6. Only 10% of dose undergoes phase 1 metabolism by cytochrome 2C9 and 2C19 and 90% of drug is excreted unchanged in faeces10,11.

As rosuvastatin is minimally metabolized, so it solely depends on its transporters for its absorption and bioavailability especially breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) encoded by gene ATP binding cassette G2 family (ABCG2). BCRP transporter is highly expressed in enterocytesand hepatocytes12,13. Being an efflux transporter, it inhibits the absorption of rosuvastatin from intestines and in liver it pumps out rosuvastatin from hepatocytes and enhances its faecal excretion through biliary route. ABCG2 34 C > T genetic polymorphism is present in chromosome 4:89061114. This SNP is present in exon 2 where valine is replaced by methionine at codon 12 which results in reduced protein expression of BCRP transporter, causing increased rosuvastatin intestinal absorption and decreased biliary excretion so enhancing the plasma levels and therapeutic efficacy of rosuvastatin11.

Although lipid lowering efficacy of rosuvastatin has been well established, but inter-individual variations in bioavailability, personalised response to lipid lowering efficacy and adverse effects have been observed in different ethnic groups of the same population. Studies have revealed that these inter-individual variations may be due to genetic variations in efflux transporters of rosuvastatin, especially SLCO1B1 and ABCG2 14. Due to variable responses to rosuvastatin therapy, a wide spectrum of muscular symptoms ranges from asymptomatic rise in creatine phosphokinase (CPK) to severe myopathy and rhabdomyolysis has been observed in patients. Statin induced myopathy (SIM) is multifactorial. High dose, higher plasma rosuvastatin levels, liver, kidney diseases and genetic polymorphism in rosuvastatin efflux transporters are the main risk factors for the development of SIM. Muscular pain is the major cause of discontinuation of rosuvastatin therapy which results in poor compliance which ultimately increases the burden of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality15.

As only 10% of rosuvastatin is metabolized so genetic variability in CYP2C9 and 2C19 has minimal impact on its pharmacokinetics. Rosuvastatin is a hydrophilic drug so for its intestinal absorption, it solely depends upon two main drug transporters which are OATP1B1 and BCRP encoded by SLCO1B1 and ABCG2 genes respectively. Impact of genetic polymorphism of SLCO1B1 on rosuvastatin pharmacokinetics has been extensively studied in different populations including Pakistani population14. But the influence of genetic variability of ABCG2 34 C > T on rosuvastatin pharmacokinetics, its efficacy and safety is yet to be determined in Pakistani population. Extrapolation of data from different population is always unreliable. This study aims to fill this gap and was undertaken to find out the frequency of genetic polymorphism of ABCG2 34 C > T (rs2231137) and its possible influence on plasma rosuvastatin levels, clinical efficacy and rosuvastatin induced adverse effects especially myopathy and hepatic toxicity in hyperlipidemic patients. Unveiling genetic determinant of rosuvastatin efficacy and safety in Pakistani cohort will allow health care professionals to tailor and optimize the treatment regimen for hyperlipidemic patients.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for study protocols

This was a quasi experimental study carried out from June 2022 to December 2023. Study was conducted in accordance with Current Good Clinical Practices (cGCP) according to Helsinki Declaration in two tertiary care hospitals of Rawalpindi and Islamabad. All the study protocols used for this research were approved by Institutional Review Committee of National University of Medical Sciences Rawalpindi (Reference letter no: IERB/9/2/R& D/2022/197). Written informed consent was taken from all study participants before enrollment. Quanto software was used for calculation of sample size by keeping the minor allele frequency (MAF) of ABCG2 34 C > T (rs2231137) at 0.30, power 80% and p ≤ 0.05 as significant. The sample size was calculated to be 384 patients16.

Study participants

Study participants were selected through non probability purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria for recruitment include: (1) both male, female patients aged 30–70 years (2) elevated LDL-c levels more than 130 mg/dl (3.4 mmoles/L) (3) have normal serum CPK levels (< 190 U/L), normal liver and renal function tests17. Exclusion criteria included (1) those patients taking antihyperlipidemic drugs other than rosuvastatin, or any other drug known to interact with rosuvastatin (2) patients suffering from hypothyroidism, hepatic and renal diseases (4) patients with any muscular disease or muscular pain of unknown origin and with raised serum CPK levels > 190 U/L (4) Pregnant and nursing females18.

Data collection

Patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were enrolled for this study. Informed consent was taken from every patient. All the patients received rosuvastatin (10 mg) once a day for 12 weeks19. At day 0, demographic data related to age, gender, weight, height and body mass index (BMI) were recorded along with medical history of co-morbid diseases like hepertension, diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease. 5 ml blood was drawn from each study participant for biochemical analysis which include total lipid profile (TC, LDL-c, VLDL-c, TGs, HDL-c) serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK), liver function tests (LFTs), renal function tests (RFTs), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) on day 0 and after 12 weeks of intervention with rosuvastatin, 10 ml blood was drawn for biochemical analysis, genotyping and for estimation of rosuvastatin plasma levels at steady state (through HPLC) in ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) containg tubes. For genotyping blood was kept at 4 °C until analysis. To determine plasma levels of rosuvastatin, centrifugation of the blood of each study participant was done at 3000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 10 min to separate plasma from blood cells and then plasma samples were stored in 1.5 ml aliquots at −80 °C for further analysis20.

Criteria for assessment of severity of myopathy

Statin induced myopathy is defined as muscular pain or weakness with creatine phosphokinase (CPK) level more than 10 times the upper normal limit. Severity of myopathy with rosuvastatin was assessed on the basis of following statin related myotoxicity (SRM 0) phenotype classification.

SRM 0: CPK < 4 X ULN (upper limit of normal) with no muscular symptoms. SRM 1. No CPK rise with mild muscular symptoms. SRM2: CPK < 4XULN with intolerable myalgias. SRM3: patientsts having CPK > 4 X UNL-<10XUNL with myopathy. SRM4: CPK > 10XULN-<50XULN with severe myopathy. SRM 5 & 6: patients with rhabdomyolysis and autoimmune mediated necrotizing myositis21,22.

Criteria used for severity/grading of rosuvastatin induced liver injury is as follows

Statin induced liver injury is defined as an increase in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 3 times higher than the upper normal limit (grade 2 liver injury is clinically significant). Criteria for assessing the severity of drug induced liver injury is as follows:

Grade 0: Total bilirubin within normal range, serum alanine transaminase (ALT) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) < 1.25XUNL Grade1: Bilirubin > 1-1.5XULN, ALT and ALP values ranges between 1.25–2.25.5XULN Grade 2: Total bilirubin > 1.5–2.5.5XULN, ALT and ALP > 2.5–5.5.0XULN. Grade 3: Total bilirubin > 2.5-5XULN, ALT and ALP > 5-10XULN Grade 4: Total bilirubin > 5XULN, ALT and ALP > 10XULN23.

Criteria for severity/grading of acute kidney injury

Drug induced acute renal injury (AKI) is defined as increase in serum creatinine 1.5 times higher than upper normal limit. Criteria for assessing the severity of rosuvastatin induced AKI is as follows.

Grade1 AKI: Creatinine 1.5-2XUNL Grade2 AKI: Creatinine 2-3XUNL Grade 3 AKI: creatinine3XUNL or > 3->4XUNL. Grade 4 AKI: Life threatening consequences & dialysis required24.

DNA extraction and genotyping of ABCG2 34 C > T (rs2231137)

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200µL of whole blood from each patient by using chelex method and was stored at −20 °C for further analysis25,26. Extracted genomic DNA was quantified by using Nanodrop 2000c (Thermo-Fischer Scientific, USA). For genotyping of single nucleotide polymorphism of ABCG2 34 C > T (rs2231137) Amplification Refractory Mutation System- Polymerase Chain Reaction (TETRA-ARMS-PCR) was used. For simultaneous detection of two alleles in a single reaction, four primers were designed which included two T and C allele specific primers (forward inner and reverse inner) with sequences AGCCATTGGTGTTTCCTTGTGAAAT, CCAGTAATGTCGAAGTTTTTATCCAAG having amplification product size of 233 bp and 179 bp respectively and two common forward outer and reverse outer primers with sequences TTCTTTCTCAACTGGTTTTCGACAAG and GATGTTCTTATCACAATGGTATGGGC having amplification product size of 360. Reconstitution of all the four primers of TETRA-ARM-PCR procedure was done according to manufacturer’s guidelines and finally 5 pmol/µl of primer mix was prepared. For detection of mutant alleles, 25 µl of PCR reaction mixture was prepared in 0.2 ml PCR tubes by adding 01 µl of primer mixture, 12.5 µl master mix (containing DNA polymerases, dNTPs, MgCl2 and Taq buffer), 8.5 µl PCR grade water and 3 µl extracted DNA. Gradient PCR was carried out in Thermal Cycler Scientific (ThermoFisher, USA) to determine the best annealing temperature for each primer set. Final annealing temperature was optimized at 60 °C for 35 cycles for respective primers via gradient PCR. Optimized PCR program consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min followed by 32 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 45 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min25,27.

PCR product was then subjected to gel electrophoresis by using 2% agarose gel in which 20 µl of amplified DNA was loaded in the wells of agarose gel and 50 bp DNA marker (ThermoScientific, Lithuania) was applied in one of the wells. 90 volts of power was supplied, until complete separation of DNA bands which were later on visulized by using Gel Documentation System (SYNGENE, USA). Variant alleles were identified according to the size of DNA fragments and are shown in Fig. 128. For PCR method validation, non-template controls and positive known controls were run on agarose gel28.

Determination of plasma rosuvastatin concentration

HPLC method was developed and validated for quantification of plasma rosuvastatin in accordance with International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) guidelines.

Method validation for estimation of plasma rosuvastatin concentration

Analytical method for estimation of rosuvastatin in plasma was validated with respect to various parameters like linearity, accuracy, precision, sensitivity, limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ). Linearity of method was established by spiking a series of standard solutions of rosuvastatin with different concentrations of 0.02 µg, 0.05 µg, 0.1 µg, 0.5 µg, 1 µg and 2.5 µg. Calibration curve for standard solutions was obtained by plotting concentration on x-axis and response on y-axis. The response was linear over a concentration range of 0.02 µg/mL to 2.5 µg/mL. Linearity of response was found to be highly satisfactory for rosuvastatin as demonstrated by regression coefficient (r2) which was 0.9966. Precision was assessed by performing three replicate injections of rosuvastatin of blank and spiked plasma under optimized chromatographic conditions and repeatability of retention time and then peak area was determined. Precision was measured in terms of percent relative standard deviation (% RSD), which was calculated as ratio of standard deviation to mean, times 100. For determination of intermediate precision (intra-day and interday reproducibility), analysis of four spiked plasma samples were carried out by using four different concentrations three times a day over three consecutive days and mean ± SD and % RSD were determined. Analytical methods for determination of plasma rosuvastatin concentration

Plasma rosuvastatin levels were determined by using Knauer K-1001 High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC, Germany) connected with ultra violet (UV) detector. Plasma samples were first thawed at room temperature followed by vortexing for 2 min. Solid phase extraction (SPE) was done by using 3 cc cartridges. 1 ml methanol was used for conditioning of cartridges. 500 µL of plasma sample was loaded on these conditioned cartridges. From SPE cartridges, rosuvastatin was eluted with 1 ml of eluent which consisted of 0.5% glacial acetic acid in methanol. Evaporation of eluent was done by using nitrogen gas. In 100 µL of mobile phase this residue was dissolved. These samples were analyzed for determination of rosuvastatin quantity in plasma by using HPLC29. Octa Decyl-Silica (ODS-3) C18 column (5 μm x 4.6 × 250 mm, GL Sciences Inc, Japan) was used for all the experiments with flow rate of 0.8 mL/min for 30 min run time in isocratic mode. Various experiments were performed with multiple mobile ratios to optimize the conditions. Best separation was achieved with mobile phase of 40:60 ratio of water (with 0.2% formic acid) to acetonitrile. Detector wave length of 254 nm was used and injection volume of 10 µL was used for all the analytical processes. All the experiments were performed at room temperature6,29.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed by using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 25. For continuous data, mean ± standard deviation (SD) was calculated. Categorical data was represented as frequencies and percentages. Genotype and allele frequencies were calculated by using online Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) calculator and p value of more than 0.05 indicated that observed alleles were consistent with HWE assumptions. Normality of data was determined by applying Kolmogorov- Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk test. Levene ‘s Test was used to assess the homogeneity of variances. For normally distributed data with homogenous variance, paired t-tests and One-Way ANOVA followed by Post-Hoc Tukey test was used. To detect significant differences between baseline and post-treatment values of demographic and biochemical parameters, paired t-tests were used. Frequency differences in genotype and demographic data was compared by Chi Square Test and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Post Hoc Tuckey Test. For grading of severity of rosuvastatin induced myopathy, hepatic and renal injury, chi square test was applied. For control of type 1 error at 5% for multiple comparisons, bonferroni correction was applied. Association of ABCG2 34 C > T genotypes with variable plasma rosuvastatin levels and clinical responsiveness were analyzed by using One Way ANOVA followed by Post Hoc Tuckey Test and binary logistic regression analysis. p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

There was a statistically significant difference (p = < 0.001*) between baseline levels and post treatment values of TC, LDL-c, VLDL-c, TGs and HDL-c in all genotype groups. Mean reduction in LDL-c which is the primary target of rosuvastatin was 63.01 ± 26.29 mg/mL in all the patients. Discussion

This study investigated the effect of genetic polymorphisms of ABCG2 34 C > T (rs2231137) on plasma levels, lipid-lowering efficacy and possible adverse effects of rosuvastatin in hyperlipidemic patients. Our study participants carrying ABCG2 34 C > T TT and CT genotype showed significantly higher plasma rosuvastatin levels subsequently causing greater percentage improvement in lipid profile as compared to patients with wild type CC genotype.

Our results revealed that genotype frequencies of ABCG2 C > T were mostly of wild type CC (55.2%) while percentage frequency of heterozygous mutant CT and homozygous mutant TT genotype are 35.9 and 8.9% respectively and minor allele frequency is 27%. This polymorphism was also studied in Polish population and frequency of wild type CC genotype was found to be 92.1% and heterozygous mutant CT 7.9% while no mutant homozygous genotype TT was found in this population17. Consistent with our results, a study conducted on Saudia Arabian population revealed that frequencies of CC, CT and TT genotype were 63.2, 33, and 3.8% respectively30. Ing and his co-workers found that in Malaysian population, percentage frequencies of CC, CT and TT were 45.3, 38.7 and 16% respectively31. Frequency of this polymorphism in Indian population was studied by Singh and his co-workers, they reported frequencies 58.6% for CC, 38.4% for CT and 3% for TT genotypes32. In contrast to these studies a study conducted on Iranean population revealed the presence of only wild type CC allele (100% frequency) while CT and TT variant genotypes were not present (0% frequency) in population28. Another study conducted on Iranean population revealed 31.8% CC wild genotype and 68.2% CT genotype while TT genotype was absent in population33.

After 12 weeks of intervention with rosuvastatin, variable plasma drug levels were reported in study participants and mean plasma concentration was 18.8 ± 9.0 ng/dL. We found that patients with homozygous mutant genotypes TT had the highest mean plasma concentration of 30.37 ± 6.4ng/mL followed by patients with heterozygous mutant genotype CT 22.27 ± 4.68 ng/mL and with wild type CC alleles 15.57 ± 8.94ng/mL There was statistically significant difference (p < 0.001) in mean plasma levels of patients with TT, CT and CC genotypes. This inter individual variability in mean plasma rosuvastatin levels may be due to genetic variant of ABCG2 34 C > T which alters the function of BCRP efflux transporter leading to variable bioavailability and plasma levels of rosuvastatin. Normal function of BCRP is to limit the intestinal absorption and increases the hepatic elimination of rosuvastatin. Non synonymous ABCG2 34 C > T variant is present on exon 2 of ABCG2 gene in which 34 C > T (rs2231137) transversion causes replacement of amino acid valine by methionine at codon 12. This alteration in amino acid results in reduced protein expression of BCRP transporter and hence its altered function leads to variable intestinal absorption and plasma levels of rosuvastatin34.

Our findings are in accordance with the results of a study in which after administration of single dose of 10 mg rosuvastatin maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of patients carrying CC and CT genotypes were 8.1 ± 1.8, 8.1 ± 1.3 ng/mL but it was significantly raised to 16.9 ± 6.0 ng/ml in patients with homozygous mutant TT genotypes indicating that ABCG2 34 C > T has a significant role in rosuvastatin biodisposition34. Consistent with our results is another study, in which plasma rosuvastatin concentration of Asians were compared with Caucasians. It was found that Chinese and Japanese carrying variant alleles of ABCG2 34 C > T and 421 C > A had greater Cmax and area under curve compared with Caucasians35. As this polymorphism is prevalent in Asians and is associated with higher plasma rosuvastatin levels, so United State Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has recommended to use low dose of rosuvastatin in Ascian population to avoid the possible adverse effects of rosuvastatin therapy10. In contrast to our findings, a study conducted on Chinese population revealed that ABCG2 34 C > T polymorphism did not significantly affect pharmacokinetics and steady state concentration of rosuvastatin36. Consistent with these findings is another study, in which no significant differences were found in Cmax of three different genotypes of ABCG2 34 C > T suggesting the role of other factors for inter-individual variation in Cmax of rosuvastatin37.

Rosuvastatin significantly reduced TC, LDL-c, VLDL-c, TGs, and increased the levels of HDL-c after 12 weeks of therapy. Mean percent changes in TC, LDL-c, VLDL-c, TGs and HDL-c were − 28.9 ± 11.83, −37.87 ± 13.56, −40.28 ± 13.4, −28.85 ± 12.9 and + 12.5 ± 10.86% respectively. When the mean percent changes of all the parameters of lipid profile were compared between wild-type and variant genotypes after 12 weeks of treatment, patients with TT and CT genotypes showed the greater percentage reduction in TC, LDL-c, VLDL-c, TGs and greater rise in HDL-c levels compared those with wild-type CC genotype. So our results revealed that rosuvastatin is more effective in hyperlipidemic patients carrying mutant genotypes TT and CT when compared with CC genotype. Our findings suggest the possible association of ABCG2 34 C > T polymorphism with elevated plasma levels leading to enhanced lipid lowering efficacy. So our study revealed that ABCG2 34 C > T may be one of the potential predictors of lipid response to rosuvastatin therapy. As there is substitution of methionine in place of valine at codon 12 which confers alteration in protein expression and function of BCRP transporter. Rosuvastatin is the substrate of this transporter and genetic polymorphism in ABCG2 alters the function of BCRP transporter which in turn enhances rosuvastatin biodisposition and might have affected efficacy and toxicity of rosuvastatin.

Our results are in accordance with a study in which, when 10 mg rosuvastatin was administered for 8 weeks to Korean patients, mean %age changes in TC, LDL-c, TGs and HDL-c were − 29.6 ± 1.0, −44.9 ± 1.4, −10.0 ± 24 and 6.0 ± 1.1% respectively38. In a recent study conducted on Indian population, it was found that rosuvastatin 20 mg reduced TC, LDL-c, and TGs by −22.6, −29.4 and − 25.4% respectively and increased HDL-c by 4.6%. Author also compared the mean % age changes in lipid parameters after administration of 20 mg rosuvastatin, 20 mg atorvastatin and 20 mg pravastatin and found that rosuvastatin was most efficacious in improving the lipid parameters as compared to atorvastatin and pravastatin probably due to its enhanced HMGCo-A reductase inhibiting properties39. In line with these findings, is another study in which rosuvastatin 10 mg caused 46% reduction in LDL-c which is the primary target of rosuvastatin therapy2. In Pakistani hyperlipidemic patients same dose of rosuvastatin reduced LDL-c by 34.15 mg/dL40.

Minor rise in CPK levels were recorded after 12 weeks of intervention. In 80.7% patients, CPK levels remained within the normal range. A wide spectrum of muscular symptoms were observed ranging from no muscular symptoms to severe myopathy. Criteria for severe myopathy is defined as when CPK levels rises 10 times the upper normal limit with severe myalgias41. SRM4 is considered as clinically significant myopathy and most of the patients with SRM4 were having either TT and CT genotypes. Our results showed that possible underlying mechanism of myopathy in variant allele carriers TT and CT may be due to polymorphism of ABCG2 34 C > T which leads to decreased function of BCRP efflux transporter which ultimately increases the rosuvastatin intestinal absorption and decreases its biliary excretion leading to increase plasma concentration which predisposes to myopathy due to impaired protein prenylation,, altered cell membrane fluidity due to reduced cholesterol content in skeletal muscle cell membrane, increased oxidative stress and apoptosis in skeletal muscles22.

Incidence of myopathy varies in different ethnic populations. In our study incidence of myopathy was 5.2%. A study conducted on Jordanian population revealed dose dependent statin induced muscular symptoms as incidence of myalgias with 10 and 20 mg rosuvastatin was found to be 10.8 and 14.6% respectively42. In another study, frequencies of myalgias in Pakistani population with rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and simvastatin were 3.2, 11.3 and 27.5% respectively. Higher incidence of myopathy with other statin compared with rosuvastatin may be due to hydrophilicity of rosuvastatin and hence less permeation in muscles43. A study conducted by Zakria and his co-workers revealed slightly higher frequency of myalgias in which 23% population developed muscular symptoms after receiving 10 mg of rosuvastatin39. In some observational studies, the incidence of myopathy with rosuvastatin was found to be 10–15%44.

After 12 weeks of therapy, there was minor rise in total bilirubin, ALT and ALP and 85.7% of study patients had all the liver enzymes within the normal range. According to latest guidelines of American College of Gastroenterology, raised levels of LFTs < 2x ULN are considered as borderline and statins should be continued without hesitation. Drug induced hepatic injury is significant only if ALT rises 3 times the upper limit of normal45,46. Only 12 (3.13%) patients in this study have ALT levels more than 3 times the upper normal limit (grade 2 liver injury). Higher incidence rate of rosuvastatin induced hepatic injury was observed in patients with TT and CT genotypes. Raised levels of LFTs may be due to alteration in lipid content of cell membrane of hepatocytes leading to increased permeability and leakage of liver enzymes47. In line with our findings, is another study in which only 1% of study participants had ALT levels more than 3XUNL with rosuvastatin therapy47. Consistent with these results, is another study in which raised levels of LFTS were observed with 20 mg rosuvastatin. Liver enzymes were slightly elevated but were less than 2 times the upper limit of normal and these derangements in liver enzymes were found to be statistically insignificant. Author further reported that rosuvastatin is a hydrophilic drug and minimally metabolized in liver, so it causes clinically insignificant rise in LFTs45.

In majority of study participants, post treatment values of renal function tests remained within the normal range. Drug induced acute renal injury becomes clinically significant when creatinine rises 1.5XULN48. Frequency of rosuvastatin induced acute renal injury in this study was 6.5%. which was mostly observed in patients of TT and CT genotypes. Our results are in accordance with previous clinical studies in which little or no significant renal adverse effects were reported except for few rare studies in which rhabdomyolysis was developed with very high doses and prolong use of rosuvastatin44. A case report revealed that when 80 mg rosuvastatin was administered for 14 months in a patient, all renal function tests were deranged, mild hematuria and albuminurea was also observed. Kidney biopsy revealed acute tubular injury. All these changes were reversed with discontinuation of rosuvastatin49. Studies have reported that during rosuvastatin biotransformation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress is produced which leads to minor elevation of liver and renal enzymes50. Another possible mechanism is suggested by ward and his colleagues is that inhibition of mevalonate metabolic pathway changes the lipid constituent of hepatic and renal cell membranes which alters its permeability and ultimately increases the leakage of liver and renal enzymes44,51.

To our knowledge, this was the first study conducted on Pakistani patients which demonstrated that genetic polymorphism of ABCG2 34 C > T can alter the functions of BCRP efflux transporter so increases the plasma rosuvastatin level and lipid lowering efficacy. Patients carrying variant genotypes TT, CT have improved lipid profile after 12 weeks of therapy with very low incidence of myopathy, hepatic and renal adverse effects with 10 mg rosuvastatin. It is therefore recommended that physician should not deter from prescribing rosuvastatin. Genotype screening should be done before initiating rosuvastatin therapy for dose tailoring, to get maximum therapeutic benefit and to mitigate the risk of rosuvastatin adverse effects which will ultimately decrease the cardiovascular disease burden in South Asians. It will also identify patients with inadequate response to rosuvastatin and will help in developing novel ways for optimizing the drug therapy and maximizing its efficacy.

The current study has certain limitations as impact of only one transporter SNP was investigated which may overlook other potential genetic variations that could influence rosuvastatin treatment outcomes. Only Pakistani subjects were recruited in this research study which further limits the generalization of our findings on other populations. Moreover gender based variations in rosuvastatin efficacy and toxicity were not studied in this research study. Absence of placebo control group might introduce response bias in clinical assessment and treatment outcome of rosuvastatin.

Conclusion

Rosuvastatin 10 mg is highly efficacious and well tolerated by most of the patients. Our results suggest that ABCG2 34 C > T polymorphism significantly influenced rosuvastatin plasma levels, lipid lowering efficacy and adverse effects. ABCG2 34 C > T variant genotypes TT and CT are associated with higher plasma levels and greater lipid lowering effect compared with wild CC genotypes. To get maximum clinical benefit and to decrease the incidence of adverse effects, genetic profiling should be done before starting rosuvastatin therapy for dose tailoring in patients carrying variant genotypes of ABCG2C > T. Genetic variations studied in this study will help in developing personalized medicine for the treatment of hyperlipidemia.
