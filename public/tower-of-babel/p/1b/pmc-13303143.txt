Longitudinal Variability of Wearable-Derived Sleep and Heart Rate as a Digital Biomarker for Early Detection of Mild Cognitive Impairment
Authors: Hyunsuk Kim, Jin Young Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study evaluated the potential of wearable-derived longitudinal data for early detection of mild cognitive impairment (MCI). We examined whether variability in nocturnal sleep and heart rate (HR) patterns reflect autonomic nervous system instability associated with cognitive decline.Materials and MethodsNocturnal data from 162 participants (111 cognitively normal; 51 MCI) were collected via smart rings over an average of 70 days. To mitigate signal dilution from long-term averaging (LTA), we derived variability-based features (including measures of dispersion, distributional properties, and time-series indices) and compared them to mean-based models. Logistic regression models were constructed through stepwise integration and validated with cross-validation. Key predictors were identified using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsMean-only models showed limited discrimination. In contrast, variability-focused models improved performance, with the model that integrated extended mean, distributional, and time-series features achieving an area under the curve of 0.861 and sensitivity of 0.820. LTA analyses revealed instability of mean effects (signed Cohen’s d changed in magnitude/direction across 35–70-day windows), supporting a shift away from long-window means. SHAP identified HR_drop variability (short-term variability, mean absolute deviation-based distributional instability, time-bin fluctuation) as top contributors, while sleep–behavior rhythm (e.g., median/trimmed mean of daily sleep count) and circadian-timing features (e.g., mode of sleep midpoint, variability in the timing of the lowest HR) provided complementary information.ConclusionLongitudinal wearable monitoring suggests that variability in sleep and HR patterns can capture subtle signs of cognitive decline more effectively than conventional, mean-based measures. These variability-based digital biomarkers may support early MCI screening and personalized intervention strategies.Keywords: Mild cognitive impairment, digital biomarkers, wearable electronic devices, sleep, heart rate

INTRODUCTION

The potential of wearable devices and smart rings

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a critical prodromal stage of dementia, with an annual conversion rate to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) of 10%–15%.1 Early detection is critical for timely intervention. However, gold-standard diagnostics, such as comprehensive neuropsychological testing and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, are often invasive, costly, and impractical for continuous longitudinal monitoring.2 Wearable devices have emerged as a non-invasive alternative for the long-term monitoring of sleep, heart rate (HR), and physical activity.3,4 While actigraphy and smartwatches provide valuable insights, considerations remain regarding physiological resolution and long-term adherence in certain contexts.5,6 In contrast, smart rings—equipped with photoplethysmography (PPG) and triaxial accelerometers—offer superior wearability and stable data collection, particularly during nocturnal sleep.7 This study utilizes smart ring-derived longitudinal data to evaluate intra-individual variability as a novel digital biomarker for early MCI detection.

Physiological mechanisms

Sleep and HR are critical indicators of cognitive health.5 Reduced slow-wave sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep are hallmarks of early MCI, often linked to impaired glymphatic clearance of amyloid-β and tau proteins.8,9 Disruptions of this nature have been shown to cause destabilization of sleep-wake and autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulatory circuits. Notably, these instabilities are often more pronounced in variability metrics than in conventional mean values.10 Heart rate variability (HRV) serves as an indicator of ANS function, where reduced HRV indicates parasympathetic withdrawal associated with cognitive decline.11,12 In MCI, irregular HRV patterns during non-REM sleep highlight the limitations of mean-based analyses and the necessity of incorporating variability measures to capture autonomic instability.12

Variability indicators in MCI detection

Emerging evidence suggests that intra-individual variability and circadian rhythm disruption precede mean-level changes in physiological signals.13,14,15 Early cognitive decline often manifests as pattern instability rather than shifts in absolute levels, a phenomenon more sensitively captured by specific variability metrics. Previous studies have identified significant associations between MCI risk and isolated variability indicators, such as dispersion [e.g., standard deviation (SD) or mean absolute deviation (MAD) of nap duration], rhythmicity (e.g., inter-daily stability/intra-daily variability), and distributional characteristics (e.g., kurtosis of HRV).13,14,15,16 Furthermore, machine learning models utilizing conventional variability measures like standard deviation of NN intervals or root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) have achieved area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.78 to 0.85.11 However, previous efforts have largely relied on selected individual parameters over mostly short-term periods (14–30 days). There remains a critical lack of research that simultaneously examines long-term nocturnal sleep and HRV through a systematic analytic framework. By integrating comprehensive metrics across dispersion, distribution, and time-series domains, this study aims to capture longitudinal ANS instability more holistically than previous fragmented approaches.

To address these gaps, this study makes several key contributions. First, we utilized longitudinal data spanning approximately 70 days to capture robust intra-individual variability. Second, we employed high-fidelity monitoring via smart ring-derived signals, ensuring stable and high-compliance ANS quantification. Third, we used linear mixed models (LMM) to assess information loss from mean-based aggregation and applied long-term averaging (LTA) analyses to evaluate signal dilution.17 Fourth, an integrated analytic framework was established by the stepwise integration of central tendency and multi-dimensional variability metrics. Finally, we identified key digital biomarkers using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to ensure clinical interpretability and provide actionable insights for MCI screening.18,19

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

This study utilized a dementia high-risk lifelog dataset from the National Information Society Agency (IRB No. 2-1041055-AB-N-01-2020-50/2020-67). This study utilized a dementia high-risk lifelog dataset from the National Information Society Agency.20 A total of 162 participants [111 cognitively normal (CN); 51 MCI] were selected from an original cohort of 300 individuals aged ≥55 years. AD patients were excluded to focus on early-stage detection. Cognitive status was verified via amyloid PET imaging, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE),21 and the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery.22

Longitudinal physiological data were collected using a ring-type wearable device (Oura Ring).23 The device employs PPG sensors to quantify nocturnal HR, HRV, and sleep structure, with prior validation studies demonstrating good agreement with polysomnography.23,24 To ensure data robustness, we included participants with ≥31 nights of valid data (average: 70 nights). The primary raw variables extracted comprised sleep timing, sleep duration and efficiency, sleep structure (awake/light/deep/REM stages), and nocturnal physiological signals, including HR, HRV, and respiratory rate. All raw wearable variables are presented in Supplementary Table 1 (only online). Daytime activity metrics (e.g., step counts, energy expenditure) were excluded to focus exclusively on nocturnal autonomic instability during sleep. Validation of longitudinal data structure

To investigate whether LTA dilutes clinical signals essential for MCI detection, we performed a four-step validation.

Group comparison

Independent samples t-tests were used to assess if individual daily data points could sufficiently distinguish between CN and MCI groups.

Hierarchical modeling

LMM accounted for the repeated-measures structure,25 specifying the diagnostic group as a fixed effect and participant IDs as random effects. This allowed for the decomposition of variance into intra- and inter-individual components to quantify information loss during mean-based aggregation.

Multiple comparison correction

False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to mitigate type I errors across all physiological variables.

LTA analysis

Daily data were averaged within 35-, 50-, and 70-day windows to obtain one summary value per variable and participant. CN–MCI differences were assessed using Welch’s t-tests and signed Cohen’s d [95% confidence interval (CI)]. Signal dilution was defined as the instability of mean-based group differences across longer averaging windows and was quantified using the attenuation index (AI=(d35-d70)/d35). Based on changes in Cohen’s d from 35→70 days, variables were classified into four patterns: attenuation (AI >0), amplification (AI <0), stable (|d35-d70|<0.05), and direction flip (sign reversal of d).

Feature engineering and modeling design

Prior to feature engineering, the dataset—already subjected to standardized quality checks during wearable data collection—underwent additional preprocessing, including removal of missing-timestamp records, correction of infinite values, verification of the absence of NaNs, and z-score standardization of numerical variables. These steps ensured a clean and reproducible dataset for subsequent analysis.

To capture the longitudinal signals of MCI, we engineered multidimensional features and developed predictive models through the following steps.

Feature definition

Daily nocturnal HR, HRV, and sleep structure data were utilized to design novel features in four domains: 1) autonomic and sleep pattern irregularity, 2) sleep rhythm variability, 3) distributional shifts in sleep stages, and 4) sleep regularity.26,27,28,29

Longitudinal aggregation

Daily metrics were aggregated per participant into five systematic domains, encompassing both central tendency and multi-dimensional variability:

• Mean-based (M): mean• Extended mean (EM): median, trimmed mean, mode• Distributional variability (Dist): minimum, maximum, MAD, kurtosis, range• Dispersion variability (Disp): SD, coefficient of variation (CV), interquartile range (IQR)• Time-series variability (TS): time-series–based volatility metrics such as short-term variability, time-bin variability, rolling CV, and moving range (Supplementary Table 2, only online)

• Mean-based (M): mean

• Extended mean (EM): median, trimmed mean, mode

• Distributional variability (Dist): minimum, maximum, MAD, kurtosis, range

• Time-series variability (TS): time-series–based volatility metrics such as short-term variability, time-bin variability, rolling CV, and moving range (Supplementary Table 2, only online)

Feature selection and modeling

Logistic regression was employed for its robustness and interpretability. Recursive feature elimination identified the optimal predictor subset, excluding variables with high missing rates to ensure clinical reliability.30

Evaluation strategy

Generalizability was assessed via 10-fold cross-validation. Metrics included accuracy, AUC, sensitivity (recall), specificity, and F1-score.

By generating metrics that capture subtle autonomic and behavioral instabilities inherently lost in mean-based approaches, this framework provides a more sensitive methodology for detecting early cognitive decline.

Stage 1 (individual feature set performance)

The five feature sets—mean-based, extended mean, distributional, dispersion, and time-series variability—were evaluated independently. This stage assessed the unique contribution of each metric group to MCI detection.

Stage 2 (combined feature set performance)

Feature sets demonstrating high discriminative power in Stage 1 were combined to investigate synergistic effects. This step aimed to recover nuanced physiological signals overlooked by single feature sets.

Stage 3 (integrated feature set performance)

Validated feature sets were progressively accumulated to determine the optimal combination for clinical application, ensuring maximum recovery of variability signals.

Model interpretation (SHAP) and derivation of digital biomarkers

To ensure clinical interpretability, we employed SHAP across all experimental stages.30 Based on game-theoretic principles, SHAP quantifies the contribution of each predictor by providing both the direction and magnitude of its impact on MCI classification. This methodology enabled the identification of longitudinal variability indicators that consistently served as robust digital biomarkers. Furthermore, SHAP facilitates personalized interpretation by quantifying feature contributions at the individual participant level. By clarifying how specific indicators, such as sleep fragmentation or HR instability, influence the risk of MCI, SHAP addresses the black-box nature of complex models. This transparency enhances clinical trustworthiness and bridges the gap between machine learning performance and bedside decision-making.30

Performance analysis by feature sets

Stage 1 (individual feature set performance)

To evaluate the unique predictive power of different data characteristics, five statistical feature sets were independently analyzed (Table 5). The mean-based model (Exp. 1) yielded the lowest performance (AUC=0.525), confirming that simple averages are insufficient for capturing subtle MCI-related signals. In contrast, time-series features (Exp. 5) achieved the highest standalone performance (AUC=0.742), followed by distributional features (Exp. 3; AUC=0.701). Extended mean features (Exp. 2) showed moderate efficacy (AUC=0.667), while dispersion-only features (Exp. 4) were comparatively limited (AUC=0.605). These results collectively highlight the limitations of mean-based summaries in isolating clinically relevant MCI signals.

Stage 2 (combined feature set performance)

Since the mean-based model (Exp. 1) performed worst in Stage 1, we excluded simple mean features and combined the better-performing extended mean with other families (Table 5). EM+TS (Exp. 8) yielded the strongest improvement (AUC=0.773, sensitivity=0.680, specificity=0.704).

EM+Dist (Exp. 6) showed modest gains (AUC=0.671), whereas EM+Disp (Exp. 7) demonstrated lower performance (AUC=0.610), suggesting redundancy between dispersion and extended mean features.

Stage 3 (integrated feature set performance)

Cumulative integration further improved diagnostic power (Table 5). The all-feature model (Exp. 10) achieved well-balanced performance (AUC=0.859, sensitivity=0.800, specificity=0.780). The variability-focused model excluding the raw mean (Exp. 11; EM+Dist+TS) achieved the highest AUC (0.861) with strong sensitivity (sensitivity=0.820, specificity=0.742). Exp. 12 (EM+Dist+Disp+TS) also performed robustly (AUC=0.831).

Collectively, these results indicate that mean-only features are not robust, whereas combining distributional, dispersion, and time-series variability yields superior discrimination for MCI screening.

Additional analysis

To ensure the robustness of model evaluation and the validity of conclusions; to document that Table 5 metrics were obtained under a consistent baseline setting (minimum ≥35 nights, 7-day variability window with data-leakage safeguards, stratified 10-fold CV); to statistically verify differences across feature families; and to assess methodological suitability via comparisons with nonlinear models, we conducted additional analyses

• Baseline configuration. All sensitivity and comparison analyses were performed under the same baseline configuration (minimum ≥35 nights; 7-day variability window) (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, only online).

• Between-experiment statistics. Exp. 10 showed statistically higher AUC than several comparators (BH-adjusted), with no loss in sensitivity at the fixed 0.5 threshold (Supplementary Table 5, only online).

• Modeling sensitivity. Using the Exp. 10 feature set, linear models (Logistic/LDA/Elastic-Net) outperformed nonlinear baselines (Random Forest, XGBoost, RBF SVM) (Supplementary Table 6, only online).

• Feature-to-sample ratio. With N=162, the FTS ratios were approximately 0.06–0.20 (e.g., Exp. 6≈0.06; Exp. 10–12≈0.20), indicating controlled model complexity relative to sample size.

SHAP-based interpretation

As shown in Fig. 2, the SHAP result (Exp. 10)—which was trained with all feature families included—model predictions were dominated by variability-centered physiological and behavioral features. The most influential predictors arose from heart-rate drop (HR_drop) variability, particularly short-term variability, MAD-based distributional instability, and time-bin fluctuations, indicating that irregular autonomic responses during sleep are more discriminative for MCI than absolute heart-rate levels. Complementary contributions came from sleep–behavior rhythm indicators (e.g., the median and trimmed mean of daily sleep count) and circadian timing features (e.g., mode of sleep midpoint, variability in the timing of the lowest HR).

Across feature families, time-series variability (e.g., short-term and time-bin variability of HR_drop and RMSSD) and distributional variability (e.g., MAD, kurtosis, maximum, range) showed the highest SHAP values, while mean-based and extended mean features (mean, median, trimmed mean, mode) had smaller effects—indicating that instability and temporal fluctuation—rather than mean-level values—primarily drive MCI classification in Exp. 10.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated the clinical relevance of longitudinal variability-based indicators derived from wearable sleep data for early MCI detection.

First, our findings highlight the limitations of mean-based approaches. Traditional mean indicators did not retain statistical significance under hierarchical modeling using LMM with rigorous multiple-comparison control via the FDR.23,32 Consistent with this, our LTA analysis demonstrated signal dilution: as aggregation windows expanded from 35 to 70 days, signed Cohen’s d frequently changed in magnitude—and occasionally in direction—producing AI patterns of seven attenuation, nine amplification, two stable, and one direction flip. These instabilities indicate that simple averaging can obscure or distort subtle pathological fluctuations that matter for early MCI detection. While daily_sleep_count became discriminative only at longer horizons (e.g., 70 days), this was an exception; most mean-level effects were not robust across windows. Collectively, these results argue against exclusive reliance on mean-only, long-window summaries and motivate variability-oriented features—including distributional shifts, dispersion measures, and short-term time-series volatility—to better capture early autonomic and behavioral dysregulation.14,17

Furthermore, variability-oriented features representations outperformed mean-based approaches, as summarized in Table 5, time-series variability (Exp. 5, AUC=0.742) and distributional features (Exp. 3, AUC=0.701) provided the strongest standalone discrimination; EM+TS (Exp. 8) offered the most favorable sensitivity–specificity balance among pairwise combinations; and the variability-focused model excluding the raw mean (Exp. 11: EM+Dist+TS) achieved the highest AUC (0.861) with high sensitivity (0.820) and competitive specificity (0.742). Conceptually, distributional metrics (e.g., MAD, kurtosis) capture non-Gaussian departures in sleep-stage composition and nocturnal physiology, while dispersion metrics (SD, CV, and IQR) index scale heterogeneity independent of central tendency. Importantly, short-term time-series measures (e.g., rolling CV, moving range, time-bin volatility) are sensitive to fragmented/irregular rhythms, thereby mitigating signal dilution under long-window averaging.14,16,17 In parallel, abnormal HR/HRV variability indexes autonomic imbalance and reduced baroreflex/vagal reactivity, weakening brain–heart coupling and promoting microarousals and sleep fragmentation.10,11,12,13,21 Mechanistically, such ANS lability is consonant with impaired glymphatic clearance (due to disrupted slow-wave dynamics) and neurovascular coupling/microvascular dysregulation, recognized antecedents of cognitive vulnerability.8,33,34,35

Overall, a variability-centered framework more faithfully reflects early autonomic and behavioral dysregulation than static means, while offering promising sensitivity–specificity characteristics for future screening applications.

The underlying physiological drivers of these results were further elucidated through SHAP-based interpretation. The SHAP analysis (Fig. 2, Exp. 10) indicates that MCI discrimination is driven less by mean-level values and more by instability, distributional distortion, and temporal fluctuation in physiological and behavioral signals. In particular, HR_drop variability—captured by short-term indices, MAD-based distributional instability, and time-bin fluctuations—ranked highest,10,11,21 consistent with blunted parasympathetic bursts, noisy baroreflex gain, and unstable cardiorespiratory coupling during sleep. Complementary contributions arose from sleep–behavior rhythm features (e.g., median/trimmed mean of daily sleep count) and circadian-timing features (e.g., mode of sleep midpoint; variability in the timing of the lowest HR), suggesting ANS desynchrony under circadian misalignment.3,14,17 Consistently, distributional (e.g., MAD, kurtosis) and time-series features (e.g., short-term variability, time-bin change) outweighed mean-based/extended mean measures, implying that autonomic lability and temporal dysregulation—rather than static level differences—carry the primary signal of early cognitive vulnerability.24

This view is reinforced by Fig. 3 (HR_drop_ratio comparison). Whereas mean values show minimal group separation, MAD and short-term variability reveal marked irregularity and larger amplitude in the MCI group.10,11,13 Crucially, cumulative MAD demonstrates that groups are indistinguishable early on but diverge over time, with MCI participants exhibiting a steeper decline and earlier stabilization of variability—an empirical pattern consistent with irregular autonomic responses, progressive ANS dysfunction, and reduced physiological adaptability with increased rigidity.10,11,13,33,34,35 Together, Figs. 2 and 3 support the view that variability-centered, distribution-aware, and temporally dynamic markers are sensitive, interpretable digital biomarker candidates for the early identification and monitoring of cognitive decline.3,14

Despite these promising results, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to data-protection constraints, individual-level demographic variables were not accessible, which limited the scope of demographic adjustment analyses. Second, the single-center design and relatively small sample size produced wide CIs in bootstrap validation; although the lower bounds remained clinically meaningful, performance showed sensitivity to resampling. Third, potential sensor noise inherent to wearable devices may have influenced signal quality. We implemented strict anti-leakage procedures and a standardized preprocessing pipeline; nonetheless, some residual bias may remain.

Future research will pursue multicenter validation for broader generalizability. Currently, an independent clinical trial is underway to standardize algorithms and enhance digtial biomarker robustness, aiming to bridge the gap between retrospective discovery and practical MCI screening.

In conclusion, this study empirically demonstrates the structural limitations of mean-based approaches in longitudinal wearable data and establishes the superiority of distributional and time-series variability indicators. By identifying key markers of instability in sleep–wake rhythms, autonomic dysregulation (e.g., HR/HRV short-term and time-bin variability), and fragmentation of deep-sleep architecture, we provide a methodological foundation for next-generation, wearable-based cognitive health monitoring. Shifting the analytic paradigm from static averages to dynamic instability yields a more sensitive and clinically relevant signature for the early detection of cognitive decline.
