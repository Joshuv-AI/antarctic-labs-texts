Immune-inflammatory and nutritional status mediate the association between age and survival in older patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zongwei Huang, Jiajia Zheng, Ying Li, Jue Wang, Qisi Zhang, Xiaoke Wang, Xiaoyu Ye, Banghong Xie, Jinghua Lai, Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune-inflammatory and nutritional status are recognized as influencing factors for survival in older patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). This study explores the relationships between age, immune-inflammatory and nutritional status and survival outcomes, and the potential mediating effect in this association.MethodsWe analyzed 977 non-metastatic NPC patients aged ≥ 60 years between January 2015 and December 2022. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) served as the primary endpoints. Mediation analyses were performed to assess the effects of age and immune-inflammatory and nutritional status on the prognosis. Restricted cubic spline curves were employed to investigate non-linear associations. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to analyze the survival endpoints.ResultsAge independently predicted poorer survival, with platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and serum albumin (ALB) emerging as significant prognostic factors in older NPC patients. Mediation analysis revealed that the indirect effects of age on OS through PLR, PNI, and ALB were 0.01%, 3.7%, and 6.2%, respectively, and the indirect effects on CSS were − 0.01%, 6.7%, and 7.7%, respectively. Among them, ALB values significantly mediated the effects of age on OS and CSS (both P_indir < 0.01), persisting after multivariable adjustment. Patients with ALB values ≥ 41.3 g/L exhibited superior 5-year OS (77.5% vs. 66.7%, P < 0.001) and CSS (80.6% vs. 71.3%, P < 0.001), with subgroup analyses confirming robustness.ConclusionsImmune-inflammatory and nutritional status mediate age-dependent survival disparities in older NPC patients. These findings underscore the clinical imperative of addressing inflammatory burden and nutritional depletion in geriatric oncology and advocate for integrated biomarker-guided management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07903-y.

Why does this paper matter?

The findings advocate for integrating the assessment of immune-inflammatory and nutritional status into clinical management to improve outcomes for older cancer patients.

Background

The continuous increase in life expectancy results in a steady increase of cancer risk, which consequently increases the population of older adults with cancer [1]. These patients frequently exhibit a complex clinical phenotype, marked by reduced physiological resilience, multifactorial frailty and a significant comorbidity burden, which collectively challenge conventional treatment frameworks and prognostic accuracy [2, 3]. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated malignancy with distinct geographical prevalence [4, 5]. Although the advent of intensity-modulated radiotherapy has substantially improved clinical outcomes, a marked survival disparity persists between younger and older individuals with NPC, highlighting an imperative to decipher the mechanistic drivers of this vulnerability [6, 7].

Similar to other cancers, advancing age is a principal risk factor for NPC, with incidence peaks in endemic regions occurring between 45 and 59 years [8]. The aetiology of this age-related survival gap is multifactorial, potentially arising from diminished tolerance to treatment toxicity, alterations in tumor biology and other geriatric impairments [9, 10]. Ageing is intrinsically linked to chronic, low-grade inflammation, and a progressive deterioration in nutritional status, both of which may independently promote a tumor-permissive milieu and compromise therapeutic efficacy [11]. Older individuals frequently present with more comorbidities and are more susceptible to severe chemoradiotherapy-related toxicities, such as oropharyngeal mucositis, xerostomia and swallowing dysfunction. These physical challenges, combined with age-related oral decline and treatment-associated psychological distress, can significantly exacerbate malnutrition [12–14]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation, a hallmark of aging and cancer progression, may further contribute to adverse prognosis [15]. However, it remains unclear whether the poorer prognosis in older individuals results primarily from their compromised nutritional and inflammatory status, or whether aging itself drives these immunometabolic alterations that, in turn, worsen outcomes.

In recent years, integrative biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status have emerged as objective prognostic tools across numerous malignancies [16, 17]. In NPC, scores such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) have demonstrated significant associations with survival outcomes [18, 19]. Previous studies have shown that older patients had worse immunonutritional status, leading to inferior prognosis [20]. Systemic inflammatory response can also lead to weight loss and malnutrition in patients with NPC, which are common in patients with NPC and may negatively influence treatment tolerance and survival [21]. Consequently, while these biomarkers are gaining prognostic traction, their specific role in mediating the effect of chronological age on survival in older NPC patients remains poorly defined.

Here, we evaluate the impact of immune-inflammatory and nutritional status on survival outcomes in older NPC patients. A comprehensive panel of established indices—including the NLR, PLR, and PNI—was assessed, alongside serum albumin (ALB). Recognizing that ALB levels reflect not only nutritional status but also inflammatory activity and fluid balance, we interpret ALB as an integral component of the PNI and as a complementary variable within a broader immunonutritional framework [22]. Employing mediation analysis, we quantified the extent to which these parameters mediate the established association between advanced age and mortality, thereby informing the development of targeted interventions and potentially advancing personalized care for geriatric patients with NPC.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Fujian Cancer Hospital (approval no. K2022-203-01). We reviewed the medical records of older NPC patients newly diagnosed between January 2015 and December 2022 at Fujian Cancer Hospital. The threshold of 60 years was used to define older patients, in accordance with the World Health Organization’s definition of older adults in the Asia-Pacific region, in line with previous studies on NPC [23, 24]. The inclusion criteria for eligible patients were as follows: (1) pathological confirmation of NPC; (2) age 60 years or older; (3) no evidence of distant metastases; (4) treatment with radical intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT); and (5) availability of complete clinical data. The primary exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of a second primary malignant tumor; (2) unknown T or N category; (3) treatment interruption; and (4) lost to follow-up. A total of 977 patients were included in our study.

Data collection and processing

Retrospective interrogation of the electronic laboratory records identified pretreatment hematological parameters, collected based on the first complete blood count after the first admission. The following indices were included to reflect systemic inflammatory response and immunonutritional status: NLR; PLR; MLR (monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio); SII (systemic immune inflammation index, calculated as platelets × neutrophils/lymphocytes); SIRI (systemic inflammatory response index, calculated as neutrophils × monocytes/lymphocytes); PIV (pan-immune-inflammation value, calculated as neutrophils × platelets × monocytes/lymphocytes); PNI (calculated as serum albumin + 5 × lymphocyte) and ALB.

We also retrospectively analyzed clinical information such as age, sex, TNM stage, EBV DNA levels and therapeutic regimen. TNM stage was reassessed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system by two experienced radiologists. Baseline plasma EBV DNA levels were categorized dichotomously using a threshold of 2,000 copies/mL, previously established as indicative of a “high-risk” profile for disease progression in older NPCs [25].

Evaluation and treatment

All patients underwent standardized pretreatment evaluations, including detailed medical histories, physical examinations, hematologic and biochemical profiling, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) DNA quantification testing, electronic nasopharyngoscopy, nasopharyngeal and neck magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), chest X-ray or computed tomography (CT), abdominal ultrasonography, and a whole-body bone scan, among others.

All patients received radical radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy. Patients in stage I received radiotherapy alone, while patients in stage II-IV underwent concurrent chemoradiotherapy, tailored to individual pretreatment features or chemotherapy tolerance, with or without induction/adjuvant chemotherapy. The concurrent chemotherapy typically encompassed cisplatin or other platinum agents. The commonly used induction/adjuvant regimens were gemcitabine or paclitaxel combined with platinum, followed by platinum plus 5-fluorouracil. Radiotherapy was delivered using the IMRT technique. Target volume delineation was determined by the fusion of treatment planning CT scans and MRI. Gross tumor volume (GTV) encompassed the primary tumor (GTV-T) and metastatic lymph nodes (GTV-N) based on imaging, physical examination, and endoscopic findings. Prescription doses with interquartile range (IQR) of 69.96–70.00 Gy and 69.30–70.00 Gy were delivered to GTV-T and GTV-N, respectively, using 31–37 fractions, given once daily, five times weekly. Radiation dose constraints for organs at risk (OARs) were determined according to RTOG 0225 protocol.

Follow-up and endpoints

Patients were followed up regularly until death or March 2025, with follow-up examinations conducted every 3 months during the first 2 years after treatment, semi-annual evaluations from the 3rd to the 5th year, and annual assessments more than 5 years. The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in patients. OS was measured from the completion of radiotherapy until the final follow-up or death from any cause, while CSS was measured from the completion of radiotherapy until the final follow-up or NPC-related death.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were expressed via median with IQR, while categorical variables were expressed via frequencies and proportions. The Cox proportional-hazards regression was used for univariate and multivariate analyses, with hazard ratio (HR) to assess the independent prognostic factors. Mediation analyses were performed to explore the indirect and direct relationships between age and prognosis via inflammatory or nutritional status using “Median” R package. In this analysis, age was designated as the predictor variable, inflammatory and nutritional indicators as the mediator variable, and OS or CSS with event in older NPC patients as the outcome variable. To estimate effect sizes and calculate 95% confidence intervals, we evaluated direct, indirect, and total effects using the bootstrap resampling method with 50 iterations [26]. The mediation analysis would estimate the following effects: (1) the effect of age on prognosis, with the regression coefficient (β) between age on prognosis represented as the total effect; (2) the effect of age on inflammatory or nutritional status, where β indicates the indirect effect; (3) the effect of inflammatory or nutritional status on prognosis when age was controlled; and (4) the effect of age on prognosis when inflammatory or nutritional status were controlled. The mediation effect is considered “complete mediation” when the indirect pathway exhibits statistical significance, whereas the direct effect remains non-significant. Conversely, “partial mediation” is identified when both the indirect and direct effects reach statistical significance [27]. Covariates were included in all regression analyses to improve the accuracy of model estimation including sex, T category, N category, induction chemotherapy, concurrent chemotherapy, and pre-treatment EBV DNA. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were constructed to examine the relationships among ALB and the prognosis of NPC. Survival outcomes were estimated using Kaplan-Meier method, and comparisons between groups were generated using the log-rank test. In addition, sensitivity analyses stratified by ALB levels were also implemented. All analyses were carried out using SPSS Statistics v25.0 and R software v4.0.4, along with Zstats v1.0 (www.zstats.net). P values below 0.05 (two-sided) were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 1072 older patients with NPC who received IMRT in our center from January 2015 to December 2022, we excluded 33 patients with treatment interruption, 4 patients lacking specific T or N category, 35 patients with previous anti-tumor therapy, 13 patients with incomplete clinical data, and 10 patients lost to follow-up. This resulted in a final cohort of 977 eligible patients. The baseline clinical characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 65 years (IQR, 62–69 years). A total of 738 patients (75.5%) were men, while 239 (24.5%) were women. A large proportion of the study cohort was locally advanced NPC patients, with 423 (43.3%) in stage Ⅲ and 394 (40.3%) in stage Ⅳa. 741 patients (75.8%) underwent induction chemotherapy, while 512 (52.4%) received concurrent chemotherapy. Nearly half of the patients (n = 455, 46.6%) presented with pre-treatment plasma EBV DNA levels above 2000 copies/mL. Prognostic analysis

During a median follow-up of 43.2 months (IQR 25.8–60.6), 226 deaths were recorded, including 189 (83.6%) attributable to NPC-related causes. The 5-year OS and CSS rates for the entire cohort were 72.2% (95%CI: 68.8%-75.8%) and 76.1% (95%CI: 72.8%-79.5%), respectively. Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed significant associations between clinical factors and survival outcomes. For OS, increasing age (HR: 1.09, 95%CI: 1.06–1.11, P < 0.001), T4 disease (HR: 2.24, 95%CI: 1.42–3.55, P < 0.001), N3 disease (HR: 1.95, 95%CI: 1.18–3.23, P = 0.009), the application of induction (HR: 0.75, 95%CI: 0.56–0.99, P = 0.047) and concurrent chemotherapy (HR: 0.64, 95%CI: 0.49–0.83, P < 0.001), and higher pre-treatment plasma EBV DNA levels (HR: 1.40, 95%CI: 1.08–1.83, P = 0.011) were significant prognostic factors (Table S2). Similar associations were observed for CSS, with age (HR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.05–1.11, P < 0.001), T4 disease (HR: 2.61, 95%CI: 1.55–4.42, P < 0.001), N3 disease (HR: 2.22, 95%CI: 1.28–3.85, P = 0.005), the application of concurrent chemotherapy (HR: 0.64, 95%CI: 0.48–0.85, P = 0.002), and higher pre-treatment plasma EBV DNA levels (HR: 1.48, 95%CI: 1.11–1.98, P = 0.007) demonstrating significant effects (Table S3). Multivariate analysis further indicated that except for T4 and N3 disease, age remained an independent prognostic factor for older NPC patients.

Associations among age, immune-inflammatory and nutritional status, and prognosis in older NPC patients

Given the potential influence of immune-inflammatory and nutritional status on treatment response and outcomes in older patients, we investigated their role as mediators between age and prognosis of older NPC patients. Initial evaluation of baseline inflammatory and nutritional parameters revealed PLR as a significant predictor for both OS (HR = 1.01, 95%CI: 1.01–1.01, P = 0.008) and CSS (HR = 1.01, 95%CI: 1.01–1.01, P = 0.002, Table 2). Among immunonutritional indices, both PNI and ALB showed significant associations with improved OS (PNI: HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.98, P < 0.001; ALB: HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99–0.99, P < 0.001) and CSS (PNI: HR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92–0.97, P < 0.001; ALB: HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99–0.99, P < 0.001).

Associations among ALB values and prognosis in older NPC patients

Our analysis revealed significant linear relationships between ALB and OS (P for nonlinearity = 0.848) or CSS (P for nonlinearity = 0.968, Fig. 1A). Mortality risk decreased progressively with increasing ALB levels, with hazard ratios (HRs) falling below 1 when ALB exceeded 41.3 g/L. Patients with baseline ALB ≥ 41.3 g/L exhibited significantly improved 5-year OS (77.5% [95%CI: 73.1%-82.2%] vs. 66.7% [95%CI: 61.6%-72.2%], P < 0.001, Fig. 1B) and CSS (80.6% [95%CI: 76.5%-85.1%] vs. 71.3% [95%CI: 66.3%-76.6%], P < 0.001, Fig. 1C) compared to those with lower ALB levels. To validate the robustness of these associations, stratified subgroup analyses were conducted, with the results presented in Fig. 2. The associations of ALB with OS remained consistent when considering additional subgroup participants, reaching statistical significance in males (HR = 0.60, 95%CI: 0.45–0.82, P = 0.001), individuals with T3 (HR = 0.61, 95%CI: 0.39–0.94, P = 0.024) or N1-2 disease (HR = 0.54, 95%CI: 0.35–0.83, P = 0.005 for N1 disease, and HR = 0.59, 95%CI: 0.36–0.96, P = 0.035 for N2 disease). Moreover, ALB remained predictive of OS irrespective of pre-treatment EBV DNA load (< 2000 or ≥ 2000 copies/mL) or chemotherapy regimen (Ps < 0.05). A comparable trend was observed for CSS across subgroups, further supporting the prognostic value of ALB.

Discussion

In this study, we identified age as an independent prognostic factor in older NPC patients, with the PLR, PNI, and ALB levels serving as important correlates of survival. Furthermore, we established that immune-inflammatory and nutritional status mediate the association between advanced age and survival. Specifically, the adverse impact of age appears partially attributable to albumin levels, a key immunonutritional marker. Serum ALB demonstrated a linear, protective correlation with both OS and CSS. Patients with baseline ALB ≥ 41.3 g/L exhibited significantly improved prognosis compared to those with ALB < 41.3 g/L, a finding consistently supported across subgroup analyses.

Chronological age is a well-established prognostic factor in oncology. Although nasopharyngeal carcinoma demonstrates a peak incidence between 45 and 59 years in endemic regions [8], we specifically focused on patients aged ≥ 60 years based on the World Health Organization’s definition of older adults in the Asia-Pacific region, consistent with previous investigations in this field [23, 24]. This age threshold represents a critical transition point where physiological resilience markedly declines, frailty becomes prevalent, and the burden of comorbidities substantially increases [2, 3]. Prior studies employing this identical age cutoff have demonstrated that NPC patients ≥ 60 years constitute a distinct subgroup with significantly inferior survival outcomes compared to their younger counterparts [7, 23], and our findings further support age as an independent prognostic factor in this geriatric population. However, the independent role of chronological age per se remains debated, as its effect may be confounded by comorbid conditions and overall physiological decline [28, 29]. Older individuals are often characterized by a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state, which reshapes the immune microenvironment and may contribute to tumorigenesis and disease progression [11]. Throughout the initiation and progression of cancer, inflammation assumes a pivotal role, intimately involved with core oncogenic processes such as transformation, proliferation, viability and metastasis of tumor cells [30]. Zeng et al. found NPC patients had a higher propensity for inflammatory indicator abnormalities than the sinusitis patients [31]. Although prior studies associate numerous pretreatment inflammatory markers (e.g., NLR, SII, SIRI, PLR) with NPC prognosis [18, 32], our analysis of older patients identified PLR as the sole significant predictor among these indices. This specificity may reflect the distinct immunobiology of aging. Age-related immunosenescence diminishes both innate and adaptive immune function, potentially confounding the utility of broad inflammatory markers [33]. Furthermore, more frequent chemotherapy modifications in older adults could attenuate inflammatory responses and obscure the prognostic role of certain biomarkers [34, 35].

The per-unit hazard ratio for PLR (HR = 1.01; 95% CI: 1.01–1.01) may appear modest when viewed in isolation. However, this effect size represents a continuous risk gradient across the full spectrum of PLR values observed in our cohort (interquartile range: 99.20–163.18). Comparing patients at the 75th percentile versus the 25th percentile yields a hazard ratio of 1.41 (95% CI: 0.99–2.01), indicating that those with higher PLR face approximately 41% greater mortality risk—a clinically meaningful difference. Although this dichotomized comparison did not achieve conventional statistical significance (log-rank P = 0.058), the consistency of the effect direction, the magnitude of the point estimate, and the inclusion of clinically important risk differences within the confidence interval collectively support the prognostic relevance of PLR in this population. Moreover, the emergence of PLR as the sole significant inflammatory marker among those tested suggests that platelet–lymphocyte dynamics may capture a distinct aspect of age-related immune dysregulation not fully reflected by other indices. Mediation analysis revealed that the indirect effect of age on survival outcomes operating through PLR was negligible (0.01% for OS; -0.01% for CSS). This lack of substantial mediation, which remained consistent after adjusting for key clinical variables, suggests that age and PLR likely represent distinct, non-overlapping pathways of biological vulnerability in the aged host. The prognostic information captured by age extends beyond systemic inflammation as quantified by this specific marker. Age-related prognostic risk may be more directly driven by other facets of the aged microenvironment, such as immunosenescence altering T-cell repertoire and function, cellular senescence promoting a pro-tumorigenic niche, or accumulated genomic instability [33]. Conversely, an elevated PLR in this cohort may reflect a specific, age-aggravated state of myeloid inflammation and platelet-driven protumor mechanisms that is not universally present in all older patients. Thus, age and PLR appear to be complementary rather than sequential risk factors.

Malnutrition associated with disease is a common problem and affects 30% of hospitalized patients [20]. Among NPC patients, the incidence of disease-related malnutrition further increases to 35–60%, significantly impairing their response to treatment [36]. This nutritional vulnerability is particularly pronounced in older NPC patients, who frequently present with age-related physiological decline, treatment-related side effects, and exacerbated inflammatory states that collectively heighten malnutrition risk [13, 37]. Consistent with this, the PNI and ALB are recognized as composite biomarkers that capture the intricate interplay between systemic inflammation and nutritional depletion [22, 38]. Their clinical significance in NPC is well established, with previous studies consistently demonstrating that decreased PNI and ALB levels serve as adverse prognostic factors [19, 21]. This association appears stronger in older adults; for example, PNI exhibited enhanced diagnostic sensitivity in patients over 75 compared to younger cohorts [39], and hypoproteinemia is specifically identified as an independent adverse prognostic factor in older NPC patients [23]. Our study further employed mediation analysis to dissect the intricate relationship among age, immunonutritional status, and survival. We found that ALB consistently and significantly mediated the effect of age on survival, a relationship that persisted after adjustment for key clinical confounders. The mediating effect of PNI was less pronounced than that of ALB, suggesting that the inclusion of immune-related variability (via lymphocyte count) may dilute the core prognostic signal driven by inflammation–nutrition axis. Notably, the mediation effects of serum albumin exhibited distinct patterns for OS and CSS after adjusting for covariables. The proportion of the total effect of age mediated by ALB decreased for OS but increased for CSS. This divergence may be attributed to the different nature of these endpoints. OS encompasses all causes of death and can also be influenced by non-cancer factors. The diminished mediation effect on OS may result from the adjustment of confounders, thereby allocating more of the total effect to direct pathways or other unmeasured mediators for non-cancer events. Conversely, the strengthened mediation effect on CSS underscores ALB’s specific link to cancer mortality. In older or frail patients, low albumin often signifies decreased physiological reserve and higher vulnerability to treatment stress. This can result in more severe toxicities, unplanned treatment modifications, and ultimately poorer survival outcomes due to disease progression [9, 40, 41].

Our findings reinforce serum albumin as a continuous, linear predictor of survival in NPC, consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that even incremental increases in albumin are associated with significant reductions in mortality risk [42]. The per-unit hazard ratio of 0.99 per 1 g/L—while seemingly modest—reflects the graded nature of this relationship and aligns closely with effect sizes reported in analogous oncological cohorts [42]. More importantly, dichotomization at the optimal cutoff of 41.3 g/L, identified via RCS analysis, revealed a 38% reduction in mortality risk for patients with higher albumin levels (HR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.48–0.81; P < 0.001), an effect size with clear clinical relevance. This cutoff closely approximates thresholds used in other malignancies (e.g., 40 g/L in bladder cancer) [43]. While a lower threshold of 35 g/L is commonly used to define clinical hypoalbuminemia [44], our higher cutoff may better identify subclinical physiological decline and elevated risk in older patients with ostensibly normal albumin levels, enabling earlier identification of those with diminished reserve who could benefit from targeted intervention [45, 46]. We also used subgroup analysis to elucidate the robust prognostic value of serum albumin levels in older NPC patients. The prognostic value of albumin was robust across most patient subgroups and independent of treatment modality, underscoring its role as a fundamental indicator of systemic health [47]. A notable exception was the lack of significant association in female patients, likely due to the cohort’s gender imbalance and reduced statistical power, though unaccounted sex-specific biological factors may also play a role [48].

We interpret serum albumin primarily as a surrogate biomarker of physiological reserve and inflammatory burden, rather than as a direct modifiable target for intervention. Its prognostic value derives from its capacity to capture the cumulative impact of disease-related inflammation, catabolism, and nutritional depletion—processes that collectively erode a patient’s ability to tolerate aggressive multimodal therapy [22]. Consequently, we do not advocate for correcting hypoalbuminaemia through albumin infusion alone, an approach lacking evidence of survival benefit in oncology populations [49]. Studies have shown that maintaining adequate immunonutritional status is critical for cancer patients to tolerate the cumulative toxicities of aggressive multimodal therapy, preserve organ function, and complete treatment as planned [50]. Our results support the imperative to integrate systematic assessment of the inflammation–nutrition axis into standard management for older patients with NPC, with serum albumin serving as an accessible, cost-effective biomarker to guide supportive care interventions and inform clinical decision-making.

Our study has several limitations. First, its design is retrospective and draws from a single-center cohort, which inherently introduces the potential for selection bias. Second, analysis was restricted to a predefined set of immune-inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers; other markers with established prognostic value in other malignancies, such as IL-6, C-reactive protein, and alkaline phosphatase, were not included. Finally, the study focuses exclusively on baseline biomarker levels and does not account for their dynamic changes throughout treatment and disease progression. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger cohorts will be essential to validate our findings and further investigate the clinical relevance of longitudinal variations in these parameters. Finally, although we frame albumin as an integrated marker of the inflammation–nutrition interplay, we acknowledge that circulating albumin levels are influenced by factors beyond these two domains, including hydration status, hepatic synthetic function and vascular permeability, which could not be fully controlled for in this retrospective analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study establishes serum albumin as a key mediator between aging and survival outcomes in older NPC patients. This finding provides new evidence for incorporating biomarker-guided systematic nutritional evaluation into personalized clinical treatment in specific target populations.
