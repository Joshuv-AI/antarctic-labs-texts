Outcomes of non-operative management of higher grade (II-IV) of blunt traumatic aortic injury
Authors: Soumaya Bahlouli, Larissa Russo, Nasim Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current clinical practice guidelines for blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI) as set by the Society for Vascular Surgery recommend non-operative management in grade I injuries and thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) or surgical repair for grade II-IV injuries. Several studies have reported successful non-operative management of grade I and II BTAI. However, non-operative management of BTAI greater than grade II has not been well-demonstrated. The purpose of this study is to examine outcomes in operative and non-operative management of patients with Grade II, III, and IV BTAI.MethodsIn this retrospective study of 24 patients, the Hackensack Meridian Health Jersey Shore University Medical Center trauma registry and electronic medical record (2011–2021) were used to identify adults (18 years old) with grade II, III, and IV BTAI. Management was characterized as non-operative or operative. The primary outcome of this study was improvement of aortic injury which is defined as a lack of lesion progression or resolution of the lesion on a follow-up CT scan.ResultsNon-operative patients had lower improvement percentages (33.3%) compared to the operative group (83.3%). Non-operative patients also had increased hospital mortality (66.7%) compared to the operative group (0%).DiscussionPatients with a higher grade BTAI showed greater improvement and had better outcomes with operative management, than non-operative management. However, large sample size studies are needed to verify these findings.

Introduction

In the United States, trauma remains the leading cause of death in adults under 44 years of age [1]. In blunt, traumatic incidents, head trauma is the most common cause of death, followed by aortic injury [2–4]. Motor vehicle collisions, particularly head-on collisions, account for approximately 80% of blunt aortic injury cases [3].

The universal mechanism of this injury is high-energy blunt trauma with rapid deceleration. Blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI) is defined as a tear in the aorta that is due to a combination of shear and stretch forces, quick deceleration, increased intravascular pressure, and compression of the aorta between the anterior chest wall and vertebrae [5–7]. The most common location for the injury is at the aortic isthmus which is a transition zone between the unfixed aortic arch and fixed descending aorta [8].

Based on the severity of the aortic injury, BTAIs are classified by the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) into four grades following computed tomographic angiography (CTA) analysis: grade I, intimal tear; grade II, intramural hematoma; grade III, pseudoaneurysm; grade IV, rupture [9]. The European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) classifies grade 1 as injury contained to the intima or vessel wall (grade I and II under SVS guidelines), grade 2 as abnormal external wall contour or external wall disruption with contained hemorrhage (pseudoaneurysm), and grade 3 as complete wall transection with free rupture [10].

The current clinical practice guidelines as set by the SVS recommend non-operative management in grade I injuries, and thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) or surgical repair for grade II-IV injuries [11]. Non-operative management of grade I injury is well-established. Non-operative management involves blood pressure and rate control with beta-1 blockade to decrease the rate of dissection and decrease further risk of pseudoaneurysm expansion or rupture [12, 13]. There are differing evidence-based opinions on whether higher-grade injuries, like grade III, qualify solely for non-operative management. Some studies state that this is a multi-factorial decision that considers the patient’s age, preexisting aortic abnormalities, degree of aortic instability, presence of neurologic injury, and concurrent polytrauma [12, 14]. Since operative management carries risks of complications such as paraplegia and stroke in the open approach, and graft leak and graft migration in endovascular repair, the decision to proceed with operative management for higher grade injury depends upon the grade of the injury, type of aortic injury, comorbid condition and concomitant injuries [15, 16].

Several studies reported successful non-operative management of grade I injuries, but there is a paucity of studies that examined the outcomes of non-operative management of higher grade injuries [17]. The purpose of this study is to compare the improvement of aortic injury in patients with Grade ≥ II, thoracic BTAI who were non-operatively managed with those who underwent endovascular or surgical repair.

Methods

The Institutional Review Board at Hackensack Meridian Health granted approval for this study. The data for this retrospective study was retrieved from the Jersey Shore University Medical Center (JSUMC) trauma registry and electronic medical record. JSUMC is a level 1 trauma center. Patients were included in this study if they had a grade II, III, or IV blunt thoracic aortic injury between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2021, and were ≥ 18 years of age. The Society for Vascular Surgery grading system was used for the classification of aortic injury [9]. Variables collected included age, sex, race, mode of injury, Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score, Injury Severity Score (ISS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, other injuries, and comorbidities.

The primary outcome of this study was improvement of aortic injury. Initial and follow-up CT scans were reviewed to determine whether there was an improvement. According to the SVS, there is no general consensus to define improvement of aortic injury or establish a follow-up timeline [9]. The guidelines broadly stated that in the absence of any imaging abnormalities including stable endograft positioning and no endoleak, frequency of follow-up imaging can be decreased [9]. Improvement was defined as lack of lesion progression or resolution of the lesion on a follow-up CT scan while no improvement was defined as progression of the lesion, as evidenced by an increase in size or grade.

Time from initial CT scan on admission to the follow-up CT scan ranged from 1 day to about 3.5 years. At the institution of this study, CT imaging is done within 1–2 h of patients’ arrival to the trauma center and diagnosis. Secondary outcomes were hospital length of stay, hospital mortality, discharge disposition, and complications.

Summary statistics were calculated for demographic, injury, and outcome variables. Continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range (IQR)) while categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate hospital length of stay. R statistical software (version 4.2.2) was used for analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

Twenty-four patients met the inclusion criteria. The most frequently reported comorbidities were hypertension (37.5%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (20.8%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (16.7%). Out of the 24 patients, 6 (25%) patients received non-operative management while 18 (75%) patients underwent operative management. The median (IQR; 1 st quartile − 3rd quartile) age of patients who underwent non-operative management was 68 years (IQR 35–84), while the median age of patients who underwent operative management was 59 years (IQR 36–68). 17/24 (70.8%) patients were male, and 50% of patients were greater than 60 years old. The mechanism of injury included motor vehicle collision (75%), pedestrian struck by car (12.5%), motorcycle collision (8.3%), and fall (4.2%). Associated injuries in patients were common; rib fractures (62.5%), abdominal organ injuries (58.3%), pelvic fractures (20.8%), lower extremity fractures (20.8%), facial fractures (16.7%), spinal fractures (16.7%), and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) (16.7%). Of the four patients who had ICH, 3 of them were managed non-operatively.

The non-operative group had a median ISS of 37 (IQR 31–47) while the operative group had a median ISS of 36 (IQR 29–42). AIS chest scores ranged from 4 to 6 for each group. In the non-operative group, 66.6% of patients had an AIS chest score of 4, 16.7% of patients had an AIS chest score of 5, and 16.7% of patients had an AIS chest score of 6. In the operative group, 55.6% of patients had an AIS chest score of 4, and 44.4% had an AIS chest score of 5. Patients managed operatively had a higher GCS score with a median of 15 (IQR 14–15), while patients managed non-operatively had a median GCS of 9 (IQR 3–15). There were 7 grade II injuries, 14 grade III injuries, and 3 grade IV injuries. Out of the 6 non-operative patients, 3 (50%) had grade II injuries, 2 (33.3%) had grade III injuries, and 1 (16.7%) had a grade IV injury. Out of the 18 operative patients, 4 (22.2%) had grade II injuries, 12 (66.7%) had grade III injuries, and 2 (11.1%) had grade IV injuries. Endovascular repair (TEVAR) comprised 94.4% (17/18) of operative management, with only 1 patient undergoing open repair for a grade II injury (Tables 1 and 2).

Outcomes

Non-operative patients had lower improvement percentages (33.3%) compared to the operative group (83.3%) and increased hospital mortality (66.7%) compared to the operative group (0%). Non-operative patients with grade II injuries had higher observed hospital mortality (66.7%), and lower improvement (33.3%) than operative patients with grade II injuries. 100% of operative patients with grade II injuries showed improvement and had zero hospital mortality. Non-operative patients with grade III injuries had higher observed hospital mortality (50%), and lower improvement (50%) when compared to operative patients with grade III injuries. Operative grade III patients had zero hospital mortality, and 83.3% showed improvement. Non-operative patients with grade IV injuries had higher observed hospital mortality (100%), and no improvement (0%). Operatively managed grade IV patients had lower mortality (0%) and greater improvement (50%). The median length of stay in the non-operative group was 34 days [95% CI 12, NA] compared to 19 days [95% CI 14,39] in the operative group (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study found that patients with higher grade (≥ grade II) BTAI showed better outcomes with operative management. Overall, patients presenting with BTAI were more likely to receive operative management than non-operative.

Blunt traumatic aortic injury was traditionally treated with open surgery until endovascular stent-grafting was introduced in 1997 [14]. In 2011, the SVS recommended endovascular repair for grade II-IV injuries [11]. Patients often present with distracting injuries that need stabilization before addressing the aortic injury. Other patients develop secondary signs of injury (SSI) such as mediastinal hematomas with mass effect, pseudocoarctation, or large hemothoraces as a result of the BTAI. These findings are seen in higher grade injuries and need immediate attention. Very few patients present with frank aortic rupture, and those who do, often present in extremis.

As CT technology and transesophageal echocardiography advanced, smaller injuries that were previously undetected are now diagnosed [18]. One study suggested that this led to an increased use of TEVAR [13]. Endovascular repair, while revolutionizing operative management, has added risk of graft failure, graft migration as younger patients age, and increased lifetime radiation exposure to monitor the graft [15, 16]. Clinicians started to question whether patients with smaller injuries, whose vitals are stable with normal chest radiographs, and little mediastinal hemorrhage, required any immediate operative therapy. These injuries have ushered in a responsibility to actively manage them, regardless of clinical outcomes.

This study of 24 patients represents a single-institution decade-long review in a high-volume trauma center. The majority of patients sustained grade II and III injuries (87.5%), and of these patients with this grade injury, 5 underwent non-operative management. Three patients sustained grade IV injuries, 1 of which underwent non-operative management. In higher-grade patients, their clinical status was more tenuous due to associated injuries that required more immediate attention. The patients in our study sustained severe injuries, with a median ISS of 37 (IQR 31–47) in the non-operative group and 36 (IQR 29–42) in the operative group. Non-operatively managed patients also had lower median GCS (9, IQR (3–15)), which coupled with higher ISS likely contributed to the higher mortality and lower improvement rates these patients experienced. Furthermore, due to the severity of their injuries, these patients were fundamentally at a higher risk for complications, irrespective of the BTAI grade or management course. According to autopsy results, expired patients had concomitant injuries to different organ systems, and it is unlikely that aortic injury is the sole cause of death.

As acute aortic rupture is likely to occur in the first 4 to 6 h after injury, patients must be carefully monitored at this time [19]. In these hours, strict blood pressure and heart rate control are needed in patients who are not immediately taken to the OR to minimize the risk of expansion of the aortic pseudoaneurysm. In patients with TBI, of whom we had four patients (75% in the non-operative group), intracranial pressure has to be managed carefully. Uncontrollable intracranial pressures withdraw patients as candidates for operative aortic procedures [13]. Medical management of BTAI with impulse control via rate and blood pressure management is not without its nuances as well. Moreso, maintenance of lower blood pressure and heart rate can be challenging with multiorgan injury. For example, keeping the systolic blood pressure (sBP) low to reduce aortic stress can worsen brain perfusion in severe head injuries. Similarly, maintenance of lower heart rate with beta-blockade for aortic injury can be contraindicated in patients who present with hemorrhagic shock.

Conservative management is often first-line therapy or used in conjunction with surgery [13]. In patients with multiple, more life-threatening injuries than the BTAI, medical management with a beta-blocker is often the only option. This approach prioritizes delayed surgical repair of the BTAI. In patients with delayed repair of BTAI injury, medical optimization often necessitated this delay. These patients often had tenuous intracranial hemorrhage, cardiac and pulmonary statuses, or concomitant injuries that needed stabilization prior to operative repair of the BTAI. There is also a complex balance of ethical considerations. The clinician has to balance the benefits of early repair with the risks of surgical complications due to competing injuries. The clinician also has to triage multiple injuries appropriately to maximize patient outcomes and safety. The SVS suggested aortic repair within 24 h from presentation barring other serious injuries, or immediately following treatment of injuries [11]. Demetriades et al. reported decreased mortality in patients who underwent delayed repair of greater than 24 h from presentation but with longer lengths of stay in the ICU [20]. The authors did disclose that this decreased mortality could be attributed to the longer stay and greater care [20]. Similarly, Pate et al. intentionally delayed aortic repair between 2 days to 6 months (median 8 d) in patients who needed stabilization and management of other injuries [21].

In a retrospective study, Caffarelli et al. reported success in non-operative management of their patients but had lower ISS (mean 32.2), compared to our study with higher ISS (median 37) [12]. This lower mean ISS is likely a contributing factor to their success. This group did not stratify their patients by aortic injury grade, but instead by location of injury to the aorta. Rabin et al. reported similar findings and success to Caffarelli’s group with their non-operatively managed grade I and II patients but in a group of patients with higher ISS [13]. Rabin et al. reported 0% survival in non-operatively managed grade III patients [13]. In our study, of the two grade III non-operatively managed patients, only one patient survived (50% mortality). Gandhi et al. in a retrospective study on non-operative and TEVAR outcomes in grade III injuries also reported higher mortality in the non-operative group [22]. This is similar to the findings in our study. This study further reported TEVAR-related complications including graft involutions, endoleaks, and distal embolization [22]. In our patients who were managed with TEVAR, we did not find any stent-sequelae or re-operation for stent-related complications. These are important to monitor with CT imaging in younger patients with an anticipated normal life span keeping in mind the increased lifetime exposure of radiation. The discussed studies report successful non-operative management of BTAI patients with lesser grade injuries and decreased success in higher grade injuries, in agreement with our findings.

Irrespective of the major trends in our study, not all patients with BTAI require surgical repair. It is necessary to assess the patient’s secondary injuries, hemodynamic status, and the presence of TBI when determining candidacy for medical or surgical management. In most patients, initial medical resuscitation is needed, followed by risk stratification based on initial blood pressure. Future directions include widening the scope to a multi-center study with a larger number of patients. In these patients, the location of the aortic injury should also be characterized to see if there is any correlation of severity with aortic arch position. Finally, blood pressure measurements should be collected from patients to better reveal indications for beta-blockade.

Limitations

There are several limitations in our research. This is a retrospective study that could suffer from uncontrolled bias. This study also has a small sample size and due to this, has poor external validity. Due to this small sample size, results of the statistical analyses should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, follow-up CT imaging from date of presentation was inconsistent, with certain patients not receiving adequate imaging compared to others. Our follow-up timelines also differed between patients, with some patients lost to follow-up. Therefore, no standardized follow up management of this patient cohort limits the interpretation of the results. Our result should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Our descriptive study on BTAI of a higher grade (II-IV) showed higher mortality in non-operative management compared to operative management. Approximately 95% of patients in the operative group underwent TEVAR. Larger sample size multicenter prospective study with standardized follow up of non-operative management is needed to validate the results.
