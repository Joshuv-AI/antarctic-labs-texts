Antimicrobial Use in Acute Myocardial Infarction–Related Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Abduljabar Adi, Rohit Jain, Satvinder Guru, Ahmed Calvo, Dechen Samdrup, Sapana Yonghang, Sami Dalati, Moein Bayat Mokhtari, Ramsis Ramsis, Marcy Miller, Joshua Roubin, Nikolaos Diakos, Matthew Pierce, Matthew Griffin, Miguel Alvarez Villela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial use (AMU) in acute myocardial infarction–related cardiogenic shock is common, but its prevalence, drivers, and outcomes are not well described.MethodsWe analyzed 1095 patients with acute myocardial infarction–related cardiogenic shock from the multicenter Northwell‐Shock Registry (2016–2022). Patients undergoing coronary artery bypass surgery were excluded. AMU was defined as ≥24 hours of continuous therapy. Clinical features and outcomes were compared between AMU+ and AMU− groups.ResultsAntimicrobials were used in 55% of patients, and 70% of them were initiated within 48 hours of admission. Positivity was low in AMU+ cultures (12.6% blood, 20.4% urine), and resistant organisms were found in only 9.5% of positive blood cultures. In 70% of patients, AMU was prolonged despite negative cultures. Broad‐spectrum agents were used in 84.9%. Anti–methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus and antipseudomonal agents were used in >70% of patients with AMU. While length of stay was longer in AMU+ patients (11 versus 4 days, P<0.01), the overall mortality rate was similar (35.2% versus 37.2%, P=0.485).ConclusionsMore than half of patients with acute myocardial infarction–related cardiogenic shock received antimicrobials. Most patients received broad‐spectrum coverage despite low microbial culture yield and the rare isolation of resistant organisms. These findings highlight the need for targeted stewardship strategies to optimize AMU in acute myocardial infarction–related cardiogenic shock.

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a life‐threatening syndrome characterized by acute cardiac failure and hypoperfusion of vital organs. It is often caused by acute myocardial infarction or decompensated heart failure, and its pathophysiological hallmark is diminished cardiac output accompanied by compensatory increase in systemic vascular resistance (SVR). A substantial subset of patients also develops systemic vasodilation and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), both of which increase the risk of death in this setting. 1 , 2

SIRS and vasodilation may be associated with a concurrent infection but they may also be secondary to aseptic inflammation. 1 For instance, in a recent large observational study, 34% of all comers with CS had SIRS, but only 11.8% of those patients had a positive blood culture. 3 In the landmark SHOCK (Should We Emergently Revascularize Occluded Coronaries for Cardiogenic Shock) trial, SIRS (defined as fever, leukocytosis, or soft‐tissue infection) occurred in about 20% of patients, and only 74% of those had confirmed culture‐positive infections. 4 Due to this overlap, differentiating between sepsis and noninfectious SIRS remains a clinical challenge in CS, particularly before microbial culture results are available. 5

Given the established benefits of early antibiotic administration in suspected sepsis, the presence of SIRS in these patients often prompts empiric antibiotic use, 6 which may lead to unnecessary antimicrobial use (AMU) in patients without confirmed infections, with a potential for development of antibiotic resistance and adverse drug‐related events.

Despite these implications, the patterns of AMU in acute myocardial infarction–related cardiogenic shock (AMI‐CS) are poorly characterized. To address this gap and improve antibiotic stewardship in this population, we describe the prevalence of AMU in AMI‐CS, the factors associated with it, and the outcomes related to their use.

METHODS

The Northwell‐Shock Registry is a retrospective, multicenter study that includes patients with CS treated within the New York metropolitan area. The registry was approved by the Northwell Health Institutional Review Board with an informed consent waiver. All patient information was anonymized and stored securely in a protected database. The analytical methods used in this study are available to researchers outside the collaborative upon request.

Study Population

Adults (aged ≥18 years) treated at 11 Northwell hospitals with a unified electronic medical record system were included. All patients discharged between January 2016 and August 2022 with a primary or secondary diagnosis of CS (R57.0) and a concomitant diagnosis of non–ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction or ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) were included. A complete list of included International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes is found in Tables S1 through S6.

Patients who underwent coronary artery bypass surgery during their AMI‐CS admission were excluded. Patients were then stratified as the antimicrobial‐use group (AMU+) and no‐antimicrobial‐use group (AMU−). Only patients receiving antimicrobial therapy continuously for at least 24 hours were considered AMU+, while patients who received antimicrobial therapy for <24 hours were labeled as AMU− (Figure 1).

Definitions

Antimicrobial data were retrieved from the electronic medical records, with their indications obtained from the prescribing provider's order. Broad‐spectrum antibiotics were defined as those with activity against methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (eg, vancomycin) or against Pseudomonas aeruginosa (eg, piperacillin–tazobactam, cefepime, aztreonam, or meropenem).

Urine and blood cultures were the only microbial cultures included. A positive blood culture was defined as the detection of a pathogen in ≥1 samples, except for coagulase‐negative Staphylococcus, which required 2 positive samples isolating the same organism. A positive urine culture was defined as the presence of >10 000 colony‐forming units of any organism in a sample.

Fever was defined stringently using an adaptation of the Infectious Diseases Society of America criteria, as ≥2 body temperatures ≥38.0 °C or a single body temperature >38.3 °C. 7 Body temperature measurements in this study were taken using oral, rectal, bladder, or pulmonary artery catheter probes.

ICD‐10 codes were used to identify comorbidities and procedural data, including interventions like pulmonary artery catheterization (PAC), mechanical circulatory support devices, mechanical ventilation, transfusions, hemodialysis, percutaneous coronary intervention, and other interventions.

We collected vital signs, laboratory results, and hemodynamic data at admission (initial) and at the point of the worst recorded value during the hospital stay. The vasoactive‐inotropic score (VIS) was calculated on the basis of the types and doses of inotropes and vasopressors administered at admission and at their peak dosage levels. 8 The severity of shock was assessed at admission and at its highest severity using the Cardiogenic Shock Working Group modification of the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) shock stage classification. 9 The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV score was determined for all patients at the time of admission to the intensive care unit. 10

Data Analysis

Data analysis and graphical representation were carried out using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or as median with interquartile range, while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. An unpaired t test was used to compare continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine variables linked to antibiotic use. Missing values in the regression were imputed using the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations package in R. A P value threshold of <0.05 was set for statistical significance in this study.

RESULTS

Among 1095 patients with AMI‐CS (64.5% STEMI and 35.5% non‐STEMI), 603 (55.1%) received antimicrobials during their hospitalization. Of these, 45.1% received them within the first 24 hours, and the median time from admission to first antimicrobial dose was 26.0 hours (17.2–59.3; Figure S1).

Vancomycin was the single most used agent, followed by piperacillin–tazobactam, cefepime, and ceftriaxone. Most patients (83.7%) received a combination of different antibiotic classes. These combinations included vancomycin 86.7% of the time, cephalosporins 66.3% of the time, and piperacillin–tazobactam 63% of the time. Antifungals were used in 13.1% of patients, and antivirals were used rarely (5.1%).

Blood and urine cultures were more frequently collected among patients who received antimicrobials (AMU+) than among those who did not (AMU−): 82.8% versus 14.6% and 40.6% versus 4.9% (P<0.01 for both), respectively. The median time from admission to blood or urine cultures was 15.2 (6.0–45.5) hours in AMU+ patients and 11.6 (4.2–39.9) hours in AMU− patients (P=0.23).

Overall, culture positivity rates were low, at 5.3% (n=58) for blood, 4.1% (n=45) for urine, and 0.6% (n = 7) for both blood and urine in the same patient (Figure 3). These rates were substantially higher within the AMU+ group, 12.6% for blood culture and 20.4% for urine culture. Notably, among AMU+ patients, those with PAC had more blood cultures drawn (85.9% versus 77.4%, P=0.01) but a similar rate of culture positivity as non‐PAC patients (12.2% versus 13.5%, P=0.7).

The most commonly isolated organism in the urine among AMU+ patients was Escherichia coli. While in the blood, the most common were gram‐negative species and coagulase‐negative Staphylococcus. Although none of the patients had methicillin‐resistant S aureus as blood culture isolates, and only a small minority (9.5%) had P aeruginosa, most patients were treated with broad‐spectrum antibiotics with activity against those organisms (Figure 2).

The most common indications cited for antimicrobial prescription were empiric coverage (31%), pneumonia (24%), and periprocedural coverage (10%) (Figure 4). Meanwhile, positive blood cultures and urinary tract infection were listed as indications in only 4.5% and 7.0% of cases, respectively. The duration of antimicrobial therapy was significantly longer among patients with positive microbial cultures; 14.1 (5.5–28.4) days versus 5.8 (3.0–11.2) days (P<0.01) (Figure S4). However, 70.3% of all AMU+ patients (n=424) received antimicrobials for >48 hours despite having negative culture results (Table S2).

Clinical Characteristics According to Antimicrobial Use

Age, body mass index, and distribution of sex were similar between AMU+ and AMU− patients (Table 2). There were fewer patients with STEMI in the AMU+ group compared with the AMU− group (61.2% versus 68.5%, P=0.01).

The AMU+ group had higher rates of comorbidities, including hypertension (66.8% versus 49.2%, P<0.01), type 2 diabetes (45.4% versus 30.7%, P<0.01), coronary artery disease (46.3% versus 31.7%, P<0.01), and heart failure (53.1% versus 31.9%, P<0.01), along with a higher Charlson comorbidity index (6.2±3.3 versus 4.8±3.0, P<0.01). The prevalence of chronic kidney disease, on the other hand, was similar between groups.

The prevalence of fever (60.9% versus 7.5%, P<0.01) and the maximal body temperature during the hospitalization were higher among AMU+ patients (Table 3). Tachycardia (heart rate >90 bpm) and leukocytosis (white blood cell count >11.5×109/L) on admission were more prevalent in AMU+ patients (38.9% versus 23.2%, P<0.01) and (69.2% versus 55.9%, P<0.01), respectively. While creatinine and hemoglobin were significantly worse in AMU+ patients, other parameters of tissue perfusion including alanine aminotransferase, and lactate were similar (Table 3).

AMU+ patients presented more often with SCAI stages D and E but less in C, and at maximal shock severity, more patients in the AMU+ group progressed to stage E (66.3% versus 41.6%, P<0.01). The distribution of antimicrobial therapy use across presenting SCAI shock stages is shown in Table S3. Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation scores meanwhile were higher among AMU+ patients at the time of intensive care unit admission (95.2±35.0 versus 77.1±41.1, P<0.01).

Although initial VISs were comparable between groups, maximum VIS values were significantly higher in the AMU+ group. A greater proportion of AMU+ patients required inotropes (34.8% versus 14.2%) and vasopressors (57.7% versus 38.0%, P<0.01 for both). Although SVR index data were available only in the subset of patients monitored with pulmonary artery catheters, baseline SVR index values were similar between groups; however, the nadir SVR index was significantly lower in AMU+ patients (1626 versus 2057 dyn·s·cm−5·m−2, P<0.01), consistent with more profound vasodilation. (Table 3).

With regard to invasive procedures, percutaneous coronary intervention and transfusion rates were similar in both groups, but rates of every other invasive therapy were higher in AMU+ patients: mechanical circulatory support (66.2% versus 56.7%, P=0.01), PAC use (63.3% versus 39.8%, P<0.01), mechanical ventilation (67.7% versus 39.8%, P<0.01), and acute dialysis (22.4% versus 4.3%, P<0.01).

Outcomes

Length of hospital stay was markedly higher for patients receiving antimicrobials; 11 (6–20) days compared with 4 (1–7) days in the AMU− group (P<0.01). Among AMU+ patients, length of hospital stay was longer in patients with culture evidence of infection: 20 (11–33.3) versus 10 (6–18) (P<0.01). A full comparison between patients with and without culture evidence of infection is provided in Table S4.

A lower proportion of patients in the AMU+ group died within 48 hours of admission (5.3% versus 24.8%, P<0.01), but the overall unadjusted mortality rates were similar in both groups (35.2% versus 37.2%, P=0.5). However, survivors in the AMU+ group were more likely to be discharged to rehabilitation facilities (41.5% versus 19.3%, P<0.01) and less likely to be discharged to home (13.9% versus 33.9%, P<0.01).

A multivariable regression model was created for hospital death. Early empiric antimicrobial use was not independently associated with death, whereas positive microbial cultures (odds ratio [OR], 0.4 [95% CI, 0.2–0.8]) and prolonged antimicrobial use (>48 hours) despite negative cultures (OR, 0.3 [95% CI, 0.2–0.4]) were associated with lower hospital mortality risk. Revascularization with percutaneous coronary intervention was also associated with a lower mortality rate, while mechanical ventilation, acute hemodialysis, and advancing age were associated with higher mortality risk (Table S5).

Factors Associated With AMU

A multivariate logistic regression model using relevant clinical variables was also used to assess the factors associated with antimicrobial prescription, other than a positive microbial culture. Culture results were excluded since, as reflected in our data, most antibiotics are given within 48 hours of admission, before culture results are available.

After adjustment for relevant variables, AMU+ was most strongly associated with fever (OR, 11.0 [95% CI, 7.2–17.0]), acute dialysis (OR, 3.7 [95% CI, 2.0–6.9]), use of PAC (OR, 1.7 [95% CI, 1.2–2.6]), white blood cell count >11.5 (OR, 1.5 [95% CI, 1.0–2.3]), heart rate >90 (OR, 1.5 [95% CI, 1.0–2.1]), older age (OR, 1.3 [95% CI, 1.1–1.5]), and maximum VIS (OR, 1.2 [95% CI, 1.0–1.4]). On the other hand, AMU was negatively associated with a STEMI pathogenesis of CS (OR, 0.4 [95% CI, 0.3–0.6]) (Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

This multicenter, contemporary analysis of AMU in patients with AMI‐CS, treated either conservatively or percutaneously, provides critical insights into an understudied aspect of critical care cardiology.

Prior studies on AMU in AMI‐CS are limited, and current expert consensus statements on cardiogenic shock management offer no specific guidance for managing confirmed or suspected infections. 11 However, infectious complications in this population are consistently associated with a higher mortality rate. This increased risk likely drives the observed pattern of AMU seen in this study, which resembles the trends seen in national studies of suspected community‐acquired sepsis, and aligns with the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guideline recommendations. 4 , 6 , 12 , 13

In this study, signs of SIRS (fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis) and the presence of vasodilation (higher VIS) were independent drivers of AMU, while other markers of CS severity such as mechanical circulatory support use and lactate elevation were not. These findings reflect the importance of mixed shock signs in driving AMU prescriptions and the difficulty in distinguishing sepsis from aseptic SIRS in AMI‐CS, where18% of patients exhibit significant systemic inflammation and where peripheral biomarkers have shown limited diagnostic utility in this regard. 4 , 14

The association between PAC use and AMU is particularly noteworthy given the increasing adoption of invasive hemodynamic monitoring in contemporary CS management. The potential for catheter‐related infections, such as central line–associated bloodstream infections, may drive prophylactic AMU in these cases, especially considering the associated morbidity and financial penalties for health care institutions associated with central line–associated bloodstream infections. This issue deserves further investigation.

While microbial cultures were obtained in most patients with AMU, their diagnostic yield was low, consistent with prior studies. 3 , 5 Moreover, clinicians appeared to have limited confidence in negative culture results, as 70% of patients with AMU continued antibiotics for ≥48 hours despite negative cultures. Markers of illness severity such as higher SCAI–Cardiogenic Shock Working Group stages and mechanical ventilation were associated with prolonged AMU, suggesting that illness severity influences the duration of antimicrobial therapy more than the presence of confirmed infection. It is unclear in this regard, whether the training background of the physicians managing these patients, often cardiologists without specialized critical care training, may influence antibiotic stewardship.

The adverse outcomes associated with AMU in this study included prolonged length of hospital stay and increased discharge to skilled nursing facilities. However, other well‐documented consequences of antimicrobial overuse, such as antimicrobial resistance, adverse drug reactions, and Clostridioides difficile infections, were not fully evaluated. These findings underscore the potential overuse of antimicrobials in AMI‐CS and highlight the urgent need for tailored clinical guidelines.

On adjusted analysis, early empiric antimicrobial use was not independently associated with hospital death, whereas the presence of a positive microbial culture and the prolonged use of antimicrobials, despite negative cultures, were both associated with a lower mortality rate. These results are intriguing and may suggest that perhaps patients with septic SIRS, treatable with AMU, compared with aseptic SIRS, not treatable with AMU, have better outcomes regardless of the presence of positive cultures.

Hence, the use of early empiric targeted antimicrobials could be justified in these patients with a focus on deescalation based on clinical progression. At a minimum, societal recommendations or consensus statements should establish frameworks for hospital‐level performance improvement programs to optimize antibiotic stewardship in this population. Such initiatives could pave the way for future research to standardize the management of suspected infections in AMI‐CS and refine antimicrobial use, ultimately improving patient outcomes and reducing the risks associated with overuse.

Limitations

Besides the limitations inherent to its retrospective observational design, our study has several others. First, we cannot assess the presence of respiratory infections in this cohort since we did not have access to respiratory culture data or chest imaging. Given the high prevalence of mechanical ventilation in this population, this could lead to underestimation of the true burden of infection. Second, given the absence of data on respiratory rate, we cannot assess the prevalence of SIRS among these patients; rather, we can only describe the individual presence of the other 3 of its components. Third, we did not have SVR data on most patients and hence can only indirectly assess the presence of vasodilation on the basis of the use of vasopressors or low mean arterial pressure. In this same line, we can only quantify the combined use of vasopressors and inotropes using VIS and not that of vasopressors alone. Fourth, we do not have information on antibiotic deescalation for patients in the AMU+ group and therefore we cannot assess whether broad‐spectrum coverage was narrowed following culture results or with changing clinical conditions. Finally, we limited our study to patients with AMI‐CS since patients with heart failure–related CS represent a highly heterogeneous population with distinct clinical trajectories compared with AMI‐CS, which would complicate the interpretation of combined results. Thus, we have included baseline characteristics and outcomes of patients with heart failure–related CS in Table S6 for context.

CONCLUSIONS

Empiric antimicrobial use with broad‐spectrum coverage is high among patients with AMI‐CS despite the low incidence of culture‐proven infections and the infrequent detection of resistant organisms. Clinical factors such as signs of SIRS, higher need for inopressors, indwelling central venous catheters, and mechanical ventilation are associated with increased antimicrobial use, whereas mechanical circulatory support and higher SCAI stages are not. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship strategies to reduce their overuse and promote timely deescalation of antimicrobials in this population frequently affected by aseptic SIRS.
