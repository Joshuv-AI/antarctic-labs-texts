Swell-driven bursts of 26 s and 16 s seismic spectral peaks in the Gulf of Guinea
Authors: Piero Poli, Fabrice Ardhuin, Tomoya Takano, Stefania Tarantino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interaction between the atmosphere, hydrosphere, and solid Earth can result in the generation of complex seismological signals, offering significant insights into remote and enigmatic physical processes. Among these signals, the persistent narrow-band seismic energy observed in the Gulf of Guinea remains unexplained. Initially thought to reflect merely the propagation of oceanic swells, these signals have more recently been linked to the interaction between the ocean and the solid Earth. However, the quantitative relationship between oceanic activity and the occurrence of these signals remains unproven, and the underlying generation mechanism is not fully understood. In this study, we quantitatively explore the relationship between bursts of seismic energy and the passage of swells generated by distant storms in the Southern Ocean. Through an interdisciplinary analysis of seismological data, ocean wave models, and satellite altimeter data, here we report spatial, frequency and time-dependent patterns that suggest seismic signals may be associated with resonance cracks dynamically triggered by swell activity.Subject terms: Seismology, Physical oceanography

Introduction

The Earth system experiences background oscillations, identified as seismic noise, that have long puzzled seismologists. While quantitatively plausible causes have been identified for the dominant broadband signals between 1 and 300 s of period1, a particularly intriguing and unresolved question in geophysics concerns the generation of narrow-band spectral signals at periods of 16 and 26 s2–9. These oscillations have been observed globally in seismic data since the 1960s2.

Early studies suggested that the excitation of seismic waves by dispersive oceanic storms could explain these signals2. However, more recent observations of persistent and narrow-band signals at 26 s and 16 s have challenged this idea, prompting scientists to reconsider the ocean-driven mechanism6–9 and propose a broader range of hypotheses.

Among these alternative hypotheses, the idea of narrow-band signals resulting from source to receiver propagation effects of the Earth’s structure has been promptly rejected due to the unrealistically high attenuation values required to generate such signals3,4. Instead, the focus has shifted towards understanding the combined response of both the ocean and the solid Earth at the source position as the likely mechanism for generating these signals6–9. This perspective suggests a coupled interaction between oceanic processes and the Earth’s geological structures, potentially giving rise to these seismic signals.

This latter idea appears in many studies3–10 that proposed more specific mechanisms, speculating on the presence of volcanic structures or fluid reservoirs beneath the region where the signals are generated. According to these studies, the 16 s and 26 s peaks may result from fluid resonance phenomena, potentially triggered by either volcanic activity or interactions with oceanic waves2–10. This latter hypothesis has been theoretically supported by numerical models simulating the interaction between oceanic waves and volcanic structures10. However, while these models suggest the viability of such a mechanism, a clear and quantitative evaluation of this theory has yet to be provided.

Overall, none of these studies has definitively resolved the question of what generates the 16 s and 26 s signals. The uncertainty is further compounded by the fact that most studies have concentrated on individual aspects of the problem, either seismic observations3–9 or modeling10, without integrating interdisciplinary approaches into a cohesive framework that can be tested against potential triggering mechanisms.

In response to this challenge, we combine high-resolution seismological observations of the time evolution of the 16 s and 26 s signals over a three-year period with frequency-dependent oceanic wave models11 and data from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) satellite mission12.

Here, we show that the integration of seismic observations, ocean wave models, and satellite measurements provides a detailed and quantitative characterization of the processes responsible for these enigmatic spectral peaks. We demonstrate a clear relationship between frequency-dependent ocean swells and the generation of microseismic signals. These observations constrain the range of possible source mechanisms and provide a framework for improved modeling of the processes responsible for their generation.

Results

We analyze four years of continuous seismic data (2021–2024) from a network of stations in southern France (Fig. 1a) to better understand the origins of the 16- and 26-s microseismic peaks from the Gulf of Guinea2–9,11. Using an array analysis technique commonly applied to volcanic tremors13, we investigate spectral peaks in vertical seismic records. While typically used at higher frequencies (>1 Hz), this method has proven effective at lower frequencies for detecting continuous spectral peaks at teleseismic distances14.

Seismic data were segmented into 20-min intervals, windowed with a Hanning function, and transformed into the frequency domain to compute cross-spectra between stations. We then estimated the cross-spectral density (CSD) by averaging over four-hour intervals with 50% overlapping segments. To enhance continuously excited signals and suppress transient events like earthquakes14, prewhitening was applied before CSD computation. The absolute value of the cross-spectral density was then used to visually identify periods when the 16- and 26-s peaks become prominent (Fig. 2b).

To infer the directionality and propagation of the signals, we used the phase information from the cross-spectral density to estimate slowness and azimuth via beamforming (14, Figs. S1 and S2). This approach helped pinpoint the azimuth of the seismic sources responsible for the observed spectral peaks and link them to specific regions of the Gulf of Guinea (Fig. 1b).

From both coherence and beamforming results (Fig. 2b, Figs S1 and S2), we identified a set of reference signals, which were then used in a similarity search (13, 14; see “Methods” section) on the cross-spectral density to create a catalog of times when seismic signals at 16 and 26 s were prominently detected across the array. With this seismological analysis, we detected 359 time windows of 2 h with a strong 16-s peak and 904 windows for the 26-s peak, respectively representing approximately 4% and 7% of the total analyzed time. For each time window, we extracted the maximum value of beamforming along with the corresponding azimuth and slowness. The seismic wavefield for these events, resolved with the array in France (Fig. 1a), consistently showed Rayleigh waves with a slowness of ~0.3 s/km, arriving from an azimuth of 179 degrees for both peaks (Fig. 1b, Fig. S2), consistent with a source in the Gulf of Guinea.

Unlike previous studies relying on near-source stations2–9, our results do not show a continuous presence of these peaks, likely due to their low amplitude at large distances and the short (two-hour) timescale used for CSD calculations. However, our approach effectively captures bursts of 16- and 26-s activity, which have been linked to oceanic processes2. Additionally, unlike amplitude-based methods9, our approach relies on the complex cross-spectral density and phase similarity, ensuring that detected signals originate from consistent source positions (13, 14; see “Methods” section).

To further constrain the source location, we analyzed data from a seismic station array in Cameroon, which was operational throughout 2006 (Fig. 1b). As data for this array are not sufficiently continuous in time, they were not used for coherence analysis. Although this array does not overlap in time with the French one, we assume that the source location of the 16 s and 26 s peaks remained stable over time, as suggested by the stable source direction resolved by the French array (Fig. S1). Using the same approach as for the French array, we estimated the cross-spectral density and source azimuth through beamforming for several large events. The source positions were then determined at the intersections between the great-circle paths inferred from the azimuths and the central locations of each array. The resulting source directions for both the 26 s and 16 s peaks pointed toward the Gulf of Guinea (Fig. 1b). Together with the results from the French array, these findings suggest a source consistent with previous studies8.

To compare the seismological observations with oceanic wave activity in the Gulf of Guinea, we extracted the power spectral density of sea surface elevation every two hours from a global ocean wave model11 based on the WAVEWATCH III framework, which uses atmospheric wind reanalysis to predict wave spectra and their evolution. The power spectral density of sea surface elevation for each frequency bin is then transformed into wave height. This model includes effects of currents on ocean waves but does not consider water level, and was validated with satellite measurements, including a specific verification of wave heights over 10 m15 and energy levels at periods longer than 20 s using the Surface Water Ocean Topography mission, as detailed in the “Methods” section (Fig. S3; see “Methods” section).

Integrated analysis

We begin exploring the relationship between the generation of 16 s and 26 s narrow-band signals by comparing the average oceanic wave height over a region encompassing the Gulf of Guinea for the 16 s and 26 s periods, alongside the corresponding seismic coherence plots (Fig. 2, Figs. S4 and S5; see “Methods” section), covering the time frame from July to August 2023. During this period, a storm catalog15 identifies the largest Southern Ocean storms, ADC-1289 and ADC-4717, which reached peak intensity on 2023-07-24T14 and 2023-08-05T22, respectively. These storms had modeled significant wave heights of up to 14.9 and 12.5 m, associated with peak periods of 16.1 and 15.4 s, and radiated swells containing energy at periods longer than the peak period, up to at least 25 s (15, Videos 1–12).

Our comparison of oceanic wave height and seismic coherence (Fig. 2) indicated that the seismic signal at 16 s becomes activated when the wave height in 10%-wide period bands exceeds several tens of centimeters (Fig. 2a, b). A similar pattern is observed for the seismic signal at 26 s (Fig. 2b, c), which is activated as the wave height surpasses a few centimeters. However, it is important to note that the seismic coherence remains high even after the wave-height peaks have passed. This suggests that while the activation of the seismic signal is triggered by the onset of significant wave events, the seismic response may persist even for days after the waves have diminished, indicating a more complex interaction between the seismic and oceanic systems that extends beyond the local waves.

In addition to the activation of the 16 s and 26 s peaks, which coincide with significant swell activity at those periods in the Gulf of Guinea, we also observe gliding signals8 with frequencies that increase over time. These signals exhibit a seismic coherence lower than the 16 and 26 s peaks, but are particularly evident in early August 2023, as well as in other periods (Figs. S4–S10). The observed frequency and time evolution of the gliding signals closely mirror the trends in the modeled wave spectrum (Figs. S6–S10), which is the known result of the progressive arrival of dispersive swells generated by distant storms16.

To gain further insights into the relationship between seismic peaks and ocean wave activity, we focus on the period from July 26 to 30, 2023, during which the 26 s seismic coherence is observed to be significantly high (Fig. 2). In this context, we illustrate the spatiotemporal evolution of wave height in Fig. 3, providing a detailed representation of storms and swell activity in the Southern Atlantic Ocean. This analysis reveals the formation of storm ADC-1289 around 8 a.m. on July 24, which radiates 26 s swells propagating across the Southern Atlantic and reaching the South African coast by 4 p.m. on July 26. During this period, seismic coherence remains low but begins to rise after 4 p.m. on July 27, as the 26 s swell reaches the coastal region of the Gulf of Guinea and the São Tomé and Príncipe Islands (Fig. 3, Fig. S15).

Spatiotemporal evolution of 26-s swell and associated seismic coherence.

Spatiotemporal evolution of wave heights for a 26 s period during the time span shown in Fig. 2b. The top panel displays seismic coherence at a 26 s period, with a red line indicating the average coherence over this time window.

In stark contrast, between midnight on July 24 and the early hours of July 27, long-period swell heights in the coastal part of the Gulf of Guinea remain notably low, accompanied by weak seismic coherence, despite relatively high swell heights offshore. However, between July 25 and 4 p.m. on July 27, swell heights are elevated along the coasts from South Africa to Namibia, to the south of the Gulf of Guinea. Nevertheless, no coherent seismic signals are observed, suggesting that unique conditions in the Gulf of Guinea amplify the generation of swell-induced seismic signals.

Similar patterns to those shown in Figs. 2 and 3 are observed for other periods (Figs. S11–S15; Videos 1–12). This spatial, frequency, and temporal analysis provides evidence that swells with periods of 16 or 26 s, generated by distant storms17, trigger seismic peaks at the corresponding periods as they propagate toward the coastal region of the Gulf of Guinea. Moreover, certain time intervals characterized by significant swell activity in the Gulf of Guinea coincide with the generation of gliding signals (Figs. S6–S10, Videos 1–12).

The activation of seismic peaks during swell passages in the Gulf of Guinea region, observed across multiple time windows, strongly suggests an ocean-driven enhancement of the mechanism that generates the seismic peaks with periods 26 s and 16 s. These findings additionally indicate that ocean swells, particularly those generated by strong Southern Ocean storms that produce waves with similar periods, play an important role in modulating the resonant seismic signals originating in the region.

Quantification of oceanic triggering

To quantitatively corroborate the hypothesis that bursts of spectral peaks observed in the seismic data are enhanced by swell activity, we systematically explore the relationships between the occurrence of these peaks and wave height at different frequencies. Building on previous studies that have investigated the triggering of seismic events, such as earthquakes and non-volcanic tremors, by external forcings, such as tides18,19, we undertake a detailed analysis of the catalog of seismically detected peaks at periods of 26 s and 16 s with our similarity search (see “Methods” section). We pair these seismic events with estimates of wave height from the time of each episode to assess whether there is a correlation between the two phenomena.

Specifically, to test whether the occurrence of the 26 s and 16 s bursts is influenced by swells of the same periods, we extracted the average wave height in the coastal region of the Gulf of Guinea, considering frequencies in the range 0.02–0.1 Hz, for each occurrence time of the 16 s and 26 s events. This region was selected based on its known association with the source of the 26 s and 16 s seismic peaks (2–8; Fig. 1b). We then generated 1000 synthetic catalogs, each containing the same number of events as the original 26 s and 16 s catalogs (see “Methods” section), but with the events randomly distributed in time. For each of the random events, we again extracted the corresponding frequency-dependent wave height. This procedure allows us to estimate the average sea state under the null hypothesis, where the activation of the spectral peaks is not driven by large swells but rather occurs randomly in time. We then estimated the ratio of the wave height estimated during 26 s and 16 s bursts against the one for random times. If there were no relationship between seismic signals and oceanic swell activity, the ratio of average wave heights between the real and random catalogs would be close to 1.

Our analysis reveals compelling results (Fig. 4). For the 16 s peak, the maximum wave-height ratio is observed at a period close to that of the seismologically observed peak, with the ratio decreasing at other analyzed periods (Fig. 4a). This observation indicates that the activation of the 16 s peak is particularly sensitive to swells at the same period. A similar pattern is noted for the 26 s peak, where the wave-height ratio reaches its maximum.

To confirm these observations, we conducted a statistical analysis using a modified Quantile–Quantile plot (QQ-plot)19 peaks occurrence (seismic events) with ocean wave heights at 16 s and 26 s. In its classical form, a QQ-plot is used to test whether a dataset follows a theoretical distribution (e.g., a normal distribution). Here, we instead examined whether the temporal distribution of seismic events depends on the temporal distribution of ocean wave heights at the same periods. We first computed the normalized cumulative distribution function (nCDF) of time vs. wave heights, which describes the fraction of time spent at or below a given wave height. We then compared it with the nCDF of seismic events vs. wave heights, which represents the fraction of events occurring at or below a given wave height (see “Methods” section, Fig. S16). If the two distributions are unrelated, meaning that high and low coherence values occur randomly in time with no preference for particular wave-height conditions, events are cumulated proportionally to time, regardless of the wave-height level. In this case, the QQ-plot should show a straight line from [0,0] to [1,1], and there is no systematic redistribution of events toward low or high wave heights (Fig. S16). On the other hand, points falling below the diagonal indicate a deficit up to the corresponding wave height. In the modified QQ-plot (Fig. 4c, d, Fig. S16), we remove the 1:1 trend line19 so that any deviation is shown directly: if there is no relationship, the result should be a flat horizontal line between [0,0] and [1,0].

We further validated our results by randomly reshuffling the coherence time series and comparing the observed values with these randomized baselines. The pronounced departure from randomness indicates a strong relationship between wave heights and peaks in seismic coherence (Fig. 4c, d, 20), with the right-skewed distribution suggesting that increasing wave height amplifies the effect on seismic activity19. In particular, we retrieve a positive correlation in both cases, as indicated by the negative amplitude of the curves (Fig. 4c, d), which show a deficit of events up to the corresponding wave heights compared to what would be expected based on the time spent at those wave heights19.

Both analyses strengthen the hypothesis that the height and specific period of swell activity play a critical role in triggering the seismic signals. Our findings strongly indicate a causal relationship between the occurrence of large swell episodes and the activation of bursts of 26 s and 16 s microseismic peaks.

Ocean–solid Earth interactions and seismic peak generation

The main result of our study is the quantification of the synchronicity between the 26-s and 16-s oceanic swell intensity in the Gulf of Guinea and the occurrence of the narrow-band seismic peak bursts at the corresponding frequencies. Building on this observation, mechanism(s) capable of generating such narrow-band signals are discussed.

Propagation effect

A possible model for the generation of narrow-band seismic signals invokes propagation effects, whereby attenuation acts as a frequency-selective filter that enhances seismic energy at specific frequencies4. Within this framework, the observed synchronization between oceanic swell and seismic signals may be interpreted in terms of the primary microseism generation mechanism20,21, producing signals that acquire a peaked spectral shape during propagation from the source to the seismic stations4. However, this explanation has largely been discounted for the narrow-band peaks observed in the Gulf of Guinea, as it would require unrealistically high attenuation values at the global scale4.

Peaks from ocean–seafloor interaction

The observed synchronicity between swell and generation of seismic signals (Figs. 2–4) implies that the generation of peaks and gliding signals involves a combined action of the ocean and the solid Earth within the source region4. A possible candidate is an enhanced primary microseismic mechanism1,20,21, associated with specific bathymetric features that promote the coupling of ocean wave energy into seismic signals20. Given the difficulty of modeling primary microseisms, since it requires detailed knowledge of seafloor slopes and bathymetry1,20,21, we only aim here at testing whether, under certain seafloor configurations, the observed peaks and gliding can be reproduced. To this end, we focus on August 2023, a period of significant swell activity in the Gulf of Guinea, driven by a series of storms originating in the Southern Ocean (Figs. 2, 3, Supplementary Videos 1–12). We then model the frequency-dependent seismic displacement recorded at the EDA Geoscope seismic station22, located along the coast of Cameroon (Fig. 1b). Using the directional spectrum from an oceanic model (11; see “Methods” section), we simulate the seismic amplitude at EDA by testing a wide range of source geometries with variable circular areas and depths, and by incorporating bottom topography spectra representative of rocky and sandy seafloors (20; see “Methods” section). Source depths are constrained to values shallower than ~100 m, since significant wave–seafloor interactions occur only when the water depth is less than the swell wavelength20,21. This condition is consistent with the bathymetric depths in the source area identified by our beamforming analysis (Fig. S18) as well as by previous studies8. We additionally consider, based on previous studies8, that the 26 s peak and gliding signals share a common source area.

The modeling results for a sandy seafloor20, consistent with the presence of sediments in the Gulf of Guinea23–26, are shown in Fig. 6 (results for a rocky seafloor are provided in Fig. S18). As expected, the modeling is affected by a trade-off between source area and sea depth1,20. Nevertheless, under specific configurations, the timing and amplitude of the displacement recorded at EDA can be reproduced at 0.05 Hz and 0.06 Hz, frequencies that fall within the gliding band. In contrast, modeling at frequencies around 0.035 Hz, which includes the 26 s peak, reveals that while the displacement amplitude can be partly reproduced, the timing of the observed signal is not well captured. Moreover, the source areas required to generate such large-amplitude signals are 10 to 100 times greater than those needed to reproduce the gliding amplitudes. Comparable results are obtained when assuming a rocky seafloor (Fig. S17).

Our modeling suggests that gliding signals may be interpreted as the response of specific seafloor conditions to swell, through an amplified primary microseism mechanism20,21. This interpretation highlights the need for improved bathymetric data to assess whether particular seafloor depth patterns exist in the Gulf of Guinea that could enable the generation of strong gliding signals. We note that the general east-west orientation of the shoreline promotes a higher amplitude of gliding signals associated with swell from the south, with the same periods arriving at the same time along coastlines from Senegal to Cameroon. In this view, and taking into account the synchronicity of our coherence measurements with the swell passage (Fig. 2, Figs. S4–S10, Videos 1–12), gliding would simply represent the seafloor’s response to the passage of swell.

Concerning the narrow-band peaks, our analysis indicates that no tested configuration for the primary microseismic origin of the peaks is able to reproduce both the amplitude and the timing of the 26 s simultaneously recorded at EDA. This evidence, together with the decay-like behavior observed for the 26 s signals (Fig. 5; see “Methods” section), suggests that although oceanic activity contributes to their generation, an additional mechanism is likely required to enhance their amplitude and alter the temporal evolution of the primary oceanic forcing.

Resonance of a fluid-filled reservoir

A plausible mechanism for the generation of narrow-band peaks is resonance driven by fluid vibrations within cracks27,28, as proposed in previous studies2,6,11. To explore this possibility, the expected resonant frequency and attenuation are forward-modeled for fluid-filled cracks with variable aperture and length, assuming that these structures are filled either with basaltic magma or with water (27, 28; see “Methods” section). The results show that crack-like structures with lengths of a few tens of kilometers and apertures of several hundreds of meters can reproduce the observed resonant frequencies and Q values when basaltic magma is assumed (Fig. 6). In contrast, when magma is replaced by water, the required crack dimensions are significantly smaller, on the order of a few kilometers in length and a few meters in aperture (Fig. 6).

The presence of fluid-filled reservoirs in the source region of the 26 s and 16 s peaks has been suggested by previous studies2,6,11 and is further supported by geological observations reporting seafloor seepage structures, such as pockmarks and mud volcanoes, as well as large oil and gas reservoirs23–26,29,30. The seepage structures on the seafloor are associated with the release of gas or fluids originating from source rocks at depths ranging from a few kilometers to approximately 10 km23–26,29,30. Fluids migrate upward along conduits with meter-scale apertures29,30, likely exploiting inherited tectonic structures that either extend from source rocks to the seafloor or span several hundreds of meters23–26,29,30 before reaching the seafloor29. Notably, the kilometric lengths and metric apertures inferred from the crack modeling, assuming water, are broadly consistent with the dimensions of such geological structures (Fig. 6).

Such a model is consistent with previous studies9, while also providing additional constraints on the length, width, and fluid properties of the resonant structures, and supporting an explanation for the generation of narrow-band tremors that does not require undocumented active volcanic systems. Additionally, the inferred kilometric lengths of the resonant structures are also consistent with the characteristic length scale of a moment magnitude ~5 source27 previously suggested for these peaks9.

It should be emphasized, however, that the model employed here, although validated for realistic crack geometries27, involves simplifying assumptions. In particular, it accounts only for viscous attenuation, neglecting other potentially relevant attenuation mechanisms27, and the inferred attenuation may be weakly influenced by propagation effects31. Moreover, alternative interpretations, such as resonance associated with thin-plate vibrations10 or more complex geometries, remain viable. Further observational and theoretical work will therefore be required to better discriminate among competing models and to constrain the most appropriate physical mechanism.

Resonance between oceanic forcing and reservoirs

Through our quantification of the oceanic triggering of the 26 s and 16 s spectral peaks (Fig. 4), a relationship previously only suggested in earlier studies2,6,10, we demonstrate the existence of a resonance between oceanic activity and the seismic source mechanism. Specifically, these spectral peaks are excited only when ocean swells with comparable periods traverse the source region.

This behavior is analogous to resonance phenomena observed between shallow water reservoirs and oceanic forcing along the west coast of Japan32, as well as to the coupling between long-period seismic waves and volcanic crack-like structures33,34. Laboratory experiments investigating the dynamic response of fluid-filled cracks have shown that dynamic shaking can induce fluid migration, with the efficiency of this process strongly enhanced when the forcing frequency approaches the crack’s resonance frequency33.

We therefore propose that a similar mechanism is responsible for the excitation of fluid-filled structures that resonate at 26 s and 16 s (Fig. 6). Within this framework, resonance is triggered by the primary microseism, either through static loading or through dynamic shaking of the crack by microseismic Rayleigh waves (Fig. 7). The resulting fluid motion within these cracks provides a plausible source for the narrow-band spectral peaks observed in the study region (Fig. 7).

Proposed mechanism for excitation of narrow-band seismic signals.

Oceanic forcing at periods of 16 s and 26 s (orange arrows) produces static (red arrows) and dynamic (black arrows) loading associated with seismic waves from primary microseisms (black lines). These loads act on fluid conduits that extend through the entire or part of the sedimentary layer and reach the seafloor at seepage structures. When the static and dynamic loading periods approach the resonance frequency of the crack, fluids may be set in motion34, generating narrow-band Rayleigh waves.

Discussion

By integrating ocean wave models and satellite observations with seismic data, we show that the 16 s and 26 s seismic bursts are modulated by ocean swells generated in the Southern Ocean and arriving in the Gulf of Guinea. The activation of these signals is temporally synchronized with swell passage over the source region, with each peak emerging as the external forcing approaches its corresponding resonance period. Modeling of the primary microseismic response indicates that the resulting narrow spectral peaks cannot be explained by standard microseism excitation alone, requiring an additional amplification mechanism, which we model as a resonating, fluid-filled crack in the shallow crust.

Taken together, these observations and modeling results support the interpretation of dynamically triggered, fluid-filled crack-like structures that resonate independently in response to frequency-dependent swell forcing. Such structures are consistent with the documented abundance of fluid-related features within the sedimentary cover of the Gulf of Guinea23–26,29,30. Overall, our results provide a coherent and physically grounded explanation for the long-standing observations of the 16-s and 26-s seismic signals.

Methods

Seismic data

We collect seismic data from an array of stations in southern France (Fig. 1a), selected for its temporal consistency and high data quality, which enables the detection and analysis of faint seismic signals over short time scales (2 h). The dataset spans 2020 to 2023 and is processed using standard techniques, including filtering, resampling to 1 Hz, and instrument response correction to obtain displacement. Additionally, we apply the same processing to data from a temporary seismic array in Cameroon (Fig. 1b) recorded in 20068. This dataset is used exclusively for signal localization via beamforming15. Finally, we process data from the Geoscope station EDA22, located in coastal Cameroon (Fig. 1b), following the same preprocessing steps.

Building a catalog of events with similarity search

To build a catalog of discrete occurrences of events for the 16 s and 26 s peaks, we employ a similarity approach based on CSD14, applied to data from 2021 to 2024. We begin by selecting representative 16 s and 26 s episodes as templates, ensuring they are distributed across all seasons and span the entire analysis period. These events represent the references for a phase-matched search13,14. In practice, we compute the similarity with coherence (ranging from 0 to 1) between the signals of each template and the rest of the dataset. This process provides a detection time series spanning 4 years with a sampling rate of 2 h. We then identify a detection threshold at a coherence level equal to 3 times the standard deviation of the coherence recorded over 4 years. Visual inspection indicates that new events are reliably detected when similarity exceeds this threshold. An example of a detection trace for a reference event is reported in Fig. S19. As the same template can detect multiple signals, we remove these multiple detections by keeping only the one with the largest coherence every 2 h. The final catalog is then composed of 359 bursts at the 16 s peak and 884 at the 26 s peak. The detection traces are available for other researchers35.

Wave height from WAVEWATCHIII

The oceanic model11 provides a wave frequency spectrum E(f), representing the power spectral density (PSD) of sea surface elevation at discrete frequencies (fi) with a geometric progression such that fi+1 = 1.1 fi. To define a frequency-dependent wave height, we use the model frequency bandwidth df=fi*(1.1−1/1.1)/2. We then calculate the wave height for each band as H(fi)=4*E(fi)*df. We note that such a frequency-dependent wave height is not a usual parameter used in oceanography, but it may be more intuitive than E(f). For plotting purposes, we use the mean significant wave height over the coastal region of the Gulf of Guinea, defined as 5°–11° in longitude and 0.5°–8° in latitude, to construct time series such as those shown in Fig. 2a, c.

Validation of the oceanic model with SWOT

The realism of the ocean wave model employed in our analysis and event timing was evaluated using wave-height measurements from satellite altimeters near the storm centers15, and sea level measurements from SWOT, which provide detailed spectral distributions of ocean wave energy for wave periods longer than 18 s. For example, Fig. S3 shows long swells with wave heights of 10 and 20 cm on 8 August and 19 September, respectively, in the spatial frequency range of 0.6 to 1.7 cycles per kilometer, corresponding to periods of 19–33 s in deep water. SWOT captures snapshots of wave fields along its track every 21 days, and the peak in swell height around 10°S aligns with the model’s predicted swell propagation within 6 h. The consistency between the spatial extent of the swell event in both the satellite data and model output supports the model’s predicted event duration. This combined analysis provided valuable insights into wave conditions during periods when seismic signals were most prominent. By comparing modeled wave heights with seismic data, we explored potential temporal correlations between spectral peak activations and changes in wave height, particularly in the frequency bands related to the observed seismic signals.

Exploring triggering

We extracted the mean significant wave height over the coastal region of the Gulf of Guinea, considering a region spanning 5°–11° in longitude and 0.5°–8° in latitude, and retaining the ocean wave spectra in the 0.02–0.1 Hz band. This region was selected because it has been consistently identified as the source area of the narrow-band peaks (2–8; Fig. 1b). For each event in the 26 s and 16 s catalogs, the corresponding wave height was sampled at the event time.

To establish a baseline expectation, we constructed 1000 synthetic catalogs in which the same number of events as in the real catalogs were randomly redistributed in time. For each realization, the associated wave heights were extracted in the same manner as for the observed events, yielding the null distribution of average wave heights under the assumption of no causal link with swell activity.

We then quantified the degree of coupling by calculating the ratio between the observed average wave heights and those obtained from the ensemble of randomized catalogs. Ratios close to unity indicate no dependence, whereas systematic deviations provide evidence for a statistical relationship between swell amplitudes and the triggering of the 26 s and 16 s seismic bursts.

Modified QQ-plot

To compare ocean wave heights and the 16 s (26 s) seismic coherence distributions, we construct a modified QQ-plot19. The method involves the following steps:

nCDF of Time versus Wave Heights – The ocean wave-height values at 16 s (26 s) period are first sorted in ascending order and divided into discrete levels. For each level, we calculate the total time spent at or below that wave height. The resulting cumulative distribution is then normalized, yielding the nCDF of time with respect to wave heights.

nCDF of Time versus Wave Heights – The ocean wave-height values at 16 s (26 s) period are first sorted in ascending order and divided into discrete levels. For each level, we calculate the total time spent at or below that wave height. The resulting cumulative distribution is then normalized, yielding the nCDF of time with respect to wave heights.

nCDF of Seismic events versus Wave Heights – A second nCDF is computed by relating seismic events at 16 s (26 s) to wave height. For each wave-height level, we cumulate the seismic events occurring at or below that level, iterating over all levels. This cumulative distribution is also normalized.

nCDF of Seismic events versus Wave Heights – A second nCDF is computed by relating seismic events at 16 s (26 s) to wave height. For each wave-height level, we cumulate the seismic events occurring at or below that level, iterating over all levels. This cumulative distribution is also normalized.

Comparison via Modified QQ-Plot – The two normalized distributions are compared in a modified QQ-plot20. Unlike a standard QQ-plot, the 1:1 reference line is removed, so that deviations can be interpreted directly as an excess or deficit of seismic events at or below a given wave-height level. This allows us to more easily assess whether a relationship exists between the two distributions.

Modeling seismic displacement from swells

We apply the primary microseismic mechanism20,21 to model the frequency-dependent displacement at seismic station EDA (Fig. 1). Unlike classical approaches21, we adopt the method by Ardhuin20, which accounts for the interaction between directional ocean dynamics and the spectrum of seafloor elevation. Since the spectrum is not available for the Gulf of Guinea, we use representative spectra from the rocky and sandy bottoms off the coasts of Ireland and Belgium, as discussed in ref. 21. As described in the main text, we also estimate the displacement for a wide range of source depths and circular source areas centered on the location inferred from beamforming (Fig. 1). For each seafloor spectrum, depth, and source area, we compute the misfit as the total absolute difference (L1 norm) between observed and modeled data. These results are presented in Fig. 6, together with the observed displacement and the modeled displacement from the three best-fitting configurations. The procedure is repeated for each frequency band analyzed.

Attenuation estimate for 26 s peaks

Focusing on the large 26 s burst in August 2023, we estimate the attenuation parameter (Q) by fitting the displacement in the time period 10–18 of August, when the displacement remains above the background level. The displacement (d) is fitted in a semi-logarithmic domain using the function log(d(t))=−at. The parameter a in this model is related to the attenuation (Q) and frequency of the peak (f) trough: a=f/2Q. Although path effects are not explicitly accounted for in our analysis, they are expected to be represented by a Q value approximately an order of magnitude smaller32 than our measurement of Q ≈ 2400 and therefore to have only a marginal impact on our estimate.

Crack modeling

The resonance frequency and quality factor of a crack are estimated using an analytical approach27, assuming the crack is filled with either basaltic magma or water. This model treats a two-dimensional, fluid-filled crack whose aperture is much smaller than the wavelength. The model linearizes the dynamics, accounting for quasi-static elastic deformation of the crack walls and for the fluid’s viscosity, inertia, and compressibility. At long wavelengths, the mode behaves as a crack wave in which wall compliance provides the restoring force. Attenuation is taken to arise solely from fluid viscosity; seismic radiation attenuation is neglected. The formulation couples width-averaged mass conservation and momentum conservation with an elastic wall displacement-stress relation and a viscous wall shear-stress boundary condition, yielding a dispersion relation Dk,f=0, with f and k being the modal angular frequencies and wavenumber, respectively. Solving D=0 determines the modal angular frequencies and, in turn, the dominant frequency and the quality factor of viscous attenuation. We assumed the following material properties. For the solid part: Poisson’s ratio of 0.25, P-wave velocity of 5 km/s, and density of 2700 kg/m3. For the fluid part: Basaltic magma has a sound speed of 1 km/s and a density of 2500 kg/m3, water has a sound speed of 1.5 km/s and a density of 1000 kg/m3. Viscosities are 102 Pa*s for basaltic magma and 10−3 Pa*s for water. Oil, which is likely to be present in the region23–26, has properties similar to water and yields comparable results.
