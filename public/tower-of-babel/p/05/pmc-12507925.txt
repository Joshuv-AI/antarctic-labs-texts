External validation of the ACS NSQIP surgical risk calculator for the prediction of surgical site infection (SSI) and its association with the postoperative occurrence of incisional hernia (IH) in midline laparotomy patients
Authors: Edgard Efrén Lozada-Hernández, Luis A Martin-del-Campo, Luis Alberto Fernández-Vázquez-Mellado, Héctor Ali Valenzuela Alpuche, Enrique Ricardo Jean-Silver, H Alejandro Rodríguez, Ricardo Reynoso González, Irma Lisseth Maldonado-Barrios, Sebastián Amador-Ramírez, Roland Kevin Cethorth Fonseca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIncisional hernia (IH) is a common complication following midline laparotomy, with an incidence of 12.8% at 23.8 months of follow-up. Surgical site infection (SSI) is a significant risk factor for IH. The ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator is a widely used tool for predicting postoperative complications, including SSI. This study aims to externally validate this calculator in a population of patients undergoing midline laparotomy.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal study was conducted on 1083 patients who underwent midline laparotomy between January 2015 and November 2022. The ability of the ACS NSQIP calculator to predict SSI and its association with the occurrence of IH was evaluated.ResultsThe incidence of SSI was 13.21%. The ACS NSQIP calculator demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC 0.88) and calibration (Brier score 0.072) for predicting SSI. An optimal cutoff point of 6.6 was identified, with a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 92%. The presence of SSI increased the risk of IH by a factor of 6 (OR 5.9, 95% CI 3.4–10.3).ConclusionsThe ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator is a valid tool for predicting SSI in patients undergoing midline laparotomy, with good discriminative ability and calibration. SSI is a significant risk factor for the occurrence of IH, suggesting that including this variable in IH prediction scales could improve their accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03807-4.Keywords: Incisional hernia, Surgical site infection, Midline laparotomy, ACS NSQIP, Risk prediction

Introduction

The main complication after midline laparotomy is incisional hernia (IH), with an overall incidence of 12.8% at 23.8 months of follow-up, although this rate may be greater in high-risk groups [1], and a 5-year recurrence rate after ventral hernia repair is greater than 40% and 70% in patients with and without mesh, respectively [2]. The variability in incidence is related to the study population evaluated, the type of incision made, the follow-up and the method with which the hernia is diagnosed [3].

IH has an enormous economic impact; in the United States, the costs associated with the care of this complication are 4 billion dollars annually, and it is estimated that a 1% reduction in the incidence could result in a savings of 32 million dollars [4]. IH is often asymptomatic; however, in a proportion of patients, it is usually a significant cause of morbidity (pain) and has a negative effect on the quality of life of patients and their body image [5]. Taken together, these data justify efforts to prevent the occurrence of this complication. According to the literature, IH patients are classified as low or high risk, the latter of whom may most benefit from modification of the closure technique or the placement of prophylactic meshes with the intention of reducing the incidence of IH [6, 7].

There is no single determining risk factor for this complication; that is, its incidence is multifactorial. Many of these factors are not modifiable, at least in emergency surgery; surgical site infection (SSI) has been noted as one of the main risk factors for the postoperative occurrence of IH and other complications, as the infection compromises the integrity of the surgical wound, weakening the tissue and fascia at the incision site, thus leading to poor healing [8]. In addition, SSI can prolong the duration of hospitalization, increase the need for additional surgical interventions to treat the hernia, and increase health care costs. Prevention of SSIs through proper surgical practices, infection control, and postoperative care is crucial to minimize the risk of IH and improve patient outcomes [9].

In recent years, several predictive scales for assessing the occurrence of IH have been published, indicating important risk factors such as emergency surgery, history of previous surgery, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, body mass index (BMI) > 25 Kg/m2, early reoperation (before 3 months) and SSI, only two of these scales incorporate SSI as a predictor variable [10–17]. The relationship between the presence of IH and SSI is clear but has been poorly explored; existing studies are retrospective, and the final models described only retrospectively report the presence or absence of infection, eliminating their predictive power.

The ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator is a tool developed by the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP). This tool is designed to estimate the postoperative risk of unfavorable results, such as complications or death and, particularly relevant to the present study, SSI. This tool has been validated in various populations and has proven valuable in surgical planning, helping physicians and patients make informed decisions about perioperative care on the basis of individualized risks [18, 19].

This study aims to externally validate the ACS NSQIP calculator, a crucial step that would confirm the applicability and precision of the tool in a clinical setting and population different from the one in which it was developed, which may extend its use in medical practice worldwide. By confirming the validity of the tool, the results can be added to scales that predict the occurrence of IH prospectively, given the high degree of association they have in the occurrence of IH and SSI, improving the predictive power of the scale.

Materials and methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD-IA guidelines [20] and approved by the research ethics committee of the Hospital Regional de Alta Especialidad del Bajío IMSS-BIENESTAR in Guanajuato, Mexico (registration number CEI/HRAEB/038/2024). Inclusion criteria comprised: age > 18 years, emergency or elective midline laparotomy (regardless of underlying diagnosis) performed between January 2015–November 2022, minimum 1-month follow-up, and documented surgical site infection (SSI) confirmed by culture or clinical records. Patients participating in other laparotomy-related research protocols were excluded.

ACS NSQIP score variables

The ACS NSQIP consists of 21 variables. The following values were obtained from the electronic files: age, sex, emergency surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, disseminated cancer status, diabetes status, history of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), dialysis, height and weight (for calculation of the BMI), type of surgical procedure, functional status, steroid use due to chronic disease, ascites in the 30 days prior to surgery, hypertension requiring medication, congestive heart failure in the 30 days prior to surgery, dyspnea, current smoking within a year, acute renal failure, systemic sepsis in the 48 h prior to surgery, and ventilator dependence. Risk factors were entered for each patient into the surgical risk calculator (http://riskcalculator.facs.org) by two researchers. All variables in our dataset met the definitions provided in the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator guidelines (supplementary material Table 1) ([18]– [19]).

Study outcomes

Primary outcome: Presence of SSI meeting either: (1) microbiological confirmation (positive aseptic wound culture), or (2) clinical criteria per NSQIP/CDC standards (purulent drainage, cardinal infection signs, or surgeon-diagnosed SSI in medical records), occurring within 30 postoperative days. This operational definition ensured consistent retrospective case identification through verifiable clinical documentation [21].

Secondary outcomes: the association between the presence of SSI and the occurrence of IH either clinically or with an imaging study after at least 2 years of follow-up as defined by the European Hernia Society (EHS): any gap in the abdominal wall with or without a bulge in the area of a perceptible or palpable postoperative scar according to clinical examination or imaging [3, 22].

Sample size

The sample size was calculated with the use of the R programming language in the R studio environment and the pmsampsize library [23]. The base prevalence was set to 12.3% according to the 2018 report by the Global Surgery Collaborative [24]. With the following parameters: type = b, c statistic = 0.9, shrinkage = 0.9, parameters = 21 (used in the ACS Calculator to determine the risk of SSI), and prevalence = 0.12. A sample size of 693 patients was necessary to externally validate the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator according to the identification of 84 events (assuming a prevalence of 12%) and an event ratio of 3.96 for each parameter used in the validation of the tool (supplementary material Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous baseline variables are expressed as the means (for normally distributed variables) with standard deviations (SDs) and medians (for nonnormally distributed variables) with the 25–75% percentiles. Categorical variables are shown as numbers with percentages. The cohort was divided into two groups according to whether they presented with SSIs. Quantitative variables were compared with Student’s t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test according to their distribution, and qualitative variables were compared with the χ2 test. The bivariate analysis was adjusted for logistic regression, and the odds ratio (OR) with its 95% confidence interval was reported. The study cohort was divided into six groups according to the predicted risk, which resulted in approximately 130 patients per group: the first group consisted of the sextile with the lowest predicted risk, and the sixth group consisted of the sextile with the highest predicted risk. To evaluate the calibration ability of the model, we graphically plotted the observed results in the six predicted risk groups with the line y = x, which expresses the ideal relationship between the predicted and the observed results. To evaluate model discriminability, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calculated the corresponding area under the curve (AUC), which can be interpreted as follows: 0.5–0.7 is poor discriminability, 0.7–0.8 is good, 0.8–0.9 is excellent, and an AUC ≥ 0.9 is outstanding discriminability. Bayesian analysis was performed with the results of the confusion matrix, and the Fagan nomogram was constructed to calculate the prevalence of the test a posteriori. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Study population

A total of 1310 patients who underwent midline laparotomy were initially analyzed; of these, 227 were initially excluded because they were duplicate records of subsequent visits. Finally, 1083 records of patients underwent surgery between January 2015 and November 2022 were finally included in the study.

Two hundred ninety-six patients were excluded because they died, the file was incomplete, or they did not complete the follow-up for various reasons. Among the 787 patients whose records were analyzed, 104 presented with SSI, for an incidence of 13.21% (Fig. 1).

Calibration ability

Figure 3 shows the calibration curve of the model, in which the predicted probabilities (risk estimates) generated by the model were compared with the observed probabilities (real results). For its analysis, the sample was divided into sextiles, guaranteeing a balanced evaluation of the calibration between the different levels of anticipated risk. The results indicate that the model is well calibrated, especially in the low- to medium-risk groups, with a Brier score of 0.072.

Calibration and ROC curve ACS calculator

Discrimination

Figure 3 shows the ROC curve of the model, which yielded an AUC of 0.88, indicating good discriminatory capacity. The best cutoff point was 6.6, with a sensitivity of 0.78, a specificity of 0.92, and a Youden index of 0.712.

Figure 4 shows the results of Bayesian analysis in the form of the Fagan nomogram, which indicates that the ACS NSQIP calculator has good diagnostic performance, significantly improving the posttest risk estimate, both in confirming and ruling out SSI with a posteriori prevalences of 79% and 5%, respectively.

Fagan Nomogram

Surgical site infection and incisional hernia

Forty (38.5%) of the patients who developed IH and 64 (9.4%) of the patients without hernias had SSIs. Thus, having an SSI increased the risk of having IH as a complication in this cohort, with an OR of 5.9 (95% CI 3.4–10.3) (p = 0.0001).

Discussion

The results of the study revealed that the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator has good discriminatory capacity (AUC 0.88) and good calibration (Brier score 0.072) in predicting postoperative SSI in midline laparotomy patients. Calibration and discrimination are fundamental elements for determining the validity of a prediction model (however, a good calibration does not guarantee good discrimination, and vice versa) [25].

SSI is one of the most common health care-associated infections and is associated with increased morbidity, hospital stay and general costs. In the United States, the CDC and the Joint Hospital Accreditation Commission have considered colon surgery and its associated complications, including SSI, as one of the main quality outcome measures of surgery. However, most studies in which the tool has been applied have reported a discrepancy between what is observed and what is expected in this type of patient with the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator, primarily reporting an underestimation with the tool; therefore, it is considered less useful for the prediction of individual risk ([25]– [26]). In this study, this discrepancy was also noted, with an observed percentage of 13.2% vs. a predicted percentage of 15.86% (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, p = 0.045), indicating an overestimation with use of the tool; however, as shown in the calibration curve in Fig. 2, the accuracy of the tool was greater for patients who achieved higher scores than those who achieved intermediate and lower scores.

The ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator had previously been validated in several studies involving different types of interventions, but few have investigated its use specifically in midline laparotomy, and existing studies are divided by the type of colon surgery [25] or emergency cases [27]. Moreover, the studies do not focus specifically on SSI but rather on mortality, hospital stay or other complications, such as pneumonia, with variable results. Therefore, it is difficult to compare their results with those of this study.

Multiple scales have been evaluated for the prediction of SSI such as the Surgical Site Infection Risk Score (SSIRS), the National Nosocomial Infection Surveillance (NNIS), the Portsmouth Physiological and Operative Severity Score (P-POSSUM), and the National Emergency Laparotomy Audit Risk Prediction Calculator (NELA), but published studies do not directly compare these scales with the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator in abdominal surgery. Mevik K et al. 2024 compared different risk scores in the prediction of SSI with a logistic regression model developed by the authors and reported that their model presented a higher accuracy (80%) than the other risk indices did (SSIRS (77%), NNIS (59%), and JSS-SSI (52%); however, in that study, the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator was not included in the comparison, which makes it difficult to compare the findings of these scales [28].

In the studies in which the score is used, a cutoff point is not established to divide the patients into low or high risk. Our study revealed an optimal cutoff point (6.6) with a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 92%. The results of Bayesian analysis and the use of the Fagan nomogram suggest that the model has good diagnostic performance, increasing the a posteriori probability to 79% in positive cases, suggesting the suitability of the model. Stratifying patients in this way could help prioritize actions that reduce the incidence of SSI and are costly.

With respect to the individual analysis of risk factors, we found that variables such as emergency surgery, diabetes mellitus, and generalized sepsis had the greatest impact on our cohort in terms of the presence of SSI, which could reflect the unique clinical characteristics of our population. Notably, the model excludes important variables identified by other authors, such as surgical time, the classification of the surgical wound, and whether two or more procedures were performed during the same surgery ([29]– [30]). The extent to which these variables could affect the diagnostic performance of the model would be an interesting topic for future studies.

SSI is an important risk factor for the presence of incisional hernia, increasing the risk 6 times over when SSI is not observed. This finding is relevant since few incisional hernia prediction scales consider infection as a predictor in their final model, while those that do present it in a dichotomous way because they involve retrospective analyses. Given the findings regarding the diagnostic performance of the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator, its inclusion as a predictor variable could be useful in future hernia prediction studies.

This study is limited by its retrospective and single-center nature, in addition to the exclusion of factors that reduce the risk of infection. These include the use of antibiotics during the perioperative period or other useful measures, such as changing gloves or instruments at the time of surgical closure [31], the use of antibiotic sutures [32], negative pressure therapy in surgical wounds [33], or even other types of approaches, such as laparoscopic and minilaparotomy techniques. However, the purpose of the study was to validate the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator, which does not account for the abovementioned variables.

Conclusions

The ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator has good discriminatory capacity (AUC 0.88) and good calibration (Brier score 0.072) in predicting postoperative SSI in midline laparotomy patients.

Our study revealed an optimal cutoff point (6.6) with a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 92% in dividing patients into low- or high-risk groups.

Surgical site infection is an important risk factor for the development of incisional hernia, increasing the risk by a factor of 6 over the absence of SSI.
