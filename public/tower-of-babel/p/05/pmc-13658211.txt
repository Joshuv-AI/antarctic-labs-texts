TPH1-dependent maternal hyposerotonemia: long-term effects on adult wild-type mouse offspring
Authors: Françoise Saurini, Carolina Jaliffa, Gwenaëlle Le Pen, Zsolt Csaba, Chantal Joubert, Jacques Callebert, Jean-Marie Launay, Catherine Senamaud-Beaufort, Oumy Seydi, Pierre Gressens, Guilan Vodjdani, Sylvie Berrard, Tania Vitalis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Numerous mood and behavior disorders have developmental origins resulting from genetic and environmental interactions. Maternal tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH-1)-dependent serotonin (5-HT) depletion alters embryonic brain development, but its long-term impact on adult progeny has never been investigated. Here we demonstrate that adult WT male offspring born to hyposerotonergic Tph1+/- dams ( ~ 60% deficit) display hyperlocomotion, impaired self-care and an increased anxiety relative to those from WT dams. Concomitantly, norepinephrine (NE) levels and dopamine (DA) turnover are significantly reduced in the mesencephalon and brainstem, whereas monoaminergic cell density is not affected. In the frontal cortex of the offspring, maternal hyposerotonergic status results in significant reduction in DA, NE and 5-HT levels, accompanied by transcriptomic changes, notably in monoaminergic system signaling, synaptic function and plasticity, methylation processes and myelination. Myelination is also impaired as revealed by a reduced thickness of the corpus callosum. Maternal hyposerotonemia thus appears sufficient to affect the phenotype of the adult WT progeny. TPH-1-dependent 5-HT deficit during pregnancy could be considered as a risk factor for neurodevelopmental disorders in the offspring.

Introduction

Maternal physiological status, behavior and lifestyle have critical impacts on the fetal and neonatal development of the offspring that may lead to long-term sequelae. During pregnancy, inadequate maternal nutrition, tobacco or alcohol consumption, exposure to endocrine disruptors, stress or depression can significantly alter the growth of the offspring, its short-term and long-term health, behavior, and can increase its susceptibility to develop diseases later in life. These life-long effects may be mediated by epigenetic modifications of the offspring’s genome that modulate gene expression1,2. The serotonergic status of the pregnant and nursing dams also stands as a major determinant of a normal in utero and early postnatal development of the progeny3,4.

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is a monoaminergic neurotransmitter that modulates a wide range of physiological processes, including autonomic, cognitive and behavioral functions4–6. 5-HT also acts as a morphogen in early stages of development before it becomes a neurotransmitter4,6–9. The activity of serotonergic neurons is mediated by seven families of 5-HT receptors encoded by at least 15 different genes, which account for the 5-HT functional diversity10,11. Despite its important role in regulating cerebral functions in mammals, only ∼5% of the total body’s 5-HT is synthesized in the brain, while most of it (∼95%) is produced in peripheral non-neuronal tissues12,13. Outside the nervous system, 5-HT is essentially synthesized in the enterochromaffin cells of the gut, from which it is released into blood circulation, rapidly taken up by platelets except a nanomolar “free” 5-HT fraction which can act as a signaling molecule14. In addition, 5-HT can be produced locally within organs where it exerts paracrine/autocrine actions15.

The rate-limiting step of 5-HT synthesis is catalyzed by tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), which converts the essential amino acid L-tryptophan into the immediate 5-HT precursor 5-hydroxy-tryptophan (5-HTP). This enzyme is present in two distinct forms arising from two different genes, Tph1 and Tph2, whose expressions are essentially in non-neuronal cells and neurons, respectively12,16.

The discovery of Tph1 and Tph2 genes led to the generation of animal models in which 5-HT synthesis is depleted exclusively either in neurons or in peripheral cells, thus allowing to assess the differential roles of neuronal and non-neuronal 5-HT. For instance, a drastic reduction of brain 5-HT from embryonic stages by constitutive invalidation of the Tph2 gene induces growth retardation17 and behavioral phenotypes at adulthood18. Deficit of peripheral 5-HT in Tph1 gene knockout (KO) mice leads, among others, to late cardiac dysfunction12, impaired lipid metabolism19,20 and insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells21,22. In the same line, maternal peripheral serotonergic status (TPH-1-dependent) could also influence the development of the placenta and of the embryonic progeny. The crucial impact of maternal peripheral 5-HT on the development of the embryos was firmly established by using the Tph1-KO mouse model. Indeed, pregnant Tph1−/− mice, which display only 6% of normal blood 5-HT levels23, carry embryos (either Tph1−/− or Tph1+/−) that present intra-uterine growth retardation, while those with the same genotype from Tph1+/− mothers are indistinguishable from their wild-type (WT) counterparts. Heterozygous embryos growing in Tph1−/− mice also exhibit striking brain abnormalities and a 30% reduction in the number of proliferating cells in the ventricular zone as compared to those of Tph1+/− dams23. Disruption of maternal 5-HT homeostasis during gestation thus profoundly affects embryonic growth and formation. Whether this embryonic condition is due to a direct deficit of maternal 5-HT or to an indirect effect resulting from maternal physiological dysfunction remains unknown and requires further investigation. However, a direct effect seems unlikely as it is now recognized that during early developmental stages, the placenta24 and possibly the yolk sac25 are major sources of 5-HT supplied to the embryo. So far, little is known about the long-term impact of maternal hyposerotonergic status on the offspring’s phenotype. Interestingly, one study reported an interplay between maternal 5-HT imbalance and the embryonic placental status, as observed in the SERT Ala56 mice, and the placenta’s capacity to produce 5-HT26. In our case, the long-term effects of maternal 5-HT decrease due to TPH1-hypofunction during pregnancy on adult progeny phenotype have been investigated.

In the present study, we investigated the effect of a ~60% deficit of maternal blood 5-HT on the WT offspring’s phenotype at adulthood. This work was performed on 2-month-old WT male mice born to either WT or to hyposerotonergic Tph1+/− dams to exclude any possible contribution of 5-HT produced by the progeny or by its placenta. Here we analyzed the phenotype of the progeny at the behavioral, anatomical and molecular levels. We show that WT offspring born to Tph1+/− dams actually display behavioral modifications associated to modified levels and turnovers of monoamines within their regions of synthesis and one of their projecting target, the frontal cortex (FC). The FC, known to orchestrate locomotor behavior and high-order functions, shows strong dysregulations in the transcriptomic landscape. In addition, the corpus callosum thickness is significantly reduced in WT offspring born to Tph1+/− dams.

Results

Maternal serotonergic status

We first compared the serotonergic status of adult WT females with that of females deficient for or lacking TPH1 by measuring the concentration of 5-HT in the whole blood (Fig. 1a). Blood 5-HT level was significantly lower in mutant mice as compared to WT animals. In Tph1−/− mice, it was ~6% of that of WT mice, confirming our previously published data12,14. We also showed that in heterozygous Tph1+/− females, the level of circulating 5-HT was significantly reduced (~60%) compared with that of WT mice (Fig. 1a). Global reduction of 5-HT levels (central and peripheral), or specifically central, during the embryonic period has been shown to modulate neurodevelopment8,27,28. Here we investigated whether selective maternal hyposerotonemia (Tph1+/− dams, ~60% reduction of circulating 5-HT level) during the developmental period modifies the adult phenotype of the WT offspring. Behavioral analyses

We first examined in detail the behavioral phenotype of the progeny. WT(+/-) mice displayed a higher spontaneous locomotor activity than WT(+/+) mice, as evaluated in a 60-min session in an actimeter (Fig. 2a). The hyperactivity of WT(+/-) mice was confirmed in the open field (OF)29 and elevated plus maze (EPM) tests, since they traveled significantly longer distances than WT(+/+) mice over sessions of 60 and 5 min, respectively (Fig. 2b, c). Additionally, WT(+/-) and WT(+/+) mice displayed a comparable thigmotaxis behavior in the OF, as the total time spent in the aversive center of the arena did not significantly differ between both groups (Fig. 2e). In the EPM, all animals spent the same amount of time in either the closed or unprotected open arms (Fig. 2f, g). Thus, WT(+/-) and WT(+/+) mice behaved similarly in these environments that can reveal anxiety. In a more stringent context, such as the light-dark box30 test, unconditioned anxiety behavioral test based on the mice natural tendency to avoid highly illuminated areas, WT(+/-) mice spent significantly less time than WT(+/+) mice in the bright compartment (Fig. 2h). This reflects their higher level of anxiety relative to WT(+/+) animals in this test.

Two other behavioral tests measuring stress-coping strategies were performed. They are centered on the mouse’s response when placed in an unavoidable stressful situation, namely the tail suspension (TST) and forced swimming (FST) tests31. Mouse immobility was used as an index of resignation, a larger time spent immobile being an indicator of behavioral surrender in coping. In both tasks, WT(+/-) and WT(+/+) mice displayed a similar mean immobility duration (Fig. 2i, j). The behavior of WT(+/-) mice was thus not affected in these two tests.

When grooming was stimulated by spraying a sucrose solution on the mice fur in the splash test, WT(+/-) mice exhibited significantly lower grooming frequency and duration than WT(+/+) mice (Fig. 2k, l). WT(+/-) thus neglected coat grooming, reflecting a deficit in self-care behavior.

Mouse cognitive performances were also investigated by testing their spatial working memory (spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze task), their social behavior (social preference task in the three-chamber interaction test) and their ability to discriminate a novel object (NOR test) or a novel congener (novelty preference task in three-chamber test) from familiar ones. WT(+/-) mice did not show any deficit in each of these tasks (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Clearly, WT(+/-) progeny displays behavioral alterations that affect locomotor activity and anxio-emotional status.

Molecular and histological analyses of monoamine producing brain regions

Monoaminergic functions are known as regulators implicated in the aforementioned behaviors32–35. We thus sought to assess whether the monoaminergic systems were affected in the brain of WT(+/-) mice.

Molecular analysis

The levels and turnovers of 5-HT, norepinephrine (NE) and dopamine (DA) were first measured in their site of synthesis [raphe nuclei and locus coeruleus (LC) in the brainstem, substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area (SN/VTA)].

We then assessed, in this brain structure, the expression of the genes involved in regulating the levels of these two monoamines. For the serotonergic system, the expression of Tph2 gene was significantly higher in WT(+/-) mice than in WT(+/+) controls (Fig. 3f), while AADC mRNA levels were not statistically different between the two groups [WT(+/+): 1.044 ± 0.31 (n = 8); WT(+/-): 1.308 ± 0.33 (n = 8); two-tailed students’ t test, t = 1,648; df = 14, p = 0.12; see Supplementary Data 1 for Fig. 3]. For the noradrenergic system, the expression of the genes encoding the two enzymes catalyzing NE synthesis, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase (DBH), was also significantly higher in WT(+/-) than in control animals (Fig. 3g, h). By contrast, the expression of genes encoding the 5-HT and NE plasma membrane transporters (SERT and NET) and the vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT2) showed no significant difference between both mouse groups (Fig. 3i–k).

Interestingly, the levels of mRNA encoding monoamine oxidases A and B (MAOA and MAOB), enzymes involved in the degradation of monoamines, were significantly higher in WT(+/-) than in WT(+/+) animals (Fig. 3l, m). By contrast, no significant differences were found between both mouse groups in the amount of mRNA encoding catechol-O-methyl transferase (COMT), the degradative enzyme of catecholamines (Fig. 3n).

We next focused on a mesencephalic brain portion, designated as “SN/VTA-enriched region” (Fig. 4a). In WT(+/-) mice, the levels of DA and of one of its metabolite 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) were unchanged in this brain area as compared to WT(+/+) mice (Fig. 4b, c), whereas the level of homovanillic acid (HVA), another DA metabolite, was significantly lower in WT(+/-) mice (Fig. 4d). Nevertheless, DA turnover via both degradative pathways was significantly altered in WT(+/-) (Fig. 4e, f). Moreover, all the genes encoding the molecular players of the dopaminergic system were significantly more expressed in WT(+/-) than in control animals. Namely this concerns the synthesizing enzymes, TH and AADC, the plasma membrane DA transporter36, vesicular transporters VMAT2, and the degradative enzymes MAOA, MAOB and COMT (Fig. 4g–m). Histological analysis of monoaminergic cell bodies

Altogether, these results indicate that maternal hyposerotonemia during the developmental period, while sparing gross monoaminergic neuron differentiation, alters permanently the regulation and tonus of monoaminergic systems in the brain of WT offspring, through modified expression of the main molecular actors of these neurotransmission systems. Of note, in the areas of monoamines production, only NE level is affected.

Molecular and histological analyses in the frontal cortex

The FC is a major target for monoaminergic projections and a key integrative center for behavioral functions, such as those that were modified in WT(+/-) mice40.

Monoaminergic fiber densities in the frontal cortex

Although monoaminergic neuron densities were similar in both groups, the decrease in 5-HT, NE, and DA concentrations observed in the FC of WT(+/-) compared with WT(+/+) mice could result from defective monoaminergic innervation. We therefore analyzed serotonergic and catecholaminergic afferents in the FC of adult WT(+/+) and WT(+/-) mice labeled for SERT and TH respectively (Fig. 7j, k). Semi-quantitative analyzes of fluorescence were performed between bregma levels +0.62 and +0.74 mm41 in the motor cortex restricted to the frontal Fr2 region42 in cortical layers I, II–III, V, and VIa. Fiber analyzes showed that the mean densities of both SERT-positive and TH-positive fibers were similar in WT(+/-) and WT(+/+) mice (Fig. 7m, n). Similarly, mean intensities of fluorescence of SERT and TH labelings revealed no significant differences between the two groups (see Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Taken together, the decrease in 5-HT and catecholamine levels observed in the WT(+/-) FC is not due to defective monoaminergic afferences but may rely on the altered metabolism and/or transport of monoamines.

Since monoamine levels were markedly decreased in the FC, we hypothesized that the alteration of specific molecular pathways may account for the modified behaviors observed in WT(+/-) vs WT(+/+) mice.

Myelination status in the forebrain

Defective myelination impairs motor inhibition and has been observed in various rodent models of hyperactivity and in patients suffering from attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [ADHD44]. Given that the expression of genes associated to myelin formation and structure was substantially lower in WT(+/-) vs WT(+/+), we performed MBP- and PLP1-immunolabelling in the anterior cortex. Discussion

Numerous disorders impacting mood and behavior have developmental origins resulting from the interaction of genetic and environmental predisposition factors43. Among these factors, maternal status is known to affect embryonic development45,46, yet the consequences of a specific maternal peripheral 5-HT reduction have been rarely reported. We have previously demonstrated that embryos from dams lacking TPH1 and displaying only ~6% of the normal level of peripheral 5-HT display neurodevelopmental alterations whatever their genotype23. However, the maternal condition induced by TPH1-deficiency, and the cascade of molecular and physiological events, leading to the embryo’s phenotype, remains unknown and needs further investigation. It has also been reported that a SERT-dependent maternal 5-HT alteration affects mouse fetal neurodevelopment26, and in humans, that maternal TPH1 variants producing low levels of 5-HT are associated with ADHD predisposition of the infants47.

This work focused on investigating the long-term phenotype of WT male progeny from TPH1-deficient dams. We show that these animals display hyperlocomotion, behavioral deficits in self-care, and a higher anxio-emotional response in the light/dark box paradigm as compared to those born to WT dams. This phenotype is associated with a significant reduction of DA turnover in the mesencephalon and NE levels in the brainstem, while no modification of monoaminergic cell density is observed. In the frontal cortex of the offspring, maternal hyposerotonemia induces a significant decrease in DA, NE, and 5-HT levels. This neurochemical alteration is accompanied by extensive transcriptomic changes, particularly affecting the monoaminergic signaling system, synaptic function and plasticity, DNA methylation processes, and myelination. Structural analyzes further reveal impaired myelination, evidenced by a reduced thickness of the corpus callosum. Whether similar alterations could be found in female offspring will be the aim of our future investigation, since it is now well documented that maternal status may affect differentially male and female progeny45,46.

In the brainstem noradrenergic system, despite increased expression of TH and DBH genes, suggesting enhanced norepinephrine synthesis, NE levels were markedly and significantly reduced in WT(+/-) mice. This reduction is likely attributable to an upregulation of degradative mechanisms involving MAOA and MAOB, whose gene expression was elevated. In contrast, within the brainstem serotonergic system, 5-HT and 5-HIAA levels, as well as 5-HT turnover, were unchanged compared with controls. Consistently, AADC expression in the brainstem was not significantly altered. As this enzyme is not rate-limiting in either 5-HT or NE biosynthesis, these findings indicate that catabolic pathways are preferentially activated, allowing maintenance of 5-HT levels but likely contributing to the reduction in NE content.

In the SN/VTA-enriched region, both the expression of anabolic and catabolic genes was significantly increased in WT(+/-) mice vs control animals, leading to normal DA levels. Overall, the modified expression of genes involved in monoamine synthesis and degradative pathways in their areas of production suggests that complex cellular processes, among which post-transcriptional regulation, occur in WT(+/-) mice.

In the FC, known to orchestrate motor and high-order functions40 the levels or turnover of DA, NE and 5-HT were significantly reduced, whereas the densities of the monoaminergic axons were unchanged. These monoamines finely tune cortical activity by acting on a large array of monoaminergic receptors expressed on specific cortical neurons48,49. RNAseq analysis performed in this structure revealed that epigenetic signatures persist in adult WT(+/-), as the expression of ASH1L (a methyltransferase) and SETBP1 (a SET DNA-binding protein), implicated in methylation processes, was higher than in the control group50. These epigenetic events may have impacted gene expression and have possibly induced molecular and/or subtle cytoarchitectonic modifications in the WT(+/-) FC, leading to the observed phenotype.

Spontaneous hyperlocomotion was observed in WT(+/-) mice throughout our study, along with a decreased DA level in the FC. This reduction is presumably due to an increased DA degradation, as suggested by the greater turnover of DA in this structure. This hypothesis is reinforced by the fact that the density of TH+ fibers appeared normal in the FC of these animals, and that the DA levels were also normal in the SN-VTA-enriched area containing the neurons that project to the FC. DA uptake and transport, notably axonal, may thus be defective at this site. Actually, hyperlocomotion has been substantially associated with dopaminergic insufficiencies in the prefrontal cortex51. Our RNAseq analysis revealed that two dopamine receptors regulating FC function, DRD1 and DRD2, were downregulated in the WT(+/-) FC. Interestingly, these genes are also downregulated in animal models displaying hyperactivity51,52. These receptors have been reported repeatedly to undergo epigenetic modifications and are actually expressed within the FC, which includes the motor and prefrontal cortices48,52,53. DRD1 is also misregulated in rodent models displaying ADHD-like phenotypes54. In humans, single-nucleotide polymorphisms in the DRD1 gene were found in a cohort of patients suffering from ADHD29. A hyperactive phenotype was also proposed to be induced by the hypofunction of DRD1-mediated regulation of GABAergic inhibitory synaptic transmission onto layer V pyramidal cells of the anterior cingulate cortex48,55.

DRD2 and ADORA2A (adenosine A 2A receptor) were downregulated in WT(+/-) mice (Fig. 9). DRD2 is known to inhibit the production of cAMP, to regulate the activity of Akt and GSK3 protein kinases, and it is strongly expressed in the FC (both in pyramidal cells and interneurons), where it participates in the regulation of emotional functions56. Although the synergistic function of DRD2 and ADORA2A has not been shown in the FC, it has been clearly demonstrated in the striatum that the two proteins heterodimerize30,57. In humans, blockade of ADORA2A by caffeine/amphetamine increases anxiety58, and variants of both DRD2 and ADORA2A genes leading to decreased levels of DRD2 and ADORA2A were identified as a risk factor of anxiety in patients suffering from ADHD59. This can be relevant in explaining the higher-than-normal anxiety-related behavior observed in the light-dark box paradigm in WT(+/-) mice.

In the cortex, the 5-HT receptors are expressed in a mosaic fashion by glutamatergic pyramidal neurons and interneurons to orchestrate cortical network activity and regulate behavior49. In hyperactive WT(+/-) mice, 5-HT2C or 5-HT1B were downregulated. Data from human analyzes also suggest that both receptors may constitute a risk factor for ADHD60,61.

Furthermore, we found that the expression of sixteen other genes implicated in the response to amphetamine or cocaine was downregulated in WT(+/-) mice. These drugs modify monoamine levels at the pre- and post-synapse by acting on reuptake or metabolic monoaminergic pathways62,63. In addition, d-amphetamine and the enhancement of DA signaling are currently used for treating ADHD64. These data reinforce an overall view that maternal blood hyposerotonemia induces a blunted monoaminergic signaling in the offspring.

A large array of genes implicated in the regulation of synaptic structure and function, known to be altered in various neurodevelopmental disorders, were strikingly misregulated in WT(+/-) mice (76 downregulated and 32 upregulated) as compared to WT(+/+). First, the decrease of a notable block of 18 RPL encoding genes suggests that synaptic remodeling and plasticity are impaired in the FC of WT(+/-) mice65. Second, GRIN2B, that encodes the well-known NMDA receptor subunit NR2B, was overexpressed in WT(+/-). In normal conditions, its expression decreases as neurons mature and this subunit is progressively replaced by NR2A. The GRIN2B overexpression suggests that some features of the glutamatergic synaptic compartments are still in an immature state. In support, overexpression of GRIN2B in Xenopus tadpoles has been shown to promote dendritic outgrowth, decrease dendritic branching and maintain tectal neurons in an immature state66. Finally, the expression of transcription factors directly sensitive to methylation was modified in WT(+/-) vs WT(+/+). Among them, the transcription factor forkhead box P2 (FOXP2) was downregulated in WT(+/-). This factor, an important regulator of synaptic plasticity, controls the development of brain regions necessary for speech and language, and is associated with “self-regulation” and predisposition to develop ADHD67. The observed upregulation of the protooncogene MET, the myocyte enhancer factor 2C (MEF2C) and reelin (RELN) could be explained by the downregulation of FOXP2 that normally acts as a repressor of these genes68. Interestingly, these molecules are involved in the regulation of cortico-striatal functions and are also highly implicated in synaptic plasticity. During development, peaks of MET expression coincide with rapid neurite outgrowth and synaptogenesis, and its expression drops prior to synapse maturation, circuit refinement and heightened plasticity. Maintaining levels of MET expression higher than normal may thus impair proper synaptic refinement68. Of note, cortical-specific deletion of FOXP2 interferes with the development of dopamine-modulated cortical circuits69.

Overall, the synaptic compartments in the FC appear less plastic and display immature features in WT(+/-) as compared to WT(+/+). However, spine densities evaluated by Golgi-Cox staining of layers III and V neurons of the anterior FC were grossly similar between WT(+/-) and WT(+/+) (data not shown).

Myelination processes are particularly sensitive to developmental insults that may occur in utero44,70,71. Oligodendrocyte development and production of components of the myelin sheaths are prone to epigenetic modifications induced by environmental factors, such as infection, inflammation or gestational exposure to deleterious exogenous compounds71,72. White matter abnormalities reflect brain immaturity or damage, and thus can lead to defective function of cortical systems implicated in autonomic motor inhibition73. In WT(+/-) mice, we showed a striking downregulation in the expression of 22 genes required for oligodendrocyte maturation, such as 2’,3’-cyclic nucleotide 3’ phosphodiesterase (CNPase), SRY-box transcription factor 10 (SOX10), and those necessary for myelination, such as MBP, PLP1, MOG and MAG74–76. This result was strengthened by our anatomical observations, revealing a reduced thickness of the corpus callosum of WT(+/-) mice while cell densities in Fr2 region appeared unchanged. Defective myelination in WT(+/-) mice might be primarily due to methylation processes interfering with oligodendrocyte differentiation, or secondarily due to reduced or defective homeostasis of the surviving oligodendrocyte precursor pool that remains in adult brain. The latter hypothesis is strengthened by the reduced expression of CNPase, an enzyme required for progenitor transition to oligodendrocyte. Moreover, the decrease in SOX10 expression suggests a defect in oligodendrocyte capacity to myelin production and maintenance75. Besides, adenosine- and neurotransmitter-signaling deficiencies have been linked to impaired oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination. Actually, adenosine via ADOR2A promotes oligodendrocyte differentiation, while DRD2 normally supports myelination77. Finally, myelin defects have been clearly observed in mice modeling ADHD and in the frontal and parietal structures of patients suffering from ADHD44.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that a maternal condition induced by a selective peripheral hyposerotonemia affects adult WT male offspring on multiple levels, possibly via epigenetic mechanisms known to influence neurodevelopment. These effects were evident not only in the offspring’s behavior but also in their brain neurotransmitter profiles. Moreover, in the frontal cortex of the progeny, altered expression levels of various gene clusters suggest disruptions in cortical molecular pathways involved in monoamine regulation, synaptic plasticity, and homeostatic regulation of myelination. Collectively, these findings support the notion that 5-HT imbalance should be taken into account as an important criterion which may act directly or indirectly in shaping neurodevelopment influencing the brain structure, function and behavior of the adult progeny.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All experiments were performed in accordance with the European Community guidelines (Directive 2010/63/EU) and the French National guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. All animal procedures were approved by the local Ethics Committees N°34, 121 and 194, and were in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://www.nc3rs.org.uk/arrive-guidelines).

Mice breeding

The targeted mutagenesis of the Tph1 gene was described previously12. Tph1-KO and WT animals were all derived from a pure C57BL/6 genetic background and bred in our animal core facility. To assess the influence of the maternal peripheral 5-HT level on the adult progeny, the phenotype of WT male mice derived either from heterozygous or WT dams was explored. All experiments were carried out with 2-month-old mouse offspring, obtained by breeding WT males to either WT or Tph1+/− females. Tph1+/− dams were obtained by breeding Tph1+/+ females with Tph1+/− males to avoid transgenerational alterations. Moreover, Tph1+/− dams were allowed to produce only three litters to minimize physiological modifications that are more likely to occur with age.

Blood serotonin measurement

Blood samples of adult females were obtained by intracardiac sampling, collected in tubes containing 5 µl of 0.5 M EDTA (15575020, InvitrogenTM) to prevent blood clotting, and stored at −80 °C. Whole-blood 5-HT levels were measured in 50 µl samples by radioenzymology as described previously78 and were expressed in nM.

Behavioral assessments

Mice used for behavioral testing were 2 to 4 months old. In a session, the two WT(+/+) and WT(+/-) groups of mice (n = 15–17/group) were subjected to behavioral tests in the following order: OF, EPM, Y-maze, NOR, LDB, three-chambered social test and actimeter. In another session, the two groups of mice (n = 17–21/group) were tested in the TST, followed by the FST and next by the splash test. Except for TST, FST and sucrose splash tests, behaviors were recorded using a Videotrack v5.31 Automated Behavioral Analysis (ViewPoint, Lyon, France) software. Experiments were conducted during the light phase between 9:00 am and 6:00 pm, with controlled temperature and light intensity, by investigators blind to mouse genotypes. Mice were habituated to the testing room for at least 30 min before initiating tests. They were returned to their home cage at the end of each test. Following testing of each animal, the apparatuses were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol to eliminate the residual odors and traces of the previously tested animal. Note that behavioral tests were performed in a fixed order to limit variability due to daily environmental fluctuations (e.g., noise, lighting, experimenter). All animals were tested in the same fixed order so that potential day-to-day differences affected all groups equally. In addition, testing was scheduled a consistent number of days after cage cleaning by the animal facility staff, to avoid variability related to handling or environmental disturbance. The tests used were also similar in both stress intensity and procedural demands, minimizing the likelihood of order effects.

Open field test

Spontaneous horizontal locomotor activity was assessed using the OF. Mice were moved in the center of the motor activity boxes (50 cm L, 50 cm l, 45 cm H), placed on an infrared floor and under a ~50-lux illumination in the center of the arena. Mice were allowed to freely explore for a 60 min period. The total distance traveled and the time spent in the center (45 cm × 45 cm) of the arena were recorded by an infrared camera system connected to the Videotrack.

Elevated plus maze

The experiment was conducted in a plus-shaped maze constructed from black Perspex, raised 50 cm from the ground, with 2 unprotected arms (open arms, 37 cm L × 6 cm l) and 2 arms closed by black Perspex walls (37 cm L × 6 cm l × 18 cm H). Light intensity was 50 lux at the level of open arms. Animals were individually placed in the center of the plus-maze facing one open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. The total distance (cm) traveled and the time (s) spent in the open and closed arms were measured. Mice that froze during testing due to a noise or other disruption were excluded from the analyzes.

Light-dark box test

This test was performed in a 40 cm × 40 cm × 30 cm box divided into two compartments of equal dimensions: a light compartment with white walls and floor, without a roof, illuminated at 400 lux, and a dark compartment with black walls, floor and roof, illuminated at 5 lux. A small opening (6 cm × 6 cm) allows mice to freely move between the two compartments. The mice were individually placed in the light part of the box facing the opening, and were allowed to explore the environment for 10 min. The time (s) spent in each compartment were recorded.

Actimeter test

Spontaneous horizontal and vertical activities were evaluated with an infrared beam system fitted to rectangular cages (40 cm × 25 cm) (ActiMot, TSE Systems GmbH, Berlin, Germany, version V5.1.9). Movement of the mice resulted in the interruption of the infrared beams. Each interruption was automatically counted, and the coordinates of the animal were determined in three dimensions (X–Y–Z axis). Each animal was placed in the center of the cage, and the number of beam breaks recorded during a 1-h period. The vertical (Z beam breaks) and horizontal (X + Y beam breaks) activities were directly analyzed from the data collected by the software system.

Tail suspension test

Each mouse was gently suspended by the tip of its tail using adhesive tape to a hook 50 cm above the surface of the bench. Behavior was videotaped during a 6-min test session, and the total duration of immobility was scored manually for each mouse. Mice were considered immobile only when they hung passively and were totally unmoving. Immobility was interpreted as a sign of depression-like behavior.

Forced swim test

Mice were individually placed in an open inescapable glass cylinder (diameter 20 cm, height 25 cm) containing 19 cm of water at 25 ± 1 °C. Behavior was recorded and time spent immobile was scored manually over the 5 min of the test. Immobility duration was calculated as the total time a mouse stopped struggling and made minimal limb movement needed to keep its head above the water. The animal was dried with a paper towel and the water was changed after each swimming test. High immobility duration of the mice is indicative of depression-like behavior.

Sucrose splash test

This test was performed in a home cage and consisted in spraying a 10% sucrose solution on the dorsal coat of the mouse. Because of its viscosity, the sucrose solution dirties the fur and mouse initiates a grooming behavior to clean it off. Mice were video recorded for 5 min after applying the sucrose solution. The total time spent grooming and the number of grooming sessions were scored. Grooming is an index of self-care and motivational behavior. Reduced grooming is considered as a depression-like behavior of mice.

Brain dissection and neurotransmitter quantification

Mouse brains were removed and the following regions were dissected: the brainstem, a SN/VTA-enriched portion (bregma level −3.80 to −2.54) and the FC (bregma level 3.20 to 0.38). Each structure was weighed, quickly frozen and stored at −80 °C.

UPLC measurements

Quantification of NE, DA and their main metabolites (DOPAC and HVA)

The levels of NE, DA and its main metabolites were quantified by UPLC (Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography) assays. Brain tissues were homogenized by sonication in 0.01 M hydrochloric acid, containing ascorbic acid (0.2 µM) and EDTA (0.2 µM), and centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected and passed through a 10,000 MW filter by centrifugation at 9000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The quantities of catecholamines (NE and DA) and main metabolites (DOPAC and HVA for DA) were then measured in a 50 µL aliquot of the ultrafiltrate by electrochemical detection on a serial array of four flow-through graphite coulometric electrodes (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific); adapted from ref.79. Analysis, data reduction and peak identification were fully automated. Concentrations were expressed as fmol/mg of tissue.

Quantification of 5HT and its metabolite (5HIAA)

The quantities of 5-HT and its metabolite 5HIAA were then measured in another 50 µL aliquot of the ultrafiltrate by fluorometric detection on a Vanquish UPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific; adapted from ref.80. Analysis, data reduction and peak identification were fully automated. Concentrations were expressed as fmol/mg of brain tissue.

RNA extraction

Brain tissues were homogenized in 1 mL TRIzolTM (15596018, Invitrogen, CA, USA) and total RNA was isolated using RNeasy micro kit RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions (740955.50, Macherey-Nagel, France). RNA was eluted in RNase/DNase-free water. The quality and concentration of the RNA extracts were assessed by spectrophotometry using the NanodropTM apparatus (Thermofisher Scientific, MA, USA).

RT-qPCR and real-time quantitative PCR

Reverse transcription (RT) was performed from 1 µg of total RNA using the iScriptTM cDNA synthesis kit (1708890, Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Real time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) reactions were carried out in triplicate in a Bio-Rad CFX 384 thermocycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Reactions were performed in 10 µl from 5 ng of cDNA using SYBR Green Super-mix (1725275, Bio-Rad) as follows: 30 s denaturation at 98 °C, 40 cycles consisting in 5 s denaturation at 95 °C, 20 s annealing and elongation at 60 °C. Amplification specificity was assessed by melting curve analyzes. Certified primers were designed using Primer3 plus or Pick Primers software and are presented in (Supplementary Table 1). Rpl13a (Ribosomal Protein L13a), a housekeeping gene, was used as normalizer. Analyzes were performed using Bio-Rad CFX Manager 3.0 (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Relative mRNA levels were calculated following the Delta-Delta CT method. Data are presented as arbitrary units with respect to the control group.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the Ecole Normale Supérieure Genomique ENS core facility (Paris, France). PolyA+ messenger RNAs were purified from 1 µg of total RNA and libraries were generated using the strand-specific RNA-Seq library preparation, the Stranded mRNA-Prep Ligation kit (20040534, Illumina, San Diego, USA), and were multiplexed by 10 on a NextSeq 1000/2000 P2 Reagents (100 Cycles) v3 (20046811, Illumina, San Diego, USA)19. A 118 bp single read sequencing was performed on a NextSeq 2000 Sequencing Systems (Illumina, San Diego, USA), and a mean of 41 ± 5 millions passing Illumina quality filter reads was obtained for 5 samples of each group.

The analyzes were performed using the Eoulsan pipeline81, including read filtering, mapping, alignment filtering, read quantification, normalization and differential analysis. Before mapping, poly-N read tails were trimmed, reads ≤40 bases were removed, and low-quality reads were discarded. The resulting pool of reads was then aligned to the Mus musculus genome from Ensembl (version 105) using STAR (version 2.7.8a)82. Alignments from reads matching more than once on the reference genome were removed using the Java version of SAMtools36. To compute gene expression, Mus musculus GTF genome annotation from Ensembl (version 105) database was used. All overlapping regions between alignments and referenced exons were counted and aggregated by genes using HTSeq-count 0.5.383. The sample counts were normalized, statistical treatments and differential analyzes were carried out using DESeq2 1.8.184. All RNA-seq data were deposited on the ENA database under the permanent accession number PRJEB92210 and following the link https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/ArrayExpress/studies/E-MTAB-15335?key=d526a053-b26f-43ea-be4f-faec39b722ec. Significant differentially expressed genes from WT(+/-) vs WT(+/+) frontal cortices were extracted following the Benjamini and Hochberg procedure85 and adjusted for p-values (p < 0.01). Heatmap was achieved with “Log2 quantile normalize” and hierarchical clustering with one minus Pearson correlation using Morpheus software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/). Enriched biological terms were obtained using Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/) as described by ref.86. Annotated relevant genes and putative functions were identified using ToppFun from ToppGene suite (https://toppgene.cchmc.org/enrichment.jsp/).

Brain anatomy and quantifications

Immunocytochemistry

Two-month-old mice were euthanized by an overdose of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg/IP) and were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffer saline (PBS). Brains were cut in the coronal plane on a freezing microtome (40 µm thick). Sections were pre-incubated in 15% goat serum diluted in PBS for 45 min and incubated overnight at room temperature with the following antibodies diluted in PBS with 0.03% TritonX (PBST): rabbit anti-TH [1:1000; GTX102424-N1C1; GeneTex Europe GmbH, Germany87], rabbit anti-TPH2 [1:1000; NB100-74555; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO 80112, USA88], rabbit anti-SERT [1:2000; 24330; Immunostar, Wisconsin, USA89] rabbit anti-proteolipid protein (PLP1; 1:1000, ab28486 Abcam, Cambridge, UK90] and mouse anti-myelin binding protein [MBP; 1:2000, MAB382 Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA91]. After washing in PBS, sections were incubated with secondary conjugated antibodies diluted in PBST containing DAPI (1:2000, D-9545, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France), goat anti-mouse Cy3 (1:300; 115-165-174; Jackson ImmunoResearch, PA, USA), goat anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (1:300; AP132JA4; Molecular Probes; Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA). Sections were rinsed in PBS and mounted in Vectashield (H-1000, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA).

Quantification of neuronal populations

The quantifications of monoaminergic neurons (TH+ cells and TPH2+ cells) were performed as described: in the LC38, the SN/VTA39, and in the serotonergic neurons of the raphe (B7d, v, l and B8)37. Images were acquired at 20× with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope for noradrenergic neurons of the LC or with a QuPath for mesencephalic TH+ neurons of SN/VTA, and of TPH2+ serotonergic neurons of the raphe. Cell countings were performed on brain sections of WT(+/+) and WT(+/-) mice at the same anatomical levels. The QuPath software allowed to precisely delineate the region and to zoom on selected sub-nuclei. The quantifications of DAPI+ cells in the cortical layers I to VIa of the Fr2 region42 at Bregma +0,74, +0,6241; were performed on confocal images acquired at 20x with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope using Fiji distribution of ImageJ.

Fluorescence measurement in selected areas

Immunofluorescent sections were analyzed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal scanning system (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with 405-nm Diode, 488-nm Ar, 561-nm DPSS lasers. Twelve-bit digital images were collected from a single optical plane using a 20× HC PL APO CS2 oil-immersion Leica objective (numerical aperture 0.75). For each optical section, double-labeling images were acquired in sequential mode to avoid potential contamination by linkage-specific fluorescence emission cross-talk. Settings for laser intensity, pinhole (1 Airy unit), range property of emission windows, electronic zoom, gain and offset of photomultiplicator, field format, and scanning speed were optimized initially for each immunolabeling and held constant throughout the study so that all sections were digitized under the same conditions. For each animal, two frontal brain sections were analyzed [Bregma +0,74, +0,6241] for quantification of the SERT, TH, MBP and PLP-1 immunolabelings. Twelve-bit images were segmented, in manually delineated FC sub-regions. Mean grey value (ranging from 0 to 4095) was measured as signal intensity, the proportion of the segmented area to the delineated area was calculated as density, and the integrated density was calculated from mean gray and area values using the Fiji distribution of ImageJ92. To calculate signal intensity and density values (in % of measured area), respectively. SERT and TH immunolabelings were quantified in layers I-VIa of the Fr2 region as previously delineated42.

Thickness measurement of the corpus callosum

The thickness of the corpus callosum was measured at bregma level (0.74) in WT(+/+) and WT(+/-) mice. Two adjacent sections and both the left and right hemispheres were analyzed. The lateral portion of the indusium griseum was taken as a lateral landmark for consistent measurement41,93.

Statistics

Statistical analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism software. The normal distribution of all data sets was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data on blood 5-HT levels followed a normal distribution and were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Holm–Sidak’s test for post-hoc multiple comparisons among mouse groups. For all other experiments comparing two mouse groups, normally distributed values were analyzed using Student’s unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction when needed, and data that failed the normality test were analyzed with Mann–Whitney U-test. Outliers were determined using the ROUT test and were excluded. Differences were considered significant when p-value < 0.05. All bar/dot plot data represent mean ± standard error of measurement. In figures, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
