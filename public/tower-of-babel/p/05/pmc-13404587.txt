An integrated strategy for screening Q-markers and elucidating anti-fibrotic mechanisms of Ganoderma lucidum
Authors: Yuanyuan Sun, Shujie Yuan, Wenhui Luo, Jie Wang, Renting Cao, Meng Su, Chijing Zuo, Can Peng, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGanoderma lucidum (G. lucidum) is a medicinal mushroom known for its hepatoprotective effects against various liver diseases.ObjectiveAddressing the variable quality of G. lucidum samples and the lack of efficacy-linked quality control criteria, an integrated strategy combining chemical analysis and biological validation was conducted to identify the Q-markers directly responsible for its anti-hepatic fibrosis (HF) activity and to preliminarily elucidate their action mechanism.Methods49 batches of G. lucidum samples were collected from 15 geographical origins across 7 provinces in China. Initially, UPLC fingerprinting integrated with chemometric analysis evaluated the quality and identified differential chemical components. The common compounds were further characterized by UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS. Subsequently, the potential targets and binding affinity were predicted by network pharmacology and molecular docking. Finally, the anti-HF activity and underlying mechanism of the core Q-marker were verified in a TGF-β1-induced HSC-T6 model using CCK-8, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and Western blotting.ResultsThe UPLC chemical fingerprint of G. lucidum was successfully established, 49 common components were identified based on UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS analysis, mostly were triterpenoids. Through chemometric analysis, 19 key differential markers were subsequently screened. Then, by integrating network pharmacology predictions with chemometric screening results, 7 triterpenoids were ultimately determined as potential Q-markers for the anti-HF activity of G. lucidum. Ganoderic acid J (GAJ) demonstrated robust binding specificity to pivotal targets within the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through molecular docking. 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL at 24 h were confirmed as low, medium, and high doses of GAJ by CCK-8, respectively. Ultimately, HF was relieved by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting apoptosis in activated HSCs and normalizing the MMP1/MMP2 equilibrium.ConclusionThe anti-HF effects of G. lucidum were mediated through multi-target synergistic actions, chiefly by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway and modulating extracellular matrix metabolism. The hepatoprotective mechanism of G. lucidum was elucidated at the molecular level, and a Q-marker-based theoretical framework was established for quality control of traditional medicines and innovative drug development.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05419-x.Keywords: Ganoderma lucidum, Hepatic fibrosis, Fingerprints, Q-marker, Network pharmacology, Experimental validation

Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis (HF), often referred to as a pathological wound-healing process, occurs in response to chronic liver damage, including viral hepatitis and excessive alcohol consumption. It’s marked by the overabundance of extracellular matrix (ECM). If left untreated, this condition may advance to cirrhosis, liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma, significantly endangering human health [1, 2]. Approximately 2 million people worldwide die from liver disease annually, with around half of these deaths caused by cirrhosis and its complications, according to epidemiological studies [3]. However, no specific therapeutic agents capable of effectively reversing or halting the progression of HF have been approved for clinical use. Therefore, the development of highly effective and low-toxicity anti-fibrotic drugs has been identified as a critical research priority.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has an extensive history of managing chronic liver diseases [4]. Ganoderma lucidum (G. lucidum), defined as the dried fruiting body of G. lucidum (Leyss. exFr.) Karst. or Ganoderma sinense Zhao, Xu et Zhang, is esteemed as an uncommon therapeutic fungus with millennia of historical application and venerated in folk medicine as the “miraculous mushroom” [5]. Contemporary pharmaceutical research has revealed that this substance boasts an impressive array of beneficial properties, ranging from immune system regulation and tumor-fighting capabilities to antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and liver-protecting qualities [6, 7]. The diverse efficacy of G. lucidum stems from the complexity of its constituents, including triterpenoids, polysaccharides, nucleotides, steroids, glycopeptides, and unsaturated fatty acids. Over 800 chemical components have been identified to date [8].

Within the realm of liver protection, it has been demonstrated that G. lucidum and its active components exhibit significant therapeutic potential in various experimental models of HF. Multiple mechanisms underlie these effects, notably suppressing hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, lessening oxidative stress, and regulating inflammatory signaling [9, 10]. According to relevant studies, G. lucidum has been shown to exert therapeutic effects on alcohol-induced liver injury [11], non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [12, 13], and cholestatic liver injury, as well as on HF resulting from chronic liver injury [14, 15]. Research has revealed that sublingual drops of G. lucidum fruit extract reduced CCl₄-induced HF in mice through multifaceted modulation [16]. In line with our findings, ethanol extracts from G. lucidum proved effective in alleviating HF by modulating the NF-κB and TGF-β1/Smads signaling pathways, while fostering crosstalk between metabolites and g_Ruminococcus strains within the gut microbiome [17]. Further evidence suggests that G. lucidum potentially thwarts HF and HSC activation through its effects on inflammation, apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and TGF-β/Smad-mediated ECM receptor interactions [9]. These collective findings demonstrate G. lucidum to be a potent prospect for anti-fibrotic medication development.

However, the remarkable clinical efficacy of G. lucidum is fundamentally dependent on the stable, uniform, and high-quality nature of its medicinal material. As with many TCM, the pharmacological activity of G. lucidum is strongly influenced by its geo-authenticity (Daodi), with the content of active constituents known to vary significantly across different growing regions [18]. In addition, extensive commercial cultivation has led to diversified origins, significant differences in growing environments, and a lack of standardized harvesting and processing protocols. Therefore, the establishment of a scientific evaluation system capable of accurately reflecting the intrinsic quality of G. lucidum and directly linking it to therapeutic efficacy has become an urgently needed priority. The concept of the Q-marker in TCM refers to chemical components present in herbal materials that are closely associated with effectiveness, measurability, and specificity, and a novel perspective has been provided for addressing the challenges in quality control [19–21]. Traditional quality control methods for G. lucidum mainly rely on a specific class of components, such as polysaccharides or triterpenoids, which cannot comprehensively reflect its multi-component, multi-target synergistic characteristics [22]. Therefore, an integrated strategy combining chemical analysis and biological verification was innovatively proposed, with the aim of accurately identifying potential Q-markers associated with the anti-HF effects of G. lucidum and thoroughly elucidating their mechanisms of action.

Chromatographic fingerprinting is widely recognized as an essential tool for holistic quality assessment, as it enables comprehensive visualization of the intrinsic chemical components of TCM [23]. However, its holistic and fuzzy nature often lead to results that are difficult to quantify objectively. To address this limitation, chemometric methods were introduced, such as similarity analysis (SA), principal component analysis (PCA), and orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). These methods allow in-depth mining of the fingerprint data and enable model-based classification, thereby facilitating data-driven and objective quality assessment [24]. Similar integrated strategies have been successfully applied in studies of Cinnamomi Cortex, Granati Pericarpium, and Chuanxiong Rhizoma dispensing granules, achieving reliable herb classification and marker identification [25–27]. Therefore, the chemical characteristics of G. lucidum were systematically profiled by integrating chromatographic fingerprinting with chemometric analysis. UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS was employed to accurately identify and structurally characterize the chemical components within G. lucidum, establishing a dependable chemical foundation for Q-markers identification.

Subsequently, network pharmacology and molecular docking techniques were employed to uncover the biological mechanisms of the Q-markers. This methodology, as substantiated by numerous research endeavors, boasts an impressive knack for revealing the fundamental elements that confer efficacy in TCM [28, 29]. By constructing a multi-dimensional “herb-active component-target-pathway” network, a strategy that aligns with the holistic and systemic characteristics of TCM [30, 31], key signaling pathways and potential molecular mechanisms can be systematically predicted. Finally, functional validation of the Q-marker and its predicted pathways was performed through cellular experiments. At present, the integrated research framework “chemical composition analysis, Q-markers screening, mechanism prediction, and experimental verification”, not only is expected to transform the role of G. lucidum in anti-HF from a panacea into a targeted therapeutic agent but also provides a feasible pathway for establishing a scientific and systematic quality control standard.

In summary, this study was initially designed to establish chemical fingerprints of G. lucidum and integrate them with chemometric analysis. Then, UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS characterized its chemical components systematically. Subsequently, network pharmacology and molecular docking were utilized to screen potential Q-markers associated with anti-HF activity and to predict key signaling pathways. Ultimately, using HSC-T6 cells as an in vitro model, the biological functions of the core Q-marker and its predicted pathways in alleviating HF were functionally validated. The comprehensive approach establishes a scientific foundation for quality control, pharmacodynamic mechanisms, and rational clinical use of G. lucidum.

Materials and methods

Regents and chemicals

The research team sourced high-purity compounds (≥ 98%) including Ganoderic acid A (PS010388), Lucidenic acid A (PS010891), Ganoderic acid B (PS011342), Ganoderenic acid B (PS010839), Ganoderic acid C2 (PS010837), Ganoderenic acid C (PS010840), Ganoderic acid D (PS000598), and Ganoderic acid F (PS210628-12) from Chengdu Push Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China). Ganoderic acid C6 (R31D11F135873) and Ganoderic acid J (M17IB210040), both with a purity of ≥ 98%, were sourced from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). All the 49 batches of G. lucidum samples (7 Provinces and 15 Cities) from different origins were numbered from S1 to S49 (Tab. S1), commercially obtained from Anhui Huangshan Yunle G. lucidum Co., Ltd (Huangshan, China). Voucher specimens had been deposited in the Herbarium of the College of Pharmacy, Anhui University of Chinese Medicine (numbered ACM‑20250701‑01 to ACM‑20250701‑49) and were authenticated by Prof. Nianjun Yu of the same institution. Acetonitrile and methanol for UPLC and UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS analysis were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

G. lucidum fingerprint establishment and chemometric analysis

Sample preparation and extraction

5.0 g G. lucidum powder was precisely weighed, dispersed in methanol (1:50, w/v), and extracted by ultrasound (250 W, 40 kHz) for 30 min at 60℃. The filtrate was subjected to additional filtering, combined, and concentrated using a rotary evaporator under low pressure. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for a duration of 15 min. A homogeneous quality control (QC) sample for qualitative analysis was prepared by combining equal portions of G. lucidum powder from 49 batches and subjecting the mixture to ultrasonic extraction. All reference standards were dissolved in methanol for standard solution preparation, then refrigerated at 4℃.

UPLC conditions for fingerprint analysis

All samples of UPLC fingerprint analysis were performed on an ACQUITY Arc system (2489 UV detector, Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). A Waters ACQUITY BEH C18 (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) column was used for separation. The mobile phase comprised 0.1% aqueous formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B), employing the following gradient elution conditions: 0 ~ 5 min, 20 ~ 25% B, 5 ~ 19 min, 32% B, 19 ~ 30 min, 35% B, 30 ~ 34 min, 45% B, 34 ~ 42 min, 60% B, 42 ~ 49 min, 70% B, 49 ~ 58 min, 90% B, 58 ~ 70 min, 90% B at 252 nm. The column temperature was 35℃, the flow rate was 0.2 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 µL.

Methodology

The method of fingerprint analysis was confirmed by accuracy, repeatability, and stability assessments [32, 33]. To evaluate accuracy, S1 sample was prepared and identified through six successive injections of the identical solution. Six parallel S1 samples were extracted and subjected to evaluate the repeatability test. Solution stability was assessed at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h.

Chemometric analysis

The chromatographic fingerprint SA was conducted using the Similarity Evaluation System for Chromatographic Fingerprint of TCM software (version 2012, Chinese Pharmacopoeia Committee, Beijing, China). Separately computed the similarity correlation coefficients between 49 G. lucidum batches (7 Provinces and 15 Cities) and the reference fingerprints. For chemometric analysis, the processed fingerprint data was imported into SIMCA-P 14.1 (Umetrics, Sweden), including PCA and OPLS-DA to identify chemical markers based on similarities and differences among different origins [34].

Qualitative analysis of 49 chemical markers by UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS

The study employed qualitative analysis using a Vanquish UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), which was paired with an Orbitrap Exploris 120 MS/MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and operated in negative ion mode. The UPLC fingerprint analysis was performed under the chromatographic conditions described in Sect. 2.2.2.

The optimal MS parameters were as follows: the electrospray voltage was 3.5 kV; the ion transfer tube temperature was 320℃; the ion source temperature was 360℃; the full scan/data dependent secondary scan (Full MS/dd-MS2) mass ranged from m/z 80 to 1200 at a resolution of 60,000; the normalized collision energy (NCE%): 20, 40, 60 eV. The raw data acquired through Xcalibur 4.4 was processed with Compound Discover 3.3 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Network pharmacology

The potential targets of co-existing components across the 49 batches of G. lucidum samples were identified using the TCMSP (http://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php) and Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) platforms. Compounds exhibiting oral bioavailability ≥ 30% and drug-likeness ≥ 0.18 were classified as active ingredients. The nomenclature of all target proteins was standardized through the UniProt website (https://uniprot.org/). HF-related genes were retrieved from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for the common targets were built utilizing the STRING (https://stringdb.org/) and rendered using Cytoscape 3.7.1, while limiting the species selection to Homo sapiens. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted through the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), and the findings were then graphically represented using the bioinformatics platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). Subsequently, an “active ingredient-target-pathway” pharmacological network was established with Cytoscape 3.7.1 to identify potential anti-HF components in G. lucidum [35].

Molecular docking

According to the constructed compounds-targets-pathways network diagram of G. lucidum’s therapeutic effects on HF, the target active ingredients were identified by taking the intersection of the top 20 compounds ranked by Degree values and the 19 compounds screened by fingerprint OPLS-DA analysis with a variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1. The target active ingredients were downloaded from PubChem (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) as ligands, while the crystal structures of key targets were acquired from RCSB PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/) as receptors. All obtained files were then converted and saved in “.pdbqt” format for further analysis. AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 conducted molecular docking, with visualization achieved through PyMOL 2.6.0.

Cell experimental verification

Cell culture

HSC-T6 cells were procured from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (CL-0116, Wuhan, China). The cellular specimens were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (C3113, Shanghai XP Biomed Ltd., Shanghai, China) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic solution containing penicillin and streptomycin. The HSC-T6 cells were cultured in 5% CO2 at 37℃ with humidity and received daily sub-culturing, with all experimental work conducted during logarithmic growth.

Cell viability assay

Based on the molecular docking results showing that Ganoderic acid J (GAJ) exhibited the strongest binding affinity to the core targets of the PI3K/AKT pathway among the seven identified Q-markers (Fig. S8), GAJ was selected for subsequent in vitro validation. Cell viability assessments were conducted with the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8, CT001, SparkJade, Jinan, China). HSC-T6 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h prior to TGF-β1 treatment [36]. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to Galunisertib (TGF-βR I kinase inhibitor, LY2157299, HY-13226, MedChemExpress, USA) treatment and varying concentrations of GAJ (1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 µg/mL) for specific time (12, 24, and 48 h). Then, each well received 100 µL of 10% CCK-8 solution.

Flow cytometry

HSC-T6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured to approximately 80% confluence. Following three gentle PBS washes, cells were trypsinized, harvested, and resuspended in binding buffer. According to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), the cell suspensions were stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. Flow cytometry was utilized to analyze cellular apoptosis (CytoFLEX SRT, Beckman Coulter, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

HSC-T6 cells were plated on adhesive slides at a concentration of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and grown to approximately 80% confluence before experimental treatments. Following drug intervention, the cells underwent three PBS washes before fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. The cells fixed and treated with Triton X-100 for 10 min were subsequently blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min. Then, the specimens underwent overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary MMP1/2 antibodies (1:1000, ab137332/ab92536, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After washing with PBS to remove unbound primary antibodies, the cells were subjected to a secondary antibody incubation (1:400, ab150079, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 2 h in the dark. Nuclear counterstaining utilized DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Ultimately, the slides were treated with an anti-fade mounting solution and observed via a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM900 with Airyscan 2, ZEISS, Germany) for fluorescence image acquisition.

Western blotting

HSC-T6 cells were harvested and disrupted with ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF (SL1020, COOLABER, Beijing, China). The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate the supernatant. The protein levels were assessed with a BCA reagent kit (EC0001, SparkJade, Jinan, China). Protein quantities were standardized for separation via SDS-PAGE (EC0023, SparkJade, Jinan, China) followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were treated with 5% skimmed milk for 30 min before being exposed to the specified primary antibodies in a 4 °C incubation overnight (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA): anti-PI3K (1:1000, 4257), anti-p-PI3K (1:1000, 17366), anti-AKT (1:1000, 9272), anti-p-AKT (1:1000, 9271), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 1:30000, 60004-1-Ig). After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, RGAG001, Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 30 min. The protein bands were visualized using ECL detection reagents (ED0016, SparkJade, Jinan, China). Protein concentrations were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were displayed in terms of the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All figures were created with GraphPad Prism 8.0. Significant differences were identified at P < 0.05.

Results

Quality evaluation of G. lucidum by fingerprint combined with chemometrics

Establishment of fingerprint spectra and identification of partially shared peaks

Forty-nine G. lucidum samples, extensively gathered from 15 representative locations across multiple core production regions in China, were used to establish the fingerprint to ensure geographical diversity (Fig. 1a). Validation of the methodology confirmed the relative standard deviations (RSD) for both the relative retention times (RRT) and relative peak areas (RPA) of all common peaks were under 3%. The findings validated the method’s adherence to the necessary criteria of accuracy, repeatability, and stability (Tab. S2).

The fruiting body samples (S1-S49) and a mixed reference solution containing 8 standard compounds were analyzed according to the chromatographic conditions described above. From the fingerprint’s peak retention times (Fig. 1b), 49 common peaks were characterized. Among them, 8 compounds were unambiguously identified by comparison with reference standards (Fig. S1). The identified peaks include Ganoderenic acid C (Peak 4), Ganoderic acid C2 (Peak 6), Ganoderenic acid B (Peak 11), Ganoderic acid B (Peak 12), Ganoderic acid A (Peak 20), Lucidenic acid A (Peak 24), Ganoderic acid D (Peak 27), and Ganoderic acid F (Peak 33).

Similarity Analysis (SA)

The similarity results revealed that the intra-group similarities of G. lucidum from 15 regions were greater than 0.990, indicating high consistency within groups. When the 15 regions were divided into 12 groups and the generated reference chromatograms (R1-R12) were compared with each batch, it was found that, except for Chizhou, Anhui (S24-S26) with intra-group similarities of 0.944, 0.946, and 0.947; Baoshan and Yingjiang, Yunnan ranging from 0.967 to 0.973; and Jiaobe City, Jilin ranging from 0.969 to 0.976, all other batches exhibited similarities greater than 0.980, suggesting relatively high inter-group similarity. G. lucidum from Congjiang, Guizhou (S38-S40), Baoshan City, Yunnan (S1-S3), Yingjiang County, Dehong Prefecture, Yunnan (S4-S6), and Jiaobe City, Jilin (S7-S11) were distinct from those of other producing areas, while the quality of G. lucidum from the remaining regions showed minimal differences, as reflected by high fingerprint similarity. Comparison of intra- and inter-group similarities indicated a certain consistency in chemical composition among G. lucidum from different geographical origins (Fig. S2). However, only SA was insufficient to elucidate the underlying reasons for compositional differences among producing regions or to identify the characteristic peaks significantly contributing to these variations, necessitating further in-depth analysis.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Based on the UPLC fingerprint, the 49 batches of G. lucidum were classified into five distinct categories by PCA according to their chemical characteristics (Fig. 1c). The samples from Baoshan (Yunnan) were separated as an independent group, indicating unique chemical profiles. Samples from Yingjiang (Yunnan), Chenggu and Yang County (Shaanxi), Jingde (Anhui), Congjiang (Guizhou), and Longquan (Zhejiang) were clustered into one category, with internal variations observed among these geographically diverse origins. Samples from Jiaohe (Jilin) and Xixiang (Shaanxi) were closely aggregated. Another cluster was formed by samples from Lu’an (Anhui), Nanping (Fujian), and Qingyun (Zhejiang), with S49 identified as an outlier. Additionally, samples from Chizhou (Anhui) and Qiannan (Guizhou) were grouped together. Further loading plot analysis revealed that the components represented by Peaks 1, 37, 16, 27, and 50 were the most significantly influenced by geographical differences (Fig. 1d).

Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA)

An OPLS-DA model was constructed for the analysis of G. lucidum samples (Fig. S3). The model parameters indicated that the explained variance of the independent variables (R2X) was 0.943, the explained variance of the dependent variables (R2Y) was 0.896, and the predictive ability (Q2) was 0.753. Both R2Y and Q2 were above 0.5, demonstrating that the model possesses good explanatory power and predictive reliability. Moreover, the model underwent 200 permutation validations, with the Q2 regression line consistently crossing the Y-axis at negative values, thus substantiating no overfitting issues. The validated model was thus considered effective and can be applied for discriminant analysis of the geographical origins of G. lucidum (Fig. S4).

The contribution of each chromatographic peak to discrimination was evaluated based on VIP score, with a threshold of VIP ≥ 1. Among all 49 chromatographic peaks, 19 key differential markers were identified. These markers were ranked in descending order of contribution as follows: Peaks 36, 40, 15, 26, 47, 34, 4, 19, 9, 6, 16, 1, 30, 35, 38, 7, 28, 29, and 11 (Fig. 1e). These key markers can be utilized as primary chemical indicators for discriminating the geographical origins of G. lucidum.

In summary, through the establishment of a UPLC fingerprint for multi-origin G. lucidum and integrated chemometric analysis, not only was the chemotype classification achieved, but 19 significantly contributing discriminatory markers were also precisely identified. A scientific and reliable modern analytical methodology and data support for quality control and geographical origin discrimination of G. lucidum was provided in this study.

Identification of the co-existing compounds in the UPLC fingerprint of G. lucidum using UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS

Network pharmacology analysis predicted the chemical compounds of G. lucidum for the treatment of HF

In this study, 44 chemical components were identified in G. lucidum using UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS, and 515 potential active ingredient-related targets were predicted through target prediction. After intersecting with HF-related targets, 120 common targets were obtained (Fig. 2c). Subsequently, networks were constructed for the “herb-active ingredients” and PPI (Fig. 2d, e), and topological analysis was performed to identify the top 20 key targets through Degree metrics (Tab. S3). The results indicated that G. lucidum may intervene in HF through multi-target synergistic actions, involving core pathological processes such as inflammation regulation (TNF, MAPKs), ECM metabolism (MMPs), and apoptosis (CASP family). Notably, the enrichment of signaling hub genes such as PIK3CA and mTOR suggested that G. lucidum’s anti-fibrotic impact could be mediated through the regulation of pivotal signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT cascade.

In addition, KEGG analysis further elucidated the multi-pathway synergistic mechanism of G. lucidum in intervening HF. Besides regulating the core inflammatory response through the TNF signaling pathway, it also simultaneously targeted the PI3K/AKT signaling axis to affect cellular viability and multiplication. Additionally, it responded to hypoxic stress during fibrotic progression via the HIF-1 signaling pathway. Enrichment of the EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance pathway suggested that G. lucidum might participate in the regulation of HSC activation by modulating the EGFR-related signaling network (Fig. 2f). Finally, an “active ingredient-target-pathway” network was constructed (Fig. 2g), in which the active ingredients were ranked according to Degree values (Table 2). The holistic regulatory framework of “multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway” for G. lucidum’s active compounds in HF treatment was visually revealed in this network. Notably, both PPI and KEGG analyses indicated that the PI3K/AKT pathway is central to the fibrosis prevention process in G. lucidum, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent molecular docking and experimental validation of key targets in this pathway.

Identification of Q-markers and molecular docking results

The top 20 components ranked by Degree values were selected as screening criteria after network pharmacology. These components were then intersected with the 19 intrinsic components influencing the quality of G. lucidum identified through chemometrics analysis. Ultimately, 7 common components were identified as Q-markers: Ganoderic acid C6, Ganoderic acid L, Ganoderenic acid F, Ganoderic acid α, Ganoderic acid H, Ganoderic acid C2, and Ganoderic acid J (Fig. S5).

Based on the network pharmacology suggesting that G. lucidum may mitigate fibrosis through PI3K/AKT pathway regulation, 7 identified Q-markers were subjected to molecular docking with proteins related to this pathway (Fig. 3, S6, S7). The results demonstrated favorable binding interactions between Q-markers and the PI3K/AKT pathway proteins. Except for Ganoderic acid C2, which exhibited a binding energy of -6.3 kcal/mol with AKT1, all other binding energies were lower than − 7 kcal/mol, supporting the hypothesis that G. lucidum’s bioactive compounds may exert anti-fibrotic effects by interacting with pivotal proteins within the pathway.

Molecular docking results of 7 Q-markers with proteins associated with the PI3K/AKT pathway

Analysis of the binding energy heatmap revealed that GAJ exhibited stronger binding interactions with PIK3CA, AKT1, and mTOR compared to the other components (Fig. S8). Therefore, GAJ was selected for subsequent in vitro validation of the anti-fibrotic mechanism.

GAJ inhibited TGF-β1-induced HF in HSC-T6 cells

CCK8 assay to assess GAJ treatment’s impact on cellular viability across various concentrations and durations

To determine the optimal concentrations of GAJ for subsequent experiments, a HF model was established in HSC-T6 cells by stimulation with TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL). The cytotoxicity of GAJ (1.25–100 µg/mL) in this model was then evaluated via CCK-8 assay at 12, 24, and 48 h (Fig. 4b). The results demonstrated that GAJ’s suppression of cell viability was both concentration- and time-dependent within the tested concentration range. Compared to the 24-h group, drug-exposed samples maintained above 90% viability after 12 h of incubation, while they fell below 80% following 48 h of incubation. In contrast, within the 24 h period, a continuous decline in cell viability was observed with increasing concentrations of GAJ. Since the cell viability dropped below 80% at higher concentrations after 24 h of treatment, three sub-toxic concentrations (5, 10, and 20 µg/mL) were selected as low, medium, and high doses, respectively, to ensure that subsequent mechanistic studies were conducted under non-cytotoxic conditions.

Apoptosis detection in HSC-T6 cells via flow cytometry post-GAJ administration

Flow cytometry was conducted to quantify apoptosis via double staining with Annexin V-FITC and PI [42].The total apoptosis rate (including early and late apoptosis) in the TGF-β1-induced model group (6.22 ± 0.13%) was significantly decreased compared to the control (9.90 ± 0.34%) (P < 0.001). However, treatment with the positive drug Galunisertib (13.59 ± 0.22%) and GAJ at 20 µg/mL (14.41 ± 0.15%) significantly reversed this effect, increasing the apoptosis rate in a dosage-responsive manner (P < 0.001). These results indicated that GAJ inhibits the progression of HF by accelerating apoptosis in activated HSC-T6 cells (Fig. 4c).

Immunofluorescence analysis of GAJ regulation of MMP1 and MMP2 expression in TGF-β1-induced HSC-T6 cells

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are crucial for remodeling ECM, and the ability to promote the degradation of abnormally deposited collagen can make all the difference in reversing HF [43]. It has been demonstrated that specifically enhancing MMP1 expression or suppressing MMP2 activity in the liver can effectively ameliorate the degree of HF in vivo [44, 45]. To investigate the effect of GAJ on the activation of HSC-T6 cells induced by TGF-β1, the expression and subcellular localization of MMP1 and MMP2 were detected by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4d). Compared with the control group, the fluorescence intensity of MMP1 was significantly weakened, while that of MMP2 was markedly enhanced in the TGF-β1-induced model group (P < 0.001). Both proteins were primarily localized in the cytoplasm, confirming the effective establishment of the HF cell model. After drug intervention, compared with the TGF-β1 group, an upregulation of MMP1 signal and a downregulation of MMP2 signal were observed in the Galunisertib group and all GAJ treatment groups (P < 0.05). Notably, the effect of GAJ exhibited a clear dose-dependent manner. GAJ can dose-dependently reverse the expression imbalance of MMP1/MMP2 in activated HSC-T6 cells, thereby restoring the dynamic balance of ECM degradation in this study.

Detection of PI3K/AKT pathway proteins by Western Blotting

To investigate how GAJ influences the PI3K/AKT pathway related proteins in HSC-T6 cells stimulated by TGF-β1, Western blotting was employed to measure the abundance of crucial pathway components such as PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference (Fig. 5). When stacked against the control group, the model group showed a significant uptick in the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios (P < 0.001). The findings demonstrated the effective activation of the PI3K/AKT survival cascade by TGF-β1, validating the successful development of the HF cell culture model. After intervention with GAJ, p-PI3K and p-AKT protein levels were significantly suppressed (P < 0.001). In conclusion, these results indicate that GAJ may attenuate HF by blocking the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Discussion

In view of the present challenges regarding inconsistent quality of G. lucidum materials and the lack of an efficacy-correlated quality evaluation system, an integrated strategy combining UPLC fingerprinting, chemometrics, UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS qualitative analysis, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and experimental verification was established to address the unclear identification of Q-markers and their anti-HF mechanisms. Seven Q-markers with anti-HF potential were successfully identified from G. lucidum, among which GAJ was confirmed as the pivotal component alleviating HF by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting apoptosis of activated HSC cells and restoring ECM dynamic balance.

One of the challenges in TCM, such as G. lucidum, lies in its complex chemical composition and geographical specificity, which directly influence its efficacy and quality. To move beyond single-component quantification, a comprehensive chemical profiling strategy was implemented. Firstly, UPLC fingerprinting was performed on 49 batches of G. lucidum collected from 15 distinct regions. A total of 49 common peaks were characterized, and 8 components were confirmed through reference standard comparisons. Subsequently, the chemical information embedded in the fingerprints was objectively decoded using chemometric analysis, including SA, PCA, and OPLS-DA. Finally, the 49 common components underwent a UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS qualitative examination, leading to the identification of 44 chemical constituents. Among these, 19 key components (VIP ≥ 1) were screened as critical chemical features for discriminating against G. lucidum from different origins, and they can be considered candidate indicators for quality assessment.

Meanwhile, a systematic perspective on the potential anti-HF mechanisms of the active constituents in G. lucidum was achieved through network pharmacology, which elucidates the “multi-component, multi-target” characteristics of TCM [46]. Subsequently, the 44 identified active components were subjected to network pharmacology against HF. By intersecting the top 20 components ranked by Degree values in the network with the 19 candidate markers obtained from chemometric analysis, seven common components were determined. This integration is crucial, as it ensures that the proposed Q-markers are not only chemically representative of the herb’s geographical identity but also pharmacologically relevant to the indicated therapeutic application-the treatment of HF. Thereby, the gap between chemical quality control and pharmacological activity is effectively bridged, aligning with the core concept of Q-markers discovery.

All 7 Q-markers are classified as highly oxidized tetracyclic triterpenoids, which represent characteristic bioactive constituents of G. lucidum. The diversity and intensity of their biological activities are primarily determined by variations in side chain modifications and the type, number, and position of functional groups, such as hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups. Ganoderma triterpenes were demonstrated to exert significant effects in suppressing lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory responses in vitro and mitigating acute liver injury in vivo, mediated through the TLR4/MyD88-mediated NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways [14]. Furthermore, as revealed by iTRAQ-based proteomic analysis in Zhao et al. [47], Ganoderma triterpene acids were shown to confer hepatoprotection through the suppression of lipid peroxidation, enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activities, and inhibition of apoptotic cell death and immune-inflammatory responses.

The PI3K/AKT pathway was identified by network pharmacology as the central hub in the anti-fibrotic mechanism of G. lucidum. Extensive research has demonstrated that suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway effectively alleviates HF [48–53]. The pathway critically influences HF evolution by modulating essential HSC functions such as activation, proliferation, and survival. Activation of this pathway facilitates the transformation of quiescent HSCs into myofibroblasts, driving ECM accumulation characteristic of fibrosis [49]. The phosphorylated (activated) forms, p-PI3K and p-AKT, serve as markers of pathway activation, with AKT acting as a key survival kinase possessing potent anti-apoptotic functions [54, 55]. A multi-pathway synergistic regulatory network for G. lucidum intervention in HF was illustrated by the integrated activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway alongside the TNF and HIF-1 pathways. Molecular docking results observed favorable binding energies between all 7 Q-markers and the core proteins of the PI3K/AKT pathway, strongly suggesting their direct regulatory potential on this pathway to exert anti-fibrotic effects. Notably, GAJ exhibited superior binding affinity compared to the other markers, supporting its selection as the candidate Q-marker for subsequent in vitro experimental validation.

The anti-fibrotic efficacy of GAJ was confirmed in vitro using TGF-β1-stimulated HSC-T6 cells. Firstly, the anti-apoptotic phenotype induced by TGF-β1 was effectively reversed by GAJ. In fibrosis, activated HSCs acquire resistance to apoptosis, thereby perpetuating their survival and excessive ECM deposition [56]. Flow cytometry revealed that GAJ dose-dependently enhanced HSC apoptosis.

Furthermore, a positive influence on ECM remodeling was exerted by GAJ. An imbalanced expression of MMPs is recognized as a hallmark feature of fibrotic progression [57, 58]. Immunofluorescence revealed that the expression of MMP1, which degrades fibrillar collagen, was upregulated in a dose-dependent manner by GAJ. The expression of MMP2, which degrades basement membrane collagen and promotes fibrosis, was downregulated. This shift in the MMP1/MMP2 balance toward a degradation-favoring state is considered a critical mechanism for facilitating the resolution of fibrosis [59].

Most importantly, the core hypothesis derived from network pharmacology and molecular docking was directly validated by Western blotting: the anti-HF effect of GAJ was mediated through the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway activation, as confirmed by the significant decrease in the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios following GAJ treatment. The overall anti-fibrotic effect was logically explained by this inhibition of the pro-survival pathway.

It is worth noting that polysaccharides, another major class of bioactive constituents in G. lucidum with well-established immunomodulatory and hepatoprotective effects, were not investigated in this study. Future studies integrating both triterpenoids and polysaccharides may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the holistic quality and pharmacological mechanisms of G. lucidum.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully bridged the gap between chemical quality assessment and pharmacological mechanisms of G. lucidum by establishing an integrated Q-marker discovery pipeline linking chemical composition with biological functions. Through UPLC fingerprinting, chemometric analysis, UPLC-Orbitrap-HRMS qualification, network pharmacology, and molecular docking, 7 Q-markers were identified for G. lucidum in the treatment of HF. Among them, GAJ was confirmed as the pivotal active component, in vitro cellular experiments confirmed that it primarily inhibits HSC activation by suppressing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, promoting apoptosis, and restoring ECM metabolic homeostasis. Except for HSC-T6 cells, ongoing verification through animal testing is essential to establish the therapeutic effectiveness and safety of GAJ. In addition, the potential synergistic or additive effects among the 7 Q-markers should be investigated to better elucidate the holistic action mechanism of G. lucidum. This work not only deepens the understanding of the anti-fibrotic mechanism of G. lucidum but provides a scientific basis for its quality control based on a “quality-efficacy” integrated model, laying a foundation for more reliable and effective clinical application.
