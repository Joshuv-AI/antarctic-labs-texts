Micro-CT analysis of calcium silicate-based cements in simulated acidic and neutral environments: a comparative study
Authors: Aliye Kamalak, Esra Balkanlıoglu, Mert Ocak, Hakan Hamdi Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in vitro investigation aimed to examine the physical characteristics of three calcium silicate-based cements—Neo MTA Plus, Biodentine, and MTA Repair HP—when used as root canal filling materials under diverse environmental conditions, employing micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis.MethodsA total of 144 extracted human maxillary anterior teeth were sectioned to produce standardized root canals. These canals were then randomly distributed into 12 groups (n = 12) according to the type of material used, the method of placement (either manual or ultrasonic), and the storage environment (acidic or phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH ≈ 7.4) served as the neutral comparison condition referred to in the title. The materials were inserted into the prepared canals and underwent micro-CT scanning both before and after a 7-day exposure to the designated environments. The resulting changes in volume, density, and porosity were measured. Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data were non-parametric, Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s post hoc tests were used (p < 0.05).ResultsUnder acidic conditions, a trend toward structural changes was observed in all tested materials. Biodentine exhibited a greater tendency toward volume reduction compared to the other materials; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Increased porosity was noted under acidic conditions, particularly in Biodentine, whereas specimens stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) showed minimal changes. No statistically significant differences were detected between the acidic and PBS conditions; however, several baseline-to-post-incubation comparisons showed statistically significant differences according to Dunn’s post hoc test.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this study, calcium silicate-based materials showed a tendency toward structural changes under acidic conditions, although these were not always statistically significant. Biodentine exhibited a greater tendency for change compared to the other materials, while placement technique had no significant effect.Keywords: Microcomputed Tomography, Volume Changes, Calcium Silicate Cements, Endodontic Biomaterials, Biodentine, Neo MTA Plus, MTA Repair HP

Introduction

A critical requirement for the success of root canal treatment is the effective three-dimensional sealing of the root canal system using an impermeable material. The ideal material for root canal filling should completely occupy the canal space, adhere firmly to the canal walls, and maintain a uniform density throughout the complex anatomy of the root canal [1]. Although primary endodontic treatments generally have high success rates, failures may still occur, especially in cases involving immature teeth or persistent periapical infections [2, 3].

Dental trauma or pulp necrosis occurring prior to the completion of root development can lead to the presence of open apices. In such instances, traditional root canal obturation methods prove inadequate due to the absence of apical constriction. Apexification is a procedure that facilitates the formation of a calcified barrier at the root tip in immature teeth [4]. Alternatively, when apical infection persists, apical surgery and retrograde filling may be necessary to eradicate the infected tissue and reestablish the seal of the root canal system [5].

In recent years, bioactive calcium silicate-based materials have been widely investigated due to their favorable physicochemical and biological properties, as well as their potential applications in procedures such as apexification, perforation repair, retrograde fillings, and revascularization. This is largely due to their remarkable biocompatibility and sealing capabilities. Products such as Neo MTA Plus, Biodentine, and MTA Repair HP represent recent developments in calcium silicate-based materials with improved physicochemical characteristics [6, 7]. These cements differ in composition and physical behavior. Neo MTA Plus contains tricalcium silicate and tantalum oxide, which enhance its radiopacity and handling properties. Biodentine, composed primarily of tricalcium silicate with zirconium oxide and calcium chloride, offers a faster setting time and improved mechanical strength. MTA Repair HP, containing tricalcium silicate and calcium tungstate, exhibits enhanced flow characteristics and improved biocompatibility. These compositional differences may influence their performance under various environmental conditions, including acidic or neutral pH [8, 9].

Nonetheless, the manipulation of these materials, particularly during their placement in confined environments, presents significant challenges. Various techniques have been proposed for their placement, including manual compaction and ultrasonic activation, both of which may affect the material’s adaptation and sealing efficacy [10].

In clinical environments, these materials frequently come into direct contact with periapical tissues and may be subjected to diverse environmental conditions, including exposure to blood, inflammatory exudates, and acidic byproducts of infection. Such conditions, particularly those characterized by low pH, have the potential to alter the physical properties of the materials, including volume stability, density, and porosity [8, 11]. In this investigation, butyric acid was employed to simulate acidic conditions, while phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used to represent a neutral environment.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) is a non-destructive, high-resolution imaging technique that permits comprehensive three-dimensional examination of internal structures without altering the specimen [12]. This technique enables accurate assessment of the volume, density, and porosity of dental materials both before and after they undergo environmental changes.

In this study, we sought to assess the alterations in the physical properties—namely volume, density, and porosity—of various calcium silicate-based materials following their application using two distinct techniques (manual and ultrasonic) and subsequent exposure to diverse environmental conditions (acidic, PBS). We posited that exposure to an acidic pH would significantly undermine the physical integrity of these materials to a greater extent than the other environmental conditions. The primary outcome of this study was volumetric change, while density and porosity were considered secondary outcomes. The null hypothesis was that environmental conditions and placement techniques would not significantly affect the volumetric change of the tested materials, nor their density and porosity.

The present study was designed as a material property investigation rather than a direct simulation of a specific clinical procedure. Therefore, the entire root canal space was filled with the tested materials to ensure standardized material volume and distribution, allowing accurate and reproducible micro-CT evaluation of volumetric and structural changes under different environmental conditions. This approach minimizes variability related to cavity design and placement technique, enabling a more reliable comparison of the intrinsic properties of the tested materials. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted primarily within the context of material behavior rather than direct clinical performance.

Sample size and power analysis

The study included 12 experimental groups (n = 12), totaling 144 teeth. A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.7, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on a one-way ANOVA (fixed effects, omnibus) indicated that 10 samples per group were required to detect a large effect size (f = 0.40) with α = 0.05 and 0.80 power. To compensate for potential sample loss, 12 specimens were used per group, providing an achieved power of approximately 0.90 for detecting large effects.

Sample collection

A total of 144 extracted human maxillary anterior teeth were collected from the oral surgery clinic at [Kahramanmaras Sutcu Imam University], following extraction due to periodontal or orthodontic reasons. Teeth were anonymized, disinfected, and stored in 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C until use. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No: 2020/04), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Inclusion criteria

In this study, 144 human maxillary anterior teeth were extracted for periodontal or orthodontic reasons (n = 12). The mesio-distal and bucco-lingual diameters of the tooth roots were measured using digital radiography (Digora Optime, Soredex, Turkey) and roots with standard dimensions (mesiodistal (MD): 6.12 ± 0.5 mm, buccolingual (BL): 5.9 ± 0.5 mm) were included in the study [1]. Radiographs were taken in both the MD and BL directions using a 60 kVp, 4 mA X-ray machine (Planmeca, Prox, Helsinki, Finland) to select teeth of similar size and to confirm that each tooth had only one root canal. The teeth to be included in the study should have completed root formation; should not have caries, cracks, fractures, or any restoration; and should have a minimal inclination. Organic residues on the root surface were removed and plaque or calcified hard tissues on the roots were scraped with periodontal curettes. Then, the roots of the selected teeth were cut from the apex region with the help of diamond disks under water cooling and 12 mm standard length root canals with open root ends were obtained. Although anatomical standardization was performed using two-dimensional radiographic measurements (mesiodistal and buccolingual dimensions), micro-CT–based volumetric matching was not carried out. This may have introduced variability in the initial canal volume among samples.

Preparation of samples

The teeth were drilled with a high-speed diamond bur (SybronEndo, Orange, CA, USA) under continuous water spray to provide straight entry into the canal. Following pulp extirpation of the teeth in each group using a barbed broach, the root canals were prepared by a single experienced practitioner employing the crown-down technique with rotary instruments (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) operated by a torque-controlled motor (VDW Gold Reciproc Endomotor, Munich, Germany) configured at 300 rpm with an in-out motion up to the F5 file. Between each preparation step, irrigation was performed with disposable syringes and 30 G NaviTip needles (Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA) using a total of 3 ml of 2.5% NaOCl (Imıcryl Endosolve, Konya, Turkey) per canal. Final irrigation with 3 ml 17% EDTA (pH 7.7) (Imıcryl E.D.T.A., Konya, Turkey) was performed at a rate of 1 ml/min for 5 min, followed by rinsing with 5 ml distilled water for 5 min. The canals were then dried using paper cones (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland).

Root canal filling

All materials (Neo MTA Plus, Biodentine, and MTA Repair HP) were prepared according to their respective manufacturers’ instructions (Table 1). In all specimens, the entire canal space was filled with the tested material to ensure standardized material distribution and to allow accurate micro-CT analysis of volumetric and porosity changes. In the manual groups, each material was delivered into the canal and compacted incrementally using #3 and #4 pluggers (Dentsply Maillefer). Excess moisture was removed using paper points. In the ultrasonic groups, after initial manual placement and compaction, an ultrasonic tip was applied at the lowest power for 1 s while in contact with the plugger to enhance material adaptation. All access cavities were sealed coronally with glass ionomer cement (Ketac Molar, 3 M ESPE, USA). Radiographs were taken to confirm the quality of obturation. The canal space was filled with the tested materials to ensure consistent material volume and distribution across all samples, thereby minimizing variability and ensuring standardization. This approach allowed accurate micro-CT evaluation of volumetric and porosity changes without the influence of confounding factors such as cavity geometry or condensation pressure. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the volumetric and structural stability of calcium silicate–based cements under different environmental conditions, rather than their performance in retrograde applications.

Micro-CT analysis

Following obturation, the samples were scanned using a high-resolution micro-computed tomography system (SkyScan 1172, Bruker-microCT, Kontich, Belgium) operated at 90 kV and 110 µA with a 0.5 mm aluminum filter, a 360° rotation, and 0.5° rotation steps. The voxel size was set to 12 μm, and approximately 1500–1600 axial sections were acquired per tooth. Raw projection images were reconstructed into cross-sectional datasets using NRecon v1.6.3 software (Bruker-microCT), and quantitative three-dimensional analysis of filling volume and voids was performed using CTAn v1.12. Voids were segmented and quantified using grayscale thresholding of the reconstructed micro-CT images. The void volume (mm³) was normalized to the total volume of the filling material to calculate the percentage of voids for each specimen. Volumetric changes, material density, and porosity were calculated from these datasets. Three-dimensional visualization and rendering were performed using CTVol v2.2.1 (Bruker), allowing detailed evaluation of the internal microstructure of the tested cements. Representative 3D reconstructions and volume renderings are presented in the Results section (Figs. 4 and 5) to illustrate structural differences under different environmental conditions.

Post-incubation assessment

After initial scanning, all specimens were stored in Eppendorf tubes filled with either phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or butyric acid and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days to simulate different environmental conditions. The acidic (butyric acid) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solutions were refreshed every 48 h to maintain stable pH levels throughout the 7-day incubation period. The acidic medium was prepared using a 1% butyric acid solution adjusted to pH 4.4 to simulate periapical inflammatory conditions. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH ≈ 7.4) served as the control group, representing a neutral physiological condition and thus eliminating the need for a separate neutral medium.

Following incubation, the samples were re-scanned using the same micro-CT protocol. The post-incubation filling volume and void ratios were evaluated to assess material performance in different environments.

Statistical analysis

The conformity of the variables to a normal distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since the data did not meet the assumptions of normality, comparisons between groups were made using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For post hoc analysis, Dunn’s test was used to identify pairwise differences. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. Results are presented as medians with minimum and maximum values. Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 3.3.2.

Discussion

This investigation explored the volumetric alterations and void formation in root canals filled with three calcium silicate-based materials—Neo MTA Plus, Biodentine, and MTA Repair HP—under distinct environmental conditions: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a pH of 7.4 and butyric acid at a pH of 4.4. The study employed two placement techniques, manual and ultrasonic, and utilized high-resolution micro-CT imaging for analysis.

Root canal filling materials are frequently exposed to inflammatory environments, which are often marked by acidity due to apical infections [2]. The properties of calcium silicate-based filling materials can be influenced by the environments in which they are applied. In our research, we selected butyric acid to replicate an inflammatory condition. This acid, a metabolic byproduct of anaerobic bacteria, plays a role in the acidification of the periapical environment, thereby altering the pH of the surrounding milieu [3]. In a similar vein, Yang et al. demonstrated that acidic environments diminish the microhardness and negatively impact the surface properties of calcium silicate-based sealers [8]. Research has demonstrated that it disrupts the formation of hydroxyapatite and interferes with the hydroxyl ion-mediated setting reaction in silicate materials, resulting in altered structural and mechanical properties. In our results, a trend toward reduced values was observed in volume and density under acidic conditions.

Recent studies have demonstrated that acidic environments negatively affect the structural integrity of calcium silicate-based materials by increasing porosity, reducing microhardness, and inducing volumetric changes [9]. In the assessment of various materials, Biodentine showed a greater tendency toward volume reduction compared to the other materials; however, this difference was not statistically significant [9, 11]. However, it is noteworthy that Biodentine showed a tendency toward increased porosity under acidic conditions compared to the other materials; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Lower porosity may indicate better compaction and setting. As the hydration process advances in calcium silicate materials, there is a notable decrease in porosity accompanied by an increase in mechanical resistance [13].

Recent studies have demonstrated that calcium silicate-based materials may exhibit increased solubility, altered setting reactions, and reduced mechanical properties under acidic conditions [8, 9, 11]. Additionally, it has been observed that acidic conditions can reduce both the microhardness and elastic modulus of calcium silicate materials. However, these effects may be partially reversible when the materials are subsequently stored under neutral pH conditions. This underscores the significance of the surrounding environment in maintaining the integrity of such materials [8].

In a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) study conducted by Akıncı et al., the investigation focused on the physical properties of calcium silicate-based materials under simulated environmental conditions, in which only the apical region was exposed to acidic media for a limited period [14].

Tronstad et al. observed that the pH levels, initially ranging from 6.4 to 7, increased to between 11.1 and 12.2 after the application of calcium hydroxide in both vital and necrotic pulp teeth, regardless of whether the apices were closed or open. Previous studies have demonstrated that pH levels in dental tissues can be significantly altered following the application of calcium hydroxide [15]. In our investigation, we subjected the entire root surface to acid exposure for an extended period of one week. This methodology diverges from previous research by applying acid to the entire root surface for a continuous seven-day period, thereby creating a more aggressive experimental model to simulate extended acidic exposure conditions.

The present findings suggest that MTA Repair HP and Neo MTA Plus tended to maintain structural properties comparable to Biodentine under acidic conditions. Although acidic and PBS conditions did not differ significantly in the main experimental comparisons, several baseline-to-post-incubation comparisons reached statistical significance, as shown in Table 2. All tested materials showed reductions in both volume and porosity, although these changes were not statistically significant. Among the tested materials, Biodentine showed the greatest tendency toward reduction. These results differ from those of other studies, such as Agrafioti et al., which found no significant differences between Biodentine and MTA under acidic conditions. This discrepancy may be due to variations in the type and concentration of acid used in the experiments [16].

In the present study, no statistically significant differences were observed between the manual and ultrasonic placement techniques in terms of volumetric stability or void formation (p > 0.05). While ultrasonic activation has been proposed as a method to enhance the adaptation of calcium silicate-based materials by improving their flow and minimizing internal voids, our findings suggest that its effects may depend on activation parameters such as duration, power settings, and tip design. In this study, ultrasonic activation was applied for a brief duration (one second) and at low power, which may have constrained its ability to affect the material’s placement characteristics. This limited activation protocol may have been insufficient to produce a measurable effect on material adaptation and void formation. It is possible that the use of higher power settings or longer activation times could result in different outcomes.

Previous studies have reported mixed outcomes regarding the efficacy of ultrasonic activation. For instance, Nekoofar et al. [17] and Camilleri et al. [18, 19] demonstrated that ultrasonic vibration can enhance the flow and marginal adaptation of MTA and Biodentine in confined spaces. Conversely, other investigation [20] has shown that ultrasonic energy does not always lead to a statistically significant reduction in voids or improvement in sealing ability. The varied outcomes across studies may be attributed to differences in methodology, including the type of ultrasonic device used, the consistency and handling properties of each material, and whether activation was performed in a confined or open environment.

Our study revealed that the ultrasonic application of Neo MTA Plus showed a tendency toward fewer voids under acidic conditions; however, this difference was not statistically significant. This outcome may be linked to the material’s favorable flow properties and smaller particle size, which could be more effectively harnessed through vibration. In contrast, Biodentine showed a tendency toward more favorable results with manual application, which may be related to its thicker initial consistency and faster setting time, potentially limiting the effect of ultrasonic energy on compaction. These findings suggest that the intrinsic properties of each material may influence its response to different placement methods and support the need for further investigation into optimized ultrasonic parameters for specific endodontic materials. The present study focused on a 7-day observation period to evaluate early-stage physicochemical changes, which are considered the most critical phase for material setting and dimensional stability. Previous studies have reported that most of the solubility and porosity changes in calcium silicate-based cements occur within the first week, with only minor variations observed thereafter [13, 21]. Torres et al. demonstrated that Biodentine and MTA exhibited noticeable increases in porosity and solubility between 7 and 30 days, whereas ZOE remained relatively stable. These findings suggest that the first week may represent an active phase of hydration and ion exchange, during which early physicochemical changes become more apparent.

It is crucial to acknowledge that during clinical procedures, materials such as MTA often interact with biological fluids. Torabinejad et al. determined that the contamination of MTA with various liquids did not significantly affect its microleakage performance [22]. Our investigation found that although all materials showed measurable alterations in porosity, density, and volume when exposed to acidic conditions compared with the control samples, these alterations were not statistically significant. This suggests that acidic stress may influence the physicochemical behavior of these materials, although the magnitude of this effect was limited under the present experimental conditions. In summary, calcium silicate-based root canal filling materials may show a tendency toward physical changes under acidic conditions. Therefore, local pH conditions and the presence of inflammation may be considered when interpreting the potential clinical performance of these materials.

A limitation of this study is that sample standardization was based on two-dimensional radiographic measurements rather than three-dimensional volumetric matching using micro-CT. Differences in the initial canal volume among specimens may have influenced the accuracy of volume and void measurements, potentially introducing bias in the results.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, exposure to an acidic environment (pH 4.4) showed a tendency to influence the structural properties of calcium silicate–based cements, although these differences were not consistently statistically significant. Among the materials examined, Biodentine showed a greater tendency toward porosity increase and volumetric loss compared with Neo MTA Plus and MTA Repair HP; however, these inter-material differences were not consistently statistically significant. Conversely, specimens maintained in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) retained their structural stability, indicating that neutral conditions appeared to better preserve the dimensional stability of these materials during the 7-day observation period.

No statistically significant differences were detected between manual and ultrasonic placement techniques, suggesting that under the parameters tested, activation method does not substantially influence the sealing or structural performance of calcium silicate–based cements.

Clinically, the present findings suggest that environmental pH may influence the physicochemical behavior of calcium silicate–based cements. However, because most comparisons between the acidic and PBS conditions were not statistically significant, these observations should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in further studies.
