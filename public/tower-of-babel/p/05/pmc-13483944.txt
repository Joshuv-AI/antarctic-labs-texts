Machine learning-assisted development of a fast Mechanochemical Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky reaction
Authors: Francesco Mele, Ana M. Constantin, Marco Barezzi, Leonardo Rossi, Alex Ergasti, Tomaso Fontanini, Sofia Civardi, Remie M. Sundermann, Paolo P. Mazzeo, Raimondo Maggi, Nicola Della Ca’, Andrea Prati, Luca Capaldo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Johnson-Corey-Chaykovsky reaction stands as an elegant approach for the synthesis of cyclopropanes and epoxides. However, most procedures still rely on the original NaH/DMSO conditions, which pose notable safety and handling issues especially in view of industrial applications. Herein, we combine Bayesian Optimization and mechanochemistry to develop a rapid, solvent-free protocol for the Johnson-Corey-Chaykovsky reaction. By prioritizing efficiency and sustainability, Machine Learning quickly identified a new set of reaction conditions for this transformation, also demonstrating that these transformations can proceed efficiently under air-equilibrated, mild conditions using an inexpensive and safe base (KOH). The method is broadly applicable, scalable, and tolerant to diverse functional groups and enabled the preparation of a wide variety of three-membered homo- and heterocycles. Time-Resolved in situ X-ray Powder Diffraction experiments highlighted the crucial role of active milling in promoting this transformation. Overall, this work establishes a foundation for the integration of Machine Learning and mechanochemistry in designing industrially relevant transformations that prioritize safety and sustainability.

Introduction

Three-membered homo- and heterocycles such as cyclopropanes and epoxides are valuable structural motifs with broad utility in organic synthesis, serving as key intermediates, mechanistic probes, and bioactive fragments. The Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky reaction (JCC)11 stands as a very elegant metal-free approach for the synthesis of both cyclopropanes and epoxides12. This manifold bypasses the formation of a carbenoid species and fully pivots on the generation of a sulfur ylide from sulfonium or sulfoxonium salts via Brønsted base-promoted deprotonation (Fig. 1B). The ylide reacts with an electron-poor olefin or a ketone to deliver the expected cyclopropane or epoxide, respectively, via the intermediacy of a betaine intermediate. The JCC reaction has been extensively studied in solution, with high-yielding protocols reported across a broad substrate scope13. However, most procedures still rely on the original NaH/DMSO conditions14, which pose notable safety and handling issues, especially in view of industrial applications15. Developing safer and more practical alternatives remains a significant yet valuable goal to fully harness the potential of this classical transformation.

Mechanochemistry has recently gained traction as an enabling technology16 that employs mechanical energy to drive chemical transformations17–26. On the one hand, among its most compelling advantages are the ability to conduct reactions under solvent-minimized conditions and the potential to access unexpected reactivity pathways27–30. On the other hand, its adoption in synthesis remains limited: practitioners are not typically trained to address solvent-less reactivity, and the interplay between solid-state phenomena and milling parameters remains difficult to capture deterministically. Moreover, more often than not31, reaction optimization proceeds sequentially via OVAT (one-variable-at-a-time) approach, resulting in wasteful experimentation and increasing the risk of identifying only local rather than global optima. Recently, Bayesian Optimization (BO) was proposed as a tool to address these types of chemical problems32. Albeit this approach has been extensively studied in other fields33–36, its application in mechanochemistry has been overlooked. During the peer-review of this work, Lamaty and co-workers reported the combination of Bayesian Optimization with twin-screw reactive extrusion37, as well as its comparison with other optimization strategies in ball-milling processes38.

Applying BO for the development of synthetic methodology in mechanochemistry (Fig. 1C) would be particularly compelling for the following reasons: (i) large datasets required for data-hungry algorithms (i.e., deep learning) are not available in the literature as ball milling is a relatively novel technology, (ii) ball milling typically enables limited parallelization, which can constrain high-throughput experimentation31, (iii) the fundamental role of mechanical forces in driving chemical reactivity is not yet fully understood39–42, making inductive prediction of optimal conditions challenging. Additionally, BO is uniquely suited to handle multi-objective optimizations, where chemists want to optimize multiple target functions (e.g., yield and selectivity or yield and productivity).

Inspired by a recent report in the field of phosphorus-based ylides43, we opted to put Bayesian Optimization (BO) to the test to identify safer and more practical mechanochemical conditions for the JCC reaction (Fig. 1D). In this work, we use Machine Learning to identify safer and more practical mechanochemical conditions for the JCC reaction, enabling a solvent-free, scalable, and rapid protocol for the synthesis of a wide variety of cyclopropanes and epoxides and smoothing the path between academia and industry for this transformation.

Results

Informed by a preliminary experiment (see Section 4.2 in the SI), we started by optimizing the cyclopropanation of unsubstituted chalcone with trimethyl sulfoxonium iodide (TMSOI) via Bayesian Optimization (Fig. 2A). To this end, we developed a dedicated environment in Python with the primary aim of enhancing user-friendliness (Section 3 in the SI)44. The full source code is openly available on the GitHub repository as part of the Supplementary Information for this work. A graphical user interface (GUI) was developed using Gradio, a lightweight framework that transforms Python functions into interactive web applications. This choice allows end-users, including those without prior programming experience, to design and execute optimization tasks directly through a browser-based interface, thereby lowering the barrier to entry. For the optimization, we selected six variables (Fig. 2B): type of base, amount of base, reaction time, milling frequency, type of LAG (Liquid-Assisted Grinding) agent, and amount of LAG agent. We decided to keep a 1:1 ratio between chalcone and TMSOI to prioritize sustainability and minimize reagent excess. Although higher yields could, in principle, be achieved using an excess, this was not considered to ensure a consistent and sustainability-oriented optimization framework. As for categorical variables, we selected two safer alternatives to hazardous NaH: the inexpensive and readily available bases KOH and KOtBu. DMSO and CH3CN were chosen as LAG additives. As for continuous variables, base loading was varied from 0 to 2 equivalents, milling time from 0 to 300 seconds, and milling frequency from 3 to 30 Hz. All experiments were conducted in stainless-steel jars equipped with one stainless-steel ball (Ø = 10 mm) at room temperature (ca. 22 °C) using a ball mill. We did not screen the materials of these mechanochemical features, however their effect was tested as part of control experiments (see Section S4.6 in the SI). Our goal was to maximize yield while minimizing the Process Mass Intensity (PMI). In fact, the former reflects reaction efficiency, while the latter is routinely used as a proxy for the sustainability of the transformation, being defined as the ratio between the input mass and the output product mass of a process (see Sections 4.3 and 5.2 for more detail).

We decided to face this multivariable optimization following two complementary approaches: a mono-objective approach based on the Euclidean distance from a utopian point and a multi-objective Bayesian optimization.

In the first approach, i.e., Utopia Point Bayesian Optimization (UPBO), all relevant parameters are combined within a single response function, and the algorithm minimizes the Euclidean distance from the utopian point (yield = 100%, PMI: 2.18). On the one hand, this approach offers several advantages: (i) it is conceptually simple and readily accessible to non-specialists in machine learning; (ii) it reduces complex multi-objective optimization tasks to a lower-dimensional problem; and (iii) it is a data-efficient model compared to other algorithms. On the other hand, UPBO is very sensitive to scaling and normalization of the dimensions and does not offer information on the trade-off between objectives.

In the second approach, i.e., Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization (MOBO), yield and PMI are treated as two separate target functions: the algorithm must maximize the former, while minimizing the latter. Importantly, this algorithm better highlights the trade-offs encountered during optimization. For example, increasing reagent stoichiometry (e.g., excess base) may enhance yield, yet possibly leads to a deterioration in PMI.

For both approaches, a two-stage optimization was carried out. Initially, a series of experiments were generated using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) to ensure well-distributed coverage across the chemical search space. This initialization strategy provided a robust foundation for subsequent optimization. Following this initial sampling phase, the Bayesian optimization algorithm (either UPBO or MOBO) built a probabilistic surrogate of the objective(s) from past data and intelligently proposed new experiments using an acquisition function that balanced exploration, targeting less-studied regions to improve the model’s knowledge, and exploitation, focusing on conditions predicted to yield the best results.

After 20 experiments (see Sections 4.5 and 4.6 of the SI for details), both the strategies converged on very similar reaction conditions shown in Fig. 2C, which offer several key advantages over the classical JCC protocol. First, the reaction can proceed without solvent and with a cheap and safe base (KOH), minimizing safety risks. Second, the reaction proceeds under air-equilibrated conditions, eliminating the need for strictly inert conditions required to handle moisture-sensitive NaH. Most strikingly, the transformation that traditionally required 4–16 h and pre-formation of the ylide now reaches completion within only 5 min under mechanochemical activation. It is important to mention that active milling was crucial to observe reactivity. Preliminary experiments on the solvent-minimized JCC reaction were reported by ref. 45; however, these conditions could not be reproduced in our hands.

In particular, UPBO suggested the use of KOH as base and DMSO as LAG (η = 0.4), milling at 30 Hz for 2.5 min. In contrast, MOBO determined the optimal conditions to be under neat conditions (without a LAG agent), using KOH and milling at 21.5 Hz for 5 min.

It is noteworthy that UPBO afforded a higher yield but a less favorable PMI. This outcome likely reflects the structure of the objective function, which combines yield and PMI into a single metric. Minimization of this function can be achieved either by increasing yield or by reducing waste. In this case, UPBO prioritized yield improvement, suggesting adjustments to instrumental parameters (e.g., frequency or milling time) to enhance energy input and, consequently, product formation. However, it did not identify solvent elimination as a strategy to reduce PMI. In contrast, MOBO was implemented to simultaneously maximize yield and explicitly minimize PMI. Under these conditions, the algorithm rapidly identified waste reduction as the more effective strategy, favoring mechanochemical conditions. Accordingly, we proceeded with the MOBO-derived conditions, as they enable solvent-free operation. For a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) analysis of the MOBO algorithm, see Section 4.8 in the SI.

By further analyzing the correlation between yield and milling variables for the MOBO approach (see Section 4.6 in the SI), we found that simply increasing the frequency would have allowed us to identify the ideal conditions for our mechanochemical JCC. In fact, when increasing the frequency to 25 Hz while keeping all other parameters unchanged, we obtained 96% 1H NMR yield with a PMI of 2.28. Pleasingly, product 1 was isolated in an excellent yield of 96% by simple filtration through Celite, thus keeping the lowest PMI possible.

We investigated the scope of the methodology across a broad range of α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds and Michael acceptors. Aromatic chalcones with electron-withdrawing (2–5, 70–95% yield) and electron-donating (6 and 7, 51–98% yield) substituents were generally well tolerated. Notably, acidic functionalities like free carboxylic acids (8, 88% yield) could be accommodated as well. Heteroaromatic substrates (9 and 10, 94–95% yield) and a ferrocene derivative (11, 81% yield) were efficiently converted, highlighting broad functional-group tolerance. Conjugated dienones (12 and 13, 94–99% yield) reacted selectively at the more internal C = C double bond, while conjugated alkynes (14; 49% yield) and non-conjugated Michael acceptors (15, 66% yield) gave moderate to good yields. Aliphatic and cyclic enones (17 and 18; 74–92% yield), including spirocyclic systems, were compatible, as were substrates containing pyrrole or pyridine heterocycles (19 and 20; 47–82% yield). Alternative Michael acceptors, such as the synthetically versatile Weinreb amide, was also successfully transformed under ball-milling conditions (21, 55% yield). Notably, all reported products were obtained as single anti-diastereoisomer, consistent with the stereoselective course of the JCC reaction from E-olefins.

Next, we turned our attention to the JCC epoxidation and adopted the MOBO algorithm to optimize reaction conditions. Thus, we defined a parameter space comprising seven variables, including the nature of the sulfur salt, base identity and loading, LAG additive type and amount, as well as milling time and frequency. To align with the sustainability considerations outlined above for cyclopropanations, the substrate-to-sulfur salt ratio was maintained at 1:1. Safer alternatives to NaH (KOH, NaOH, and KOtBu) were chosen as bases, while THF was introduced alongside DMSO and CH3CN as LAG additives. Continuous parameters were varied across broad ranges (base loading: 1–3 equiv., reaction time: 5–60 min, frequency: 3–30 Hz, η: 0–1). Reactions were performed in stainless-steel jars with a single ball of the same material (Ø = 10 mm) at room temperature (ca. 22 °C). The optimal conditions found by Machine Learning are summarized in Fig. 4A: in those conditions, compound 22 was obtained in 99% 1H-NMR yield with a corresponding PMI of 4.11. The optimization revealed a clear positive correlation between reaction yield and the amount of LAG agent. The newly developed mechanochemical JCC epoxidation displayed broad generality across a variety of ketones (Fig. 4B). Aryl ketones furnished the corresponding epoxides in high efficiency (22 and 23, >53%). Aliphatic and cyclic ketones (24 and 25), including the natural product menthone, demonstrated that sterically encumbered frameworks remain accessible under these conditions. Beyond carbocyclic substrates, heteroaryl derivatives were also well tolerated (26–28, 56–65%), the formation of pyrazine epoxide being a notable example.

To expand the reaction to aldehydes, we evaluated the three top-performing conditions from the ketone optimization (see Section 5.6 in the SI). The most effective setup involved increasing the loading of the base (3.0 eq.), the LAG amount (η = 0.7), and milling time (60 min). Under these conditions, the reactivity could be extended to aromatic (29–34, up to 80%) and, at a minor extent, aliphatic (35, 51%) aldehydes.

To gain deeper insight into the mechanochemical reactivity, we monitored the time course of the cyclopropanation reaction under the optimized conditions (Fig. 5A left). Specifically, aliquots were collected at defined milling intervals, and the crude mixtures were analyzed by ¹H NMR spectroscopy. Thus, the transformation proceeded remarkably fast, reaching complete and clean conversion within five minutes. No reaction intermediates were spectroscopically detected. Since the adopted BO approach is intrinsically agnostic of the underlying reaction mechanism, we resorted to Time-Resolved in situ (TRIS) X-ray Powder Diffraction investigation to characterize in deeper detail the chemical pathway of the reaction, thus monitoring its progression over time and detecting any potential intermediate in operando conditions46–49. Due to the setup constraint, the mechanochemical JCC cyclopropanation was conducted in a custom X-Ray-transparent PMMA jar (see Section 9 in the SI for further details). Semiquantitative Phase Analyses (sQPA) was performed via sequential Rietveld Refinements following the consumption of the TMSOI as well as the formation of the crystalline KI produced as co-product. The experiment did not reveal the presence of any crystalline intermediate along the reaction path nor significant induction period (Fig. 5A).

Next, to evaluate the robustness and scalability of the process, we examined the performance of the optimized protocols on a preparative scale (Fig. 5B). Gratifyingly, the cyclopropanation of the model chalcone afforded the corresponding cyclopropane 1 in 96% isolated yield on a gram scale, using a 50 mL stainless-steel jar charged with five stainless-steel balls (Ø = 10 mm). The reaction reached completion after 20 min of milling, and the product was isolated by simple filtration through Celite. It is worth noting that, upon scaling the reaction to synthetically relevant quantities, we evaluated the E-factor and PMI, also considering the waste generated during the purification (see Section 8 of the SI for full details). Gratifyingly, the process exhibited a low material footprint, with a PMI of 15.2 and an E-factor of 14.2. Moreover, the epoxidation also proceeded with comparable robustness on gram scale, delivering 22 in 92% yield.

In addition, we demonstrated that biorelevant complex molecules are also amenable to this new set of conditions for the JCC reaction (Fig. 5C). In fact, N-methyl isatin, d-mannose, estrone, and pregnenolone were effectively functionalized, delivering the expected JCC products 36–39 in 45–75% yield.

In conclusion, Bayesian optimization proved instrumental in developing sustainable, safe, and efficient mechanochemical protocols for the Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky cyclopropanation and epoxidation. By rapidly identifying optimal conditions, BO establishes mechanochemistry as a viable route for the safe scale-up of JCC reactions, helping bridge the gap between academic discovery and industrial application for this widely used transformation.

After screening different algorithms, we selected Multi-Objective BO as it provided a better understanding of the yield-PMI trade-off. Mechanochemistry enabled the reactions to proceed under solvent-minimized or even neat conditions, allowing the use of a safer, inexpensive base (KOH) in place of hazardous NaH. The optimized protocol operates efficiently under ambient atmosphere, eliminating the need for inert conditions, and delivers complete conversion within minutes (cyclopropanation) and ~1 h (epoxidation), representing a rate acceleration of more than one order of magnitude compared to conventional solution methods. Additionally, no pre-formation of the sulfur ylide is required. The identified conditions were broadly effective across diverse substrates, with few exceptions: in these cases, substrate-specific optimization could be envisaged, possibly via automated workflows. Time-Resolved in situ X-ray Powder Diffraction experiments allowed to study the reaction in time, highlighting the fast nature of the cyclopropanation reaction. Overall, this study demonstrates how the synergy between Machine Learning and mechanochemistry can be harnessed to design industrially relevant transformations that prioritize safety and sustainability. Investigation is underway to expand the present approach for the preparation of aziridines50–52.

Methods

General information

All reagents and solvents were bought from Sigma Aldrich, TCI, Fluorochem, and BLDPharm and used as received unless otherwise specified. The solvents employed in this work were purchased from Carlo Erba or Sigma Aldrich and used as received. Disposable syringes were purchased from B. Braun. TLC analyses were performed using Silica on aluminum foils TLC plates (F254, Merck) with visualization under ultraviolet light (254 and 365 nm) or appropriate TLC staining (potassium permanganate). Compounds SM2–SM15, SM18–SM21, SM36, and SM37 have been synthesized as described in the Supplementary Information file (see Section 6.1).

NMR spectroscopy

1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE 400 or a JEOL 600 MHz ECZ600R spectrometers in CDCl3, using the solvent signals as internal reference (7.26 and 77.16 ppm for 1H and 13C, respectively). For 19F NMR, JEOL 600 MHz ECZ600R spectrometer was used. The terms m, s, d, t, q, and quint represent multiplet, singlet, doublet, triplet, quadruplet, and quintuplet, respectively. NMR data were processed using the MestReNova 14 software package. The identity of known compounds was confirmed by comparing the corresponding 1H NMR and 13C NMR with those reported in the literature retrieved via the Reaxys Database.

Machine Learning methods

The system was developed in Python to provide an integrated environment for data-driven experimental design based on Bayesian Optimization44. The architecture combines a web-based user interface and an optimization back-end, both designed to facilitate the exploration and refinement of chemical experiments. The front-end of the system is implemented using Gradio, which serves as an interactive web interface accessible through any modern browser. All user interactions are managed through a Gradio server, which can be accessed either locally or remotely within a network. Remote connections can be established by accessing the server’s IP address (e.g., http://<server_ip>:<port>).

The system is built entirely in Python, featuring a user-friendly interface powered by Gradio, a framework that creates intuitive web applications from Python functions. The optimization backend is based on BoTorch and PyTorch, providing a flexible and efficient implementation of Bayesian Optimization (BO). The system can take advantage of GPU acceleration to speed up model fitting and acquisition function optimization.

The system developed is publicly available on GitHub. A more detailed description of the architecture, as well as comprehensive installation and usage instructions, are provided in the repository to facilitate deployment and experimentation.

Time-resolved in-situ PXRD

Time-resolved in-situ (TRIS) monitoring via X-ray powder diffraction data were performed at the i11 beamline at the Diamond Light Source (DLS) synchrotron facility. The beam energy was set at approximately 15 KeV (λ = 0.824348 Å) with a beam size of 100 × 100 μm pointing at the PMMA milling jar mounted on the ball milling device (Fritsch Pulverisette 23) installed on an XYZ moving stage in the experimental hutch (EH) 2. The milling device was remotely controlled from the control hutch as well as its fine position with respect to the beam. XRPD data were collected every 15 s with an accumulation time of 10 s over a 2D Pilatus detector placed behind the jar.

General procedure for the Bayesian optimization of the JCC cyclopropanation

A 5 mL stainless-steel milling jar equipped with a hardened steel ball (Ø = 10 mm) was charged with TMSOI (44 mg, 0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.), chalcone (41.6 mg, 0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.), the selected base (x equiv.), and LAG (η). The vessel was mounted onto the holding station of a Retsch MM400 mixer mill, and milling was performed at a frequency of x Hz for x s. Upon completion, the crude reaction mixture was recovered using ethyl acetate (EtOAc, 5 mL), concentrated under reduced pressure, and the sample was analyzed via 1H NMR spectroscopy (CDCl3, using 0.1 mmol of CH2Br2 as internal standard). This value was inserted as the input for the web-interface for the next cycle of BO.

General procedure for the preparative JCC cyclopropanation

A 5 mL stainless-steel milling jar equipped with one 10 mm hardened steel ball was charged with TMSOI (44.0 mg 0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.), the selected olefin (0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.), and KOH (11.2 mg, 0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.). The vessel was mounted onto the holding station of a Retsch MM400 mixer mill, and milling was performed at a frequency of 25 Hz for 5 min. Upon completion, the crude reaction mixture was recovered using EtOAc (10 mL), concentrated under reduced pressure, and directly purified by a short filtration through Celite or by flash chromatography (Hexane/EtOAc in different ratio) to yield the target compound.

General procedure for the preparative JCC epoxidation

A 5 mL stainless-steel milling jar equipped with one 10 mm hardened steel ball was charged with TMSI (40.8 mg, 0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.), the selected ketone (0.2 mmol, 1 equiv.), KOH (16.8 mg, 0.3 mmol, 1.5 equiv.) and DMSO as LAG (η = 0.6). The vessel was mounted onto the holding station of a Retsch MM400 mixer mill, and milling was performed at a frequency of 14 Hz for 60 min. Upon completion, the crude reaction mixture was recovered using EtOAc (10 mL), washed with water, and extracted with EtOAc (3 × 10 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The product was purified by flash column chromatography (Hexane/EtOAc in different ratio) to yield the target compound.
