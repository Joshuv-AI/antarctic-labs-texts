GroupCeptionNet: a lightweight model for classifying chili seed germination with macro images
Authors: Changyu Ao, Tianpei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChili seed germination potential is a significant element influencing planting yield. Many researchers focus on feature extraction analysis methods to detect undesirable seed germination characteristics and improve classification accuracy. In deep learning, data collection and model complexity often limit practical application in production environments. This work includes a dataset of macroscopic images of chili seeds. This paper proposes a lightweight model GroupCeptionNet to process macroscopic images for screening non-germinated chili seeds before planting. Experimental results show that GroupCeptionNet achieves 94.66% accuracy and 94.65% F1-score under 1.40 M parameters, outperforming the classical CNN and Transformer models. We also explore the impact of background removal and GroupCeptionNet ablation and variant structures. The visualization results further validate the consistency of the model’s focus areas with human-annotated regions. The proposed dataset and model provide technical references for a low-cost and efficient chili seed screening process.Keywords: Chili seeds, Germination classification, Macro imaging, Lightweight model, Convolutional neural networks, Inception moduleSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Plant sciences

Introduction

Seed quality is the basis of agricultural productivity and has a direct impact on crop yield and quality. Early studies1,2 showed that environmental or physical factors easily affect seed quality and decreased yield and quality. Another study3 found that screening seeds for physical and physiological qualities in advance increases crop yield by 15–20%. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) claims high-quality seeds boost crop yields by as much as 100%4. Chili pepper (Capsicum spp.) is one of the major cash crops in the world. In 2021, the United States harvested over 31,000 acres of bell peppers and 10,000 acres of chili peppers, totaling more than 536 million dollars5. These findings show that improving seed quality is important for increasing crop yields and economic returns.

The germination screening of pepper seeds significantly improved the quality of cultivated seeds. Traditional classification methods such as visual inspection, flotation test and staining methods have limitations6. These methods rely on the experience and skills of inspectors, and fatigue makes the results subjective. A study7 shows that traditional methods are still effective in some specific cases, but modern agriculture requires greater efficiency and accuracy. Accurate screening of germinated seeds increases land resource utilization rate, yield and cultivation quality, especially for large-scale cultivation8. With advanced agricultural production automation, researchers use structured data such as seed length, width and weight or image analysis technology to determine whether chili seeds germinate more effectively9. Finer feature extraction necessitates high-quality acquisition equipment and complex algorithms, placing additional demands on the data processing capabilities of the devices.

On the agriculture applications side, the CNN method as a part of agricultural deep learning on seed classification and defect detection is due to its superior skill in feature extraction10. The traditional CNN model requires a large amount of computing resources and memory, making it difficult to achieve the goals of cheap cost and low power consumption in actual agricultural applications11. Deep learning researchers proposed many lightweight modules and network structure optimization methods12, which apply them to scenarios that require fast processing or limited resources. These studies optimized the convolution approach and network structure to improve the computational efficiency of the model while retaining the feature extraction ability13. The lightweight CNN method provides an effective technical solution for solving practical problems in agriculture14, which is critical for the advancement of smart agriculture.

Existing chili seed datasets15–17 are mostly used for species categorization and have not been tagged for germination. The study18 has shown that seed appearance features are strongly connected with germination ability, and image features are employed as an effective analysis for germination classification. Most of the existing studies use traditional imaging methods for image acquisition, which cannot deeply capture the microscopic details of the seed surface, limiting the accuracy of the model to identify subtle damage and color change19. Some studies have employed flatbed scanners or hyperspectral devices to take images of chili seeds, but these methods have limited image quality or are more expensive. Macro photography is cheap and simple to take. A smartphone with macro mode or an affordable macro lens module both produce high-quality photographs.

Related work

Seed image germination classification and variety classification both rely on the extraction and analysis of seed surface features. Olaes et al.20 proposed a method based on image processing and fuzzy k-nearest neighbor (KNN)21 to realize the classification of pepper seeds. Diosse et al.22 evaluated various classification techniques using the MATLAB toolbox. Weighted KNN and boosted trees have the highest classification accuracy. Loddo et al.23 designed SeedNet with a simplified network structure, which increased the inference speed while maintaining a classification accuracy of 97.47%. Kurtulmus et al.15 integrated feature selection with multilayer perceptron (MLP)24. The scanner captures the color, shape and texture features of pepper seeds, resulting in an accuracy of 84.94%. Sabanci et al.16 combined deep features of ResNet25 with a support vector machine (SVM). This CNN-SVM combination has 99.02% accuracy. The above studies demonstrate the efficiency of the seed image classification method based on surface morphological features. Seed surface characteristics not only identify the variety type but also correlate with germination potential.

The seed germination classification task initially studied transforming image features into structured data for classification. Huang et al.26 developed an automatic sorting system to capture the shape, color and texture features of kale seeds. The positive feature selection increases the final accuracy of the system to 90%. Men et al.27 employed infrared thermal imaging technology to record temperature changes during water absorption. Learning these temperature changes by SVM results in a new lossless way of identifying seed germination. Deep learning models based on CNNs directly learn and recognize image features. For example, Huang et al.28 employed the GoogLeNet29 model to classify corn seed defects with an accuracy of 95%. Zhao et al.30 proposed a real-time recognition system using MobileNetV231. They employed a mobile microscope to collect soybean seed images with an accuracy of 97.84% and an inference speed of 35 FPS, which is suitable for resource-constrained devices. Learning the features of chili seeds using the model provides a solution for germination classification.

The classification of chili seed germination has many similarities with other seeds. Existing research also employs surface morphology or structural features. Tu et al.32 employed machine vision to collect 15 features of chili seeds such as color and shape through a scanner. MLP training delivered a germination classification accuracy of 79.10%. Pornpanomchai et al.33 developed a pepper seed germination analysis (CSGA) system. The system combines a handheld microscope camera to extract color and texture features. Euclidean distance and the neural network method reach an accuracy of 71.71%. In addition, Hong et al.34 combined hyperspectral and X-ray imaging to evaluate seed germination with a CNN model. The prediction accuracy increased to 92.51% after combining two imaging information. However, the high cost and complexity of such imaging modalities limit their practical application range. In summary, while these studies validate the feasibility of using image analysis for seed germination classification, they reveal several key limitations. Firstly, many methods rely on expensive or complex equipment, hindering their adoption in low-cost, large-scale agricultural settings. Secondly, some models that achieve high accuracy are built on deep, complex architectures that demand significant computational resources, making them unsuitable for deployment on resource-constrained devices. Lastly, there is a scarcity of research focused on creating lightweight models specifically optimized for the fine-grained surface features captured by accessible macro imaging. Therefore, a gap exists for a solution that balances high accuracy with computational efficiency, utilizing easily obtainable high-resolution images.

To address this gap, this paper employs macroscopic image techniques to obtain seed surface features. Macroscopic imaging is more detailed than traditional imaging and less expensive than sophisticated imaging modalities, making it an ideal choice. Building on the exploration of lightweight paths in previous studies, we designed the GroupCeptionNet model. By controlling the convolution depth to reduce redundancy and using a multi-scale approach to maintain feature extraction capabilities, the model’s computational requirements are significantly lowered. This work thus provides an efficient and practical solution for classifying pepper seed germination.

Dataset

The dataset used in this study contains 3840 macro images of the chili pepper seed ‘SuJiaoDaQingXiang F1’, which was purchased from Shuanglong Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. in Xiao County, China, and stored in a sealed bag. Data collection uses the macro mode of an iPhone 15 Pro with a fixed height of 1.7 cm, a stabilized bracket, and an annular shadowless lamp to ensure uniformity of angle, illumination and high image quality. Each seed was placed on a black PVC matte background board to increase the contrast between the seed and the background. The original images were captured at a resolution of 3024 × 3024 pixels. Through the macro lens, the effective area occupied by a single seed is approximately 1024 × 1024 pixels. This high effective resolution ensures that even after resizing to the model input size (224 × 224), the high-frequency surface details—such as subtle wrinkles and initial seed coat rupture—are well-preserved via downsampling. In contrast, standard photography without a macro lens typically captures the seed at a much lower effective resolution (estimated around 140 × 140 pixels), leading to a loss of these microscopic features. The labels of the dataset were generated based on a standard germination experiment: the seeds were removed from the sealed bag, photographed, and then immersed in water at 30°C for 6 hours. Finally, they were moved to a thermostat at 26°C and placed on a wet paper bed for cultivation. The samples that germinated during the 14-day cultivation period were counted. The target variable for the classification task labels ‘Germinated’ seeds as 1 and ‘non-germinated’ seeds as 0.

Figure 2a shows examples of germinated and non-germinated samples obtained using macro photography. Germinated samples usually have a smooth surface and a complete form. Non-germinated samples have broken surfaces, wrinkles, or local color changes. These surface differences are more pronounced under macro photography. Figure 2b shows the effect of standard photography. The photo distance causes standard photography to capture only the approximate shape and color information of the seeds. These photos make it hard to discriminate between germinated and non-germinated samples solely on fuzzy information. Macro photography provides richer features through clear surface texture and structure. These morphological features assist the model in more precisely identifying seed states of germination.

Table 1 shows the distribution of germinated and non-germinated samples in the pepper seed dataset, distinguishing between ‘untreated’ and ‘treated.’ The dataset includes 2524 untreated seeds, of which 1997 germinated and 527 non-germinated. To balance positive and negative samples, the dataset was augmented with non-germinated samples by intervention, including both physical damage and fungal infection methods. Physical damage in the natural environment is simulated by breaking, perforating and mutilating seeds. The fungal infection method involves placing the seeds with rotten bananas in a damp petri dish. Fusarium and Aspergillus spp. are the most common causes of decay in bananas. These two fungi frequently cause mold and rot in seeds. The intervention method added 569 physically damaged samples and 747 fungal-infected non-germinated seeds for a total of 1316. The total samples in the dataset reached 3840 after the expansion, and the sample distribution was more balanced. A balanced dataset improves the stability and accuracy of the model on germination classification.

Data preprocessing

Figure 3 illustrates the five data preprocessing steps of this system. These steps effectively improve the training effect and generalization ability of the model. Firstly, the background segmentation method based on deep learning (rembg library)35 removes the background of the original image to reduce unnecessary interference. The contour detection is then utilized to locate the minimum enclosing rectangle of the seed, resulting in a square image containing only the seed body. The cropped images are uniformly resized to 224 × 224 pixels to suit the input criteria of our model and most other comparison models (299 × 299 pixels for InceptionV336. Next, a random horizontal and vertical flip with a probability of 0.5 and a random ± 10° rotation with a probability of 0.25 increase the diversity of the image. This allows the model to adapt to variations in seed orientation. Color jitters randomly adjust brightness, contrast, and saturation. The adjustment factor was set to 0.25 to represent anticipated variations in lighting conditions. Color jitter keeps the model from becoming overly sensitive to the color features of the seed surface. Pixel values are normalized using the mean and standard deviation of the ImageNet dataset37 before input to the model. A standard distribution of input values improves model training stability.

Compared with ResNet18, GroupCeptionNet has several optimizations in the convolutional layer design. GroupCeptionNet replaces the first two-layer convolutional block (ResNet block) in each stage with a ResNeXt block. The number of groups is dynamically set to based on the number of channels. This design not only reduces convolution parameters but also solves the problem of whether the convolution channel is too shallow or too deep. The fixed group scheme adopted by ResNeXt has insufficient feature expression due to overly shallow convolution channels in the initial stage. In the final stage, overly deep channels yield high parameters. The fixed depth scheme achieves an appropriate compromise between feature extraction and lightweight. The GroupCeptionNet block design effectively combines the advantages of two types of improved blocks. The GroupCeptionNet model improves the representation of complex image features while keeping the computational overhead low. Each GroupCeptionNet block is considered a stage, following the design of the ResNet18 convolutional stages and iterating through the four stages to extract and enlarge features gradually. The spatial dimensions (height and width) of the feature maps are halved, while the channel depth is doubled after each stage. The feature map starts from 56 × 56 × 64 and finally reaches 7 × 7 × 512 in the fourth stage. The incremental magnification design enables the model to smoothly capture the details and global features.

After feature extraction, the GroupCeptionNet model converts the feature map into a global feature vector via global average pooling. 2-D fully connected layer and a softmax layer combine to produce the final classification. This modular design enables the GroupCeptionNet model to fully adapt to the complexity and diversity of chili seed images.

Experimental settings

In this experiment, the dataset was split into training set, validation set and test set as 6:2:2, and the distribution is shown in Table 3. Notably, the test set was only randomly selected from the images of untreated seeds and 50.00% germinated. This data splitting ensures fairness for each model during the testing phase and more accurately measures the generalization ability in practical applications. The training parameters are set to batch size 32, epochs 40, and learning rate 1e-04. The optimal optimizer (SGD/Adam)40,41 is selected for each model through multiple experiments and combined with weight decay 5e-05 to prevent overfitting. Optimizers in training use the CrossEntropyLoss function42. The final model is saved as the model with the highest F1-score for the entire epoch. Experiments were run on an i7-13700KF CPU, 64GB RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with CUDA version 11.8. All models were implemented using PyTorch 2.0.1.

To rigorously evaluate the model’s performance specifically on detecting natural germination potential, the Test Set was constructed exclusively from ‘Untreated’ (natural) seeds. We randomly selected 384 germinated and 384 non-germinated seeds from the untreated group. Crucially, no artificially intervened samples (physical damage or fungal infection) were included in the Test Set. In contrast, the Training Set incorporated all three categories: untreated seeds, physically damaged seeds, and fungal-infected seeds. This mixed training strategy utilizes the intervened samples as ‘hard negatives’ to help the model learn robust decision boundaries. Consequently, the exclusive use of untreated seeds in the testing phase ensures that the evaluation metrics strictly reflect the model’s capability to identify subtle, naturally occurring non-germination features in real-world scenarios, rather than merely detecting obvious intervention traces.

Furthermore, the decision to use a balanced (50:50) Test Set was necessitated by the extreme class imbalance observed in the natural data (approx. 4:1). A balanced test set prevents the ‘accuracy paradox’ and forces the model to demonstrate equal competence in identifying both germinated and non-germinated seeds, providing a more rigorous assessment than the natural skewed distribution.

This study selected a variety of classic CNNs and Transformer-based models for comparative analysis to comprehensively evaluate the GroupCeptionNet model’s lightweight and classification performance. The comparison models include AlexNet43, VGG series (11/13/16/19)39, ResNet series (18/34/50/101/152)25, ResNeXt1838, MobileNet series (V2/V4_Small/V4_Medium/V4_Large)31,44, GoogLeNet29, InceptionV336, as well as VisionTransformer45 and SwinTransformerV246. The VGG series has a simple structure, but the last two fully connected layers make the parameters and memory footprint large. The ResNet series achieves a relatively low computational cost while maintaining a deeper structure by introducing residual links. The MobileNet series optimizes the convolutional structure for embedded scenarios, significantly reducing the parameters and computational complexity. The Transformer series uses self-attention mechanisms to capture long-distance feature dependencies. Compared with other comparison models, GroupCeptionNet achieves the smallest parameters, reducing resource consumption.

This study sets experiments of background removal and retention to analyze to better understand the influence of retaining backgrounds on model accuracy. Background information interferes with the extraction of chili seed features. The frosted background of the images used in this study also contains a certain amount of noise. The experiments compare the model performance under different image background preprocessing conditions. A comparison between background removal and retention experiments determines its necessity in data preprocessing.

This study complements the confusion matrix analysis and the heat map visualization experiment with Intersection over Union (IoU) to comprehensively analyze the performance of the GroupCeptionNet model on different dimensions. Confusion matrix analysis shows the classification accuracy and balance of the model among categories. This analysis provides insight into the bias and misclassification trends during the classification task. Heatmap visualization and IoU evaluation quantify the degree of overlap between the area of attention and annotation of the model. The areas with a heatmap value higher than 0.7 are considered attention areas.

In this study, accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score are key indicators for assessing model performance. Accuracy measures the overall classification accuracy of the model for all samples. Precision indicates the reliability of the model in predicting positive samples, and recall indicates the proportion of all actual positive samples that the model correctly identifies. The F1-score reflects the balance of the model in identifying positive and negative samples in a classification task. These indicators provide an essential basis for analyzing model performance.

Table 5 and Fig. 4 show that the GroupCeptionNet model achieved the highest classification performance on several key evaluation metrics, with accuracy and F1-score of 94.66% and 94.65%, respectively, demonstrating a significant advantage in the classifying chili seed germination task. It also demonstrates excellent lightweight and efficiency with 1.4 M parameters, 65.65 MB memory usage and 0.47 GFLOPs. This result shows that GroupCeptionNet not only has efficient feature extraction capabilities but is also highly applicable in resource-constrained environments.

In the VGG series, VGG13 and VGG16 achieved accuracies of 94.40% and 93.62%, respectively, proving that a simple structure is more effective for this task. The high parameter counts of 128.96 M for VGG13 and 134.27 M for VGG16 and the large memory footprint of 691.38 MB and 731.54 MB limit their application flexibility in resource-constrained environments.

The ResNet series offers a wide range of options in terms of performance and lightweight. Among them, ResNet18 and ResNeXt18, which have the advantage of being lightweight, have accuracies of 93.62% and 92.84%, respectively, and GFLOPs of 1.82 and 2.51. This result proves that residual connection convolutional layers enhance the feature learning ability of CNN models. Grouped convolutions optimize computational efficiency, making them suitable for broader applications.

The MobileNet series of models achieves better efficiency through depthwise separable convolution. MobileNetV4_Medium and MobileNetV2 have accuracies of 91.54% and 92.32%, respectively, which are slightly inferior to GroupCeptionNet. MobileNet has high application potential in resource-limited scenarios. In particular, MobileNetV4_Small has 0.18 GFLOPs, much lower than other models.

GoogLeNet and InceptionV3 rely on multi-scale feature extraction to achieve accuracies of 91.93% and 93.23%, respectively, demonstrating the effectiveness of multi-scale feature capture. Compared to GroupCeptionNet and VGG13, the more complex convolutional stage design did not significantly improve performance. AlexNet, CNN’s foundation, had a 92.58% accuracy in this task. These three models again demonstrate that relatively simple structures are more effective in this task.

The GroupCeptionNet model achieves a better balance between accuracy, lightweight and resource efficiency than other models. This result is attributed to the combination of ResNeXt block and Inception block within the stage of ResNet18. GroupCeptionNet meets the high-performance requirements of classifying chili seed germination tasks and provides a reliable technical reference for this application scenario.

In this study, the model classifies by learning the delicate features of the seed surface, and the background information obscures these fine features to some extent. After applying background removal, GroupCeptionNet’s accuracy and F1-score reached 94.66% and 94.65%, respectively, an improvement of 1.69% and 1.69% compared to when the background was retained. Similarly, classical CNN models such as VGG, ResNet, and GoogLeNet also achieved performance improvements of varying degrees after background removal. For example, VGG19’s accuracy and F1-score improved by 1.30% and 1.32%, respectively, while ResNeXt18 achieved 2.08% and 2.10% improvements.

Although all models have gained better performance due to background removal, the degree of performance improvement varies due to differences in model architecture. For example, the accuracy and F1-score of SwinTransformerV2, which uses a multi-head self-attention mechanism, improved by 5.08% and 5.12%, respectively, while AlexNet’s improvement was relatively small, at only 0.65%. These results show that background removal significantly affects models primarily using deep convolutions and self-attention mechanisms. This result shows that removing the background during data preprocessing can improve the classification accuracy of the model.

In addition to accuracy improvements, we evaluated the computational efficiency of the background removal step. On our experimental setup, the model inference for background removal takes an average of 0.0761 s per image. This rapid processing speed indicates that the background removal step introduces negligible latency, making it highly suitable for real-time deployment in automated agricultural sorting systems.

Analysis of image resolution necessity

To empirically verify the necessity of high-resolution macro images for detecting fine seed germination features, we conducted an ablation study focusing on the effective acquisition resolution.

As described in Sect. 3.1, our macro imaging setup captures an effective seed area of approximately 1024 × 1024 pixels. To simulate non-macro acquisition scenarios, we employed a “Simulation of Acquisition Resolution” strategy during the validation phase. We downsampled the cropped seed images to effective resolutions of 140 × 140 pixels (simulating standard smartphone photography) and 70 × 70 pixels (simulating low-quality or distant capture) before resizing them to the model’s input size of 224 × 224 pixels. This process mimics the physical loss of detail inherent in non-macro photography while maintaining the model architecture constant.

The results in Table 8 demonstrate a clear dependency on image resolution. When the effective resolution drops to 140 pixels (standard photography), the accuracy decreases by 2.52% to 92.14%, falling to a level comparable with conventional CNNs lacking macro-optimized features. A further reduction to 70 pixels leads to a sharp performance degradation (Acc 87.89%).

Heatmap visualization and IoU evaluation

To interpret the models’ decision-making process and localize discriminative regions, we utilized the Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) technique. Specifically, for all compared models, we selected the final convolutional layer of the last feature extraction stage as the target layer, as it preserves the most abstract semantic spatial information before the global average pooling or fully connected layers. The class activation maps were generated by computing the gradients of the target class score with respect to the feature maps, applying a ReLU activation to suppress negative contributions, and upsampling the resulting heatmap to the original input resolution for visualization.

Figure 6 shows the heatmap visualization comparison of four representative seed samples and quantitatively evaluates the model by calculating the Intersection over Union (IoU) of the attention area and annotated area. The red box indicates the annotated area, and the blue boxes indicate the attention areas of the model (threshold > 0.7).

Discussion

Experimental results show that GroupCeptionNet’s design performs well in classifying pepper seed germination. The combination of the improved ResNeXt block with the Inception block is the key to performance improvement. Grouped convolution significantly reduces the parameters and the amount of computation. By limiting the channel sense field range of each convolutional kernel, the feature independence of the model is improved, and redundancy between features is reduced. GroupCeptionNet dynamically adjusts the number of groups. The shallow stage focuses on basic feature extraction, and the deep stage extends the diversity of the feature space. This design achieves an accurate capture of pepper seed features.

The multi-scale feature aggregation of Inception blocks further enhances the model’s ability to capture complex features. Compared to the complex parameters designed by GoogLeNet for the ImageNet dataset with 1 K categories, GroupCeptionNet reduces redundancy in the two-category pepper seed germination classification task. The GroupCeptionNet design prioritizes mesoscale features as the primary target, treats large-scale features as secondary, and utilizes small-scale features for dimensionality reduction. Such a channel allocation strategy avoids unnecessary computational overhead while preserving multi-scale information.

The ablation and variant experiments together reveal the need for synergy between grouped convolutional blocks and Inception blocks. The grouped convolutional block provides diversified feature representations by reducing parameter redundancy and enhancing feature independence, while the Inception block strengthens the model’s ability to capture complex features through the aggregation of multi-scale features. The combination of the two blocks not only overcomes the performance limitations of a single block but also realizes the feature complementarity between the blocks.

GroupCeptionNet’s design demonstrates a high degree of adaptability in classifying pepper seed germination. This lightweight strategy does not come at the expense of classification performance, but by dynamically adjusting the number of subgroups and optimizing the feature extraction module, it accurately captures the key features on the surface of chili seeds while avoiding unnecessary computational redundancy. The analysis of the visualization results further validates the advantages of GroupCeptionNet in locating critical regions and classification balance. This lightweight design demonstrates GroupCeptionNet’s potential to operate efficiently in real-world scenarios like resource-constrained or embedded devices.

In summary, the GroupCeptionNet design fully demonstrates the effectiveness of grouped convolution and multi-scale feature extraction in classifying chili seed germination. Through the innovative combination of ResNeXt and Inception blocks, GroupCeptionNet not only realizes lightweight and efficient feature expression but also demonstrates deep adaptability to the task requirements. Both in terms of classification performance in experimental results and the consistency of focus areas with human-annotated regions in visualization results, GroupCeptionNet demonstrates its significant advantages as a model customized for binary classification tasks. These features make it an efficient and reliable solution for seed germination classification and provide a solid foundation for further developments in this field.

Conclusion

In this paper, a lightweight GroupCeptionNet model is proposed and successfully applied to classify a macroscopic image dataset of pepper seed germination. The model achieves an ideal balance between resource efficiency and classification performance by innovatively combining ResNeXt and Inception blocks. Experimental results show that GroupCeptionNet outperforms multiple comparative models in terms of accuracy and classification balance, while keeping the number of references and memory requirements low, demonstrating excellent adaptability and reliability. The study proves that a reasonably designed combination of modules and an efficient preprocessing process significantly improves the performance of the seed classification task. This provides an efficient and reliable solution for seed quality inspection in smart agriculture, especially in application scenarios such as resource-constrained or embedded devices.

Future research can further optimize the model structure and explore more ways of combining modules to enhance classification performance and model adaptability. Meanwhile, expanding the diversity of datasets, especially for the collection of unprocessed negative samples, will help enhance the generalization ability of the model. In addition, applying the process proposed in this paper to automated scenarios in real production and developing portable systems for embedded devices may provide broader support and application prospects for agricultural seed quality testing.
