Narrow ungual tuberosity associated with ingrown toenail: a case-control study based on foot imaging
Authors: Zhenlu Cao, Zhengdan Wang, Shengquan Ren, Chunlei Liu, Caifeng Wu, Xiaoheng Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIngrown toenails are a common condition, yet their underlying cause remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study investigates the association between the width of the distal phalanx of the hallux and the development of ingrown toenails.Methods and materialsThis case-control study included 86 patients diagnosed with ingrown toenails and 102 healthy control subjects.ResultsThe mean age of patients in the ingrown toenail group was 31.71 ± 15.55 years, compared with 34.57 ± 12.30 years in the control group. The widest distance of the tuberositas unguis (TW) and the width of the nail bed (NB) were significantly lower in the ingrown toenail group than in the control group (9.97 ± 1.47 vs. 11.49 ± 1.04, p < 0.001; and 16.01 ± 2.65 vs. 17.14 ± 1.89, p = 0.001, respectively). In comparison, the width of the lateral nail fold (NF) was significantly greater in the ingrown toenail group than in controls (27.62 ± 4.43 vs. 25.82 ± 2.13, p = 0.001). Binary multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that only TW was negatively associated with ingrown toenail occurrence (odds ratio [OR] 0.279, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.143–0.544, p < 0.05).ConclusionAbnormal narrowing of the ungual tuberosity in the distal phalanx of the hallux appears to be a risk factor for ingrown toenails. These findings provide further insight into the etiology of this condition.

Introduction

Ingrown toenail, also known as onychocryptosis, is a common and often debilitating toenail disorder. One study reported a prevalence of 2.5–5% in the general population, with both incidence and prevalence increasing [1]. It is commonly associated with significant pain, erythema, and purulent discharge. The resulting discomfort can substantially impair quality of life, making it difficult for patients to wear closed shoes, limiting walking ability, and causing cosmetic concerns [2, 3].

Various treatment options are available for ingrown toenails [4]. However, there is still no formal consensus on the optimal management approach, largely because the condition’s pathogenesis remains unclear [5]. As a result, treatment has traditionally been challenged by high recurrence rates and low patient satisfaction [6]. Several risk factors have been linked to ingrown toenails, including poorly fitting footwear, infection, trauma, improper nail trimming, and the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) or epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors [7, 8]. Previous studies have also suggested an association between structural foot abnormalities and ingrown toenails, such as hallux valgus, hallux varus, and flatfoot deformities [1, 9–11]. However, these factors do not fully explain the condition.

It is well recognized that nail morphology is strongly influenced by the structural support provided by the underlying phalanx [12]. In clinical practice, many patients with ingrown toenails appeared to have an abnormally narrow distal phalanx relative to the width of the germinal matrix area. However, this observation has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, the width of the ungual tuberosity of the great toe distal phalanx in patients with ingrown toenails was measured to evaluate the association between phalangeal width and the development of this condition.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients

After approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (QYFYWZLL 50029), a retrospective case-control study was conducted. The study included 86 patients diagnosed with ingrown toenails between January 2023 and November 2025. The inclusion criteria for the ingrown toenail group were as follows: (1) presence of clinical manifestations with a confirmed diagnosis of ingrown toenail; (2) involvement of the nail fold; and (3) availability of foot X-ray images. The exclusion criteria were: (1) tumors of the distal phalanx of the hallux; (2) previous hallux trauma; (3) foot X-rays unsuitable for measurement; (4) skeletal alignment abnormalities, including hallux valgus, hallux varus, or flatfoot; and (5) age younger than 18 years at the time of consultation, as incomplete development of the bones and nail unit in minors could affect the results.

A total of 102 individuals without ingrown toenails who were treated during the same period were randomly selected as the control group. Foot X-ray images for the control group were obtained from patients presenting with blunt foot trauma who were admitted to the Department of Orthopedics or the Emergency Department of our hospital. The inclusion criteria for the control group were: (1) no current or previous history of ingrown toenail; and (2) availability of dorsoplantar foot X-ray images. The exclusion criteria were identical to those used for the ingrown toenail group: (1) tumors of the distal phalanx of the hallux; (2) previous hallux trauma; (3) unavailable or unsuitable foot X-rays; (4) skeletal alignment abnormalities, including hallux valgus, hallux varus, or flatfoot; and (5) age younger than 18 years at the time of consultation.

The study protocol included collecting data on age, sex, and affected side. Basic demographic and clinical information was obtained for all patients and controls. The demographic characteristics and clinical data of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

Imaging acquisition and assessment

All subjects underwent standing dorsoplantar foot X-ray examinations. A standardized radiological protocol was applied to all participants using a computerized imaging system capable of magnifying images up to 500%, allowing more precise measurements. X-ray assessments were performed by visually comparing the affected and unaffected feet. Measurements were obtained from anteroposterior foot radiographs and included the basal width (BW) of the distal phalanx of the great toe, the width at the tuberositas unguis (TW), the width of the nail bed (NB) at the same level, and the width of the lateral nail fold (NF) at the same level (Table 2; Figs. 1 and 2). All parameters were measured independently by two junior doctors following standardized training. The mean of their measurements was used as the final value.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test was used to assess data normality. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies. For categorical variables (sex and affected side), comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For continuous variables (age, BW, TW, NB, NF, BW/TW, NB/TW, and NF/TW), the independent-samples t-test was used. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare multiple groups. Binary multivariate logistic regression analysis of demographic and imaging variables was conducted using a backward stepwise regression method. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The foot X-ray measurements showed that, compared with the control group, participants in the ingrown toenail group had lower TW and NB values, higher NF values, and slightly higher BW values; however, the difference in BW was not statistically significant. The mean TW was significantly lower in the ingrown toenail group than in the control group (9.97 ± 1.47 vs. 11.49 ± 1.04, p < 0.001). Similarly, the mean NB was significantly lower in the ingrown toenail group compared with controls (16.01 ± 2.65 vs. 17.14 ± 1.89, p = 0.001). In comparison, the mean NF was significantly higher in the ingrown toenail group than in the control group (27.62 ± 4.43 vs. 25.82 ± 2.13, p = 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between Ingrown toenails and the morphology of the ungual tuberosity of the distal phalanx of the hallux, and found that there was an association between the width of the ungual tuberosity and the occurrence of Ingrown toenails. Although the relationship between abnormal morphology of foot bones and Ingrown toenails has been discussed previously, the ungual tuberosity may affect the occurrence of Ingrown toenails was formally proposed for the first time. The occurrence of Ingrown toenails may be caused by a relatively narrow ungual tuberosity. Just as when the distal phalanx is damaged after trauma, the loss of bone support may lead to Hook-nail deformity [13].

Our findings showed that the ungual tuberosity of the distal phalanx of the hallux was significantly narrower in the ingrown toenail group than in the control group. In comparison, no significant difference was observed in the basal width of the distal phalanx between groups. This suggests that the occurrence of onychocryptosis is more closely related to the distal ungual tuberosity than to the overall phalangeal width. The BW/TW ratio further supports this interpretation. Multivariate logistic regression showed that a narrower TW was independently associated with ingrown toenails. These findings indicate that reduced width of the distal phalangeal ungual tuberosity may represent an important anatomical risk factor for onychocryptosis.

The width of the distal phalangeal ungual tuberosity with clinical severity was also investigated. The results showed no significant relationship between TW and disease stage. However, NF tended to increase with more advanced stages. This may reflect progressive inflammation, edema, and soft tissue hypertrophy of the lateral nail fold in more severe disease [14]. Further studies are needed to clarify this observation better.

Hand surgeons have long recognized that normal nail shape depends on close structural contact with the distal phalanx [14, 15]. Anatomical changes in the toe may therefore directly or indirectly contribute to pathological nail changes, and many nail disorders are associated with abnormalities of the foot or toes [16]. In 2012, Peter J. Mahaffey first proposed that a narrow distal phalanx might be a cause of ingrown toenail [12]. However, this hypothesis has received limited attention, and few studies have attempted to verify it. A similar pattern was observed in our clinical practice: when a relatively wide nail grows over a narrow bony base, the unsupported lateral nail edge may curve or collapse beyond the phalanx margin. With additional pressure from footwear, this may traumatize the adjacent nail fold, leading to ulceration and inflammation. In this context, nail spicules or improper trimming may be secondary rather than primary causes.

A wide range of treatments for ingrown toenails is currently available, including conservative approaches such as cotton packing, gutter splinting, elastic taping, CO₂ laser therapy, and external correction devices [17–20], as well as surgical procedures such as wedge resection, phenolization, terminal Symes amputation, and wire techniques [3, 21, 22]. Based on our findings, modification of the underlying bony anatomy may represent a new therapeutic concept. First, patients with significant distal phalangeal narrowing may require earlier active intervention, as conservative treatment alone may be less effective. In such cases, phalangeal stenosis can be a useful indicator when considering surgery. Second, procedures aimed at increasing the width of the distal phalangeal tuberosity may offer a novel surgical strategy. This concept requires higher-level evidence, and developing precise methods to modify phalangeal width safely will be an important direction for future research.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was modest, and patient selection could be further improved. For example, ideally, the control subjects would be healthy individuals with standardized imaging, and patients with forefoot deformities potentially related to ingrown toenails were excluded. Further cases will be collected in future studies to strengthen the evidence. Second, weight-bearing X-ray imaging has limitations for evaluating soft tissues. A more accurate assessment of NB and NF width would require higher-resolution modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Third, although the anatomical association was confirmed, the proposed biomechanical mechanism remains hypothetical. Moreover, it does not fully explain how abnormal narrowing of the distal phalangeal ungual tuberosity leads to ingrown toenail formation.

Conclusion

In most cases of ingrown toenails, the width of the distal phalangeal ungual tuberosity is abnormally reduced, and this narrowed morphology appears to be associated with a greater risk of developing the condition. These findings provide further insight into the etiology of ingrown toenails.
