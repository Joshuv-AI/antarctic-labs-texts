Validation of the Ottawa Ankle Rule in Blunt Ankle and Midfoot Injuries in a Trauma Care Centre in Nigeria
Authors: Eric Anyanwu, Christian Madubueze, Abdurazaq Alada, Habila Umaru, Byat Dzong, Abdulmajid Sharif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:Ankle and midfoot injuries are common presentations in every emergency department. The Ottawa Ankle Rule was developed to reduce the need for unnecessary radiography. This study aimed to validate this rule in a population.Materials and Methods:This study recruited 110 patients in a single-trauma care centre presenting with closed ankle and midfoot injuries. All patients were examined using the Ottawa Ankle Rule by orthopaedic surgeons and findings were recorded before radiographs were obtained. The radiographs were interpreted by a consultant radiologist blinded by the clinical examination findings. This was the standard against which the Ottawa Ankle Rule was tested.Results:The sensitivity of the Ottawa Ankle Rule protocol in ankle and midfoot injuries was 100% and 95%, respectively. The specificity was 37.2% for ankle injuries and 54.8% for midfoot injuries. The negative predictive value of the rule was 100% and 95.1% for ankle and midfoot injuries, respectively. Application of this rule would have led to a 31% reduction in radiography amongst patients in this study.Conclusion:The Ottawa Ankle Rule is a valid decision-making tool for patients with closed ankle and midfoot injuries. It has a high sensitivity for detecting fractures with moderate specificity. Application of the rule can result in a significant reduction of; treatment costs, waiting times at the emergency department, and unnecessary radiation exposure to patients.

Introduction

Ankle injuries are the most common presentations in emergency units. They account for 10% of all emergency visits and constitute 34% of lower limb injuries.[1] Of these injuries, 85% are ankle sprains, and the remaining 15% are ankle fractures.[2] Most of these patients undergo radiography with no positive findings of a fracture in radiographs. The Ottawa Ankle Rule was developed by Stiell et al.[3] to reduce the need for unwanted radiography.

The clinical implication of this means that unnecessary radiation could be prevented in patients without significantly missing out on fractures. Despite awareness of this rule, physicians have been reluctant to implement this in clinical settings.[4] Notable reasons include fear of missing fractures and medico-legal concerns.

The Ottawa Ankle Rule has been validated in several countries.[5,6,7] Sensitivities have ranged from 93% to 100%.[4] No study to the best knowledge of the authors has been conducted in West Africa. Moreover, clinical decision rules have been shown to perform differently outside of where they were developed. We sought to validate this rule to improve the confidence of physicians in our setting to implement the rule as well as reduce the overall radiation exposure to the patient and the cost of treatment.

Materials and Methods

This was a prospective cross-sectional study carried out at the emergency and trauma unit of a hospital. All patients who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. The study was conducted between October 2019 and January 2021 (14 months). All patients aged from 18 to 65 years of age with blunt ankle and midfoot injury of any mechanism within 10 days of the incident were recruited. The exclusion criteria included: age <18 or >65 years, patients presenting >10 days from injury, patients with referred radiographs, open ankle and midfoot injuries, patients with altered mental status, and multiple traumatic injuries. The sample size was calculated using the minimum sample size calculator and a finite correction factor for adjusted sample size.[8,9]

The research tool was a predesigned proforma. Ethical consideration was obtained from the ethics committee of the hospital before the commencement of the study. Informed consent was obtained from eligible patients before enrolment. Recruitment and examination of the patients followed the same criteria set out by the original inventors [Figure 1].[3] A detailed history including the mechanism of injury, and the time between injury and clinical assessment were recorded. Patients were subsequently examined by senior orthopaedic registrars in the department using the Ottawa Ankle Rule for blunt ankle and midfoot injuries. They were trained on the use and application of the rule before the commencement of the study. All patients went for anteroposterior + lateral radiographs of either ankle or foot. All images of patients in the study were reviewed and reported by a consultant radiologist blinded to the patient’s history and examination findings. Each patient had their proforma filled as “YES” or “NO” after radiologist assessment based on the presence or absence of a fracture, respectively.

A significant fracture on a radiograph would be a fracture fragment >3 mm in dimension. Radiograph with fragments <3 mm was considered as no fracture. This was the initial criteria used by Stiell et al.[3,10,11] in developing the Ottawa Ankle Rule. The outcome measures for the study were to determine the sensitivity, specificity, negative, and positive predictive values of the Ottawa Ankle Rule as well as the likelihood ratio. We also determined the percentage reduction in radiography if the rule was applied to the study population. Statistical analysis was done using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 2020 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and a 2 × 2 contingency table was used to present the results of the rule against the radiological assessment with a 95% confidence interval.

Results

A total of 110 patients were recruited for the study. Of these, 68 patients presented with blunt ankle injuries, whereas 42 patients had blunt midfoot injuries. The mean age of patients was 37.4 ± 11.0 years and the median age of 36.5 years. Overall ratio of male-to-female was 1:1 [Table 1]. Motor vehicular injury was the mechanism of injury in 51 patients (46.4%) [Figure 2].

Mechanism of injury amongst study participants

The majority of the patients (50.9%) spent between 1 and 8 h before presentation with a mean presentation time of 28 h [Figure 3]. No patient in the study presented >7 days after injury. The mean time duration spent between assessment and radiography by patients was 1.5 h with a range of 0.42–6.0 h. About 62 patients (56.4%) spent 1 h between assessment and radiography, whereas 23 patients (20.9%) spent 2 h. About 25 patients (22.7%) sustained ankle fractures, whereas 11 patients (10%) had midfoot fractures.

A total of 25 patients (100%) with ankle fractures after radiological assessment were positive for the rule. The false positive rate was 62.8% (27 patients), whereas 16 patients (37.2%) were true negatives after radiologic assessment. This showed a sensitivity of 100%, specificity of 37.2%, positive predictive value of 48.1%, and negative predictive value of 100%. The positive likelihood ratio and negative likelihood ratio for the rule in this study were 1.6 and 0, respectively [Table 2].

Percentage reduction in radiography

A total of 16 patients with ankle injuries were Ottawa negative after clinical examination and none had a fracture after radiological assessment. There would be a 24% reduction in the need for radiographs (16 out of 68 patients) amongst ankle-injured patients in this study if it was implemented. About 18 patients with midfoot injuries were Ottawa negative after examination and only one patient had a fracture after radiographic assessment. There would have been a 43% reduction in foot radiography while missing one fracture if the rule had been implemented in this study. The combined overall reduction in radiography in this study would be 31% had the rule been implemented [Figure 4].

Discussion

Clinical decision rules are designed to make health systems more effective. These decision rules are known to perform less effectively when applied outside of where they were developed. The Ottawa Ankle Rule was calibrated to a high sensitivity to detect fractures following ankle and midfoot injuries without exposure of all patients to radiation.[10]

The fracture rate has been variable in studies that validated the Ottawa Ankle Rule. Murwanashyaka et al.[12] reported a fracture rate as high as 73%, whereas Broomhead and Stuart[13] had a fracture rate of 15.5%. This study identified 36 patients in total with fractures out of 110 patients, giving a fracture rate of 32.7%. This study was conducted in a busy trauma care centre that receives referral from neighbouring cities. Motor vehicular injury being the predominant cause of injuries in this study may have accounted for this rate. We also found that isolated lateral malleolus fracture was the most common type of fracture seen in patients with ankle fractures (44%), whereas fracture of the fifth metatarsal was the frequent pattern in midfoot fractures (82%).

The sensitivity of the rule for ankle and midfoot fractures in this study was 100% and 91%, respectively. This was in tandem with a study done in East Africa by Tharao et al.[14] with a sensitivity of 97.9%. The reduced sensitivity for midfoot injuries was due to one fracture missed during the study. This was a navicular fracture diagnosed by the managing team and corroborated by the radiologist blinded to the examination findings. A review of the clinical details of the patient showed a delayed presentation to the emergency of 7 days after the injury. Most patients presented within 3 days of injury except for this index patient. The negative findings on examination could be explained by the absence of acute inflammatory signs around the foot due to the delay. This underscores the importance of timely application of the rule. We advise careful application of the rule in those presenting late to the emergency to follow such patients up closely. The missed fracture rate in this study was 2.8% (1 fracture out of 36 fractures). This compares to the fracture rates from other validation studies.[13,15,16]

The specificity of the rule has a wide range of 10%–72%.[17] We found a specificity of 37.2% and 54.8% for ankle and midfoot injuries, respectively. We know that patients from different cultural backgrounds and races respond to pain differently as well as patients with a low pain threshold who may respond vividly during clinical examination.[18] The different palpation techniques by physicians can also be a contributing factor. These could be some explanations for the wide variation of the specificity of the rule in the literature. Other researchers have shown a significant increase in specificity by combining the Ottawa Ankle Rule with the Visual Analogue Scale score,[19] the use of a tuning fork,[20] and ultrasound.[21] However, this study found a negative predictive value for ankle and midfoot injuries to be 100% and 95%, respectively. This shows that there is a very high probability of the patient not having a fracture after a negative examination finding. This can reliably exclude patients from exposure to unnecessary radiation.

We estimated the percentage reduction in radiography if we had applied the Ottawa Ankle Rule in this study. This showed a 31% reduction in radiographic requests if the rules were implemented. This finding corroborates the general findings in similar studies with estimates between 13% and 40%.[7,16,18,22] This would have translated to a total number of 34 patients not requiring radiography. This would have led to a significant reduction in the cost of overall health care. Anis et al.[23] showed that between US$614,226 and US$3,145,910 per 100,000 patients was saved when the Ottawa Ankle Rule was implemented in a group compared with a routine practice where all patients underwent radiography. In a resource-limited setting like ours, and developing countries, applying this decision rule can lead to huge cost savings especially when most patients still pay out of their pockets with limited insurance coverage for health care.

This study had some limitations. This was a single-centre study in a tertiary hospital. This may not represent the different ethnic and geographic populations of the country. The sample size was relatively small due to the short duration of the study. We recommend conducting a multi-centre study in different geographic and population groups with a large sample size to further strengthen the validation of the rule. We recommend that an implementation study to assess the real impact of the application of the rule be conducted.

Conclusion

The findings from this study demonstrate that the Ottawa Ankle Rule is a valid clinical decision-making tool in patients with blunt ankle and midfoot injuries, especially in a resource-constrained setting. It has a very high sensitivity and moderate specificity. It can reliably exclude fractures hence reducing unnecessary exposure of all such patients to radiation. The implementation of the rule in this study would have led to a 31% relative reduction in radiographic requests leading to a reduced cost of treatment and waiting times at the emergency.
