Kraak porcelain decorative pattern restoration using generative AI: a pilot study
Authors: Ren Yujie, Lu Haotian, Liu Chi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to explore the generation and restoration of Kraak porcelain decorative patterns, employing a stable diffusion model that has been augmented with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and ControlNet. Kraak porcelain, a historic blue-and-white ceramic traded along the maritime Silk Road, features intricate radial “Kaiguang” patterns that blend Chinese and Islamic artistic styles. The rules governing pattern generation have not been systematically deciphered through traditional methods, and as a result, innovation has remained constrained. The study established a multi-dimensional cultural database of 1530 Kraak porcelain images to facilitate training of a stable diffusion model. The model was fine-tuned using LoRA to adapt to small-sample data and integrated with ControlNet to impose structural constraints via edge maps. Experiments demonstrated that combining LoRA and ControlNet improved structural control over Kaiguang patterns and secondary motifs, validated by metrics like SSIM and PSNR. However, despite the improvement in pattern consistency, difficulties persisted in reproducing fine details and achieving seamless restoration of damaged patterns. Future work will focus on refining incremental learning strategies and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration, with the goal of bridging the gaps between technical implementation and cultural interpretation. Overall, this approach provides a framework for the digital preservation and innovative regeneration of cultural heritage.Keywords: Kraak porcelain, Stable diffusion, LoRA, ControlNet, Decorative pattern generationSubject terms: Scientific data, Computational science

Introduction

Kraak porcelain, a type of blue-and-white porcelain, served as a significant material medium traded to Europe via the maritime Silk Road from the late of the sixteenth century to the early seventeenth century, and was highly regarded both domestically and internationally. Kraak porcelain is predominantly cyan and white, most commonly in the form of plates, and exhibits decorative techniques inspired by an Islamic artistic style. Patterns featuring 6–12 groups of “Kaiguang” are popular. The vessel is divided into multiple decorative zones by the continuous Kaiguang form, and the ornamentation within the radial Kaiguang is organized systematically1. Cultural heritage represents cultural achievements with historical, artistic and scientific value that have been gradually formed through human social practice and are continuously inherited and preserved2. Kraak porcelain not only carries the aesthetic essence of Chinese blue and white porcelain technology, but also reflects the reconstruction logic of artistic symbols in cross-cultural trade, possessing high cultural heritage value and artistic anthropology research value. Although existing researches have largely addressed historical provenance and stylistic classification of Kraak porcelain, they have left significant gaps in systematically understanding the underlying rules governing pattern generation. Traditional iconographic research methods are limited by the constraints of artificial feature extraction, making it difficult to quantify the combination paradigms and cultural metaphors of pattern elements, resulting in the innovative design of patterns often remaining at the level of style imitation. Consequently, the innovative design of patterns lacks generative exploration based on structure.

In recent years, deep learning has revolutionized the field of image generation, leading to substantial improvements in the quality and diversity of generated images. Ho et al.’s DDPM theory enhanced training stability3.The CLIP theory proposed by Radford et al. facilitates the transformation from text to image by calculating the feature cosine similarity between text and image4. Rombach et al.5 proposed the Stable Diffusion model, which combined DDPM with the CLIP model, and achieved significant progress in the field of visual art generation. Currently, generative AI exhibits remarkable characteristics such as efficient content generation, strong scalability, and diversified interaction, offering a new pathway for the digital regeneration of traditional patterns6. These studies provide a theoretical basis for research on pattern creation and design using generative AI techniques, particularly stable diffusion. Due to its advantages—high fidelity, high-resolution outputs, and support for diverse input modalities—Stable Diffusion is widely adopted in image generation research.

Recently, mature deep learning methodologies have been applied across many fields such as natural language generation, computer vision-based image generation, 3D generation, audio and video generation. Moreover, deep learning technology has increasingly been employed in the domain of pattern generation7. For example, Li et al. conducted a comparative study on the generation results of ethnic patterns by Stable Diffusion and other generative artificial intelligence technologies8. Ran Erfei et al.9,10 used CycleGAN and Stable Diffusion techniques to imitate and generate the style characteristics of blue calico patterns respectively. Wang Weiji et al.11 discussed the digital generation of ancient Shu brocade patterns based on an improvement to the GAN network. However, significant challenges remain in AI-based pattern generation research. First, while numerous studies have examined cultural feature extraction, the majority of pattern generation research has focused on textile patterns, leaving patterns on artifacts, particularly Kraak porcelain, largely unexplored due to the scarcity of available samples. Second, many studies have not systematically curated training datasets, and comprehensive cross-modal databases for cultural heritage remain undeveloped.

To advance digital heritage research on Kraak porcelain, we conducted a pilot study focused on the generative AI-based generation and restoration of decorative patterns. Firstly, we constructed a multi-dimensional database capturing the cultural characteristics of Kraak porcelain patterns, comprising 1530 images of Kraak porcelain plates collected from museums and exhibitions worldwide and their corresponding textual annotation files. The majority of the images are original photographs, while a small portion is sourced from official museum websites or related online resources. Leveraging this unique dataset, we investigated the potential of the Stable Diffusion model for Kraak porcelain pattern generation and restoration. Specifically, we introduced the low-rank adaptation method and ControlNet network to enhance the domain applicability and controllability of the standard Stable Diffusion model. The fine-tuned SD model demonstrates the ability to reproduce and restore the cultural characteristics of Kraak porcelain plate decorative patterns, and explores methods for generating patterns that exhibit both cultural continuity and innovation. This study not only provides a technical paradigm for the digital preservation of traditional crafts, but also aims to promote the living heritage and contemporary transformation of cultural heritage by utilizing AI-driven generative logic to inform theoretical interpretations of the evolution rules of Kraak porcelain patterns.

Methods

The Kraak porcelain decotative pattern dataset

We constructed the first publicly available Kraak porcelain plates dataset, named Kraak Porcelain Decorative Pattern, available at https://zenodo.org/records/15588830. The Kraak Porcelain Decorative Pattern dataset includes 1530 samples, each consisting of an image file and a corresponding text annotation file, making it suitable for training generative AI models. The data primarily belonged to the Kraak Porcelain Collection of the City University of Macau, supplemented by some public museum data. Initial data collection

The file “Kraak main database.rar” contained 1530 image-text pairs in total. Among them, 1075 pairs are the Kraak porcelain plates sampled from the collection of the City University of Macau. This portion of the database was classified based on the artifact’s age, production provenance, and pattern type. These images were taken from actual artifacts, and each sample was cataloged at the City University of Macau, with its age, dimensions and basic pattern features annotated. Additionally, the dataset included 455 images collected from the official websites of various museums and institutions, were organized into 12 categories, including the Macao Museum, the Palace Museum, the Netherlands Rijksmuseum, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, the Shanghai Museum, the Zhangzhou Museum, National Gallery of Victoria, the Vietnam National Museum of History, and auction houses, etc.

The stable diffusion model

Stable diffusion is a text-to-image generation model based on latent diffusion models (LDM) developed by CompVis, stability AI and LAION. The core technology originated from work by Patrick Esser from Runway and Robin Rombach from the machine vision and learning group at LMU Munich5. This class of model learns the underlying probability distribution of the data through a process of denoising the data, gradually generating the target image from an initially fully noisy point. The core innovation of this model lies in migrating the diffusion process from pixel space to a low-dimensional latent space, significantly reducing computational complexity and improving generation efficiency4. Functionally, Stable Diffusion can generate high-resolution images aligned with textual semantics using text prompts and supports multi-modal conditional input13. Stable Diffusion can also be applied to image inpainting, outpainting and style transfer, with the degree of generative variability regulated by adjusting the guidance scale or noise intensity.

LoRA and ControlNet

Given the high-dimensional parameter space and complexity of large Stable Diffusion models, the traditional fine-tuning paradigm applied to small databases is prone to excessive parameter adaptation, leading to a significant decrease in model generalization ability. The study by Edward Hu, Yelong Shen and colleagues proposed a low-rank adaptation (LoRA) method, performing low-dimensional internal adjustments to the model14. Through low-rank decomposition of the parameter matrices, this optimization method effectively constrains the dimensionality of the solution space for parameter updates. By updating the latent structure of the parameters, this mechanism not only significantly reduces the model’s dependence on large quantities of labeled data, but also offers a more suitable optimization strategy for small-sample feature databases, such as those of Kraak porcelain decorative patterns, by controlling the magnitude of trainable parameters.

ControlNet is a neural network architecture built upon diffusion models and seeks to improve the controllability of generative models by incorporating supplementary conditional inputs, including edge maps, depth maps, and semantic segmentation maps15. This architecture connects the original model to the copy module through zero-initialized convolutional layers (zero convolution), which ensures that conditional inputs during initial training do not affect the generation ability of the pre-trained model, enabling gradual realization of condition control during the fine-tuning process. In practice, ControlNet is mainly used to improve the accuracy and diversity of image generation tasks. It enabled the generation of high-quality images that are closely aligned with conditional structures, using inputs such as edge detection maps16, human pose key points17, and depth maps. Typical applications include converting line art to full-color images, and generating style-specific scenes based on semantic segmentation maps. Furthermore, its application extends to image inpainting, style transfer, 3D modeling and other domains, such as generating inpainting results based on damaged images and corresponding inpainting masks.

For this research, we trained a LoRA model based on the collected database, and integrated a ControlNet component to improve the controllability of the image generation process. The resulting generation and restoration performance were respectively evaluated through quantitative metrics and ablative experiments.

Experimental results and analysis

Experimental platform

The experimental platform was equipped with a processor with 16 vCPU Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8352 V CPU @ 2.10 GHz, an RTX 4090 graphics card with 24 GB of video memory. The implementation relied on the PyTorch deep learning framework, version 2.3.1, and Cuda 12.1.

Data pre-processing

For image-based model training, Kraak porcelain plate patterns were classified into three structural groups: (1) The main pattern, located at the core of the plate’s visual center, typically features a distinct theme and a natural and vivid composition. Compared with other patterns, it possesses a more complete and independent structure; (2) The secondary patterns, positioned around the periphery of the main pattern, serve as pattern transition elements and auxiliary themes between the main pattern located at the center of the plate and the additional patterns situated around the rim; (3) Additional patterns, primarily located on the rim of the porcelain plate, function as decorative elements and provide contrast, often featuring Kaiguang decoration. Together with the main and secondary patterns, these additional patterns form the complete decorative structure of Kraak porcelain plates, as shown in Fig. 2.

Kraak porcelain pattern structural division. The diagram illustrates the fundamental structure of Kraak porcelain. The red area delineates the scope of the primary pattern, with its position and characteristics annotated by red lines. Correspondingly, the orange, cyan, and green areas demarcate the range and denote the features of the secondary patterns, Kaiguang, and complementary patterns, respectively.

The input images were preprocessed by standardizing the size into 512 × 512. For LoRA training, the optimizer was set to Adam W18, with a training batch size of 1 for 10 training epochs. The learning rates for the U-Net and the text encoder were trained with learning rates of 0.001 and 0.0001, respectively, while a learning rate scheduler dynamically adjusted the rates according to a cosine schedule.

During the experiment, the model also processed the text data from a file sharing the same name as the corresponding image. The final trained model learned features representing the visual content in the image minus the features described by the text tag. Therefore, it is necessary to annotate any elements in the accompanying text that do not pertain to the distinctive features of Kraak porcelain, in order to prevent the style-specific characteristics from being mistaken for unrelated features. Method for evaluating algorithm performance

The evaluation generation performance comprises subjective assessment and objective metrics. Subjective assessment relies on the intuitive judgment obtained by direct visual observation, while objective evaluation involves modeling the brightness, structural features, texture and other properties of the the generated image to compute corresponding evaluation scores to draw conclusions. The quantitative metrics selected in this study are: (1) The Frechet Inception Distance (FID), quantifying the difference between the distribution of the generated image samples and that of the real image samples to assess the quality and diversity of the generated images; (2) the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), used to measure structural similarity between two pictures based on their brightness, contrast and structure; (3) The Feature similarity index measure (FSIM), which extracts feature similarity to evaluate the visual quality similarity between two images; (4) The Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS), which measures the perceptual similarity between two images; (5) The Peak signal-to-noise Ratio (PSNR), used to evaluate image compression, transmission, and the performance of reconstruction algorithms. The Peak Signal to Noise Ratio serves as an indicator of image similarity, with higher values reflecting lower quality loss. In this study, generation performance was evaluated using a twofold approach: subjective assessments, based on visual inspection of the generated images, and objective metrics, obtained by comparing the generated results with the corresponding actual porcelain plate patterns.

Formal training and control optimization

From the 1530 image-text pairs in the database, 307 image-text pairs exhibiting clear and complete images along with appropriate textual annotations (TAGs) were selected for training. The positive prompt words used were “white_background, front view, porcelain plate, blue and white porcelain, blue white porcelain, <LoRA: Kraak porcelain v1.7:1>, center flower and bird pattern, 8 Kaiguang”. The negative prompt words used were: “NSFW, logo, text, bad quality, lowres, low quality, blurry, worstquality”. Images were generated using a random seed with the number of inference steps set to 30.

As the number of samples increased and the annotations became more refined, many problems encountered in initial training were significantly improved, and the model’s ability to capture the distinctive characteristics of Kraak porcelain decorative patterns improved accordingly. Specifically, the generated images more closely matched the color and texture of real porcelain plates. Additionally, the issue of a central hole appearing in pre-training outputs was effectively resolved, allowing the model to reliably generate the central region with Kraak porcelain decoration themes. The overall structure also became clearer, enabling stable generation of important structural elements of Kraak porcelain such as Kaiguang. Moreover, the model exhibited a better understanding of image details and produced results with improved clarity.

Although incorporating the LoRA model for fine-tuning facilitated the generation of Kraak-style decorative patterns, substantial errors remained when rendering more complex details. The most significant issue was the difficulty in maintaining the characteristic overall structure and layout of Kraak-style porcelain patterns. When simply using “<LoRA: Kraak porcelain v1.7:1>” included in the positive prompt for generation, the output generated after applying LoRA fine-tuning was shown in Fig. 4. The generated image demonstrates a partial capture of the Kraak porcelain decorative style, preserving fundamental characteristics such as circular form, symmetry, presence of Kaiguang, and a main pattern. However, the number of Kaiguang divisions varied inconsistently, with outputs depicting eight Kaiguang, six Kaiguang, and ten Kaiguang occurring simultaneously within the same prompt. Controlling the shape and size of Kaiguang remained challenging. Although a relatively uniform Kaiguang structure could be generated, as illustrated in the right image of Fig. 4, instances of uneven, overly elongated, or excessively wide Kaiguang also occurred, as shown in the left image, highlighting poor performance in this aspect. Furthermore, the secondary patterns surrounding the main pattern were inconsistently rendered, with only a subset of generated images accurately depicting them. Other problems included detail inaccuracies, such as incorrect shadow generation for parts of the porcelain plate inconsistent with realistic lighting, or the erroneous generation of a support base under parts of the porcelain plate. The latter issue was attributed to potential contamination from numerous porcelain plate image data depicting stands in the database. Therefore, additional measures were considered necessary to achieve finer control over the details of the generated images.

Generation results using only LoRA fine-tuning. The figure presents three Kraak porcelain images generated exclusively by the LoRA fine-tuned model.

To address these issues, a ControlNet component was introduced to provide additional structural conditions for image generation. This study focused on the most representative eight Kaiguang styles of Kraak porcelain. Building on the classification and extraction of typical Kraak patterns by Ren et al.19, structural diagrams of the additional and secondary patterns were integrated to develop a comprehensive structural outline encompassing both Kaiguang and secondary motif information. This outline was then input into the lineart module of ControlNet to impose structural constraints. The positive prompt remained “<LoRA: Kraak porcelain v1.7:1>” to facilitate comparison with the results obtained without using the control network. Using outline diagram A as the control condition, the model successfully generated Kraak porcelain decorative patterns exhibiting the correct structure as was shown in Fig. 5.

Generation results after imposing structural control using outline diagram A. The figure demonstrates that the four images on the right are generated based on the two key parameters displayed on the left: the extracted structural diagram of the porcelain plate (top left) and the critical prompt employed (bottom left).

As was shown in Fig. 6, an exemplar comparison was made among the generation results using LoRA only and LoRA with ControlNet. On the one hand, the additional patterns display a specific ratio range between the inner and outer diameters. Deviations from this range, whether excessive or insufficient, produce patterns that deviate from the characteristic Kraak porcelain style. Images generated using LoRA alone are less constrained and therefore more prone to structural inconsistencies. In contrast, incorporating ControlNet enforces strict adherence to this ratio through the structural framework, ensuring that the generated images conform to the structural standards of Kraak porcelain. On the other hand, the Kaiguang structure in authentic images is clearly defined with distinct closed edges. When When generation relies solely on LoRA, symmetric and visually similar Kaiguang-like structures can appear within the additional patterns; however, these structures tend to be relatively open and lack well-defined closed boundaries, resulting in inferior structural generation compared to the results obtained with ControlNet.

Comparison of characteristics of different image generation methods. This image shows the comparison of image features generated using LoRA alone and LoRA plus ControlNet. While LoRA alone often produces structurally unrealistic and open-boundary open-light patterns, the integration of ControlNet enforces strict geometric constraints, resulting in closed-edge structures closely consistent with the characteristics of traditional Kraak porcelain.

The introduction of the control network guiding image edges clearly enhanced the model’s understanding of Kraak porcelain decorative patterns, enabling the generation of relatively complex examples. Specifically, the size and structure of the Kaiguang were effectively regulated. Although the generation quality of the secondary patterns remained suboptimal, their intended structure was accurately maintained. This verifies the feasibility of using LoRA-fine-tuned Stable Diffusion models to generate structurally consistent Kraak porcelain decorative patterns.

Ablation study and pattern restoration

Ablation study

To further evaluate the quality of the generation method proposed, five Kraak porcelain plate patterns were generated using identical textual prompts, alongside five patterns produced by the baseline GAN model. A comparative analysis was then performed between the results of the proposed method—with and without ControlNet—and those generated by the baseline FastGAN20 model. A Random Seed (Seed = − 1) was used for pattern generation, the number of inference steps was set to 20, the CFG scale was set to 7, the image size was set to 512*512, the Clip skip was set to 2. The positive prompt was: “white_background, front view, porcelain plate, blue and white porcelain, blue white porcelain, <LoRA: Kraak v1.7:1>, center flower and bird pattern, eight Kaiguang,” and the negative prompt was uniformly set to “NSFW, logo, text, bad proportions, lowres, low quality, blurry, worstquality”. The pattern generation results were presented in Fig. 7. The first row corresponded to the authentic Kraak porcelain plate patterns, the second row displayed patterns generated using FastGAN, the third row showed patterns produced using LoRA only, and the fourth row illustrated patterns generated by integrating LoRA method with ControlNet. Results indicated that, even when “eight Kaiguang” was used as a training label in the LoRA model to stabilize the number of Kaiguang, relying solely on LoRA for generation failed to control their size and uniformity, leading to inconsistent dimensions or instances of excessive elongation or width. This limitation was effectively addressed with the introduction of ControlNet, which enforced structural constraints. In the improved images, the distribution of color blocks better aligned with the characteristic layout of eight-Kaiguang Kraak porcelain plates, and the overall style more closely resembled authentic patterns.

As shown in the table, the FID score of the results generated with ControlNet was 196.42, higher than the value of 172.35 observed without ControlNet, yet lower than the GAN baseline model’s score of 210.48. This indicated that generation performance in terms of FID fell between the baseline model and the approach without ControlNet. Nevertheless, ControlNet outperformed the method without ControlNet on other evaluation metrics. For instance, the PSNR value for the ControlNet group was measured at 9.4632 dB, higher than both the GAN group’s 8.1760 dB and the LoRA group’s 7.9328 dB, suggesting that the images generated with ControlNet contained a lower level of noise and thus exhibited closer proximity to the authentic porcelain plate patterns. Regarding SSIM, FSIM, and LPIPS scores, the ControlNet group surpassed both the GAN and LoRA groups. This indicated that the combined LoRA and ControlNet approach generated images with enhanced structural fidelity and perceptual similarity, markedly outperforming both Stable Diffusion with LoRA alone and the traditional GAN-based method.

The degradation in FID score was directly associated with the conditional constraints introduced by ControlNet. Unlike metrics such as SSIM, FSIM, and PSNR, FID primarily measures the similarity in the overall feature distribution between two sets of images, placing greater emphasis on generation diversity and naturalness. Incorporating ControlNet constrained the generated image set to follow the selected structural framework. However, because not all images in the authentic porcelain plate dataset strictly adhered to this extracted framework, this approach markedly improved the fidelity of the generated results to the edge map conditioning while simultaneously reducing output diversity and naturalness. This decline could be attributed to conditional overfitting, causing the generated images to exhibited relatively rigid overall visual characteristics. Consequently, the statistical distribution of the generated set diverges further from that of the natural image set, resulting in a deterioration of the FID metric.

In conclusion, ControlNet achieved high fidelity to conditioning and accurate reconstruction maintaining a degree of generative diversity albeit at the cost of moderate overall naturalness. While this approach might introduce certain uncertainty when generating highly diverse and creative images, it substantially improved generation quality in scenarios that demanded precise replication of a specific structure, such as using ControlNet to control the structure during Kraak porcelain plate pattern generation.

The restoration results of the pattern

The LoRA model and generation method developed in this study were applied to restore patterns on incomplete porcelain plates. Firstly, incomplete Kraak porcelain plates requiring restoration were selected as as experimental targets. Since the selected plates did not fully conform to the structural outline predefined by ControlNet, restoration was carried out using the trained LoRA model both with and without enabling the ControlNet structural control component.

The target image was input into the img2img functionality, and the area requiring restoration was manually masked. The prompt “white_background, front view, porcelain plate, blue and white porcelain, blue white porcelain, <LoRA: Kraak porcelain v1.7:1>,” served as the positive prompt, while “NSFW, logo, text, bad proportions, lowres, low quality, blurry, worstquality,” was used as the negative prompt. The number of denoising steps was set to 30, the mask blur was set to 0, the inpainting strength was set to 0.95, and the guidance scale (CFG) was set to 9 for generating the restored image. However, because this approach depended entirely on the model’s imagination, significant issues remained in rendering detailed features, even though the overall circular shape of the repaired image was preserved. For instance, the model’s limited understanding of the specific style and pattern of the original image resulted in substantial discrepancies between the restored pattern and that of the original artifact. When the ControlNet was activated to enforce structural control, the quality of pattern restoration improved, allowing stable generation of the Kaiguang pattern structure. However, owing to a mismatch between the applied pattern structure template and the original pattern structure of the target porcelain plate. The newly generated patterns struggled to visually integrate with the remaining original patterns. This was exhibited through discrepancies in structural attributes such as the direction and curvature of the lines, and in some cases, even noticeable misalignment. To address this issue, the restoration approach proposed in this study could be applied. Specifically, when the intended structural framework does not match the actual artifact, structural outlines should first be extracted and then completed. The resulting completed diagram can subsequently guide the model to perform more refined restoration.

Discussion

Several limitations of this study are noted. First, the training of generative AI models is constrained by dataset size. In practice, cultural artifacts such as export porcelain are rare, and fragments recovered from shipwrecks or excavations are scarce and often severely damaged, making data collection challenging. As future work, continued acquisition of such artifacts will help improve model performance. Second, the restoration model relies on regular structures for supervision, limiting its applicability to heavily damaged porcelain plates and aggravating the data scarcity problem. To address this, future research will explore semi-supervised or self-supervised restoration approaches. Despite these data challenges, we focued on Kraak porcelain, a relatively rare category of porcelain with substantive practical significance. Firstly, its discovery in Macao signifies the region’s role as a crucial hub for ancient ceramic trade during the Ming and Qing dynasties. The patterns of Kraak porcelain bear witness to the cultural integration between China and the West, reflecting Sino-West Asian cultural exchanges, thereby contributing to cultural heritage preservation. Secondly, the symmetric and regular structure of Kraak porcelain patterns makes them more suitable for AI-based training and restoration compared to irregular motifs. Furthermore, as a pilot study on Kraak porcelain, our approach can be extended to other ceramics featuring symmetrical or regular decorative structures, such as Japanese Arita ware “Fuyoude” (ふようで), Dutch Delftware, or Chinese Zhangzhou ware “Swatow Ware”. Future work will involve more detailed pattern categorization and explore the restoration of three-dimensional decorative motifs.

Effective Technological innovation requires deep integration with the needs of cultural heritage protection. This study proposes an innovative methodology for digital heritage conservation, addressing the challenge of efficiently restoring artifacts with regular patterns in archaeology. By enabling rapid updates with small amounts of newly unearthed porcelain data, the approach minimizes the need for repeated full retraining. Consequently, it enhances both the efficiency and consistency of virtual restorations for damaged areas of ceramics and other cultural relics. Furthermore, future research should focus on advanced learning frameworks such as incremental learning, semi- and self-supervised learning. In parallel, we should promote interdisciplinary collaboration, combining the pattern deconstruction expertise of art historians with the technical optimization capabilities of AI engineers, to advance the integrated development of digital archaeology and structured heritage preservation. This approach is crucial for bridging the current gap between “technology-driven” and “cultural interpretation” methodologies, ultimately supporting the digital preservation and living heritage of cultural assets.

Conclusion

In this study, a database of Kraak porcelain plates was constructed, and a model was trained based on this database. We proposed a method for fine-tuning the generation of Kraak porcelain decorative patterns within the Stable Diffusion model framework. This method employs the Low-rank adaptation method (LoRA) to learn the decorative pattern features of Kraak porcelain plates, combines this with a pre-trained Stable Diffusion model to leverage its rich semantic representations for guiding image content, and incorporates ControlNet to enforce structural constraints on the edges of the generated image elements. By inputting appropriate prompt words, this method achieves the desired output of Kraak porcelain decorative patterns. The final fine-tuned model capably generates authentic Kraak porcelain decorative patterns, and the pattern structure of the plate can be modified by supplying a different structure outline to the ControlNet component. While the LoRA model combined with ControlNet structural control technology has yielded notable achievements in generating and restoring Kraak porcelain patterns, the current model’s ability to capture the semantic meaning embedded in cultural symbols still relies heavily on manual annotation and prompt engineering. Moreover, the continuity and seamlessness of the restoration results require further improvements.
