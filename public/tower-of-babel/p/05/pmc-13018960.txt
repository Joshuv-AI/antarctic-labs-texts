A platelet-rich plasma-loaded chitosan-based hydrogel for promoting type 2 diabetes wound healing
Authors: Na Wang, Huirui Zhu, Dongkun Li, Xuena Wang, Ziyan Zhang, Bao Chai, Meining Li, Tao Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHard-healing or non-healing diabetic wounds feature complex pathogenesis, posing significant challenges to clinical treatment. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is rich in various growth factors and possesses significant therapeutic potential, while its clinical application is largely limited by the rapid release of these bioactive factors. In this study, a multifunctional hydrogel dressing (GACP@PRP) based on PRP was fabricated for the promotion of type 2 diabetes wound healing via the physical cross-linking of graphene oxide-silver nanoparticles (GO-Ag NPs), chitosan (CS), and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM). The wound dressing possesses injectability, self-healing, favorable porosity, water absorption, moisture retention, adhesiveness, antibacterial, and hemostatic functionalities. Moreover, the GACP@PRP hydrogel protects PRP to sustain growth factors release, promote cell proliferation, and migration, which significantly accelerated wound healing rate in SD rats full-thickness skin defect model. Together with its great biocompatibility and biosafety, GACP@PRP meets the multiple functional requirements of diabetic wound dressing and is promising for clinical diabetic wounds management.Subject areas: biological sciences, engineering, materials science

Introduction

As reported by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) in 2024, the global prevalence of diabetes has reached 589 million, with a projected increase to 853 million by 2050.1 Diabetes severely impairs skin wound healing by interfering the inflammatory response, reducing granulation tissue formation and the secretion of growth factors, and inducing peripheral neuropathy and inhibiting of angiogenesis etc.2,3,4,5 Hard-healing or non-healing wounds, as a diabetic associated chronic complications, impose a substantial burden on societies and families.6,7,8 It is estimated that with 1 in 3 to 1 in 5 patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) will develop chronic non-healing wounds in their lifetime, such as a diabetic foot ulcer (DFU), with limited treatment regimens.9,10,11 Consequently, the effective management and successful treatment of diabetic wounds have emerged as a critical challenge.

Diabetic wounds are typical chronic wounds characterized by a prolonged repair process and suboptimal healing outcomes. When dermal injury occurs, pathogenic microorganisms (e.g., bacteria) can infiltrate the body through the lesion, potentially triggering localized or systemic infections, which not only would delay the healing process and exacerbate patient discomfort but also elevate the risk of severe complications, including osteomyelitis and sepsis.12,13,14,15 Wound dressings are crucial therapeutic tools for promoting wound healing.16,17 However, although some of the currently used wound dressing materials provide fundamental physical protection, typically suffer from several multiple limitations, such as inadequate antimicrobial activity, weak mechanical performance, poor absorption of wound exudates, and failure to maintain a moist wound environment etc.18 During the wound repair process, diabetic wounds remain highly susceptible to bacterial colonization and invasion, which can severely impair the efficiency and quality of tissue regeneration. Consequently, the development of innovative multifunctional wound dressings has emerged as an effective way to facilitate the healing of wounds.

Hydrogels are biomaterials composed of three–dimensional cross–linked polymer networks. Owing to their tunable physicochemical properties and excellent biocompatibility, these materials exhibit enormous potential in disease therapy, with particularly relevance to wound healing19,20 and drug delivery systems.21,22 As promising candidates for medical wound dressings, hydrogels possess inherent advantages including high water content, soft and flexible structures, porous architectures, and favorable biocompatibility.23,24,25 To date, hydrogels have been successfully applied in the treatment of skin defects,26 infected wounds,27,28 burn wounds,29 diabetic feet ulcers (DFUs),30,31 and internal moist wounds.32 Furthermore, hydrogels share numerous physicochemical similarities with the natural extracellular matrix (ECM), and can be easily loaded with drugs or cytokines that specifically target the core challenges of diabetic wound healing, such as blood sugar control agents, antibacterial agents, and angiogenic factors, which provide a more ideal therapeutic strategy for the management of diabetic wounds.33,34

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous serum enriched with high concentrations of platelets and growth factors. Its regenerative capacity is generally attributed to the supraphysiological concentrations of growth factors—such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)—released from activated platelets.35,36,37 Numerous studies have confirmed that PRP-derived growth factors act synergistically to promote tissue regeneration and accelerate wound healing.38,39 PRP has been successfully applied in multiple medical fields, such as orthopedics,40,41,42 dermatology,43 oral-maxillofacial surgery,44,45 urology,46 otolaryngology,47 and gynecology,48 among others. Specifically, in the treatment of chronic wounds, PRP has shown promising experimental and clinical outcomes.35 Therefore, integrating PRP with hydrogels to construct a stable and controlled-release delivery system has emerged as a key research focus in this field. Clinical studies have reported significant benefits of adjuvant PRP therapy for diabetic wounds, such as Kontonpoudis et al.49 conducted a retrospective study on the use of PRP on diabetic chronic wounds for 72 patients with concomitant peripheral disease and found that 72.2% of patients achieved a rate of healing higher than 90%. Despite its promising potential in treating refractory diabetic wounds, PRP’s therapeutic efficacy is limited by several inherent drawbacks: the rapid release of growth factors, short duration of activity, and the instability of its liquid form, which causes it to easily disperse.50 Therefore, combining PRP with hydrogels to create a stable and controlled release system has become an important research focus.

To further enhance the releasing efficiency of growth factors, herein, we prepared a PRP-loaded multifunctional hydrogel (GO-Ag NPs-CS-PNIPAM@PRP, GACP@PRP) including injectable, self-healing, adhesive, antibacterial, and hemostatic abilities via the physically cross-linking PNIPAM, CS, and GO-Ag NPs (Figure 1). In vitro experiments revealed that the GACP@PRP hydrogel exhibited favorable adhesion, injectability, photothermal performance, bacteriostatic activity, and hemostatic capacity. Furthermore, a comprehensive biosafety evaluation of the synthesized GACP@PRP hydrogel was conducted, encompassing hemocompatibility, cytocompatibility, and histocompatibility. All data confirmed that GACP@PRP hydrogel possesses excellent comprehensive properties. More importantly, in vivo experiment results demonstrated that application of the GACP@PRP hydrogel significantly promoted wound repair in a murine model of type 2 Diabetes. Taken together, the GACP@PRP with self-healing and good mechanical property offer significant promise as dressing materials for wounds of type 2 diabetes.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of GACP@PRP hydrogel

The multifunctional GACP@PRP hydrogel was synthesized via a one-pot method, which exhibited a translucent black appearance (Figure S1). The key components of GACP@PRP hydrogel were characterized by UV-vis spectroscopy. The spectrum of the as-synthesized GO-Ag NPs nanohybrid showed characteristic peaks at approximately 290 nm and 400 nm, corresponding to GO and Ag NPs, respectively, confirming the effective deposition of Ag NPs on the GO sheets (Figure 2A). In addition to the characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to GO-Ag NPs (290 nm and 405 nm), the spectrum of the GACP hydrogel retained the absorption peaks of PNIPAM at approximately 290 nm, which indicated that PNIPAM was successfully loaded into the hydrogel (Figure 2B). Additionally, the release of growth factors was evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Figures 2C–2F). It is evident that the GACP@PRP hydrogel successfully achieved a sustained release of growth factors, in stark contrast to the burst release observed from free PRP. While free PRP released the majority of TGF-β1 (384.63 ± 12.33 pg/mL), VEGF (11.54 ± 2.12 pg/mL), PDGF (732.65 ± 48.71 pg/mL), and EGF (27.93 ± 5.77 pg/mL) within 2 h, the GACP@PRP hydrogel released only a fraction of these amounts at the same time point and continued the release for over 48 h. In summary, GACP hydrogel was successfully synthesized and functionalized by loading PRP to create the GACP@PRP hydrogel.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the microstructure of the GACP@PRP hydrogel (Figures 2G and 2H), suitable for cell permeation and gas exchange. The observed increase in crosslinking density is attributed not only to BIS but also to the contributions of GO and CS. This more compact structure resulted in a significantly lower porosity (48.476 ± 1.27%) compared to the PNIPAM control (∗∗p < 0.01), which subsequently improved the hydrogel’s water uptake capability.

The prepared GACP@PRP hydrogel was characterized by fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Figure 2I). The infrared spectroscopy data revealed that the GO-Ag NPs showed a hydroxyl stretching vibration peak of GO at 3444 cm−1 and a C-O-C stretching vibration peak at 1091 cm−1. The absence of a C=O stretching vibration peak at 1728 cm−1 suggests changes in the oxygen-containing functional groups on the GO surface during the synthesis of GO-Ag NPs, confirming the successful loading of Ag NPs onto the GO sheets. Additionally, the peak at 3442 cm−1 is attributed to O-H and N-H stretching vibrations, the peak at 1390 cm−1 corresponds to C-H bending vibrations of CS (C-N stretching vibration), and the peak near 1166 cm−1 is assigned to asymmetric stretching of the ether bond (C-O-C) and C-N stretching of amide III. Compared with PNIPAM, the broadening of the absorption peak at 3436 cm−1 in the GACP@PRP hydrogel spectrum results from the overlapping contributions of O-H and N-H stretching vibrations from PNIPAM, GO-Ag NPs, and CS. These observations indicate successful copolymerization among PNIPAM, GO-Ag NPs, and CS, demonstrating the successful preparation of the GACP@PRP hydrogel.

The surface potentials (zeta potentials) of PNIPAM, GO-Ag NPs, CS, and GACP hydrogels dispersed in double-distilled water were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90. As shown in Figure 2J, the zeta potential of the GO-Ag NPs stabilized at approximately −23.73 mV, which suggested that functional groups such as –COOH and –OH on GO effectively interacted with the silver nanoparticles, likely forming stable Ag–COO- and Ag–OH- complexes following the reduction process, thereby facilitating the stable anchoring of Ag NPs on the GO surface. PNIPAM exhibited a zeta potential of −17.69 mV, likely due to the dissociation of its carboxyl groups (-COOH). Conversely, the quaternary chitosan derivative had a positive zeta potential of +27.22 mV, a charge that derives from the quaternization of the chitosan’s inherent -OH and -NH2 functional groups. The combination of negatively charged PNIPAMs (−17.69 mV), GO-Ag NPs (−23.73 mV), and positively charged chitosan (+27.218 mV) led to GACP composite hydrogel maintained a negative zeta potential of −13.24 mV, albeit with a reduced absolute value. Notably, the incorporation of PRP (GACP@PRP) did not induce a significant shift in zeta potential, which attributed to the weak alkalinity (pH = 7.35–7.45) of the serum-derived PRP. The slightly negative surface charge of GACP@PRP hydrogel (−14.65 mV) may influence its biological interactions in wound healing contexts.

The elemental composition of the GACP@PRP hydrogel was determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As illustrated in Figure 2K, the wide-scan spectra of the GACP@PRP hydrogel exhibited absorption peaks at 284.48, 399.18, 531.08, and 367.48 eV, which correspond to the binding energies of C 1s, N 1s, O 1s, and Ag 3d, respectively. These results confirm the presence of C、N、O and Ag in the hydrogel. Figure 2L further presented the fine spectrum of Ag 3d, in which the Ag 3d5/2 and Ag 3d3/2 peaks were located at 367.55 eV and 373.66 eV, respectively, corresponding to the spin-orbit splitting of metallic silver.

Mechanical properties of GACP@PRP hydrogel

As a wound dressing, the GACP@PRP hydrogel exhibits excellent tissue adhesiveness, allowing it to firmly adhere to various substrates (such as plastics and metals) and porcine skin both above and below its critical phase transition temperature. Notably, the hydrogel adheres firmly to porcine skin, maintaining structure integrity without detachment even when the skin is subjected to bending, flattening, twisting, or stretching (Figures 3A and 3B).

The injectability of the GACP hydrogel facilitates rapid and adaptable coverage of irregularly shaped wounds. Upon dissolution at low temperatures, the solution can be injected via syringe to achieve conformal coverage of the wound bed (Figure 3C). Additionally, its suitability for injection is directly evidenced by a pronounced shear-thinning response across a shear rate range of 0.01–100 s−1, as shown in Figure 3D.

Mechanical performance is a crucial characteristic for the hydrogel. Our results reveal that the energy storage modulus (G′) of GACP@PRP hydrogel was greater than the loss modulus (G″) over the frequency range of 1–55.6 Hz in the fixed strain frequency test, demonstrating its significant elastic capacity and robust gel strength (Figure 3E). At a frequency of 55.6 Hz (where G′ ≈ G″), the hydrogel reached a critical transition point, indicating exists in a solid-liquid intermediate state. A further increase in frequency resulted in G″ surpassing G′, suggesting the breakdown of the hydrogel’s internal structure and its transition from a gel to a sol state. The strain scanning test results are shown in Figure 3F. Within the linear viscoelastic region, both G′ and G″ remained constant with increasing strain, indicating structural stability. The crossover point of G′ and G″ at a strain of 360% marks the critical strain threshold for structural integrity. Beyond this critical point, a pronounced decrease in G′ was observed, which is associated with a considerable disruption of the hydrogel’s microstructure. The rheological properties of the GACP@PRP hydrogel were further investigated at 37°C (Figure S2). Frequency sweep measurements revealed that both G′ and G″ remained relatively constant across the tested frequency range, exhibiting plateau-like behavior, and were significantly higher than those observed at room temperature. Additionally, strain sweep tests indicated a notable narrowing of the linear viscoelastic region. These changes can be attributed to the phase transition occurring above lower critical solution temperature (LCST), which transforms the hydrogel from a swollen, soft elastomer into a collapsed, dense solid-like state.

The self-recovery ability of the GACP@PRP hydrogel was subsequently performed with the use of successive step-strain tests (Figure S3). After the first high strain (400%), the G′ of the recovered hydrogels decreased significantly from 107 to 18 Pa, and G′′ > G′, indicating the collapse of the hydrogel network. When at low strain (1%), the G′ returned to 103 Pa, suggesting most of the cross-linked network of the hydrogel had been recovered. After six high and low strain cycles tested, the healed hydrogels showed similar G′ and G′′ values as the first cycle, confirming the great self-healing properties of the GACP@PRP hydrogel.

Hydrogel swelling, degradation, and photothermal properties

Swelling capacity is a crucial function of wound dressings. As shown in Figure 4A, both hydrogels exhibited the most rapid liquid absorption within the first 90 min. After 24 h, the equilibrium swelling ratios of the PNIPAM and GACP hydrogel reached 716.5% and 1149.3%, respectively. The higher swelling rate of the GACP hydrogel suggests a highly cross-linked network with smaller pores, which slows down water diffusion into the matrix and suggested its exhibited good liquid absorption capacity.

The biodegradability of biomedical materials is a critical consideration. The degradation behavior of the hydrogels was assessed in a lysozyme solution in this research. Our results indicated that both materials are biodegradable (Figure 4B). The PNIPAM hydrogel, characterized by a looser network, showed a degradation rate of 15.87 ± 1.19% on day 8. Notably, the GACP@PRP hydrogel, possessing a higher CS content, degraded more rapidly, reaching a rate of 29.61 ± 1.46% at the same time point.

The photothermal properties of the GACP@PRP hydrogel were evaluated using an infrared thermal imager under near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation (2.0 W/cm2). The thermal images captured at different time points under NIR laser irradiation are shown in Figure 4C, which suggest that the GACP@PRP hydrogel exhibited a rapid and continuous temperature increase upon irradiation, reaching 51.77°C within 5 min (Figure 4D). The GACP@PRP hydrogel displays exceptional photothermal stability, as evidenced by the largely consistent temperature profiles across four successive heating-cooling cycles, with the maximum temperature remaining around 52.46°C (Figure 4E). These findings confirm the superior photothermal conversion capability and stability of the GACP@PRP hydrogel, underscoring its strong potential for photothermal therapy (PTT)-based antibacterial applications.

Analysis of GACP@PRP hydrogel for promoting cell proliferation, antibacterial, and hemostatic properties

Cell migration is critical to wound healing. Hydrogels have the potential to facilitate cell migration by imitating the physical characteristics of the extracellular matrix. This study evaluated the effect of GACP hydrogel on cell migration by mouse NCTC clone 929 L (L929) cells scratch assay (Figure 5A). Notably, the GACP@PRP and the (GACP@PRP + NIR) groups demonstrated the most pronounced enhancement in wound closure, with the remaining wound area being only 50.28% and 48.82% after 24 h, respectively, which was significantly smaller than that of all other groups (NC group 91.36%, PRP group 76.25%, GACP group 72.94%, ∗p < 0.05) (Figure 5B). These findings demonstrated that the employment of GACP@PRP hydrogel as a wound dressing material significantly enhances fibroblast migration, thereby accelerating the wound healing process.

The proliferation of L929 cells was assessed using fluorescence staining (Figure 5C). The results showed that all groups exhibited strong green fluorescence at 0 h, indicating high initial cell viability. After 24 h of culture, the GACP@PRP and (GACP@PRP+NIR) groups demonstrated markedly more intense green fluorescence than the NC, PRP, and GACP groups, suggesting superior pro-proliferative activity. Moreover, cell counting results further quantified this effect (Figure 5D). While the proliferation rates of the PRP (123.66%) and GACP (129.72%) groups were comparable, the GACP@PRP group showed a significant increase to 148.46 ± 3.64% (∗∗∗p < 0.001). The most pronounced enhancement was achieved with the (GACP@PRP+NIR) treatment, which yielded the highest proliferation rate of 151.53 ± 2.91%, confirming that near-infrared irradiation synergistically enhances the pro-proliferative effect of the composite hydrogel.

A key innovation in functional hydrogel research is the achievement of rapid hemostasis. The whole blood clotting time (WBCT) serves as a direct metric for evaluating a material’s hemostatic efficacy. Our results (Figure 5E) demonstrated a significant reduction in WBCT for the experimental groups compared to the negative control (NC) group (244 ± 23 s), such as the GACP hydrogel group, the PRP group, and the GACP@PRP hydrogel achieved WBCTs of 149 ± 18 s, 127 ± 18 s, and 83 ± 9 s, respectively. Notably, the GACP@PRP composite hydrogel exhibited the shortest clotting time, underscoring its superior hemostatic performance.

Furthermore, the hemostatic efficacy of the GACP@PRP hydrogel was evaluated using a liver injury hemorrhage model in ICR mice. The clotting process and blood loss were visually recorded and quantitatively measured, as shown in Figures 5F and 5G, respectively. The NC group exhibited severe and continuous bleeding over 120 s, with a total blood loss of 511.65 ± 31.78 mg. Treatment with PRP moderately alleviated bleeding, reducing the blood loss to 327.92 ± 36.62 mg. In contrast, the GACP@PRP hydrogel demonstrated superior hemostatic performance, achieving a significantly lower blood loss of 194.00 ± 27.47 mg, which was statistically different from the NC group (∗p < 0.05). These in vivo findings are consistent with the results obtained from the in vitro WBCT assay.

The photothermal antibacterial efficacy of the GACP@PRP hydrogel was evaluated using the plate colony counting method. As shown in Figures 6A and 6B, both the NC and PRP groups exhibited a high number of bacterial colonies of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) under both treatment conditions. Without NIR irradiation, the bacterial survival rates for the GACP and GACP@PRP hydrogel were 72.63% and 69.57%, respectively. However, upon exposure to an 808 nm laser, the survival rates decreased dramatically to 2.36% and 1.13%. A similar trend was observed against Escherichia coli (E. coli) (Figures 6C and 6D). The NC and PRP groups showed high bacterial survival with or without laser treatment, indicating no inherent antibacterial effect. In contrast, the GACP@PRP hydrogel demonstrated a significant light-dependent antibacterial activity, with survival rates of 47.83% without irradiation and 4.29% with 808 nm laser treatment. The results against E. coli were consistent with those against S. aureus (ns: not significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). Furthermore, to assess the disruption of bacterial membrane integrity, we measured the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). As shown in Figure S4, the LDH release from S. aureus treated with GACP@PRP under NIR irradiation was significantly higher (78.57%) than all other control groups, confirming that the combined treatment induces severe and irreparable membrane damage, which is a direct contributor to bacterial cell death.

Furthermore, a comprehensive biocompatibility evaluation of the GACP@PRP hydrogel was conducted, encompassing histocompatibility, cytocompatibility, and hemocompatibility. As shown in Figure 7A, compared to the NC group, all treatment groups exhibited no significant pathological damage to visceral tissues, as evidenced by mild inflammatory responses and normal cellular morphology and tissue architecture, indicating the GACP@PRP hydrogel exhibits excellent histocompatibility.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay results (Figures 7B and 7C) demonstrated a significant enhancement in cell viability. Compared to the NC group, the PRP and GACP hydrogel groups showed cell viabilities of 122.93% and 124.73%, respectively, while the GACP@PRP hydrogel group exhibited a markedly higher cell viability of 150.21% (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001),which indicates that the GACP@PRP hydrogel is not only non-cytotoxic but also promotes cell proliferation, which is consistent with the aforementioned cell proliferation experiments. Furthermore, the cell viability of the (GACP@PRP+NIR) group slightly increased to 152.26%, indicating that near-infrared irradiation did not exhibit significant toxicity to normal L929 cells and demonstrated good safety.

Additionally, hemocompatibility assessment (Figures 7D and 7E) revealed severe hemolysis in the positive control group (H2O). In contrast, the hemolysis rates for the GACP hydrogel, GACP@PRP hydrogel, and (GACP@PRP+NIR) groups were only 0.24%, 0.43%, and 0.28%, respectively (∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001), all of which fall within the acceptable range for excellent hemocompatibility. These results collectively confirm that the GACP@PRP hydrogel possesses favorable blood compatibility.

In vivo wound-healing assessment

Type 2 diabetes was successfully induced in the SD rats by injecting streptozotocin (STZ) (Figure S5). To investigate the therapeutic potential of GACP@PRP hydrogel in a diabetic wound model, a refractory skin wound model was established in above diabetic rats. Representative images of the wound area at 0, 6, 9, and 14 days postsurgery are shown in Figures 8A and S6. The results revealed that on day 6, the wound healing rate in the (GACP@PRP+NIR) group significantly increased to 33.46 ± 1.48% compared to the NC group and the (NC + NIR) group. By day 14, the wounds in the (GACP@PRP + NIR) group exhibited near-complete healing, achieving rates exceeding 99.9% (∗∗p < 0.001) (Figures 8B and 8C).

Histopathological analysis of the regenerated skin at the wounds in ICR mice on day 14 post-treatment was performed using H&E and Masson staining. Compared with the other groups, the (GACP@PRP+NIR) treated group exhibited a significant reduction in inflammatory cells, with wounds exhibiting a trend toward more organized and dense collagen deposition compared to the control groups, indicating a potentially favorable outcome for subsequent tissue maturation and reduced scar formation (Figure 8D). These results indicate that the GACP@PRP hydrogel effectively promotes wound healing and tissue regeneration in a murine model of skin infection and injury.

Discussion

To summarize, a PRP-loaded multifunctional hydrogel wound dressing (GACP@PRP) was designed in this study, which accelerated diabetes wound healing. Most current materials rely on a single antibacterial mechanism or a single therapeutic effect, while partially promoting healing, they often lack the integrated functions of antibacterial properties, antioxidant capabilities, controlled drug release and inflammation regulation, making them insufficient for complex wound environments. For example, silver ion chitosan hydrogels rely solely on Ag+ for sterilization, showing poor tissue repair promotion, wound closure rate of 90.35% on day 1451; The kaolin/OMCC-NHS/PAA hydrogel exhibits excellent efficacy only in hemostasis for emergency bleeding.52,53 Compared with existing hydrogel dressings, the GACP@PRP hydrogel uses PNIPAM as the matrix and integrates CS, graphene oxide-silver nanoparticles (GO-Ag NPs), and PRP through physical crosslinking. It achieves the synergistic integration of hemostasis, antibacterial activity, photothermal therapy, and controlled drug release, significantly accelerating the wound healing rate in the full-thickness skin defect model of SD rats.

Notably, the GACP@PRP hydrogel possesses a net negative zeta potential. This characteristic might initially give rise to concerns regarding its biological performance. However, the hydrogel’s inherent compositional and structural attributes serve to effectively mitigate any such potential limitations. In terms of hemostasis, although negatively charged surfaces are generally considered less favorable for platelet adhesion due to electrostatic repulsion, the presence of CS, a cationic polymer, and the physical microstructure of the hydrogel likely facilitate platelet entrapment and activation via alternative mechanisms, such as von Willebrand factor-mediated binding.54 Regarding antibacterial activity, the negative surface potential may contribute to repelling similarly charged bacterial membranes (e.g., S. aureus and E. coli possess negatively charged surfaces), thereby reducing initial adhesion and enhancing the efficacy of photothermal and Ag+-mediated killing.55 For mammalian cell adhesion, while moderate negative charges can sometimes hinder cell attachment, the incorporation of PRP provides abundant adhesion motifs (e.g., fibronectin and vitronectin) that override charge effects and promote integrin-mediated L929 cells adhesion and spreading, as observed in our cell experiments.56 Thus, the net negative zeta potential of GACP@PRP does not preclude its biological functionality; rather, it is counterbalanced by compositional and structural features that collectively support hemostasis, antibacterial action, and cell proliferation.

Furthermore, the potent NIR-dependent antibacterial activity of GACP@PRP is primarily attributed to the synergistic photothermal effect of GO-Ag NPs and the release of Ag+ ions. It is well-established in the literature that both localized hyperthermia from PTT and Ag+ ions can induce substantial ROS production, which in turn peroxidizes lipids in the bacterial membrane, compromises its integrity, and ultimately leads to cell lysis, a process consistent with the observed high LDH release.57,58

Limitations of the study

While this study demonstrates that GACP@PRP hydrogel significantly accelerates diabetic wound closure and promotes tissue regeneration within a 14-day observation period, it primarily captures the early and active phases of healing. A notable limitation of the current work is the lack of long-term (e.g., 4–8 weeks) evaluation of scar quality, skin barrier function restoration, and tissue remodeling stability-key metrics for assessing the ultimate success of diabetic wound therapy. In subsequent studies, quantitative evaluation of collagen architecture via histomorphometry, measurement of transepidermal water loss for barrier function, and assessment of the biomechanical properties of the regenerated skin will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term therapeutic benefits and clinical potential of the GACP@PRP hydrogel dressing.

Additionally, despite the advantages of PRP-based hydrogels, this study has limitations. While the PRP we used offer unique advantages in wound healing, its clinical application is limited by insufficient source availability and uncertainty regarding whether blood extracted from patients can be prepared into qualified PRP. This study places greater emphasis on regulating the inflammatory response for the healing of diabetic wounds, while the exact mechanism of hydrogel dressing interaction with macrophages and angiogenesis has not been fully elucidated. Furthermore, identifying the optimal balance between controlling infection and promoting healing is crucial for improving diabetic wound treatment, further studies are required to investigate the underlying molecular pathways. Future research should focus on understanding the specific components and mechanisms involved in wound healing to enhance the clinical translation of these findings.

Taken together, the development of GACP@PRP shows substantial potential for advancing the treatment of diabetic wounds, as well as providing ideas for the development of subsequent medical materials.

Experimental model and study participant details

Diabetic mice model

Eight-week-old SD rats were selected for this study, which were provided by the Animal Center of Shanxi Medical University. All animal procedures were performed in strict compliance with the relevant regulations and guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Protection and Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University. In the experiment, STZ (Aladdin, Cat. No. 18883-66-4) was prepared with sodium citrate buffer at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. Each rats received intraperitoneal injections for five consecutive days (from day 1 to day 5) at a dose of 55 mg/kg. The tail vein blood was collected from each rat to measure blood glucose levels on day 14. A blood glucose concentration exceed 16.7 mmol/L indicated the successful establishment of a type 2 diabetes model in SD rats.

We performed an in vivo investigation to assess wound contraction and histological alterations in SD rats aged 8 weeks. Following the induction of type 2 diabetes in these mice using a protocol involving repeated low doses of STZ, we created two circular full-thickness skin wounds (1 cm in diameter) on the back of each mouse using surgical scissors. Subsequently, the wounds were treated with different dressing hydrogels, including the Negative Control (NC) group (no material intervention), Positive Control group (topical PRP), GACP, GACP@PRP, and (GACP@PRP + NIR). Wound healing progress was asessed by capturing photographs on days 0,6, 9, and14. Histological samples were collected on day 13.

Cell culture

L929 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium high-glucose medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher, US) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The cell line was authenticated by STR profiling and confirmed to be negative for contamination with Mycoplasma spp. by PCR.

Microbe strains

E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 35556)were used in this study. For the cultivation of these two bacterial strains, LB solid and liquid media were prepared separately, and the bacteria were cultured via the spread plate method. Bacteria species were incubated in an incubator at 37°C.

Method details

Material

Quaternary ammonium chitosan (degree of substitution≥95%), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM), trisodium citrate, and ammonium persulfate (APS). N, N'- Methylenebisacrylamide (BIS), graphene oxide (GO), silver nitrate, sodium borohydride, and N', N'-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Graphene oxide-silver nanoparticles (GO-Ag NPs) were prepared under laboratory conditions. Calcium/PI cell activity and cytotoxicity assay kit, along with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology. All other reagents were of analytical grade or higher.

Isolation of PRP

PRP was isolated from cardiac puncture of rats with two-stage centrifugation method. Blood was taken from 10 SD rats, under general anesthesia with a 2-4 ml cardiac puncture. Briefly, whole blood samples were collected in 5 mL Centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was separated and transferred to another tube. In the next step, it was centrifuged for 20 min at 4000 rpm. One-thirds of the supernatant, PPP, was discarded, and the remaining Two-third of the plasma, along with the platelets, was separated as PRP. The resulting PRP was stored at -20°C for subsequent use.

Preparation of GACP@PRP hydrogel

Preparation of GO-Ag NPs

GO-Ag NPs were prepared by in situ reduction method. GO was first dispersed in deionized water at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and sonicated for 3 h. Subsequently, the silver nitrate solution (4 mg/mL) was added into the GO dispersion. After the mixed solution was stirred at 151°C for 30 min, sodium citrate solution (3.5mg/mL) was added to the mixture. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 1.5 h until the solution turned brownish-yellow. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min with deionized water used for washing during centrifugation, followed by freeze-drying to obtain the GO-Ag NPs.

Preparation of PNIPAM hydrogel

The synthesis of PNIPAM hydrogel involved the combination of NIPAM (1g), BIS (2 mg), and APS (30 mg) in deionized water (26 mL). TEMED was added as a catalyst to the solution. The hydrogel rapidly polymerized following mild agitation. Subsequently, the hydrogel was immersed in water for 24 h to eliminate any unreacted monomers.

Preparation of GACP@PRP hydrogel

The GACP hydrogel was synthesized by dissolving 20 mg of GO-Ag NPs, 200 mg of CS, 300 mg of NIPAM, 20 mg of BIS, and 30 mg of APS in 26 mL of distilled water. The mixture was subjected to continuous mechanical stirring for 20 minutes, followed by the addition of TEMED as a catalyst. The hydrogel formed rapidly upon gentle stirring. Subsequently, it was immersed in water for 24 h to remove any unreacted monomers. PRP and hydrogel with a volume ratio of 1:3 were mixed homogeneously to obtain PRP-loaded GACP@PRP hydrogel at room temperature.

Characterization and analysis

The UV-Vis absorption spectra of the samples were using a UH5300 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired with a Bruker INVENIO FTIR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Zeta potentials of the samples were determined using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were acquired using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Waltham, USA). For morphological characterization, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were performed on a Zeiss Sigma 300 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany). Prior to SEM imaging, the hydrogel samples from different groups were freeze-dried under vacuum. The rheological properties of the hydrogels were measured with an MCR302 modular compact rheometer (Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria).

Hemostasis performance evaluation of hydrogel

In vivo mouse liver hemostasis experiment: The mice were anesthetized and positioned on a surgical plate. Induction of type 2 diabetes

Following a week of adjustment, rats were given a diet high in fat and sugar for 4 weeks. Then, 12-16 h after fasting, rats were intraperitoneally injected streptozotocin solution with a dose of 55 mg/kg (the modeling effect of STZ injection alone is more economical and controlled).59 STZ was freshly dissolved in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.2). On the five days after STZ injection, tail vein blood samples were collected to measure fasting blood glucose (FBG). Animals whose glucose levels were 16.7 mmol/L or higher were identified as having type 2 diabetes and were then selected for the experiments. All animal experiments should comply with ARRIVE guideline 2.0.

In vivo wound-healing examination

Type 2 diabetic SD rats were randomly divided into six groups (n=3 per group) to evaluate the effects of different treatments on wound healing: a negative control group (no treatment), a (NC+NIR) group, a positive control group (treated with PRP), a GACP hydrogel group, a GACP@PRP hydrogel group, and a (GACP@PRP+NIR) group (which received 808 nm NIR irradiation after hydrogel application).
