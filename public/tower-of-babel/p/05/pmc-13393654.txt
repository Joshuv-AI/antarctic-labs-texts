Combined effects of fenugreek-milk thistle-ginkgo and thyme-cinnamon-oregano feed additives on physicochemical and oxidative stability profiles of broiler meat
Authors: Xiaofeng Zhang, Guangjie Chen, Chenglin Jin, Xibin Zhang, Yanbing Zhao, Hu Zhang, Jiecheng Zhang, Xiaoyu Song, Xiaomei Luan, Peng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chicken breast meat is susceptible to lipid and protein oxidation during post-mortem processing and storage, which adversely affects its physicochemical properties, nutritional value, and market acceptability. Most existing research has concentrated on single-component plant extracts as feed additives, whereas the combined effects of multi-plant additives remain insufficiently characterized. This study evaluated the effects of two composite plant-derived feed additives (Group A: Trigonella foenum-graecum, Silybum marianum, and Ginkgo biloba; Group K: Thymus vulgaris, Cinnamomum verum, and Origanum vulgare) and their combined supplementation (Group A+K) on the quality and oxidative stability of broiler breast meat, with particular emphasis on myofibrillar protein (MP) structure and water dynamics. A total of 32,000 one-day-old Ross 308 broilers were assigned to four dietary treatments (control, A, K, and A+K) and fed for 35 d. The analyzed indicators included shear force, texture profile, water-holding capacity (WHC), MP secondary structure, oxidative markers (carbonyl and thiol contents), surface hydrophobicity, solubility, particle size, and water distribution. Group A showed the greatest tenderization response, with an approximately 50% reduction in shear force compared with the control (P < 0.05). The A+K group showed enhanced combined responses, including a 9% increase in WHC and an 8.02% decrease in carbonyl content compared with the control (P < 0.05). All treatment groups exhibited an increased MP alpha-helix proportion, reduced surface hydrophobicity and particle size, and improved solubility (P < 0.05). The A+K group also improved adhesiveness and cohesiveness. LF-NMR analysis indicated that the dietary treatments promoted a shift from free water toward immobilized water, which was associated with improved WHC. Overall, these composite plant-derived additives, especially the A+K combination, may regulate broiler meat quality through multiple targets by attenuating protein oxidation, maintaining MP structural stability, and optimizing water distribution. These findings provide a practical basis for developing plant-derived feed formulations for high-quality and oxidatively stable broiler meat.

Introduction

As one of the most widely consumed meats globally, the quality characteristics of chicken, particularly the texture, flavor, and nutritional stability of chicken breast, directly influence consumer acceptance and market value(Tijare et al., 2016; Qi et al., 2025a). Chicken breast is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids and proteins; however, it is highly susceptible to lipid and protein oxidation during processing and storage, which leads to meat quality deterioration, reduced nutritional value, and flavor loss(Cortinas et al., 2005). Therefore, how to improve the oxidative stability and physicochemical properties of chicken meat through nutritional regulation has become a research focus in the field of meat science.

In recent years, natural plant extracts, as feed additives, have shown great potential in improving meat quality, enhancing antioxidant capacity, and regulating metabolism(Vlaicu et al., 2022). Polyphenolic compounds such as pterostilbene have been proven to significantly inhibit the oxidative denaturation of post-mortem muscle proteins through dietary supplementation, maintain the structural stability of myofibrillar proteins, and thereby improve their gel properties and water-holding capacity(Wang et al., 2022b; Meng et al., 2024). In addition, prebiotics such as inulin can regulate intestinal flora, enhance the body's antioxidant status, and affect the transformation of muscle fiber types, thereby improving the functional properties of meat(Sun et al., 2024). These studies indicate that pre-slaughter nutritional intervention is an effective strategy for improving meat quality.

However, most current studies still focus on the effects of a single plant extract, and in-depth research is still lacking regarding the combined use of multiple plant-derived additives and their systematic impacts on chicken meat quality (Tashla et al., 2020; Savvidou et al., 2023). Trigonella foenum-graecum (fenugreek), Silybum marianum (milk thistle), and Ginkgo biloba are rich in flavonoids and polyphenolic compounds and exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (Dixit et al., 2005). Thymus vulgaris (thyme), Cinnamomum verum (cinnamon), and Origanum vulgare (oregano) are rich in volatile oils and polyphenolic compounds and possess antibacterial and antioxidant properties (Gavaric et al., 2015; Proaño et al., 2025). The combined use of these plant extracts may improve the physicochemical properties and oxidative stability of chicken meat through complementary antioxidant and metabolic pathways. Because no formal interaction analysis was conducted in the present study, the term combined effect is used rather than synergistic effect.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of Trigonella foenum-graecum-Silybum marianum-Ginkgo biloba (Group A), Thymus vulgaris-Cinnamomum verum-Origanum vulgare (Group K), and their combined supplementation (Group A+K) as feed additives on broiler breast meat quality. The study focused on their effects on muscle protein structure, oxidative stability, textural properties, and water distribution, with the goal of providing a theoretical basis and technical support for the development of green, efficient, and sustainable feed additives for high-quality chicken meat.

Materials and methods

Animal management and plant-derived feed additives

A total of 32,000 one-day-old Ross 308 broilers (males and females; New Hope Group Co., Ltd., China) with similar initial body weights (30.05 ± 0.50 g) were selected. Birds were assigned to four dietary treatments, with approximately 8,000 birds per treatment. At the end of the 35-d feeding trial, 400 broilers were selected from each treatment for slaughter and sampling, resulting in 1,600 sampled birds. The treatments were as follows: the control group was fed a basal corn-soybean meal diet; Group A was fed the basal diet supplemented with Trigonella foenum-graecum, Silybum marianum, and Ginkgo biloba extracts; Group K was fed the basal diet supplemented with Thymus vulgaris, Cinnamomum verum, and Origanum vulgare extracts; and Group A+K was fed the basal diet supplemented with all six plant extracts. The commercial composite preparations were obtained from New Hope Group Co., Ltd. AV3 supplied the fenugreek-milk thistle-ginkgo composite, and KERLAIT supplied the thyme-cinnamon-oregano composite. The product-level inclusion rates were as follows: Group A, AV3 at 50 g/t feed (equivalent to 50 mg/kg feed); Group K, KERLAIT at 50 g/t feed (equivalent to 50 mg/kg feed); and Group A+K, AV3 at 30 g/t feed (equivalent to 30 mg/kg feed) plus KERLAIT at 50 g/t feed (equivalent to 50 mg/kg feed). Because AV3 and KERLAIT were supplied as commercial composite products, the inclusion levels are reported at the product level. Detailed information is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Supplementary Table S1 lists the botanical components, supplier, commercial form, inclusion level, and major active compounds of AV3 and KERLAIT. The unit g/t refers to grams of additive product per metric ton of complete feed; numerically, 1 g/t is equivalent to 1 mg/kg feed.

The chicks were reared in the chicken houses of Xinrun Broiler Farm. The average house temperature and relative humidity during the feeding trial were 27.72 degrees C and 74.90%, respectively. The lighting schedule was 24 h/d, and mechanical ventilation was provided to maintain hygienic conditions and air quality. Broilers had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the 35-d trial. The rearing period lasted from August 25, 2024 to September 30, 2024.

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Qingdao Agricultural University. Broilers were slaughtered at a commercial abattoir following standard commercial slaughter procedures, and all efforts were made to minimize animal suffering during the feeding trial, slaughter, and sample collection.

Sample collection and preparation

After completion of the feeding trial, the birds were fasted for 12 h and slaughtered at a commercial abattoir following standard commercial slaughter procedures. Breast muscles of uniform size and thickness were excised, and visible fat, fascia, and connective tissues were removed. Samples were vacuum packaged in retort pouches and frozen at −80 degrees C until analysis. Before physicochemical analyses, frozen samples were thawed at 4 degrees C for 12 h unless otherwise stated, and no repeated freeze-thaw cycle was applied. Frozen-thawed breast meat was used for shear force, texture profile analysis, WHC determination, and LF-NMR analysis. Specifically, breast meat slices were used for WHC determination, and breast meat blocks were used for LF-NMR analysis. MP solution was prepared from thawed breast meat. Freeze-dried MP powder was used for FTIR analysis.

Preparation of MP solution

The extraction of MP was performed according to the method described by Wang et al. (2022b) and Choi and Chin (2024), with minor modifications. Briefly, 10 g of minced frozen-thawed chicken breast meat was mixed with 5 volumes of phosphate buffer I (20 mM phosphate buffer containing 0.1 M NaCl, pH 7.0) and homogenized for 30 s. The mixture was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm at 4 degrees C for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. This washing and centrifugation step was repeated three times to obtain the precipitate. The precipitate was then mixed with 5 volumes of phosphate buffer II (25 mM phosphate buffer containing 0.6 M NaCl, pH 7.0), followed by homogenization at 10,000 rpm for 20 s, repeated three times. The resulting MP solution was stored at 4 degrees C and used on the day of preparation. The protein concentration of MP was determined using the biuret method.

MP physicochemical properties

Shear force

Frozen-thawed breast meat samples were heated in a water bath at 70 degrees C for 30 min and then cooled to room temperature. Rectangular samples (1 cm x 2 cm x 1 cm) were cut parallel to the muscle fibers for shear force determination. Shear force was analyzed using a digital muscle tenderness meter (Model C-LM3, College of Engineering, Northeast Agricultural University, Harbin, China). The maximum force value was recorded as the shear force required to cut the chicken breast samples.

Water holding capacity (WHC)

The determination of WHC was conducted with minor modifications based on the methods described by Sun et al. (2023b) and Pan et al. (2024). After thawing, 2.0 g of breast meat slices (2 cm x 2 cm x 0.5 cm) were cut from each sample and weighed as m2. Each meat slice was wrapped with 18 layers of qualitative filter paper and pressed using a meat press at 70.0 mm/min under 350 N for 300 s, with a displacement of 100 mm. After pressing, the sample was removed from the filter paper and weighed as m3. WHC was calculated as follows, where m2 is the sample weight before pressing and m3 is the sample weight after pressing:WHC(%)=(m3/m2)×100

Textural properties

The textural properties were measured using a texture analyzer (Model TA.TOUCH, Baosheng Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Frozen-thawed breast meat samples were cut into regular blocks (1 cm x 2 cm x 1 cm) along the direction of muscle fibers. Samples were placed upright under the P/36R cylindrical probe. The measurement parameters were as follows: pre-test speed, 1 mm/s; test speed, 1 mm/s; post-test speed, 2 mm/s; compression ratio, 50%; trigger force, 5 g; and determination time, 5 s. Six replicate measurements were performed for each treatment.

Protein secondary structure

Protein secondary structure was determined using FTIR spectroscopy with minor modifications based on Wang et al. (2022b) and Choi and Chin (2024). Before determination, MP samples were freeze-dried and ground into powder. Approximately 0.1 g of freeze-dried MP powder was placed directly on the crystal of an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. Spectra were collected in absorbance mode over the range of 4,000-400 cm-1 at a spectral resolution of 4 cm-1 with 32 scans per sample. Background spectra were collected before each measurement. The amide I band (1,600-1,700 cm-1) was baseline corrected, smoothed, and subjected to Fourier self-deconvolution and Gaussian curve fitting. Peak assignment was conducted as follows: beta-sheet, 1,610-1,640 cm-1 and 1,680-1,700 cm-1; random coil, 1,640-1,650 cm-1; alpha-helix, 1,650-1,660 cm-1; and beta-turn, 1,660-1,680 cm-1. The relative content of each secondary structure was calculated from the fitted peak area as a percentage of the total amide I peak area.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

LF-NMR was performed using the technique described by Sun et al. (2023b), with minor modifications. Frozen-thawed breast meat blocks (approximately 2.0 g;2 cm × 2 cm × 1 cm) were placed in NMR tubes and equilibrated at 25 degrees C before measurement using an NMR analyzer (NMI20-040V-I; Niumag Analytical Instrument Corporation, Suzhou, China). The instrument was calibrated with a standard oil sample, and the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence was used for scanning. The parameters were set as follows: SW = 200 kHz, P1 = 8 us, P2 = 16 us, TW = 3,000 ms, NS = 4, TE = 0.3 ms, and NECH = 8,000. The T2 relaxation spectra were obtained by multi-exponential inversion using the instrument software. T2b, T21, and T22 were assigned to bound water, immobilized water, and free water, respectively, based on their relaxation-time ranges and peak positions. The peak area proportions were expressed as PT2b, PT21, and PT22.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity was measured following the procedure described by (Sun et al., 2023a; Chorhirankul et al., 2024). The protein concentration of each sample was adjusted to 5 mg/mL. One milliliter (1 mL) of the sample was mixed with 40 μL of bromophenol blue (BPB) solution (1 mg/mL), shaken at 25°C for 10 minutes, and then centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. Phosphate buffer was used as the blank control. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 595 nm. The surface hydrophobicity was calculated as following:

Surface hydrophobicity (μg BPB bound) = 40 × (OD control − OD sample) /OD control.

Thiol Content

The thiol content was determined following the procedure described by (Cheng et al., 2022). The protein concentration of each sample was adjusted to 5 mg/mL. One milliliter (1 mL) of the sample was mixed with 9 mL of EDTA buffer (0.01 M EDTA, 0.1 M K₂HPO₄, pH 6.0) and 100 μL of Ellman's reagent (10 mM DTNB, 0.01 M KH₂PO₄, pH 6.0). The mixture was incubated in the dark for 25 minutes. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm, with a molar extinction coefficient of 13,600 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹.

Carbonyl content

The carbonyl content was evaluated with minor modifications based on the method described by (Jin et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2024). The concentration of the myofibrillar protein (MP) solution was adjusted to 5 mg/mL. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of the MP solution was reacted with 0.5 mL of 2 M HCl solution (containing 0.02 M DNPH) at 25°C for 40 minutes. Thereafter, 0.5 mL of 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added to the aforementioned solution, and the mixed solution was centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 minutes. Next, the supernatant was discarded, and the MP precipitate was washed with 1 mL of ethanol solution (containing 50% ethyl acetate). The precipitate was resuspended in 1 mL of 6 M guanidine hydrochloride, followed by incubation in a water bath at 37°C for 30 minutes. Finally, the mixed solution was centrifuged again at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 minutes. The carbonyl content was calculated by measuring the absorbance at 370 nm, using a molar absorption coefficient of 22,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹.

Particle size

The particle size of myofibrillar protein (MP) was analyzed with reference to the protocol reported by (Chang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). First, the MP solution was diluted to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. Subsequently, a 1 mL aliquot of the adjusted MP solution was transferred into the sample cell of a particle size analyzer (Model McChik S3500, MicroTRAC, USA) for measurement.

Solubility

The MP was determined with minor modifications based on the method described by (Sun et al., 2024; Chai et al., 2025). For solubility analysis, the MP solution was adjusted to a concentration of 1 mg/mL, and 5 mL of this MP solution was centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 15 minutes. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected for protein content determination. The solu­bility was expressed as:Solubility(%)=Proteincontentofsupernatant/TotalproteincontentofbeforecentrifugedMPsolution×100.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. Differences among dietary treatments were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Different lowercase letters in tables and figures indicate significant differences among treatment groups. Because the experimental design did not include a full factorial interaction model for all single additives and their combinations, the term combined effect was used to describe the A+K response, and formal synergism was not claimed.

Results and discussion

Tenderness

Measuring meat shear force is of great significance for assessing meat tenderness and overall quality. Shear force is defined as the force required to shear a meat sample under specific conditions, and it exhibits a negative correlation with meat tenderness—specifically, a lower shear force value generally indicates tenderer meat(Li et al., 2025a). Through scientific determination and analysis of shear force, we can more accurately evaluate the freshness and sensory texture of meat products. Enhanced eating quality is characterized by low shear force, high tenderness, and abundant juiciness in post-slaughter meat, among which tenderness is a crucial factor determining the overall food quality. (Yu et al., 2021; Siddique et al., 2024) Fig. 1 illustrates the effect of feeding different diets on the tenderness of chicken breast meat. The shear force values of Group A and Group A+K were significantly lower than that of the control group (P < 0.05), with Group A showing the lowest shear force (8.65 N)—a reduction of nearly 50% compared to the control group (16.92 N). This indicates that the addition of plant-derived feed additives in Group A was the most effective in improving the tenderness of chicken breast meat. Additionally, the inclusion of plant-derived feed additives in Group K also resulted in a certain decrease in shear force, though the effect was not significant. Therefore, the addition of different plant-derived feed additives to the diet can improve the tenderness of chicken meat. (Liu et al., 2024a) reported that the supplementation of quercetin in the diet could increase meat tenderness to a certain extent, which is consistent with the results of our study. Meat tenderness is of great importance to consumers, as tender meat is more popular among them (Haščí­k et al., 2015; Siddique et al., 2024). The above experimental results demonstrate that the added different plant-derived feed additives all improved meat tenderness to a certain degree, thereby making the meat more acceptable to consumers. In terms of hardness, both the single additives A (51.35 ± 0.76 gf) and K (46.44 ± 4.6 gf) significantly increased compared to the control group (45.7 ± 3.16 gf) (P < 0.05), whereas the composite additive A+K (52.36 ± 1.95 gf) showed no significant difference from the control. The chewiness of all treatment groups decreased significantly (P < 0.05), with the K group exhibiting the lowest value (51.53 ± 5.23 gf), which is consistent with the improvement in tenderness (Siró et al., 2009; Blanco - Lizarazo et al., 2025). Adhesiveness was significantly increased only in the composite additive A+K group (122.56 ± 22.48 gf). Springiness was significantly decreased in the K group (0.54 ± 0.07), while the A group exhibited the highest value among all groups. Cohesiveness was significantly higher in both the A+K and K groups compared to the control. Resilience was significantly improved in the A group but significantly reduced in the A+K group.

In summary, feed additives exerted significant modulatory effects on the texture of chicken breast meat. Additive A primarily enhanced resilience and reduced chewiness; additive K decreased both chewiness and springiness while increasing hardness and cohesiveness; and the A+K combination showed a distinct combined response by increasing adhesiveness and cohesiveness while reducing resilience. These alterations may reflect changes in the muscle protein network structure and water distribution (Zhu et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2025b). The results indicate that rational selection of plant-derived additive combinations may allow targeted modulation of meat texture, which has practical implications for meat processing and product development.

Protein secondary structure

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), as a commonly used analytical technique, is widely applied in the qualitative and quantitative studies of meat products. The absorption signals in the amide I band region (1600–1700 cm⁻¹) can be utilized to assess the secondary structure composition of proteins (Mi et al., 2022) . These results demonstrate that the C—O stretching vibration holds significant value in this analysis (Goliomytis et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2024b) . By applying Fourier deconvolution to this region, multiple characteristic infrared absorption peaks can be resolved (Fig. 3).

The changes in the relative content of secondary structures of muscle protein (MP) are shown in Fig. 2C. Compared with the control group, the proportion of α-helix structure significantly increased in the A, K, and A+K treatment groups, reaching 22.67% (P < 0.05), 22.33% (P < 0.05), and 20.33% (P < 0.05), respectively. Correspondingly, the proportion of β-sheet structure in these groups decreased to 31.67%, 33.33%, and 34.67% (P < 0.05). No consistent trends were observed in the proportions of random coils and β-turns among the groups. The content of α-helix is closely related to the stability of hydrogen bonds formed between carbonyl oxygen (-CO) and amino hydrogen (-NH) in the polypeptide chain.(Li et al., 2019; Saleem and Ahmad, 2016), An increase in this structural proportion typically indicates a more stable protein conformation (Du et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2025b) . In contrast, existing studies have reported that the direct introduction of chlorogenic acid or mulberry extract into pork or beef protein solutions leads to a decrease in the proportion of α-helix, along with an increase in β-sheet and β-turn proportions (Huang et al., 2022; Saleem and Ahmad, 2016) . This discrepancy may be related to the protein type or the structural characteristics of phenolic compounds, but a more critical factor likely lies in the timing of addition: introducing polyphenols during the post-mortem phase tends to trigger protein-polyphenol interactions, promoting peptide chain unfolding and inducing secondary structure transitions from α-helix to β-sheet or random coil (Zhou et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022b) . In contrast, supplementing animal diets with plant-derived additives during the rearing phase can leverage their antioxidant components to inhibit in vivo protein oxidation, thereby maintaining the stability of the natural protein conformation. This postulated mechanism is also consistent with the observed reduction in carbonyl content in the present study.

Water-holding capacity and water distribution

Water-holding capacity (WHC) is one of the key indicators for evaluating the ability of breast meat to retain water during processing, reflecting the integrity of muscle structure and its ability to reduce water loss. (Li et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023) . As shown in Fig. 4A, compared with the control group, the WHC of breast meat was increased by 6%, 4%, and 9% in the A, K, and A+K groups, respectively (P < 0.05).Studies have shown that during the gelation process of MP, water is primarily captured within the network structure through hydrogen bonding interactions (Liu et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2024) . However, oxidative reactions occurring during the processing and storage of meat products post-mortem can disrupt the protein structure (Estévez, 2015; Nie et al., 2024). Weakening the capacity of carbonyl (–CO) and amino (–NH) groups in protein molecules to engage in hydrogen bonding results in a reduction in the water-holding capacity (WHC) of myofibrillar protein (MP) gels. (Quan et al., 2019).The plant-derived feed additives used in this experiment (such as fenugreek, milk thistle, ginkgo, thyme, cinnamon, and oregano) are natural sources of phenolic compounds. Existing studies have reported that phenolic compounds exhibit significant antioxidant activity, enabling them to effectively inhibit protein oxidation and degradation (Zhang et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022a). The findings of this study demonstrate that the inclusion of the aforementioned single or combined plant-derived feed additives in the pre-slaughter diet can effectively exert antioxidant activity within the body, mitigate oxidative stress-induced damage to muscle protein structures, facilitate the development of a more compact gel network structure, and consequently enhance water-holding capacity, as evidenced by a significant increase in WHC.

The T2 relaxation times of gels from diets fed with different plant-derived feed additives are shown in the Fig. 4B.Three peaks were detected in the T2 curve, representing bound water (T2b), immobilized water (T21), and free water (T22), respectively. A gel is a dynamic system primarily composed of water and proteins, in which water molecules predominantly exist in the form of immobilized water, consistent with the findings reported by (Wang et al., 2021). The binding between proteins and water molecules in the gel system inhibits proton migration, leading to shortened relaxation times and reduced water mobility (Sun et al., 2023b; Zhao et al., 2023a).

As shown in Fig. 4C, no significant differences were observed in T2b among all groups (P > 0.05). For T21, the A and K groups exhibited significantly lower values than the control group (P < 0.05), while the A+K group showed a significantly higher value (P < 0.05). These results indicate that the addition of plant-derived feed additives affected proton migration and water distribution in chicken breast meat. This phenomenon may be attributed to structural modifications of myofibrillar proteins induced by the additives, which promote the formation of a three-dimensional gel network and enhance the water-binding capacity of the gel, thereby restricting the freedom of proton migration (Han et al., 2008, Wang et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2025c; Chang et al., 2024).

Herein, PT2b, PT21, and PT22 represent the peak area proportions of T2b, T21, and T22, respectively. Fig. 4D reflects the distribution of three types of water in breast meat. No significant differences were observed in PT2b levels among all groups (P > 0.05). With the addition of plant-derived feed additives (fenugreek, milk thistle, ginkgo, thyme, cinnamon, and oregano), the proportion of PT21 in groups A and A+K increased by 3% and 2%, respectively. This indicates that the incorporation of these plant-derived feed additives can enhance the proportion of immobilized water (Liu et al., 2013) suggested that the proportion of immobilized water is positively correlated with water-holding capacity (WHC), which is consistent with the findings of this study. Compared with the control group, the PT22 values of groups A and K decreased by 2%. These results demonstrate that the addition of plant-derived feed additives can reduce the proportion of free water and promote its conversion into immobilized water, aligning with the findings reported by (Wang et al., 2022b; Meremäe et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024).

Multiple studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between the antioxidant capacity of myofibrillar proteins and their water-holding capacity as well as the content of non-exudative water, indicating that greater antioxidant capacity is associated with improved water retention(Wang et al., 2022b; Luo et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024). Previous research has consistently shown that type I muscle fibers exhibit higher antioxidant capacity compared to type II fibers (Shah et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2023b). Consequently, muscles with a higher proportion of type I fibers tend to possess enhanced overall antioxidant activity. This inference is further supported by the carbonyl content measurements presented in Fig. 3C. Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity is an important indicator of the degree of exposure of hydrophobic groups in protein molecules (Wei et al., 2023). The exposure of hydrophobic groups usually means the unfolding or conformational change of the protein's three-dimensional structure, resulting in the exposure of the hydrophobic regions originally embedded inside to the aqueous environment. This change will weaken the interaction between proteins and water molecules, reduce their hydrophilicity, and thus affect the solubility and stability of proteins. Meanwhile, exposure of hydrophobic regions often promotes hydrophobic interactions between protein molecules, leading to protein denaturation and aggregation, which may ultimately affect their functional properties (Wei et al., 2023). Therefore, accurate determination of surface hydrophobicity is helpful for a deeper understanding of the structure-activity relationship of proteins and their stability changes during processing and storage.

The surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar protein (MP) in different feed feeding groups is shown in Fig. 5. Compared with the control group, the surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar proteins in the treatment group supplemented with three types of feed, A, K, and A+K, was significantly reduced (P < 0.05), with the A group sample having the lowest surface hydrophobicity value. From this, it can be seen that functional ingredients such as A, K, and A+K added to feed can effectively mask or reduce the exposure of hydrophobic groups in myofibrillar protein molecules, thereby weakening the non-specific aggregation tendency caused by hydrophobic interactions between protein molecules. This function helps maintain the structural integrity and stability of myofibrillar proteins during processing and storage, and has positive physiological significance and application potential for improving the functional properties of proteins and the quality of final products. This finding is consistent with the research results of (Jin et al., 2021) and (Wang et al., 2022b), who reported that adding resveratrol and pterostilbene to the diet can significantly reduce the surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar proteins.Fig. 5Surface hydrophobicity of chicken MP from different dietary supplementation groups. Based on the above analysis, it can be concluded that adding functional ingredients such as thyme, cinnamon bark, oregano, fenugreek, milk thistle, and ginkgo to feed can significantly reduce the surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar proteins, which has a positive impact on improving their functional characteristics and product quality. This result is consistent with previous studies reporting that polyphenolic substances can reduce the surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar proteins, further supporting the potential application value of functional feed additives in regulating protein structure.

Sulfhydryl

Thiols are active groups located on the surface of proteins that are easily oxidized to form disulfide bonds, which can affect the tertiary and quaternary structures of proteins (Li et al., 2025b). As shown in Fig. 6, the feeding of Group A and Group K in the four treatment groups was significantly higher than that of the control group, indicating that the addition of Thyme, Cinnamomum verum, oregano, fenugreek, milk thistle, and Ginkgo biloba feed could effectively improve the thiol content of MP (P < 0.05). This suggests that adding two types of feed separately to the diet can reduce the thiol loss caused by protein oxidation in chicken breast meat during post slaughter storage. However, after adding plant-based additives such as thyme, cinnamon bark, oregano, fenugreek, milk thistle, and ginkgo to the feed, no significant differences were observed in the measured indicators compared to the blank control group. This indicates that the supplementation of thyme, cinnamon bark, oregano, fenugreek, milk thistle, and ginkgo did not alter the thiol content of MP. This result is consistent with the report by (Han et al., 2022c), which also indicated that adding inulin to the myofibrillar protein system did not cause significant changes in thiol content. One important reason for this result may be related to the composite addition method of multiple plant materials. Due to differences in the biological activity, mechanism of action, and metabolic pathways of active ingredients in different plants, as well as possible antagonistic effects between the components during feeding, they may inhibit each other and weaken the physiological efficacy of a single component, thereby affecting its overall regulatory effect on duck meat quality. On the other hand, the concentration of active ingredients added to plant materials may not have reached the effective threshold for their biological activity in vivo. Due to multiple factors such as processing, storage, and animal feeding, the actual bioavailability of active ingredients is limited, and they have not fully participated in the body's metabolism or biochemical reactions in muscle tissue, thus failing to have observable and significant effects on the physicochemical properties of chicken meat.Fig. 6Sulfhydryl content of chicken MP from different dietary supplementation groups. Carbonyl

When proteins are attacked by reactive oxygen species, peptide chains may break and form carbonyl derivatives; therefore, carbonyl content is often used as an important biochemical indicator of protein oxidative damage (Cong et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. 7, the carbonyl content in the control group was 0.9567 nmol/mg. Dietary supplementation with fenugreek-milk thistle-ginkgo (Group A) and thyme-cinnamon-oregano (Group K) significantly decreased carbonyl content by 4.54% and 2.09%, respectively, compared with the control. This indicates that these two additive combinations attenuated MP oxidative damage. The plant components in Group A and Group K may exert antioxidant effects through multiple mechanisms: first, their phenolic and flavonoid components may directly scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS); second, some components may contribute to intestinal fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production, indirectly enhancing resistance to oxidative stress (Wan et al., 2020). The A+K group showed an 8.02% reduction in carbonyl content compared with the control, which was numerically greater than the reductions observed in Groups A and K. Because a formal interaction analysis was not performed, this result is interpreted as an enhanced combined antioxidant response rather than definitive synergism. In addition, Jin et al. (2021) suggested that lower carbonyl content may reflect improved overall muscle oxidative stability. Previous studies have shown that type I muscle fibers have stronger antioxidant capacity than type II fibers (Li et al., 2023). Thus, changes in muscle fiber characteristics may partly contribute to reduced carbonyl formation, although this mechanism should be confirmed in future work.Fig. 7Carbonyl content of chicken MP from different dietary supplementation groups. The antioxidant capacity of myofibrillar protein is positively correlated with its water holding capacity and the proportion of non flowing water, indicating that the stronger the antioxidant capacity, the higher the water holding capacity (Jin et al., 2021); (Wang et al., 2022b). Solubility and particle size

Solubility is an important indicator of the binding ability of myofibrillar protein (MP) to water molecules (Han et al., 2022a). As shown in Fig. 8, compared with the control group, the solubility of MP in the three dietary supplementation groups was significantly increased (P < 0.05). Among the three dietary additive groups, the solubility of A+K group was lower than that of A and K groups (P < 0.05), indicating that dietary additives increased the solubility of chicken MP. The results of this study are consistent with the report by (Jin et al., 2021), which indicated that supplementing 300mg/kg resveratrol in feed can significantly increase the solubility of duck meat myofibrillar protein. The increase in solubility indicates an enhanced structural stability of myofibrillar proteins and a reduced exposure of their hydrophobic groups. Changes in MP structure and exposure of hydrophobic groups typically weaken the binding ability between proteins and water molecules (Cheng et al., 2022; Han et al., 2022c; Sun et al., 2023b). The surface hydrophobicity measurement results obtained in this study (Fig. 5) further support this conclusion.Fig. 8Solubility of chicken MP from different dietary supplementation groups. Particle size can be used as a key indicator to characterize the aggregation status of myofibrillar proteins (MP)(Xu et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 9, the particle size of the three feed treatment groups was significantly lower than that of the control group (P < 0.05), indicating that the supplementation of the three feed supplements changed the size of chicken MP. In addition, multiple studies have shown that the solubility of myofibrillar protein (MP) is closely related to its particle size (Chen et al., 2021; Han et al., 2022b; Zou et al., 2022). Reducing particle size can significantly increase the specific surface area of MP, thereby enhancing its interaction with water molecules and ultimately improving protein solubility. On the other hand, changes in particle size are also related to the degree of protein oxidation (Xu et al., 2023). When MP undergoes oxidation, its natural structure unfolds and hydrophobic groups are exposed, thereby promoting cross-linking and aggregation between protein molecules, leading to an increase in particle size. The results of this study showed that fenugreek, milk thistle, Ginkgo biloba, Thyme, Cinnamomum verum, and oregano effectively inhibited the oxidation of MP due to their strong antioxidant capacity (Fig. 8), which may be one of the important mechanisms leading to particle size reduction. In the A+K group, carbonyl content decreased while the particle size of MP was also reduced, suggesting that the combined supplementation may have attenuated oxidative aggregation and promoted a more dispersed MP structure. This may be related to the antioxidant components in the combined additives, which could reduce oxidative unfolding and aggregation of MP molecules. Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with composite plant-derived feed additives (Group A: Trigonella foenum-graecum-Silybum marianum-Ginkgo biloba; Group K: Thymus vulgaris-Cinnamomum verum-Origanum vulgare) and their combined use (Group A+K) exerted multi-target regulatory effects on the quality and oxidative stability of broiler breast meat. Group A produced the most pronounced tenderization response, reducing shear force by nearly 50%, which may be associated with attenuation of protein oxidation and maintenance of MP structural integrity. Group K mainly influenced textural properties by increasing hardness and cohesiveness while decreasing chewiness. The A+K combination produced enhanced combined responses, including a 9% increase in WHC and an 8.02% reduction in carbonyl content compared with the control. All treatments improved MP conformational stability by increasing the alpha-helix proportion, reducing surface hydrophobicity and particle size, and improving solubility, thereby facilitating a shift from free water toward immobilized water. These results support the potential use of combined plant-derived feed additives as a practical nutritional strategy for improving the physicochemical quality and oxidative stability of broiler breast meat. Owing to the limited replication structure and lack of formal interaction analysis, future studies should use factorial designs and standardized active-component quantification to verify potential interactive effects among individual plant additives.
