Clinical phenotype and long-term damage in paediatric Behçet’s disease: comparative performance of ISG, ICBD and PEDBD classification criteria in a retrospective endemic cohort
Authors: Burcu Bozkaya Yücel, Şeyda Doğantan, Ömer Gökhan Çelik, Semanur Elmas, Nurçin Öğreten Yadigaroğlu, Hilal Eser Öztürk, Gökçen Öz Tunçer, Ünal Akça, Burcu Çalışkan, Aslıhan Sanrı, Canan Albayrak, Zuhal Keskin Sarılar, Özlem Aydoğ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives To compare the sensitivity of International Study Group (ISG, 1990), International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD, 2014) and the Paediatric Behçet’s Disease (PEDBD, 2016) classification criteria in paediatric Behçet’s disease (pBD) within an endemic cohort and to assess organ involvement, treatment patterns, outcomes and cumulative damage using the Behçet’s Syndrome Overall Damage Index.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 69 consecutive children with clinician-diagnosed pBD (symptom onset ≤18 years) at a tertiary centre in Türkiye (2020–2025). Classification criteria were applied at the last visit. The primary outcome was sensitivity versus clinician diagnosis; secondary outcomes included sex-stratified risks, therapies/adverse events and cumulative damage.ResultsICBD classified 97.1% of cases, compared with 58.0% for ISG and 53.6% for PEDBD. Neurovascular involvement occurred in 18.8% (mainly cerebral venous thrombosis), vascular in 20.3% and ocular in 17.4% (posterior-segment 11.6%). Boys had higher risks of neurovascular disease, while girls more often had genital ulcers. Treatment followed steroid-sparing pathways (colchicine 98.6%, azathioprine 63.8%, antitumour necrosis factor 21.7%); adverse events occurred in 15.9% and non-adherence in 11.6%. Cumulative damage was low to moderate, with a mean BODI score of 0.45 (range 0–3).ConclusionsIn this endemic paediatric cohort, ICBD demonstrated superior sensitivity, while ISG and PEDBD classified only half of cases. Morbidity was driven by neurovascular events and, less frequently, posterior-segment ocular disease. Findings support an ICBD-anchored approach with co-reporting of ISG/PEDBD for comparability. Low-threshold neuro-ophthalmic evaluation and timely steroid-sparing escalation are critical for sight/central nervous system-threatening disease. Targeted vascular imaging and adherence-focused adolescent care remain priorities.Keywords: Behcet syndrome, classification, uveitis, vasculitis, treatment

Introduction

Paediatric Behçet’s disease (pBD) is a rare but clinically consequential vasculitic syndrome with marked geographic clustering in endemic regions, where background prevalence and referral patterns complicate recognition and follow-up. Paediatric phenotypes often evolve over time, producing diagnostic delay and heterogeneous morbidity spanning mucocutaneous, ocular, neurological, vascular and gastrointestinal (GI) domains.1,3

Several classification systems are used in practice. The International Study Group (ISG) criteria—oral aphthosis plus two additional features—are highly specific but may miss early or atypical paediatric presentations.1 The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) employ a weighted point system and generally yield higher sensitivity across phenotypes and geographies.2 The Paediatric Behçet’s Disease (PEDBD) criteria were designed for childhood disease, with recent registry-based validation refining their performance characteristics.3 4 In parallel, consensus frameworks standardising ocular inflammation (Standardisation of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN))5 6 and EULAR recommendations for major organ involvement7 have improved comparability and management.

Despite these advances, the head-to-head performance of ISG, ICBD and PEDBD in real-world paediatric endemic cohorts remains incompletely defined. Moreover, the relationship between classification and organ-specific morbidity—particularly neurovascular events (eg, cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT)) and posterior-segment ocular disease—has not been systematically evaluated.8,11 Long-term cumulative damage, although central to patient outcomes, has rarely been quantified in children, and the Behçet’s Syndrome Overall Damage Index (BODI) has not been widely applied in paediatric cohorts.12 13

We therefore conducted a retrospective single-centre cohort study in Türkiye (2020–2025) including consecutive children with clinician-diagnosed BD (symptom onset ≤18 years) to:

Compare the sensitivity of ISG, ICBD and PEDBD criteria against clinician diagnosis.

Characterise organ involvement, sex-stratified risks, therapies, adverse events and time-to-event outcomes.

By situating classification performance alongside neuro-ophthalmic and vascular risk, our study aims to inform both clinical decision-making and the design of future pBD research in endemic settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective, single-centre cohort study at the Samsun Training and Research Hospital (Türkiye), a tertiary paediatric rheumatology centre in an endemic BD region. The study period was 1 January 2020–30 June 2025. Multidisciplinary care involved paediatric rheumatology, ophthalmology, neurology, dermatology, gastroenterology and haematology, in line with contemporary practice and EULAR guidance.7 Methods and reporting adhered to institutional and field standards for retrospective cohort research.

Participants

Eligibility

Consecutive patients with symptom onset ≤18 years and a treating paediatric rheumatologist’s clinical diagnosis of pBD were included, consistent with endemic paediatric cohorts. Diagnosis of pBD was verified independently by two paediatric rheumatologists.

Classification criteria

Each patient was assessed for fulfilment (at any time during follow-up) of ISG,1 ICBD2 and PEDBD3 based on original publications and the recent international paediatric validation.4

Exclusion criteria

Patients with alternative diagnoses (eg, monogenic vasculitis/periodic fever, inflammatory bowel disease, PFAPA (periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, adenitis)) or <6 months of follow-up after the index visit were excluded.

Cohort flow

Of 74 screened, five were excluded (three incomplete records, two alternate diagnoses), yielding n=69 (onlinesupplemental figures 1 2). Missing data were addressed with a complete case analysis. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that exclusion did not alter the main findings.

Classification assessment

Fulfilment of ISG, ICBD and PEDBD was scored at any time during follow-up using the original criteria.1,3

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Sensitivity of each classification criterion set (proportion fulfilling among clinically diagnosed cases).

Secondary outcomes

Sex-stratified risks (neuro-BD, any vascular involvement, posterior-segment ocular disease); treatment exposures; CTCAE-graded adverse events; adherence and cumulative damage at last follow-up (BODI).712,23

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.21, with a two-sided significance level of α=0.05. Continuous variables are reported as mean±SD or median (IQR); categorical variables as n/N (%). Sensitivities used Wilson 95% CIs.5 6 Group comparisons used χ² test or Fisher’s exact test (categorical) and t-test or Mann-Whitney U test (continuous), as appropriate. Analyses were conducted using available-case denominators without imputation. Given the exploratory design, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Data governance

Therapeutic decisions adhered to EULAR recommendations where applicable7; ophthalmic, neurological and vascular assessments followed SUN and vascular BD standards.56 9,11 The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline24 was used to draft this manuscript, and the STROBE reporting checklist25 was applied during editing (included in online supplemental file A).

Results

Cohort and baseline characteristics

We analysed 69 children/adolescents with paediatric-onset BD (female 44/69, 63.8%). Mean age at symptom onset was 13.2±2.7 years; mean age at diagnosis was 13.9±2.9 years; mean diagnostic delay was 8.0±10.7 months and mean follow-up was 33.2±23.5 months. Consanguinity was present in 14/69 patients (20.3%) and a family history of BD in 6/69 patients (8.7%) (table 1).

Mucocutaneous, skin and gastrointestinal involvement

Oral aphthosis was universal (69/69, 100%). Genital ulcers occurred in 48/69 patients (69.6%), and were more frequent in females (81.8%) than males (48.0%). Skin involvement affected 33/69 patients (47.8%), evenly distributed across papulopustular lesions, erythema nodosum and folliculitis (each 15.9%). GI complaints occurred in 31/69 patients (44.9%), dominated by abdominal pain (36.2%); chronic diarrhoea (4.3%), GI bleeding (4.3%), perforation (1.4%) and ileocolonic ulcers (1.4%) were rare (table 2).

Ocular involvement

Any ocular involvement was present in 12/69 patients (17.4%). Anterior uveitis was rare (1/69, 1.4%). Posterior-segment disease included posterior uveitis (4/69, 5.8%) and panuveitis (4/69, 5.8%), yielding a posterior-segment composite of 8/69 patients (11.6%). Neurovascular spectrum

Neurological involvement occurred in 13/69 patients (18.8%), predominantly non-parenchymal (10/69, 14.5%), including CVT (9/69, 13.0%) and one case of raised intracranial pressure (ICP) without CVT (1/69, 1.4%). Parenchymal disease was observed in 3/69 patients (4.3%). Any vascular involvement occurred in 14/69 patients (20.3%), comprising systemic deep venous thrombosis (3/69, 4.3%), CVT (9/69, 13.0%; overlapping with neurological involvement), Budd-Chiari syndrome (1/69, 1.4%) and pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA) (1/69, 1.4%). All neurovascular diagnoses were confirmed by imaging (table 4).

Sex-stratified risks

Males had higher risks of neurological involvement (32.0% vs 11.4%; OR 3.67, 95% CI 1.05 to 12.87; p=0.035) and any vascular involvement (40.0% vs 9.1%; OR 6.60, 95% CI 1.85 to 23.60; p=0.006). Genital ulcers were more frequent in females (81.8% vs 48.0%; OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.61; p=0.003). Ocular and articular involvement did not differ significantly by sex (p=0.275 and p=0.667, respectively). GI involvement was more common in females (54.5% vs 36.0%), but the difference was not statistically significant (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.17 to 1.29; p=0.138) (table 5, online supplemental figure 2).

Genetics and pathergy

HLA-B*51 was positive in 21/49 tested patients (42.9%; overall 30.4%). MEFV variants were identified in 7/54 patients (13.0%), most commonly M694V (n=3), M680I (n=2) and V726A (n=2). Pathergy was positive in 14/46 patients (30.4%).

Treatment

Colchicine was prescribed in nearly all patients (68/69, 98.6%). Additional therapies included azathioprine (44/69, 63.8%), systemic corticosteroids (27/69, 39.1%; pulse methylprednisolone 4/69, 5.8%), ciclosporin A (6/69, 8.7%) and methotrexate (2/69, 2.9%). Anti-TNF agents were used in 15/69 patients (21.7%), with adalimumab (12 exposures) most frequent, followed by infliximab (3) and etanercept (3); several patients required switching between agents (table 6).

Patients were managed using a steroid-sparing strategy. The mean number of steroid-sparing agents used per patient was 1.03±0.97 (range 0–4). Systemic glucocorticoids were prescribed in 39.1% of patients, and pulse methylprednisolone was administered in 5.8%, exclusively in those with severe neurovascular or ocular involvement. The median duration of azathioprine therapy was 24 months (IQR 12–36), and the median duration of anti-TNF therapy was 27 months (IQR 22.5–35). Pulse methylprednisolone exposure was short-term, with a median duration of 5 days (IQR 4.75–5). Colchicine was initiated at diagnosis and continued throughout follow-up in all patients; therefore, colchicine exposure duration was approximated by individual follow-up duration.

Safety

Adverse events occurred in 11/69 patients (15.9%), most commonly azathioprine-related hepatotoxicity or GI intolerance, typically leading to dose reduction or discontinuation. Colchicine-related abdominal pain or diarrhoea occurred in three patients, ciclosporin caused gingival hypertrophy in one patient and methotrexate caused nausea in one patient (switched to azathioprine). Non-adherence was documented in 8/69 patients (11.6%) (table 7).

Cumulative damage

Cumulative damage was low to moderate, with a mean BODI score of 0.45±0.74, median 0 (IQR 0–1) and range 0–3.

Classification performance

ICBD2 classified nearly all clinician-diagnosed cases (67/69, 97.1%; 95% CI 90.0 to 99.2), whereas ISG1 and PEDBD3 criteria identified only about half (40/69, 58.0%; 95% CI 46.2 to 68.9 and 37/69, 53.6%; 95% CI 42.0 to 64.9, respectively). Domain-specific analysis of classification criteria demonstrated marked differences in sensitivity across clinical manifestations. ICBD criteria consistently showed the highest sensitivity across all domains, particularly in genital, vascular and neurological involvement. ISG criteria performed moderately, with lower sensitivity in GI and vascular domains. PEDBD criteria demonstrated balanced but lower sensitivity across mucocutaneous and systemic domains. These findings highlight that ICBD criteria capture a broader spectrum of pBD manifestations, whereas ISG and PEDBD criteria may underperform in systemic domains (table 8, online supplemental figure 3).

Discussion

This single-centre cohort from an endemic region provides current, real-world evidence on pBD. We compared the performance of commonly used classification criteria, described sex-specific risks, identified priority domains for serious morbidity—neurovascular involvement (particularly CVT) and, less frequently, posterior-segment ocular disease—and related these findings to surveillance and treatment-escalation strategies. We also summarised stepwise treatment, safety and adherence, and reported cumulative damage using the BODI index.

In head-to-head comparison, the ICBD classified 67/69 clinician-diagnosed children (97.1%), whereas the ISG and PEDBD criteria captured approximately half. This gradient aligns with paediatric and Türkiye-based experiences reporting higher ICBD sensitivity across phenotypes,1,326 and with contemporary paediatric validation studies highlighting context-dependent effects for PEDBD.4 In our design, criteria were applied at the last visit to reflect real-world accrual of manifestations over follow-up, and sensitivity was reported with Wilson 95% CIs.5 6 Although the ICBD demonstrated broader case capture, this may come at the expense of specificity; therefore, clinical context remains essential.

In paediatric settings with high BD prevalence, ICBD demonstrated broader capture in our cohort, while co-reporting ISG/PEDBD facilitates cross-study comparability.1,426

Boys had higher risks of neuro-BD and vascular involvement, whereas girls more often exhibited mucosal disease, notably genital ulcers. This pattern accords with Turkish and other endemic cohorts and reviews describing male-skewed thrombotic and neurological involvement and a female-skewed mucosal burden.7,10 Both biological explanations (eg, hormonal and immunogenetic influences) and sociocultural factors (eg, earlier recognition of mucosal symptoms in girls and delayed presentation in boys) may contribute.

Serious morbidity centred on neurovascular involvement; posterior-segment ocular disease was less frequent. In our cohort, irreversible visual loss reflected chronic papilloedema secondary to intracranial hypertension, leading to optic atrophy and bilateral blindness, rather than posterior uveitis. Overall ocular involvement was infrequent (17.4%), and the posterior-segment composite (posterior uveitis or panuveitis) accounted for 11.6%—a phenotype associated in paediatric series with a higher risk of visual loss than isolated anterior uveitis. These findings support standardised assessment using the SUN framework, rapid referral and timely steroid-sparing/biologic escalation when vision is threatened.5 6 15 16

Neurological morbidity centred on CVT and papilloedema-related optic nerve compromise. These observations support urgent brain MRI/MRV for new headache, papilloedema or focal deficits; neuro-ophthalmic evaluation with opening-pressure measurement when safe and coordinated care with neurology/haematology when neuro-BD is suspected.18,20 Management should follow guidance emphasising timely immunosuppression, appropriate anticoagulation in selected cases and ICP-lowering measures when indicated.718,20 Prognostically, our data suggest that long-term morbidity in pBD is driven more by neurovascular events than by ocular disease, in contrast to the adult cohort.

We identified PAA in a patient with persistently elevated acute-phase reactants. Although rare, PAA carries high mortality. In endemic paediatric settings, persistent or recurrent C reactive protein/erythrocyte sedimentation rate elevation should prompt targeted thoracic vascular imaging (eg, CT angiography/MR angiography)—especially in boys or when chest pain, haemoptysis or unexplained dyspnoea are present.21,23 This aligns with vascular BD literature stressing early recognition and aggressive immunosuppression for life-threatening vascular phenotypes.21 22

Care followed a steroid-sparing pathway: near-universal colchicine for mucocutaneous disease, azathioprine as the main steroid-sparing agent in organ-threatening phenotypes and anti-TNF escalation for sight-threatening or CNS-threatening disease—mirroring EULAR recommendations and paediatric practice summaries.4 7 18 Adverse events were largely mild to moderate and manageable with standard monitoring (CTCAE).14 Non-adherence (~12%), clustering in adolescents, likely increases relapse risk—supporting family education, simplified regimens, reminder systems and structured transition to adult care.7 These findings underscore real-world feasibility: even in resource-limited settings, a stepwise, guideline-concordant pathway can be implemented.

Inflammatory activity and long-term damage are distinct constructs. To reflect long-term impact, we summarised cumulative damage at last follow-up using BODI, a validated damage index for BD.12 13 In our cohort, BODI was low to moderate overall, consistent with paediatric timeframes and steroid-sparing care. Clinically, BODI helps (i) communicate long-term risk to families, (ii) distinguish ‘scar’ versus ‘flare’ when deciding escalation and (iii) enable intercentre comparisons in endemic settings. Although paediatric validation of BODI remains limited, its use here enhances comparability with adult cohorts. For higher-impact reporting, pairing organ-specific hard outcomes (posterior-segment activity/vision, neurovascular events) with BODI provides a fuller picture of both short-term control and long-term accrual.18,20

Our sensitivity gradient (ICBD>ISG/PEDBD) mirrors paediatric data from Türkiye and elsewhere, supporting ICBD as the primary classification with co-reporting of ISG/PEDBD for comparability.1,426 Overall ocular involvement—and the proportion with the posterior-segment composite—fell within ranges reported in ophthalmology-based paediatric series; yet irreversible vision loss in our cohort stemmed from intracranial hypertension-related papilloedema causing optic atrophy, not posterior uveitis.15 16 Neurovascular patterns were concordant with endemic reports, with CVT prominent and risks skewed towards boys.7,1018 Therapeutic pathways and outcomes aligned with EULAR-informed paediatric practice, including early colchicine and selective immunosuppressants/biologics in refractory disease.4 7 18

Beyond national cohorts, recent international collaborative studies further contextualise our findings. Akgün et al reported higher biologic use in European pBD centres compared with Türkiye, contrasting with our cohort where biologics were reserved for sight-threatening or CNS-threatening disease.29 Kaya Akca et al identified distinct clinical clusters across Türkiye, Europe and Iran, underscoring heterogeneity in pBD phenotypes and treatment pathways.30 Our findings also contrast with Japanese pBD cohorts, where ocular involvement has historically been more frequent and intestinal-type BD is increasingly recognised.31,33 Yamaguchi and Fujikawa reported lower sensitivity of ISG, ICBD and PEDBD criteria in Japanese children compared with our cohort.31 Nationwide analyses further highlight phenotypic evolution in Japan, with decreasing rates of HLA-B*51-positive complete type and rising intestinal involvement.32 33 Integrating our data with these international experiences highlights both shared features—such as universal oral aphthosis and frequent genital ulcers—and regional differences in ocular, neurovascular and therapeutic patterns.

Strengths include an endemic paediatric cohort with systematic ascertainment of posterior ocular and neurovascular phenotypes; transparent comparisons of classification criteria (sensitivity with Wilson 95% CIs and descriptive cross-tabulations) and integrated reporting of safety and adherence.

Limitations include retrospective, single-centre design with potential information bias; criteria assessed ‘at any time’ may inflate sensitivity; incomplete genetic testing and limited power for adjusted analyses and rare outcomes. Importantly, international comparability is enhanced by aligning our reporting with widely used indices and criteria.

Taken together, our data support adopting an ICBD-anchored classification with co-reporting of ISG/PEDBD for comparability,1,426 and stratifying surveillance by sex: routine posterior-segment ophthalmic follow-up for all patients; lower thresholds for neurovascular imaging in boys and structured mucosal disease control and quality-of-life tracking in girls.5,1015 16 We further support maintaining a low threshold for MRI/MRV in new headaches, papilloedema or focal neurological deficits, with early neuro-ophthalmology input and prompt escalation to steroid-sparing/biologic therapy when vision or the CNS is threatened.18,20 Targeted thoracic vascular imaging to exclude PAA should be pursued when inflammation persists, particularly in the presence of respiratory symptoms.21,23 Finally, embedding adolescent adherence and structured transition programmes, and reporting BODI alongside organ-specific end points, may better integrate disease activity and cumulative damage over time.12 13

Conclusion

In this endemic, single-centre paediatric cohort, the ICBD criteria identified nearly all clinician-diagnosed BD cases, whereas the ISG and PEDBD criteria captured only about half. An ICBD-anchored classification with co-reporting of ISG/PEDBD therefore best balances comprehensive clinical capture with cross-study comparability. Cumulative damage was predominantly neurovascular, underscoring the need for early recognition and aggressive management of cerebral venous thrombosis and ICP-related complications. Posterior-segment ocular disease was uncommon but, when present, carried a substantial risk of irreversible vision loss, highlighting the importance of systematic ophthalmic surveillance. Persistent inflammation should prompt targeted vascular imaging to exclude life-threatening vasculitis, such as PAA. Care pathways in our cohort aligned with contemporary guidance, and cumulative damage remained low to moderate, emphasising the value of adherence support and structured transition programmes for adolescents. Future multicentre, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to refine paediatric-specific surveillance and treatment strategies.
