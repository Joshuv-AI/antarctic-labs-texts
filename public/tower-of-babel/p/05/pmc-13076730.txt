Treatment Outcomes with Lanadelumab Every 2 Weeks and Garadacimab are Very Similar in Patients with Hereditary Angioedema
Authors: Irmgard Andresen, Natalie Khutoryansky, Sarah L Feeny, Alissa Dangel
Published: 2026

We read with interest the recent publication titled “Network meta‑analysis of pharmacological therapies for long‑term prophylactic treatment of patients with hereditary angioedema” [1]. Using a Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA), the authors indirectly compared the efficacy and safety outcomes of garadacimab (200 mg monthly), lanadelumab (300 mg every 2 weeks [Q2W] and 4 weeks [Q4W]), subcutaneous C1INH (60 IU/kg twice weekly), and berotralstat (150 mg daily) as long-term prophylaxis in patients with hereditary angioedema. In the absence of head-to-head clinical trials, indirect comparisons are often conducted to compare different treatment options. However, there are inherent limitations to NMAs that must be considered to contextualize any clinical conclusions drawn from such analyses. We have identified several limitations in the NMA conducted by Walsh and colleagues that we feel must be highlighted.

Most importantly, data on the efficacy and safety of lanadelumab were drawn from the HELP Study [2], in which study participants were randomly assigned to receive placebo, lanadelumab Q2W, or lanadelumab Q4W at initiation. However, the SmPC/label for lanadelumab requires all patients to initiate lanadelumab on Q2W treatment, with a dose interval extension to Q4W appropriate for patients who are stable/well controlled (e.g., attack free) on lanadelumab Q2W [3]. In the HELP Study, study participants did not receive this dosing regimen (starting Q2W then extending dosing intervals to Q4W once stable/well controlled). Therefore, data from the HELP Study on lanadelumab Q4W do not reflect the effectiveness of lanadelumab Q4W in clinical practice, and thus the comparison between garadacimab and lanadelumab Q4W in the NMA should not be incorporated into treatment decision-making processes.

For the garadacimab evaluation, data from a 12-week phase II study [4] were combined with the 6-month phase III VANGUARD study [5] and compared with lanadelumab’s 6-month phase III HELP Study [2]. Combining 12-week and 6-month garadacimab data and comparing it with 6-month lanadelumab data favors garadacimab for non-time-normalized endpoints of the proportion of attack-free patients and adverse events, as the longer a patient is in a study the more likely they are to experience an event.

On NMA methodology, the authors conducted both random-effects and fixed-effects models in their analysis, basing their conclusions on relative treatment effects solely on the fixed-effects model. In the random-effects model results (presented in supplementary materials of the Walsh et al. NMA [1]), there were no significant differences for any endpoints between garadacimab and lanadelumab at Q2W or Q4W. The fixed-effects model, selected given the sparsity of networks, assumes a single true effect across all studies. This is implausible in studies of patients with hereditary angioedema, given the heterogeneity of study designs, endpoint definitions, and dosing. The authors do not discuss that their analysis of the two models showed that the random-effects model better fitted the data, with meaningfully lower deviance information criteria and residual deviance. This finding is unsurprising given that the widely cited paper by Borenstein et al. describes why “in the vast majority of meta-analyses, the random-effects model would be the more appropriate choice” [6]. In the fixed-effects model, the only significant difference between garadacimab and lanadelumab Q2W was the time-normalized number of moderate or severe attacks. However, definitions of attack severity were not the same in the garadacimab VANGUARD and lanadelumab HELP studies. In HELP, a mild attack was defined as one that required no medical intervention, while in VANGUARD, attacks that required treatment with medications such as analgesics could be categorized as mild. Thus, the same attack profile could have been categorized as being of moderate severity in HELP but mild in VANGUARD, which undermines the core NMA assumption of consistency, precluding comparison between studies.

The authors conclude that “overall, garadacimab ranked as the most probable effective treatment among all comparators assessed, with lanadelumab Q2W or subcutaneous C1INH ranking second, across most outcomes,” based on the probability of being best (p-best) and Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking curves. However, such rankings do not consider the absolute magnitude or clinical relevance of the probable difference between treatments. For example, the authors calculated that the p-best for the primary endpoint of time-normalized number of attacks was 73% for garadacimab, but the uncertainty of the effect of garadacimab relative to lanadelumab (95% credible interval 0.58–1.32) shows the difference between the two is not statistically significant. Furthermore, mean monthly attack rates in VANGUARD for garadacimab and HELP for lanadelumab Q2W were very similar at 0.27 (95% confidence interval 0.05–0.49) and 0.26 (95% confidence interval 0.14–0.46), respectively, questioning the clinical relevance of any differences in the probabilities.

Finally, the authors claim their report adheres to the PRISMA-NMA standards [7], but there are several areas where this is not the case. For example, the authors do not provide simple summary data for each intervention group and treatment outcome, which would have demonstrated the similarity of clinical outcomes between lanadelumab Q2W in HELP and garadacimab in VANGUARD. Nor did the authors provide any information on the public availability of datasets, statistical code, or model specifications required by the PRISMA 2020 statement [8], preventing independent validation of the reported findings. Bayesian methods, as applied in the NMA, have been criticized for allowing subjectivity, as the results can depend on the choice of prior distributions. To minimize this, it is considered best practice to pre-specify the priors used for key model parameters (e.g., treatment effects), especially with small sample sizes, where priors can have a stronger influence. The authors do not report their choice of prior distributions, preventing assessments of how the results might have been affected by this aspect of the modeling.

As the authors note, “multiple sources of evidence such as trial data, patient preferences, and clinician expertise, must be considered alongside comparative efficacy estimates from indirect treatment comparisons.” Lanadelumab has been available since 2018, and there is a wealth of longer term effectiveness data from single-arm real-world studies that cannot be included using the methodology and narrow parameters selected by Walsh et al. We believe the limitations we identified in the NMA raise concerns about the appropriateness, validity, and credibility of the conclusions drawn regarding different long-term prophylaxis treatments, specifically the comparison between lanadelumab and garadacimab, and particularly lanadelumab Q4W.
