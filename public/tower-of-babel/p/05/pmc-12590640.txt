The stronger predictive role of early life exposures versus current lifestyle behaviors on the development of childhood severe obesity and metabolic dysfunction
Authors: MengYan Tang, Yuan Li, XiaoMei Liu, Ping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent studies tend to stress on the overall prevention of pediatric obesity, whereas few of them focus on effective measures of preventing diagnosed obesity from deteriorating into severe obesity and from developing cardio-metabolic comorbidities.ObjectiveThis study was conducted to thoroughly analyze: (i) the cardio-metabolic characteristics, and (ii) the importance ranking and warning model of risk factors of metabolic disorder in children and adolescents with severe obesity.MethodsThis is designed as a nested cohort study with data sourced from an ongoing Southwest China Pediatric Obesity Prevention and Control Study (SCPOPCS). Children and adolescents with obesity aged 3–18 years were enrolled. Anthropometric indices, vital signs, metabolic laboratory tests, and self-administrated questionnaire were collected at first outpatient visit.Results236 cases of children and adolescents with obesity were enrolled (176 mild; 60 severe). Results showed that systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and heart rate, as well as weight, height, BMI, body circumferences, triceps and abdominal skinfold were significantly higher in children with severe obesity. Besides, a remarkably higher prevalence of metabolically unhealthy obesity was shown in children with severe obesity. Moreover, five independent contributing risk factors of severe obesity were derived from multivariate analyses, namely: Allergy diagnosed before 3 years old (aOR 2.696; 95% CI 1.350–5.436; p = 0.005), Pregnancy complicated with obesity (aOR 3.881; 95% CI 1.213–12.471; p = 0.021), Pregnancy complicated with cholestasis (aOR 4.641; 95% CI 1.141–19.582; p = 0.031 ), Frequency of siesta per week (aOR 1.773; 95% CI 1.177–2.693; p = 0.006 ) and Frequency of dining out per week (aOR 1.804; 95% CI 1.040–3.144; p = 0.036), as well as one independent protective factor, which was Bedtime on weekday nights (aOR 0.571; 95% CI 0.339–0.938; p = 0.030).ConclusionsThis study attempted to understand the physiological changes induced by severe obesity, and we found that severe obesity had higher risk of metabolic syndromes, including especially hypertension, abnormal glucose metabolism, fatty liver and abnormal uric acid metabolism. A predictive model with good fitting degree was built, from which we derived five independent risk factors and one protective factor of severe obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06045-1.Keywords: Severe obesity, Children, Cardio-metabolic characteristics, Random forest model, Nomogram

Introduction

Severe obesity in childhood and adolescence is now recognized as a distinct clinical entity, posing markedly greater and often less reversible health risks than moderate obesity. Children and adolescents with a body-mass index (BMI) at or above 120% of the age- and sex-specific 95th percentile face significantly higher risks of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and seven-fold higher hazard of premature cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in adulthood than that of peers with milder excess weight [1, 2]. Once established, the structural and metabolic damage caused by severe obesity—such as myocardial hypertrophy or persistent insulin resistance—can be partially irreversible, underscoring the need for prevention before such end-organ injuries occur [3, 4].

Childhood obesity continues to increase globally and nationally. According to national surveillance data from 2015 to 2019, the prevalence of obesity among Chinese children and adolescents aged 6 to 17 years was 7.9% [5]. In addition, a large cross-sectional study (PRODY Study, 2017–2019) involving over 200,000 students from 11 provinces reported that approximately one-third (29.0%) of children with obesity met the criteria for severe obesity [6]. This indicates a substantial and accelerating shift toward more extreme forms of obesity in the pediatric population. Nevertheless, most preventive programs and epidemiological studies still treat childhood obesity as a homogeneous condition, offering little guidance on how to prevent progression from mild to severe forms or to identify those who are most likely to develop serious cardiometabolic complications. A critical gap persists in robust early-warning tools that integrate prenatal exposures with early-life and current behaviors, which represents an unmet need particularly pressing for Chinese pediatric populations, whose risk profiles may diverge from those reported elsewhere.

Given the grave disease burden caused by severe pediatric obesity, it is vital to establish a targeted clinical management mode and develop public healthcare policies by defining the adverse outcomes of severe obesity and identifying its potential risk factors, and also to prevent severe obesity and its cardio-metabolic comorbidities from developing among the vast numbers of children and adolescents with mild obesity. The present nested cohort study addresses this gap by (i) delineating the cardio-metabolic profile of Chinese children and adolescents with severe obesity, and (ii) ranking and modelling the key predictors of severe obesity and metabolic dysfunction. Using detailed perinatal data, early-life health histories and contemporaneous lifestyle information, we applied multivariable logistic regression and a random-forest algorithm to quantify the relative contributions of early exposures versus current behaviours. By clarifying which factors carry the greatest predictive weight and by providing an easily interpretable risk-warning model, we aim to inform targeted surveillance and timely intervention for children at highest risk of deteriorating into severe obesity and its often intractable complications.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We designed a nested case-control study with data sourced from an ongoing Southwest China Pediatric Obesity Prevention and Control Study (SCPOPCS) based on the ‘Pediatric Obesity Clinic at West China Second Hospital, Sichuan University’. This cohort study enrolls children and adolescents with obesity aged 3–18 years through outpatient service in accordance with inclusive and exclusive criteria under the principle of informed consent. Participants with their parents will receive follow-up visits every 3 months and acquire intervening measures including personalized prescriptions of exercise and diet through an intelligent obesity management platform developed by West China Second Hospital, Sichuan University. Diagnoses of children with obesity aged 3–5 years and 6–18 years are respectively based on the BMI reference values of cut-off points published in ‘the Growth Curves of the Body Mass Index of Chinese Children and Adolescents aged 0–18 years’ [7], and on China’s Health Standards (WS/T 586–2018) of ‘Overweight and Obesity Screening of School-age Children and Adolescents’ [8]. Children under following conditions are excluded from the cohort: (i) diagnosed with secondary obesity, which is caused by central nervous system diseases, or by endocrine metabolic diseases (Cushing’s syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome, hypothalamic-pituitary disease, etc.); (ii) diagnosed with drug-induced obesity, which is caused by Glucocorticoids, antiepileptic drugs, and antipsychotic drugs (clozapine, olanzapine, etc.). For this nested study, participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling method. The data for this study was collected from the May 2022 to October 2023. All patients who met the inclusion criteria from the parent SCPOPCS cohort from May 2022 to October 2023who met the inclusion criteria during this period were invited to participate. This resulted in a final sample size of 236 cases. Given that the sample size was determined by the total number of eligible patients enrolled within a defined time frame from an ongoing cohort, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. These 236 participants were then evaluated for their demographic characteristics, anthropometric indices, vital signs, metabolic laboratory indices, self-reported personal/family medical history, and lifestyles concerning diet, sleep, exercise, and environment.

Anthropometric indices, vital signs measurements and metabolic laboratory tests

Anthropometric indices, vital signs measurements and metabolic laboratory tests were performed during first outpatient service. Anthropometric indices and vital signs were measured by nurses of the children’s healthcare department. Blood samples were collected by nurses of the clinical laboratory department in the pediatric clinic and were preserved by the biology laboratory. Abdominal ultrasonic inspections were conducted by technicians of the ultrasonic department. Anthropometric indices included heights, Weights, upper arm, chest, waist and hip circumferences, as well as triceps and abdominal skinfold. Vital signs included systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and heart rate. Measurement method is detailed in the supporting information 1[9]. Laboratory tests included abdominal ultrasonic inspections (hepatic/biliary/pancreatic), blood routine, electrolyte, hepatic and renal function, lipid metabolism, serum insulin level, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and insulin growth factor 1 (IGF-1).

Case definition

Class of obesity was the primary outcome of our study. 236 cases of pediatric obesity diagnosed by Chinese standards in this cohort were classified as being ‘obese’ or ‘severely obese’. Being obese was also defined as class I obesity, while being severely obese defined as class II or class III obesity. Detailed definitions include:

Class I obesity: BMI at or above 95th percentile to less than 120% of the 95th percentile.

Class II: BMI at or above120% to less than 140% of the 95th percentile, or BMI at or above 35 kg/m2.

Class III: BMI at or above 140% of the 95th percentile, or BMI at or above 40 kg/m2.

Other definitions involved in this study include:

I)Impaired fasting blood glucose: fasting blood gulcose (FBG) ≥ 5.6mmol/L; impaired glucose tolerance: 2 h postprandial blood glucose (2hPBG) ≥ 7.8mmol/L and<11.1mmol/L; type 2 diabetes: FBG ≥ 7.0mmol/L or 2hPBG ≥ 11.1mmol/L; insulin resistance: homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)>3.

II)Prehypertension: systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ P90 and< P95; hypertension: systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ P95.

III)Dyslipidemia: triglyceride (TG) ≥ 1.47mmol/L; abnormal lipid metabolism: high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)<1.03mmol/L, or non-HDL ≥ 3.76mmol/L, or TG ≥ 1.47mmol/L.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): fatty liver indicated by liver imaging, and alanine transaminase (ALT) ≥ 1.5 × (60U/L) for over 3 months.

VI)Metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO): diagnosed by obesity with any one of the following cardio metabolic alterations - abnormal glucose metabolism, hypertension, abnormal lipid metabolism, abnormal purine metabolism, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; metabolically healthy obesity (MHO): obesity with none of the above cardio metabolic alterations; metabolic syndrome (Mets): central obesity above the age of 10 with any two of the following cardio metabolic alterations: abnormal glucose metabolism, hypertension and abnormal lipid metabolism.

Metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO): diagnosed by obesity with any one of the following cardio metabolic alterations - abnormal glucose metabolism, hypertension, abnormal lipid metabolism, abnormal purine metabolism, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; metabolically healthy obesity (MHO): obesity with none of the above cardio metabolic alterations; metabolic syndrome (Mets): central obesity above the age of 10 with any two of the following cardio metabolic alterations: abnormal glucose metabolism, hypertension and abnormal lipid metabolism.

Data collection and ethics

The data for this study was collected from May 2022 to October 2023. We derived risk factors of the outcomes from data of medical history and lifestyles. After signing informed consents, parents of recruited patients used smart phones to register in an intelligent obesity management platform developed by West China Second Hospital, Sichuan University. Research nurses added their IDs to the platform after an audit, before the platform sent a self-administered questionnaire for the parents to complete containing 15 questions on demographic characteristics, 24 items on personal/family medical history, and 35 items on the child’s lifestyles of diet, sleep, exercise, and environment. The questionnaire was completed on the spot, during which the research nurses were available for consultation but not allowed to induce desired responses. Data of the questionnaires were saved on this intelligent obesity management platform. This study has been approved for ethical review by West China Second Hospital, Sichuan University (ID:2013YFS0034). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study. We certify that the study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Statistical analyses

We investigated 15 demographic characteristics, 13 vital signs/anthropometric indices, 11 metabolic laboratory indices, 24 variables of personal/family medical history and 35 variables of lifestyles in our study. Among them, we excluded 4 variables due to missing data of over 30%, which were ‘introduction of complementary food during infancy’ in personal/family medical history, and ‘daily milk consumption in recent 3 months’, ‘usual speed of eating’, ‘usual frequency of defecation’ in lifestyles. For missing data, we used the R (4.3.2 version) package “mice” to impute by using a predictive mean matching (PMM) method. We applied a descriptive analysis and a difference analysis to the ultimate 15 demographic characteristics, 13 vital signs/anthropometric indices and 11 metabolic laboratory indices and conducted an analysis of risk factors on 23 variables of personal/family medical history and 33 variables of lifestyles. In the descriptive analysis, we described continuous variables by averages and standard deviations, and categorical variables by case numbers and percentages. We performed independent t-test and Chi-square test to take the difference of demographic characteristics, vital signs/anthropometric indices and metabolic lab indices between children of obesity and severe obesity. We applied a univariate logistic regression to screen out statistically significant variables, which were then included in the multivariate regression to obtain independent risk factors of children’s severe obesity. This two-step logistic regression approach was chosen for statistical inference and to identify a parsimonious set of predictors. Furthermore, we put the independent risk factors into the random forest regression to assess their relative importance in prediction and to build a predictive nomogram for visualization. To verify the nomogram we obtained the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve and the calibration curve. Ultimately, to assess the robustness of our results, we employed a sensitive analysis using the patient-specific propensity score matching method (PS-matching), where age- and sex-specific patients were matched at a ratio of 1:2. All P values were calculated as two-sided. Except that a significance level of 0.1 was used in the univariate logistic regression analysis to select the variables for the multivariate logistic regression, a significance level of 0.05 was used for all other statistical tests. All statistical analyses were performed by R software.

Discussion

Cardio-metabolic characteristics in children and adolescents with severe obesity

In the variation analysis on the vital signs, anthropometric indices and metabolic laboratory indices of children’s obesity and severe obesity, we found that waist circumferences and waist/height ratios were significantly larger in the group with severe obesity. Waist/height ratio ≥ 0.5 is normally used as the clinical marker of central obesity[10], and our results showed that among the Chinese children and adolescents aged 3–18 enrolled in this study, severe obesity has a tendency towards central obesity. Latest research has verified that abdominal and visceral fat is associated with cardiovascular disease risks [11]. Therefore, when making clinical diagnoses and deciding on medical treatment of obesity, one should no longer take BMI and class of obesity as early warning indicators of children’s metabolic syndromes but should more importantly assess the patient’s body fat distribution. For those with central obesity, early screening and movement therapy ought to be used carefully considering cardiovascular disease risks. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that the group of severe obesity had significantly higher systolic/diastolic blood pressure, and remarkably higher morbidity of hypertension, abnormal glucose metabolism, fatty liver, and abnormal uric acid metabolism than those with obesity, indicating higher risks of cardiac structure alterations, coronary cardiopathy, diabetes, impaired kidney function and impaired liver function for severe obesity. In general, severe obesity in children and adolescents is associated with increased risks of metabolic syndromes.

In recent years, large amounts of data have been obtained about the association between severe obesity and metabolic syndromes in children and adolescents, with the majority of studies concentrating more on the relationship between severe obesity and cardiovascular metabolism risks, which are often embodied by cardiovascular metabolism risk indices including BP, TC, LDL, HDL, TG, FG and HbA1c. Cross-sectional studies have shown that morbidity of hypertension in children with severe obesity is 9%−17%[12–14], while in our study, the rate is 16.7%, significantly higher than in their counterparts with obesity. Another cross-sectional study based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (1999–2012) investigated the TC, HDL, LDL, TG, BP and FG of 8579 cases of children and adolescents aged 3–19. The linear model constructed in the study revealed that when variables of age, race and sex were controlled, children with obesity class III had lower HDL, and higher systolic/diastolic blood pressure and TG than those with obesity class I, which was consistent with the study’s original hypothesis that severe obesity is associated with higher prevalence of cardiovascular metabolism risks [14]. Thereafter, a systematic evaluation included 60 original studies to explore the association between severe obesity and cardiovascular disease prevalence in children and adolescents, and its point estimation indicated that in children with severe obesity, the incidence of at least one cardiovascular metabolism risk indicator was 67%−86%[1]. In our study, instead of presenting the specific values of the cardiovascular metabolism risk indicators (TC, LDL, HDL, TG, FG etc.), we identified in participants hypertension, abnormal glucose metabolism and dyslipidemia, and explored their association with obesity class, and the results still indicated higher prevalence of hypertension and abnormal glucose metabolism in the group with severe obesity, despite the usage of relatively generalized indicators. A similar study to ours worked on the relationship between obesity class IV and metabolic indicators by identifying Cardiovascular Risk Factor Cluster (CVRFC), and the results showed that during follow-up visits, obesity class IV was associated with increased CVRFC (OR 17.26; p < 0.001), and a similar degree of association was also found in obesity class III (OR 12.26; p < 0.001) [7].

Previous basic experiments have found that insulin resistance appears to have an important influence in the physiological changes caused by severe obesity [15]. Insulin resistance can lead to reduced production of glycogen in liver, causing impaired glucose homeostasis, which therefore increases the output of glucose in liver, synthesis of the proinflammatory cytokines and secretion of the triglyceride by liver, as well as reduces the HDL and adds to LDL [16, 17]. In accordance with these results, our study revealed a significantly higher proportion of insulin resistance among severe obesity, indicating an increased risk of insulin resistance and more complicated physiological changes of liver, muscle and adipose tissue brought about by severe obesity in children and adolescents. The correlation between severe obesity and pathology of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes has been specified in various studies [18, 19]. One study found that with the class of obesity in children deteriorating from overweight to obesity class III, the incidence of prediabetes increased dramatically, coming to its peak in those with obesity classes II and III, and thus proposed the necessity of early screening of prediabetes in children with severe obesity [18]. It’s also worth noting that previous studies have verified the positive correlation between prevalence of NAFLD and obesity [20], which is consistent with our results that children’s fatty liver is associated with abnormal metabolism of glucose and lipoprotein, hinting that fatty liver is also one of the manifestations of metabolic syndromes [21].

Unfortunately, there is a paucity of research on the risk factors of severe obesity. The impact of severe obesity on children’s body systems is much more complicated, grave and far-reaching, and therefore more relevant studies are needed to identify the risk factors of severe obesity in children and adolescents, and to establish risk models, which are expected to provide reliable basis for developing early prevention measures, establishing clinical management models and guiding graded prevention measures.

Risk factors for cardio-metabolic abnormalities in severe obesity and the predictive models

Causes of obesity are perplexing, promoted by a series of policies including international conflicts, trade and economic development and sociocultural norms from a macro point of view, which influence individuals through specific measures concerning people’s livelihood including urbanization process, architectural environment, food system and ecological development; from an individual point of view, in-utero exposure, genetic susceptibility, obesogenic genes, psychosocial factors, education and income, early-life experience, dietary habits, nutrition, physical activity etc. are all contributing factors of obesity [22]. To further identify the risk factors of severe obesity in children and adolescents, we divided these factors into 4 classes: Personal and Family History, Lifestyle - Diet, Lifestyle - Sleep, Lifestyle - Exercise, and Lifestyle - Environment. We conducted a nested-cohort study to establish the risk model of severe obesity and used the random forest model as well as a visualized nomogram to rank the importance of these severe obesity related factors.

One of the intriguing outcomes of our study was that, according to the random forest model, the top two contributing factors of severe obesity came from the class of Lifestyle: Sleep. Specifically, earlier bedtime was a protective factor of severe obesity, while siesta habit turned out to be a risk factor. Previous studies have demonstrated a certain relationship between sleep deprivation and obesity. Melyssa Roy et al. [23] conducted a cohort study on the correlation between bedtime and Z-scores of BMI or obesity risk in children aged 2–5 years, and they found that every one hour later in bedtime, the OR of obesity were respectively 1.05 (95% CI 1.003-1.100) and 1.35 (95% CI 1.080–1.690) in children aged 3 years and 5 years, and that longer sleep duration within 24 h was associated with lower risk of obesity at the age of 3. Similarly, our results also revealed a potential correlation between earlier bedtime and milder class of obesity, indicating that sleeping early is a protective factor of severe obesity. Various studies have analyzed this phenomenon, coming to an agreement that earlier bedtime often indicates reduced food consumption in the evenings and that regular schedule prevents excessive calorie intake, which therefore reduces the incidence of obesity and severe obesity [24, 25]. Nevertheless, existing studies more often focus on the relation between obesity and sleep deprivation such as insufficient overall sleep duration and late bedtime, instead of looking into the impact of siesta habit, and relevant studies in children and adolescents are even more rare. A systematic evaluation on the relationship between siesta and obesity included 12 original studies based in the UK, Spain, the US and China [26], and the summarized analysis demonstrated that people with siesta habit had higher risk of obesity (OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.10–1.35; p ˂0.001). Moreover, subgroup analyses based on age in the US population showed that siesta added to the risk of obesity for both adults and children, with the prevalence significantly rising in those who nap for over one hour in the afternoon. Our results again verified that frequent afternoon-naps tended to increase the severity of obesity in children and adolescents. Admittedly, siesta habit is beneficial for balancing children’s energy spent during the day and serves as a supplement for overall sleep duration, but meanwhile siesta also activates the sympathetic nerve [27], and excessive siesta is related to elevated cortisol levels at night, possibly facilitating insulin resistance, causing dyslipidemia and abnormal fat distribution, therefore promoting obesity [28]. Unfortunately, current research fails to provide the optimal siesta duration for children of different ages, which subsequent studies are expected to lay more stress on.

Dietary lifestyle is another crucial risk factor of obesity. In our study, dining out is the third most important risk factor of severe obesity in children with obesity, i.e. the risk of severe obesity rises with the frequency of dining out. Other studies have acquired similar results. Wei et al. found that dining out no less than once a day but less than twice a day is positively correlated with obesity [29]. In the China Nutrition and Health Survey (CNHS) (2015), the frequency of dining out was 14–21 times per week for men aged 18–59, whose risk of obesity was 1.8 times that of those who do not dine out [30]. Dining out is referred to as dining at places other than home, including fast food restaurants, cafeterias, convenient stores or snack stalls. However whether dining at canteens in schools or at work counts still remains controversial. With the development of China’s economy, family dining out is highly prevalent. One monitoring report based on CNHS (2015–2017) revealed that the proportion of residents above 6 years old who dined out in the past week was up to 46.3% among 150,682 subjects from 2015 to 2017, indicating that the habit of dining out have fitted in common people’s lifestyle, no longer being a minor practice [31]. The reason that excessive dining out leads to increased risk of obesity lies in the fact that when dining out, people tend to take in larger amount of food, and the energy density of the food varieties being consumed is usually higher than when dining at home, and as the energy obtained from food exceeds that consumed by the body, fat accumulation occurs. Meanwhile, consumption of sugar-containing drinks is a common practice during dining out, which can also induce obesity [32]. So far, dietary intervention remains to be the primary preventive measure against obesity in children and adolescents. The Chinese government has come to realize the vital importance of balanced nutritional intake and healthy dietary behaviour in preventing pediatric obesity and severe pediatric obesity, and has issued a series of comprehensive policies including The Program for Child Health in China (2021–2030), The Healthy Children Action Plan (2018–2020): Child Nutrition Improvement Actions and The Civil Nutrition Plan (2018–2030) to raise domestic awareness of a healthy diet and promote a healthy and balanced diet in schools.

Additionally, we found that in individual and family medical history, risk factors of severe obesity included allergy history, maternal pregnancy combined with obesity and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy. Significant correlation has been verified in previous research between obesity and asthma [33], but there are other allergic diseases including allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, and cow’s milk protein allergy. One study used Mendelian randomization to analyze the genetic relationships between class of obesity and various allergic diseases [34]. It was discovered in the study that there was causality between children’s obesity and childhood allergic history (under 16 years old), allergic asthma and atopic dermatitis. Specifically, obesity classes I and II were significantly correlated with childhood allergic history (under 16 years old), obesity class I was associated with allergic asthma, and obesity class III with atopic dermatitis. We found a relation between severe obesity and diagnoses of allergic diseases before 3 years of age. Concerning the mechanisms of allergic diseases that cause obesity, current research has inferred that it is obesity that alters the activated state of immunocytes and enhances the generating of inflammatory factors and eosinophile granulocytes, therefore resulting in allergic diseases induced by immune system malfunction in children [35]. Besides, many studies have focused on maternal factors associated with children’s obesity, including maternal weight, gestational weight gain and gestational diabetes mellitus. A systematic review concluded the existing evidence of gestational weight gain and offspring’s long-term outcome, stating that excessive gestational weight gain was correlated with heavier offspring’s childhood weight and higher BMI value, and also influenced offspring’s early adulthood BMI value and systolic pressure [36]. However, while few studies have paid attention to gestational weight gain’s relationship with severe obesity in offspring, our study confirmed the conclusion that pregnancy combined with obesity was an independent risk factor of severe obesity in offspring, which filled the vacancy of previous studies. Another unexpected discovery of our study was that intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) also served as a risk factor of severe obesity in offspring. ICP, a complicated gestational metabolic disease, is associated with hypercholesteremia and dyslipidemia of the mother’s body, but little has been known about how ICP affects offspring’s metabolism. A birth cohort survey based in northern Finland from 1985 to 1986 demonstrated that, among the offspring of mothers diagnosed with ICP, boys’ BMI value and girls’ waist and hip circumferences tended to increase, compared with those of mothers without comorbidities in pregnancy [37]. The research group further studied the effects of maternal cholestasis on offspring’s adulthood metabolism in mice, finding that severe obesity, diabetic phenotype and liver steatosis occurred in female mice with maternal cholestasis, while none of these were found in those of the control group which were not exposed to cholestasis in utero [37]. Both human and animal experiments have indicated that ICP may influence offspring’s metabolic regulation through lipid metabolism pathway, thereby inducing the risk of metabolic diseases. Nevertheless, current research is insufficient about the relationship between ICP and obesity in offspring, of which the influence mechanism is yet unclear, and further exploration on such topics remains to be made in the future.

Potential application and further validation

The predictive model developed in this study may assist in the early identification of children with obesity who are at elevated risk of progressing to severe obesity and related metabolic disorders. In clinical settings, the key predictors identified—such as early-onset allergic conditions, maternal pregnancy complications, sleep habits, and frequency of dining out—are variables that can be routinely assessed during pediatric consultations. These factors could be incorporated into structured risk assessment tools to support individualized management and timely intervention. In public health practice, the model may be useful in guiding targeted screening efforts and resource allocation within school-based or community-based obesity prevention programs. Nevertheless, before being translated into clinical or public health application, the model requires further validation in independent, multi-center cohorts, and its predictive performance should be assessed in prospective, real-world settings.

Strength and limitations

This study contributes to the growing body of literature by specifically addressing the clinical and metabolic distinctions between mild and severe obesity in children and adolescents, a topic that remains insufficiently explored. Using data derived from an integrated electronic information system, we examined the cardio-metabolic characteristics of severe pediatric obesity and constructed a data-driven early warning model incorporating both early-life and current lifestyle risk factors. The combination of logistic regression and random forest modeling allowed us to assess variable importance while reducing model complexity. Admittedly, this study has limitations. The relatively small sample size may limit the stability and generalizability of our predictive models. To mitigate this, we employed a two-step approach, using logistic regression to first select a small number of potent predictors before applying the random forest model. The robustness of these predictors was further confirmed in a sensitivity analysis using propensity score matching, which added the credibility of our findings. Other limitations include a cohort follow-up duration that is not yet sufficiently long and the exclusion of four variables due to a high rate of missing data. However, with the growth of our cohort, further analyses will continue to be conducted to persistently explore the impact of severe obesity on children and adolescents.

Conclusion

We conducted this nested-cohort study to understand the physiological changes induced by severe obesity in children and adolescents, and we found that severe obesity had higher risk of metabolic syndromes, including especially hypertension, abnormal glucose metabolism, fatty liver and abnormal uric acid metabolism. At the same time, we used the random forest model to find that the risk factors of severe obesity in children and adolescents were frequency of taking nap per week, allergic history before 3 years old, frequency of dining out per week, pregnancy combined with obesity and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy ordered by importance. Besides, we also conducted a sensitive analysis using the PS-matching method where age- and sex-specific patients were matched at a ratio of 1:2, and the outcome was almost consistent with the original results. As the prevalence of severe obesity rises increasingly, we hope that our results can lead more researchers to realize the profound adverse impacts by severe obesity on children and adolescents, and to formulate practical and effective interventions to prevent severe obesity according to these meaningful risk factors explored in our study.
