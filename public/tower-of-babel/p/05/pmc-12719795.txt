Impact of standardized team‐based care on cardiogenic shock outcomes over time
Authors: Behnam N Tehrani, Carolyn M Rosner, Adetokunbo Fadahunsi, Kimberly Barker, Wayne B Batchelor, Vanessa Blumer, Lindsay Clevenger, Shashank Desai, Michelle Ferri, Evan Franke, Jamie Kennedy, Christopher King, Edward Howard, Iyad Isseh, Seiyon Ben Lee, Aditya Mehta, Mitchell Psotka, Ramesh Singh, Shashank S Sinha, Matthew W Sherwood, Daniel Tang, Megan Terek, Alexander G Truesdell, Ilan Vavilin, Christopher M O'Connor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsLimited data exist regarding the impact of standardized care on cardiogenic shock (CS) outcomes over time. We aimed to investigate the influence of multidisciplinary management on CS outcomes following implementation of team‐based protocols in 2017.MethodsA total of 1453 consecutive patients enrolled into a single‐centre CS registry were divided into two time periods, 2017–2019 versus 2020–2022. Baseline characteristics, management and outcomes were compared. The primary endpoint was adjusted in‐hospital mortality. Multivariable regression analysis was performed to evaluate change in outcomes over time.ResultsCompared with 2017–2019, more patients with CS were treated in 2020–2022 (930 vs. 523; P < 0.01). They more often presented to the Level 1 centre (52% vs. 45%; P = 0.01), with a higher proportion of heart failure‐related CS (72% vs. 58%; P < 0.01) and Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions B and C CS (64% vs. 49%; P < 0.01). They were less likely to be managed with percutaneous ventricular assist devices (11% vs. 24%; P < 0.01) but more likely to receive veno‐arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (14% vs. 4%; P < 0.01) and heart transplantation (9% vs. 4%; P < 0.04). No differences were noted in in‐hospital mortality [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 0.81; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.56–1.16; P = 0.25], major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (aOR 1.21; 95% CI: 0.87–1.68; P = 0.26), stroke (aOR 1.11; 95% CI: 0.65–1.91; P = 0.71) or renal replacement therapy (aOR 0.95; 95% CI: 0.66–1.37; P = 0.77).ConclusionsStandardized care for CS was associated with consistent in‐hospital mortality over time despite changes in presentation and management. Further research is needed to identify the optimal care model during the vulnerable post‐discharge period.Keywords: acute myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock, heart failure, systems of care, team‐based care, temporal trends

Introduction

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a time‐sensitive and highly morbid syndrome characterized by profound haemometabolic derangements and multi‐organ system dysfunction. 1 Despite advances in temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) and the evolution of contemporary cardiac intensive care units (CICUs), the management of CS remains variable, with in‐hospital mortality ranging from 30% to 40%. 2 , 3 CS is also heterogeneous, encompassing a broad spectrum of aetiologies, including acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and heart failure (HF), with varying severities of critical illness and comorbidities. 4 Recent data from dedicated CS registries suggest that the implementation of team‐based protocols may be associated with enhanced short‐term survival. 5 , 6 Accordingly, the 2021 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for the Treatment of Acute and Chronic Heart Failure recommend that the care of patients with CS be centralized at advanced HF centres with multidisciplinary expertise. 7 Whether the benefits of multidisciplinary shock care can be sustained over time, however, remains unknown. Our aim was to evaluate the impact of standardized management for CS over a 6 year period following the implementation of team‐based protocols in a large, contemporary single‐centre registry. We also characterize the changes in clinical presentation, management and short‐term outcomes of these patients during this time frame.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective analysis included 1453 consecutive CS patients treated by our multidisciplinary shock team in our quaternary care medical centre between 3 January 2017 and 31 December 2022, following implementation of team‐based protocols. Criteria for diagnosis of CS, due to either AMI‐related (AMI‐CS) or HF‐related CS (HF‐CS), are in Figure 1. 8 We excluded patients who did not have CS as the primary clinical diagnosis, if the shock state was not due to AMI‐CS or HF‐CS or if they were transferred from our hub centre to another facility within 24 h of arrival. The Inova shock team composition, protocols and regional CS network have been described previously. 5 , 6 Briefly, the Inova CS Initiative was launched in July 2016 following an institutional review of outcomes, which demonstrated a 30 day survival rate of 47%. 5 A multidisciplinary taskforce was formed and developed a standardized protocol for CS identification and management focused on rapid identification, early invasive haemodynamics, tailored treatment and serial reassessment. The Inova CS programme participated in collaborative regional outreaches, and algorithms were shared within our shock network. 6 The main tenets of the initiative remained consistent throughout the study period, with CS protocols updated over time to include CS severity scores, advances in MCS and specific guidance for all aspects of acute care including evaluation and management by aetiology (Figures S1–S4). All patients in the registry were ultimately cared for at the Inova Fairfax Medical Campus, a quaternary care centre with resources and capabilities consistent with an American Heart Association Level 1 CS centre. 6

Data collection, risk stratification and outcomes

The study was approved by the Inova Health System's institutional review board. Data were collected retrospectively via electronic medical records. All patients were assigned a Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) shock score corresponding with disease severity at the time of diagnosis by two members of the Inova CS Team (B.N.T. and C.M.R.). To study the impact of team‐based care on CS outcomes, we studied the potential relationship of time from the implementation of the Inova CS programme and clinical outcomes. The patients were divided into two time‐period cohorts following the employment of our shock programme: 2017–2019 and 2020–2022. The analysis was dichotomized in this way as our initial experience from 2017 to 2019 was previously reported, and our aim was to evaluate the impact of team‐based care over the following 3 years, coinciding with changes to the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) allocation system for orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT), the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic and updates to our institutional protocols. 5 , 6 , 9 The primary outcome was adjusted in‐hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included the following: (1) adjusted 30 day post‐hospital discharge mortality; (2) major bleeding, defined as Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) Type 3a/3b/5 bleeds; (3) vascular access site complications related to MCS; (4) stroke; (5) need for renal replacement therapy (RRT); and (6) major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs), defined as the composite of in‐hospital mortality, major bleeding complications and stroke. 10 , 11

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics are presented as a mean ± standard deviation (SD), median [quartiles (Q1, Q3) with inter‐quartile range], or frequency and per cent, as appropriate. Comparisons were made via Student's t test, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, where appropriate. In‐hospital and 30 day post‐hospital discharge mortality rates were also assessed using adjustment for SCAI severity of illness based on historic probabilities computed by taking the average of the estimated SCAI‐adjusted in‐hospital and 30 day mortality for SCAI stages A–E for patients in the 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 cohorts. 12 , 13 To determine the potential relationship between time period from launch of the Inova CS programme and the primary and secondary outcomes, we fit multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for clinical and haemodynamic variables historically associated with outcomes in CS: AMI‐CS, SCAI CS stage (C vs. D–E), age (per 1 year increments), diabetic status, cardiac arrest, baseline log lactate, right atrial (RA) measurement and any MCS use. 14 An internal sensitivity analysis using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), a measure of goodness of fit with respect to model complexity, suggested that a log transformation of both baseline lactate and cardiac power output (CPO) provided a better model fit rather than raw values, which were incorporated into the statistical model. Risk of the primary and secondary outcomes in the 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 patient cohorts was compared and calculated using the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each endpoint. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05 for two‐sided tests. For the secondary endpoint of vascular access complications, MCS was removed from the regression modelling, as the development of a vascular complication was sensitive to the presence of an MCS device. All statistical analyses were performed using R (4.4.1) software.

For the entire cohort, the median age was 63 years, 71% were male, 43% were diabetic, and the median baseline creatinine was 1.5 mg/dL. Fifty‐one per cent of patients (n = 737) initially presented to a Level 2 or 3 CS centre, and 33% (n = 484) had AMI‐CS. Cardiac arrest occurred in 27% (n = 386) of patients, either at presentation or during their hospitalization. The median baseline left ventricular ejection fraction was 25%. Right heart catheterization was performed in 79% of patients (n = 1145). Forty‐one per cent (n = 597) presented with SCAI shock severity scores of D or E. Baseline haemometabolic evaluations demonstrated a median lactate level of 2.4 mg/dL, a cardiac index of 1.9 L/min/m2, RA pressure of 14 mmHg and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 24 mmHg.

Compared with the 2017–2019 cohort, patients in the 2020–2022 cohort more often presented to the Level 1 CS centre (52 vs. 45%; P = 0.01) with HF‐CS (72% vs. 58%; P < 0.01) and less often had SCAI D or E CS (35% vs. 51%; P < 0.01). Patients in the 2020–2022 cohort were more likely to have vasopressors initiated at the time of shock diagnosis (77% vs. 71%; P = 0.01) and had higher rates of prior cardiac interventions (36% vs. 30%; P = 0.02). The latter cohort had a higher baseline cardiac index (1.9 vs. 1.8 L/min/m2; P = 0.04), pulmonary arterial pulsatility index (1.6 vs. 1.4; P < 0.01) and pulmonary vascular resistance (222 vs. 180 dyn/s/cm2; P < 0.01) and lower systemic vascular resistance (1421 vs. 1555 dyn/s/cm2; P < 0.01). There were 22 patients in the latter group who had COVID‐19 infection complicated by CS.

Clinical management, device utilization and short‐term outcomes

The management and outcomes of patients in the 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 cohorts are shown in Table 2. Further analysis based on AMI‐CS and HF‐CS phenotypes is provided in Table S2, and year‐over‐year trends are illustrated in Figure 2B,C. Among the entire cohort, 45% of patients with CS (n = 650) received a temporary MCS device, with 181 of these patients (28%) receiving more than one device platform. During the 2020–2022 period, there were lower rates of percutaneous ventricular assist device (pVAD) implantation (26% vs. 45%; P < 0.01) but higher rates of veno‐arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA‐ECMO) support (33% vs. 9%; P < 0.01). A stacked bar graph depicting the final highest level of temporizing MCS implant for each time period and among the AMI‐CS and HF‐CS patients is depicted in Figure S5. The patients in the second time period were less likely to receive durable left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) (4% vs. 7%; P = 0.04) but underwent higher rates of OHT (9% vs. 4%; P < 0.01). Among all patients presenting with AMI‐CS, 68% (n = 333) underwent percutaneous coronary intervention, with similar rates in the 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 time periods (68% vs. 68%; P = 0.39).

No differences were noted between the 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 cohorts with regard to adjusted in‐hospital mortality (34% vs. 33%; P = 0.86), adjusted 30 day mortality (36% vs. 34%; P = 0.41), major bleeding (22% vs. 23%; P = 0.56), stroke (8% vs. 8%; P = 1.00), acute kidney injury requiring RRT (21% vs. 18%; P = 0.21), MACCE (44% vs. 42%; P = 0.70) and median total hospital length of stay (11 vs. 12 days; P = 0.42). Lower rates of vascular access complications were seen during the 2020–2022 period (11% vs. 18%; P < 0.01). Among major bleeding events, non‐access site‐related haemorrhagic sequelae occurred more frequently during the latter time period (18% vs. 9%; P < 0.01). The 2017–2019 cohort was more likely to require post‐discharge rehabilitation at an acute care facility (11% vs. 10%; P = 0.02), whereas the 2020–2022 cohort was more often discharged to home (50% vs. 44%; P = 0.02) or to hospice care (7% vs. 6%; P = 0.02).

Multivariable regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression revealed the following independent predictors of in‐hospital mortality among the entire cohort: (1) SCAI shock stage D/E, (2) cardiac arrest, (3) baseline log lactate, (4) diabetes mellitus and (5) age (Figure 3). Clinical presentation and management during 2020–2022 were not associated with an increased adjusted risk for the primary outcome (aOR 0.81; 95% CI: 0.56–1.16; P = 0.25). This finding was consistent for the secondary endpoints of 30 day mortality (aOR 0.99; 95% CI: 0.71–1.39; P = 0.96), stroke (aOR 1.11; 95% CI: 0.65–1.91; P = 0.68), acute kidney injury requiring RRT (aOR 0.95; 95% CI: 0.66–1.3; P = 0.77) and MACCE (aOR 1.21; 95% CI: 0.87–1.68; P = 0.26) (Figures S6–S9). However, the 2020–2022 period was associated with an adjusted high risk for major bleeding (aOR 1.59; 95% CI: 1.11–2.3; P = 0.01) and a lower adjusted risk of vascular access site complications (aOR 0.44; 95% CI: 0.25–0.77; P < 0.01) (Figures S10 and S11).

Discussion

While previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of shock teams with the employment of contemporary practice patterns and enhanced short‐term survival, a critical gap in clinical knowledge has been the temporal durability of this approach. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first and largest single‐centre registry to examine the impact of standardized care on CS outcomes over time. Our main findings are that despite a nearly two‐fold increase in the number of patients presenting to our institution with a primary diagnosis of CS during the 2020–2022 time period, with changes in clinical presentations and associated management strategies, short‐term outcomes were sustained compared with 2017–2019, with no differences in (1) adjusted in‐hospital and 30 day mortalities, (2) stroke, (3) acute kidney injury requiring RRT and (4) MACCE. We further report lower rates of access site‐related bleeding and vascular access complications seen in the latter time period (Graphical Abstract).

With an ageing population and increase in global prevalence of HF, the incidence of CS has increased. 15 There has also been a significant shift in the epidemiology of the disease state, with CS attributed to HF now accounting for >50% of all cases. 16 There are noteworthy distinctions in the characteristics and clinical trajectories of these HF‐CS patients as compared with AMI‐CS, particularly in cases of acute on chronic myocardial dysfunction, where compensatory adaptations may lead to more insidious, ambulatory clinical presentations. 16 In this analysis, the number of HF‐CS patients encountered in 2020–2022 more than doubled versus 2017–2019, with this phenotype of patients accounting for two thirds of the entire registry. HF‐CS patients throughout the study time were more likely to have SCAI severity B/C CS, as compared with the AMI‐CS cohort who more often had SCAI D/E shock. However, the HF‐CS group also had higher RA and pulmonary capillary wedge pressures, systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance, and lower cardiac indices compared with patients with AMI‐CS. These findings highlight the distinctions between the two phenotypes and underscore the importance of early recognition, including invasive haemometabolic assessments, to guide the pheno‐profiling and tailored application of therapies across the dynamic continuum of the shock state. 4

With limited evidence‐based recommendations to guide care, there remain significant regional variations in the management of CS. 17 Level 1 CS centres are more likely to be in high‐population density and urban areas, while most patients with CS initially present to a Level 2 or 3 CS community centre in more rural areas. 17 A volume‐to‐outcomes relationship has been described, with patients undergoing triage at a Level 1 CS centre more likely to have access to contemporary, advanced therapies and at lower risk for in‐hospital morbidity and mortality. 17 A significant focus of our shock team effort was the development of regional collaborations, and we noted that more than one half of our registry patients initially presented to a Level 2 or 3 CS centre. Over time, the number of centres participating in our CS network nearly doubled from the initial cohort to the second, from 34 to 63, encompassing an area spanning over 5900 mi2. Consistent with our protocol, 73% of patients who initially presented to a Level 2 or 3 CS underwent invasive haemodynamic assessment, and importantly, similar to our earlier findings, a sensitivity analysis taking into consideration the site of initial triage demonstrated that for the entire cohort, presentation to a Level 2 or Level 3 centre was not associated with a difference in in‐hospital mortality (aOR 1.09; 95% CI: 0.77–1.55; P = 0.649), MACCE (aOR 0.93; 95% CI: 0.68–1.27; P = 0.67) or 30 day mortality (aOR 0.87; 95% CI: 0.63–1.20; P = 0.39). 6 These findings demonstrate the benefits of developing regional and centralized expertise for CS care as recommended by the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines to offer similar standards of care regardless of the site of initial presentation. 7

MCS devices are increasingly utilized to temporize and optimize the haemodynamics of patients with CS, despite most studies failing to demonstrate a survival benefit with these platforms, particularly intra‐aortic balloon pump (IABP) and VA‐ECMO. 18 , 19 Recently, the US guidelines afforded the use of micro‐axial flow pumps in select cases of ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (MI) complicated by refractory CS, a Class IIa (Level of Evidence: B) recommendation following the Danish‐German Cardiogenic Shock (DanGer Shock) trial, which demonstrated reductions in all‐cause death at 180 days. 3 , 20 However, given the risks for major bleeding complications, limb ischaemia and renal failure associated with these devices, the timing of implantation and how to best integrate them into routine CS care remain uncertain. We observed a decline in device utilization, from 52% in 2017–2019 to 40% in 2020–2022, with platform‐specific differences noted in the two time periods, with greater utilization of pVADs in 2017–2019, primarily in AMI‐CS, and more patients undergoing cannulation for VA‐ECMO during the 2020–2022 period. We postulate several reasons to account for these temporal changes: (1) the marginal haemodynamic benefits of IABPs in AMI‐CS; (2) reduction in the number of transfers from spoke institutions, which often have limited access to advanced MCS; and (3) increased caution with unrestricted use of MCS given the risk for bleeding and vascular complications and practice changes following the revised UNOS OHT allocation system in 2018, with earlier use of biventricular support devices, particularly in HF‐CS patients, as a bridge to cardiac replacement therapies. 3 , 21

Reductions in vascular access site complications were also noted in 2020–2022 versus 2017–2019, although there was a numeric rise in major bleeding, related primarily to an increase in non‐access site‐related haemorrhage (haemoptysis, gastrointestinal bleeding, haematuria and haemothorax following cardiopulmonary resuscitation), reflecting the multi‐organ system insults seen with the shock syndrome. We attribute the decrease in access site complications to institutional multidisciplinary initiatives around patient safety bundles and quality improvement: (1) adoption of algorithms emphasizing selective and tailored deployment of MCS devices; (2) adherence to best practices for large‐bore vascular access, indwelling device management and closure; (3) utilization of parenteral antiplatelet agents in AMI‐CS with rapid pharmacokinetic clearance; and (4) protocols for limb perfusion and device weaning and escalation protocols in the CICU. 22

Lastly, it is important to acknowledge that despite the sustained short‐term outcomes seen across our 6 year experience, CS remains a disease state with high rates of morbidity and mortality beyond the index hospitalization. 16 In addition to disparities with the use of guideline‐directed medical therapies as well as the risk for re‐hospitalization due to myocardial and non‐cardiac organ dysfunction, the recovery period for survivors of CS is fraught with challenges related to impairments in physical ability, cognitive and mental health, and quality of life, collectively termed post‐intensive care syndrome. 16 , 23 , 24 Recent data suggest that CS survivors are susceptible to these multidimensional disabilities up to 5 years following the index event. 25 We similarly noted high rates of post‐discharge adverse sequelae, with 30 day rates of hospital readmission and MACCE of 14% and 52% across the entire registry, respectively. These findings underscore the importance of developing multidisciplinary and longitudinal care models during this equally important but less recognized time period and of addressing the social determinants that may impact the health trajectory of CS survivors. 16 , 23

Limitations

While this large, single‐centre study is unique and advantageous with regard to the scope, duration and granularity of data, we acknowledge several important limitations. First, the study is retrospective and observational and may be inherently limited by selection bias and unmeasured confounding variables. This includes selection bias with potentially earlier disease recognition as our shock protocols matured over time. In turn, this may result in shock diagnosis in the earlier stages of the disease state when patients may be less acutely ill, with reduced haemometabolic derangements, and hence, they may be more salvageable. This could have contributed to a potential Type 2 error. Second, while the ‘all‐comer’ population includes a large number of patients with HF‐CS, we did not differentiate between a de novo occurrence and an acute exacerbation of chronic HF, two entities with distinct presentations and in‐hospital trajectories. 26 Third, we were unable to account for CS patients who presented to a spoke centre but were not transferred to the hub, thus suggesting a potentially unmeasured survival bias among these patients. Fourth, we do not report outcomes beyond 30 days after discharge, and long‐term outcomes in CS survivors remain an area of fertile investigation. Notably, there is a growing body of evidence that survivors of CS have a significant risk for rehospitalization and mortality in the year following a CS event. 16 Fifth, only 2% of patients in the 2020–2022 cohort had concomitant COVID‐19 infection at the time of CS diagnosis, limiting our ability to fully understand the impact of this virus on the clinical trajectory of shock. Although a rare occurrence, CS resulting from COVID‐19 is associated with a five‐fold increased risk for in‐hospital mortality, with multiple mechanisms for cardiac injury. 27 Sixth, among patients with AMI‐CS, we did not differentiate between those with ST‐segment MIs and non‐ST‐segment MIs. While both conditions are associated with poor outcomes, the latter have historically experienced greater short‐term mortality rates due to a higher burden of comorbidities and delays in revascularization. 28 Seventh, despite the sustainability and clinical effectiveness of our team‐based care model for CS, we did not perform an economic evaluation of our shock programme. The care of CS patients is inherently time‐intensive and resource‐intensive, requiring multidisciplinary expertise. An observational analysis from the Utah Shock Registry demonstrated that integrating team‐based protocols for CS was not only clinically superior but also more cost‐effective than standard of care. 29 Lastly, it should be noted that despite the sustainability of our team‐based model for CS care, there remains an opportunity to further improve short‐term survival. Future efforts to better understand the pathophysiological mechanics of more advanced stages of CS (i.e., SCAI D and E) through molecular profiling are warranted, as outcomes in this stage of the disease spectrum remain suboptimal, even with advances in circulatory support platforms.

Conclusions

In this large, contemporary, single‐centre registry of CS patients, we noted that despite increased clinical volumes and changes in shock presentation, phenotypes and treatment strategies, patients cared for from 2017 to 2019 and from 2020 to 2022 experienced similar rates of adjusted in‐hospital and 30 day post‐discharge mortality, MACCE, stroke and need for RRT following the implementation of standardized protocols. These findings highlight the merits and sustainability of team‐based care for CS in the acute phase of disease management. Further research is needed to identify optimal care delivery models to sustain these favourable outcomes beyond the index hospitalization during the vulnerable post‐discharge period.
