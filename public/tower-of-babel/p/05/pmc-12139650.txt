Cardiac Involvement in Chronic Kidney Disease: Epidemiology, Pathophysiology, and Complications
Authors: Eleni Tzatzaki, Matthaios Didagelos, Eleni Karlafti, Christina Melexopoulou, Dimitrios Tsavdaris, Antonios Ziakas, Smaragdi Marinaki, Haralambos Karvounis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
There is a complex interaction between the heart and kidneys. Cardiac and renal function are both affected physically, chemically, and biologically. In end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of mortality. The pathophysiological mechanisms leading to cardiac manifestations in end-stage chronic kidney disease are the decreased oxygen supply to the myocardium, the increased preload, and the left ventricular (LV) afterload alterations. The cardiac complications observed in these patients are LV hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, diastolic dysfunction of the left ventricle, coronary artery disease, calcification of the mitral annulus and the aortic valve, and pulmonary hypertension. Several biomarkers that may lead to prevention and early identification of heart complications in these patients are being investigated. In conclusion, the complex relationship between cardiac and renal functions significantly affects patient outcomes in ESRD, highlighting the urgent need for early recognition and intervention strategies to limit cardiovascular complications and improve overall patient care.

INTRODUCTION

Several studies have shown that patients in the early stages[123] of chronic kidney disease (CKD) are at 25–100 times higher risk of cardiovascular events. In patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of mortality.[1] There is a complex interaction between the heart and kidneys. Heart and renal function are both affected physically, chemically, and biologically.[2] This is because cardiovascular disease and renal failure share common risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes. The activation of cytokines and growth factors leads to endothelial dysfunction and then promotes the development of kidney and heart disease.[3]

PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL MECHANISMS IN CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE LEAD TO IMPAIRED HEART FUNCTION

CKD is characterized by the presence of kidney damage or decreased kidney function (glomerular filtration rate [GFR] <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) for 3 or more months. The presence of abnormal levels of albuminuria is the most frequently assessed marker of kidney damage. Both reduced GFR and albuminuria are more frequent in high-risk patients represented by patients with diabetes mellitus and hypertension.[15] Traditional risk factors of cardiovascular disease, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking and age, contribute to renal failure-related risk factors, such as anemia, volume overload, oxidative stress, inflammation, high parathyroid hormone levels and hypercalcemia, and affect cardiac function, leading to decreased oxygen supply to the myocardium, increased preload and afterload changes[1617181920] [Figure 1].

Pathophysiology and complications of heart disease in chronic kidney disease

Decreased oxygen supply to the myocardium

As LV hypertrophy, hypertension, anemia and coronary heart disease work synergistically, oxygen supply to the myocardium decreases in ESRD patients.[16] Despite the improvement in dialysis treatment and medication, kidney disease still causes abnormal myocardial remodeling.

The main cause of decreased myocardial oxygen supply is LV hypertrophy. It is the most common heart abnormality (80%) seen in patients with CKD before replacement therapy is started. Various factors such as volume overload, hypertension, anemia, hyperparathyroidism, and high concentrations of angiotensin II contribute to the development and worsening of hypertrophy.[16] In the general population, hypertension is the leading cause of LV hypertrophy; however, in patients with ESRD, clinical and experimental studies have shown that hypertrophy can also occur in normotensive patients.[21]

The second most important factor in the reduction of oxygen supply is atherosclerosis, which develops rapidly in patients with ESRD. It is mainly due to uremia, which leads to the formation and circulation of atherosclerotic agents. Secondary dyslipidemia, disturbance of calcium and phosphorus metabolism and activation of the immune system are some of the complications of uremia. These factors work on top of the traditional risk factors of coronary heart disease seen in patients with normal renal function such as age, hypertension, smoking, sex and insulin resistance.[222324] Many vascular disorders can also be observed in patients with CKD although they may not develop clinically significant atherosclerotic disease. Vascular calcification and wall stiffness are also common.[16]

Increased preload

Chronic volume overload, anemia and high flow in arteriovenous anastomosis are the main factors that increase preload.

Most patients with ESRD have no or decreased urination, which leads to an increase in the circulating blood volume between intervals of dialysis sessions.[16] This excess fluid is removed by dialysis in scheduled sessions that usually take place two or three times a week. With dialysis, patients return to their ideal weight.[16] Incorrect calculation of the ideal weight leads to chronic volume overload and has serious consequences for cardiac function.

Anemia and increased flow through the arteriovenous anastomosis are secondary factors that increase preload. Anemia is mainly due to the reduced synthesis of erythropoetin and increases preload through increased venous return. Furthermore, increased sympathetic activity and inotropic factors lead to increased left ventricle contractility.[16]

Left ventricular afterload alterations

Many factors are involved in afterload alterations in various ways. Reduction of the afterload is observed mainly after dialysis, when the patient returns to his ideal body weight. Afterload reduction can also be the result of anemia because it leads to a decrease in vascular resistance.[16]

An increase in the afterload on the other side is due to a number of factors. These include hypertension, atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, sympathetic system stimulation and volume overload observed in the intervals between dialysis sessions.[16]

CARDIAC COMPLICATIONS IN CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE

Among the major complications observed in patients with ESRD is LV hypertrophy. It coexists with LV dilatation and systolic dysfunction in CKD-related cardiomyopathy. It is known that from the early stages of kidney disease, diastolic dysfunction of the left ventricle is observed, and then, as the disease progresses, systolic dysfunction is also observed. Further, in a large percentage of patients, mainly in the final stage, calcification of the mitral ring and aortic valve, pulmonary hypertension and coronary heart disease coexist [Figure 1]. Echocardiography is the gold standard for the assessment of cardiac structures and function.[1617181920]

Left ventricular hypertrophy

LV hypertrophy may be a strong and independent risk factor for cardiovascular events in both the overall population and patients with CKD. A special feature of these patients is that both patterns of hypertrophy, eccentric and concentric, are present. Eccentric hypertrophy, which affects 33% of end-stage patients, occurs due to volume overload, and concentric hypertrophy, which affects 42% of end-stage patients, is a result of hypertension and increased overload.[1617181920] As shown by various studies, the modification of risk factors may inhibit the development of hypertrophy. LV hypertrophy is diagnosed through echocardiography and magnetic resonance imaging of the heart.

Myocardial fibrosis

Another cardiac manifestation of CKD is myocardial fibrosis. It usually presents a widespread distribution with collagen deposition surrounding cardiomyocytes and capillaries leading initially to LV hypertrophy and dilatation as the disease progresses. The extent of LV fibrosis has also been associated with a worse prognosis and increased risk of cardiac death.[2526]

Diastolic dysfunction of the left ventricle

LV diastolic dysfunction affects 50%–60% of the patients with ESRD. The evaluation of LV diastolic dysfunction is crucial, especially in patients with preserved ejection fraction (EF).[27] Tissue Doppler imaging plays a vital role in its diagnosis. However, studies have shown that large preload changes (≥3 L) for adults with ESRD and (≥1.5 L) for adolescents with ESRD affect measurements both for tissue Doppler and pulse wave Doppler, reducing their reliability. For smaller changes, however, tissue Doppler appears to be volume-independent, remains reliable and can be used to assess LV diastolic function.[282930] The ratio of mitral peak velocity of early filling E to early diastolic mitral annular velocity e’ (E/e’) is the most important prognosticator in most cardiovascular disorders, including both systolic and diastolic heart failure, LV hypertrophy, myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, and subclinical myocardial diseases.

Coronary artery disease

Endothelial dysfunction is considered to be one of the mechanisms that lead to atherosclerosis. In patients with kidney disease, the endothelial dysfunction is related to the degree of proteinuria, and it concerns both large and small arteries. However, as mentioned above, in addition to endothelial dysfunction, several factors are associated with atherosclerosis. Coronary atherosclerosis is more severe in ESRD patients. Managing risk factors in the early stages is crucial.[313233]

Microangiopathy is more common than macroangiopathy in patients with ESRD. Thus, studies have shown that nuclear medicine is very important, perhaps more important than invasive cardiology, in the diagnosis of coronary heart disease as a normal coronary angiogram does not rule out the presence of small vessel disease, the diagnosis of which can be made using Thallium-201 myocardial perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography.[28]

Calcification of the mitral annulus and the aortic valve

Valvular disease develops within the presence of inflammation, oxidative stress, diabetes, malnutrition and disturbance of calcium and phosphorus metabolism. Valvular calcification increases with the reduction of GFR. Stenosis and/or regurgitation are present. In CKD patients with aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation, mortality is 50% higher.[3435]

Boninger, in 1908, was the first that described mitral ring calcification as a pathological entity. Calcification of the mitral annulus is observed mainly in patients in the 4th and 5th stage of CKD. The diagnosis can be made even with a simple chest X-ray or during an echocardiographic study. It is 2.3 times more common in patients with CKD than in people of the same age and sex with normal renal function.[27] Functional mitral or tricuspid regurgitation is also common in ESRD on hemodialysis and is related to volume overload and left atrial dilatation.[34]

Aortic valve calcification occurs 10–20 years earlier and is twice as common as in the general population and affects 25%–55% of patients with ESRD. A characteristic of the disease is that it gradually leads to the narrowing of the valve.[36]

Pulmonary hypertension in chronic kidney disease

Pulmonary hypertension remained an unknown disease for several years. In the last few years, significant progress has been made in understanding its mechanism and in diagnosing and treating it. It is worth noting that in the mid-1980s, the life expectancy of patients with pulmonary hypertension was only 2.8 years from the time of diagnosis. Studies and literature on pulmonary hypertension in CKD are very limited. Notably, in 2009, ESRD was included in the list of causes of pulmonary hypertension in the European Society of Cardiology Guidelines.

The disease remains clinically silent, and when the function of the right ventricle is affected, symptoms appear. The time interval from the appearance of the first symptoms until the full development of the disease and its diagnosis can reach up to 3 years. Pulmonary hypertension is generally rare; however, it is common in high-risk groups such as patients with sickle cell disease, systemic sclerosis, or human immunodeficiency virus.[36]

According to some studies, the hormonal and metabolic disorders observed in patients with ESRD lead to vasoconstriction and increased resistance to pulmonary circulation. The main disorders are endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, decreased pulmonary capillary contractility, and hyperparathyroidism.[37383940]

Some researchers observed an increase in pulmonary artery pressures in patients with ESRD and argued that pulmonary arterial hypertension is an independent mortality factor in patients with ESRD.[37]

BIOMARKERS

Biomarkers are proteins as well as other substances that are detected in the blood serum and facilitate many signaling pathways. In heart failure, biomarkers appear to be associated with neurohormonal activation, ventricular dysfunction, cardiac remodeling and myocardial damage.[41] Their levels may also be related to the prognosis of the disease. Biomarkers that reliably provide information on CKD can also be used in the diagnosis and prognosis of heart failure.[41]

N-terminal pro-b-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) is a prognostic indicator of the progression of kidney disease in nondiabetic patients with CKD. Two other peptides that appear to contribute to prognosis are Adrenomedullin (ADM) and A-type natriuretic peptide (ANP).

The mild-to-moderate kidney disease study[42] was a 7-year prospective study involving 177 nondiabetic patients with CKD that revealed an association between disease progression and plasma ANP and ADM levels. The study also showed a continuous increase in the plasma levels of NT-proBNP, and midregional epitopes of ANP and ADM (MR-proANP and MR-proADM) at various stages of CKD.[42] Bosselmann et al.[43] also observed the effect of renal impairment on the prognostic value of biomarkers in patients with systolic heart failure. The study involved 424 patients with LV systolic dysfunction. It was reported that NT-proBNP, pro-ADM, pro-ANP, and high-sensitivity troponin T are associated with an increased risk of mortality and that their prognostic value is not affected by renal impairment.[43] In a recent study, ADM was associated with septal E/e’ in hemodialysis patients with preserved LV EF. In the same study, ADM represents an independent predictor of diastolic dysfunction in ESRD patients on hemodialysis.[27]

Identifying high-risk patients remains a challenge for the medical community. Biomarkers may lead to the prevention and early identification of heart complications in CKD patients.

CONCLUSION

CKD is closely associated with a high risk of cardiovascular complications, which are the leading cause of mortality in ESRD. The most common cardiac complications in patients with CKD include LV hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, diastolic dysfunction, coronary artery disease, and valvular calcifications. Pathophysiological mechanisms such as reduced myocardial oxygen delivery, increased preload, and afterload changes contribute to these complications. Biomarkers are being investigated for early detection and management of cardiovascular risks. Understanding the interaction between CKD and heart disease is critical to improving patient outcomes and reducing cardiovascular-related mortality.
