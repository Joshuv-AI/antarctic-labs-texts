Clinical characteristics and risk factors for mortality in pulmonary mucormycosis among patients with non-hematologic malignancies
Authors: Renwen Zhang, Yongzhe Liu, Ran Li, Li Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary mucormycosis (PM) is a life-threatening fungal infection typically reported in neutropenic patients with hematologic malignancies (HM), but its features in non-hematologic malignancy (NHM) patients remain unclear. This study aimed to characterize the clinical presentation, treatment, and outcomes of PM in NHM patients.MethodsThis retrospective study (March 1, 2017-July 31, 2024) at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital included 46 NHM-PM patients, predominantly diabetes mellitus (DM) and solid organ transplantation (SOT). The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox regression, with adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes mellitus. The proportional hazards assumption was verified (GLOBAL p = 0.515).ResultsAmong 46 PM patients with NHM, DM was present in 71.7%. The 90-day mortality rate was 37.0% (17/46). Non-survivors had higher rates of respiratory failure, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and septic shock, along with lower hemoglobin and higher D-dimer and procalcitonin (all P < 0.05). Univariable Cox regression identified solid organ transplantation (HR = 6.97), the use of immunosuppressants (HR = 5.58), elevated D-dimers, and elevated procalcitonin as significant mortality predictors (all P < 0.01), whereas longer hospital stay and longer time to treatment initiation were protective (both P < 0.05). Multivariable Cox regression confirmed the use of immunosuppressants as an independent risk factor (adjusted HR = 7.51, 95% CI: 2.72–20.74, P < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier curves showed significantly worse survival for the use of immunosuppressants and SOT recipients (log-rank P < 0.001 for both).ConclusionsThe use of immunosuppressants and SOT are both important factors associated with 90-day mortality in NHM-PM patients. These findings highlight the poor prognosis associated with immunosuppression and support early, aggressive intervention in this high-risk population. Validation in larger cohorts is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04474-x.

Background

Mucormycosis is a rare but life‑threatening invasive fungal infection caused by Mucorales fungi, with an increasing prevalence reported in recent years [1]. Pulmonary mucormycosis (PM) is the third most common clinical presentation after rhino‑orbital‑cerebral and cutaneous involvement, representing a frequent subtype of the disease [2].

PM predominantly occurs in immunocompromised patients, with major risk factors including hematologic malignancies (HM) and solid-organ transplantation (SOT) [3–5]. A French study conducted between 2005 and 2007 identified 101 mucormycosis cases (58% male), of which 50% occurred in HM patients, 23% in diabetes mellitus (DM) patients, and 18% in trauma patients; the 3-month mortality rate was 56% [6]. The incidence of invasive mucormycosis in HM patients has been increasing annually, ranging from 0.07% to 4.29%, with mortality rates exceeding 50% in most series [7]. Diagnosis of PM remains challenging due to the limited sensitivity and specificity of conventional diagnostic techniques [8]. Prognosis depends on timely identification, appropriate antifungal therapy, and surgical intervention when feasible. Nevertheless, mortality remains high, likely attributable to delayed diagnosis, economic burden, and limited treatment options [9].

In contrast to the well-characterized PM in HM patients, research on PM in non-hematologic malignancy (NHM) populations is limited. Available evidence suggests that DM is an important risk factor after HM, and other associated factors include SOT [10, 11]. Rare presentations, such as endobronchial mucormycosis in DM patients, may mimic tumors, leading to diagnostic delays and subsequently high mortality [12, 13]. A French multicenter study of 114 PM cases further highlighted DM as an important host factor and emphasized that diagnostic delay is a key contributor to high mortality, reporting a 90-day mortality rate of 59% [13].

Unlike most PM studies that focus on HM, our tertiary referral center predominantly manages PM patients with NHM, primarily DM. This unique composition reflects our institutional admission patterns and offers valuable insights into PM in NHM patients, particularly those with DM. Given China’s large and growing DM population, PM in this group is increasingly relevant. Nevertheless, systematic studies on PM in NHM populations-including patients with DM, chronic lung disease, chronic renal disease, or no obvious immunodeficiency, remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the clinical features, outcomes, and factors associated with mortality in Chinese NHM patients with PM, providing evidence to support early recognition and optimized management in this understudied population.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethics No. [2026-KE-660]). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective design using anonymized clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

The study is reported following the STROBE guidelines for cohort studies, and the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University-a tertiary referral center for invasive fungal diseases in the region. The center receives complicated and critically ill patients with pulmonary infections from multiple surrounding cities; therefore, the study cohort may be subject to referral bias, potentially overrepresenting patients with more severe illness, greater diagnostic complexity, or prior treatment failure for PM.

Participants

We retrospectively reviewed patients hospitalized with PM at our institution between March 1, 2017, and July 31, 2024, who had complete clinical data. All patients underwent HIV antibody testing upon admission; no HIV infections were identified in this cohort.

Inclusion criteria: Patients who met the diagnostic criteria for PM according to the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) consensus definitions (see below) [14].

Exclusion criteria: Patients with HM (including but not limited to acute leukemia, chronic leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, and myelodysplastic syndromes) were excluded from this study.

Sample size: No formal sample size calculation was performed. All consecutive patients who met the eligibility criteria during the study period were enrolled. A total of 48 patients with PM were identified, of whom two had hematologic malignancies. These two patients were excluded from the analysis, as they accounted for only 4.2% of the cohort and were not the focus of this study (NHM). Accordingly, 46 patients with NHM were included in the final analysis.

Diagnostic criteria for pulmonary mucormycosis

PM was diagnosed and classified according to the EORTC/MSG consensus definitions, which categorize invasive fungal diseases into proven, probable, and possible [14]. The possible category was not applicable in this study.

Proven PM (applicable to all patients): Isolation of Mucorales fungi by culture from a sterile site (percutaneously or bronchoscopically biopsied tissue, blood, pleural fluid, etc.) or identification of characteristic hyphae (broad, pauci-septate or non-septate, right-angle branching) with associated tissue damage on histopathology or cytopathology of a needle or biopsy specimen. When necessary, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and DNA sequencing of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue were performed for species-level identification.

Probable PM (applicable only to immunocompromised patients meeting specific host factors): Required all three of the following criteria: (a) Clinical criteria: Symptoms and signs consistent with pulmonary infection (fever, cough, chest pain, dyspnea, etc.); (b) Radiographic criteria: Chest high-resolution computed tomography (CT) findings consistent with PM, including consolidation, mass, nodules, cavities, or the reversed halo sign. (c) Microbiological criteria (at least one): Detection of characteristic hyphae by direct microscopy or cytology in sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), or detection of Mucorales nucleic acids by standardized PCR in BALF or sputum.

Variables and data collection

Data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records, including: general data(sex, age, underlying diseases, clinical symptoms); laboratory tests (results of routine tests obtained at the first admission); pathogen detection (culture, histopathology, cytopathology, PCR results); imaging(chest CT findings).Arterial blood gas analysis was performed at admission, and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio was calculated to assess baseline respiratory function. A PaO2/FiO2 ratio < 300 was defined as hypoxemia, and < 200 as severe hypoxemia. Bronchoscopic findings (purulent secretion, tracheal stenosis, tracheal occlusion); Treatment and outcomes(antifungal regimens, surgical intervention, and predictors of 90-day mortality).

Classification of variables

Pre‑existing risk factors: Characteristics present before infection onset associated with PM risk (neutropenia, diabetes mellitus, immunosuppressant use, transplant status, etc.).

Severity markers: Indicators reflecting illness severity after disease onset ( ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, septic shock, respiratory failure, hypoproteinemia, etc.). These are not interpreted as independent predictors.

Imaging and bronchoscopy

All chest CT images were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists. For patients admitted with “PM” as the presenting diagnosis, the earliest imaging within 72 h before to 72 h after admission was selected. For patients admitted for other diseases, the imaging obtained on the date PM was first suspected (within ± 72 h) was selected. Bronchoscopic results were included if obtained within ± 72 h of a confirmed PM diagnosis.

Wedge consolidation is defined as a pleural‑based, wedge‑shaped (triangular) opacity on computed CT, with the base abutting the pleura and the apex pointing toward the hilum. According to the EORTC/MSG 2020 revised criteria [14], this finding is considered a typical radiological sign of invasive fungal disease, corresponding to hemorrhagic pulmonary infarction resulting from fungal angioinvasion [15].

Definitions of key time points and treatment

Date of PM diagnosis (time zero): The earliest date of first positive microbiological culture, pathological examination (histopathology or cytopathology), or radiological evidence strongly suggestive of PM ( reverse halo sign or hypodense sign on CT, etc.). The date of symptom onset was not used due to non‑specific early symptoms and unreliable recall in a retrospective design.

Time to antifungal treatment initiation: Calculated from the date of PM diagnosis to the date of first administration of antifungal agents, such as amphotericin B(AmB), posaconazole(POS), isavuconazole(ISA), etc.A total of 32 patients (69.6%) received amphotericin B therapy, including 8 who received liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB, 3–5 mg/kg/day) and 24 who received amphotericin B deoxycholate (AmB-D, 0.5-1.0 mg/kg/day). Treatment duration: From the date of first antifungal therapy initiation to the last date of antifungal use (including oral maintenance therapy after discharge).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 90 days after diagnosis. Death was attributed to PM if it occurred during the acute phase with evidence of progressive disease, septic shock with microbiological confirmation, or clinical judgment by the treating team in the absence of other identifiable causes. To ensure objectivity, all-cause mortality was used as the primary outcome.Survival status at day 90 was available for all 46 patients (100% completeness), as all deaths occurred during hospitalization and all discharge patients survived to day 90 post‑diagnosis.Follow-up after hospital discharge was continued through outpatient clinic visits or telephone contacts to ensure complete 90-day outcome ascertainment.

Definitions of predisposing factors

Glucocorticoid use: Prednisone equivalent ≥ 30 mg/day for > 14 days, or < 30 mg/day for > 1 month.

Immunosuppressant use: Includes antiproliferative agents (mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, etc.) and calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine A, tacrolimus, etc.).

COVID‑19/influenza virus infection: PM diagnosed concurrently with or within 3 months of COVID‑19 or influenza virus infection, as previously described [16].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and comparisons were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed and compared using independent samples t‑tests. Non‑normally distributed data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, 25%-75%) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test, “day 0” on the x-axis was defined as the date of PM diagnosis. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify factors associated with mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals; the global test yielded a non-significant p-value (p = 0.515), and no individual covariate violated the assumption (all p > 0.05). Due to the limited number of death events (n = 17), the multivariable Cox model included only immunosuppressant use (primary exposure) and three prespecified covariates (age, sex, diabetes). Results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Collinearity between SOT and immunosuppressant use was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF value was 3.48 (Tolerance = 0.288), indicating moderate collinearity. Although this value did not exceed the conventional threshold for severe collinearity (VIF > 5 or > 10), the strong clinical overlap and the limited number of death events (n = 17) precluded simultaneous inclusion of both variables in the multivariable Cox model. Therefore, immunosuppressant use was selected as the primary exposure variable, as it represents the common underlying mechanism of immunosuppression across both transplant and non-transplant patients.All statistical tests were two-sided, with a p-value of less than 0.05 deemed statistically significant. The data analysis was executed using Free Statistics software (version 1.9.2).

Results

Patient characteristics

The clinical characteristics, demographic data, and underlying conditions of the patients are summarized in (Table 1). A total of 46 PM patients were enrolled, including 17 non-survivors (37.0%) and 29 survivors (63.0%). All survivors completed 90-day follow-up with no additional deaths. According to EORTC/MSG criteria [14], 18 patients (39.1%) were classified as proven PM and 28 (60.9%) as probable PM. The cohort comprised 30 males (65.2%) and 16 females (34.8%), with a mean age of 54.4 ± 14.8 years. The most common clinical symptom was cough, occurring in 41 patients (89.1%). Compared with non-survivors, a significantly higher proportion of survivors presented with dyspnea (P = 0.024).

The most common predisposing factor was DM (33/46,71.7%), with diabetic ketoacidosis occurring in four patients (8.7%). Sixteen patients (34.8%) had a history of glucocorticoid exposure before admission, eight patients (17.4%) had received chemotherapy or other immunosuppressive agents, and six patients (13.0%) were solid organ transplant recipients (five kidney transplants, one liver transplant). Among the six SOT recipients, immunosuppressive regimens varied widely, including calcineurin inhibitors, antimetabolites, and corticosteroids. Patients in the early post-transplant period (< 6 months) appeared to have more rapid disease progression. Immunosuppressants were frequently paused upon diagnosis of mucormycosis, but mortality remained high (83.3%).

The median length of hospital stay was 14.5 days (IQR: 10.0–20.0). The rapid disease progression was reflected by a median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation of 1.0 day (IQR: 0-3.5) and a median time from diagnosis to death of 9.0 days (IQR: 6.0-16.5) in non-survivors. At admission, the median oxygenation index (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio) was 237.9 (range:111.4-307.2); 12 patients had a ratio < 300, and eight had a ratio < 200.

As summarized in Table 1, comparisons between non-survivors (n = 17) and survivors (n = 29) revealed several significant differences. A greater proportion of non-survivors developed respiratory failure (P = 0.002), required mechanical ventilation (P = 0.011), were admitted to the ICU (P = 0.003), and presented with septic shock (P = 0.013) or chronic renal disease (P = 0.040). Furthermore, SOT recipients and patients receiving immunosuppressants had significantly higher mortality rates (P = 0.038 and P = 0.004, respectively). Non-survivors also had a significantly shorter hospital stay compared with survivors (P = 0.002). No significant differences were observed between the two groups for the remaining demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, or predisposing factors (Table 1). Sensitivity results for the 18 pathologically confirmed cases are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

As shown in Table 2, non-survivors had significantly lower hemoglobin, platelet, and albumin levels than survivors (P = 0.012, P = 0.012, and P < 0.001, respectively). In contrast, non-survivors had significantly higher creatinine levels (P = 0.006). C-reactive protein, D‑dimer, and procalcitonin levels were also significantly elevated in non-survivors compared with survivors (P = 0.009, P = 0.007, and P = 0.001, respectively). Furthermore, non‑survivors exhibited lower CD4 + T-cell counts, CD8 + T-cell counts, and natural killer (NK) cell counts (P = 0.025, P = 0.019, and P = 0.022, respectively). Sensitivity results for the 18 pathologically confirmed cases are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Bronchoscopic findings and microbiological analysis

A total of 46 patients were included, of whom 17 died (non-survivors) and 29 survived (survivors). Bronchoscopy was performed in 35 patients (76.1%), with purulent secretions being the most common finding (24/35, 68.6%), followed by tracheal stenosis (10/35, 28.6%) and tracheal occlusion (6/35, 17.1%). However, no significant differences were observed between non-survivor and survivors in the prevalence of purulent secretions (75.0% vs. 51.7%, P = 0.709), tracheal stenosis (33.3% vs. 20.7%, P = 0.706), or tracheal occlusion (25.0% vs. 10.3%, P = 0.391) (Table 4). Sensitivity results for the 18 pathologically confirmed cases are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Histopathological confirmation of mucormycosis was obtained in 18 patients (39.1%) via transbronchial lung biopsy (n = 15), percutaneous lung biopsy (n = 2), or surgical specimen (n = 1). We compared clinical outcomes between the histopathology-confirmed group (n = 18, 37.5%) and the mNGS-diagnosed group (n = 28, 62.5%). As shown in the Supplementary Table S10, the proportion of combination therapy was higher in the histopathology-confirmed group (66.7%) than in the mNGS-diagnosed group (42.9%), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.115). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in in-hospital mortality (P = 0.301), 90-day mortality (P = 0.301), or length of hospital stay (P = 0.830). Regarding diagnostic methods, there were no significant differences between the two groups. Regarding the causative species, Rhizopus was the most common in both groups (52.9% in non-survivors vs. 55.2% in survivors, P = 0.883), including Rhizopus arrhizus and Rhizopus microsporus. Rhizomucors were relatively more common in survivors, mainly Rhizomucor pusillus; however, none of the differences were statistically significant, as shown in (Table 4).

Treatments and prognosis

Among 46 patients who received antifungal therapy, 14 (29.2%) received prophylaxis before diagnosis (voriconazole, n = 10; fluconazole, n = 2; echinocandin, n = 4). Non‑survivors had a significantly shorter time from diagnosis to treatment than survivors (median 1.0 vs. 5.0 days, P = 0.009). Using the ≥ 6-day threshold to define delayed therapy [17], delayed therapy was paradoxically more common in survivors (44.8% vs. 11.8%, P = 0.021), confirming that treatment delay was not the driver of mortality in our cohort. A total of 32 patients (69.6%) received AmB therapy: 8 received L-AmB and 24 received AmB-D.Surgery combined with antifungal agents was used only in the survival group (5/29, 17.2%), but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.186), possibly due to the small number of surgical cases. As shown in Table 5, POS/ISA monotherapy was significantly more common in non-survivors than survivors (52.9% vs. 17.2%, P = 0.011), whereas combination therapy (POS/ISA + AmB) was more frequent in survivors (62.1% vs. 29.4%, P = 0.032). In univariable analysis, combination therapy (POS/ISA + AmB) was associated with lower mortality, whereas POS/ISA monotherapy was more common among non-survivors. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and potential selection bias. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to proven cases (n = 18), the survival benefit of combination therapy did not reach statistical significance, which most likely reflects insufficient statistical power rather than a true absence of treatment benefit. Sensitivity results for the 18 pathologically confirmed cases are detailed in Supplementary Table S5.

Discussion

Unlike most published PM cohorts that predominantly include patients with HM, our study cohort exclusively comprised NHM patients after excluding two HM cases. The cohort was dominated by DM (71.7%) and SOT (13.0%). This unique distribution reflects both our institutional referral patterns and the high prevalence of DM in China. Consequently, our findings are primarily applicable to NHM patients with PM, and caution is warranted when extrapolating to HM populations or non-referral centers.

In this study, immunosuppressant use and SOT were identified as significant independent predictors of 90‑day mortality (adjusted HR = 7.51 for immunosuppressants). Notably, corticosteroids, despite being frequently discussed in the PM literature, were not identified as an independent risk factor in our cohort. This discrepancy warrants further discussion. Given that almost all SOT recipients received immunosuppressants, there is a risk of collinearity between these two variables in statistical analyses. Therefore, the independent effect of immunosuppressant use requires further validation in larger, multicenter studies.

Several factors may explain why immunosuppressants, rather than corticosteroids, emerged as the dominant drug‑related risk factor. First, depth and mechanism of immunosuppression: The predominant use of calcineurin inhibitors and antimetabolites in SOT recipients causes profound T‑cell dysfunction, which may confer higher susceptibility to PM compared with corticosteroid‑mediated immunosuppression alone. Notably, calcineurin inhibitors have direct antifungal activity against Mucorales, and their combination with antifungal agents has shown synergistic effects in vitro [18]. Second, duration and cumulative dose: Corticosteroid exposure in our non‑transplant patients was often limited in duration and cumulative dose, which may have been insufficient to independently increase PM risk. By contrast, immunosuppressants were typically used continuously for graft maintenance. Third, synergistic effects with DM: Hyperglycemia impairs macrophage adhesion, phagocytosis, and induces macrophage apoptosis, facilitating Mucor invasion [19]. The complex interplay between immunosuppressants and DM may synergistically increase PM susceptibility [19].

Previous studies from China have consistently reported DM as the most common underlying disease for PM [20–23], aligning with our findings. A systematic review identified DM, corticosteroid use, HM, and SOT as major risk factors for PM [23]. While our results align with the identification of SOT as a risk factor, we did not find corticosteroids to be independently associated with mortality. This may reflect differences in patient population, dosing regimens, or the fact that corticosteroid use was often co‑administered with other immunosuppressants, making independent effects difficult to isolate. The low proportion of patients receiving high‑dose, prolonged corticosteroid therapy in our study may also explain this finding.

Although delayed antifungal therapy is associated with increased mortality in PM, the only quantitative definition of “delayed therapy” derives from HM patients [17]. For NHM patients, no dedicated evidence‑based time window exists. In our cohort, non‑survivors had a significantly shorter time from diagnosis to treatment than survivors. This counterintuitive finding reflects disease severity rather than “earlier treatment causing worse outcomes.” Non‑survivors often presented with fulminant disease, prompting immediate empiric therapy, whereas survivors had more indolent presentations allowing diagnostic workup. Using the ≥ 6‑day threshold, delayed therapy was paradoxically more common in survivors, confirming that treatment delay was not the driver of mortality in our cohort.

Posaconazole (POS) and isavuconazole (ISA) are effective azole options against mucormycosis and can be used as alternatives or salvage treatments for amphotericin B [8, 24].Importantly, treatment allocation was non-randomized and subject to confounding by indication. Patients receiving combination therapy may have had better baseline organ function or less severe disease at presentation, while those receiving monotherapy may have had contraindications to amphotericin B (e.g., renal impairment) or more rapid disease progression precluding combination regimens. Furthermore, survivor bias cannot be excluded, as patients must survive long enough to receive combination therapy. Therefore, our data are hypothesis-generating only, and future prospective or propensity-matched studies are needed to clarify the role of combination therapy and surgery in PM.

Similarly, surgery was performed in only a few patients, all of whom survived, but the difference was not statistically significant. Importantly, treatment allocation was non-randomized and subject to confounding by indication. Therefore, our data are hypothesis-generating, and future prospective or propensity‑matched studies are needed to clarify the roles of combination therapy and surgery in PM.

The survival pattern in this cohort was characterized by a steep decline early after diagnosis, with 94.1% of deaths occurring within the first 20 days. Thereafter, the survival curve plateaued, with no deaths recorded beyond day 33. The 30‑day survival rate exceeded 60%, and median survival was not reached. This pattern largely explains the relatively short hospital stay observed in our study. Non‑survivors had significantly lower platelet counts and more severe lymphopenia (including reduced CD4+, CD8+, and NK cells) compared with survivors. These findings align with previous reports that thrombocytopenia and lymphopenia are predictors of poor prognosis in PM [25–27]. Platelets may play a direct role in antifungal defense by interfering with fungal virulence and synergizing with antifungal drugs [28, 29]. The observed T-cell dysregulation further supports the importance of adaptive immunity in controlling PM [30].

Regarding imaging, wedge consolidation and tracheal stenosis/occlusion were more common in non-survivors, consistent with previous studies [17, 31, 32]. Bilateral lung involvement was observed in 73.9% of patients, which limited surgical options and emphasizes the importance of early antifungal therapy. Lymphadenopathy was also more frequent in non-survivors, suggesting that extensive mediastinal involvement may portend a worse prognosis. Only 18 patients (39.1%) were diagnosed by histopathology; the remainder met clinical diagnostic criteria. The low culture positivity rate (13.0%) may reflect low clinical suspicion, suboptimal culture techniques, or prior antifungal exposure. The widespread use of mNGS has improved diagnostic yield in recent years. The predominant species identified were Rhizopus and Rhizomucor, consistent with reports from Asian countries [25]. No significant difference in species distribution was observed between survivors and non-survivors. Although not statistically significant, the numerically higher rates of smear and culture positivity in non-survivors may suggest a higher pathogen burden, which could contribute to poorer outcomes. Further studies are needed to explore the relationship between fungal load, diagnostic test performance, and prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design may introduce selection and referral bias. The near-absence of HM patients likely reflects institutional referral patterns, limiting generalizability to HM populations or non‑referral centers. Additionally, our findings are primarily applicable to HIV-negative individuals, and caution should be exercised when generalizing to other immunocompromised populations. Second, the small sample size (n = 46) and limited death events (n = 17) reduced statistical power. In sensitivity analysis restricted to proven cases, effect estimates remained consistent but lost significance due to reduced sample size. Third, over 60% of cases were mNGS-diagnosed rather than histopathology-confirmed; while all met EORTC/MSG criteria, mNGS cannot confirm viable organisms or tissue invasion, and lack of pathological confirmation remains a major limitation. Fourth, treatment allocation was non-randomized and subject to confounding by indication.The apparent survival benefit of combination therapy may be confounded by disease severity, baseline organ function, and survivor bias. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causal treatment effects. Fifth, several variables (ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, septic shock, respiratory failure) are disease severity markers rather than independent predictors; thus, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Sixth, the exact date of symptom onset could not be reliably captured; therefore, treatment timing was defined from diagnosis rather than symptom onset, which may underestimate total diagnostic delay. Additionally, the only available quantitative definition of delayed therapy (≥ 6 days) derives from HM populations and may not apply to our NHM cohort. Seventh, the survival benefit of combination therapy should be interpreted with caution due to small sample size and selection bias; non-significant findings in the proven-case subgroup likely reflect insufficient power.Larger, prospective, or propensity‑matched studies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In this cohort of NHM patients with PM, the use of immunosuppressants and SOT were both important factors associated with 90-day mortality. Most deaths occurred within the first three weeks after diagnosis.Combination therapy (POS/ISA + AmB) was associated with lower mortality in the full cohort, although this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and potential selection bias, including treatment-selection bias and survivor bias. Early diagnosis and prompt antifungal therapy remain critical. Given the high prevalence of DM in China, NHM-associated PM represents an emerging clinical challenge. Larger prospective or propensity-matched studies are needed to validate these findings and optimize treatment strategies for this understudied population.
