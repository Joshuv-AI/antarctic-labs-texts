Chimeric switch scaffold protein augments CAR synapse formation and signaling networks
Authors: Cho I Park, Segi Kim, Hwanyong Shim, Minh Ha Nguyen, Won-ki Cho, Chan Hyuk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has achieved clinical success in hematologic malignancies, but remains limited in solid tumors due to immune checkpoint-mediated suppression and intrinsic signaling constraints. Unlike T-cell receptors (TCRs), CARs fail to efficiently recruit the linker for activation of T cells (LAT) signalosome, resulting in suboptimal signal propagation and unstable immunological synapse organization.MethodsTo address this limitation, we engineered a programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)–LAT (PLAT) chimeric switch scaffold that couples PD-1 engagement to LAT-dependent proximal signaling. We compared the signaling kinetics, synaptic architecture, and antitumor efficacy of PLAT-expressing HER2 CAR T cells against those expressing conventional PD-1-CD28 switch receptors or dominant-negative PD-1 using biochemical signaling assays, quantitative imaging analysis, chronic antigen stimulation models, and in vivo xenograft validation.ResultsPLAT enhanced LAT phosphorylation and nucleated LAT-associated signaling complexes on programmed death-ligand 1 engagement. This reorganized the CAR immune synapse into a concentric, TCR-like architecture, restoring proximal signaling strength. This signaling reprogramming resulted in increased calcium flux and NFAT/NF-κB activation, driving enhanced cytotoxicity, sustained proliferation, and resistance to functional exhaustion under chronic antigen exposure. In direct comparisons, PLAT outperformed conventional PD-1–CD28 switch receptors in early functional responses and demonstrated superior functional durability and antitumor activity in vivo compared with dominant-negative PD-1 strategies.ConclusionsThese findings establish PLAT as a scaffold-based strategy that directly addresses intrinsic CAR signaling deficiencies by converting immune checkpoint engagement into LAT-dependent proximal signaling restoration. This work provides a new framework for engineering CAR T cells with improved function and persistence in immunosuppressive tumor environments.

Background

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in patients with relapsed or refractory hematologic malignancies.13 However, its therapeutic potential in solid tumors remains limited, primarily due to CAR T-cell dysfunction driven by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).4 5 A diverse array of inhibitory pathways, including immune checkpoint receptors, cytokine receptors, and metabolite-sensing molecules, contribute to T-cell suppression following antigen engagement.6 7 While genetic disruption of these inhibitory signals has shown promise, synthetic approaches offer a more flexible and programmable alternative. For instance, dominant-negative receptors retain ligand-binding capability but lack intracellular signaling domains, thereby serving as competitive inhibitors.8 9 Building on this concept, chimeric switch receptors (CSRs) have been developed to convert inhibitory signals into activating ones by replacing the intracellular domain of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or other checkpoint receptors with costimulatory domains such as CD28 or 4–1BB.1012

Despite these advances in circumventing external immunosuppressive signals, intrinsic limitations in CAR architecture may still limit their efficacy in solid tumors. Although CARs are designed to mimic T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling, they lack several structural components critical for full activation. Typical CAR constructs include an extracellular single-chain variable fragment fused to intracellular CD3ζ and one or more costimulatory domains.13 14 Unlike the native TCR complex, which transmits signals via multiple CD3 subunits and proximal adaptor proteins, CAR engages downstream signaling through a simplified pathway that omits CD3ε, γ, and δ.15 This structural simplification results in diminished activation strength and inefficient phosphorylation of proximal molecules such as ZAP70 and LAT, ultimately reducing antigen sensitivity and effector function.15 16 To compensate, recent efforts have attempted to enhance signaling strength by fusing additional effectors such as ZAP70 or adaptor proteins like SLP76 and PLCγ1 to the CAR backbone.17 18 However, these strategies have generally produced only incremental gains in activation.

Given the pivotal role of linker for activation of T cells (LAT) in coordinating early TCR signaling, directly leveraging this scaffold protein presents a compelling yet underexplored opportunity. LAT acts as a central hub for assembling proximal signaling complexes at the immunological synapse. On phosphorylation, LAT recruits key downstream effectors, including SLP76 and PLCγ1, to propagate signals required for calcium mobilization, MAPK activation, and cytoskeletal remodeling.1922 Despite its functional importance, direct incorporation of LAT into CAR T cells has been limited by technical hurdles such as low expression levels and unintended tonic signaling.15 17 18 Prior attempts to indirectly recruit LAT through SH2 domain fusions (eg, Grb2 or GRAP2 to CD3ζ) have shown modest benefits in preclinical models, but a direct, controlled implementation strategy remains lacking.15 Thus, spatially and temporally controlled recruitment of LAT represents an attractive strategy to restore early signaling fidelity without inducing tonic activation.

To overcome these limitations, we designed a chimeric switch scaffold (CSS) that simultaneously relieves PD-1-mediated inhibition and restores LAT-dependent signaling. This construct, termed PD-1–LAT (PLAT), replaces the intracellular domain of PD-1 with full-length LAT, enabling the conversion of PD-L1-mediated inhibitory signaling into productive signalosome assembly. Unlike conventional CSRs that rely solely on costimulatory signaling domains, PLAT engages proximal activation machinery via LAT to enhance signal fidelity and amplitude. CAR T cells co-expressing PLAT exhibit improved immunological synapse organization, heightened proximal signaling, and superior effector function both in vitro and in vivo. These findings highlight PLAT as a modular strategy to rewire early signaling and improve CAR T-cell efficacy in the immunosuppressive setting of solid tumors.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and engineered cell lines

The MDAMB231, HCC1954, SKOV3 cell lines were obtained from Calibr-Skaggs at Scripps Research (San Diego, USA) and genetically modified by lentiviral transduction to express ZsGreen (Zs) or to generate programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) knockout (KO) and PD-L1-overexpressing variants (MDAMB231-PD-L1 KO, MDAMB231-PD-L1, MDAMB231-Zs-PD-L1 KO, MDAMB231-Zs-PD-L1, HCC1954-Zs, SKOV3-Zs). For PD-L1 KO, the lentiCRISPR v2-puro vector was used with the guide RNA sequence 5’- TGGGAAAUGCUGCACUUCAG −3’. The Lenti-X 293T cell line was purchased from Takara Bio. All tumor cell lines were maintained in DMEM (Welgene), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Opti-Gold; Genedepot) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Jurkat cells (ATCC) were engineered via lentiviral transduction to express human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) CAR or HER2 CAR-mcherry and truncated low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor (tLNGFR), dominant-negative PD-1 (DNP)-tLNGFR or PLAT-tLNGFR. Nalm6, Nalm6-PD-L1 were obtained from Calibr-Skaggs at Scripps Research (San Diego, USA) and Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco), and 10 mM HEPES (Sigma-Aldrich).

Lentiviral production and transduction

Lentivirus was produced as previously described.23 For transduction, 1×106 cells were incubated with lentivirus containing medium supplemented with 12 µg/mL of protamine sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich). Spin inoculation was performed at 1,000×g for 90 min at 32°C, followed by incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2. After 24 hours, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium.

Genetic engineering of primary human T cells

Peripheral blood was obtained from an anonymous healthy donor through ASAN Medical Center (Seoul, Korea) under protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) (IRB-18-071). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Sepmate-50 (STEMCELL Technologies) and cryopreserved in 90% FBS with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). After thawing, CD3+T cells were purified using a Pan T-cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and activated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:1 ratio. Following 24 hours of activation, 1×106 T cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding HER2 CAR. A second lentiviral transduction was performed the next day to introduce chimeric switch scaffold protein and controls (tLNGFR, DNP-tLNGFR, or PLAT-tLNGFR). The medium was replaced the day after the second transduction. Dynabeads were magnetically removed 24 hours later. On day 6 post-transduction, tLNGFR+cells were enriched using magnetic cell separation with anti-LNGFR Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec). Sorted cells were maintained at 0.5–1×106 cells/mL in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 55 µM 2-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% non-essential amino acids (Gibco), and 300 IU/mL recombinant human IL-2 (Proleukin).

Flow cytometry

For surface marker analysis, 1×105 CAR T cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in flourescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS)) for 20 min at 4°C. To discriminate viable from dead cell population, cells were stained with eFluor 780 viability dye (eBioscience, 65–0865-14) for 10 min at 25°C. HER2 CAR expression was detected using AF488 or AF647 anti-trastuzumab antibodies (R&D Systems, FAB95471G or FAB95471R), PD-1 and tLNGFR were analyzed with BV421 anti-CD279 (BioLegend, 367422) and APC or PE anti-CD271 antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–113-418 or 130–113-421). CD4 and CD8 markers were stained using PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-CD4 (BioLegend, 357414) and APC anti-CD8 (BioLegend, 344722). Early activation markers were detected using efluor 450 anti-CD25 (48–0251-82, Invitrogen) and FITC anti-CD69 (310904; BioLegend). Differentiation markers were analyzed using FITC anti-CD95 (BioLegend, 305605), BV421 anti-CD45RO (BD, 562641), and PE anti-CCR7 (BioLegend, 303504). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit (BD Biosciences, 20 min at 4°C), followed by staining with APC anti-granzyme B (BioLegend, 396408) and PE anti-Perforin (BioLegend, 353304). Proliferation and apoptosis were assessed using FITC anti-Ki67 (BD, 556027) and AF594 anti-Annexin V (Thermo Fisher, A13203). Exhaustion-associated markers were analyzed using APC anti-human TIGIT (BioLegend, 372705), PE anti-human LAG-3 (BioLegend, 369305), BV421 anti-human TIM-3 (BioLegend, 345007), and PE anti-TOX (Invitrogen, 50–245-515). Flow cytometry was performed using an LSRFortessa X-20 cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Quantification of cell conjugation

Engineered HER2 CAR Jurkat cells were labeled with 5 µM CellTrace Violet (CTV; Thermo Fisher) solution for 20 min at 37°C and washed with medium. 1×105 Jurkat cells were incubated with 1×105 ZsGreen expressing MDAMB231-PD-L1 KO and PD-L1 cells at 37°C for 0, 30, or 60 min. Cells were fixed with cold Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences) for 20 min at 4°C and washed two times with washing buffer. Conjugate formation was quantified as a percentage of CTV+ZsGreen+ double-positive events using flow cytometry.

Cytokine measurement

To assess cytokine production in response to target cell stimulation, engineered HER2 CAR T cells were incubated in interleukin (IL)-2 free T-cell culture medium for 24 hours to deplete residual IL-2 prior to the assay. 2×105 MDAMB231-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates 24 hours before co-culture. 5×105 engineered HER2 CAR T cells were resuspended in IL-2 free T-cell culture medium were added to the target cells. After 24 hours of co-culture, supernatants were collected and cytokine levels (IL-2, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ) were measured using the Human Th1/Th2 Cytokine Kit (BD Biosciences) using flow cytometry.

Western blotting analysis

To assess the phosphorylation of LAT and PLCγ1, engineered HER2 CAR Jurkat cells were co-cultured with MDAMB231-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 5:1 for 0, 10, 45, or 90 min. Cells were lysed in NP40 lysis buffer (Elpis Biotech) supplemented with a proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) and phosphatase inhibitor (Roche). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were mixed with 4×lithium dodecyl sulfate (LDS) sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), boiled at 70°C for 10 min, and separated on 4–12% Bis-Tris sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using an iBlot2 Dry blotting system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked with 4% BSA in Tris-buffered saline with 0.5% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 hour at 25°C, incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After three washes with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at 25°C. Following the three washes with TBS-T, bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad). Image analysis was performed using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad). The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: anti-PLCγ1 (Cell Signaling, 2822), anti-pPLCγ1 (Ser1248) (Cell Signaling, 8713), anti-LAT (Cell Signaling, 45533), anti-pLAT (Tyr220) (Cell Signaling, 3584), anti-pLAT (Y132) (Invitrogen, 44–224), anti-FLAG (MBL, M185-3L), anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A2228) and anti-PD-L1 (Abclonal, A19135). HRP-anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen, 31430) and HRP-anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, 31460).

Immunoprecipitation analysis

2×106 engineered HER2 CAR Jurkat cells were co-cultured with 1×106 PD-L1 MDAMB231 target cells for 90 min. Cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation buffer (150 mM KCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 20% glycerol, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.05% NP40, 20 mM Tris, pH 7.9). Lysates were incubated with anti-FLAG antibody pre-coupled to Protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen) overnight at 4°C. After washing and elution, immunoprecipitates were analyzed by western blotting using anti-GADS (Sigma Aldrich, 06–983), anti-Grb2 (BD, 610111), anti-LAT (Cell Signaling, 45533), HRP-anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen, 31430) and HRP-anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, 31460).

Calcium flux detection

Engineered HER2 CAR Jurkat cells were labeled with 1 µM Fluo4-AM calcium indicator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min at 37°C and washed with medium. A total of 1×105 labeled cells was seeded 96-well plates, calcium influx was stimulated by the addition of 1×105 cells MDAMB231-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 tumor cells. Fluorescence intensity was recorded every 2 min using a Lionheart FX automated microscope (Agilent). Calcium mobilization was quantified by normalizing fluorescence to basal levels measured prior to stimulation.

NFAT and NF-κB reporter activity

To generate the NFAT and NF-κB reporter, Jurkat cells were sequentially transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding either NFAT-iRFP-670 and NF-κB-iRFP-670 reporter constructs, followed by HER2 CAR, HER2 CAR_DNP, and HER2 CAR_PLAT constructs. The NFAT reporter vector contained three tandem NFAT response elements, and the NF-κB reporter vector contained five tandem NF-κB response elements upstream of the iRFP-670 reporter.24 2×105 MDAMB231-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 cells were seeded into 24-well plates before 24 hours of co-culture. 2×105 engineered HER2 CAR Jurkat cells were added into target seeding plates. After 24 hours, reporter activity was quantified by flow cytometric measurement of iRFP-670 fluorescence after 24 hours of co-culture. Reporter signals were normalized to the corresponding anti-CD3/CD28-stimulated positive-control condition within each experiment.

Immunofluorescence

1×105 engineered HER2 CAR-mCherry Jurkat cells and 1×105 MDAMB231-Zs-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 cells were mixed and seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips. The cells were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at 25°C. They were then permeabilized with blocking buffer containing 4% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.02% Tween-20 in PBS for 10 min. After washing with 0.5% Tween-20 in PBS, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies and fluorescent dye: anti-Flag antibody (MBL, F4049), anti-LAT antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 45533) and Alexa-647 Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A22287). After washing, cells were stained with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at 25°C: AF488 goat anti-mouse (Invitrogen, A28175) and AF488 goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, A110088). Coverslips were mounted using DAPI-containing mounting solution (VECTASHIELD, H-1800) and imaged by confocal microscopy.

Fixed cell imaging

Immunofluorescence-labeled cells were imaged using a Nikon spinning disk confocal microscope (CSU-W1) equipped with an Apochromat TIRF 100X/1.49 oil objective lens (Nikon). Fluorophores were excited with 405 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm lasers corresponding to blue, green, and red channels, respectively. Fluorescence detection was performed using an iXon Life 888 EMCCD camera. Imaging was conducted with a single frame per channel at 200 ms per frame, capturing z-stacks comprising ~50 optical sections at 0.2 µm intervals to facilitate three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction. Z-stack images were subsequently reconstructed using Imaris software. The 3D reconstructions were resliced to specifically visualize the immunological synapse.

Image analysis

For comparative analysis of intensity fold changes between the immunological synapse (IS) and surrounding regions, a representative two-dimensional focal plane containing the synaptic interface was selected from reconstructed images. Two subimages were generated from this selected focal plane: one encompassing only the IS region and the other encompassing the entire donor cell, including the IS region. Within each subimage, regions of interest were selected using a custom threshold in ImageJ. To calculate the mean intensity of the donor cell region (D), excluding the IS region (referred to as the donor-only region), the area (A) and mean intensity (I) were measured in each subimage. The mean intensity of the donor-only region was determined using the following equation: Mean intensity of donor-only region = ((AD × ID) − (AIS × IIS)) / (AD − AIS). Finally, the mean intensity of the IS was divided by the mean intensity of the donor-only region, yielding the fold change in IS intensity relative to other regions.

Cytotoxicity assay

2×104 MDAMB231-Zs-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 cells, SKOV3-Zs and HCC1954-Zs were seeded in 96-well plates and co-cultured with engineered HER2 CAR T cells at each E:T ratio. The percentage of killing was calculated as follows (killing% = 1 − ((initial intensity − 48 hours green intensity) / initial intensity × 100). Fluorescence intensity was monitored every 2–4 hours using the IncuCyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius). Cytotoxicity was quantified by measuring the decrease in ZsGreen fluorescence intensity over time or by CytoTox LDH assay kit based on manufacturer’s instruction (Promega, G1780) with Nalm6, Nalm6-PD-L1, MDAMB231-WT, MDAMB231-PD-L1 cell lines.

Repeated tumor challenge

For repeated cytotoxicity assays, 2×104 MDAMB231-Zs-PD-L1 KO or PD-L1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and co-cultured with 4×104 engineered HER2 CAR T cells. Every 72 hours, fresh tumor cells were added at the same density without changing the effector cells. ZsGreen fluorescence was measured every 4 hours using the IncuCyte S3 to monitor serial killing capacity. For expansion assay, 2×105 MDAMB231-Zs-PD-L1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with 10 µg/mL mitomycin C (Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 hours at 37°C to prevent proliferation. After treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and co-cultured with 1×106 engineered HER2 CAR T cells in complete T-cell medium. After 48 hours, T cells were transferred to fresh T-cell medium supplemented with 60 IU/mL IL-2 and cultured in 6-well plates. Cells were counted every 4 days using a Countess II Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with trypan blue (Gibco) for viability exclusion. Stimulation with freshly mitomycin C-treated MDAMB231-PD-L1 cells was repeated every 4 days for a total of three rounds.

Xenograft mouse model

All animal experiments were conducted under protocols approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of KAIST (KA2023-118). A total of 5×106 MDAMB231-PD-L1 tumor cells suspended in 100 µL of PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of 8–10 weeks old female NSG mice (Jackson Laboratory). Once the tumor reached ~100 mm3, 1×106 engineered HER2 CAR T cells in 100 µL of PBS were administered intravenously via tail vein injection. Tumor volume was measured every 2–4 days using digital calipers. To evaluate CAR T-cell persistence and phenotypes, peripheral blood was collected from the cheek bleeding on day 8 post-T cell injection. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer (Gibco), and remaining cells were washed with FACS buffer. Cells were stained with viability dye and fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against human CD3 (APC, BioLegend, 317318), HER2 CAR (AF488) with counting beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and human CD45RO and human CCR7. Samples were analyzed using flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Western blot band intensities were quantified using ImageJ. Graphs and statistical analyses were generated with GraphPad Prism. Statistical comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test (paired/unpaired, two-tailed), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Tukey’s multiple comparisons), and two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (Holm-Sidak’s test). A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are denoted as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001.

Results

Design and characterization of PLAT-expressing HER2 CAR T cells

We designed a chimeric switch scaffold protein, PD-1–LAT (PLAT), to convert PD-1-mediated inhibitory signals into activating signals in T cells (figure 1a). To express PLAT, we constructed a lentiviral vector encoding the extracellular (EC) and transmembrane domains of PD-1 fused to the intracellular domain of LAT and tagged with a FLAG epitope. A P2A-linked tLNGFR was included as a selection marker. Control vectors encoding FLAG-tagged DNP-tLNGFR and tLNGFR alone were also generated (figure 1b, online supplemental table 1; see online supplemental material).

Human primary T cells were sequentially transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding a second-generation HER2 CAR with a 4–1BB costimulatory domain and either PLAT or control constructs (figure 1c and online supplemental figure 1a,b). After magnetic enrichment based on tLNGFR expression, flow cytometry analysis showed that HER2 CAR expression was comparable across all groups (30–40%), and that DNP or PLAT was robustly expressed in the corresponding groups (figure 1d–e). Further phenotypic analysis revealed that the expression of PLAT or DNP did not affect the CD4/CD8 ratio or the distribution of T-cell memory subsets in CAR T cells (online supplemental figure 1c,d). Protein expression of DNP and PLAT was also confirmed by western blotting using anti-FLAG and anti-LAT antibodies (figure 1f).

To determine whether PLAT can engage LAT-associated signaling molecules, we generated HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells (online supplemental figure 2a) and used HER2-positive MDAMB231 cells engineered to overexpress PD-L1 (MDAMB231-PD-L1) as target cells (online supplemental figure 2b). PD-L1 overexpression was set to levels comparable to those observed on physiologic PD-L1 induction by IFN-γ. Following 90-minute co-culture, FLAG-tagged PLAT was immunoprecipitated, and associated proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting. Interestingly, PLCγ1, Gads, and Grb2, canonical components of the LAT-mediated signalosome, were detected in the immunoprecipitates (figure 1g). Together, these results demonstrate that PLAT is stably expressed in T cells without altering its CAR expression, phenotypic profile of T-cell subsets, and effectively recruits canonical LAT-associated adaptor proteins on stimulation.

PD-L1-dependent recruitment of PLAT enhances CAR synapse organization and stability

We next investigated the spatial relationship between PLAT and CAR at the IS using confocal microscopy. Endogenous LAT, which localizes to both the plasma membrane and intracellular compartments in resting T cells, is recruited to the synapse on antigen engagement. Due to its minimal extracellular domain, LAT recruitment is known to be tightly regulated by intracellular trafficking machineries.2527 In contrast, PLAT contains the extracellular domain of PD-1, which may alter its recruitment dynamics compared with endogenous LAT. To test this, we employed MDAMB231 target cells either lacking or overexpressing PD-L1, while maintaining comparable HER2 expression across all derivatives (online supplemental figure 2b–d). Confocal imaging showed that HER2 CAR molecules were recruited to the synapse in all groups, with a modest increase observed in HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells under PD-L1-expressing target conditions (figure 2a and b). Endogenous LAT co-localized with CAR at the synapse in HER2 CAR and HER2 CAR_DNP cells regardless of target-cell PD-L1 expression (figure 2a and c). In contrast, the synaptic localization of PLAT and DNP, detected via FLAG staining, was strictly dependent on the presence of PD-L1 (online supplemental figure 3a,b). Consequently, total LAT intensity at the immunological synapse was significantly higher in HER2 CAR_PLAT cells compared with other groups only when co-cultured with PD-L1-overexpressing MDAMB231 cells (figure 2c).

Previous studies comparing CAR-mediated and TCR-mediated IS have shown that CAR synapses lack the well-organized concentric supramolecular activation clusters (SMACs) characteristic of TCR synapses.28 29 CAR T cells exhibit altered synaptic architecture, including smaller synapse size, more rapid actin clearance, and shorter-lived contacts. In contrast, TCR synapses typically form a “bull’s-eye” structure with central and peripheral SMACs and robust actin accumulation. Consistent with these findings, our en face confocal imaging of HER2 CAR Jurkat cells engaging MDAMB231-PD-L1 target cells revealed a disorganized synapse with diffuse CAR and actin distribution (figure 2d). A similar pattern was observed in HER2 CAR_DNP Jurkat cells. Interestingly, HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells more frequently formed synapses with a more organized architecture, characterized by central CAR accumulation surrounded by a ring-like structure of F-actin (figure 2e, online supplemental figure 4). These cells also displayed moderately increased synaptic actin intensity compared with control groups (figure 2f), suggesting that PLAT-mediated enhancement of LAT signaling may promote improved actin organization and a more TCR-like synapse structure.

To investigate whether PD-L1-dependent localization of PLAT to the CAR synapse contributes to synapse stabilization, we co-incubated fluorescently labeled CAR Jurkat cells and ZsGreen-expressing MDAMB231 target cells and assessed doublet formation by flow cytometry (figure 2g). When co-cultured with PD-L1 KO MDAMB231 cells, HER2 CAR, HER2 CAR_DNP, and HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells formed doublets at comparable frequencies (figure 2h). In contrast, co-culture with PD-L1-overexpressing target cells resulted in significantly higher doublet formations in both HER2 CAR_DNP and HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells compared with HER2 CAR Jurkat cells, with HER2 CAR_DNP Jurkat cells showing the highest frequency. This likely reflects the additional interaction between PD-L1 on tumor cells and the engineered PD-1 EC expressed on HER2 CAR T cells. Although tLNGFR expression levels were comparable between DNP-expressing and PLAT-expressing cells, the DNP group showed modestly higher surface PD-1 expression, which may have contributed to the increased conjugation frequency (online supplemental figure 2e,f). Collectively, these results suggest that, unlike endogenous LAT, PLAT is recruited to the CAR synapse in a PD-L1-dependent manner via its PD-1 EC domain, thereby contributing to enhanced synapse organization and stabilization.

PLAT enhances LAT-dependent signaling and activation in CAR T cells

After confirming that PLAT is recruited to the CAR IS in a PD-L1-dependent manner, we next evaluated its contribution to CAR signaling using engineered Jurkat cells. On co-culture with PD-L1-overexpressing MDAMB231 cells, phosphorylation of endogenous LAT was maintained across groups, whereas HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells showed robust additional phosphorylation of the PLAT at both Y132 and Y226, sites associated with recruitment of PLCγ1 and adaptor proteins involved in LAT signalosome assembly (figure 3a, online supplemental figure 5). Consistent with this, HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells exhibited more sustained PLCγ1 phosphorylation for up to 90 min, whereas control groups showed a gradual decline over time (figure 3b and c), suggesting that PLAT enhances the stability and duration of signal propagation in CAR T cells.

To further assess early signaling events, we monitored intracellular Ca²+ flux using time-lapse imaging. In the absence of PD-L1 expression on target cells, all groups displayed comparable Ca²+ flux responses in terms of peak intensity and kinetics (figure 3d and e and online supplemental figure 6). In contrast, with PD-L1-expressing target cells, HER2 CAR_DNP Jurkat cells exhibited enhanced calcium signaling compared with HER2 CAR Jurkat cells, likely attributable to the effect of blocking PD-1-mediated inhibitory signaling. Notably, this enhancement of Ca²+ flux was even more pronounced in HER2 CAR_PLAT Jurkat cells out of all tested constructs, indicating that PLAT may further augment T-cell signaling beyond classical checkpoint inhibition.

As these proximal signals converge on transcriptional activation, we next measured NFAT and NF-κB reporter activities. NFAT-responsive and NF-κB-responsive promoters driving iRFP-670 expression were used to quantify transcription factor activation. HER2 CAR and either PLAT or control genes were sequentially introduced to generate reporter cell lines, which were then co-cultured with target cells to assess activity based on fluorescence induction (online supplemental figure 7). Under PD-L1 KO conditions, as expected, all groups showed comparable fluorescence signal responses. However, on PD-L1 engagement, PLAT-expressing cells exhibited a marked increase in both NFAT and NF-κB reporter activity, whereas control groups remained largely unchanged (figure 3f and g).

To determine whether these Jurkat-based signaling findings were recapitulated in primary human T cells, we next evaluated activation marker expression. HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells upregulated CD25 and CD69 more robustly than control groups, but only when co-cultured with PD-L1-overexpressing target cells (figure 3h). Importantly, under antigen-negative conditions using HER2-negative Nalm6 cells with or without PD-L1 expression, PLAT did not induce PD-L1-dependent increases in CD25 and CD69 expression (online supplemental figure 8). These results indicate that PD-L1 engagement alone is insufficient to activate T cells and support a model in which PLAT functions as an antigen-dependent signaling amplifier rather than an autonomous activating receptor, thereby enhancing proximal CAR signaling and downstream transcriptional activity through reinforcement of the LAT signaling network.

PLAT enhances effector function and proliferation of HER2 CAR T cells

When co-cultured with PD-L1 KO MDAMB231 cells, all groups exhibited comparable tumor cell lysis (figure 4a). In contrast, in the presence of PD-L1-expressing targets, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells demonstrated significantly enhanced cytotoxicity across multiple E:T ratios compared with both control groups (figure 4b and c).

In line with this, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells expressed higher levels of cytolytic effector molecules, granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin (PFN), relative to controls (figure 4d and e). In addition, PLAT expression augmented cytokine production on PD-L1 engagement. Specifically, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells secreted significantly greater amounts of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 compared with control groups following co-culture with PD-L1-overexpressing MDAMB231 cells (figure 4f), indicating enhanced effector functionality.

We next examined whether PLAT modulates CAR T-cell proliferation and differentiation under PD-L1-mediated immunosuppressive conditions. HER2 CAR T cells were labeled with CTV and co-cultured with target cells. After 3 days, CTV dilution analysis revealed that a significantly greater fraction of HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells had undergone division compared with control groups in the presence of PD-L1-expressing target cells (figure 4g and h). To evaluate memory differentiation, we analyzed CD45RO and CCR7 expression after 48 hours of co-culture. In PD-L1-positive conditions, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells exhibited a higher frequency of CD45RO+CCR7+ central memory cells compared with control groups (figure 4i, online supplemental figure 9), suggesting that PLAT promotes an early memory phenotype associated with long-term persistence and therapeutic durability. These results demonstrate that PLAT enhances CAR T-cell effector function, proliferation, and memory differentiation specifically under PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression.

Importantly, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells showed enhanced cytotoxicity against unmodified MDAMB231 cell with endogenous PD-L1 expression (MDAMB231-WT), although to a lesser extent than that observed against MDAMB231 PD-L1 cells (figure 4j, online supplemental figure 10a), which may be attributable to in situ upregulation of PD-L1 induced by IFN-γ released from activated CAR T cells during co-culture (online supplemental figure 10b). To assess whether the functional benefit of PLAT extends across additional tumor context, we evaluated cytotoxicity against two HER2-expressing tumor cell lines—HCC1954 (breast cancer) and SKOV3 (ovarian cancer)—which express comparable levels of HER2 but differ markedly in their baseline PD-L1 expression (high in HCC1954 and low in SKOV3; online supplemental figure 11a,b). PD-L1 upregulation was observed in both cell lines following IFN-γ treatment as well as under physiologically relevant co-culture conditions (online supplemental figure 11c). Consistent with our earlier in vitro findings, in the PD-L1high HCC1954 model, HER2 CAR_DNP T cells exhibited only modest improvement in cytotoxicity compared with HER2 CAR T cells, whereas HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells showed significantly enhanced killing both at baseline and following IFN-γ stimulation (figure 4k). In the PD-L1low SKOV3 model, all CAR T groups demonstrated comparable cytotoxicity under unstimulated conditions. However, on IFN-γ-induced PD-L1 upregulation, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells exhibited a significant cytotoxic advantage (figure 4l), indicating that PLAT confers PD-L1-dependent functional enhancement even in tumors with initially low PD-L1 expression when exposed to inflammatory stimuli. Together, these findings suggest that the functional benefit of PLAT is maintained across physiologically relevant conditions involving dynamic PD-L1 upregulation, as well as across multiple tumor contexts with distinct baseline and inducible PD-L1 states.

To further evaluate the functional impact of PLAT relative to an established and structurally analogous PD-1-based switch receptor strategy, we compared PLAT with the previously reported PD-1–CD28 (PCD28) chimeric switch receptor.10 The PCD28 receptor, which comprises the extracellular domain of PD-1 fused to the transmembrane and intracellular domains of CD28, was generated (online supplemental figure 12a) and introduced into HER2 CAR T cells. on co-culture with PD-L1 MDAMB231 tumor cells, both HER2 CAR_PLAT and HER2 CAR_PCD28 T cells exhibited enhanced tumor cell killing over 48 hours compared with conventional HER2 CAR T cells. Notably, PLAT-expressing CAR T cells eliminated more than 65% of target cells within the first 24 hours, whereas PCD28-expressing CAR T cells achieved approximately 40% lysis at the same time point (online supplemental figure 2b,c). These findings suggest that although both constructs improve overall cytotoxicity, PLAT provides a distinct kinetic advantage during the early phase of target cell engagement.

PLAT sustains antitumor activity of HER2 CAR T cells under chronic stimulation

Chronic antigen exposure induces progressive dysfunction in CAR T cells, a process exacerbated by PD-1-mediated inhibition.30 To assess whether PLAT expression mitigates this effect, we performed a repeated tumor rechallenge assay. Under PD-L1 KO conditions, all CAR T-cell groups maintained comparable cytotoxic activity throughout multiple rounds of stimulation (figure 5a). However, in the presence of PD-L1-expressing targets, HER2 CAR_PLAT T cells preserved robust tumor-killing activity across four sequential challenges, whereas HER2 CAR and HER2 CAR_DNP T cells exhibited a marked decline in function (figure 5b). Correspondingly, expression levels of GZMB and PFN remained more than twofold higher in PLAT-expressing cells compared with controls after the fourth stimulation, indicating sustained cytolytic capacity (figure 5c and d).

We next examined proliferative dynamics under chronic stimulation. While initial expansion was comparable across all groups, PLAT-expressing CAR T cells demonstrated significantly greater expansion following the third challenge, reaching nearly 80-fold, in contrast to ~20-fold fold in the control groups (figure 5e). This enhanced proliferation was accompanied by improved cell survival and cycling activity: Annexin-V positivity remained below 5% in the PLAT group, compared with approximately 15% in controls (figure 5f), and Ki-67 expression reached ~80% versus ~40% in the respective groups (figure 5g). Discussion

While CAR T-cell therapy has shown curative potential in hematologic malignancies, its efficacy in solid tumors remains limited. This limitation stems not only from the immunosuppressive TME but also from intrinsic constraints in CAR architecture, which recruits relatively fewer proximal adaptor molecules essential for organized and sustained signaling compared with the native TCR complex.1 13 31 In particular, while TCR activation leads to robust phosphorylation of LAT, a central scaffold for organizing the signalosome, CAR engagement induces significantly weaker LAT phosphorylation and fails to efficiently recruit LAT-associated effectors.15 LAT orchestrates early T-cell signaling events by recruiting key molecules that are critical for optimal T-cell activation and function.20 32 However, directly incorporating LAT into CAR T cells has been technically challenging due to issues such as low expression levels and tonic signaling.15 18 We therefore designed a synthetic strategy to spatially control LAT function by fusing it to the extracellular and transmembrane domains of PD-1, creating a chimeric switch scaffold (PLAT) that promotes LAT-derived signalosome assembly at the CAR immunological synapse in a PD-L1-dependent manner.

Our data demonstrate that PLAT differs fundamentally from endogenous LAT in its recruitment dynamics. While endogenous LAT is recruited to the synapse on antigen stimulation regardless of ligand context, PLAT localization was strictly dependent on PD-L1 engagement, mediated by its PD-1 extracellular domain. This ligand-gated enrichment allowed for localized and conditional assembly of the LAT signalosome only in the presence of tumor-derived PD-L1, thereby providing spatial specificity while potentially avoiding tonic activation in PD-L1-negative environments. In addition, in the absence of cognate CAR antigen, PD-L1 engagement alone was insufficient to induce measurable T-cell activation, indicating that PLAT functions as a conditional signaling amplifier rather than an autonomous activating receptor.

Importantly, PLAT-expressing CAR T cells formed more structured immunological synapses compared with control groups. En face imaging revealed that, unlike conventional HER2 CAR or HER2 CAR_DNP T cells, HER2 CAR_PLAT-expressing T cells more frequently exhibited a concentric synapse architecture characterized by central CAR accumulation and peripheral F-actin enrichment—a pattern reminiscent of canonical SMACs observed in native TCR signaling. Previous studies have shown that conventional CAR, while capable of forming phosphotyrosine-rich microclusters on antigen engagement, generally fail to establish a stable central supramolecular activation cluster (cSMAC) or exhibit the typical concentric SMAC organization.28 29 33 Dong et al demonstrated that LAT is dispensable for CAR microcluster formation and actin remodeling and that CAR signaling can bypass LAT by directly engaging downstream effectors such as Gads and SLP-76 to activate actin polymerization.34 However, this LAT-independent configuration may result in a disorganized and short-lived synapse in the majority of CAR T cells, lacking the stability and spatial compartmentalization characteristic of TCR-induced synapses.35 36

LAT is known to act as a key scaffold that nucleates signaling complexes essential for cytoskeletal remodeling, including SLP-76, which in turn recruits the Rho-GEF VAV1 and the adaptor protein NCK via its N-terminal phosphotyrosine residues.37 38 These interactions activate actin regulatory pathways that promote F-actin polymerization and reorganization at the T cell-antigen-presenting cell (APC) interface.19 39 Prior studies have shown that disruption of the LAT–SLP-76–VAV/NCK signaling axis leads to impaired actin polymerization and defective SMAC formation.21 35 Moreover, actin cytoskeletal dynamics are required for the supramolecular rearrangement of receptors into mature cSMAC and peripheral SMAC (pSMAC) structures and for maintaining synapse stability over extended periods.4042 In this context, PLAT-mediated LAT recruitment likely reconstitutes this cytoskeletal coordination machinery, allowing CAR T cells to form a more TCR-like, stable, and compartmentalized synapse. These results suggest that the enhanced signaling and effector function observed in PLAT-expressing cells may be directly linked to their ability to reconstruct the spatial organization of the immunological synapse through regulated cytoskeletal remodeling. Consistent with this, recent imaging studies have highlighted the importance of LAT-associated membrane organization at the CAR synapse for efficient signaling and cytolytic function,43 supporting our PLAT-mediated synapse organization and stability enhancement contributes to augmented CAR T-cell activity.

Building on prior approaches aimed at overcoming inhibitory signals within the TME, our results highlight the distinct advantages of PLAT. In contrast to DNP, which blocks PD-1 signaling by acting as a decoy receptor, PLAT offers an active mechanism for signal conversion. Although DNP enhanced conjugate formation with PD-L1-expressing targets, it failed to restore downstream signaling or synapse structure. PLAT, by contrast, induced robust recruitment of LAT-associated effectors, promoted sustained PLCγ1 phosphorylation, and enhanced calcium flux and NFAT/NF-κB activation, leading to improved cytotoxicity and cytokine production in a PD-L1-dependent manner. These results underscore the importance of reconstituting early signalosome components, rather than merely relieving inhibitory signaling, for restoring T-cell functionality in suppressive environments. Moreover, compared with conventional CSRs such as PD-1–CD28, PLAT provided superior early cytotoxic kinetics and more durable function. This kinetic benefit likely reflects the proximal position of PLAT in the CAR signaling cascade, enabling immediate activation of downstream effectors without reliance on slower costimulatory pathways.

Recent studies further support the importance of reinforcing proximal adaptor signaling to enhance CAR T-cell function. In one study, a LAT-based CAR module was incorporated into a bicistronic system alongside a conventional CAR, resulting in improved CAR T-cell sensitivity under antigen-limited conditions.44 Similarly, a membrane-tethered SLP76 approach demonstrated that strengthening downstream adaptor signaling can enhance CAR T-cell activity.45 While these approaches are conceptually aligned with our strategy in targeting proximal signaling pathways, they primarily aim to increase signaling output within the existing activation framework. In contrast, PLAT was designed to couple proximal signal reinforcement with checkpoint-dependent signal conversion, enabling PD-L1 engagement to drive LAT-dependent signalosome assembly and confer context-dependent activation within the immunosuppressive TME.

Despite these findings, this study has several limitations. Our in vivo evaluation was restricted to a single xenograft model, selected to assess PLAT function under a defined immunosuppressive context. Evaluation in additional tumor models, particularly in immune-competent settings that better recapitulate suppressive TMEs, will be important to further support this approach. In addition, evaluation across diverse tumor antigens and the development of LAT-based chimeric switch scaffolds targeting other inhibitory receptors will further define the broader applicability of the CSS framework.

Taken together, our findings identify PLAT as a dual-function synthetic scaffold protein that couples checkpoint inhibition with localized restoration of early T-cell signaling. By enabling ligand-dependent recruitment of LAT to the CAR synapse, PLAT reconstitutes a more organized and durable signaling architecture reminiscent of the native TCR. This strategy not only overcomes PD-1-mediated suppression but also addresses fundamental signaling deficits in current CAR designs, offering a new framework for enhancing CAR T-cell performance in solid tumors.
