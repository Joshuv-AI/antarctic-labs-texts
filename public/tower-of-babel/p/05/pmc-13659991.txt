Characteristics of referral networks for stroke patients among medical institutions under a tiered healthcare system: a social network analysis
Authors: Pengjie Zhang, Zihui Zhou, Ke Yao, Waiguang Hu, Ting Zhang, Lin Zou, Xiaofang Liu, Yanhong Liu, Jingcheng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke represents a major cause of mortality and long-term disability in China, placing a substantial burden on the healthcare system. Within the tiered healthcare framework, an effective two-way referral system is crucial for ensuring timely acute care and coordinated rehabilitation. However, current research primarily focuses on aggregate metrics like referral rates, leaving the structural characteristics of the referral network underexplored. This study uses social network analysis to assess upward and downward referrals for stroke inpatients.MethodsBased on provincial inpatient data, we constructed upward and downward referral networks and analyzed patterns using social network analysis. Indicators at the network, subgroup, and node levels were assessed, including density, K-core, degree centrality, and betweenness centrality. Network visualization was performed using NetDraw 2.140 and Gephi 0.9.2.ResultsThe study included 14,334 inpatient stroke cases, of which 4,948 (34.52%) involved upward transfers across 468 institutions, and 3,954 (27.58%) involved downward transfers across 490 institutions. Both referral networks were primarily characterized by transfers between secondary and tertiary hospitals, accounting for 75.68% of upward and 68.30% of downward referrals. The overall referral network density was low: 0.02 for the upward referral network and 0.01 for the downward referral network. Core subgroups (4-core in the upward referral network and 5-core in the downward referral network) were mostly composed of secondary and tertiary hospitals, with minimal participation from primary care institutions (11.76% and 16.66%, respectively). Node analysis revealed that secondary hospitals exhibited higher degree centrality and betweenness centrality compared to primary care institutions.ConclusionThe upward referral rate for stroke in Hunan Province slightly exceeds the downward referral rate. Both the upward and downward referral networks exhibit a clear hierarchical stratification characterized by core, intermediate, and peripheral layers identified through K-core decomposition, with tertiary hospitals occupying a central position and secondary hospitals as key receiving and coordinating hubs. However, primary care institutions show limited participation, and overall network connectivity remains sparse with insufficient regional collaboration. These findings suggest that facilitating the smooth transfer of patients during the rehabilitation phase from tertiary hospitals to lower-level institutions, strengthening the transitional role of secondary hospitals, and enhancing the rehabilitation capacity of primary care institutions may help optimize the stroke tiered care system.

Background

Stroke is a major global public health challenge, being the third leading cause of death and a leading contributor to disability worldwide. In 2021, there were approximately 93.8 million prevalent cases of stroke, 11.9 million new incident cases, and 7.3 million stroke-related deaths, accounting for 10.7% of all deaths worldwide. Over 12 million people suffer a first stroke each year, and more than 100 million individuals worldwide are living with the consequences of stroke [1]. In China, the burden of stroke is particularly substantial. In 2019, there were approximately 28.76 million stroke patients in China and stroke caused about 2.19 million deaths, accounting for more than one-third of global stroke deaths, with a crude mortality rate of 153.9 per 100,000 population [2]. The economic burden of stroke in China has exceeded 1 trillion yuan [3], imposing significant pressure on society and families. Stroke care requires urgent upward referral for acute treatments, such as intravenous thrombolysis (typically within a 4.5-hour time window from symptom onset) or endovascular thrombectomy (up to 6 ~ 24 h in selected patients with large vessel occlusion)as well as organized downward referrals for long-term rehabilitation and secondary prevention during the recovery phase to optimize functional outcomes and reduce disability [4, 5]. Since the 2015 release of the State Council General Office’s “Guiding Opinions on Promoting the Construction of a Tiered Healthcare System,” China has been progressively establishing a healthcare order based on “primary care at the grassroots level, two-way referrals, separation of acute and chronic care, and coordinated efforts across levels.” Policies like medical consortium development and regional medical center planning have further promoted healthcare resource integration and service coordination. Therefore, two-way referral for stroke not only ensures that patients with varying disease severities receive appropriate medical care but also serves as a critical measure under the tiered healthcare system to conserve healthcare resources, enhance service efficiency, and promote equitable access to medical services [6, 7].

Social network analysis elucidates institutional patient-sharing patterns and can inform optimization of referral pathways and service delivery [8]. This approach is particularly applicable in studying multi-institutional stroke care systems, as it can uncover how healthcare institutions collaborate and how their connections can be leveraged to optimize patient referrals and access to high-quality care [9].

Existing studies on referral patterns in China indicate that the downward referral rate is lower than the upward referral rate [10–12]. Furthermore, both tertiary and secondary hospitals tend to favor referrals within the same tier, with limited collaboration with primary care institutions. This creates a situation where patients can be referred upward but face challenges when being referred downward (“easy to move up but hard to move down”). However, current stroke referral studies primarily focus on population-level metrics such as “referral frequency”, “referral rate”, and “influencing factors” [13], while overlooking the structural characteristics of referral networks between medical institutions. Moreover, existing studies on stroke referral networks [12] fail to simultaneously differentiate between upward referrals in the acute phase and downward referrals in the rehabilitation phase, which results in a lack of targeted guidance for practice.

This study, based on inpatient data from Hunan Province in 2023, aims to explore both upward and downward referral patterns among stroke patients. This study adopted social network analysis to investigate the structural characteristics of separately constructed upward and downward referral networks, with the goal of generating scientific evidence and empirical insights to enhance stroke care accessibility, efficiency, and patient outcomes.

Methods

Study population and data sources

The study population consisted of medical institutions in Hunan Province that admitted stroke patients in 2023. Hunan, located in Central China, is a populous inland province with approximately 66 million residents and a mix of urban and rural healthcare settings, with Changsha serving as the provincial capital and economic hub. The province experiences a higher stroke burden than southern coastal regions, particularly with an elevated incidence of intracerebral hemorrhage [14]. This study included 468 institutions participating in upward referrals and 490 institutions participating in downward referrals (tertiary, secondary, and primary-level hospitals), all of which recorded inpatient admissions for stroke during the study period, representing a broad spectrum of healthcare settings across the province. The data were sourced from the Health Statistics Information Network Direct Reporting System of the Hunan Provincial Health Commission, including: patient medical record front page data, which contains demographic information, the hospitalization process, and diagnosis; and annual reports from medical institutions providing information such as institution level and type. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xiangya School of Public Health, Central South University (Approval No.: XYGW-2023-97) and all ethical principles were strictly adhered to throughout the process.

Data collection and organization

Data preprocessing

Database construction

This study matched the medical record front page data with institutional annual report data using institution codes. Each institution was assigned a unique five-digit code structured as follows: the first two uppercase letters represent the city location, the third letter denotes the institution type, the fourth letter indicates whether the institution is a member of the Hunan Stroke Union, and the fifth digit serves as the serial number for institutions of the same type within the same city. For example, ༂CSay1༂ refers to a tertiary medical institution located in Changsha that belongs to the Hunan Stroke Union.

Screening of referral patients

The operational definition of referral [15–17]: A patient who is discharged and transferred to another medical institution within the same province for treatment of the same disease within 30 days. Evidence indicates that, among stroke patients, referral rates are highest during the 7–30 day period following discharge and decrease with longer post-discharge intervals. Consequently, this study examines referrals that occur within 30 days of discharge [18].

Transferred patients were included if they met the following criteria: they were hospitalized within Hunan Province with a primary discharge diagnosis of stroke or post-stroke sequelae (ICD-10 codes: I60, I61, I63, I64, I69.0, I69.1, I69.3, I69.4); their admission dates fell between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, with discharge dates between January 1, 2023, and January 30, 2024; they were aged 18 years or older; they had at least two hospitalizations (n ≥ 2); the interval between consecutive hospitalizations did not exceed 30 days; and the transfer occurred between different levels of medical institutions (i.e., upward or downward referral).

Patients were excluded if they had zero or negative hospitalization costs, missing medical institution codes, or duplicate admission records with identical patient names, identification numbers, and admission dates.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis

The variables used in this study were categorized into categorical and quantitative variables. Categorical variables included referral type (upward or downward) and hospital level (tertiary, secondary, and primary healthcare institutions), which were used to describe the composition of referral patterns and core subgroups. Quantitative variables included the number of stroke cases, the number of participating institutions, network density, and node-level centrality measures (degree centrality and betweenness centrality).

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages (%), while quantitative variables were presented using counts or calculated network metrics.

Construction of the referral network

This study separately constructed an upward referral network and a downward referral network, where nodes represented healthcare institutions admitting stroke patients in Hunan Province in 2023, and directed edges represents the referral relationship and direction between healthcare institutions.

Analytical software

The software used in this study included: ① SAS 9.4 and Excel 22.0 for data cleaning; ② IBM SPSS 26.0 for descriptive analysis; ③ Ucinet 6.0, Gephi 0.9.2, and NetDraw 2.140 for calculating network centrality metrics and visualizing the network structure.

Results

Basic characteristics of inpatient institutions for stroke referrals in Hunan Province

In 2023, Hunan Province recorded 14,334 inpatient admissions for stroke. Among these, 3,954 patients were involved in downward referrals, engaging 490 medical institutions. Additionally, 4,948 patients were involved in upward referrals, involving 468 medical institutions.

As shown in Table 1, within the 2023 upward and downward referral networks, the number of referral relationships between medical institutions was 2,479 for upward referrals and 1,981 for downward referrals. The majority of referrals occurred between secondary and tertiary hospitals, comprising 75.68% of upward and 68.30% of downward referrals, respectively. In contrast, primary care institutions played a minimal role.

Characteristics of referral networks

Overall network characteristics

The upward referral network comprised 468 institutions with 2,479 connecting edges, while the downward referral network comprised 490 institutions with 1,981 edges. These edges represent the institutional referral relationships. The density was 0.02 for the upward referral network and 0.01 for the downward referral network. The average referral weight was 2.00 for both networks, meaning that, on average, each existing referral relationship involved two patient transfer events (or two referral actions) during the observed period.

Node characteristics

Figure 1 displays, from left to right, the in-degree, out-degree, and betweenness centrality within the upward referral network. In-degree and out-degree represent the number of referral relationships received and sent by the institution separately [8], and betweenness centrality refers to the degree to which the institution acts as an “intermediary” in referral relationships between two other medical institutions [20]. Within both referral networks, transfers primarily occurred between secondary and tertiary hospitals: in the upward referral network, secondary hospitals exhibited the highest out-degree while tertiary hospitals had the highest in-degree; conversely, in the downward referral network, tertiary hospitals showed the highest out-degree, and secondary hospitals had the highest in-degree. Secondary hospitals exhibited relatively high betweenness centrality in the upward referral network, but demonstrated weaker betweenness centrality in the downward network, with only a few showing both favorable in-degree and betweenness centrality. Primary care institutions generally demonstrated relatively lower in- and out-degree.

Subgroup characteristics

The K-core analysis results are shown in Table 2; Fig. 2. The K-core decomposition of the upward and downward referral networks yielded four and five subgroups, respectively, ranging from the 1-core to the 4-core for upward referrals, and to the 5-core for downward referrals. The K-value indicates that a subgroup consists of institutions with a degree no less than K; this means that each member has referral ties to at least K other medical institutions. A higher K-value suggests that institutions within the subgroup have more referral collaborations with other institutions [21] .

Upward referrals: The 4-core was the core subgroup, comprising only 17 institutions dominated by secondary (47.06%) and tertiary hospitals (41.18%) and concentrated in Changsha City. The 3-core subgroup included 129 institutions, primarily secondary hospitals (53.49%), distributed across Yongzhou, Chenzhou, and Hengyang. The two subgroups primarily received patients, manifesting as tertiary hospitals receiving patients from secondary hospitals.

The 2-core and 1-core subgroups occupied the network periphery, primarily comprising primary care institutions and secondary hospitals, with tertiary hospitals accounting for only 17.53% and 11.72%, respectively. They were predominantly distributed in Yiyang, Zhangjiajie, and Yongzhou. These two subgroups primarily involved outbound patient transfers, manifesting as referrals from secondary hospitals to tertiary hospitals and transfers from primary care institutions to secondary hospitals.

Downward referrals: The 5-core was the core subgroup, comprising 24 institutions with equal shares of secondary and tertiary hospitals (41.67% each) in Changsha and Hengyang. The 4-core and 3-core subgroups formed intermediate layers, located in Changsha, Loudi, Yongzhou, and Shaoyang, still dominated by secondary hospitals. In terms of patient flow, all three subgroups primarily involved outbound patients, mainly manifesting as tertiary hospitals transferring patients to secondary hospitals.

The 1-core and 2-core subgroups occupied the network periphery, with primary care institutions accounting for 37.23% and 24.02%, respectively. These subgroups were primarily distributed in Chenzhou, and Yiyang. Both subgroups primarily received patients, manifesting as secondary hospitals accepting referrals from tertiary hospitals.

Discussion

Current status of referrals for hospitalized stroke patients

In Hunan Province, the ratio of upward to downward transfers for stroke patients was 5:4, with upward transfers slightly exceeding downward transfers. This ratio is lower than both the 2013 in Chengdu City (approximately 20.3:1) [22] and the 2018 national average (1.4:1) [23], which suggests the preliminary effectiveness of the tiered healthcare system. However, the following issues persist:

(1) Upward referrals primarily involved secondary to tertiary hospital transfers, with minimal participation from primary care institutions, accounting for only 24.32% of all upward transfers. This likely results from critically ill patients initially seeking treatment directly at higher-level hospitals.

(2) Downward referrals mostly involved tertiary hospitals transferring patients to secondary hospitals (68.30%), with secondary hospitals infrequently referring patients further down to primary care institutions (only 18.53%). This reality stands in contrast to the three-tier stroke rehabilitation network advocated by the Chinese Stroke Prevention and Treatment Guidelines (2021 Edition). The guidelines propose that after receiving initial acute-phase treatment in general hospitals, stabilized patients should transition to specialized rehabilitation facilities and ultimately to community-based care [24]. The lower density of the downward referral network, combined with the peripheral position of primary healthcare institutions and the high betweenness centrality of secondary hospitals, suggests that referral pathways between secondary hospitals and primary care institutions were relatively weak. Previous studies and policy reports have proposed several factors that may contribute to this pattern, including: first, the profit-driven operational mechanism of hospitals, coupled with the competing economic interests between community health service institutions and higher-level hospitals, may create a structural incentive for patient retention rather than facilitating referrals [25]; second, insufficient flow of quality medical resources to the grassroots level [26]; third, the phenomenon of “emphasizing treatment over rehabilitation” persists within the healthcare system; fourth, existing policy incentives, such as medical insurance payment and performance evaluation systems, may not effectively promote downward referrals; fifth, primary care institutions’ limited rehabilitation service capacity, which may contribute secondary hospitals to “hesitate to transfer“ [27]. However, because the present study was based on administrative referral data and lacked detailed clinical and organizational information, the relative contribution of these factors could not be directly assessed. Therefore, these explanations should be regarded as potential interpretations rather than confirmed determinants of the observed network structure. Future research integrating administrative, clinical, and organizational data is needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying referral patterns and to identify the most effective strategies for improving stroke rehabilitation referral systems. The findings of this study may provide useful insights for ongoing efforts to improve stroke rehabilitation referral systems. Potential areas for improvement are as follows. Firstly, by reforming hospital operational mechanisms and aligning economic incentives between institutions, the system can shift from profit-driven patient retention to facilitating coordinated referrals. Secondly, expanding and decentralizing high-quality resources, through national/provincial medical centers, clinical specialty development, and professional training will directly enhance the flow of resources to the grassroots. Thirdly, a dedicated focus on strengthening rehabilitation services is needed to correct the historical emphasis on treatment over recovery. Fourthly, policy incentives such as medical insurance payment models and performance evaluations should be optimized to actively support and incentivize downward referral. Lastly, building rehabilitation service capacity at the primary care level will address the current hesitancy of secondary hospitals to transfer patients. However, further research is required to evaluate the effectiveness of these approaches and to determine their relative contribution to referral network development.

Characteristics of the referral networks for hospitalized stroke patients

Overall network characteristics

Network density, reflects the closeness of referral connections among medical institutions within the network, ranging from [0,1], with higher values indicating a more tightly connected network structure [18]. The upward referral network exhibited a density of 0.02, whereas the downward referral network had a density of 0.01, indicating that only a small proportion of all possible referral relationships were realized. Similar characteristics have been reported in other healthcare referral networks. For example, Landon et al. reported physician-sharing networks in the United States with network densities ranging from 0.01 to 0.67 across different hospital referral regions and thresholding strategies [28], while Tsai et al. reported a density of 0.24 in an emergency referral network involving 199 healthcare institutions in Taiwan [29]. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in network scale, node definitions, and referral mechanisms, these studies indicate that healthcare referral networks typically exhibit selective rather than fully connected interaction patterns.

Combined with the relatively high centralization observed in the present study, the low density suggests that stroke referrals in Hunan Province were concentrated among a limited number of institutions, while many potential referral links remained unrealized.

Network subgroup characteristics

Analysis from a subgroup perspective reveals the following key insights: Firstly, the 4-core (upward) and 5-core (downward) subgroups represent the network’s core. Institutions within these subgroups maintain close connections and are concentrated in Changsha and Hengyang. Notably, Changsha occupied the highest core layer in the upward referral network, whereas both Changsha and Hengyang were embedded in the highest core layer of the downward referral network. This pattern suggests that the two cities play distinct roles within the referral system. Changsha functions primarily as the provincial hub for acute-care referrals, attracting patients from a broad geographic area, while Hengyang appears to play a more prominent role in facilitating regional rehabilitation and downward referral coordination in southern Hunan. These hubs undertake substantial provincial-level critical care and referral tasks, primarily between secondary and tertiary hospitals. Beyond their stable referral partnerships with at least four to five designated institutions, these hubs also receive upwardly referred patients from local and other prefectural hospitals and coordinate their onward transfer after the acute phase. Their central positions within the network underscore their role as key provincial referral hubs that effectively advance the goal of “managing major and critical illnesses within the province“ [30]. Secondly, the 3-core upward transfer and 3-core/4-core downward transfer subgroups occupy the intermediate layer of the network hierarchy. These subgroups represent institutions with a moderate level of connectivity, positioned between the highly cohesive core and the sparsely connected peripheral nodes. These subgroups primarily comprise secondary and tertiary hospitals in cities such as Loudi, Chenzhou, and Yueyang. These institutions maintain strong connections with local medical institutions, facilitating regional referral collaboration to some extent. They form localized regional medical centers that progressively work toward the goal of “addressing common illnesses at the city/county level“ [31, 32] Thirdly, disparities in regional distribution within the upward and downward referral networks reflect an uneven distribution of healthcare resources, particularly in peripheral subgroups like 1-core and 2-core (predominantly comprising primary care institutions and secondary hospitals) where inter-institutional referrals are insufficient. These are predominantly found in cities like Yiyang and Zhangjiajie, where uneven distribution of medical resources and lack of referral collaboration with surrounding regional medical centers, especially insufficient coordination with primary care institutions, indicate that local regional medical centers have not fully leveraged primary care institutions. Without strong vertical integration, primary care institutions remain weakly connected to higher-level hospitals, limiting resource sharing, technical guidance, and capacity building at the grassroots level. This lack of effective vertical linkage in the referral process hinders the comprehensive implementation of tiered healthcare and impedes the goal of “addressing routine illnesses at the primary care level“ [33]. Fourthly, this study’s analysis of the stroke upward and downward referral networks in Hunan Province revealed limited participation of primary care institutions within the core subgroups. However, certain key nodes emerged. Specifically, six institutions located in Hengyang and Changsha performed notably well: two had established upward referral links with multiple secondary hospitals, and four served as crucial downstream receiving units. Their success may be attributed to regional policy impetus, because Changsha serves as the provincial comprehensive medical hub and Hengyang as the regional medical center for southern Hunan, granting them early advantages in medical alliance development and resource connectivity. This suggests that targeted regional policies may effectively break down resource barriers, and such policy pilots should be promoted to create initial momentum for referral collaboration. Fifthly, collaboration within subgroups is strong, reflecting the initial success of tiered healthcare initiatives such as medical consortiums and integrated medical consortiums. However, connections between different subgroups remain weak, which may hinder collaborative development among healthcare institutions within the province and overall improvement of medical service standards. Currently, the focus is primarily on urban medical alliances and county-level medical alliances. Therefore, promoting cooperation between different subgroups by establishing a smooth intra-provincial referral system among medical institutions, particularly facilitating cross-regional referrals, could be key to enhancing overall healthcare service efficiency [34].

Network node characteristics

This study, based on social network analysis theory, examined the functional positioning and influence of medical institutions at various levels within the upward and downward referral networks for stroke inpatients. The stroke inpatient referral network in Hunan Province exhibits a distinct hierarchical stratification characterized by core, intermediate, and peripheral layers identified through K-core decomposition. Tertiary hospitals occupy the core of the network, primarily responsible for acute-phase treatment and the downward transfer of recovery/rehabilitation patients. Their dominant position in the network is evidenced by their central location within high K-core subgroups and their extensive referral connections with other institutions, indicating a key role in organizing and directing patient flows. Secondary hospitals function as intermediate hubs, effectively receiving patients transferred down from tertiary hospitals. However, their capacity for downward referrals remains underutilized, and they fail to efficiently transfer convalescent patients to primary care institutions. Strengthening their role as transfer hubs is essential. Primary care institutions exhibit peripheral clustering and core sparsity within the network. Their importance is inadequately reflected, and their capacity to provide rehabilitation services is not fully realized [22]. These findings reveal significant gaps in the systematic integration and continuity of the current three-tier stroke rehabilitation network. They suggest the need to optimize collaboration mechanisms among healthcare institutions at all levels, enhance the role of secondary hospitals as transfer hubs, and strengthen the rehabilitation service capacity of primary care institutions to promote the effective implementation of the tiered healthcare system.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, Hunan is a mid-tier central province with typical Chinese public health challenges. While relevant, our findings have limited generalizability due to sole reliance on 2023 inpatient data from Hunan, and may not represent other provinces with different healthcare resources, policies, or medical consortium development. Second, the administrative data lacked detailed clinical and decision-making variables, constraining the depth of our analysis. At the patient level, previous studies have shown that post-stroke referral and rehabilitation transitions are influenced by clinical need, rehabilitation capacity, information continuity, caregiver perceptions, and inter-institutional coordination. However, we could not adjust for clinical factors such as stroke type (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic), severity scores (e.g., NIHSS), or patient age and functional status, which likely influence referral decisions. At the institutional level, the data cannot elucidate the underlying reasons for specific referrals, including patients’ personal and family preferences, or the detailed financial arrangements and informal agreements between hospitals. Therefore, the present study could not assess the extent to which referral patterns were driven by clinical needs versus health-system factors. In addition, Community Health Centers and Township Health Centers were grouped together as primary healthcare institutions. Although this classification is consistent with China’s hierarchical healthcare system, differences in service capacity, resource availability, and referral practices between urban and rural primary care institutions could not be examined in the present study. Third, as a retrospective observational study based on social network analysis, our research identifies associations and structural characteristics but cannot establish causality. For example, while we found that secondary hospitals had high betweenness centrality, we cannot definitively conclude that this central position directly causes or results from specific policy effects. Finally, the 30-day referral window, while operational and based on existing evidence, might have missed some delayed referrals for rehabilitation or readmissions due to complications occurring outside this period. Consequently, the participation of rehabilitation and primary care institutions may have been underestimated, and it is recommended to extend the follow-up period in future studies.

Conclusion

Among stroke inpatients in Hunan Province, upward referrals slightly outnumber downward referrals. Upward referrals primarily involve tertiary hospitals receiving patients from secondary hospitals, while downward referrals mainly consist of tertiary hospitals transferring patients to secondary hospitals. Primary care institutions show limited participation. Concurrently, referral collaboration between medical institutions is weak and exhibits regional heterogeneity. Tertiary hospitals occupy a central position in the two-way referral network, yet the crucial roles of secondary hospitals and primary care institutions in the referral process remain underutilized. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen the functional roles of institutions at all levels, enhance the service capacity of primary care institutions, encourage the downward flow of resources from secondary hospitals, and foster greater collaboration with primary care institutions. These efforts may contribute to promoting more equitable and accessible healthcare services.
