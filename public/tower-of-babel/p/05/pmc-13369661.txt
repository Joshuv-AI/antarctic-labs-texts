Effectiveness of guided endodontic microsurgery using a trephine bur in critical anatomical regions: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Rami Kaddoura, Thuraya Lazkani, Yasser Alsayed Tolibah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis randomized controlled clinical trial evaluated the effectiveness of guided endodontic microsurgery (GEMS) compared to conventional endodontic microsurgery (EMS) in critical anatomy regions.Materials and methodsTwenty-eight patients (ages 19–58 years) requiring endodontic microsurgery in critical anatomical regions were randomly allocated to guided surgery (n = 14) using 3D-printed surgical guides with trephine burs or conventional microsurgery (n = 14). Primary outcomes included surgical procedure time. Secondary outcomes were periapical lesion size measured by CBCT at 6, 12 months and success rate at 1-year follow-up and complications. statistical analysis employed independent t-tests and chi-square tests.ResultsThe guided group demonstrated significantly shorter surgical time (18.21 ± 3.45 min vs 26.07 ± 4.29 min) (independent t-test, p < 0.001). Complete lesion resolution at 1 year was achieved in 92.9% of guided cases, compared with 71.4% in conventional cases (chi-square, p = 0.139). No complications were observed.ConclusionsGuided endodontic microsurgery using a 3D-printed surgical guide with trephine burs significantly reduces surgical time. The technique demonstrates a clinically relevant trend toward improved healing outcomes, supporting its adoption for complex endodontic surgical cases.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05283252Subject terms: Root canal treatment, Dental equipment

Introduction

The concept of guided surgery, well established in implant dentistry, has recently been adapted to endodontic microsurgery, with encouraging outcomes [1]. Guided endodontic (GEMS), commonly referred to as targeted endodontic microsurgery (TEMS), employs computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology to fabricate surgical guides that transfer virtual surgical planning directly to the operative field [2]. This approach aims to enhance surgical precision, minimise tissue trauma, shorten operative time, and improve predictability, particularly in cases with complex anatomical considerations [3]. Early reports of guided endodontic surgery demonstrated the feasibility of using 3D-printed templates to guide osteotomy and facilitate apex localisation [4, 5]. The fabrication of three-dimensional printed surgical guides for endodontic microsurgery requires a sequential workflow that includes CBCT data acquisition, dental impression/intraoral scanning to capture soft-tissue surfaces, virtual planning using dedicated software, and guide production using 3D printing [6].

Trephine burs were originally developed for implant surgery but have recently been adapted for endodontic applications [7]. Trephine burs have introduced a notable change in endodontic surgical practice by allowing osteotomy and root-end resection to be performed in a single step [8]. When used with surgical guides, these hollow cylindrical instruments enable controlled removal of the cortical bone and root apex in accordance with the virtual surgical plan [8]. This technique has been associated with several potential advantages, including shorter operative time, reduced bone removal, a lower risk of root perforation, and greater consistency in root-end bevel angulation [6]. Appropriate selection of trephine bur dimensions is an important factor in achieving predictable surgical outcomes. The size selection guided by root morphology, lesion dimensions and the proximity of adjacent anatomical structures [8].

Endodontic microsurgery (EMS) in critical anatomical regions requires careful preoperative planning to avoid iatrogenic injury to adjacent vital structures. The mental nerve and inferior alveolar nerve are at risk during surgery on mandibular premolars and molars. The nerve injury may result in temporary or permanent paresthesia [9]. In the maxilla, the proximity of root apices to the maxillary sinus presents unique challenges, particularly for palatal roots of molars and premolars [10, 11]. Thick cortical bone plates, particularly in the mandibular molar region, can obscure visualisation and complicate apex localisation during conventional endodontic microsurgery [12].

While numerous case reports and case series have documented the technical feasibility and short-term outcomes of guided endodontic microsurgery, comparative studies evaluating clinical outcomes against conventional microsurgery techniques remain limited [12, 13]. A recent scoping review found that guided endodontic microsurgery resulted in minimal iatrogenic errors, reduced chair time, and no postoperative issues [14]. Hawkins et al. conducted a surgical simulation study comparing targeted endodontic microsurgery (TEMS) with traditional endodontic microsurgery (EMS) and found that TEMS significantly reduced surgical time, achieved bevel angles more closely approaching 0°, and resulted in significantly less root resection volume [15]. However, these articles highlighted the need for randomised controlled trials with adequate follow-up and objective measurement of operative times and clinical outcomes. A recent systematic review concluded that guided apical microsurgery using 3D-printed surgical guides demonstrates clear advantages over conventional techniques in terms of precision and predictability [16]. However, the authors noted that most available evidence consists of case reports and in vitro studies, emphasising the need for well-designed clinical trials to establish the clinical efficacy of guided approaches.

Despite increasing evidence supporting the advantages of guided endodontic microsurgery (GEMS) with trephine burs, there is a paucity of randomised clinical trials evaluating clinical outcomes, such as surgical time, healing, and long-term success rates, in critical anatomical regions. Furthermore, the impact of guided surgery on surgical efficiency remains unclear. The present randomised controlled clinical trial was designed to address these knowledge gaps by comparing guided endodontic microsurgery (GEMS) with 3D-printed surgical guides and trephine burs with conventional endodontic microsurgery (EMS) in patients requiring treatment in critical anatomical regions.

The present randomized controlled clinical trial was designed to compare guided and conventional endodontic microsurgery in critical anatomical regions, with surgical procedure time as the primary outcome measure and treatment success rate and complication profile evaluated as secondary exploratory outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design, settings, and ethical approval

This prospective, parallel-group, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted at the Department of Endodontics (Faculty of Dentistry, Damascus University, Syria). The study protocol was approved by the Damascus University Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 0153-08-2022-DAM) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05283252). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study was reported according to CONSORT guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits.

Patient selection

Patients referred to the Department of Endodontics for endodontic microsurgery during the study period were screened for eligibility. The principal investigator assessed all cases based on clinical and radiographic examinations to confirm their suitability for inclusion.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients in good general health, aged between 18 and 50 years, presenting with anterior or posterior teeth indicated for endodontic microsurgery due to their proximity to critical anatomical structures, and who agreed to participate in the study. Critical anatomical regions were defined as areas where the root apex located in close proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve, mental nerve, maxillary sinus floor, greater palatine artery/foramen, or regions with thick cortical bone compromising surgical access as confirmed by preoperative CBCT.

Patients were excluded if they had a history of allergy to any materials or medications used in the trial, had serious systemic diseases contraindicating surgical procedures, or were pregnant. Additionally, teeth were excluded if they were non-restorable, associated with vertical root fractures, exhibited probing depths greater than 5 mm, or presented with pathologies incompatible with endodontic microsurgery. All eligible patients who agreed to participate signed a written informed consent form after receiving a full explanation of the study procedures.

Sample size calculation

As no previous randomized clinical trials evaluating guided endodontic microsurgery in critical anatomical regions were available, sample size estimation was based on the most relevant available comparative study and was specifically powered to detect clinically meaningful differences in surgical procedure time [17], whereas analyses of the remaining outcomes were considered exploratory. The sample size calculation was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) software based on the primary outcome of surgical procedure time. Assuming a mean difference of 6 min between groups [15], a standard deviation of 4 min, an alpha level of 5%, and a power of 80%, a minimum of 12 cases per group was required. Accounting for the potential for withdrawal from the study, the target enrollment was increased to 14 per group, for a total of 28 participants.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Following confirmation of eligibility and completion of baseline assessments, cases were randomly allocated to either the guided surgery group or the conventional surgery group using a computer-generated randomization sequence with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Randomization was performed by an independent researcher not involved in patient care using sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. Due to the nature of the surgical intervention, blinding of the surgeon was not possible. However, the outcome assessors for radiographic measurements and wound-healing scores were blinded to group allocation.

Preoperative assessment and CBCT imaging

All patients underwent a comprehensive clinical examination, including percussion, palpation, periodontal probing, and assessment of tooth mobility. Preoperative CBCT scans were obtained using the PaX-i3D Green system (Vatech, South Korea) with a standardised protocol (120 kV, 5 mA, 0.2 mm voxel size, field of view 8 × 8 cm) to evaluate periapical pathology, root anatomy, cortical bone thickness, and proximity to critical anatomical structures. Periapical lesion size was measured in three dimensions using Materialise Mimics software (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium), and lesion volume was calculated in cubic millimetres (mm3). For patients allocated to the guided surgery group, CBCT DICOM files were exported for surgical planning. Intraoral optical scans of the surgical site were obtained using a desktop 3D scanner (Medit, Seoul, Korea), and the digital data were exported as stereolithography (STL) files. The CBCT data and intraoral scan files were imported into implant surgical planning software (Blue Sky Bio, Langenhagen, Germany) and superimposed to create a virtual three-dimensional guide.

Surgical guides were designed as tooth-bone-supported appliances with extensions to adjacent teeth for enhanced stability. The surgical guide design files were exported in STL format and fabricated using a Peopoly Moai Laser SLA 3D printer (Peopoly, Los Angeles, CA, USA) with biocompatible resin. Guides were post-processed according to manufacturer instructions, including washing in isopropyl alcohol and post-curing under UV light. Trephine burs were selected based on the planned osteotomy dimensions. Trephine burs with outer diameters of 4.0, 4.5, or 5.0 mm and lengths of 10–12 mm were used, depending on the specific case requirements. All trephine burs featured a hollow cylindrical design with cutting edges on the apical end (Fig. 1).

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by the same experienced endodontist (R.K.) under local anaesthesia (2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine) and using a surgical operating microscope Zumax OMS2350 (Zumax Medical Co, Ltd, Jiangsu, China).

Postoperative care

All patients received standardized postoperative instructions including: application of ice packs for the first 24 h, soft food for 2 days, gentle oral hygiene and suture removal at 7 days. Postoperative medications included: ibuprofen 400 mg every 6 h for 3 days (or as needed for pain), amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 h for 5 days (or clindamycin 300 mg every 8 h for penicillin-allergic patients) according to the European Society of Endodontology guidelines [18], and chlorhexidine 0.12% mouth rinse twice daily for 7 days.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution for all variables, which indicated that the data followed a normal distribution. Independent samples t-test was used to compare continuous quantitative variables (surgical time, lesion volume reduction) between the two groups. Categorical variables and frequencies (success rates) were analyzed using the chi-square test. A significance level of 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Ethics approval

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Damascus University (approve number: approval Number: 0153-08-2022-DAM).

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 32 patients were initially screened for eligibility. Four patients were excluded (2 due to inadequate bone volume, 1 due to vertical root fracture, and 1 declined participation). Twenty-eight cases were enrolled and randomly allocated to the guided surgery group (n = 14) or conventional surgery group (n = 14). All 28 cases completed the 1-year follow-up with no dropouts (Fig. 4).

CONSORT flowchart of the study showing patient screening, randomization, allocation, and follow-up.

28 cases were randomized (14 per group), and all completed 1-year follow-up with no dropouts.

The study population consisted of 12 males (42.86%) and 16 females (57.14%) with ages ranging from 19 to 50 years. The distribution of surgical sites included: mandibular premolars, mandibular molars, maxillary premolars and maxillary molars. All cases met the criteria for critical anatomical regions with mean distance to critical structures of 2.1 ± 0.6 mm.

Surgical procedure time

The guided surgery group demonstrated significantly shorter surgical procedure time compared to the conventional surgery group (Fig. 5). The mean surgical time for the guided group was 18.21 ± 3.45 min (range 13-25 min) versus 26.07 ± 4.29 min (range 19-34 min) for the conventional group (independent t-test, p < 0.001), representing a 30.1% reduction in surgical time.

Comparison of surgical time (min) between guided and conventional groups (independent t-test, p < 0.001).

Complications

No intraoperative complications were observed in either group. Specifically, there were no cases of root perforation, excessive bleeding requiring intervention beyond routine hemostatic measures, or nerve injury. No postoperative complications including infection, wound dehiscence, or paresthesia were reported in either group during the 12-month follow-up period.

Periapical lesion size reduction

Both groups demonstrated progressive reduction in periapical lesion size over the 12-month follow-up period (Fig. 6). At 6 months, the guided group showed a mean lesion volume reduction of 68.4 ± 18.2% compared to 58.7 ± 21.5% in the conventional group (independent t-test, p = 0.198). At 12 months, the guided group achieved a mean lesion volume reduction of 89.3 ± 12.4% compared to 78.6 ± 19.8% in the conventional group (independent t-test, p = 0.089). While the differences did not reach statistical significance, the guided group consistently demonstrated greater lesion resolution at both time points.

Comparison of Periapical lesion healing between guided and conventional groups over 12-month follow-up (independent t-test, p = 0.198 at 6 months, p = 0.089 at 12 months).

Success rate

At 1-year follow-up, the guided surgery group achieved a higher success rate compared to the conventional surgery group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7). Complete lesion resolution (100% reduction in lesion volume) was observed in 13 of 14 cases (92.9%) in the guided group versus 10 of 14 cases (71.4%) in the conventional group (chi-square test, p = 0.139). One case in the guided group (7.1%) showed incomplete healing with 65% lesion reduction but remained asymptomatic and was classified as a success. Four cases in the conventional group (28.6%) showed incomplete healing with lesion reductions ranging from 52% to 78%, all remaining asymptomatic and classified as successes. No failures (persistent symptoms or lesion enlargement) were observed in either group.

Comparison of Treatment Success Rate between guided and conventional groups over 12-month follow-up (chi-square test, p = 0.139).

Discussion

The study presented a comparison of guided endodontic microsurgery using three-dimensional-printed surgical guides and trephine burs with conventional microsurgery in critical anatomical regions. The findings indicate that guided surgery significantly reduces operative time and demonstrates a clinically meaningful trend toward improved periapical healing. These findings support the adoption of guided endodontic microsurgery as a valuable technique for managing complex cases where precision and safety are paramount.

The 30.1% reduction in surgical time observed in the guided group (18.21 min vs 26.07 min; p < 0.001) represents a clinically significant improvement in surgical efficiency. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating time savings with guided approaches [15]. The time savings in guided surgery can be attributed to several factors: elimination of exploratory osteotomy to locate the apex, precise predetermined osteotomy dimensions, minimizing unnecessary bone removal and simultaneous osteotomy and root-end resection with the trephine bur [3, 7, 15]. The use of trephine burs in guided surgery represents a qualitative shift from the traditional approach of osteotomy followed by root-end resection. Nagy et al. described this single-step technique and reported complete bony regeneration at 1-year follow-up [7].

The present study confirms the clinical viability of this approach in a randomized trial. The guided technique is advantageous, as evidenced by the root-end resection achieved without root perforation, excessive resection and complications that can occur with freehand techniques [15, 22]. Previous studies have demonstrated the superior precision of guided approaches. Zhao et al. reported that 3D-printed surgical guides resulted in significantly less deviation in apical resection length and angle than conventional surgery [23]. Antal et al. found a median angular deviation of 3.95° and a mean apex removal error of 0.19 mm with guided surgery using trephine burs [1]. These accuracy improvements translate into more predictable clinical outcomes, particularly in critical anatomical regions where millimeter-level precision is essential to avoid iatrogenic injury [24, 25].

Both groups demonstrated excellent periapical healing over the 12-month follow-up period, with 100% success rates when success was defined as the absence of symptoms and >50% reduction in the lesion. The guided group showed a clinically relevant trend toward greater lesion resolution, with 92.9% achieving complete resolution compared to 71.4% in the conventional group (p = 0.139). While this difference did not reach statistical significance, the 21.5 percentage point difference represents a clinically meaningful improvement that warrants consideration. The high success rates observed in both groups (100%) are consistent with contemporary endodontic microsurgery outcomes reported in the literature. A systematic review by Setzer et al. reported success rates of 87–94% for modern endodontic microsurgery [26]. The present study’s success rates may reflect careful patient selection, the use of a surgical microscope, ultrasonic root-end preparation, and Bioceramic root-end filling—all factors known to enhance outcomes. The greater lesion resolution observed in the guided group (89.3% vs. 78.6% reduction at 12 months, p = 0.089) suggests that the improved precision of guided surgery may translate into more complete healing. Accurate root-end resection that removes all apical ramifications and isthmuses while maintaining adequate root length is critical for healing [15, 23]. The consistent 3 mm resection level achieved with the trephine technique, combined with the perpendicular resection angle facilitated by the guide, may optimize conditions for periapical tissue regeneration [1, 7].

All cases in this study were performed in critical anatomical regions with a mean distance to vital structures of 2.1 ± 0.6 mm. The absence of nerve injury, sinus perforation, or other anatomical complications in the guided group demonstrates the safety of this approach in high-risk scenarios. Smith et al. evaluated the implications of the greater palatine artery for maxillary palatal root surgery and found that a 2 mm safety margin could be achieved in less than half of cases [25]. The present study included maxillary molar cases with similar anatomical constraints, and the guided approach successfully navigated these challenges without complications. The ability to plan safe surgical corridors in three dimensions using CBCT and to precisely translate this planning into the operative field represents a significant advancement in managing anatomically complex cases [27].

The present study demonstrates the safety and efficacy of guided surgery across a range of critical anatomical regions in a randomized trial. While this study evaluated static surgical guides, dynamic navigation systems represent an alternative approach to guided endodontic surgery [28, 29]. Dynamic navigation offers the advantages of real-time feedback and the ability to adjust the surgical plan intraoperatively, but it requires expensive equipment, specialized training, and may have a steeper learning curve [28, 29].

Static surgical guides, as used in the present study, offer several practical advantages: lower cost, no specialized equipment beyond a 3D printer required, the ability to plan and fabricate guides in advance, and potentially a shorter learning curve for surgeons already familiar with guided implant surgery [1, 3]. The choice between static and dynamic guidance may depend on case complexity, available resources, and surgeon preference. Future studies directly comparing static and dynamic approaches in clinical settings would be valuable.

The findings of this study have several important clinical implications. Guided endodontic microsurgery using 3D-printed surgical guides with trephine burs should be considered as a valuable option for cases in critical anatomical regions where precision and safety are paramount. The technique offers significant advantages in terms of surgical efficiency, reduced postoperative morbidity, and trends toward improved healing outcomes [14, 16]. The integration of CBCT imaging, digital planning software, and 3D printing technology into endodontic practice represents a natural evolution of the speciality toward digital workflows. Endodontists should consider investing in these technologies and developing the necessary skills to implement guided surgery protocols [1, 3]. The demonstrated safety and efficacy of guided surgery in critical anatomical regions may expand the indications for surgical endodontic treatment to cases that might otherwise be considered too risky or be referred for extraction and implant placement [10, 24]. Reduced surgical time with guided surgery may improve practice efficiency and patient throughput, potentially offsetting some of the additional costs of guide fabrication. Finally, the objective, reproducible nature of guided surgery may facilitate teaching and training of endodontic residents and continuing education for practicing clinicians [1, 15].

The present study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations related to sample size. A total of 28 patients (14 per group) were included. Although the sample size was adequately powered to detect clinically meaningful differences in the primary outcome of surgical procedure time, it may not have provided sufficient statistical power to detect smaller differences in secondary outcomes such as treatment success rates and complication profiles. To the best of our knowledge, no previous randomized clinical trials have specifically evaluated the use of surgical guides in endodontic microsurgery; therefore, the present study provides important initial clinical evidence in this area. Comparisons of treatment success and complications should be interpreted as exploratory findings. Consequently, the absence of statistically significant differences in these outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect limited statistical power rather than a true lack of differences between treatment approaches. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are recommended to confirm these findings and establish more definitive clinical recommendations.

Conclusion

The guided endodontic microsurgery using 3D-printed surgical guides and trephine burs significantly reduces surgical time compared to conventional microsurgery in critical anatomical regions. The technique shows a clinically relevant trend toward improved periapical healing, with 92.9% complete lesion resolution at 1 year, compared with 71.4% with conventional surgery. No complications were observed in either group, confirming the safety of both approaches. These findings support the adoption of guided endodontic microsurgery as an effective technique for managing complex cases where precision, efficiency, and patient comfort are important considerations. Further research with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods is needed to confirm these findings and evaluate the cost-effectiveness and broader applicability of guided surgical approaches in endodontics.
