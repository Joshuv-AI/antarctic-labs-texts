Strengthening public health data governance: A Co-regulatory framework for integrating wearable health data into health systems
Authors: Chien-Hung Sung, Jui-Chu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis perspective article addresses systemic governance gaps in the integration of wearable health technologies into public health infrastructures. While high-frequency physiological data offer potential for chronic disease management and epidemiological surveillance, they remain largely siloed due to fragmented regulatory frameworks and limited data trustworthiness. Recent empirical evidence underscores the magnitude of this gap: a global meta-analysis of 141,193 participants reports a 77.2% willingness to share health data, while only 26.5% of U.S. wearable users actually share their data with clinicians despite a 78.4% stated willingness.1,2 We propose the Good Data Service Practice (GDSP) as a conceptual co-regulatory governance framework designed to bridge the limitations of existing regimes such as the European Union (EU) General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the United States (U.S.) Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in handling continuous, dynamic data streams. Using a transparent, replicable comparative public-health policy analysis with pre-specified document inclusion/exclusion criteria and a structured coding protocol, we examined three purposively selected jurisdictions—the EU, the U.S., and Taiwan—across five operationally defined governance dimensions (consent architecture, accountability allocation, interoperability requirements, oversight capacity, and public-health usability). Drawing on design-science research principles,3 the GDSP framework was iteratively derived from the identified gaps through an explicit gap-to-principle mapping. We theoretically propose—rather than empirically demonstrate—that GDSP creates the institutional preconditions under which wearable data could, in principle, support clinical decision-making and public-health surveillance. No empirical validation, stakeholder consultation, or pilot implementation was conducted; subsequent Delphi consensus, expert panel review, and pilot evaluation are required before policy adoption. This framework offers a policy-relevant governance roadmap for strengthening digital health infrastructures and supporting equitable public health data use.Keywords: public health governance, wearable health data, co-regulation, data trust, design-science policy analysis, data stewardship, health equity, health systems resilience

Significance for public health

The exclusion of wearable health data from formal public health infrastructures represents a quantifiable governance failure: despite a 77.2% population-level willingness to share health data, only 26.5% of wearable device users in the United States actually share data with clinicians. This perspective identifies structural governance gaps in three major regulatory frameworks (GDPR, HIPAA, and Taiwan’s Personal Data Protection Act) that perpetuate this willingness–action gap. The proposed Good Data Service Practice (GDSP) framework offers public health authorities a conceptual co-regulatory pathway to transform consumer-generated wearable data into governance-verified, interoperable inputs for population surveillance, chronic disease monitoring, and preventive intervention. By embedding accountability and auditability at the infrastructure level, GDSP is hypothesised to reduce reliance on individual consent behaviour and institutional fragmentation, with the potential to strengthen public health system capacity and resilience—contingent on subsequent empirical validation through expert consensus, stakeholder consultation, and pilot implementation.

Introduction

Wearable health technologies continuously generate fine-grained physiological data that hold substantial promise for enhancing population-level health monitoring and preventive care. Their persistent exclusion from formal infrastructures represents a measurable constraint on public health system capacity. Recent population-level evidence indicates that 36.36% of U.S. adults (n = 2,033/5,591) now use at least one wearable health device, yet only 26.5% of these users actively share wearable data with a healthcare provider, compared with 78.4% who state a willingness to do so. 1 A similar willingness–action gap has been documented across 34 countries, where 77.2% of respondents (95% CI [71%, 82%]) are willing to share health data for secondary use, but willingness drops to 25.4% for for-profit recipients. 2 These figures quantify the governance-induced divergence between data availability and data usability for public-health purposes. While traditional systems provide population-level prevalence, they lack the temporal resolution needed for continuous exposure and behavioural characterisation—noting that granularity alone does not establish causal inference, 3 which requires an appropriate study design. 4 Integrating these streams into official infrastructures is increasingly viewed as a strategic necessity to advance the World Health Organization (WHO) 5 mandate for resilient digital health systems. From the perspective of public health authorities, this gap limits the capacity to integrate real-time, population-generated data into routine surveillance and prevention systems.

The exclusion of wearable data from formal health infrastructures results in concrete constraints on public health capacity: first, the inability to reliably mobilise these data streams limits chronic disease management; second, health systems lack the high-frequency data necessary for robust pandemic surveillance and early risk detection; and third, the absence of a trustworthy data link constrains the formulation of proactive, preventive public health policies. Existing regulatory frameworks such as the GDPR and HIPAA rely on static consent and were not designed for the continuous data flows of consumer-grade devices, creating a gap that, if left unaddressed, may undermine health system resilience. This perspective article therefore proposes, at a conceptual level, that the Good Data Service Practice (GDSP) framework can reframe these fragmented outputs into governance-verified inputs that, under appropriate implementation conditions, could support clinical decision-making and public-health use. GDSP differs from existing regulatory frameworks by functioning not as a legal compliance instrument, but as a conceptual operational governance infrastructure embedded within data systems.

This study adopts an explicit conceptual model that situates governance as an upstream structural determinant of health-system capacity. The model proposes a chain of relationships: governance design → institutional data trust → data interoperability and usability → public-health system capacity → downstream health-system outcomes (surveillance quality, preventive reach, equity). In this model, governance arrangements are not treated as post hoc compliance tools but as the structural conditions that determine whether digital health data can be trusted, interpreted, and operationalised.6–8 Building on this model, this perspective makes a specific contribution by conceptualising GDSP as an operational co-regulatory instrument that translates abstract governance principles into auditable, infrastructure-level mechanisms for public health data integration. Methodology and approach

This perspective article employs a structured, transparent comparative public-health policy analysis to examine why high-frequency wearable health data remain insufficiently integrated into formal public-health and clinical systems, despite their recognised relevance to population health monitoring, disease prevention, and health system resilience. Rather than measuring individual-level health outcomes, the methodological focus is placed on governance and institutional conditions that determine whether health-relevant data can be reliably used for public health purposes. This approach is consistent with public health research traditions that treat regulatory design, institutional trust, and data governance as structural determinants of health system capacity.6,8 In data-intensive health systems, the availability of data alone does not ensure public health benefit; governance arrangements shape whether data can be interpreted, shared, and operationalised for population-level decision-making. 9

Analytical workflow and reproducibility

To enhance reproducibility, the analytic procedure followed five sequential steps. Step 1 (scoping): the policy question was framed as governance gaps that limit the public-health usability of continuous, consumer-generated wearable data. Step 2 (source identification): primary regulatory documents and authoritative supranational guidance were retrieved from official portals—EUR-Lex for EU law, the U.S. Government Publishing Office for federal statutes, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights for HIPAA guidance, the Taiwan Laws and Regulations Retrieving System for Taiwanese statutes, and the WHO and OECD repositories for global guidance. Step 3 (peer-reviewed literature search): Web of Science, PubMed/MEDLINE, and Scopus were searched between 1 January 2018 and 30 April 2026 using the Boolean string ((‘wearable’ OR ‘patient-generated health data’ OR ‘consumer health data’) AND (‘governance’ OR ‘regulation’ OR ‘policy’) AND (‘GDPR’ OR ‘HIPAA’ OR ‘Taiwan’ OR ‘public health’)). Step 4 (selection): inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed publications or official regulatory documents in English or Mandarin Chinese, published between 2018 and 2026, addressing at least one of the five pre-specified governance dimensions in the EU, U.S., or Taiwan; exclusion criteria were editorials lacking original analysis, sources covering only device hardware without governance content, and duplicates. Foundational works on regulatory theory, polycentric governance, and design-science research3,6,8 were retained regardless of date because they ground the theoretical framework. Step 5 (coding and synthesis): each retained source was coded by both authors against the five operationally defined dimensions (see Table 1 below); coding discrepancies were reconciled by consensus. Governance gaps were operationalised as the difference between the dimension’s stated regulatory intent and the documented capacity of the framework to support continuous, high-frequency, consumer-generated data streams. The synthesis produced a jurisdiction×dimension gap matrix that informed the design-science derivation of GDSP principles described below.

Operational definitions of the five governance dimensions

The five analytical dimensions were operationalised prior to data extraction as discrete, comparable variables. (i) Consent architecture: the granularity, revisability, and purpose-specificity of the consent mechanism, indicated by support for dynamic or staged consent, granular purpose tags, and machine-readable retraction. (ii) Accountability allocation: the explicit assignment of legal liability across data controllers, processors, and certified intermediaries, indicated by the presence of statutory duty bearers and enforceable sanctions. (iii) Interoperability requirements: the mandate to use a defined syntactic and semantic standard (e.g., HL7 FHIR, LOINC, SNOMED CT, UCUM), indicated by the specification of conformant data representations. (iv) Oversight capacity: the existence of an independent statutory regulator with audit, investigation, and enforcement powers, indicated by jurisdictional scope, statutory powers, and budget independence. (v) Public-health usability: the degree to which the framework permits use of consumer-generated data for surveillance, prevention, and policy, indicated by lawful bases for secondary use, research exemptions, and explicit support for federated or linked data. Each dimension was scored as gap-absent/partial gap/substantive gap based on the coded evidence; the complete coding rubric is summarised in Table 1.

Jurisdictional selection rationale

Three jurisdictions were purposively selected to maximise variation across regulatory archetypes while keeping the comparison analytically tractable. The European Union exemplifies the rights-centred, horizontal-regulation archetype anchored in the GDPR. The United States represents the sectoral, market-based archetype in which HIPAA governs only covered entities while consumer-grade wearables fall outside its perimeter. Taiwan illustrates the state-linked, near-universal-insurance archetype, with the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) containing over 70 billion records yet, to date, no extension of the governance architecture to consumer-generated wearable data. 10 Other influential systems—the United Kingdom’s post-Brexit framework, Japan’s Next-Generation Medical Infrastructure Act, and Singapore’s TRUST/Healthier SG programme—were considered but excluded from the principal comparison for the following reasons: the UK framework remains close to the EU model, risking duplication of the rights-centred archetype; Japan combines features of the EU and Taiwan archetypes and would not add a structurally distinct case; and Singapore, although institutionally innovative, operates at a population scale an order of magnitude smaller than Taiwan’s NHIRD, limiting its comparability for large-scale public-health usability analysis. We acknowledge this scope choice as a limitation and identify the UK, Japan, and Singapore as priority extensions for future comparative work (see Limitations).

Working definitions

Co-regulatory governance refers to a hybrid regulatory arrangement in which public authorities delegate defined technical and operational oversight functions to certified private actors, while retaining ultimate legal accountability, audit rights, and enforcement authority.6,11 Auditability denotes the structural property of a data system whereby the provenance, transformation, and use of a given data element can be independently verified by a qualified third party through machine-readable logs and predefined metadata schemas. Public-health usability refers to the degree to which a dataset can be reliably interpreted, linked, and operationalised for population-level surveillance, preventive intervention design, and evidence-informed policy development—contingent upon trustworthiness, semantic interoperability, and governance-verified quality.

Phase 1: Identification of public health governance gaps

The first phase consisted of the structured comparative analysis described above. Analytical dimensions were pre-specified prior to extraction and included the five dimensions defined in Table 1. For each dimension, governance gaps were operationalised as the difference between stated regulatory intent and the documented capacity of the framework to support continuous, high-frequency, consumer-generated data streams. The analysis focused on how the GDPR (EU) and HIPAA (U.S.) address key governance dimensions relevant to public health data use, including consent mechanisms, accountability structures, interoperability requirements, and oversight capacity. Particular attention was paid to the limitations of static consent models and sector-based regulatory boundaries when applied to continuously generated wearable data. The objective of this phase was to identify governance gaps that are associated with—rather than directly cause—limited public-health usability, such as the limited incorporation of wearable data into population health surveillance, preventive intervention design, and evidence-informed policy development. By treating governance frameworks as determinants of public-health capacity, this phase highlights how regulatory misalignment can contribute to the systematic exclusion of potentially valuable health data from public-health systems.

Phase 2: Design-science construction of the GDSP framework

In the second phase, the GDSP framework was constructed as a system-level governance intervention designed to address the governance gaps identified in Phase 1. Framework development was grounded in the design-science research (DSR) methodology of Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, and Chatterjee, 3 which prescribes a six-stage process: (1) problem identification, (2) definition of objectives, (3) design and development, (4) demonstration, (5) evaluation, and (6) communication. In the present study, stages 1–3 and 6 are completed: problem identification and objective definition were carried out through the comparative analysis in Phase 1; design and development proceeded iteratively against the five operationalised analytical dimensions; and communication is achieved through this perspective article. Stages 4 and 5—demonstration and evaluation—are acknowledged as not yet conducted and are explicitly flagged in the Limitations section as priorities for subsequent empirical validation (expert panel review, Delphi consensus, pilot case application, and stakeholder workshops).

Each of the five GDSP principles was derived from the corresponding gap profile uncovered in Phase 1, following an explicit gap-to-principle mapping (Table 2 below). Drawing on insights from the comparative analysis, GDSP is conceptualised as a co-regulatory model that embeds accountability, auditability, and interoperability directly within digital health infrastructures. Unlike static, ex post compliance approaches, the GDSP framework emphasises continuous oversight and delegated public authority to support the reliable integration of wearable health data into public health and clinical workflows. By operationalising governance functions across the data lifecycle, GDSP seeks to enhance data trustworthiness and interpretability, thereby theoretically enabling wearable data to function as a credible evidentiary base for population health monitoring, clinical decision support, and health-system resilience. This methodological approach aligns with policy-oriented public health research that emphasises system-level interventions over individual-level behavioural explanations.

Comparative governance landscape

Public health systems increasingly depend on timely, high-quality data to support population health surveillance, preventive interventions, and evidence-informed decision-making. However, the governance frameworks that regulate health data were largely designed for episodic, institution-based data flows rather than continuous, high-frequency data generated by wearable devices. This section reports the comparative analysis of existing governance architectures in the EU, the U.S., and Taiwan, highlighting how regulatory design shapes the public-health usability of wearable health data.

Limitations of existing regulatory frameworks

In the EU, the GDPR establishes a comprehensive legal framework for personal data protection, emphasising individual rights, lawful processing, and accountability. While GDPR offers strong safeguards for privacy, its reliance on static consent mechanisms and purpose limitation creates practical constraints for the secondary use of continuously generated wearable data in public health contexts.9,12 Empirical consumer-perception evidence from eight EU countries suggests that public willingness to share health data is strongly conditional on the receiving entity and on opt-in consent, 13 indicating that static consent alone cannot adequately capture dynamic, context-specific preferences.

In the U.S., HIPAA governs health information within defined healthcare entities and covered transactions. Although HIPAA provides clear rules for clinical data protection, its sector-based scope excludes much of the wearable data generated by consumer devices and digital platforms. 14 Consistent with this observation, a latent-class analysis of 23,994 U.S. consumers 15 identified three distinct preference groups—Wary (36.8%), Discerning (47.9%), and Permissive (15.3%)—with highly divergent willingness to share wearable data across stakeholders, a heterogeneity that HIPAA’s entity-based scope is structurally ill-equipped to reflect.

Taiwan’s Personal Data Protection Act similarly emphasises individual consent and data protection across sectors. Taiwan’s data governance landscape has been substantially reshaped by two landmark developments. First, Constitutional Court Judgment No. 13 (2022) ruled that existing PDPA provisions on NHI secondary use were constitutionally ambiguous regarding oversight accountability and individual opt-out rights, mandating legislative reform within three years; the PDPA was subsequently amended in May 2023 to designate an independent Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC), whose preparatory office was formally established in December 2023.10,16 Second, and more structurally significant for the present analysis, Taiwan’s National Health Insurance system—which provides near-universal population coverage (>99%) and an NHIRD containing over 70 billion medical records—has not yet extended its governance architecture to consumer-generated wearable data. 10 This omission is precisely the governance gap that GDSP is designed to address: while Taiwan’s institutional infrastructure for claims-based health data is comparatively advanced, its regulatory framework for continuous, device-generated data streams remains structurally underdeveloped, mirroring the limitations observed in the EU and U.S. contexts. These challenges illustrate a broader structural issue shared across jurisdictions: existing regulatory tools prioritise legal compliance but provide limited guidance on how data can be rendered trustworthy and usable for public-health action.

Comparative global analyses indicate that most digital-health systems struggle with data silos and fragmented privacy enforcement. 17 The GDSP framework aligns with the WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health (2020–2025), 5 which mandates that digital integration must be ethically grounded, sustainable, and interoperable to move beyond the “static” compliance models of the past.

Public health implications of governance fragmentation

Across all three jurisdictions, regulatory fragmentation is associated with a systematic exclusion of wearable health data from formal public-health infrastructures. This exclusion has tangible public-health implications. As Haendel et al. 7 argue, treating real-world health data as a federated public utility—rather than as proprietary assets—can realign incentives among clinical providers, commercial vendors, and public authorities, a position consistent with the governance redirection proposed here. First, it constrains the ability of health authorities to incorporate real-time physiological data into population-health surveillance systems. Second, it limits the development of preventive interventions that rely on longitudinal, high-resolution data. Third, it undermines institutional trust in data quality and accountability, which is essential for evidence-informed public-health decision-making.

From a public-health perspective, these governance gaps represent a form of system-level limitation rather than a purely technological one. Data are generated at scale, yet governance arrangements constrain their responsible use for population-health benefit. This misalignment between data production and public-health utilisation highlights the need for governance approaches that extend beyond static compliance toward mechanisms that actively support public-health capacity.

GDSP as a comparative governance innovation

In contrast to existing frameworks, the GDSP framework is proposed as a co-regulatory governance model explicitly oriented toward public-health usability. Table 3 summarises the five core operating principles of the framework before the jurisdictional comparison presented in Table 4. Rather than functioning as a point-in-time compliance mechanism, GDSP is conceptualised as a dynamic governance layer that would govern system behaviour over time. By embedding accountability, auditability, and interoperability directly within digital-health infrastructures, GDSP seeks to enable wearable health data to function as a reliable evidentiary base for population health monitoring and clinical decision support. The clinical utility of wearables—particularly for heart-rate monitoring and activity recognition in older-adult populations—is documented, yet scalability is often hindered by the lack of clear clinical pathways. 18 By mandating semantic and governance standards, GDSP is designed to facilitate the integration of wearable signals into electronic health records, a prerequisite for transitioning from reactive care to proactive prevention. Governance safeguards are essential to ensure that algorithmic applications remain accountable and clinically interpretable over time.

Illustrative case narrative: Atrial fibrillation screening

We describe a hypothetical comparative scenario in which a public-health authority wishes to operate a community-level atrial-fibrillation (AF) screening programme using data from consumer wrist-worn photoplethysmography (PPG) devices. Under the current state, devices across three vendors export proprietary heart-rate variability and rhythm alerts that are neither semantically aligned with HL7 FHIR Observation resources nor linked to longitudinal consent records; the authority must negotiate bespoke data-sharing agreements with each vendor, cannot independently verify algorithmic provenance, and cannot audit retraction requests. Under the GDSP state, certified data-service providers expose vendor-agnostic FHIR R4 Observation resources (LOINC code 60831-0 for cardiac rhythm; SNOMED CT 49436004 for AF), append machine-readable algorithmic provenance metadata, and honour dynamic consent via standardised purpose tags. The authority queries a federated endpoint, retrieves population-level AF incidence stratified by age and region, and receives audit-trail attestations. Accountability, auditability, and semantic interoperability are thereby operationalised for a defined public-health purpose. This illustrative scenario reflects emerging pilot initiatives in wearable-enabled screening programmes, although a full GDSP-based implementation has not yet been formally evaluated in real-world public health systems. This use case illustrates how GDSP is conceptualised to operationalise governance functions into verifiable system behaviours, thereby narrowing the translation gap between regulatory intent and public health data usability.

The comparative dimensions summarised in Table 4 are reflected throughout the governance analysis. First, differences in governance logic illustrate how GDPR and HIPAA prioritise legal compliance and sectoral accountability, whereas GDSP adopts a performance- and action-oriented logic aimed at enabling public-health use. Second, distinctions in data nature highlight the limitations of existing frameworks in governing high-frequency, continuously generated wearable data, which GDSP explicitly addresses as a core governance object. Third, variations in compliance mechanisms demonstrate the contrast between static, ex post authorisation models and GDSP’s emphasis on continuous oversight and co-regulation. Fourth, interoperability requirements emerge as a critical determinant of public-health usability: while GDPR and HIPAA provide limited or indirect support for semantic interoperability, GDSP mandates standardised data representations to facilitate integration into public-health and clinical systems. Finally, differences in public-health potential and primary function underscore how existing frameworks tend to constrain secondary data use, whereas GDSP is designed to actively support population health surveillance, preventive interventions, and system-level decision-making. Together, these dimensions help explain why governance design shapes—though does not deterministically dictate—public-health system capacity in data-intensive health environments.

By reframing data governance as a determinant of public-health capacity, this comparative analysis underscores the importance of governance innovation for strengthening health system resilience. The GDSP framework offers a policy-relevant pathway for transforming fragmented wearable data outputs into trustworthy inputs for public-health surveillance, prevention, and long-term health system planning.

GDSP in medical and health system contexts: Conceptual implications

The integration of wearable health data into medical and public-health systems has long been constrained by concerns regarding data quality, accountability, and interpretability. Although wearable technologies generate large volumes of physiologically relevant data, their exclusion from formal health infrastructures has limited their contribution to clinical care, population-health monitoring, and preventive public-health strategies. From a public-health perspective, this exclusion reflects a systemic governance problem in addition to any technological limitation. The GDSP framework is proposed as a governance infrastructure that could enable wearable health data to be reliably incorporated into medical and public-health contexts. By embedding accountability, auditability, and interoperability into data services, GDSP is hypothesised to support the transformation of fragmented, consumer-generated data into trustworthy inputs for health-system use.

Technical standards and integration mechanisms

A governance framework cannot remain agnostic to specific technical standards. GDSP therefore recommends the use of HL7 FHIR release R4 as the minimum baseline, with migration pathways to R5 where implementation maturity permits, using the following resource anchors: Observation for time-series physiological signals, Device for provenance and model/firmware identification, Patient for individual linkage, and Consent for granular purpose and retraction records. Terminologies include LOINC for laboratory and observational concepts, SNOMED CT for clinical findings, and UCUM for units of measure. For integration architectures, GDSP prescribes that wearable vendors connect to certified data-service providers through validated FHIR APIs, which in turn expose governance-verified endpoints to electronic health records (EHRs) and public-health authorities via standards-based Subscription or Bulk Data ($export) operations. Responsibility is allocated as follows: vendors are responsible for producing conformant FHIR bundles; certified data-service providers are responsible for auditability, consent enforcement, and semantic mapping; public-health authorities and EHR custodians are responsible for query design, receipt of population-level summaries, and feedback on fitness-for-purpose.

Privacy, security, consent, and ethical stewardship

GDSP incorporates privacy and security as first-class design requirements rather than ancillary concerns. At the technical layer, GDSP calls for end-to-end encryption of data in transit and at rest, hardware-backed key management, role-based access controls, and differential-privacy safeguards for any aggregated population-level outputs. At the procedural layer, GDSP aligns with GDPR Articles 5, 25, and 35 (purpose limitation, privacy-by-design, and data-protection impact assessments) and with HIPAA Security Rule technical safeguards, while adding dynamic consent infrastructure that records and propagates user preferences in real time.2,19 Ethical stewardship is institutionalised through mandatory publication of algorithmic impact assessments, multi-stakeholder adjudication panels, and enforceable retraction pathways that honour user withdrawal requests within a defined service-level window. These provisions are designed to address the principal concerns documented in the empirical literature—privacy, consent, and transparency2,20—and to mitigate the ethical risks of algorithmic opacity and unequal benefit distribution.

Multi-stakeholder participation in Co-regulatory governance

Although GDSP is a co-regulatory framework by design, a governance model is only operational if it specifies how stakeholders participate. GDSP proposes four participation channels. First, a Standing Governance Council composed of public-health authorities, clinician representatives, certified data-service providers, device manufacturers, and patient advocates is responsible for ratifying technical standards and adjudicating disputes. Second, a Public Comment Cycle opens every proposed standard or interpretive guidance to structured public input with published adjudication of material comments. Third, a Clinician–Vendor Technical Working Group operationalises semantic alignment between wearable outputs and clinical information models. Fourth, Patient Advisory Panels review consent interfaces, retraction workflows, and impact-assessment results, with their findings binding on governance-council decisions. These mechanisms are consistent with the consumer-preference heterogeneity documented by Nagappan and Zhu 15 and with the equity concerns raised by Nino de Rivera et al., 21 in which 28% of Black respondents reported decreased willingness to participate in research under the 2023 NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy, underscoring the need for explicit community voice in governance design.

Government as a potential risk actor and institutional safeguards

GDSP must not assume that public authorities are uniformly benign or competent custodians of wearable health data. Governments can themselves function as risk actors. Documented hazards include regulatory capture by dominant device vendors or platform operators, political interference in algorithmic risk thresholds, state-surveillance overreach when continuous biosignals are repurposed for non-health objectives, corruption in the awarding of certification or audit contracts, and uneven enforcement that disadvantages marginalised populations. These hazards are intensified in the wearable-data context because continuous physiological streams can reveal location, behaviour, social ties, and identity at a granularity unavailable to earlier surveillance infrastructures.

GDSP therefore embeds five complementary safeguards against state-side risk. (i) Independent statutory oversight: certification, audit, and enforcement powers are vested in a body structurally separated from the ministry that procures health data services, with ring-fenced funding and fixed-term commissioners. (ii) Judicial review: every delegation, withdrawal of delegation, and enforcement decision is reviewable by an administrative court on substantive and procedural grounds, ensuring that affected data controllers, processors, and data subjects can seek judicial remedies. (iii) Transparency obligations: all certifications, audit findings, algorithmic impact assessments, and dispute adjudications are published in a public register with structured machine-readable metadata. (iv) Audit accountability: independent third-party auditors are rotated on a maximum five-year cycle, may not consult for entities they audit, and their reports are tabled before a parliamentary or legislative committee with public-health expertise. (v) Civil-society and patient participation: the Standing Governance Council includes voting representatives from patient advocacy organisations and civil-society digital-rights groups, and the Patient Advisory Panel’s findings are binding on council decisions (see preceding subsection). Together, these safeguards operationalise the polycentric checks that Black 6 and Ostrom 8 identify as essential to legitimate regulatory regimes, and they align with the federated-public-utility model advocated by Haendel et al. 7 We acknowledge that no governance design eliminates state-side risk; sustained civil-society engagement and an independent press remain indispensable external checks.

Digital equity and institutional trust

Beyond clinical utility, the GDSP framework directly addresses the growing concern of digital health equity. As noted by Richardson et al., 22 the “digital determinants of health” can exacerbate existing disparities if marginalised populations lack access to reliable digital infrastructures. By standardising the integration of heterogeneous wearable data, GDSP aims to ensure that health monitoring is not a privilege of the digitally literate but a public-health resource. The GDSP framework also addresses equity concerns associated with differential digital literacy and socioeconomic status. Individuals with lower digital literacy or limited access to digital health resources are often under-represented in data-driven health systems, resulting in systematic blind spots in public-health surveillance. Empirical evidence corroborates this: wearable adoption among U.S. adults is concentrated in higher-income (OR = 3.2 for income >US$75,000) and younger groups, 1 while 5,078 Nordic respondents show that willingness to share digital health data decreases with age and increases with education. 23 GDSP seeks to mitigate this risk by shifting responsibility for data standardisation, validation, and governance from individual users to institutional actors. By embedding interoperability and accountability requirements at the service and system levels, GDSP could reduce reliance on individual users’ technical capacity to manage or interpret their own data, supporting more inclusive public-health surveillance.

Conditions for improved clinical decision-making quality

Clinical decision-making increasingly relies on longitudinal and contextual health data to support diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment planning. However, wearable data are often excluded from clinical workflows due to uncertainty regarding their provenance, consistency, and regulatory legitimacy. GDSP is hypothesised to mitigate these barriers by establishing governance mechanisms that ensure data traceability, standardised processing, and continuous oversight across the data lifecycle; empirical validation of clinical impact remains a priority for future work. By supporting semantic interoperability through standardised data mapping mechanisms, such as HL7 FHIR–based translation, GDSP would enable wearable data to be integrated into electronic health record systems in a manner that preserves clinical interpretability. Improved institutional conditions for clinical decision-making could, in principle, contribute to earlier detection of health risks, more personalised disease management, and reduced reliance on episodic clinical encounters. GDSP does not directly generate clinical outcomes; it is conceptualised to strengthen the institutional conditions under which wearable data can be responsibly used to inform clinical judgment.

Implications for population health monitoring and prevention

At the population level, wearable health data offer significant potential for enhancing public-health surveillance and preventive interventions. An emerging illustration is the use of sweat-based wearable biosensors for real-time monitoring of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) such as bisphenols, phthalates, parabens, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. 24 These devices generate biologically meaningful, longitudinal exposure data that complement conventional biomarker monitoring, directly supporting this article’s argument that continuous, wearable-generated data streams can enrich public-health surveillance—provided that governance-verified trustworthiness, auditability, and interoperability are in place. GDSP is proposed as exactly this governance substrate: without it, sweat-based biosensor outputs remain proprietary data silos; with it, they could become population-level exposure signals actionable by public-health authorities. Continuous data streams can support early identification of emerging health risks, monitoring of chronic disease trends, and evaluation of population-level behavioural patterns. Yet without governance structures that ensure data reliability and accountability, public health authorities remain unable to incorporate these data into routine surveillance systems.

GDSP contributes conceptually to population health monitoring by enabling continuous oversight and standardised governance of data flows across platforms and institutions. By clarifying responsibilities among data platforms, healthcare providers, and regulatory authorities, the framework is intended to enhance institutional trust in data quality and fitness for public-health use. This trust is a prerequisite for integrating wearable data into preventive public-health strategies, such as risk stratification, targeted interventions, and long-term health planning. From a preventive perspective, GDSP could support a shift from reactive, event-driven public-health responses toward more proactive and data-informed approaches. While the framework does not prescribe specific interventions, it provides the governance infrastructure necessary for public-health authorities to responsibly experiment with and scale wearable-data–enabled prevention initiatives.

System-level health impact and policy-relevant implications

Beyond individual clinical encounters and population surveillance, GDSP has broader implications for health-system resilience and policy design. Health systems increasingly operate in environments characterised by rapid technological change, data-intensive care models, and growing reliance on digital tools. In such contexts, governance frameworks function as determinants of system capacity, shaping whether innovations can be safely adopted and sustained. GDSP is posited to contribute to health-system resilience by addressing the institutional fragility associated with consumer-grade health data. Through co-regulatory arrangements and continuous oversight, the framework is intended to reduce fragmentation across commercial, clinical, and public-health domains. This system-level coordination is hypothesised to support the scalable and responsible integration of digital health technologies, including medical artificial-intelligence applications that depend on high-quality longitudinal data.

From a policy perspective, GDSP offers a governance blueprint that complements existing legal frameworks without replacing them. By shifting the focus from static compliance toward dynamic co-regulation, the framework is designed to align regulatory oversight with the operational realities of modern health systems. This alignment could enhance the capacity of public-health institutions to harness digital health innovations while maintaining institutional trust in public-health data governance, legal legitimacy, and accountability. A significant challenge for future digital-health infrastructures is “data poverty,” where benefits accrue primarily to those with high digital literacy. GDSP is conceptualised to mitigate this by lowering the technical barriers for public-health authorities to utilise diverse data streams. Moreover, addressing historical institutional mistrust is essential; the dynamic oversight mechanisms of GDSP are intended to foster transparency and community engagement, which the literature identifies as catalysts for building long-term institutional trust in data ecosystems.6,8

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a conceptual policy analysis rather than an empirical evaluation; the claims advanced here are theoretical propositions that require subsequent validation through expert Delphi panels, pilot implementation studies, and outcome measurement against the five GDSP principles listed in Table 3. Second, implementation feasibility varies across health systems with divergent digital infrastructures, legal traditions, and fiscal capacity; adoption pathways in low- and middle-income settings will differ substantially from those in the jurisdictions analysed here. Third, regulatory resistance is an anticipated constraint: established regulators may view delegated authority as a dilution of sovereignty, and commercial data controllers may resist auditability requirements. Fourth, jurisdictional selection was purposive and confined to three archetypes (EU, U.S., Taiwan); other regulatory traditions—such as the United Kingdom’s post-Brexit framework, Japan’s Next-Generation Medical Infrastructure Act, and Singapore’s TRUST platform—were not examined and would enrich the comparative basis. Fifth, equity concerns persist even under GDSP: the willingness–action gap 1 and stakeholder-specific distrust15,21 will not be eliminated by governance redesign alone, and must be complemented by investment in digital literacy, multilingual consent infrastructures, and culturally appropriate outreach. Sixth, although this manuscript proposes specific standards (HL7 FHIR R4/R5, LOINC, SNOMED CT, UCUM), real-world interoperability will ultimately require iterative profile development and conformance testing that is outside the scope of a conceptual framework paper.

We additionally flag five practical barriers that GDSP alone cannot resolve. (a) Device accuracy: consumer wearables vary in measurement validity across heart rhythm, blood oxygenation, and sleep-stage detection, and clinical decisions should not be made on the basis of raw vendor outputs without independent validation. 18 (b) Clinician workload: integrating continuous physiological streams into electronic health records risks alert fatigue and increased documentation burden unless decision-support filters and escalation thresholds are co-designed with frontline clinicians. (c) Algorithmic opacity: many proprietary on-device algorithms are not open to inspection, and even with GDSP’s mandated algorithmic impact assessments, residual opacity can mask bias against darker skin tones, smaller wrist sizes, and atypical body compositions. (d) Commercial platform dominance: a small number of vendors control most of the consumer wearable market, and certified data-service providers risk becoming dependent on platform application programming interfaces (APIs) whose terms can change unilaterally. (e) Persistent inequities in wearable adoption: even with infrastructure-level governance, adoption remains skewed toward higher-income, younger, and more digitally literate users,1,23 so GDSP must be paired with public investment in device access, multilingual user interfaces, and community-led digital-health outreach to avoid reproducing existing disparities.

Conclusion

The rapid expansion of wearable health technologies has created unprecedented opportunities to strengthen population-health monitoring, preventive care, and data-informed clinical decision-making. However, the continued exclusion of wearable health data from formal medical and public-health systems reflects a systemic constraint on public-health system capacity in addition to any technological limitation. Without governance mechanisms that ensure data trustworthiness, accountability, and interpretability, the public-health value of wearable data remains largely unrealised.

This perspective article has proposed, at a conceptual level, the GDSP framework as a co-regulatory governance approach designed to address the governance gaps identified through structured comparative analysis. The present study limits its demonstrated contributions to two: (a) a structured, transparent identification of governance gaps in the GDPR, HIPAA, and Taiwan’s Personal Data Protection Act with respect to wearable data; and (b) the articulation of a conceptual co-regulatory framework—GDSP—grounded in design-science principles 3 and operationalised through five defined principles (Table 3) derived through an explicit gap-to-principle mapping (Table 2). Claims regarding enhanced clinical decision-making, strengthened system resilience, and improved public-health outcomes are framed as theoretical propositions requiring subsequent empirical evaluation, and no empirical validation, stakeholder consultation, or pilot implementation was conducted in this study.

GDSP does not assume high levels of centralised state capacity, nor does it assume that public authorities are uniformly benign custodians of wearable health data; its co-regulatory design embeds explicit safeguards against state-side risks (independent oversight, judicial review, transparency obligations, rotated third-party audit, and binding civil-society participation). From a health-equity perspective, the GDSP framework highlights how governance design can influence who is included in data-driven public-health systems. By institutionalising accountability and interoperability at the system level, GDSP aims to reduce the risk that individuals with lower digital literacy or socioeconomic resources are systematically excluded from population-health monitoring.

From an implementation perspective, the GDSP framework can be understood as a phased governance strategy. In the short term, implementation focuses on technical standardisation, including semantic interoperability, data-quality requirements, and auditability mechanisms. In the medium term, legal and institutional authorisation becomes critical, particularly in clarifying the regulatory responsibilities of data-service providers, public-health authorities, and intermediaries involved in secondary data use. Over the long term, sustained application of GDSP is hypothesised to contribute to the development of institutional trust in public-health data governance by institutionalising accountability, transparency, and equitable inclusion. By positioning GDSP as an operational governance instrument rather than a purely normative framework, this perspective article advances the policy discourse from regulatory compliance toward infrastructure-based accountability in digital health systems.

Future research should prioritise empirical validation of the GDSP framework through a sequential two-phase design. In the first phase, a Delphi study involving a purposively sampled panel of eight to twelve international experts in public health governance, health informatics, and digital health regulation should be conducted to evaluate the conceptual coherence, operational feasibility, and completeness of GDSP’s five core principles (Table 3). Panellists should be recruited from the three regulatory archetypes examined in this study—the EU, the U.S., and Taiwan—and, in subsequent rounds, expanded to include the United Kingdom, Japan, and Singapore to test cross-jurisdictional generalisability, with consensus defined as ≥70% agreement across two iterative rounds. 25 In the second phase, a pilot case application should be carried out within a real-world trusted research environment (TRE) or regulatory sandbox—such as Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Administration (NHIA) secondary-use data platform or Finland’s Findata service—to assess the operational adequacy of GDSP’s auditability and dynamic consent mechanisms against the five pre-specified governance dimensions introduced in Phase 1 of the present study. Outcome indicators should include data usability scores, consent operationalisation fidelity, and audit-trail completeness rates. Together, these two phases would address Stages 4 (demonstration) and 5 (evaluation) of the design-science research methodology adopted in this study, 3 providing an empirically grounded basis for iterative refinement of the framework prior to wider policy uptake.

In closing, effective public-health responses in increasingly digitalised health systems depend not only on technological innovation but also on governance infrastructures that enable data to be used responsibly and at scale. By reframing wearable health data governance as a structural determinant of public-health capacity, the GDSP framework provides a conceptually grounded, empirically testable policy pathway for supporting preventive public-health strategies, evidence-informed decision-making, and long-term health-system resilience.
