The pathobiont role of Akkermansia muciniphila in colorectal cancer: a systematic review
Authors: Maryam Soheilipour, Ali Noursina, Mohadese Nekookhoo, Elena Malekpour, Seyed Arsham Mirzaei, Mohammad Hossein Shafieyoun, Hanieh Rouzbahani, Mohammad Reza Chitsazi, Farzad Safari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Emerging evidence highlights the gut microbiota, particularly Akkermansia muciniphila, as a key modulator in CRC pathogenesis. While often regarded as a beneficial commensal, its role in CRC appears complex and potentially dualistic.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to comprehensively analyze the context-dependent role of A. muciniphila in CRC, evaluating its potential as a pathobiont.MethodsWe systematically reviewed human, animal, in vitro, and ex vivo studies on associations between A. muciniphila and CRC risk or outcomes. Database searches identified 34 eligible studies. Study quality was assessed using the GRADE criteria.ResultsFrom 244 initial records, 34 studies met the inclusion criteria. Findings reveal a stark dichotomy: non-viable forms of A. muciniphila—including pasteurized bacteria, outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), and purified proteins (e.g., Amuc_1100, Amuc_2172, Amuc_1434*)—consistently demonstrate anti-tumorigenic effects. These components enhance anti-tumor immunity by activating CD8 + T cells, promoting M1-like macrophage polarization via TLR2/NLRP3 signaling, and sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Conversely, administration of live A. muciniphila can exert pro-tumorigenic effects, particularly in inflammatory contexts (e.g., colitis-associated CRC) or post-antibiotic settings, likely due to excessive mucin degradation and gut barrier disruption. Host factors such as obesity, sex, genetics, and microbial interactions further modulate its impact.ConclusionA. muciniphila functions as a pathobiont in CRC, with its role critically dependent on its biological state and host context. Therapeutic strategies should shift from live probiotics to targeted postbiotic applications of its beneficial components to maximize efficacy and safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04293-0.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent type of cancer that affects the mucosal lining cells of the intestine. It is the fourth most common malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States. This type of cancer is caused by genetic mutations that result in the uncontrolled growth of cells, which can eventually attack the intestinal wall and spread to other parts of the body [1]. In Western countries, CRC is the third most common type of cancer, with over 135,000 new cases and 50,000 deaths annually in the United States alone. Globally, approximately 600,000 deaths from CRC occur annually, which accounts for 8% of all cancer-related deaths [2].

Various factors, including the gut microbiota composition, influence CRC. Dysbiosis, an imbalance in the gut microbiota, is associated with several gastrointestinal diseases, including CRC [3]. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating host metabolism and immune response, and its disruption is one of the primary causes of gastrointestinal diseases [4]. When dysbiosis occurs, the protective bacteria are lost, and disease-causing and cancer-promoting bacteria proliferate. These bacteria can enhance cancer-related functions such as angiogenesis, loss of apoptosis, and cellular proliferation [5].

Akkermansia muciniphila is an anaerobic gram-negative bacterium that resides in the intestinal mucosa. This bacterium degrades mucin, a glycoprotein present in mucus, and uses it as its sole source of carbon and nitrogen [6]. A. muciniphila is abundant in the mucosal layer of the host intestine, especially in the cecum, where most mucin is produced [7]. It has been shown that A. muciniphila has an inverse relationship with obesity, diabetes, and inflammation, and dietary interventions increase the abundance of this bacterium [8].

As there is not enough research on the potential role of A. muciniphila for CRC, we plan to review relevant studies published in trustworthy databases. We aim to explore the possible role of A. muciniphila on CRC development and progression.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist [9]. The study aimed to identify observational (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional) and interventional studies investigating the association between Akkermansia muciniphila (P: Patients with colorectal cancer or precancerous lesions; I: Presence/abundance of A. muciniphila; C: Comparison groups with normal gut microbiota or disease controls; O: Risk, progression, or outcomes of colorectal cancer). Studies without a control group, reviews, or non-English papers were excluded.

The search strategy prioritized MeSH terms and supplemented them with entry terms, title/abstract filters, and spelling variants (British/American English, singular/plural forms). The following databases were searched until November 2023: PubMed/MEDLINE (via MeSH), Scopus, and Web of Science.

Study selection

The process of study selection is presented in Fig. 1. Initially, 244 articles were found. Of these, 52 records were duplicates. The titles and abstracts of 192 studies were assessed. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All human, animal, in vitro, and ex vivo studies that assessed the relationship between A. muciniphila and CRC were included. Only English language studies were selected.

Case studies or case series, book chapters, letters, conference abstracts, review papers, editorials, duplicate papers, and studies with insufficient data were excluded.

Data extraction

It is essential to take into account the following factors while examining the study - the article’s first author, the year it was published, the design of the study, the sample size, the intervention used, the age and gender of the participants, the health outcomes observed, and a summary of the results. Two independent reviewers conducted the data extraction process, which a third reviewer verified.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The GRADE checklist [10] was utilized in all studies as it contains crucial questions that assess the study design, risk of bias, inconsistency across studies, indirectness to the clinical question, imprecision of effect estimates, and publication bias. This helps to evaluate the quality of evidence for each outcome, which is graded as high, moderate, low, or very low based on these factors. A high grade means that there is high confidence that the actual effect is close to the estimate. Very low-grade evidence needs more confidence in the summary effect estimate. Typically, randomized trials start with high-quality evidence but may be downgraded based on the other factors assessed. Observational studies start as low quality but may be upgraded. This approach provides a transparent, systematic framework for evaluating the certainty and strength of recommendations that can be made from the evidence. The key is to assess the critical factors that affect confidence in the reported effects.

Molecular mechanisms of A. muciniphila in CRC modulation

The influence of A. muciniphila on colorectal carcinogenesis is not a monolithic effect but rather the result of a complex and multifaceted dialogue between the bacterium and its host. This interaction is mediated by a sophisticated toolkit of bacterial components, metabolites, and the subsequent activation or inhibition of specific host signaling pathways, culminating in a profound modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. Understanding these molecular underpinnings is crucial to deciphering the bacterium’s context-dependent role as both a protective symbiont and a potential pathobiont.

The bacterium’s toolkit: bioactive components and metabolites

The ability of A. muciniphila to communicate with the host is largely dependent on the specific molecules it presents to the intestinal epithelium and resident immune cells. These include structural components of its cell membrane, secreted proteins, extracellular vesicles that act as cargo carriers, and metabolic byproducts of its unique niche specialization.

A key finding that permeates literature is that the beneficial immunomodulatory effects of A. muciniphila can be recapitulated by non-viable, pasteurized bacteria or even by single, purified molecular components [11, 27, 29, 34, 36, 37]. This critical observation suggests that many of the bacterium’s protective signals can be decoupled from its live metabolic activity, a concept central to understanding its dual role.

Outer Membrane and Secreted Proteins

Multiple studies have identified specific proteins from the bacterium A. muciniphila as key mediators of its effects. Amuc_1100, a highly conserved outer membrane protein, was introduced by Wang et al. (2020) as a potent immunomodulatory molecule. Administration of the purified protein can inhibit colitis-associated tumorigenesis in animal models by modulating the activity of CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [36]. This process occurs through the activation of Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) on host cells, triggering immune cascades [27, 29]. Furthermore, Amuc_2172, an acetyltransferase identified by Jiang et al. (2023), is packaged into small extracellular vesicles (SEVs) and delivered to the tumor microenvironment; this protein suppresses tumor growth by reprogramming immune cells and activating CTLs [11]. Additionally, A. muciniphila secretes proteases with direct anti-cancer effects. For instance, Amuc_1434*, an aspartic protease, was shown by Meng et al. (2020) to not only degrade the mucin MUC2 (Meng et al., 2019) but also directly induce apoptosis in human colorectal cancer cells (LS174T cell line) via a TRAIL-mediated pathway [34, 37]. These findings demonstrate that specific proteins of A. muciniphila play critical roles in colorectal tumor suppression through immune regulation and apoptosis induction, making them promising targets for cancer therapy.

Extracellular Vesicles

Beyond individual proteins, A. muciniphila releases EVs, which are nanoscale, lipid bilayer-enclosed particles that function as sophisticated intercellular communication systems. These vesicles are loaded with a cargo of bioactive molecules, including proteins like Amuc_2172, lipids, and nucleic acids [11, 44]. Studies have shown that EVs from A. muciniphila can independently regulate intestinal homeostasis by reinforcing the gut’s physicochemical barrier [45]. Critically, in the context of cancer, research from Xu et al. have shown these EVs have been shown to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, likely by delivering immunomodulatory signals that prime the tumor microenvironment for a more robust anti-tumor response. They represent a key mechanism by which the bacterium can exert effects on the host from a distance, without direct cell-to-cell contact [33].

Based on the studies conducted by Jiang et al. (2023), beyond individual proteins, A. muciniphila secretes EVs, which are nanoscale, lipid bilayer-enclosed particles that act as complex intercellular communication systems [11]. These vesicles carry bioactive molecules, including proteins such as Amuc_2172, lipids, and nucleic acids (Jiang et al., 2023; Chelakkot et al., 2018) [11, 44]. According to research by Zheng et al. (2023), EVs from A. muciniphila can independently regulate intestinal homeostasis by reinforcing the gut’s physicochemical barrier [45]. Furthermore, as demonstrated by Wang et al. (2023), in the context of cancer, these vesicles enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, likely by delivering immunomodulatory signals that prime the tumor microenvironment for a stronger anti-tumor response [15]. Therefore, extracellular vesicles represent a key mechanism by which the bacterium can exert effects on the host remotely, without direct cell-to-cell contact. Overall, the evidence suggests that A. muciniphila and its EVs play a predominantly protective role in maintaining gut health and modulating anti-cancer immunity, rather than acting as a risk factor.

Metabolic Footprints: Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs)

SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are widely acknowledged for their beneficial roles in maintaining gut health by exerting anti-inflammatory effects, modulating epigenetic mechanisms, and preserving the integrity of the intestinal barrier [46]. A. muciniphila contributes to these protective functions by degrading mucin and producing SCFAs, mainly acetate and propionate, which promote a healthy luminal environment (Li et al., 2023; Grajeda-Iglesias et al., 2021) [13, 30]. Finally, Weir et al. (2013) identified distinct differences in stool SCFA profiles between colorectal cancer patients and healthy controls, underscoring the role of these metabolites in tumor development [43]. Collectively, these studies highlight the key contribution of A. muciniphila-derived SCFAs to gut homeostasis and anti-cancer effects, mainly through reinforcement of the intestinal barrier and modulation of tumor-related immune responses.

Modulating host signaling pathways

The bioactive molecules produced by A. muciniphila exert their effects by engaging specific receptors and triggering intracellular signaling cascades within host cells. This modulation of host pathways is highly nuanced, involving both direct activation of immune signaling and indirect regulation of metabolic and proliferative pathways [12, 27].

The TLR2/NLRP3 Inflammasome Axis

A primary mechanism for the anti-tumor immune effects of A. muciniphila involves the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on innate immune cells. Fan et al. demonstrated that A. muciniphila, likely through surface components such as Amuc_1100, directly engages TLR2 on macrophages. This binding event triggers the activation of the transcription factor NF-κB, which in turn primes the NLRP3 inflammasome [27]. The subsequent assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome lead to the processing and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18 [27, 47]. This specific inflammatory milieu is crucial for polarizing tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from a pro-tumorigenic M2-like phenotype towards an anti-tumor M1-like state, ultimately suppressing colorectal tumorigenesis [27]. This pathway represents a direct stimulate mechanism, where the bacterium actively stimulates a potent anti-cancer immune response.

The AhR/β-catenin Pathway

In contrast to direct immune activation, Zhang et al. uncovered a more subtle, indirect anti-tumor mechanism involving metabolic interference [12]. Their research showed that A. muciniphila supplementation leads to an inhibition of tryptophan metabolism in the gut. Tryptophan and its metabolites are endogenous ligands for the Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor (AhR). In the context of CRC, the activation of AhR has been linked to the promotion of the pro-proliferative Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [48]. By reducing the pool of available tryptophan-derived ligands, A. muciniphila effectively dampens release-of-inhibitionactivation, which in turn suppresses aberrant Wnt/β-catenin signaling in cancer cells and counters CRC progression [12, 48]. This represents a release-of-inhibition mechanism, where the bacterium prevents a pro-tumor pathway from being engaged.

Modulation and coordination of the immune response: enhancing synergistic effects with cancer therapies

The integration of these molecular interactions induces a significant transformation within the tumor immune microenvironment, shifting it from an immunosuppressive state that supports tumor growth to an immunostimulatory milieu that enhances antitumor immunity. This fundamental restructuring underlies the synergistic effects observed between A. muciniphila and conventional anticancer therapies.

Wang et al. demonstrated that A. muciniphila promotes the maturation and activation of dendritic cells (DCs) in the intestinal mucosa via its outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) [15], while Shi et al. showed that this bacterium and its outer membrane protein Amuc activate DCs through the TLR2 signaling pathway [32], playing a key role in enhancing antitumor immune responses and presenting tumor antigens to T cells. Moreover, Fan et al. showed that A. muciniphila drives the polarization of TAMs towards the anti-tumor M1 phenotype and, most critically, enhances the recruitment, infiltration, and effector function of CD8 + CTLs and CD4 + T helper cells within the tumor [27]. This influx of activated T cells creates a microenvironment that is highly susceptible to immune-mediated killing.

A. muciniphila significantly potentiates cancer immunotherapy, particularly enhancing the effects of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. It achieves this by converting immunologically “cold,” non-responsive tumors into “hot” ones. Research by Xu et al. (2020) confirms that it improves anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy in challenging cases like microsatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer by modulating metabolic pathways [33]. The bacterium’s broad utility in immunotherapy is further highlighted by Shi et al. (2020), whose findings show that combining probiotics like A. muciniphila with IL-2-based immunotherapy also leads to a markedly enhanced antitumor immune response and superior tumor clearance. This demonstrates a versatile immune-priming capability that extends beyond a single therapeutic pathway [32]. Mechanistically, a membrane protein from A. muciniphila directly drives this response by modulating and increasing the infiltration of critical CD8 + T cells into the tumor (Wang et al., 2020) [36]. This recruitment ensures that when ICIs “release the brakes,” a powerful, targeted immune force is present. This entire process is supported by the bacterium’s ability to regulate gut homeostasis via components like outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), creating a healthy foundation for a robust systemic immune response (Wang et al., 2023) [15].

Furthermore, A. muciniphila can act as a “chemosensitizer.” Hou et al. demonstrated that its presence potentiates the anti-tumor efficacy of the standard CRC chemotherapy regimen FOLFOX (oxaliplatin, fluorouracil, and calcium folinate). The study also revealed a positive feedback loop, where FOLFOX treatment itself led to an increased abundance of A. muciniphila. The proposed mechanism involves the modulation of tumor cell metabolism, specifically related to dipeptides containing branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which re-sensitizes the cancer cells to the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy [26]. Therefore, the therapeutic potential of A. muciniphila may lie not only in its capacity as a monotherapy but, perhaps more powerfully, in its role as a crucial adjuvant that lowers the threshold for both chemotherapy and immunotherapy to succeed.

A. muciniphila in the context of the CRC Microbiome and IBD

No single microbe acts in isolation. The role of A. muciniphila in CRC can only be fully appreciated when viewed within the broader ecological context of the gut microbiome and in relation to precursor inflammatory conditions. Placing it alongside other key microbial players and examining its function within the well-established inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) to CRC progression axis provides a more complete and clinically relevant picture.

The IBD-CRC axis: A proving ground for A. muciniphila’s role

The progression from inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) to colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) serves as a critical proving ground for the dual and context-dependent role of A. muciniphila. This high-stakes axis, defined by chronic inflammation and intestinal barrier dysfunction, reveals both the bacterium’s protective potential and its paradoxical capacity for harm [23, 27, 36, 41, 42, 49].

On one hand, strong evidence supports a protective role for A. muciniphila in the IBD-CRC axis. Dingemanse et al. (2015) showed that A. muciniphila colonization increases mucin-producing goblet cells and mucus layer thickness, thereby reinforcing the intestinal barrier in tumor-prone mice [42]. Consistently, López-Siles et al. (2018) reported a significant depletion of A. muciniphila in the colonic mucosa of IBD patients, underscoring its association with gut health and barrier integrity [41].

Mechanistically, Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated that a purified membrane protein from A. muciniphila could blunt colitis-associated tumorigenesis in mice by modulating the anti-tumor immune response, specifically by enhancing CD8 + T cell activity [36]. Complementing these findings, Fan et al. (2021) elucidated that A. muciniphila induces the polarization of macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype via TLR2/NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This macrophage polarization promotes anti-tumor immunity by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, which suppress colorectal tumor growth. Furthermore, A. muciniphila mitigates pro-tumorigenic inflammation by inhibiting critical signaling pathways like IL-6, thus modulating the tumor microenvironment to favor cancer suppression [27].

However, this same IBD-CRC axis uncovers a detrimental potential of A. muciniphila. Wang et al. (2022) and Wang et al. (2023) reported that administration of live A. muciniphila exacerbated disease severity and promoted tumor development in mouse models of active colitis and colitis-associated colorectal cancer. This harmful effect is primarily attributed to A. muciniphila’s enzymatic degradation of mucin in the intestinal mucus layer. While mucin degradation is generally beneficial by contributing to mucosal homeostasis in a healthy gut, under conditions of active inflammation and barrier disruption, such degradation may further erode an already fragile mucus layer, worsening barrier dysfunction and promoting carcinogenesis [23, 49]. These findings highlight a critical caution when considering live bacterial administration in compromised gut environments.

Therefore, the dual role of A. muciniphila in the IBD-CRC axis underscores the complexity of host-microbe interactions in colorectal carcinogenesis. It suggests promising therapeutic potential for approaches harnessing the bacterium’s immunomodulatory molecules or pasteurized forms that avoid the risks associated with live bacterial mucinolytic activity. These nuanced insights are essential for developing safe and effective microbiota-targeted interventions for preventing and treating colitis-associated colorectal cancer.

A comparative analysis: a. muciniphila vs. established oncomicrobes

In CRC, the dynamic interplay between the gut commensal A. muciniphila and oncomicrobes such as Fusobacterium nucleatum is critical, with their relative abundances serving as key biomarkers for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic response.

A. muciniphila and F. nucleatum exert distinct effects on CRC progression, as demonstrated by studies conducted by Osman et al. (2021), Li et al. (2021), and Zhou et al. (2020) [22, 31, 38]. Osman et al. found that both F. nucleatum and A. muciniphila are enriched in CRC patients, with F. nucleatum promoting tumor progression through inflammatory pathways and β-catenin signaling, whereas increased levels of A. muciniphila may be associated with mucosal degradation and impaired intestinal barrier function [31]. Li et al. reported that F. nucleatum is linked to chemotherapy resistance, while A. muciniphila enhances chemotherapeutic response by bolstering immune activity and promoting gut health [22]. Zhou et al. demonstrated that vitamin D deficiency leads to an increase in F. nucleatum and a reduction in A. muciniphila, thereby elevating CRC risk, whereas vitamin D supplementation helps restore this microbial balance [38]. In conclusion, the likelihood that these two bacteria act in a synergistic or aligned manner is very low. Instead, their interaction appears to reflect a competitive or antagonistic balance within the gut ecosystem, which significantly influences the development and progression of colorectal cancer. Modulating this balance—through interventions such as vitamin D supplementation—may offer a promising strategy for CRC prevention and therapy.

Analysis of heterogeneity and contradictory findings

Analysis of heterogeneity and contradictory findings

The body of literature examining the interplay between A. muciniphila and CRC is characterized by a significant degree of heterogeneity, presenting findings that are often contradictory. Studies report outcomes ranging from potent tumor suppression to a significant exacerbation of carcinogenesis. This variability is not stochastic; rather, it appears to be driven by a confluence of specific, often unstated, experimental variables. A critical analysis of the available evidence reveals that the functional role of A. muciniphila is not intrinsic but is profoundly shaped by its biological state (live vs. non-viable), the specific host context in which it resides, and the methodological framework used to study it.

The “live vs. pasteurized vs. component” dichotomy

A primary axis of divergence in the literature is the form of A. muciniphila administered in interventional studies. A clear pattern emerges wherein non-viable preparations and isolated molecular components consistently exert protective effects, while live bacteria produce highly variable and often deleterious outcomes. This distinction suggests that the beneficial immunomodulatory properties of the bacterium can be uncoupled from the potentially detrimental metabolic activities of the live organism.

The antitumor effects of non-viable components are remarkably consistent across multiple studies. This line of inquiry indicates that specific structural elements of A. muciniphila are potent immunomodulators capable of suppressing tumorigenesis. The work by Wang et al. (2020) established that both pasteurized A. muciniphila and a purified outer membrane protein, Amuc_1100, blunted colitis-associated tumorigenesis by expanding and activating tumor-killing CD8 + T cells [36]. Building on this, Jiang et al. (2023) isolated secreted extracellular vesicles (SEVs) and a specific acetyltransferase protein within them, Amuc_2172. They demonstrated that SEVs, and more potently the purified Amuc_2172, suppressed CRC by reprogramming the tumor microenvironment to promote a cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) response [11]. Further evidence for the protective capacity of isolated components comes from Meng et al. (2020), who identified another purified component, the aspartic protease Amuc_1434*, which directly inhibited the viability of human CRC cells in vitro [34]. The work by Wang et al. (2023) expanded this concept by demonstrating that outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) from A. muciniphila not only exerted protective effects but also enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in a murine model of CRC by modulating gut homeostasis [15]. Similarly, Kang et al. (2023) found that conditioned medium from A. muciniphila cultures, containing its secreted metabolites, exhibited anti-proliferative effects on CRC cell lines [17]. Furthermore, the study by Grajeda-Iglesias et al. (2021) provided a biochemical explanation for this phenomenon, showing that pasteurized A. muciniphila was more effective than the live bacterium at elevating systemic levels of anti-cancer metabolites such as spermidine and short-chain fatty acids [30]. Collectively, these studies establish that non-viable bacterial fractions—from single proteins and OMVs to secreted metabolites—can robustly engage the host immune system to elicit an antitumor response.

In stark contrast, studies administering live A. muciniphila report conflicting and often pro-tumoral outcomes. Several investigations have documented that live bacteria can exacerbate CRC. For instance, Wang et al. (2022) demonstrated that oral administration of live A. muciniphila worsened the development of colitis-associated CRC in mice [23]. Likewise, Dingemanse et al. (2015) found that mono-colonization of genetically susceptible Apc mutant mice with live A. muciniphila significantly increased the number of intestinal tumors [42]. However, the effect of live bacteria is not uniformly negative. The study by Fan et al. (2021) provides a crucial counterexample, showing that supplementation with live A. muciniphila suppressed colon tumorigenesis in Apc Min/+ mice by inducing a protective M1-like macrophage response [27]. The study by Frühbeck et al. (2021) adds another layer of complexity, showing that both live A. muciniphila and its conditioned medium could induce the expression of inflammasome genes (e.g., NLRP3, NLRP6) in colon cancer cells, a mechanism that could help resolve chronic inflammation and thus confer protection [24].

The divergence between the effects of live and non-viable A. muciniphila points to a fundamental mechanistic distinction. The protective effects are almost exclusively immune-mediated, driven by the activation of CD8 + T cells and M1 macrophages [11, 27, 36]. These responses are likely triggered by structural components, such as outer membrane proteins and proteins within vesicles, which function as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). These PAMPs are present in both live and non-viable forms and are sufficient to elicit a beneficial immune response [11, 27, 36]. However, live bacteria possess an additional, critical function: active metabolism, defined by their mucin-degrading capacity. This metabolic activity can become detrimental under certain conditions, as detailed by Qu et al. (2023), who showed that over-colonization of live bacteria leads to excessive mucin degradation and intestinal barrier damage [19, 37].

Therefore, the beneficial effects of A. muciniphila appear to stem from its structural components engaging the host immune system in a favorable manner. Pasteurization or component isolation effectively “purifies” this beneficial immunomodulatory signal by removing the potentially harmful metabolic activity of the live organism.

The influence of Host-Specific factors: the pathobiont nature of a. muciniphila

The role of A. muciniphila in CRC is not that of a simple commensal or pathogen, but rather a pathobiont, whose function is profoundly conditional upon the host’s specific physiological and environmental context. The conflicting reports of its pro- and anti-tumorigenic effects can be largely reconciled when examining the influence of host-specific factors. The evidence suggests that the impact of A. muciniphila on CRC development is dictated by a complex interplay of the host’s diet, metabolic status (BMI), genetic background, the prevailing metabolic and inflammatory environment, and interactions with microbial neighbors.

The metabolic health of the host appears to be a critical determinant. In the context of obesity, a condition associated with a high BMI and a known risk factor for CRC, the gut microbiota is significantly altered. A study by Kang et al. (2023) found that microbiota from obese individuals, which was depleted of A. muciniphila, promoted colon tumor formation in mice [17]. This suggests its absence contributes to a pro-tumorigenic environment in states of metabolic dysregulation. This is further supported by Frühbeck et al. (2021), who linked obesity-associated inflammation to a pro-cancer microenvironment and showed that A. muciniphila could beneficially modulate inflammasome expression, which is dysregulated in obesity [24].

Specific dietary components also directly modulate the abundance and effect of A. muciniphila. For example, Zhou et al. (2020) established a clear protective axis involving vitamin D, demonstrating that its supplementation increased the abundance of A. muciniphila and enhanced colon barrier integrity, thereby inhibiting CRC development [38]. In contrast, Liu et al. (2023) found that excessive dietary iron supplementation, a factor that promotes colorectal tumorigenesis, significantly depleted A. muciniphila. Restoring its levels was sufficient to attenuate the pro-tumorigenic effects of the high-iron diet, indicating that host diet directly shapes the conditions under which A. muciniphila is beneficial or detrimental [18].

Perhaps the most striking evidence for its conditional nature comes from studies on host sex, genetic factors, and the background inflammatory state. The host’s underlying inflammation is paramount. In models of colitis-associated cancer, where chronic inflammation is the primary driver, Wang et al. (2022) reported that A. muciniphila administration exacerbated CRC development [23]. Furthermore, intrinsic host factors like sex create distinct microenvironments. Wang et al. (2023) revealed that male mice exhibit a gut microbiome and metabolome profile that is biased towards aggravating CRC development compared to females, with Akkermansia being one of the key differentiators [20]. The host’s genetic background also interacts with these factors. For example, Song et al. (2021) demonstrated that the knockout of the Nrf2 gene induces sex-specific changes in the gut microbiome that relate to colon cancer, observing that in male Nrf2-knockout mice, the abundance of Akkermansia was significantly decreased [28]. Similarly, Howe et al. (2018) suggested that in Pten-deficient mice, a different genetic context, a decrease in A. muciniphila conferred a tumor-preventive microenvironment [39].

Finally, the function of A. muciniphila is not intrinsic but is modulated by interactions with its microbial neighbors. The study by Dingemanse et al. (2015) provides a compelling example of this microbial interplay. They found that in Apc-mutant mice, mono-colonization with A. muciniphila significantly increased intestinal tumor numbers. However, when co-colonized with Helicobacter typhlonius, this pro-tumorigenic effect was completely abrogated. This demonstrates that the functional output of A. muciniphila can be reversed by the presence of another specific microbe, highlighting that its role must be considered as part of a complex ecosystem [42].

In conclusion, these findings collectively support the classification of A. muciniphila as a pathobiont whose therapeutic potential can only be realized through a personalized approach that considers the multifaceted host context.

The unexplored frontier: Strain-Level diversity

The contradictory findings on the role of Akkermansia muciniphila in colorectal cancer strongly suggest that the functional effects of this bacterium are not uniform across the species. A critical, yet largely unexplored, factor that could explain this heterogeneity is the strain-level diversity within A. muciniphila. It is plausible that different strains of A. muciniphila possess distinct genetic and functional capabilities, leading to divergent, and even opposing, effects on the host.

Interestingly, several of the key studies with conflicting outcomes, including Wang et al. (2022), Fan et al. (2021), Jiang et al. (2023), and Dingemanse et al. (2015), utilized the same strain, ATCC BAA-835 [11, 23, 27, 42]. While this might initially seem to challenge the strain-diversity hypothesis, it is crucial to note that the majority of studies included in this review did not specify the particular strain of A. muciniphila employed. This lack of specificity is a significant gap in the literature. Furthermore, even within a single strain, functional expression can be influenced by environmental conditions and host factors.

The identification of specific molecular effectors with anti-tumor properties, such as the Amuc_2172 acetyltransferase (Jiang et al., 2023) [11], the Amuc_1434* aspartic protease (Meng et al., 2020) [34], and the Amuc_1100 outer membrane protein (Wang et al., 2020) [36], provides compelling evidence for this hypothesis. It is biologically plausible that different strains of A. muciniphila possess different alleles of the genes encoding these proteins or, more likely, vary significantly in their expression levels under different environmental conditions. A strain that highly expresses Amuc_1100 and Amuc_2172 might be strongly anti-tumorigenic, whereas a strain that predominantly expresses Amuc_1434 could be pro-tumorigenic due to its effects on barrier integrity.

Therefore, future research must move beyond the species level and delve into the genomic and functional diversity of A. muciniphila strains. Comparative genomics of strains isolated from healthy individuals and CRC patients, as well as from individuals with different CRC subtypes, could reveal genetic loci associated with pro- or anti-tumorigenic phenotypes. Functional characterization of these strains in relevant in vitro and in vivo models will be crucial to validate these findings and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. A deeper understanding of the strain-level diversity of A. muciniphila will be essential to reconcile the current contradictory findings and to unlock the full therapeutic potential of this fascinating gut commensal in the prevention and treatment of colorectal cancer.

Methodological considerations

The paradoxical role of A. muciniphila in CDC reflects methodological pluralism rather than biological contradiction. Critical variables include experimental model choice, sampling strategy, bacterial formulation, and analytical depth.

Inflammation-driven models (AOM/DSS) consistently show detrimental effects of live A. muciniphila [19, 20, 23], while genetic models (ApcMin/+) demonstrate protective effects [11, 27]. Immunocompromised xenograft models reveal innate immunity-mediated protection, while immunocompetent syngeneic models highlight adaptive immune mechanisms [27, 32].

Fecal samples typically show increased A. muciniphila in CRC patients [22, 43], while mucosal biopsies present complex patterns [31, 41]. Critical stratification factors include ethnicity [14, 40] and tumor molecular subtype, particularly mismatch repair status [13].

Live bacteria show context-dependent effects, pasteurized forms demonstrate consistent protection [36], and purified components provide mechanistic precision [11, 15]. The balance between beneficial signaling molecules and pro-inflammatory components determines net effects.

Based on the comprehensive analysis of 34 studies, A. muciniphila emerges as a context-dependent microorganism whose effects on CRC are fundamentally determined by host inflammatory status, immune competency, and the specific bacterial formulation employed. While live bacteria may exacerbate tumor development in inflammatory environments, pasteurized forms and purified bioactive components consistently demonstrate protective effects across diverse experimental contexts. The therapeutic potential of A. muciniphila appears to reside primarily in its specific molecular components rather than the whole organism, suggesting that targeted delivery of beneficial proteins and metabolites represents the most promising avenue for clinical translation.

Limitation

This systematic review has several limitations to consider. First, only studies published in English were included, which may exclude relevant research published in other languages. Second, meta-analysis of the data was not possible due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures and insufficient reporting. Third, most of the included human studies had a cross-sectional design, restricting causal inferences about the influence of A. muciniphila on CRC development and progression. Fourth, our review excluded conference abstracts and gray literature, which may result in publication bias.

Fifth, most of the included animal studies induced CRC via chemical means (AOM/DSS) or inoculation with cell lines, which may not fully recapitulate human colorectal carcinogenesis. Sixth, while study quality was appraised using the GRADE criteria, risk of bias assessments were not done for individual studies, which would have further informed overall confidence in the evidence. Finally, the variations in baseline characteristics across study groups, differences in techniques used to assess gut microbiota changes, lack of standardized cut-offs to define dysbiosis, and potential confounding variables were not consistently accounted for across the analyzed studies.

In summary, the abovementioned limitations restricted a more definitive characterization of the relationship between A. muciniphila and CRC. The results must be interpreted cautiously, considering these caveats. More robust human cohort studies controlling for confounders and mechanistic investigations are warranted to address these gaps. Standardization of microbiome analysis techniques and reporting protocols would greatly benefit future examinations into links between specific bacteria and CRC pathogenesis.

Conclusion

This systematic review reinforces the emerging consensus that A. muciniphila functions as a pathobiont in the CRC landscape. Its role is not fixed but is critically dependent on the surrounding host environment and, most importantly, on whether the whole, live organism or its specific molecular components are being considered. Our analysis highlights a stark functional dichotomy: while the administration of live A. muciniphila has produced contradictory outcomes and may even become pro-tumorigenic in the presence of underlying inflammation, its non-viable counterparts tell a different story. The pasteurized bacterium and its key bioactive constituents—including isolated proteins like Amuc_1100, extracellular vesicles carrying molecules such as Amuc_2172, and beneficial metabolites—consistently exert protective and anti-tumor effects, primarily through sophisticated modulation of the host immune system.

This distinction carries profound therapeutic implications and signals a necessary shift in clinical strategy. The evidence strongly suggests that future interventions should pivot from a traditional probiotic approach, which relies on the live organism, to a more targeted, postbiotic-style therapy focused on harnessing these specific, non-viable bioactive components. Such a strategy would not only mitigate the safety risks associated with introducing a live pathobiont into a potentially compromised gut environment but would also offer a more predictable and standardized therapeutic effect.

Ultimately, while the mechanistic evidence from preclinical models is compelling and paints a clear picture of potential, its translation to human health remains the most critical hurdle. The promising findings discussed herein are almost exclusively derived from in vitro and animal studies. Therefore, the urgent need for well-designed, robust human clinical trials cannot be overstated. These trials are the essential next step to validate the safety and efficacy of A. muciniphila-derived components, and to determine if this fascinating microbe can finally be translated from a subject of research into a cornerstone of clinical cancer therapy.
