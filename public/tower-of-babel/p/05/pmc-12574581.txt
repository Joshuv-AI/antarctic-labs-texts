HPV vaccination efficacy in primary and tertiary prevention of vulvar and vaginal HPV-related high grade dysplasia and cancers: A systematic review
Authors: Rosa Montero-Macías, Marie Adam, Roman Rouzier, Claire Bonneau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe impact of HPV vaccination on cervical cancer and condylomas is well-established, but its effectiveness against high grade vulvar and vaginal lesions (VIN/VaIN) and related cancers remains less clear due to their rarity. This systematic review assessed the efficacy of quadrivalent (qHPV) and 9-valent (9vHPV) vaccines in both primary and tertiary prevention of these conditions. Eleven studies met the inclusion criteria. qHPV showed 100% efficacy against HPV16/18-related VIN/VaIN and cancers in per-protocol populations, with reduced but still significant efficacy (71%) in intention-to- treat analyses. The 9vHPV vaccine further reduced risks associated with additional HPV types (31/33/45/52/58). Real-life data revealed decreased incidence of vulvar and vaginal cancers following vaccination, particularly among younger women. While vaccination appeared to reduce recurrence rates after initial treatment of HPV-related lesions, randomized trials have not confirmed a clear adjuvant benefit. Overall, HPV vaccination proves highly effective in preventing vulvar and vaginal disease, especially when administered before HPV exposure.PROSPERO number: CRD420250638054

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the cause of 99% of cervical cancer.1 HPV is also related to other gynecologic malignancies as it is responsible for preinvasive lesión (98% of vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VaIN) and 87% of vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN)), 71% of vaginal cancers, and 18% of vulvar cancers (the latter being mainly developed from vulvar lichen).2 Currently, high grade VIN has an incidence of 2.5 to 8.8 per 100 000 women/year and 20,000 new cases of invasive vulva and vagina cancers are declared in Europe per year.3 Their incidence has been increasing as HPV infection or re-exposure is more and more frequent. In the USA, between 1973 and 2000, there was an increase of +400% of VIN and +20% in vulvar cancers.4

Based on epidemiological studies, we know that for HPV-related diseases, HPV 6/11/16/18 are responsible for 80.9% of high grade VIN, 65.7% of high grade VaIN, 78% of vulvar cancers and 64% of vaginal cancers.4,5 HPV vaccination is the cornerstone of current recommendations for primary prevention of HPV-related diseases. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommended since 2015 HPV vaccine for routine vaccination at age 11 or 12 years. Since 2019, catch-up HPV vaccination is recommended for all persons through age 26 years. For adults aged 27 through 45 years, public health benefit of HPV vaccination in this age range is minimal; shared clinical decision-making is recommended because some persons who are not adequately vaccinated might benefit.6

Numerous studies have shown the benefit of the vaccine (quadrivalent and then nonavalent) with a high level of evidence in reducing the risk of HPV-related cervical malignancies and the recurrence of these diseases. Nonetheless, the role of the vaccine for the primary or tertiary prevention for HPV-related vulvar and/or vaginal malignancies has been less studied. The objectives of this systematic review were to evaluate the efficacy of the HPV vaccine in preventing vulvar or vagina high grade lesions and HPV-related cancers and secondly to evaluate the reduction risk of recurrence of vulvar and vagina HPV-related malignancies in a population with previous history of HPV-related diseases.

Material and methods

Data sources and search strategy

We performed a comprehensive systematic computer literature search on PubMed and EMBASE using the following Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms:. “Human papillomavirus,” “HPV,” “vaccine,” “vulvar cancer,” “vaginal cancer,” “vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia” and “vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia.”

Separate searches were performed with MeSH terms on Pubmed to extract all relevant literature available. A first selection of articles was made following reading of abstracts. The final selection of articles was made following the reading of the full text of the pre-selected articles. The search strategy was performed using PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses) guidelines.7

Our Systematic review was submitted to the PROSPERO register and published in the database with the registration number: CRD420250638054.

Study selection

The population of this study includes all women who received the HPV vaccine.

Inclusion criteria were studies published in English between January 2005 and May 2023. All types of meta-analyses, prospective, retrospective, and systematic reviews were eligible for inclusion in the current review related to HPV vaccine and vulvar/vaginal pathologies. All retrieved studies were evaluated for eligibility by two authors (C.B. and M.A). If there was a disagreement between these authors, a third author collaborated in the selection (R.M.).

Exclusion criteria were studies concerning exclusively cervical cancer, or gynecologic cancers not related to HPV, case reports, editorials, gray literature and short communications. The studies needed to be related to the 4-valent-HPV vaccine (qHPV) and/or the 9-valent human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine (9vHPV). Bivalent HPV vaccine studies are not included in this review because vulvar and vaginal lesions are not studied in their main related studies.

Studies were divided in i) “primary prevention” studies if their objective was to evaluate the efficacy of HPV vaccine to prevent HPV-related vulvar or vagina high grade lesions and cancers in a population without history of HPV-related diseases or in ii) “tertiary prevention” if their objective was to evaluate the reduction risk of recurrence of HPV-related vulvar or vagina high grade lesions or cancers in a population with previous history of HPV-related diseases.

Quality assessment of studies

The methodological quality of cohort studies was evaluated using the “Quality Assessment Tools of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute” (NHLBI).8

Results

Selection of studies

The systematic search found 62 relevant studies. Among them, 51 were excluded as they did not provide high grade VIN/VaIN or cancers HPV-related, or there was not a high quality of evidence (Figure 1). Finally, 11 studies conformed to the predefined inclusion criteria. Seven studies were about primary prevention and four studies about tertiary prevention. Primary prevention of vulvar or vaginal malignancies

Randomized trials

About the primary prevention FUTURE I9 was the first randomized, placebo-controlled, double- blind trial conducted to evaluate the efficacy of a prophylactic qHPV vaccine in preventing anogenital diseases associated with HPV types 6, 11, 16 and 18. It included from 2002 through 2003, in 16 countries, healthy women between the ages of 16 and 24 years, no history of anogenital warts (AGWs) or abnormal results on cervical cytology testing and had a lifetime number of no more than four sex partners (Table 1). The coprimary composite endpoints were the incidence of AGWs, high grade VIN or VaIN, or vulva/vagina cancer associated with HPV 6, 11, 16 or 18. A total of 5455 women met the inclusion criteria and were followed for an average of 3 years. It is noteworthy that the average age at inclusion was 20 years old with a mean of 2 sex life partners. Ten percent of women had abnormality on cervical cytologic examination and 27% were positive for HPV on PCR analysis or serologic testing. Vaccine efficacy against high grade VIN or VaIN associated with HPV targeted by the vaccine was 100% with 95% Confidence Interval (95%CI) [49–100] for PP, 91% 95%CI [37–100] for unrestricted population and 62% 95% CI [0–89] for ITT. In the ITT population, all events in the vaccine group occurred within the first 18 months following inclusion and then their incidence reached a plateau. Meanwhile in the placebo group, this incidence continued to increase over time. After two years, there was a tendency to a decrease of all anogenital lesions not covered by the vaccine suggesting a cross-type protective effect of the qHPV vaccine against other HPV types. During the follow-up, no vulvar or vaginal invasive cancer occurred.

Meanwhile another randomized double-blind placebo-controlled study, Villa et al.10 evaluated efficacy of qHPV vaccine on HPV-associated disease (defined as CIN, VaIN, VIN, external genital warts or cervical, vulval, or vaginal cancer) using a similar population that FUTURE I. Disease associated with HPV-vaccine types decreased by 100% 95%CI [16–100] in per protocol analysis and by 100% in ITT cohort in women assigned vaccine compared with those assigned placebo. Nonetheless, VaIN/VIN occurred, only in the placebo group, but the incidence (4 cases) during the follow-up was too low to reach significance. After FUTURE I and using the same inclusion criteria, another randomized controlled trial, FUTURE II20 evaluated the qHPV vaccine for the prevention of high grade cervical lesions but did not described specially VIN/VaIN incidence.

In 2007, Joura et al.11 conducted a combined analysis of those three randomized clinical trials: FUTURE I, FUTURE II and Villa et al.9,10,20 The main objective was to assess the efficacy of the qHPV vaccine against high grade VIN/VaIN. It included 18 174 healthy women between 16 and 26 years were analyzed in 24 countries. 15% and 20% of women were HPV positive on PCR and serological analysis, respectively, at inclusion. 22% had a previous pregnancy. There was a total of 80 high grade VIN/VaIN cases including 40 cases HPV16/18-related. The vaccine was 100% effective 95%CI [72–100] against high grade VIN/VaIN HPV16/18-related in the PP population, 97% 95%CI [79–100] in the unrestricted population and 71% 95%CI [37–88] in the ITT population. Considering all VIN/VaIN lesions, regardless of the HPV type, the vaccine efficacy in ITT was 49% 95%CI [18–69]. Analyzing the occurrence of events over time, they observed the same result with a “plateau effect” at 24 months after vaccine in this population. This study did not provide data about vulvar or vaginal invasive cancer.

Then, the 9vHPV vaccine was developed to enhance coverage of the different HPV types and Joura et al. evaluated its efficacy and immunogenicity.12 For this, they conducted a randomized, international, double-blind, phase 2b-3 study in 14,215 women aged from 16 to 26 years old. Because of ethical reasons and proven efficacy of the qHPV vaccine, the study used the qHPV vaccine as an active comparator versus the 9vHPV vaccine. 48% patients had an HPV serologic test or PCR assay positive for any HPV type at inclusion. The mean age was 21.9 years old at inclusion was slightly higher than in former studies. In a modified ITT population (at least one dose of vaccine), the efficacy for high grade cervical, VIN and VaIN related to the vaccine HPV types was 100% 95%CI [70.4–100]. There was no efficacy for patients HPV positive at inclusion. More descriptive analyses with a longer follow-up were conducted by Huh et al.13 In PP population, the efficacy of a 9vHPV versus a qHPV vaccine against all HPV31/33/45/52/58-related vulvar and vaginal diseases was 94.4% 95%CI [67.7–99.7].

Thus, they could not demonstrate a significant protection against high grade VIN/VaIN due to a lack of power (only 2 cases of high grade VaIN, all in the qHPV vaccine group). Finally, Giuliano et al. published a post-hoc analysis14 to compare 9vHPV vaccine efficacy (9vHPV arm from Huh et al. trial13) against related genital diseases with historic placebo populations (placebo arm from FUTURE I and II trials9,20). In the unrestricted population, the efficacy against high grade VIN/VaIN of a 9vHPV vaccine versus placebo was 100% 95%CI [85.7–100].

Real life data

Only one study is available about prevention offered by HPV vaccine specifically for VIN and VaIN.15 Guo et al. conducted a real-life study with a retrospective design, using the United States Cancer Statistics 2001–2019 database. They compared the age-adjusted incidence of usually HPV-related cancers among patients aged 15–34 years between two periods: before (2002–2006) and after vaccine introduction (2015–2019). They supposed a causal relation between HPV and cancer according to the anatomic site of the cancer, which is partially true for vulvar cancer for example. They observed a decrease of vulvar cancers after vaccination with a rate ratio (RR) at 0.18 95%CI [0.13–0.21] for women between 15 and 24 years and 0.54 95%CI [0.48–0.59] for women between 25 and 34 years old and a decrease for vaginal cancers for women between 25 and 34 years old with a RR of 0.65 95%CI [0.47–0.90].

Tertiary prevention of vulvar or vaginal malignancies

About tertiary prevention, Joura et al.16 conducted an ancillary study with FUTURE I and II cohorts. Their objective was to determine the effect of qHPV vaccine on the risk of developing disease after treatment for CIN or diagnosis of AGWs or VIN/VaIN.

About 17,622 women underwent 1:1 randomization to vaccine or placebo. Among them, 229 vaccine and 475 placebo patients had a diagnosis of vulvar or vaginal HPV-related disease. For those patients, vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of any subsequent HPV-related high grade VIN/VAIN or worse disease by 63% 95%CI [34.8%–93.2%] related to qHPV types and by 23.5% 95%CI [63.3%–66.4%] irrespective of causal HPV type.

Hence, a vaccination anterior to the first HPV-related event was partially protective against the recurrence of HPV-related disease.

More recently, Ghelardi et al.17 investigated adjuvant HPV-vaccination and the rate of recurrence in women undergoing surgery for high grade VIN HPV-related in a prospective case–control study with 149 women between 18 and 45 years. The study group received adjuvant vaccination after standard surgery for VIN while the control group was patients treated by standard surgery and which refused vaccination. Clinical disease relapse (CDR) was defined as a clinical disease recurrence, histologically confirmed, of high grade VIN during the seven years follow-up period. There was a difference in CDR between qHPV vaccination and controlled cases but it was not statistically significant (19% vs 32%, p = .19). By analyzing the recurrence, they found a reduction of 78.5% CDR related to incident/reactivated HPV infection with vaccination (p = .01).

Di-Donato18 published a meta-analysis summarizing the currently available data on the efficacy of HPV vaccination for tertiary prevention in patients after a first HPV-related disease. Only the two studies previously described (Joura et al. and Ghelardi et al.16,17) evaluated HPV-related recurrences after a first vulvar or vaginal HPV-related disease. It grouped a total of 740 patients treated for a high grade VIN/VaIN (34% in the vaccinated and 66% in the unvaccinated group). No difference in VIN/VaIN recurrence rate was observed. Nonetheless, there are strong limitations about this meta-analysis. First, Joura et al. included the population vaccinated in the primary prevention context whereas Ghelardi et al. included the population receiving adjuvant vaccination after a first event (tertiary prevention). Secondly, their definition of recurrence was heterogeneous: only VIN recurrences were considered in Ghelardi et al., whereas all HPV-related events were considered for Jura et al. (including AGWs, cervical, vulvar and vaginal malignancies).

In 2019, Stankiewicz19 initiated a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial protocol (NCT03051516). They aimed to compare a prophylactic 9vHPV vaccine versus placebo with the hypothesis that it can reduce the risk of high grade VIN recurrence by 50% in previously unvaccinated individuals recently treated for anal or vulvar high grade lesions. Target enrollment objective was 345 individuals, aged 27 to 69. However, the study was terminated early as an interim analysis found the intervention to be futile, as the vaccine did not decrease the likelihood of recurrent high grade lesions and finally only 185 participants were included.

Side effects

In all the randomized trials included in this review for both, the qHPV or 9vHPV vaccine, no clinically meaningful differences in serious adverse events were noted between the study groups and similar proportions of vaccine and placebo recipients reported a serious adverse event. The adverse events related to injection site were more common in the 9vHPV group than in the qHPV group.12

Discussion

The quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines demonstrated in the main randomized trials a 100% efficient direct protection against high grade vulvar, and vaginal disease or cancers related to HPV types targeted by the vaccines among females negative for HPV at the time of the vaccination with a complete vaccination.11,12,14

Initially, the qHPV (6/11/16/18) vaccine was thought to be sufficient to cover other HPV types via a crossed immunity between serotypes. However, numerous events related to others HPV occurred in the vaccinated population. To improve the efficacy of the vaccine, the 9vHPV vaccine covering HPV 6/11/16/18/31/33/45/52/58 was developed. The 9vHPV clearly demonstrated a better efficacy versus a qHPV vaccine against all HPV31/33/45/52/58-related ano-genital diseases. However, the power and/or follow-up were insufficient to demonstrate a significant protection specifically for analytical cohorts that included HPV infected women.

Efficacy of the vaccine was lower for women infected with HPV before the completion of the vaccine scheme. This ITT population can be compared to real life when vaccine was proposed at an older age. A recent article shows the average lifetime probability of acquiring HPV among those with at least one opposite-sex partner to be around 85% for women.21 That is why it is recommended to vaccine girls to have the same results for the PP population. The plateau effect during the first 2 years after vaccination suggested a protection against HPV not already encountered.

It is still controversial whether vaccination is useful for infected patients with prior HPV exposure. Some data suggest that adjuvant HPV vaccination in women treated for cervical HPV diseases reduces recurrent diseases whereas HPV vaccination does not reduce progression to cervical precancers in women with ongoing infections at the time of vaccination.22 Following treatment for high grade VIN, almost 50% of lesions will recur within 5 years following treatment. Recurrent lesions necessitate repeated treatments and may lead to compromised quality of life, functional deficits, and high medical costs.23,24 New emerging data has highlighted that HPV vaccination might have a beneficial role in the adjuvant setting, especially for cervical disease.18 Follow-up studies of vaccinated cohorts demonstrate efficacy against disease occurrence following treatment of dysplastic lesions at 24 months,16 presumably through protection against subsequent infection with a vaccine-related HPV type. But we need to follow this population longer to know efficacy over time. The unique adjuvant vaccination randomized trial failed to demonstrate efficacy after a first VIN/VaIN event.

One of the frequently attributed effects to the HPV vaccine that is cited in the literature is the onset of autoimmune disease25 but the results of a meta-analysis published in 2018 demonstrated no correlation between autoimmune disorders and HPV vaccines OR 1.03, 95%CI [0.68–1.56]24 for women until 26 years old. There is a lack of knowledge about safety for older women concerning adjuvant vaccines and further studies are needed to better evaluate the risks. Given the approval of HPV vaccination at the discretion of the attending physician for individuals aged 26–45 years old, this point needs to be addressed if we want to target patients after a first HPV-related event.

In our knowledge, this is the first Systematic Review about the efficacy of HPV vaccines in the prevention of vaginal and vulvar HPV-related neoplasia. A strength is that the selected studies have a diverse population across world regions and demographic characteristics, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. Next, the trials utilized common protocols, eligibility criteria, same population (PP, ITT), case definition, diagnosis standardization, minimizing variability in exposure and endpoint assessment providing high-quality evidence from randomized controlled trials and real- life data.

This systematic review provides useful data for clinical practice. We know that in Western countries vaccination hesitancy is a global health problem. Providing patients and their families with clear and objective information on the primary and tertiary preventive effect of the HPV vaccine on other HPV- associated pathologies (besides cervical) can help to alleviate this problem. Such data can also help health systems in countries where HPV vaccination is not yet widely implemented to objectively see the benefit on the population for different HPV-related pathologies in different population groups.

The main limitation of this systematic review is that the average age of the patients included is around 20 years including a lot of sexually active patients and of the trials had a relatively short duration of follow-up, which may not capture the long-term efficacy and impact of HPV vaccination on VIN/VaIN and related cancers. It is known that most vulvar and vaginal cancer and VIN/VaIN occur in patients older than 50 years, but this age group is not included in the patients studied. Also it will be interesting to update in 2–3 decades data of the studies included in this Systematic review. The second limitation is that women with HIV or other immunosuppressive conditions were not enrolled in most of the articles even if it is know that HIV-infected women have higher HPV acquisition, lower HPV clearance and higher to develop HPV-related gynecologic malignancies.26,27 Specifically for tertiary prevention, studies are more limited and heterogeneous than for primary prevention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review showed that the prophylactic administration of HPV vaccine prevented VIN/VaIN related to HPV with a high level of proof for at least the first decade following vaccination. The protection in a tertiary prevention setting, specifically for VIN/VaIN could not be demonstrated in this review.
