The use of terlipressin to treat refractory hypotension during allograft kidney transplantation: a report of 4 cases and literature review
Authors: Wen-He Yang, Xin-Lu Chang, Liang Zhang, Lei Wan, Yi Cheng, Yi-Chen Zhu, Fu-Shan Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntraoperative hypotension is a recognized risk factor for delayed graft function (DGF) after kidney transplantation and may compromise both short- and long-term allograft outcomes. However, the controversy persists regarding the optimal vasopressors and inotropes for maintaining stable blood pressures intraoperatively and achieving good allograft outcome after kidney transplantation. Terlipressin has demonstrated the potential in stabilizing hemodynamics and enhancing renal blood flow in patients with hepatorenal syndrome and sepsis. Additionally, terlipressin may effectively correct efractory hypotension in those receiving renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi). However, it is unclear whether terlipressin is suitable for optimizing perioperative blood pressures and is favorable for postoperative graft function in patients with kidney transplantation.Cases presentationWe reported 4 cases of patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing allograft kidney transplantation who developed intraoperative hypotension unresponsive to dopamine or fluid resuscitation. Intravenous terlipressin rapidly stabilized hemodynamics and maintained adequate mean artery pressure throughout surgery, with a reduced consumption of other vasopressors. All patients demonstrated satisfactory early graft function within the first postoperative week, with improved glomerular filtration rate, and normal serum potassium concentration and urine output. Furthermore, renal function was stable at one-year follow-up.ConclusionsTerlipressin may represent an effective and renal-protective alternative for intraoperative blood pressure maintenance during kidney transplantation. Further controlled studies are warranted to validate the potentials of terlipressin for maintaining perioperative blood pressure and optimizing postoperative allograft function in patients undergoing kidney transplantation.

Background

Kidney transplantation is a treatment most frequently used for patients suffering from end-stage renal disease (ESRD) which may provide the best opportunity for long-term and dialysis-free survival [1]. However, intraoperative hypotension often occurs in the post-anastomosis reperfusion stage, which may result in delayed graft function (DGF) [2]. DGF, which is defined as the requirement for dialysis within a week after transplantation surgery, is one of early postoperative complications that may increase short- and long-term morbidity and mortality of the recipient [3]. As a special kind of acute kidney injury (AKI) in the setting of kidney transplantation, DGF may lead to oliguria, increased allograft immunogenicity and risk of acute rejection episodes, and significantly worsen longer-term outcomes [4, 5]. It is generally believed that optimizing intraoperative blood pressure is essential for maintaining sufficient renal blood flow and is significantly associated with decreased risk of adverse allograft outcomes, such as acute tubular necrosis, DGF, and graft loss [2, 6–9].

Vasopressors and inotropes are commonly used to maintain a stable hemodynamic status in the perioperative period. However, there’s still controversy about which can provide the best allograft outcome after kidney transplantation, and the optimal intraoperative blood pressure target has not been determined. It is reported that exposure to catecholamine vasopressors such as dopamine and norepinephrine is associated with an increased risk of DGF and may lead to more postoperative arrhythmias and worsened early postoperative allograft function [10]. Available evidence indicates that terlipressin can effectively correct refractory hypotension that occurs during surgery in patients who are taking RASi [11–13]. Furthermore, vasopressin and its analog terlipressin have the potential to protect renal function in hepatorenal syndrome and sepsis [14–19]. Despite the evidence about the renal protective effects of terlipressin in above conditions, whether it is suitable for optimizing perioperative blood pressures and is favorable for postoperative graft function in patients with kidney transplantation has not yet been validated. Here, we reported our experience on 4 cases of blood pressure maintenance with terlipressin during kidney transplantation and further discussed the possible advantages over other vasopressors in the patients undergoing kidney transplantation based on available literature.

Cases presentation

All 4 patients underwent allograft kidney transplantation at the Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University in Beijing, China, from June 2022 to April 2023. These cases complied with the Helsinki Congress and this report was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Friendship Hospital (Reference Number: 2024-P2-356-01). All donors were voluntarily donated from the Chinese civilians after circulatory death and no kidney grafts from executed prisoners were used. Each transplant was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Friendship Hospital, strictly under the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and the current laws and regulations of the Chinese government. The written informed consent for participation in the study had been obtained from all 4 patients. Furthermore, the written consent to publish these details has also been obtained from all individuals.

The characteristics of the recipient and donor are summarized in Table 1. All participants underwent hemodialysis on the day before surgery and received the same anesthetic management and surgical procedures according to our institution’s standard protocols for allograft kidney transplantation. After the induction of general anesthesia, a radial arterial line was established for continuous blood pressure monitoring, along with a central venous catheter for fluid or vasopressor infusion. Anesthesia was maintained with intravenous infusions of propofol and remifentanil, and cisatracurium besylate was used for neuromuscular blockade. Following the initiation of anesthesia, intravenous infusion of dopamine was started at a rate of 1–5 µg/kg/min with a infusion pump and adjusted by the anesthesiologist based on the patients’ blood pressure status. The intravenous infusion rate of dopamine was adjusted as needed. Additionally, bolus doses of calcium or norepinephrine were administered intravenously on demand to ensure that mean artery pressure (MAP) during and immediately post-reperfusion exceeded 100 mmHg, one of important hemodynamic targets in our routine practice.

Case 1

A 37-year-old female with hypertension and ESRD by glomerulonephritis was admitted to our hospital for transplantation surgery on June 15th, 2022, and baseline blood pressures were 150–170/110–120 mmHg with no history of antihypertensive medications. She received peritoneal dialysis for 20 months and then underwent hemodialysis for 4 months before surgery. Thirty minutes after the initiation of surgery, her MAP showed a slow and continuous decline to below 100 mmHg, with a trend to further decrease. In view of the possibility that reperfusion hypotension occurred at the time of unclamping, 1 mg of terlipressin was diluted to 10 ml with saline and infused within 10 min of the arterial anastomosis stage. The MAP was gradually increased above 100 mmHg before reperfusion and remained stable for the remainder of the surgery (Fig. 1). After terlipressin fully worked, moreover, intravenous infusion rate of dopamine was reduced to 0.1 µg/kg/min and then stopped. No additional vasopressor was needed even after the unclamping.

No DGF occurred postoperatively. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation was 3.5 ml/min/1.73 m2 at baseline and recovered over 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 on postoperative day (POD) 7. The eGFR changes from the first postoperative week to 1 year after surgery are represented in Fig. 2. The serum potassium concentration decreased from 4.76 mmol/L at baseline to 3.6 mmol/L in the first postoperative week (Fig. 3). The average daily urine output in the first postoperative week was 4051.4 ± 868.6 ml/day. At 1-year follow-up, the graft survived well and no further renal replacement therapy was needed. She suffered from a transient renal dysfunction in the 12th month after surgery but recovered after antirejection drug adjustment.

Case 2

A 50-year-old male suffered from ESRD with IgA nephropathy and had well-controlled hypertension with a regimen of nifedipine and telmisartan, with baseline blood pressures of 130–140/80–90 mmHg. He had received hemodialysis for 4 years until transplantation surgery. After surgery began, MAP gradually decreased below 90 mmHg in 30 min and did not respond well to fluid resuscitation and additional norepinephrine. After 1 mg of terlipressin was administered in the same way as in the case above, MAP rised gradually above 100 mmHg within 10 min and was kept stable until the end of surgery (Fig. 1). No bolus dose of dopamine or other vasopressorswas needed after that.

No DGF occurred after surgery, and the eGFR increased from 4.15 mmol/L at baseline to over 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 on POD 7 and 1-year post-surgery (Fig. 2), with significant decreases in serum potassium levels from 5.5 at baseline to 3.7 mmol/L 7 days after transplantation (Fig. 3). The average daily urine output in the first 7 days after surgery was 3627.1 ± 824.5 ml/day. One year follow-up showed no need for renal replacement and the graft survived well.

Case 3

A 53-year-old female with glomerulonephritis caused ESRD for over 3.5 years. She had a well-controlled hypertension with amlodipine, valsartan, and bisoprolol. She had baseline blood pressures of 130–140/80–90 mmHg and had been receiving hemodialysis for over 36 months before surgery. After general anesthesia, her MAP decreased slowly over time to under 90 mmHg. Fluid resuscitation and increasing dopamine infusion rate to 10 µg/kg/min did not recover blood pressures to our targeted levels and her heart rate simultaneously rose over 85 bpm from 60 to 65 bpm at baseline. Then, 1 mg of terlipressin was diluted to 10 mL with saline and infused in 10 min. In the next 20 min, MAP gradually increased and well remained around 100 mmHg, while the intravenous infusion rate of dopamine was reduced to 1 µg/kg/min (Fig. 1) and heart rate returned to the baseline.

No DGF occurred postoperatively and the eGFR increased from 3.87 ml/min/1.73 m2 at baseline to over 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 after first postoperative week and 1-year post-surgery (Fig. 2), accompanied by significant decreases in serum potassium levels from 5.51 mmol/L at baseline to 4.14 mmol/L on POD 7 (Fig. 3). The average daily urine output in the first postoperative week was 4242.9 ± 990.6 ml/day. A 1-year follow-up demonstrated good graft function.

Case 4

A 33-year-old female with ESRD by glomerulonephritis without pre-existing hypertension was admitted to undergo kidney transplantation on April 3, 2023. Same as case 3, her MAP decreased gradually after anesthesia. One mg of terlipressin was diluted in 10 mL of saline and utilized within 10 min after unsatisfactory therapeutic responses of fluid resuscitation and increasing intravenous infusion rate of dopamine to 10 µg/kg/min. The MAP not only recoveried over 100 mmHg before unclamping, but also it was well maintained without the use of dopamine or other catecholamine drugs (Fig. 1). Furthermore, her heart rate decreased from 85 to 65 bpm afterward.

No DGF was found postoperatively and eGFR increased from 14.1 ml/min/1.73 m2 at baseline to over 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 on POD 7 and remained stable in the first-year follow-up (Fig. 2). The serum potassium levels significantly decreased from 5.14 mmol/L at baseline to 4.14 mmol/L on POD 7, as shown in Fig. 3. The average daily urine output in the first 7 days after transplantation was 3145.7 ± 781.6 ml/day. She suffered from B19 virus infection in the second month after surgery but rapidly recovered with steroid treatment. The follow-up showed appropriate graft function at one year after surgery.

Discussion and conclusions

It is well known that intraoperative hypotension is significantly associated with postoperative kidney injuries [20, 21]. Most importantly, for kidney transplantation surgery, an impaired auto-regulatory function of allograft kidney by denervation may lead to a high dependence of graft blood flow on systolic blood pressure [22]. It has been shown that intraoperative hypotension may adversely influence early recovery of allograft function and is a significant risk factor for developing DGF, which can significantly predict worsened graft outcomes and decreased patient survival after kidney transplantation [23]. To avoid the occurrence of postoperative DGF, available studies have mainly been focused on finding a reasonable intraoperative MAP threshold for kidney transplantation. Malyala et al. [2] demonstrated that post-anastomosis MAP<75 mmHg was an independent risk factor of DGF in the donation after cardiac death kidney recipients. Sicova and colleagues [24] revealed that the accumulated time of intraoperative MAP below 55 or 65 mmHg was associated with the development of DGF, and even a 5-min duration of MAP below 75 mmHg might lead to a trend of worsened graft function. Pongpruksa et al. [25] also found that the durations of intraoperative MAP below 75, 80, or 85 mmHg were the significant predictive factors for the occurrence of DGF. Especially, Kinoshita and colleagues [6] were concerned about blood pressure during reperfusion phase and noted that a systolic artery pressure of 150 mmHg or more at the time of unclamping was associated with better graft function and cardiac function, as manifested by an earlier initial urination and a larger intraoperative urine output. Even two studies reported that a MAP over 93 mmHg [26] or 100 mmHg [27] may result in a better graft performance, as mentioned in the consensus statement by the Committee on Transplant Anesthesia of the American Society of Anesthesiologists [28]. However, a widely recognized intraoperative blood pressure target for kidney transplantation has still not been reached due to the lack of evidence.

The optimal choice of vasoconstrictive and inotropic drugs for kidney transplantation recipients is still controversial. Dopamine has been using for the maintenance of circulatory function during kidney transplantation in our center. The use of a low-dose dopamine (i.e., < 5 µg/kg/min) has been shown to have renal protective effects, such as improving renal blood flow and urine output in healthy people and basic researches [29, 30]. However, in clinical settings, the evidence regarding the renal protective effect of a low-dose dopamine is inconsistent, and even a meta-analysis indicates no significant benefit to graft function [31–33]. Furthermore, administration of dopamine may be associated with an increased renal arterial resistive index, which is an accurate marker for detecting graft function [34]. A literature review about perioperative dopamine use in renal transplantation even highlights that a low-dose dopamine may lead to unpredictable adrenergic receptor agonism due to individual differences in dopamine clearance, with unpredictable potential risks to the recipient [35].

Norepinephrine is another widely used vasopressor that restores intraoperative blood pressure and temporarily improves renal function with fewer side effects [36]. It has been shown that the use of norepinephrine compared to dopamine may result in better GFR and hourly urine output [34]. However, a large retrospective cohort study demonstrates that the cumulative dosage of norepinephrine in non-cardiac surgery is one of the independent risk factors for postoperative AKI, suggesting a potential risk of norepinephrine on renal function [37]. In our routine practice, norepinephrine is not recommended because its α1 adrenergic effect may contribute to excessive restriction of the afferent arteriole. It has also been shown in a sepsis model that restoring blood pressure with norepinephrine may further exacerbate ischemia and hypoxia in the intrarenal medulla when blood flow redistribution is attributable to the development of AKI [38]. Other studies also demonstrate that the perioperative use of overall catecholamine drugs is significantly associated with a poor graft function, even DGF, and a higher risk of postoperative arrhythmias [10, 39].

In contrast, non-catecholamine drugs, such as vasopressin and its analogs, are preferred over other pressors as they have relatively safe cardiovascular profiles and their binding to V1a receptors of glomerular efferent arterioles may increase glomerular filtration [14]. As compared with norepinephrine, vasopressin has been shown to have a better renal protective potential in terms of improving diuresis and inulin clearance, decreasing renal lactate, and normalizing renal adenosine triphosphate in a rat model of endotoxic shock [40], and reducing the use of renal replacement in septic patients [41]. Hajjar et al. [42] report that vasopressin is superior to norepinephrine in reducing postoperative atrial fibrillation and a composite of mortality or severe complications including stroke, AKI and others. A recent work demonstrates that vasopressin can be safely used for controlling hypotension post-deceased donor kidney transplantation [43]. However, vasopressin has some serious drawbacks restricting its perioperative use. These include a short biological half-life, the lack of specificity for V1 and V2 receptors, and side effects [44].

Terlipressin is a long-acting analogue of vasopressin with a plasma half-time of 240–360 min and a highly selective vasoconstrictive effect by V1a receptors located predominantly within the splanchnic circulation, which may result in an increased renal blood flow and a beneficial impact on renal function in patients with hepatorenal syndrome-related AKI [15, 17]. It is reported that a slight stimulating ability of terlipressin on V2 receptors can contribute to dilation of arcuate vessels of the kidneys with an increased blood flow in the parenchymal layer [45]. The renal protective effects of terlipressin have been well established in the setting of liver transplantation and hepatorenal syndrome, which can provide better postoperative renal function [19] and prevent renal function deterioration in a significant proportion of patients with hepatorenal syndrome 1 [15]. Similarly, Fayed et al. [46] reveal that intravenous infusion of terlipressin may significantly decrease renal arterial resistive indices and intraoperative vasoactive support, increase MAP, and improve postoperative renal function. Consistently, the contrast-enhanced ultrasound monitoring show that terlipressin improves renal perfusion, increases stroke volume, decreases norepinephrine consumption and heart rate in patients with septic shock [18]. A retrospective study about hemodynamic management with terlipressin in hypotension brain-dead potential organ donors reveals that it can adequately maintain MAP and improve renal function without additional burden of vasoactive support [47]. While available evidence supports renal protective potential of terlipressin, there has been no research determining its clinical usefulness and safety for kidney transplant patients.

It is recommended by Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) to initiate the use of RASi for managing blood pressure in patients with CKD [48]. However, RASi have been associated with an increased risk of hypotension during non-cardiac surgery [49], which can lead to postoperative complications including AKI, myocardial injury and stroke [50]. In our case series, two of four patients were chronically taking RASi, which may be attributable to their intraoperative hypotension unresponsive to rortine dopamine or fluid resuscitation. They were effectively treated with terlipressin, which was known for its rapid action in correcting hypotension, especially in the patients with a reduced adrenergic vasoconstrictive response due to RASi [11–13]. This may be was attributable to the action of terlipressin’s on the vasopressin-humoral vasopressor system.

In our retrospective cases, terlipressin stabilized intraoperative hemodynamics, with reducedneed of other vasopressors. All patients exhibited adequate postoperative eGFR recovery without DGF, and demonstrated ideal one-year graft function after surgery. No terlipressin-associated adverse effects were observed [51], though this may reflect a small sample size.

This case series demonstrate that terlipressin can effectively correct intraoperative hypotension unresponse to fluid resuscitation and commonly used vasoactive drugs and may improve postoperative graft function in patients undergoing allograft kidney transplantation, supporting its potentiial as a effective and renal-protective alternative for intraoperative blood pressure maintenance during kidney transplantation. However, the small sample size (n = 4) and single-center nature of this case series may have limited generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, due to the absence of a comparison group and the data about Doppler renal arterial blood flow and velocity before and after terlipressin, a critical question that this case series cannot answer is if good postoperative graft function of patients should be attributable to improved renal blood flow perfusion with terlipressin. Thus, further randomized controlled clinical trials with strict design and large sample size are still needed to validate our incipient findings in this case series and determine whether the use of terlipressin provides additional benefits for allograft kidney transplantation.

Conclusion

Terlipressin may represent an effective and renal-protective alternative for intraoperative blood pressure maintenance during kidney transplantation.
