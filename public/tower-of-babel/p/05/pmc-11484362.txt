Healthcare leadership practice and associated factors among primary healthcare managers in East Gojam Zone, Northwest Ethiopia: a mixed method study
Authors: Endalew Minwuye Andargie, Wubshet D. Negash, Abebe Kassa Geto, Asmamaw Atnafu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeadership plays a critical role in the delivery of person centered, safe, efficient, and effective healthcare services globally. Poor healthcare leadership contributes to low staff motivation, patient dissatisfaction, and reduced healthcare-seeking behavior. However, there is limited evidence on healthcare leadership practice and associated factors among primary healthcare managers in the study area and at large in Ethiopia. Therefore, the findings of this study will contribute to improved healthcare leadership practices among primary healthcare managers.MethodFacility-based cross-sectional study supplemented with qualitative inquiry was conducted in the East Gojam zone among 532 primary healthcare managers selected by a two-stage stratified random sampling technique. A pre-tested and structured self-administered questionnaire was used to collect the quantitative data. Data were entered into Epi-Data version 4.6 and exported into Stata version 14.0 for analysis. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with healthcare leadership practice, and statistical significance was declared at a p-value < 0.05 with a 95% CI. Six key informant interviews were conducted, and thematic analysis was used for the qualitative study.ResultThe magnitude of healthcare leadership practice among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone was 45.7% (95% CI: (41.4, 50.0)). Factors associated with healthcare leadership practice were age [β = 0.44, 95% CI: (0.16, 0.71)], training in healthcare leadership practice [β = 1.19, 95% CI: (0.19, 2.20)], experience sharing [β = 1.68, 95% CI: (0.59, 2.76)], organizational communication [β = 0.19, 95% CI: (0.05, 0.33)], managerial working experience [β = 0.94, 95% CI: (0.18, 1.69)] and emotional intelligence [β = 0.35, 95% CI: (0.31, 0.38)].Conclusion and recommendationThe magnitude of healthcare leadership practice among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone was low as compared with a previous study in Ethiopia. It was significantly associated with age, training in leadership, experience sharing, organizational communication, managerial working experience, and emotional intelligence. Thus, human resource managers at primary healthcare level should consider older and more experienced candidates during assigning managers, organize training on healthcare leadership and experience sharing with model managers to improve the leadership practice of primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11712-7.

Introduction

The health care sector is complex industry, and needs a strong and effective leadership in order to provide successful healthcare services [1, 2]. Leadership is generaly recognized as the ability to influence the attitudes, beliefs, and skills of employees to meet organizational goals and objectives [3]. Healthcare leadership is the cross-cutting component of the health system building blocks, which helps to strengthen the other five building blocks (health information system, health care financing, health workforce, medical equipment and supply, and health service delivery) [4]. It is also one of the four pillars of excellence to achieve equity, efficiency, and universal health coverage in the healthcare system [5]. Healthcare managers’ leadership capacity determined the health system’s ability to deliver quality healthcare services [6]. Leadership plays a critical role for healthcare organisations to illustrate continuous reforms for safe, person centered, efficient and effective service delivery at regional and global levels [7, 8]. Leadership is also vital and an influential factor to deliver high-quality, compassionate and patient-centered care through improving organisational performance to solve challenges of good governance [9]. Leadership could be influenced by various organisational and personal factors, such as variations in gender, job commitments, work experiences, organisational culture and communication, difference in profession, and emotional intelligence [10–12].

Residents of low-income countries around the world suffered from poor health outcomes due to poor healthcare leadership caused by unstable working conditions due to decentralization and frequent health sector reforms [13, 14]. The African health system is characterised as fragile and weak associated with its poor leadership despite leadership plays a major role for better performance of managers at primary healthcare units [15, 16]. In developing countries, poor leadership practices in healthcare organizations are characterized by different epidemiological, social, political, demographic, economic, and technological uncertainties [14]. Poor healthcare leadership in healthcare organizations has also contributed to increased costs of service, reduced efficiency and effectiveness, low staff motivation, low patient satisfaction, and decreased society’s health level [17–21]. In Ethiopia, lack of leadership commitment is the main drawback to perform health reforms [22]. Unavailability of health workers associated with poor leadership can be the cause for low utilization of health service, increased maternal and child morbidity and mortality [23]. On the contrary, effective leadership has a significant positive impacts on overall organisational goals, and brought differences in healthcare performance including improved management systems, work environments, and readiness to face new challenges, and better utilisation of maternal, and child health services, thereby contributing to a reduction in maternal, and child morbidity and mortality [23, 24].

The World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted different initiative strategies to strengthen the health system of which healthcare leadership is one component [25]. One such example is in Sub-Saharan Africa, Leadership Development Programmes (LDPs) including leadership-related training have been integrated into the existing healthcare system to improve the leadership skills of healthcare managers [26, 27]. Ethiopia has also implemented a plan to transform the primary healthcare sector, which needs strong healthcare leadership from the health sector to achieve its vision of becoming a middle-income country by 2025 [28]. In Ethiopia, despite healthcare leadership being one of the four pillars of excellence to achieve universal health coverage, equity, and efficiency in the healthcare system, poor healthcare leadership is one of the major challenges to realizing health sector reforms, including the health sector transformation plan [22, 29]. Some studies arround the globe evidenced that healthcare managers’ emotional intelligence and organizational communication have a significant association with healthcare leadership practice [30–34]. In the Ethiopian context, studies on healthcare leadership practice were limited in some geographical area such as selected areas of Eastern Ethiopia [35]. Moreover, these studies did not include some important factors including organizational communication and emotional intelligence, which significantly influenced healthcare leadership practice [35–37]. To the best of the investigator’s knowledge, evidence on healthcare leadership practice and associated factors among primary healthcare managers in the study area and at large in Ethiopia is limited.

Thus, this study aimed to assess healthcare leadership practice and associated factors among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone, Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia.

to highlight gaps in workforce training and inform future educational policy development. The findings of this study will also help policymakers and planners have a better understanding of the underlying issues, design and implement strategies to improve the leadership practice of healthcare managers. Besides, since healthcare leadership is one of the six building blocks of the health system and one of the four pillars of excellence in achieving equity, efficiency, and universal health coverage, a study on healthcare leadership practice and associated factors will contribute to a strong health system.

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam Zone, Amhara National Regional State. According to the East Gojam zonal health department’s 2023 s-quarter report, the total population of the zone was estimated to be 2,646,613. Of which, 1,328,600 were males and 2,117,290 were living in rural areas. The zone has nine primary hospitals, 18 district health offices, 100 health centers, and 418 health posts. At the primary hospital level, there are 14 healthcare managers including, a chief executive officer, a medical director, and 12 case team heads. At the health center level, there were seven healthcare managers including, the head of the health center, a health extension worker supervisor, and five case team heads. Similarly, at the district health office level, there were three healthcare managers including, the head of the district health office, the acting or vice head of the district health office, and the health extension program coordinator. Thus, in the study area, there were a total of 880 primary healthcare managers.

Study design and period

A facility-based cross-sectional study supplemented by qualitative study was conducted among primary healthcare managers from March 28 to April 28, 2023.

Population

Source population

The source population for this study was all healthcare managers in health centers, district health offices, and primary hospitals in the East Gojam zone.

Study population

The study population for the quantitative study was randomly selected healthcare managers in the health centers, district health offices, and primary hospitals of the East Gojam zone. The study population for the qualitative study was purposefully selected healthcare managers in the health centers, district health offices, and primary hospitals of the East Gojam zone.

Eligibility criteria

All healthcare managers in the health centers, district health offices, and primary hospitals of the East Gojam zone who have more than six months of managerial working experience were included in this study.

Variables and measurements

Healthcare leadership practice was the outcome variable while socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, current marital status, professional work experience, managerial work experience, educational level, family size, and net monthly salary), training-related factors (profession, duration of pre-service training, pre-service training institution, pre-service training program, and training on healthcare leadership practice), health system factors (organizational communication, experience sharing with peers, job description, mechanism of assignment to a managerial position, mode of career advancement, interference in decision making, facility type, and supervision), and emotional intelligence were independent variables.

Healthcare leadership practice was measured based on responses to 18 statements; each item has a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = very often). The summated score, which is continuous was considered a dependent variable during the linear regression model. The magnitude of healthcare leadership practice was computed as a percentage mean score using the summated mean score [35].

For this study, primary healthcare managers included managers in the primary hospital (chief executive officer, medical director, and case team heads), the district health office (head of the district health office, the vice head of district health office, health extension program coordinator), and health center (head of the health center, the health extension worker supervisor, and case team heads) [5]. Emotional intelligence is the ability to understand, and manage emotions, to distinguish between them, and exercise in guiding one’s thoughts and practices [38]. It was measured with 40 items [39]. Each item has a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = always) [40]. Organizational communication refers to the exchange of information and ideas within the organization. It was measured with a five-point Likert scale for each of the six items, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) [41].

Sample size determination

The sample size for healthcare leadership practice was determined using a single population mean estimation formula by considering a 95% confidence level, a standard deviation of 4.5 from a previous similar study conducted in Ethiopia [35], a 5% margin of error, and a 10% non-response rate yielding a total sample size of 515. The sample size for associated factors was calculated with Epi Info version 7.2.5.0 by the power approach method using associated factors of healthcare leadership practice among primary healthcare managers from a previous study [35]. Finally, a 10% non-response rate and a design effect of 1.5 were considered. 95% confidence level, 80% power, and the ratio of non-exposed to exposed were taken as an assumption for the power approach method for a cross-sectional study. As such, the final sample size for this study was 532. For the qualitative part, the maximum sample size was determined by saturation level or information redundancy. For this particular study, the sample size for the qualitative portion was six (Table 1).

Sampling procedure

The study participants for the quantitative portion of the study were selected using a two-stage stratified random sampling procedure. The study area was stratified into a primary hospital, a district health office, and a health center. The total number of study participants randomly selected from primary hospitals, district health offices, and health centers were based on the population proportion formula. East Gojam Zone had a total of nine primary hospitals, 18 district health offices, and 100 health centers. Eight primary hospitals, 16 district health offices, and 80 health centers were randomly selected from the total of 9 primary hospitals, 18 district health offices and 100 health centers in the East Gojam zone. Over all, there were a total of 720 (112 from primary hospitals, 48 from district health offices, and 560 from health centers) primary healthcare managers in the selected primary healthcare facilities in the study area. Thus, using the population proportion formula (83 from 112 healthcare managers in primary hospitals, 35 from 48 healthcare managers in district health offices, and 414 from 560 healthcare managers in health centers) were selected using simple random method using the Open Epi program (Fig. 1).

For the qualitative study, a purposive sampling technique was used to select primary healthcare managers for key informant interviews based on position (chief executive officer from primary hospital, head from district health office, and health center), and experience (those who have high years of experience as compared to others) i.e. Informants that were thought to give rich information about healthcare leadership practice were selected purposefully. For this particular study a total of six key informant interview was selected purposefully until saturation level or information redendancy has been achieved.

Data collection tools and procedures

Quantitative data was collected using structured, self-administered questionnaires adapted from reviewing different literature related to healthcare leadership practice and associated factors. To measure healthcare leadership practice, a standardized tool was adapted from a previous similar study in the astern part of Ethiopia [35]. The questionnaire also included possible associated factors for healthcare leadership practice among healthcare managers in the related literature review, including socio-demographic characteristics, training-related factors, health system factors, and emotional intelligence [35, 41–47]. The tool to measure the emotional intelligence of healthcare managers was based on the Quick Emotional Intelligence Self-Assessment (QEISA) tool, which was adapted from the 2015 Paul Mohapel model [39]. A simple random sampling technique was used to select study participants from each stratum (i.e., to select 83 from 112 healthcare managers in primary hospitals, 35 from 48 healthcare managers in district health offices, and 414 from 560 healthcare managers in health centers) using the Open Epi program.

Items for the qualitative data include socio-demographic characteristics, questions to explore healthcare leadership practice, and reasons to influence healthcare leadership practice among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone. A smart phone and a semi-structured interview guide were used as data collection tools for qualitative data. A key informant interview was conducted using a semi-structured interview guide, audio was recorded using smart phone and field notes were also taken. All interviews were recorded with the permission of the interviewees. The length of interviews was ranges from minimum of 20 min to maximum of 35 min.

Data quality control

The questionnaire was prepared in English and translated into the local language, Amharic and back to English to check for consistency. A pre-test was done on 27 (5%) primary healthcare managers in the Adiet district in the West Gojam zone to check the internal consistency of the tool used to collect quantitative data. The scale reliability of assessment tools to measure healthcare leadership practice, emotional intelligence, and organizational communication was checked with Cronbach’s alpha value. The Cronbach’s alpha value for healthcare leadership practice (18 items), organizational communication (6 items), and emotional intelligence (40 items) was 0.81, 0.85, and 0.92 respectively. Five data collectors (health officers) and two supervisors (a master of public health) were recruited and received a two-day training on data collection procedures. Data collectors were monitored by the two supervisors. The filled-out questionnaire was inspected daily by the two supervisors and the investigator to check for completeness.

For the qualitative inquiry, the semi-structured interview guide was prepared in the local language of Amharic before being translated into English. To ensure the quality of the data, proper designing of interview guide was done and expert judgement (involved from multidisciplinary professions) was applied. The key informant interview (KII) and audio recording processes were made by the principal investigator to maintain the original meaning conveyed by study participants. The validity of qualitative data was also ensured by minimizing information bias through the purposive sampling method. A private space was used during the interview process to ensure confidentiality. The audio was recorded using two smartphones and a power bank was availed to charge them if their battery goes to low. So as to improve the guarantee objectivity of the interpretation of results, all authors participated during analysis and interpretation process for the qualitative portion.

Data processing, analysis, and interpretation

The collected data was entered into Epi Data version 4.6, and codes were given appropriately by the principal investigator. Then, it was exported into Stata version 14.0 for analysis. Data cleaning was performed to check for accuracy, and missed values of variables. To describe the study population with relevant variables, descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were carried out. Simple and multiple linear regression analyses was employed to assess the association between healthcare leadership practice and associated factors. The coefficient of determination and significance test of beta coefficients were used to assess the fitness of the linear regression model. All variables with p-values less than 0.2 were entered into a multiple linear regression model to control the confounding effect.

LINE assumptions were checked accordingly. A linear relationship between dependent and independent variables was assessed with a scatter plot for each continuous variable. The assumption of multicollinearity was checked using Durbin-Watson statistics, and its value was 1.9. The normality of the residual was also checked with a normal p-p plot, histogram, and density graph after predicting the residual. The skewness and kurtosis tests for normality were done and they were non-significant (Ho: Normality assumption is fulfilled). The assumption of equality of variance was also checked with a scatter plot.

Multicollinearity between independent variables was checked based on the variance inflation factor (VIF), and values for all independent variables were below ten. Beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to measure the strength of the association between healthcare leadership practice and associated factors for both simple and multiple linear regression model. Statistical significance was declared at a p-value < 0.05 in multiple linear regression analysis. Finally, the result of the analysis was presented using a textual description, table, and graph.

For the qualitative part, after the data were collected with key informant interviews, the audio-recorded data was heard repeatedly. Then, it was transcribed verbatim and translated to English, and it was changed in to plain text format, and entered into open-code software version 4.03. It was coded line-by-line. The commonly reported responses from the interview on healthcare leadership practice and influencing factors were grouped into themes, subthemes, and codes were developed based on the specific objectives of the study. Overall, there were a total of two themes were (healthcare leadership practice and influencing factors), and six sub-themes. The two sub-themes (good healthcare leadership practice and poor healthcare leadership practice). Influencing factors for healthcare leadership practice had also four sub-themes (managers level, organizational level, employees’ level, and at the community level. Thematic analysis was used. The final result was reported with a quoted reporting method supporting quantitative findings.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 521 primary healthcare managers participated in this study with a response rate of 97.9%, and the rest, 2.1% of primary healthcare managers didn’t fill out the questionnaire completely. Ninty 5% of primary healthcare managers were male. Primary healthcare managers in the study area had a mean age of 31.3 years (± 2.5 SD). 68% of primary healthcare managers were married. Primary healthcare managers in the study area had an average net monthly salary of 7587 ETB (± 1422 ETB). The majority of primary healthcare managers (62%) had a Bachelor of Science (BSc) degree. Primary healthcare managers in the study area had a mean professional working experience of 3.8 years (± 1.6) and managerial working experience of 2.6 years (± 1.2) (Table 2).

Healthcare leadership practice

The overall mean score for healthcare leadership practice was 2.98 ± 0.46. The actual minimum and maximum scores for healthcare leadership practice were also 35 and 76 respectively, with summated mean score of 53.7. Accordingly, the percentage mean score of primary healthcare managers for healthcare leadership practice was 45.7% (95% CI: (41.4, 50.0)).

34% of primary healthcare managers in the study area thought that time spent worrying about team morale is time that’s wasted (mean = 2.35 and SD = 1.12). 19% of primary healthcare managers had encouraged everyone to work toward the same overall objective very often (mean = 3.27 and SD = 1.30). Ninty 8% of primary healthcare managers also believe that personal feelings should not be allowed to affect in the way of performance measurement (mean = 3.29 and SD = 1.12). 12% of primary healthcare managers in the study area did not enjoy planning for the future (mean = 3.06 and SD = 1.34). 40% of primary healthcare managers feel threatened when someone criticizes them (mean = 2.16 and SD = 1.15) (Table 4). These finding are also supported by qualitative study findings from KII.

Factors associated with healthcare leadership practices

In simple linear regression analysis, sixteen variables (age, family size, educational level, professional work experience, net monthly salary, training on healthcare leadership, job description, supervision, ways of nominating healthcare managers, experience sharing, mode of career advancement, profession, managerial working experience, organizational communication, facility type, and emotional intelligence) had a p-value less than 0.20.

In simple linear regression, only variables with a P-value less than 0.20 were considered as candidate variable for multiple linear regression analysis. The final multiple linear regression model predicts 76% of the variability in healthcare leadership practice (adjusted R-squared = 0.76 and it was significant at P < 0.001). Accordingly, the analysis result showed that six variables (age, training on healthcare leadership, experience sharing, organizational communication, managerial work experience, and emotional intelligence) were found to have an independent and significant association with healthcare leadership practice (Table 5).

Discussion

This study assessed healthcare leadership practice and associated factors among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone, Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia. In this study, the percentage mean score of healthcare leadership practice among primary healthcare managers was 45.7% (95% CI: (41.4, 50.0)). Age, managerial working experience, training on healthcare leadership, experience sharing, organizational communication, and emotional intelligence are factors associated with healthcare leadership practice.

The magnitude of healthcare leadership practice (45.7% (95% CI: (41.4, 50.0))) in this study is lower than a similar previous study done in the eastern part of Ethiopia where the overall magnitude of healthcare leadership practice was 52% [35]. The possible reason for this difference might be due to time variation and related factors. The previous study was conducted in the eastern part of Ethiopia in 2018. During 2018–2019, there was leadership, management, and governance training for primary healthcare managers and staff as part of primary healthcare sector transformation in four regions (Amhara, Tigray, Oromia, and Southern Nation Nationalities and Peoples). This training might contribute to enhanced knowledge, skill, and motivation for healthcare leadership practice, and this might be responsible for a little larger magnitude as compared with the current study findings.

The finding of this study is also lower than previous studies in Ethiopia (Oromia, Benishangul, and Addis Ababa) where the percentage mean scores of healthcare leadership practice were 70%, 68%, and 61% respectively [37]. The possible reason for this discrepancy may be due to the difference in the measurement of healthcare leadership practice. The previous studies conducted in Ethiopia (Oromia, Benishangul, and Addis Ababa) during 2018-19 used secondary data from hospital self-assessment reports received for DHIS 2, but this study used primary data using eighteen items to measure healthcare leadership practice among primary healthcare managers. The other possible reason could be the difference in the study setting in that the current study was conducted in health centers, district health offices, and primary hospitals, whereas those previous studies were done at the hospital level only.

This finding was also higher than a previous study conducted in Oromia, West Hararghe Zone where the overall healthcare leadership practice was 31.6% [36]. This discrepancy might be due to methodological differences in that the current study was conducted among healthcare managers at health centers, district health offices, and primary hospitals, but the previous study was conducted among health care providers in primary hospitals only, and the sample size was small (n = 204). The other possible reason could be the difference in the measurement of healthcare leadership practice in that the current study used eighteen items to measure healthcare leadership practice, but the previous study used Nine items to measure healthcare leadership practice. The finding of this study was also in line with previous studies conducted in Uganda, the United States, Brazil, and Finland where the percentage mean scores of healthcare leadership practice were 48%, 43%, 47%, and 50% respectively [48–51].

It was identified that age has a significant positive association with healthcare care leadership practice in which older primary healthcare managers were found to have better healthcare leadership practice. It implies that older primary healthcare managers with growing knowledge are capable of better understanding others, and might serve as a source of inspiration for their employees in the facility [52]. This finding is consistent with previous studies done in South Africa, the United Kingdom, Sweden, and the United States in which older age and healthcare leadership had a significant positive association [45, 53–55]. This association might be explained by the fact that older primary healthcare managers are more likely to be flexible, capable of assessing their surroundings, may find it less difficult to build trust and develop interpersonal skills [54].

There was a positive and significant relationship between healthcare leadership practice and managerial working experience in which more experienced primary healthcare managers were found to have better healthcare leadership practice. It implies that more experienced primary healthcare managers might have better problem-solving, critical thinking, and decision-making skills in healthcare leadership practice. This finding is in agreement with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, and Sweden [43, 44, 54, 56]. This association may be due to the fact that primary healthcare managers who work with relatively long tenure are familiar with the facility’s structure, work processes, and administrative activities and are allowed to learn and grow professionally. This improves performance in the healthcare field.

The current study revealed that taking Leadership related training and healthcare leadership practice have a significant positive association. It is found that those primary healthcare managers who took leadership related training were found to practice better healthcare leadership. This finding is in line with previous studies done in Ethiopia, Iran, the United States, and Saudi Arabia [23, 47, 57, 58]. This association can be explained by the fact that leadership-related training might contribute to providers’ motivation, teamwork, a positive working atmosphere, enhanced resource management, and in turn creats favorable conditions for healthcare leadership practice [57].

It was also found that healthcare leadership practice is significantly and positively associated with experience sharing with peers. This finding is consistent with a previous study in the eastern part of Ethiopia in which primary healthcare managers who had got experience sharing with peers had practiced better healthcare leadership [35].

This study evidenced that organizational communication and healthcare leadership practice had a significant positive association in which primary healthcare managers with better organizational communication performed strong healthcare leadership practice. This implies that organizational communication creates a harmonious and positive working environment which in turn can motivate employees toward organizational goals, and facilitate team work. This finding is also consistent with previous studies conducted in Brazil, Slovakia, and the United Kingdom [33, 34, 59]. This association might be due to the fact that good organizational communication create favorable condition for receiving suggestions and opinions from stakeholders, for delivering the right message, gathering timely feedback from different decision making bodies, and to handle conflicts appropraitely using proper communication channel [60, 61].

Emotional intelligence was also found to have a significant positive association with healthcare leadership practice. It is evidenced that more emotional intelligent primary healthcare managers were found to have better leadership practice. This finding is in line with previous studies conducted in Egypt, South Africa, India, the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom [3, 31, 32, 61–66]. The possible reason for this association might be primary healthcare managers with high emotional intelligence would be more likely to understand employee’s emotion, manage interpersonal relationships, create a positive work culture within their facility, and recognize, anticipate, and manage emotions to collaborate with team members. In the contrary, less emotionally intelligent healthcare managers might not be effectively collaborate and communicate with others so that employees will be less eager to share their ideas, and are less likely to reach their full potential in the practice of healthcare leadership [67].

Study limitations

The quantitative study findings were supported by qualitative study findings from key informant interviews, and this finding would generate strong evidence about the underlying issue. To control the hetrogeneity effect on the study finding, this study used a stratified random sampling procedure to select study subjects and design effect was considered so that representativeness of the study finding has been ensured.

The use of a self-administered questionnaire may have introduced minimal social desirability bias which may occur when respondents give socially acceptable responses to questions. To minimize this social desirability bias, data collectors briefly explained about the objective of the study-to-study subjects before the actual data collection, the confidentiality of information was maintained, and private space was used for key informant interviews. Self-reporting bias might also have occurred when respondents erroneously interpreted questions. To minimize this self-reporting bias, the questionnaire was prepared in the local language Amharic. Moreover, this study couldn’t ascertain cause and effect relationship since it is a cross-sectional study.

Conclusion

This study has assessed healthcare leadership practice and associated factors among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone, Amhara National Regional state, Ethiopia. Accordingly, the magnitude of healthcare leadership practice (45.7% (95% CI: (41.4, 50.0))) among primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone was low as compared with a similar previous study in the Eastern part of Ethiopia where the magnitude of healthcare leadership practice was 52%.

Primary healthcare managers in the East Gojam zone those who were older age, more experienced, took training on healthcare leadership, got experience sharing with peers, working at facilities with good organizational communication, and those who are emotionally intelligent were found to have better healthcare leadership practice.
