Anomalous inferior vena cava (IVC) with azygos continuation is a rare vascular anomaly characterized by the absence of the hepatic segment of the IVC, with venous blood redirected through an enlarged azygos vein draining into the superior vena cava. The estimated prevalence is approximately 0.2% to 3% in the general population,1 with an overall incidence of approximately 0.6% among patients with congenital heart disease.2

The left atrial appendage (LAA) is widely recognized as the predominant source of cardioembolic thrombi in patients with nonrheumatic atrial fibrillation.3 Oral anticoagulation, administered either as vitamin K antagonists or direct-acting oral anticoagulants, constitutes the cornerstone of therapy for mitigating the risk of cardioembolic stroke in this population.4

Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) is most commonly indicated for stroke prevention in patients at high thromboembolic risk who have contraindications to oral anticoagulation, or in those who are eligible for oral anticoagulants but have a compelling reason to pursue a nonpharmacological alternative.5,6 LAAO is typically performed via the common femoral vein, advancing through the IVC, followed by transseptal puncture to access the left atrium.7 However, certain challenging anatomical or procedural factors may preclude the use of standard transfemoral access, necessitating alternative vascular approaches.

We report a case of a patient with an anomalous IVC with azygos continuation in whom successful implantation of a Watchman (Boston Scientific) LAAO device was achieved via the right internal jugular vein after previous unsuccessful attempts using femoral access.

Case Summary

An 82-year-old man with a recently diagnosed congenital anomalous IVC with azygos continuation (Figure 1), and a prior unsuccessful transfemoral attempt at LAAO, was admitted for a planned transjugular LAAO procedure. His past medical history was significant for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 6 and a HAS-BLED score of 5, sick sinus syndrome with subsequent dual-chamber permanent pacemaker implantation, prior transient ischemic attack, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease not requiring dialysis.

He reported multiple episodes of falls while on warfarin therapy, necessitating several interruptions of anticoagulation. He denied chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope. Given his elevated stroke risk and difficulties maintaining long-term anticoagulation, he expressed strong interest in pursuing LAAO as an alternative stroke-prevention strategy.

Given the anatomical constraints, computed tomography (CT) angiography of the head and neck (Figure 2), along with a dedicated cardiac CT of the LAA (Figure 3), was performed for anatomical assessment and preprocedural planning. CT angiography of the neck confirmed patency of the right internal jugular vein and its suitability for access. Cardiac CT demonstrated a Watchman landing-zone diameter of 20.5 × 27.1 mm, a circumference of 75 mm, an area of 427 mm2, and a landing-zone length of 41.8 mm. The optimal C-arm angulation for device deployment was determined to be 30° right anterior oblique and 6° caudal. Based on these measurements, a 31-mm Watchman device was selected for LAAO via the right internal jugular vein.

How We Did It

Procedural Steps

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia in accordance with standard clinical practice. Vascular access was obtained via the right internal jugular vein (primary access site) and the right radial artery (arterial line) under combined ultrasound and fluoroscopic guidance. Two Perclose ProStyle devices (Abbott) were predeployed in the right internal jugular vein. The initial 8-F sheath in the right internal jugular vein was then upsized to an 18-F Cook Check-Flo Performer introducer sheath, followed by the introduction of the Watchman TruSteer (WTS) access system (17 F).

Given challenging angulation and difficulty in directing the WTS system toward the interatrial septum, the system was exchanged for an Agilis NxT medium-curl steerable introducer (Abbott). The septum was subsequently crossed using an Astato XS 20 wire (Asahi Intecc) inside a Turnpike LP 135-cm microcatheter (Teleflex) under both transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and fluoroscopic guidance (Figure 4A). Intraprocedural heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time above 300 seconds.

The Astato XS 20 wire was exchanged for a 0.014-mm Grand Slam wire (Asahi Intecc) positioned in the left superior pulmonary vein. Septal dilation was initiated using a 4.0 × 30 mm Emerge PTCA dilatation catheter (Boston Scientific) (Figure 4B). A Navicross 0.035-inch microcatheter (Terumo) was then advanced over the wire, after which an Amplatz Super Stiff wire (Boston Scientific) was introduced. This was followed by further transeptal dilation using a 7 × 60 mm Armada balloon dilatation catheter (Abbott) (Figure 4C).

The 18-F Cook Check-Flo Performer introducer sheath was exchanged for a 20 × 33 mm Gore DrySeal Flex introducer sheath, which had been modified using a heat gun to accommodate the angulation of the superior vena cava. The WTS delivery sheath was then advanced without a dilator and telescoped over a 5 × 60 cm Armada balloon to the distal LAA under both TEE and angiographic guidance (Figure 5A).

A 6-F pigtail catheter was introduced alongside the WTS delivery sheath, and an LAA angiogram was performed. A 31-mm Watchman device was subsequently deployed within the LAA (Figures 6 and 7). Before device release, the PASS criteria were fully satisfied: position (appropriate protrusion into the left atrium), anchoring (confirmed by tug test), size (adequate device compression), and seal (absence of significant peridevice leak). TEE and angiography demonstrated no meaningful residual peridevice flow. The flow across the transseptal defect was left-to-right and was left untreated.

Potential Pitfalls

Transjugular access introduces a more angulated trajectory to the right atrium, increasing the technical demands of sheath advancement and catheter manipulation. Ultrasound-guided puncture is essential to minimize the risk of carotid artery injury, venous laceration, and neck hematoma with potential airway compromise.

Transseptal puncture via the jugular approach is more challenging because of altered catheter angulation and atrial geometry, modifying the transseptal needle orientation and increasing the risk of inadvertent aortic root or posterior atrial wall puncture. Meticulous TEE and fluoroscopic guidance are therefore essential to ensure safe and precise septal access.

LAA cannulation may be technically demanding because of suboptimal coaxial alignment, increasing the risk of perforation, peridevice leak, or device embolization. Meticulous TEE assessment of sheath position, depth, device seating, and compression ratio before and during deployment is therefore critical.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates a technically challenging approach to LAAO in a patient with an anomalous IVC with azygos continuation, where standard femoral access was not feasible. Transjugular access proved safe and effective, highlighting a viable alternative route. Success relied on meticulous preprocedural planning, multimodality imaging, and adaptive strategies, including innovative modifications to optimize device positioning.
