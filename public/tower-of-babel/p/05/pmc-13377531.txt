Individual causal effect estimation accounting for latent disease state modification among bipolar participants in mobile health studies
Authors: Charlotte R Fowler, Xiaoxuan Cai, Habiballah Rahimi-Eichi, Lisa Dixon, Justin T Baker, Jukka-Pekka Onnela, Linda Valeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with bipolar disorder tend to cycle through disease states such as depression and mania. The heterogeneous nature of disease across states complicates the evaluation of interventions for bipolar disorder patients, as varied interventional success is observed within and across individuals. We hypothesize that disease state acts as an effect modifier for the causal effect of a given intervention on health outcomes. To address this dilemma, we propose an N-of-1 approach using an adapted auto-regressive hidden Markov model, applied to longitudinal mobile health data collected from individuals with bipolar disorder. This method allows us to identify a latent variable from mobile health data to be treated as an effect modifier between the exposure and outcome of interest while allowing for missing data. A counterfactual approach is employed for causal inference and to obtain a g-formula estimator to recover said effect. The performance of the proposed method is compared with naive approaches across extensive simulations and application to a multi-year smartphone study of bipolar patients, evaluating the individual effect of social activity on sleep duration across different latent disease states.Keywords: latent variable analysis, missing data, precision medicine, time series

Introduction

Severe mental illness serves as a class to highlight the most serious and long-lasting mental illness conditions, including schizophrenia, severe bipolar disorder, and severe major depression. The treatment of these individuals is complicated by the heterogeneous nature of SMI between patients and disease states, resulting in varied interventional success (Mills et al., 2021; Mohr et al., 2004). Heterogeneity arises not only from differences in the effect of a given intervention between individuals, but also from differences in the disease states one experiences, such as mania, depression, and euthymia. Even when two patients have the same diagnosis, this does not guarantee that they both experience the same disease states or that their disease states manifest in similar ways (Angst, 1978; Angst et al., 2004). This heterogeneity necessitates an individualized analysis approach that can contend with complex causal relationships.

Mobile health (mHealth) studies rely on digital technology to record exposures and outcomes in real time (Onnela and Rauch, 2016; Torous et al., 2016). This study design provides rich longitudinal data for each participant and can fill in informational gaps between in-person follow-ups with patients. Here, we consider the Bipolar Longitudinal Study, a cohort study from McLean Hospital that collected mobile health data from 74 patients with bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, or schizoaffective disorder for up to five years (Cai et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). Due to the heterogeneous nature of individuals living with SMI and the data collected, we employ an N-of-1 approach where participants and their relative causal effects are studied at the individual level, rather than the aggregate (Cai et al., 2022; 2024; Daza, 2018).

However, we face several challenges in estimating the individual causal effect, including not directly observing the disease state and missing data. Bipolar disorder is characterized by a patient exhibiting mood states of mania, hypomania, and depression (Brady et al., 2017; Grande et al., 2016). It has also been shown that a disease state framework is helpful to understand the mood and impairment faced by individuals with schizophrenia across time (Mohr et al., 2004). In both populations, it is understood that disease state can generate heterogeneous intervention effects within an individual (Grande et al., 2016; Mohr et al., 2004; van den Ameele et al., 2020), thus acting as a modifier. Accurately estimating the causal effect of a given intervention on health outcomes across disease states for individuals living with SMI will inform clinicians when giving recommendations to improve quality of life and disease prognosis. For example, when studying the effect of physical activity on sleep, the impact of physical activity on sleep might be different depending on the presence of a depressive or manic state, thus modifying the observed effect. Unfortunately, it is difficult to observe an individual’s disease state directly from mHealth data.

Latent modifiers are common in heterogeneous treatment effect literature, where the modifier is used to explain the differences in observed effects between individuals in the sample (Kim et al., 2019; Lyu et al., 2023; Shahn and Madigan, 2017). However, these methods rely on the identification of latent subgroups among individuals in the sample. In the context of latent disease states modifying the individual causal effect, we instead aim to identify the latent classes explaining heterogeneous effects across time points for a given individual. Notably, when differences in the impact or direction of a given treatment exist and are undetected, we may spuriously conclude a treatment to be beneficial or harmful by aggregating across all time.

To account for a latent variable representing disease state that acts as an effect modifier for the effect of interest, we employ a forward and backward algorithm to learn the disease state from our observed multivariate time series. The hidden Markov model (HMM) provides a framework to identify a latent state from its response time series through estimation of the initial state probabilities, a transition matrix, and the response distribution conditional on the latent state (Rabiner and Juang, 1986; Rabiner, 1989). It is a natural choice to identify latent disease states from mHealth data, given its design for time series data with a latent multinomial covariate. We propose a novel use of HMM that allows for auto-regression, adjusts for missing data through marginalization and recursive multiple imputation, and provides estimation and inference of individual causal effects while accounting for the uncertainty introduced by the latent modifier.

In the remainder of this paper, we will first define our causal estimand of interest. Next, we summarize traditional notation and algorithms for constructing an auto-regressive hidden Markov model (ARHMM) and explain our adaptations to allow us to fit the model to mobile health data. To evaluate our proposed methods, we compare them in simulations, in application to the Bipolar Longitudinal Study leveraging both actively and passively recorded data. Lastly, we discuss the strengths and limitations of our work, and the future directions needed.

Inference

We employ a block bootstrap approach to obtain confidence intervals for the frequentist implementation, as is common in time series analysis literature (Bühlmann, 2002). We specifically follow the block bootstrap procedure proposed by Clémençon and Tressou (Clémençon et al., 2009) for an HMM, which accounts for discontinuities that might be induced by separating blocks at random points when an underlying latent state is present. This method uses the predicted sequence of the latent state to generate blocks to be sampled with replacement, such that the observed data is split at each time point the predicted returns to the most frequently occurring state from a different state at . The blocks are then sampled with replacement based on probabilistic weights calculated by the block length until a new time series is obtained with a length as long as the original data. The model is re-fit on each resulting multivariate time series of observed variables, and we use a percentile confidence interval from the bootstrap estimates. We denote this bootstrap with ‘B’ for block bootstrap (HMM-F-S-B, HMM-F-M-B, or HMM-F-ML-B, depending on the imputation procedure). In the supplementary material, we additionally consider a parametric bootstrap as was proposed by Visser et al. (2000). In Figure A.2 of the supplementary material, we visually display the differences in implementation and missingness approach of the six proposed frequentist algorithms.

Application

The Bipolar Longitudinal Study (BLS) is an ongoing mobile health study at McLean Hospital where 74 patients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder are followed for up to 5 years. Their anonymized call and text logs, GPS movements, accelerometer behavior, and daily symptom, emotion, and behavior survey responses are recorded via the Beiwe application (Onnela et al., 2021). The patients additionally participate in approximately monthly in-person visits where a range of assessments are conducted. Sample descriptive statistics for BLS are available in the supplemental material.

Discussion

We introduce two HMMs designed to perform causal estimation and inference while addressing latent modification, complex hypothesized causal relationships, and missing data. We demonstrate in our simulation that the proposed models provide unbiased estimation of the contemporaneous average individual causal effect of interest and high prediction accuracy of the latent state. The simulation also demonstrates that confidence intervals resulting from the blocked bootstrap for the models provide adequate coverage. In addition to the two frequentist-proposed models, we also explore the performance of an equivalent Bayesian model implemented using a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm through STAN (Carpenter et al., 2017) in the supplementary material. This model is similarly able to handle missingness and provides coverage intervals. In simulations, the prediction and estimation accuracy of the Bayesian approach is comparable to the two frequentist models; however, the approach fails to achieve sufficient coverage. Despite this limitation, we anticipate this method may be of interest in the future for sensitivity analyses, through the incorporation of informative priors.

In an application to the BLS, we see how the proposed frequentist HMMs can recover a bipolar individual’s disease states using mobile health data in the context where sufficient data and variability are observed; however, we fail to find statistically discernible results of the effect of digital socialization on sleep duration. We also identify in the application the limitations to the proposed methods when there is short follow-up, a high rate of missing data, or lack of variability in the observed data. Overall, in the application we see that as long as sufficient data and variability between states are recorded, the model is able to capture and identify the underlying disease states of interest and make inferences.

In general, we recommend the use of the single imputation approach (HMM-F-S-B) in cases with low rates of missing data, as the model is more computationally efficient and achieves superior prediction accuracy in simulations. However, in cases with higher rates of missingness or limited follow-up, we recommend the model with multiple imputation (HMM-F-ML-B) as it is more robust at handling the increased uncertainty generated from the missingness and less prone to underflow. To select the number of latent states, we recommend either fitting the model across a range of plausible numbers of states and selecting the model with the lowest AIC, or using prior participant knowledge, such as from validated in-clinic scores, to identify the hypothesized true number of states. We note that while here we focus on applying the proposed methods to a singular participant at a time using an N-of-1 approach, we hypothesize the models could be extended to hierarchical modeling where estimation and inferences are made not only at the individual level, but also across a sample of participants.

Our proposed methods provide an approach to estimate causal effects at the individual level in multivariate time series data, in the presence of latent modification, missing data, and complex causal relationships. This represents a substantial contribution to the latent modification literature, which primarily focuses on identifying latent subgroups of individuals who respond to treatment differently, rather than grouping time points within time series data (Kim et al., 2019; Shahn and Madigan, 2017). However, there are still several limitations to our approach.

First, we assume that stationarity holds after conditioning on the latent state. This notably relaxes the assumption of stationarity used in most analyses of intensive longitudinal data (Aledavood et al., 2017; Luckett et al., 2020; Tewari and Murphy, 2017). It additionally reflects the prior findings of Cai et al. (2022) that identified period-stable causal estimates in the Bipolar Longitudinal Study, assuming that said periodicity was due to an underlying disease state. However, the stationarity assumption conditional on latent states may be violated in some mobile health studies. If stationarity is presumed to be unrealistic, it can be evaluated using tools such as the augmented Dickey–Fuller test on residuals (Dickey and Fuller, 1979; Fowler et al., 2024). Further work is needed to extend methods that are robust to violations to stationarity, such as state space modeling, to a setting with underlying latent states.

We additionally assume that the missingness is ignorable. It is possible in mobile health studies that the missingness is not at random, and extensions of our proposed methods incorporating a hypothesized informative missing mechanism are needed. Another limitation is the limited resources for inference for HMMs. More work is needed to identify the ideal method for performing a bootstrap with a multivariate time series with an underlying latent state or to develop asymptotic inference results for complex HMMs. We additionally note that our proposed methods are most accurate with low to moderate correlation between observed variables. The methods suffer from underflow, or loss of numerical precision due to very small probabilities, when many observed variables are specified as descendants of the latent state, or when follow-up is short (e.g., less than 300 time points). Since mobile health studies may have numerous strongly autocorrelated time series, an extension that incorporates a shrinkage penalty is likely warranted.

Lastly, more work is needed to comprehend the implications of a violation of the causal assumptions for estimates. Specifically, the assumption of conditional exchangeability and thus no unobserved confounding likely does not hold for observational mobile health studies. In intensive longitudinal data, further research is needed to evaluate the extent to which including additional observed lagged information can compensate for the bias introduced by unmeasured confounders. In addition, it would be of interest to extend the proximal causal inference framework to a time series setting to formally address this limitation (Tchetgen Tchetgen et al., 2024; Ying et al., 2023).

Importantly, in our context, we note that given our hypothesized DAG, the latent state is a confounder for the relationship between the exposure () and outcome (). While predicting the approximate latent state and adjusting for it in estimation should reduce the bias introduced by the latent confounder compared to a naive approach, more work generally is needed to examine the extent to which latent confounding can be handled through latent state prediction, and under what assumptions the effect of interest would still be identifiable.
