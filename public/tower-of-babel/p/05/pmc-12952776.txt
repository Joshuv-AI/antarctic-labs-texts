A proposal for expedited stem cell transplantation for TP53-mutant myelodysplastic neoplasms and acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Shyam A Patel, Steven M Devine, Jan Cerny
Published: 2025

TO THE EDITOR:

TP53 mutations define a high-risk subset of patients with myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), including hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) recipients.1, 2, 3, 4 Outcomes after HCT remain poor, largely because of relapsed disease.5,6 Patients with biallelic TP53 represent the ultrahigh-risk subset of this heterogeneous disease (compared to monoallelic TP53). Single-center studies have shown overall survival (OS) benefit for HCT.7,8 The multicenter BMT CTN 1102 trial showed that HCT improved OS in TP53-mutant MDS or AML.9 Allograft has been the traditional management paradigm for biallelic TP53, although there may be subversion of the graft-versus-leukemia effect because of an immunosuppressive microenvironment.10 Immune evasion is a consequence of dysfunctional p53 in cancer cells, which may contribute to adverse outcomes in the post-HCT setting.

Studies have attempted to identify which subgroups derive the most benefit from HCT. Inferior post-HCT outcomes have not been shown in patients without del(17p) or complex karyotype.11 Factors that favored superior 1-year OS included low comorbidity index, favorable performance status, and achievement of complete remission (CR) before HCT.6 Other favorable factors in recipients include lower variant allele frequency and absence of complex cytogenetics.12 Herein, we outline the critical barriers and propose a framework for collaborative study toward solutions for expedited HCT in TP53-mutant MDS and AML.

Critical challenges

A major challenge is appropriate selection of first-line therapeutics, which affects the likelihood of achieving CR before HCT.8 Patients can incur cumulative toxicity and develop infections while awaiting HCT. Logistical aspects that can lead to delayed HCT include identifying an ideal HLA-matched donor and meeting thresholds for medical clearance. Delays can result in an irreversible loss of HCT candidacy. Critical points on the patient journey from presentation through post-HCT intervention include the ability to tolerate first-line therapy and achieve CR, the ability to undergo intensive conditioning chemotherapy, and the ability to tolerate graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis.

Proposed solutions

1.Liberalization of donor selection. Once a patient is deemed an HCT candidate, the donor search should commence. This may involve a matched related donor or matched unrelated donor (MUD).13 Contemporary guidelines recommend assessing donor availability in the context of urgency for the recipient.14 We can cautiously extrapolate from hypothesis-driven research aimed at expanding the National Marrow Donor Program (NMDP) donor pool. For example, the ACCESS trial aims at extending the therapeutic reach of HCT and allows for the use of mismatched unrelated donors (MMUDs).15

Expansion of donor selection is also supported by data from the PHYLOS trial involving MMUDs.16 The European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT) showed reduced risk of relapse and improved OS with use of younger MMUDs compared to older MMUDs.17 High-resolution HLA typing may facilitate the use of MMUDs.18 BMT CTN 1702 showed no survival difference in patients who were very likely to find a MUD compared to those who were not; the strategy was to proceed immediately to an alternative donor.19

2.Acquisition of morphologic CR before HCT. Most authorities recommend acquisition of a morphologic CR as a sine qua non for proceeding to HCT. Patients who are not in morphologic CR or unfit should not proceed with HCT. Minimal residual disease (MRD) status is also critical to consider, and a debate persists. In a study of HCT for patients with AML in first CR, 79.7% had persistent mutations before HCT, and MRD positivity did not have an independent impact on survival.20 A recent study of HCT in AML showed that 5-year OS was 23% for those who were not in remission.21 A study of TP53-mutant MDS showed similar prognoses for pre-HCT MRD negativity vs positivity.22 Patients may lose HCT candidacy during attempts to induce deeper remissions, and permission of MRD may allow for early HCT. A study by Italian Group for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (GITMO) showed that bridging therapy with decitabine and venetoclax can facilitate HCT in older patients.23

Although hypomethylating agent (HMA) therapy has been the preferred frontline regimen, the data are debatable. Neither venetoclax nor CPX-351 improves OS in TP53-mutant AML.24, 25, 26 Characteristics of an optimal induction regimen include acquisition of a morphologic CR with modest treatment-related toxicity so that transplant can be delivered safely with minimal transplantation-related mortality. The choice between HMA/VEN vs HMA alone for HCT candidates is based on optimizing CR rate, allowing for more patients to proceed with HCT, rather than emphasizing outcomes with long-term therapy with either agent.24 Future studies may aim to explore the ideal regimen for HCT candidates.

3.Mitigation of GVHD risk. The introduction of effective medications against GVHD allows for improved donor options, including haploidentical donors.27,28 The use of post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) for GVHD prophylaxis might mediate the disparities in outcomes between MUD and MMUD HCTs.29 Cord blood HCT can also be considered, because this has been shown to confer similar survival as MUD HCT.30 The choice of conditioning regimen may be best tailored to each patient. Rapid tapering of immunosuppression may be beneficial to help prevent relapses, given the wide availability of effective alternative GVHD therapies.

4.Utility of posttransplant maintenance. Disease relapse is the most common cause of HCT failure in patients allografted for TP53-mutant AML, and strategies to address this are urgently required. Post-HCT maintenance has been studied using eprenetapopt.31 The VIALE-T trial is currently studying post-HCT maintenance using azacitidine and venetoclax, and the field will benefit from dedicated subgroup analysis for TP53-mutant AML. The AMADEUS trial studied post-HCT azacitidine in high-risk MDS and AML, and the MO-TRANS trial is studying mocravimod for post-HCT maintenance.32,33 Prophylactic donor lymphocyte infusion may prevent relapse, although this has not been specifically studied for TP53-mutant AML.34 Low-dose decitabine plus recombinant human colony stimulating factor (rhG-CSF) maintenance therapy can reduce the risk of relapse after HCT.35,36 Post-HCT MRD monitoring via duplex sequencing may benefit patients if performed on a regular basis such that relapse can be detected and intervened upon early.37 The BMT CTN State of the Science Symposium proposed the MEASURE study to assess MRD follow-up and recommended a platform-type maintenance trial.38 Evidence-based MRD assessment recommendations are lacking. We recommend post-HCT bone marrow assessment at least once every 3 months, beginning 1 month after HCT, to assess for relapse or allograft loss. To date, maintenance therapies remain investigational.

5.Design of dedicated prospective trials for TP53-mutant MDS or AML. The design of prospective randomized clinical trials that are unique to the TP53-mutant subset may be warranted. Such trials may seek to examine the optimal conditioning regimen, donor subtype, and GVHD prophylaxis regimen. Fitness for patients might be determined by physiological functional assessment or frailty index. Clinical trial enrollment dedicated to patients with TP53-mutant MDS/AML can be challenging given that these patients often have suboptimal performance status; therefore, appropriate patient selection will be critical.

6.Modification of reporting metrics for HCT. The mandatory reporting of the 1-year all-cause mortality metric poses a barrier to proceeding with HCT.3 Although HCT may indeed be the best option, it is highly challenging to ensure life expectancy up to 1 year for these patients. Such considerations have led transplant centers to not offer HCT to patients. A better metric might be 6-month OS or rolling 3-year OS. To achieve policy-level changes, governing bodies might need to permit separate reporting for this subgroup; this may help prevent skewing the publicly visible 1-year OS data in an unfavorable direction. The addition of a TP53-mutant AML variable is currently under consideration by the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research for use in the annual center-specific analysis. These changes might have regulatory implications. Policy-level changes may have payer implications, and resource burden would also need to be considered. Operational changes may include more stringent or granular reporting of pre- or post-HCT status or implementation of guidelines set forth by multiple consortia in the field of HCT.

In summary, further work regarding TP53-specific prospective trials remains to be performed. The field will also benefit from considering the feasibility of early HCT, including resource allocation, effect of added volume of high-acuity patients at HCT centers, and health care costs. We suggest exploration of the MMUD pool to help expand donor options and improve early access to HCT for eligible patients.
