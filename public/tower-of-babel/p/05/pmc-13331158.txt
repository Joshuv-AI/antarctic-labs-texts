Appropriateness of paediatric vitamin prescribing in Vietnamese outpatient settings: associated factors
Authors: Em Canh Pham, Tuong Vi Thi Le, Hong Tuoi Thi Do, Thanh Chi Le, Nhan Thanh Vo, Hien Duc Le, Nam Tran Nguyen, Tien Minh Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveVitamin supplementation is widely practised in paediatric populations. This study aims to describe and evaluate vitamin prescribing practices in paediatric outpatients.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingA tertiary referral paediatric hospital in Vietnam.Patients400 paediatric outpatients prescribed vitamin supplements.Main outcome measuresPrescribing patterns were analysed by vitamin type, age group, body mass index (BMI) category and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10)-coded diagnosis. Dosage and age appropriateness were assessed against international guidelines (American Academy of Pediatrics/National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements/U.S. National Academy of Medicine).ResultsMultivitamin complexes (41.3%) and vitamin B1 (27.0%) accounted for the majority of prescriptions, predominantly in children aged 1–10 years and in association with respiratory disorders (60.3%). Although 74.2% of patients had normal BMI, this group received 63.3% of combined vitamin and dietary supplement prescriptions. Overall adherence was high, with 91.5% of prescriptions meeting recommended dosages and 98.8% being age-appropriate. In multivariable analysis, gastrointestinal disorders were associated with a higher likelihood of inappropriate dosing (adjusted OR 4.92, p=0.005), while no demographic factors remained significantly associated with age-inappropriate prescribing after adjustment.ConclusionPaediatric vitamin prescribing in Vietnamese outpatient settings showed high adherence to recommended dosage and age criteria. A proportion of prescriptions exhibited dosage deviations, particularly among multivitamin and combination products. Gastrointestinal disorders were associated with an increased likelihood of inappropriate dosing. To improve dosing accuracy in resource-limited settings, the integration of weight-adjusted dosing algorithms and electronic prescribing alerts may be considered. Further studies are needed to clarify contributing factors and optimise prescribing practices.Keywords: Child Health, Therapeutics, Toxicology, Pharmacology, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Vitamins and micronutrients play a fundamental role in child growth, immune development and metabolic regulation.1 In paediatric populations, inadequate vitamin intake may contribute to impaired growth, increased susceptibility to infections and long-term health consequences.2 Consequently, vitamin supplementation is recommended in specific clinical situations, such as vitamin D supplementation during infancy or vitamin A supplementation in young children at risk of deficiency, according to international public health and clinical guidelines.2 3 The WHO and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) emphasise that vitamin supplementation should be targeted to individuals with documented or high-risk deficiencies and strictly evidence-based, in order to maximise clinical benefits while avoiding unnecessary exposure or excessive intake.3 4

Despite these recommendations, vitamins and dietary supplements (DS) are widely used in paediatric clinical practice worldwide.5 Studies have shown that approximately one-third of children and adolescents use DS, with multivitamin (MulV) products being the most commonly consumed formulations.6 7 While supplementation may help address specific nutrient deficiencies, routine or indiscriminate use in otherwise healthy children is generally not recommended because adequate nutrition should primarily be obtained through diet.5 Inappropriate supplementation may lead to excessive nutrient intake, increased healthcare costs and potential safety concerns.6 8

The double burden of malnutrition—coexisting undernutrition with micronutrient deficiencies and rising overnutrition—poses a major public health challenge in low- and middle-income countries undergoing nutritional transition. 9 In Vietnam, this paradox is pronounced in children, where stunting and micronutrient deficiencies persist alongside increasing overweight and obesity.9 10 National data show that undernutrition affects nearly one-fifth of children under 5, while overweight prevalence has risen to approximately 27% in school-aged and adolescent populations, heightening future non-communicable disease risk.9 Micronutrient deficiencies, particularly vitamin A, iron and zinc, remain prevalent and contribute to impaired growth and immune dysfunction, even as excess caloric intake promotes metabolic imbalance.9 10

In clinical practice, vitamins are frequently prescribed to paediatric outpatients to support recovery from common illnesses such as respiratory or infectious diseases.5 6 However, prescribing practices may not always align with evidence-based guidelines.4 8 Previous studies have reported notable levels of inappropriateness with recommended paediatric supplementation practices, including inappropriate dosing or age indications.8 10 11 For example, analyses of paediatric outpatient prescriptions have identified guideline non-adherence rates of approximately 21% for age recommendations and over 14% for dosage limits, particularly in MulV formulations and in younger children.10

These issues may be particularly relevant in low- and middle-income countries, where prescribing patterns of DS remain insufficiently characterised.10 11 Understanding how vitamins are prescribed in routine paediatric care is essential for identifying potential gaps between clinical practice and international recommendations.4 8 Nevertheless, existing research in this area remains limited, particularly regarding the evaluation of prescribing patterns and the appropriateness of vitamin use in paediatric outpatient settings.11 12

In Vietnam, vitamins and MulV products are widely used in paediatric care, yet systematic evaluations of prescribing patterns and their adherence to international guidelines are scarce. Given the increasing availability of complex MulV formulations and the potential risks associated with inaccurate dosing, examining the appropriateness of paediatric vitamin prescribing is necessary to support rational pharmacotherapy and improve child health outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to analyse patterns of paediatric vitamin prescribing in Vietnamese outpatient settings and to evaluate their appropriateness with respect to international guideline recommendations, with particular attention to MulV dosage accuracy.

Methods

Study objects

City Children’s Hospital, a leading 1000-bed tertiary paediatric facility under Ho Chi Minh City’s Department of Health in southern Vietnam,13 served as the study site.

Research design

This study employed a cross-sectional design using paediatric outpatient prescription data collected between January and December 2024.

Sample size and sampling procedure

In 2024, a total of 9984 outpatient prescriptions containing DS were dispensed at three pharmacy counters of City Children’s Hospital and extracted from the ISOFH hospital information system. The required sample size was calculated using the single-proportion formula: n = Z2 × p (1 − p) / d2, where n is the sample size, Z is the Z-score corresponding to a 95% confidence level (Z=1.96), p is the expected proportion of appropriate vitamin prescribing and d is the margin of error. A conservative estimate of p=0.50 was used to maximise sample size, with d=0.05. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was 384 prescriptions. To improve precision and account for potential exclusions, a total of 400 prescriptions were included in the final analysis. Eligible vitamin prescriptions were randomly selected from the sampling frame using a computer-generated randomisation procedure, in which each prescription was assigned a unique identification number and selected through a simple random sampling technique.

Eligible prescriptions included outpatient vitamin prescriptions issued in 2024 with complete information on patient age, body mass index (BMI) or BMI Z-score (according to WHO/U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) standards),14 15 as well as detailed information on vitamin type and composition. Prescriptions were excluded if they lacked essential information such as patient demographics, prescriber details, dispensing time or dosage and administration instructions, or if they were issued for inpatients. If a randomly selected prescription did not meet the eligibility criteria or contained incomplete data, it was excluded and replaced by another prescription randomly selected from the remaining eligible prescriptions in the sampling frame using the same computer-generated randomisation procedure. This process was repeated until the predetermined sample size was reached.

Study variables

Study variables were clearly defined to ensure accurate assessment of paediatric nutritional status. Nutritional status was determined using age-specific and sex-specific Z-scores. Children <5 years were classified by weight-for-height Z-scores according to WHO 2006 standards:14 severe acute malnutrition (SAM) (Z < −3 SD), moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) (−3 ≤Z < −2 SD), normal (−2 ≤Z ≤ +1 SD), overweight (+2 < Z ≤ +3 SD) and obesity (Z > +3 SD). Children aged 5–19 years were classified using BMI-for-age Z-scores based on WHO 2007 references:15 severe thinness (Z < −3 SD), thinness (−3 ≤Z < −2 SD), normal (−2 ≤Z ≤ +1 SD), overweight (+1 < Z ≤ +2 SD) and obesity (Z > +2 SD).

For uniformity, nutritional status across all ages was grouped as severe undernutrition, underweight malnutrition, normal, overweight or obesity according to WHO criteria. For statistical analysis, categories were further consolidated into three groups: underweight (including SAM, MAM, severe thinness and thinness), normal and overweight (overweight and obesity). Primary diagnoses were coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10).

The appropriateness of vitamin dosage and age indication was assessed against current guidelines from the AAP, the National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements (NIH-ODS), the U.S. National Academy of Medicine (formerly the Institute of Medicine, IOM) and manufacturer-approved product information from the Vietnam Drug Administration.16,18 Prescriptions were classified as appropriate if the prescribed dosage and age indication fell within the recommended ranges of these references. Prescriptions were considered inappropriate if the dosage fell outside the recommended age-specific range or if the vitamin was prescribed for an age group not indicated by guideline recommendations or product labelling.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted in three steps: (1) identification of all DS-containing outpatient prescriptions from the ISOFH hospital information system database; (2) extraction of clinical and demographic variables, including age, sex, anthropometric indices, residence, primary diagnosis coded according to ICD-10 and vitamin characteristics (composition, dosage, strength, quantity dispensed and route of administration); and (3) data processing and statistical analysis using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics (V.26.0) (online supplemental figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistics were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous data were expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR), depending on normality. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the χ² test. Univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with inappropriate vitamin prescription; results were reported as ORs with 95% CIs. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.13

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was not incorporated into the design, execution or dissemination of this study due to the use of de-identified retrospective data.

Results

Paediatric patient characteristics

The mean age was 47.11 (SD 38.43) months (range: 0.02–192 months). Most patients were aged 1–10 years (80%, n=320), while infants <1 year accounted for 16% (n=64). Males slightly predominated (53.2%, n=213). The mean BMI was 16.57 (SD 3.11) kg/m² (range: 7.7–39.0 kg/m²). Undernutrition affected 6.8% (n=27), while 19.0% (n=76) were overweight or obese. Respiratory disorders were the most common diagnoses (60.2%, n=241; ICD-10 J00-J99), followed by infections-parasitic diseases (12.3%, n=49; A00-B99) and gastrointestinal conditions (8.2%, n=33; K00-K95); other categories each accounted for 1–3% (table 1 and online supplemental table S1).

Across age groups (table 1 and online supplemental table S1), differences were observed in demographic and clinical characteristics. Infants <1 year (n=64) had a mean age of 5.99 (SD 3.40) months, weight of 7.06 (SD 1.93) kg and BMI of 16.28 (SD 2.05) kg/m². Most had normal BMI (87.5%). Respiratory diseases were most frequent (65.6%), followed by infections-parasitic (7.8%) and gastrointestinal disorders (6.3%). The mean duration of vitamin supplementation was 6.58 (SD 6.09) days.

Children aged 1–10 years constituted the majority of the cohort (80%, n=320), with a mean age of 49.86 (SD 29.69) months and a mean BMI of 16.54 (SD 3.50) kg/m2. Overweight and obesity were observed in 22.2% (n=71), representing a 4.7-fold increase compared with infants <1 year (p<0.05). Respiratory disorders predominated (58.8%), followed by infections-parasitic diseases (13.1%) and psychological-neurological conditions (2.5%). Additionally, adolescents aged 11–16 years accounted for 4% of patients (n=16), with a mean age of 156.63 (SD 17.96) months and a mean BMI of 18.41 (SD 3.21) kg/m2. Most were male (75%) and had normal BMI (81.2%). Respiratory disorders remained predominant (68.8%), while gastrointestinal, endocrine and neurological diagnoses were absent. Vitamin supplementation duration was shortest in this group (6.56 (SD 6.89) days).

Vitamin utilisation patterns

MulV complexes were the most frequently prescribed supplements (41.3%, n=165), followed by vitamin B1 (ViB1) (27.0%, n=108). Vitamin C (12.3%, n=49) and vitamin D3 (ViD3) (8.5%, n=34) were also prescribed. Other formulations were less common (≤3.5%), including combined ViB1-ViD3 prescriptions (3.5%, n=14), while ViD3-vitamin K1 (ViK1) and vitamin PP (ViPP; niacinamide/vitamin B3) each accounted for ≤1.0% (online supplemental figure S2).

Across age groups, most prescriptions combined vitamins with other DS (85.2%, n=341), while vitamin monotherapy accounted for 14.8% (n=59). MulV predominated across all ages, particularly in children aged 1–10 years (35.7%, n=143). Single-vitamin preparations containing ViK1, vitamin K2 (ViK2) or ViPP were rarely used (≤1.2%) and largely absent in adolescents. In addition, prescriptions most commonly included one (59.7%) or two products (32.0%) (table 2, figure 1A and online supplemental figure S3A).

Assessment of vitamin dose and age appropriateness versus recommendations

Overall adherence to paediatric vitamin safety criteria was high (figures 2 and 3). A total of 91.5% of prescriptions (n=366) complied with recommended dosages, while 8.5% (n=34) were classified as inappropriate (figure 2A). Most dosage deviations occurred in children aged 1–10 years (82.4%). Age appropriateness was 98.8% (n=395), with five mismatches identified, primarily in infants <1 year and none in adolescents aged 11–16 years (figure 3A).

Stratified by nutritional status (figures 2B and 3B), dosage appropriateness remained 91.5%, with the normal-BMI group accounting for the majority of prescriptions and 73.5% of inappropriate cases. Dosage deviations were observed across all BMI categories. Age appropriateness remained 98.8%, with all mismatches occurring in the normal-BMI group.

By diagnostic category (figures 2C and 3C), prescriptions were predominantly concentrated in respiratory diseases (group 1), accounting for 56.75% of dose-appropriate prescriptions (227/400) and 60.00% of age-appropriate prescriptions (240/400), with low proportions of inappropriateness (3.50% and 0.25%, respectively). Rare disease categories (groups 7–11) accounted for <10% of prescriptions and showed minimal inappropriate use.

By formulation (figure 4A), most single-vitamin products achieved 100% dosage appropriateness. In contrast, MulV complexes accounted for the majority of dosage deviations (30/34 cases), corresponding to 18.2% of MulV prescriptions (30/165) and 7.5% of the total cohort. Isolated deviations were observed for vitamin C (one case) and ViD3-ViK2 (three cases). Overall, MulV accounted for 88.2% of all dosing deviations.

Age appropriateness remained high across formulations (98.8%) (figure 4B). All single-vitamin products and most simple combinations achieved 100% age-appropriate prescribing. Only five mismatches were identified, involving MulV complexes (n=4) and vitamin C (n=1), representing 1.2% of prescriptions. These mismatches occurred in children aged <1 year or 1–10 years.

Assessment of associations

Bivariate Pearson χ² analysis (online supplemental table S3) identified several factors associated with dosage and age appropriateness. Dosage appropriateness was significantly associated with diagnostic category (p=0.0096), number of DS products per prescription (DS/P) (p=0.0354) and vitamin formulation (p<0.0001). The strong association with formulation was driven by higher dosing errors in MulV, whereas most single-vitamin products showed near-perfect appropriateness. Sex, residence, BMI classification and nutritional status were not associated with dosage errors (all p>0.05). Age appropriateness was significantly associated with age group (p=0.0004) and diagnostic category (p=0.038), while no other variables were significant.

The logistic regression analysis demonstrated that most demographic and clinical variables were not significantly associated with the appropriateness of recommended dosage or age after adjustment. Sex, age group, nutritional status and the number of DS/P showed no statistically significant relationship with dosage appropriateness. In addition, the wide CIs for the diagnosis categories associated with age appropriateness, particularly gastrointestinal diseases (crude OR=15.48, 95% CI 1.36 to 175.78; adjusted OR=20.94, 95% CI 0.81 to 540.85), indicate limited precision and estimate instability, likely due to sparse data and small subgroup sizes. However, diagnosis and vitamin composition remained important determinants. Compared with respiratory conditions, prescriptions for gastrointestinal diseases were significantly more likely to involve inappropriate dosage (adjusted OR=4.92, 95% CI 1.61 to 15.06, p=0.005) (table 4A). In addition, formulations other than MulVs were associated with substantially lower odds of inappropriate dosing (adjusted OR=0.08, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.25, p<0.001). Regarding age appropriateness, children aged ≥1 year had significantly lower odds of age-related prescribing errors than infants <1 year (adjusted OR=0.01, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.16, p=0.001). Furthermore, non-MulV formulations were associated with a reduced likelihood of age-inappropriate prescriptions compared with MulVs (adjusted OR=0.04, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.70, p=0.027) (table 4B).

Discussion

The present findings regarding nutritional burden and paediatric disease patterns are consistent with Vietnamese and global data, confirming respiratory diseases as the leading cause of paediatric healthcare visits and associated supplement use (~60.2%).10 11 This high prevalence reflects the well-documented susceptibility of young children due to immature immune function.11 A clear ‘dual burden of malnutrition’ was observed, with 6.8–7% undernutrition and 19% overweight/obesity, in line with trends in Southeast Asia and globally.11 19 Comparable data from Ho Chi Minh City reported lower rates of overweight/obesity (14.5%), suggesting a rising trajectory and increasing clinical relevance, particularly given its association with more severe respiratory conditions.10 11 20 21 Meanwhile, persistent undernutrition highlights an ongoing public health challenge relative to high-income settings. The study population was predominantly children aged 1–10 years (80%), with respiratory conditions accounting for the majority of cases (60.2%), consistent with national reports identifying acute respiratory infections as key contributors to paediatric morbidity and nutritional deficits.22 23 Notably, stunting peaked at 18–23 months, a critical period of dietary transition and declining maternal immunity.22 Age-related differences in disease patterns were also evident, with acute infections more common in younger children and chronic conditions emerging in adolescents. Additionally, the predominance of respiratory disorders, particularly among children aged 1–10 years, underscores their central role in paediatric outpatient care. This pattern is consistent with global evidence indicating that acute respiratory infections remain a leading cause of morbidity in children.24 25 The observed BMI distribution further highlights the coexistence of undernutrition and overweight/obesity, reflecting the growing double burden of malnutrition and its implications for child health and clinical management.26 27

This study also reveals distinct supplementation practices. A clear divergence in supplementation patterns is observed between the present study and recent reports describing a ‘probiotic-centric’ approach. In contrast, MulV complexes predominated in this cohort, accounting for 41.3% of prescriptions,11 aligning more closely with data from the USA and Turkey, where MulV-mineral products remain the primary choice for general health support.5 6 10 This preference may reflect a pragmatic, physiology-oriented strategy, as acute respiratory infections—comprising 60.3% of cases—are associated with increased metabolic demands, thereby supporting the use of B-complex-containing formulations.10 Regional comparisons further highlight differences in supplementation priorities. While the Vietnamese cohort emphasised broad-spectrum MulV and ViB1 (27.0%) for children aged 1–10 years, Indian hospital-based studies reported a predominance of vitamin D (70.2%), largely targeting infants with neonatal conditions,28 whereas outpatient data favoured B-complex use in older children.29 Although international guidelines recommend targeted supplementation (eg, vitamin D for breastfed infants),30 the current pattern reflects a broader, preventive approach aimed at addressing potential subclinical deficiencies and supporting recovery during acute illness. The high utilisation of MulV complexes and ViB1 suggests a preference for broad-spectrum supplementation in routine practice. Similar trends in paediatric populations have been reported, with widespread use of MulV and combined DS.6 7 The frequent use of multiproduct regimens may reflect clinician and caregiver perceptions of nutritional benefits, despite limited evidence supporting routine supplementation in otherwise healthy children.7 10 11 In addition, supplementation across all BMI categories, including undernourished and overweight children, suggests that nutritional status may influence prescribing decisions, although patterns appear broadly consistent across groups.

From a safety perspective, this study demonstrates high adherence to safety recommendations, with age-appropriate prescribing in 98.8% of cases and correct dosing in 91.5%. This level of appropriateness contrasts with global concerns regarding paediatric supplement use, where the absence of clear regulatory guidance and non-professional initiation often leads to misuse or overdose.31 Despite this overall appropriateness, dosing errors remained the most frequent type of prescribing deviation, consistent with international systematic reviews and multicentre studies in paediatric pharmacotherapy.32 33 Notably, the current analysis identifies MulV complexes as the principal source of these deviations, accounting for 88.2% of dosing errors. This finding suggests that the primary safety challenge in paediatric outpatient prescribing is not age misclassification but the computational complexity of estimating cumulative micronutrient intake from multicomponent formulations during poly-supplementation.10 Similar concerns have been raised in regulatory discussions, where ambiguous labelling and the lack of standardised paediatric reference values complicate accurate dosing of DS.34 International evidence supports these observations. Studies from the USA and Turkey report widespread use of MulV-mineral products in children, highlighting similar challenges related to formulation complexity and dosing accuracy.6 11 Systematic reviews further indicate that prescribing errors in paediatrics occur predominantly during the prescription stage and are strongly associated with medication complexity rather than demographic factors such as sex or nutritional status.11 32 33 Complex multicomponent supplements may therefore increase the likelihood of exceeding recommended intake levels due to overlapping nutrient profiles.11 In the present study, the concentration of dosing deviations among children aged 1–10 years and the association between gastrointestinal disorders and increased odds of inappropriate dosing suggest that both prescribing complexity and clinical context may influence dosing accuracy. Previous studies have shown that inappropriate prescribing, particularly in paediatric populations, is often associated with clinical complexity and variability in dosing practices.10 11 32 35 36 Collectively, these findings indicate that prescribing appropriateness in paediatric supplementation is primarily influenced by therapeutic complexity and formulation characteristics rather than patient demographics, underscoring the need for clearer paediatric dosing references and improved labelling standards for multinutrient products.6 11 32

Importantly, this study provides new insights into paediatric vitamin supplementation in Vietnam, addressing a gap in available data on outpatient prescribing patterns and safety. While overall adherence to age and dosage recommendations was high, MulV complexes were commonly associated with dosing deviations, suggesting that formulation complexity may play a role in prescribing inaccuracies. These findings add to the existing literature on paediatric pharmacotherapy and indicate that vitamin utilisation patterns may be influenced by the coexistence of nutritional challenges and common paediatric illnesses in developing healthcare settings.8 9 11 From a practical perspective, these findings highlight potential areas for improving prescribing practices, including the use of clinical decision support tools, such as electronic prescribing alerts and standardised weight-based dosing references, as well as clearer labelling for multicomponent supplements to support safe use in paediatric populations. Furthermore, the identification of formulation type, particularly MulV products and diagnostic category as factors associated with dosing deviations provides additional insight into potential targets for intervention. These findings are consistent with prior evidence indicating that complex formulations and combination products may be associated with a higher risk of inappropriate dosing or misuse in children.10 11 Optimising prescribing strategies may therefore be beneficial, particularly in settings where combination supplementation is common.

This study also has several important strengths and limitations. This study is strengthened by a robust methodology, including computer-generated random sampling to minimise selection bias and enhance statistical power in a tertiary paediatric setting. The use of internationally recognised standards (WHO/CDC growth references and AAP, NIH-ODS guidelines) ensures rigorous evaluation of prescribing appropriateness. Moreover, multivariable logistic regression allowed for a detailed assessment of dosing error determinants, emphasising the role of formulation complexity beyond basic demographic factors. While the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and the single-centre setting may constrain generalisability, these are common features of hospital-based pharmacoepidemiological studies. In addition, the diagnostic categories associated with age appropriateness showed wide CIs, likely due to the limited number of observations or events in certain subgroups; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future prospective multicentre research across diverse healthcare settings in Vietnam is warranted to confirm these findings, explore longitudinal supplementation patterns and assess the clinical impact of dosing deviations, thereby supporting the development of context-specific national guidelines.

Conclusion

This study suggests a generally high level of adherence to paediatric vitamin safety standards in Vietnam, with age appropriateness exceeding 98% and dosage appropriateness reaching 91.5%. The findings indicate that prescribing inaccuracies may be more closely related to the computational complexity of MulV formulations than to patient demographics, as these products account for a large proportion of dosing errors. While supplement utilisation appears to align with the clinical demands of respiratory conditions and the ‘dual burden’ of malnutrition, the observed higher risk of errors in certain subgroups, such as gastrointestinal patients, highlights the potential need for more risk-stratified approaches. To further support patient safety and reduce the risk of cumulative micronutrient overdose, the integration of clinical decision support tools, including electronic prescribing alerts and standardised weight-based dosing tables, may be beneficial.
