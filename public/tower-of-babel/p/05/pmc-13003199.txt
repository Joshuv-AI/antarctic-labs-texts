Validating Dental Histology for Perinatal Age Estimation Using Human Deciduous Teeth
Authors: Ani Martirosyan, Javier Irurita, Assumpció Malgosa, Judit Molera, Patrick Mahoney, Xavier Jordana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study tests the accuracy of dental histology for perinatal age estimation using deciduous teeth from modern human infants of known age. We evaluate whether enamel and dentin incremental structures, particularly the Neonatal Line (NNL), can be used to provide a reliable estimate of chronological age.Samples and MethodsSeventeen unerupted deciduous teeth from 14 full‐term infants (0–164 days old) were analyzed from the Granada identified skeletal collection in Spain. Thin sections were examined under transmitted light and confocal microscopy to identify the NNL, cross‐striations, and von Ebner's lines. Daily secretion rates (DSR) were measured across crown and root to calculate crown formation time (CFT) and root formation time (RFT), which were compared with the documented ages at death.ResultsMean enamel DSR was 3.38 μm/day, and mean root dentin DSR was 3.24 μm/day. Enamel DSRs varied between tooth types and crown regions. The NNL was identified in all individuals who survived birth and was absent in one stillbirth, confirming its reliability as an indicator of live birth. Prenatal CFTs fell within ranges reported for full‐term gestation, supporting the interpretation that all individuals were born at term. Histologically derived ages differed from recorded ages by an underestimation of 8 to an overestimation of 1 day, with a mean difference of 1 day.ConclusionsDeciduous dental histology provides an accurate estimate of chronological age for human juveniles in the year after birth. These findings suggest that perinatal age estimates derived from dental histology can be applied in bioarchaeological and forensic contexts.

Introduction

Biological rhythms, ranging from circadian to annual cycles, are widespread in living organisms and often leave structural imprints in hard tissues, such as tree rings, marine organism shells, and dental tissues (Scrutton 1978). In teeth, incremental features in enamel and dentin have long been recognized as key indicators of biological rhythms, offering insights into the development, chronology, and life history of extant and fossil hominins (Boyde 1963; Bromage and Dean 1985; Dean et al. 2001; Macchiarelli et al. 2006; Mahoney 2015; Mahoney et al. 2021; Nava, Coppa, et al. 2017; Nava et al. 2019, 2024; Peripoli et al. 2023). These incremental structures form through the regular deposition of dental enamel by ameloblasts, which results in microscopic features such as cross‐striations and Retzius lines (RLs) that reflect rhythmic variations in matrix secretion, composition, and growth dynamics (Boyde 1989; C. Dean 2000; Smith and Avishai 2005). Cross‐striations represent circadian rhythms corresponding to daily increments in enamel prism growth (Boyde 1963), and are matched by von Ebner lines in dentin (M. C. Dean 2012b; Smith 2008). Studies have shown that clock genes such as Bmal1, Clock, Per1, and Per2 oscillate in ameloblasts with a 24‐h periodicity (Lacruz et al. 2012; Zheng et al. 2011, 2014), regulating stage‐specific enamel proteins like amelogenin (Amelx) and kallikrein‐related peptidase 4 (Klk4) (Zheng et al. 2013). RLs reflect longer periodicities, typically 6–12 days in human permanent teeth, which are thought to be associated with about weekly biological patterns (Reid and Dean 2006; Smith et al. 2007). RLs are paralleled in dentin by and Andersen lines (M. C. Dean 2012b; Smith 2008).

Experimental evidence has confirmed the circadian timing of cross striations. Bromage (1991) demonstrated through fluorescent labeling in macaques that enamel cross‐striations correspond to daily increments of matrix deposition, providing direct support for circadian periodicity in enamel formation. Later, Smith (2006) applied the same technique to developing macaque teeth and found that both cross‐striations and the longer‐period RLs appeared at predictable intervals, further supporting their use in reconstructing aspects of enamel formation time. These findings were consistent with earlier observations of periodic structures in primate enamel (e.g., Risnes 1986; Schour and Poncher 1937) and underscored their potential for calculating crown formation time (CFT).

Accentuated lines (ALs) appear outside these rhythms and signal temporary interruptions in normal enamel formation due to systemic stress, thereby impacting ameloblast activity. Such disruptions can be recorded histologically as more prominent, irregular incremental features, which do not follow the regular rhythmic deposition of enamel and are thought to reflect episodes of systemic physiological stress such as illness or nutritional deficiency (FitzGerald and Saunders 2005; Guatelli‐Steinberg and Huffman 2012; Witzel et al. 2008).

The Neonatal Line (NNL) is a notable AL that appears in primary teeth and the mesiobuccal cusp of first permanent molars after birth (Antoine et al. 2009; Mahoney 2008; Nava, Bondioli, et al. 2017). It reflects biological stress at birth, during the transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life, including changes in nutrition and physiology (Janardhanan et al. 2011; Kurek et al. 2016). Characterized by changes in enamel prism size, direction, and mineralization, the NNL provides a critical chronological marker of birth and is especially valuable for distinguishing prenatal from postnatal growth (Birch and Dean 2009). Research indicates that the NNL becomes visible in individuals who survive at least 10–15 days postnatally, though it may form even earlier (Fitzgerald and Hillson 2009; Schwartz et al. 2010; Smith and Avishai 2005). The NNL allows researchers to estimate biological age in teeth that are still forming by counting cross‐striations from the line to the end of enamel formation (Witzel 2014). Therefore, unerupted primary teeth crowns with still‐forming enamel provide a unique material for determining how long an infant survives by quantifying enamel deposition after birth (Martirosyan et al. 2024).

Biological age reconstructed from dental histology is not identical to chronological age, but the two are closely correlated. Two studies have been undertaken on permanent teeth. Antoine et al. (2009) tested the reliability of prism cross‐striation counts in developing permanent teeth from known‐age human juveniles in the Spitalfields crypt (London). Their analysis of three permanent teeth from an individual in which a NNL was identified revealed a minimum underestimation of 14 days when compared with the known chronological ages. Smith et al. (2006) applied standard histological methods to identify the NNL, count incremental lines, and reconstruct the duration of crown and root formation in five pig‐tailed macaques ( Macaca nemestrina ) of known age at death. Their results corresponded with the known documented ages, with estimates showing an overestimation of at least 8 days to an underestimation of up to 10 days.

Our understanding of the accuracy of histologically derived age estimates for deciduous teeth from human infants is limited. Recent research by Ramirez Rozzi et al. (2025) highlighted potential applications for human infants. Using an estimated rate of enamel secretion, they reported that the age‐at‐death determined from central incisor crowns for two infants was within 2 weeks of the known chronological age for one infant and a few days for the other.

The aim in this study is to estimate the age‐at‐death for 14 human infants using histological analyses of deciduous enamel and roots. We focus on unerupted deciduous teeth from individuals with documented birth and death records, all of whom were full‐term at birth. We calculated daily secretion rates (DSRs), CFTs, and root formation times (RFTs) directly from the teeth of these 14 infants. Our histologically derived estimates were then compared with the known chronological ages of each infant to evaluate the precision of this methodological approach.

Materials and Methods

Samples

We analyzed 17 unerupted deciduous teeth from 14 individuals, aged between 0 (birth) and 164 days, obtained from the Granada identified skeletal collection of infant individuals housed at the Department of Legal Medicine, Toxicology, and Physical Anthropology at the University of Granada, Spain (Alemán et al. 2012). The collection originates from legally acquired, unclaimed individuals transferred from municipal cemeteries, and their study is conducted exclusively for scientific and educational purposes. All procedures comply with current Spanish legislation on biomedical research (Law 14/2007 on Biomedical Research, 3rd July) and follow the ethical principles outlined in the University of Granada's Code of Ethics (ACG178/2, approved by the Governing Council on 25 February 2022) for research involving human remains, ensuring respect for human dignity and the minimization of damage to the remains. Law 14/2007 is the primary legislation in Spain governing research on human biological samples that ensures the protection of personal privacy and confidential treatment of personal data in biomedical research. All individuals in this collection are fully anonymized for research purposes, and any personal information associated with them is strictly restricted, with access limited solely to the collection's curator.

The main inclusion criterion was the selection of still‐forming teeth. Because the histological procedure is destructive, sampling was limited to fractured specimens that were unsuitable for morphometric analysis but could provide a complete mid‐labiolingual section. The final sample comprised 12 central deciduous incisors, 3 lateral deciduous incisors, 1 first deciduous molar, and 1 s deciduous molar (Table 1). Their developmental stages ranged from three‐quarters of crown completion (Cr 3/4) to initial root formation with diverging edges (Ri) (AlQahtani et al. 2010; Table 1). Tooth identification and nomenclature followed the FDI World Dental Federation notation (ISO 2016).

Histological Sections

The thin sections were obtained using an approach based on previous methodologies (Martirosyan et al. 2024). Teeth were embedded in ExpoxiCure 2 mounting resin (Buehler) and sectioned labiolingually using a Minitom equipped with a diamond cut‐off wheel (Struers). The resulting sections were then attached to the histological slides using the same mounting resin (ExpoxiCure 2), and thin sections of 450–350 μm were obtained with the Minitom (Struers). To achieve an optimal thickness of 80–100 μm, 2000 μm P silicon carbide abrasive paper was used (Silva et al. 2011). The resulting section was dehydrated in ethanol (96%, and 100%), immersed in a histological clarifying agent (Histolemon, Carlo Erba Reagents S.A.S), and mounted with a coverslip using DPX mounting media (PanReac AppliChem ITW Reagents).

The histological sections were observed under a transmission petrographic optical microscope with a Leica DM2700 P dual polarization system at 5×, 10×, and 20× magnifications, using Micrometrics SE Premium Software. Additionally, a confocal Olympus FluoView FV1000 microscope equipped with a Hamamatsu ORCA‐Flash4.0 LT monochrome camera at 20× magnification was used for better visualization of the cross‐striations. Photocompositions of the images were created using MosaicJ (Schindelin et al. 2012).

Calculation of Daily Secretion Rate

The buccal side of the tooth was prioritized for analysis due to better preservation and clearer visualization of the NNL. When significant taphonomic alterations were observed, the lingual side was used instead. Enamel DSR was determined by measuring the distance between cross‐striations in enamel prisms across three regions of the crown obtained by dividing the length of the EDJ into three equal segments. Throughout this study, we refer to these regions as upper, middle, and inferior when describing the full sample, because not all teeth have fully formed crowns. When referring specifically to teeth with a complete crown, these same regions correspond to the cuspal, lateral, and cervical portions of the crown (Figure 1a). This approach follows established methods (Birch and Dean 2014; Mahoney 2012, 2015; Mahoney et al. 2016). The linear distance along the path of the enamel prism, spanning three to five cross‐striations, was measured and then divided by the number of cross‐striations within that span to calculate the average DSR (Figure 2). To standardize comparisons across regions, measurements focused on the mid‐enamel portion within each of the three crown regions (Mahoney et al. 2024). Between 15 and 20 measurements were made per region, resulting in a total of 45–60 DSR values per tooth. For each region, the mean DSR and its associated 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated (Martirosyan et al. 2024).

In teeth with crowns that are fully completed but have evidence of root growth, dentin DSRs are needed to calculate RFT (Mahoney 2019; Smith 2008). For those specimens, root dentin DSRs were obtained following the same protocol used for enamel, with measurements taken along clearly visible von Ebner's lines oriented parallel to the dentinal tubules (Figure 2). Between 15 and 20 measurements were collected per tooth, and the mean DSR, with its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was calculated for each specimen. For comparison, indirect dentin DSRs were estimated from enamel prism lengths (Mahoney 2019; see Table S1).

Calculating Formation Time

CFT was calculated from the time taken to form the EDJ (e.g., M. C. Dean 2012a). We traced consecutive 200 μm lines along enamel prisms in a zig‐zag path guided by long‐period RLs or accentuated markings (Figure 1a), starting at the dentin horn and continuing until the cervix and the end of crown formation (M. C. Dean 2012a). The number of days required to form each 200 μm segment was reconstructed using a local mean DSR, and its 95% confidence interval (95 CI) for the respective crown regions (upper, middle, inferior). The total CFT was then obtained by summing the durations of all segments. Additionally, we calculated the prenatal crown formation time (pCFT), which is the interval between the initiation of enamel formation until the NNL is reached.

The presence of the NNL was identified when at least 75% of its length was visible and/or it was the first and most marked AL, as seen in Figure 1 (Adserias‐Garriga and Visnapuu 2019; FitzGerald and Saunders 2005). In teeth lacking a recognizable NNL, the entire crown was traced to estimate total CFT, which in these cases corresponded to the pCFT (95% CI). For teeth with a visible NNL, postnatal survival age was estimated by tracing the NNL to its intersection with the EDJ. From this point, the process used to calculate CFT was repeated until the end of enamel growth, summing the time required to form each 200 μm segment to obtain the postnatal CFT (95% CI).

To calculate RFT in individuals with fully formed crowns, we followed the procedure applied by Dean and Cole (2013) and Mahoney (2019) for reconstructing root growth. Consecutive 200 μm lines were traced along the cement–dentin junction (CDJ) in a zig‐zag trajectory guided by ALs, starting at the cervical margin and continuing toward the apex (Figure 1a). For each segment, the number of days required to form 200 μm of dentin was estimated using the mean root dentin DSR. The cumulative RFT was reconstructed by summing the time intervals required to form all successive root segments, yielding a total duration from the onset of root dentin formation at the CDJ to the most apical preserved point.

All specimens except one formed less than 200 μm of root dentin. Therefore, only a single individual allowed the application of the full zig‐zag trajectory along the CDJ (Figure 1). In individuals with developing roots, the estimated value represents the duration of root formation up to the preserved apex, which, when added to the postnatal CFT, yields the total postnatal survival age.

Results

Enamel Daily Secretion Rate

A total of n = 765 DSR measurements were obtained across all specimens and crown regions. The overall mean DSR in this study was 3.38 μm/day (minimum of 1.58 μm/day to a maximum of 6.68 μm/day), indicating a broad distribution of DSR across the sample. Crown Formation Time

The pCFT of all samples fell within the documented ranges for full‐term birth (Figure 4). NNL was identified in all teeth except for one (tooth 71 from G‐371), supporting the classification of the individual as a full‐term stillbirth. In the remaining individuals, the histological analysis of the pCFT and the presence of the NNL aligned with known records of live birth (Figures 4 and 5).

For samples with crowns still in formation, postnatal age was estimated using the postnatal CFT, which closely matched the known chronological ages at death (Figure 5). G‐346 (tooth 81) and G‐363 (tooth 55) yielded a postnatal CFT of 8 days (7–8) and 23 days (22–24), compared to recorded survivals of 8 and 25 days, respectively. G‐512 (tooth 81) and G‐225 (tooth 71) produced postnatal CFT of 30 days (29–32) and 44 days (43–46), corresponding to a known age of 31 and 44 days. G‐393 (teeth 52 and 62) provided slightly lower values of 83 days (78–89) and 85 days (81–89) than the recorded survival of 91 days. G‐309, represented by two teeth, had a known age at death of 164 days, which aligns with the postnatal CFT of 164 days (154–176) from tooth 54, whose enamel formation was still in progress.

For samples with completed crowns, but developing roots, there was a close correlation between the histologically derived age and the documented ages at death (Figure 5). G‐399 (tooth 71), G‐362 (tooth 71), G‐405 (tooth 61), and G‐414 (tooth 61) yielded values of 60 days (56–63), 61 days (59–64), 65 days (62–68), and 77 days (73–82), respectively, consistent with their recorded survivals of 60, 62, 65, and 78 days. At older ages, G‐380 (tooth 61) provided an estimate of 117 days (109–125) compared to 116 days, and G‐215 (tooth 81) gave 126 days (114–139) compared to 129 days. G‐189 (teeth 51 and 61) yielded 135 days (126–146) and 136 days (128–144), both overlapping with the documented survival of 135 days. Finally, G‐309 (tooth 52) provided 164 days (157–172), encompassing the recorded survival of 164 days.

Discussion

In this study, histologically derived age estimates from deciduous enamel and roots differed from known chronological ages by an underestimation of 8 days to an overestimation of 1 day among 14 individuals. This level of accuracy surpasses that of traditional osteological methods, which are often limited by variability in growth rates and cannot reliably distinguish preterm births, stillbirths, or short‐term postnatal survival (Saunders and Barrans 1999). Factors such as poor maternal nutrition, placental insufficiency, or maternal stress can restrict intrauterine growth, resulting in infants who are small at birth but were carried to term (Lewis 2009; Mulder et al. 2002). Our results confirm that dental histology provides a precise alternative for perinatal age estimation, effectively addressing these limitations. This finding aligns with previous studies on small samples of human teeth, which also reported a strong correlation between histologically derived and documented chronological ages (Antoine et al. 2009; Ramirez Rozzi et al. 2025).

Daily Secretion Rates

The mean enamel DSR across all tooth types in our study was 3.38 μm/day (ranging from 1.58 to 6.68 μm/day). Our mean and range of values are consistent with previous research (e.g., Birch and Dean 2014; Dean et al. 2020; Mahoney 2012, 2015; Mahoney et al. 2016; McFarlane et al. 2021; Nava, Coppa, et al. 2017; Nava, Bondioli, et al. 2017; Peripoli et al. 2023). For dentin, the mean DSR across all tooth types was 3.24 μm/day (ranging from 1.28 to 5.65 μm/day), which also falls within the ranges reported in the literature (e.g., Mahoney 2019; Schour and Poncher 1937). Indirect dentin DSRs yield similar values with a mean of 3.27 μm/day (ranging from 2.34 to 4.69 μm/day; see Table S1).

Our dataset revealed substantial variation between tooth types and crown regions (e.g., Beynon et al. 1991). Overall, comparison of mean enamel DSR values and standard deviations showed that molars consistently displayed lower secretion rates than incisors (Table 2). In addition, post hoc comparisons showed that upper deciduous central incisors had higher DSR values than lower incisors across crown regions (Figure 3). Previous studies have reported inter‐ and intra‐tooth as well as inter‐population differences in secretion rates (e.g., Mahoney 2015; Nava, Bondioli, et al. 2017; Peripoli et al. 2023), and our results further demonstrate the variation in the rate at which ameloblast cells deposit enamel during the secretory stage of formation. These differences likely reflect the influence of multiple biological and developmental factors, as odontogenesis and enamel mineralization in utero can be interfered by genetic variation, intrauterine improper nutrition, maternal physiological and psychological stress, preterm birth, or delivery complications (Collignon et al. 2022).

In line with earlier studies showing variation by tooth type and crown region (e.g., Aris et al. 2020; Birch and Dean 2009; Mahoney 2012), our results confirm that DSR are influenced by sex and individual growth patterns. Therefore, standard average values, whether derived from the literature or from different teeth within the same dentition, are not appropriate for developmental reconstructions. Instead, DSR should be calculated directly from the specific tooth and crown region under study to ensure a robust and biologically meaningful reconstruction of enamel formation, which in this case is age at death.

Prenatal Formation Time

The NNL was observed in all the samples from individuals who survived birth (Figure 5). Three samples (G‐393, G‐399 and G‐414) had been previously analyzed using Synchrotron X‐ray Fluorescence, confirming birth through the presence of a Zn peak coinciding with the histological NNL that is associated with the high Zn content of colostrum and liver release at birth (Martirosyan et al. 2024, 2025). The only individual without the presence of the NNL is G‐371, who did not survive birth (Figure 5).

The pCFT for deciduous central incisors ranged from 129 to 185 days, and for deciduous lateral incisors from 130 to 154 days. These values overlap with published pCFT for deciduous incisor teeth, which span 78 to 124 days in modern human samples and fall within the broader ranges of 100 to 179 days documented for a Roman archaeological population (Mahoney 2012; Nava, Bondioli, et al. 2017). For the deciduous upper molars, the pCFT ranged from 119 to 135 days for the first molar and 72 to 78 days for the second molar. These values are consistent with reported prenatal molar enamel formation ranges of 87–167 days for first molar and 37–135 days for second molar in modern populations (Mahoney et al. 2025). The wide variability observed in deciduous central incisors may reflect individual differences in the timing of the onset of mineralization (Dean et al. 2020), a pattern that cannot be evaluated in deciduous lateral incisors and molars due to the limited sample size of these groups.

The individuals included in this study are considered to have been full‐term at birth, based on their recorded causes of death, which do not indicate prematurity (Table 1). This interpretation is further supported by the fact that all pCFTs coincide with the established range for a full‐term birth (Birch and Dean 2014; Mahoney 2012, 2015), suggesting that this calculation may help in validating the presence of the NNL and differentiating it from other prenatal or postnatal ALs.

Age‐At‐Death

Across all samples, the average range (95% CI) spanned 8 days for postnatal CFT estimates and 6 days for RFT estimates. In individuals with still‐forming enamel (G‐346, G‐363, G‐393, G‐512, G‐225, and G‐309), the calculated chronological age was underestimated by up to 2 days (Figure 5). This discrepancy may be related to the time required for NNL formation, as noted in previous studies, although our results suggest a shorter duration (Nava, Bondioli, et al. 2017; Witzel 2014).

In individuals with completed crowns, chronological age reconstructed using both postnatal CFT and RFT consistently matched the known ages at death (Figure 5). Among these samples (G‐189, G‐215, G‐362, G‐380, G‐399, G‐405, and G‐414), postnatal CFTs varied considerably. Deciduous central incisors ranged from 23 to 90 days, with a mean of 41 days, whereas lateral incisors ranged from 82 to 90 days, approaching the 107–119 days reported by Birch and Dean (2014).

Our results indicate that age estimates derived from deciduous enamel and dentin are accurate to within approximately a week of the documented chronological ages. Earlier work on permanent teeth reported substantially larger discrepancies, with reconstructed ages falling short of the known values by at least 2 weeks and often more (Antoine et al. 2009). Despite these potential deviations, tooth histology appears to be a reliable method for age determination, as conventional anthropological methods primarily estimate developmental age at death but are limited in their ability to address birth‐related factors such as preterm birth or stillbirth (e.g., Smith 2006; Smith et al. 2010).

The use of dental histology in age estimation has applications in both bioarchaeology and forensic anthropology (Aris 2024; Martirosyan et al. 2024). The identification of the NNL and the calculation of pCFT are essential for distinguishing between individuals born at term, perinatal deaths, and prenatal losses (Martirosyan et al. 2024; Nava, Coppa, et al. 2017; Peripoli et al. 2023). It can also provide insights into survival at birth, stillbirths, and miscarriages, especially in cases where remains are fragmented, in an advanced decomposition, deteriorated, or skeletonized, since teeth are better preserved (Martirosyan et al. 2025; Peripoli et al. 2023; Witzel 2014).

In bioarchaeological contexts, histology allows the reconstruction of infant mortality patterns in past populations using age categories comparable to those in demographic studies, providing insights into living conditions and demography (Martirosyan et al. 2024). In forensic cases, it has proven valuable in distinguishing live births from stillbirths, particularly in investigations of neonaticide, where determining whether an infant survived postnatally is critical (Janardhanan et al. 2011). Moreover, NNL width has been shown to be inversely proportional to labor duration, suggesting that prolonged labor may influence amelogenesis and alter the formation of this structure (Canturk et al. 2014; Hurnanen et al. 2017). Other studies have highlighted the interpretive potential of ALs, which have been associated with stress episodes in ancient populations (van Dalen Luna et al. 2025; Fitzgerald et al. 2006). More recently, histological studies of Phoenician and Neanderthal deciduous teeth have reinforced the importance of maternal health and nutrition in shaping infant survival and development, while also revealing that Neanderthals followed growth and weaning trajectories broadly comparable to those of modern humans (Mahoney et al. 2021; Nava et al. 2020; Peripoli et al. 2023).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the utility of deciduous enamel and root histology for estimating age‐at‐death in perinatal individuals. The consistent presence of the NNL in individuals who survived birth and its absence in the stillbirth case confirm its value as a clear marker distinguishing prenatal from postnatal life. Similarly, the obtained prenatal CFT values fall within the established ranges for full‐term births, validating the effectiveness of this method in differentiating full‐term births from prematurity or prenatal losses. The close correspondence between histologically derived and documented chronological ages, typically within a few days, validates the precision of this approach, confirming that dental histology remains the most precise method for age determination in perinatal individuals, surpassing the limitations of conventional anthropological techniques based on biological age estimation.

By integrating quantitative histological analysis with documented modern reference data, this study reinforces the potential of dental tissues as a biological archive of developmental timing. These findings have broad implications for both bioarchaeology and forensic anthropology, improving reconstructions of perinatal mortality patterns and enabling more accurate differentiation between stillbirth and short‐term postnatal survival in past and modern populations.
