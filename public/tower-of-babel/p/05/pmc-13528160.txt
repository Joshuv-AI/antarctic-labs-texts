Bridging preclinical development and clinical manufacturing: a translational GMP-Platform for lentiviral vector production in academic CAR T-Cell therapy
Authors: Maribel Lara-Chica, Blanca Arribas-Arribas, Raquel Muñoz-García, María Dolores de la Rosa-Garrido, Miguel A Montiel-Aguilera, María Bermejo-González, Esteban Márquez-Pérez, Paola Hernández-Díaz, Beatriz Guijarro-Albaladejo, Belén Sierro-Martínez, José Antonio Pérez-Simón, Gloria Carmona-Sánchez, Estefanía García-Guerrero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe manufacturing of lentiviral vectors (LV) under Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) remains a critical bottleneck limiting the clinical translation of academic CAR T-cell therapies. To address this challenge, we established and validated an integrated GMP facility (ViPro-IBiS-UPRC) for aseptic LV production within a public healthcare setting.MethodsA stepwise optimization strategy was implemented to bridge preclinical development and GMP manufacturing, including refinement of transfection conditions, vector harvest timing, and scale-up surface transition. GMP-compliant production processes and quality control (QC) frameworks were subsequently developed and validated for HEK293T Lenti-X master and working cell banks (MCB/WCB) and for LV manufacturing.ResultsCell banks demonstrated high viability (≥94%), robust expansion capacity, confirmed identity by DNA fingerprinting, and absence of microbial and viral contaminants, including adventitious and endogenous retroviruses. GMP-produced LV batches achieved functional titers ranging from 9.95 × 107 to 3.07 × 108 TU/mL and fulfilled all release criteria, including sterility, absence of mycoplasma, endotoxins, and replication-competent lentivirus. Residual host cell DNA and protein levels remained within international regulatory predefined acceptance thresholds. Stability studies demonstrated preservation of vector functionality up to 12 months under cryopreservation conditions. Following regulatory inspection, ViPro-IBiS-UPRC obtained GMP certification from the Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS) for LV production, supporting the manufacture of CARTemis-1, an anti-BCMA CAR T-cell product under clinical evaluation in relapsed multiple myeloma patients.ConclusionsCollectively, this work establishes a GMP-compliant academic platform for scalable LV manufacturing, enabling decentralized, cost-effective, and clinically compliant supply. This point-of-care manufacturing model strengthens the accessibility of academic CAR T-cell therapies within public healthcare systems.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08566-4.Keywords: Lentiviral vectors, Good manufacturing practices (GMP), Academic CAR T-cell therapy, Aseptic manufacturing, Point-of-care production

Introduction

Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy revolution has led to impressive results in patients treated with commercially available CAR-T cells. The availability of academic CAR-T cells in clinical trials in hospitals belonging to the Spanish public health system has increased in recent years.

A bottleneck in the production of the academic CAR-T cells is the generation of lentiviral vectors (LV) under Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP). Accordingly, the availability of LV manufactured according to GMP has been identified as a limiting factor in the production of CAR-T cell products. To overcome this limitation, our multidisciplinary team at Institute of Biomedicine of Seville (IBiS), University Hospital Virgen Del Rocío (HUVR) and Cell Production and Reprogramming Unit in Seville (UPRC), has established an in-house GMP-compliant LV manufacturing facility called ViPro-IBiS-UPRC, located at IBiS. LV are manufactured under aseptic conditions at our clean room, in order to ensure a good supply for both our approved clinical CAR T-cell therapy trials and the ones that may arise nationwide.

The transition from preclinical-scale production to GMP-compliant manufacturing represents a critical step toward clinical translation. In this regard, optimization of the preclinical-scale LV production process was performed prior to its transfer to ViPro-IBiS-UPRC. Parameters assessed included both biological variables (cell density, plasmid amount/ratio, LV harvest timing) and material considerations, such as selection of cell culture plasticware and transfection reagents. This approach minimized the need for redundant validation steps, thereby accelerating GMP implementation and facilitating technology transfer.

GMP LV manufacturing for CAR T-cell therapy encompasses three main stages: GMP generation of HEK293T Lenti-X cell banks (packaging cells), individual production of the plasmids (three helper and one CAR anti-target gene) necessary for HEK293T transfection and GMP production of LV. According to Part II of GMP, it has been interpreted that the plasmids for viral vectors are not required to be produced under GMP. The LV used to produce CARTemis-1 (LVC1) were manufactured using a BCMA-CAR plasmid as the anti-target CAR gene.

The present study shows how to achieve the transition from preclinical-scale production to GMP-compliant manufacturing and successfully perform the GMP validation of both the production processes and the quality controls (QC) for all the stages of LV manufacturing, according to Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS). Specifically, QC tests and their required analyses have been developed and proven (such as the analysis of residual DNA and protein) following AEMPS recommendations.

After evaluating the investigational medicinal product dossier (IMPD) and inspecting the facilities, AEMPS certified our platform ViPro-IBiS-UPRC to produce LV for CAR T-cell manufacturing. LVC1 was produced under GMP conditions as a starting material of a medicinal CAR-T product (CARTemis-1), which is currently being evaluated for biocompatibility and biofunctionality in relapsed multiple myeloma patients after allogeneic transplantation [1]. Altogether, our AEMPS certified platform ViPro-IBiS-UPRC is now available as a source of GMP manufactured LV for ATMPS-approved trials in Spain.

Methods

Preclinical LV development

HEK293T subclone (Lenti-X) cell line was selected as the packaging cell line for LV production. HEK293T is a cell line isolated from human female embryonic kidney (HEK), transformed with adenovirus type 5 DNA and expressing the SV40 large T antigen (SV40 LTA). This subclone is highly transfectable and enables high levels of viral protein expression. For preclinical LV development, HEK293T cells (Takara Bio, Kyoto, Japan) were thawed, expanded and seeded in 10 cm2 cell culture dishes or 75 cm2 flasks using DMEM (DMEM-Gmax, ThermoFisher Scientific, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA). Once the cells were attached, four plasmids (three helper plasmids (Rev, Gag/Pol and envelope of VSV-G) and the target gene plasmid) were transfected into them using either CalPhos (CalPhos Mammalian Transfection Kit, Takara Bio, Kyoto, Japan) or PEIPro (Polyplus, Illkirch, France), at ratios plasmid DNA:PEIPro 1 µg:1 µL and 1 µg:2 µL. Media was replaced 16-18 h after transfection. Lentiviral supernatants were collected at 24 h, 48 h or 72 h post-media exchange-post-transfection and clarified by centrifugation and filtration (0.45-mm filter). Then, they were concentrated by ultracentrifugation and the LV-containing pellets were resuspended in DPBS (DPBS, ThermoFisher Scientific, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA) and stored at 80 °C until use.

HEK293T banking

One vial of HEK293T cells (Takara Bio, Kyoto, Japan) was thawed and centrifuged (100–300 g, 5 minutes), counted and seeded in 25 cm2 flasks at 40,000 cells/cm2 with medium (DMEM-Gmax, ThermoFisher Scientific, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA), supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum of Australian origin for the reduction of the risk of bovine spongiform encephalopathy (FBS; ThermoFisher Scientific, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA). After expansion to reach a minimum of 170 × 106 cells (two passages), the MCB was generated by cryopreserving cells in 90% FBS and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 10 × 106 cells per mL. One cryovial from the MCB was thawed and cells were seeded at 60,000 cells/cm2. HEK293T were subcultured for at least three additional passages to obtain a WCB. The WCB manufacturing process was carried out three consecutive and independent times (3 batches) for validation according to GMP Part IV [2] in aseptic conditions. Manufacturing operations were performed under Grade A conditions within a Grade B background, according to EU GMP requirements.

Adventitious virus

In accordance with AEMPS recommendations and following ICHQ5A [3], a thorough analysis of potential adventitious viruses was performed to characterize the MCB of HEK293T. The HEK293T cell line was produced using porcine trypsin. Some porcine viruses such as porcine parvovirus, transmissible gastroenteritis virus and porcine encephalitis were analyzed by Exopol (Zaragoza, Spain) using reverse transcription and qPCR (RT-qPCR) or qPCR. Moreover, a viral screening of the MCB was carried out by co-culturing cell lysates for 14 days with three different cell lines (Vero, RD, MRC-5) that are highly sensitive to a wide range of porcine, bovine and human viruses that cause cytopathic effects in their presence [4]. Cytopathic effect tests were performed by the Clinical Laboratory at Hospital Universitario Virgen del las Nieves of Granada, Spain. In addition, endogenous retroviruses were analyzed by F-PERT, due to the RT activity is associated with retroviruses. This test was performed by ViruSure (Vienna, Austria) using fluorescence qPCR product-enhanced reverse transcriptase (F-PERT). Finally, specific viruses (HIV, HBV, HCV, EBV, CMV) were analyzed in the MCB and the WCB by RT-PCR and PCR at Laboratorio Echevarne (Barcelona, Spain).

Identification of HEK293T cells

For identification purposes a DNA fingerprinting was performed by short tandem repeats (STRs). Takara provided a reference of nine loci (D5S818, D13S317, D7S820, D16S539, VWA, TH01, TPOX, CSF1PO, D21S11) for its HEK293T Lenti-X line in addition to amelogenin, which defines sex. This profile was compared with MCB produced cells. The DNA fingerprinting was analyzed by Hospital Universitario Virgen del Rocio de Sevilla (HUVR).

Stability of the cryopreserved HEK293T cells

Cell cryopreservation was performed as previously described using FBS 90% and DMSO 10% at 10 × 106 cells/mL and stability was verified by adequate cell survival and viability over time. After cryopreservation, the frozen cells were thawed according to internal Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) to determine the % of viability and cell recovery. These studies were performed on three batches of WCB on three independent vials and three independent counts for each thawed vial.

Genetic stability of HEK293T cells

Genetic stability of the producer cells should be tested at or beyond the maximum number of cell doublings used for production. HEK293T were seeded and expanded (n = 3) for more than 12 passages obtaining greater than 26 population doubling levels (PDL) or accumulated cell doublings were reached, calculated using the formula [5]:

N0: Initial number of cells; N: Final number of cells; S: is the starting PDL

After reaching more than 26 HEK293T cell doublings accumulated, genetic stability was assessed using DNA fingerprinting [6] based on STRs using amelogenin (to determine the sex) and 23 additional STR loci (D3S1358, D1S1656, D2S441, D10S1248, D13S317, Penta E, D16S539, D18S51, D2S1338, CSF1PO, Penta D, TH01, vWA, D21S11, D7S820, D5S818, TPOX, D8S1179, D12S391, D19S433, SE33, D22S1045, FGA). The DNA fingerprints of the 26 accumulated HEK293T cell doublings were compared with the STR profile of the MCB cells, which had fewer than eight accumulated cell doublings and were identified as the HEK293T cell line (with 16 loci) and 7 additional loci that also matched the MCB profile, for a total of 23 loci. The DNA fingerprinting was analyzed by Hospital Universitario Virgen del Rocio de Sevilla (HUVR).

Plasmids

To ensure suitability for use in GMP lentiviral vector production, plasmids were released following predefined analytical quality controls performed under Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) established according to GMP principles. Specifically, analytical methods requiring validation, including sterility and endotoxin testing, were performed in accordance with the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur. 2.6.1 and 2.6.14 and 5.1.10 respectively). Plasmid concentration was determined by absorbance at 260 nm using a microvolume spectrophotometer (NanoDrop), following Ph. Eur. recommendations. Purity was assessed through spectrophotometric absorbance ratios (A260/A230 > 1.8 in all cases). Plasmid identity was confirmed by restriction enzyme analysis according to Ph. Eur. specifications.

Lentiviral vector manufacturing

Three WCB vials were thawed and cells were expanded and seeded in 20 75 cm2 flasks for each LV production. round. Each batch consisted of two independent production rounds, resulting in a total of 40 T-75 flasks per validated batch (3000 cm2). Once the cells were attached, the plasmids (three helper plasmids (Rev, Gag/Pol and envelope of VSV-G) and the target gene plasmid BCMA-CAR) were transfected into them using PEIPro (Polyplus, Illkirch, France) at ratio Plasmid DNA:PEIPro 1 µg:1 µL. 72 h post transfection (48 h after washing step), the supernatant with the LV released by the cells is collected, centrifuged at 2450 g for 15 minutes, clarified by 0.45 µM filtration (polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane) and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 57,850 g for 2,5 hours using polypropylene tubes. The LV-containing pellets were resuspended in DPBS (DPBS, ThermoFisher Scientific, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA) and stored at 80 °C until use for formulation and cryopreservation. The LVC1 manufacturing process was carried out three consecutive and independent times (3 batches) for validation according to GMP Part II [7] and IV [2] under aseptic conditions. Manufacturing operations were performed under Grade A conditions within a Grade B background, according to EU GMP requirements.

Functional titer

Increasing volumes of LV product are added to Jurkat cells (human immortalized T lymphocyte cell line) seeded in 48-well plates: 0 µL (untransduced control); 0.05 µL; 0.1 µL; 0.25 µL; 0.5 µL; 1 µL; 2 µL. Polybrene (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA) was used to enhance the cells´ transduction. After incubating the cells four hours at 37 °C and 5% CO2, medium was added to reach a final volume of 1 mL. Following incubation of the cells for 48 hours at 37 °C and 5% CO2, their transduction levels were checked by flow cytometry (FACS) after labelling them with CD3 (Pacific Blue CD3, BD, NJ, USA) and EGFR (APC EGFR, BioLegend, CA, USA) monoclonal antibodies for 20 minutes at 4 °C, centrifuging them at 308 g for 5–7 minutes and washing them with PBS. The viability of the cells was analyzed by 7-AAD labelling. The transduction levels were calculated as transduction units (TU) per millilitre (TU/mL).

* To avoid overestimation, the value used was the average of the flow cytometry results obtained from transduced Jurkat cells with transduction levels above and below 50%.

Microbiological assays

Sterility testing of cells (MCB or WCB) was performed by inoculation them in aerobic and anaerobic media (Biomerieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) that were incubated in a BACT/ALERT®3D automated microbial detection system (Biomerieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) and validated according to the 2.6.27 monograph of the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur) [8].

Sterility for LV was performed by inoculating the viral vector in trypticase soy broth (TSB) and thioglycolate medium (Biomerieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), then incubating the media for 14 days and validating the method according to the 2.6.1 monograph of the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur) [9]. Mycoplasma tests were performed by PCR using the Venor® Gem Mycoplasma PCR Detection kit (Minerva Biolabs, Jena, Germany) according to the 2.6.7 Ph. Eur. [10].

Endotoxin tests were performed and validated using the Endosafe PTS test system (Charles River, Charleston, SC), according to the chromogenic kinetic method of Ph. Eur. [11] and following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Residual DNA

Residual DNA from both HEK293T and plasmids, was quantified using a Qubit® fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). More specifically, residual quantification of specific genes of HEK293T and plasmid DNA was additionally performed using a digital PCR at HUVR (Applied Biosystem, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA, QuantStudio™ Absolute Q™ Digital PCR System) with a specific Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA,). To avoid interference, the samples were purified by DNA extraction and according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Positive controls with viral vectors were used to detect potential interferences, being the negative control the water used to extract the DNA and dilute the samples (matrix of the samples). To detect residual Ad E1A/E1B and the SV40 LTA genes of HEK293T, primers with FAM fluorescence were used for the E1A gene and CY5 for the SV40 LTA (AcroBiosystem, Beijing, China). To detect the residual ampicillin resistance gene of the plasmids, custom primers with FAM fluorescence (Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) were designed and used. The analysis´ result was divided by the number of times the sample was concentrated after DNA extraction for obtaining the quantification in the original sample.

Residual of host cell protein (HCP)

Residual protein of HEK293T was analyzed using an Elisa kit (ENZ-KIT162-0001, Enzo Biochem, NY, USA), according to the manufacturer´s protocol adapted to the internal SOP. Then, software from ATT Bioquest® (ATT Bioquest®, CA, USA [12]) was used to convert the optical density into concentration units.

Replicative competent lentiviral (RCL)

The LVC1 must not be able to replicate. The presence of RCL was determined by infecting a susceptible cell line (i.e. Jurkat cells) with LV and performing serial passaging of successive cell supernatant to achieve the RCL amplification. The RCL amplification was measured by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), by detecting VSV-G-specific nucleic acids (i.e., gene of the vector envelope protein) [13]. The DNA purification prior qPCR was performed according to the instructions provided by the kit manufacturer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Real-time PCR was performed on the purified sample according to the reagent manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA,). The RNAse P gene was used as a housekeeping gene (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA), and a custom reagent was used to analyzed the number of copies of the VSV-G gene (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Copies were analyzed with the software Copy Caller v2.1 (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± S.D. Significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or with Student’s t-test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA).

Results

Rational optimization of lentiviral vector production parameters to enable GMP translation

Building on the experience of our research team in lentiviral vector production for CAR T-cells in vitro in previous studies [14–16], the preclinical-scale manufacturing process was systematically optimized by evaluating key biological parameters as well as materials and reagent-related variables.

To optimize transfection conditions, LV production using CalPhos and PEI was compared. Functional titers were normalized to surface area (TU/cm2) to enable comparison. PEI-mediated transfection resulted in higher functional titers (3,69 × 105 TU/cm2) compared to CalPhos (9,84 × 104 TU/cm2), p value = 0.0026 (Fig. 1A). Notably, upon scaling up production surface area, PEI maintained consistent performance, with comparable titers at 500 cm2 and 1000 cm2 (3.67 × 105 vs 3.70 × 105 TU/cm2), whereas CalPhos showed a decline in yield at larger scale (1.13 × 105 vs 8.39 × 104 TU/cm2), p values < 0.05 (Fig. 1B). Given the larger culture surface area provided by flasks, a higher total LV yield was expected (total TU). We therefore assessed whether the transition from dish- to flask-based production would preserve vector production efficiency. Notably, efficiency was not only maintained but improved, particularly under PEI-mediated transfection, supporting the scalability of the process (5.21 × 107 dish total TU vs 2 × 108 flasks total TU) p value < 0.05 (Fig. 1C). Thus, flask-based culture systems were selected as the standard format for manual LV production. In addition, the transition from dish- to flask-based culture was motivated not only by productivity, but also by safety and process handling considerations, as flasks can be securely closed, are easier to manipulate during manufacturing, and offer increased culture surface area. In addition to enhancing vector production efficiency and supporting scalability, PEI minimized salt precipitation during transfection-mix preparation compared with CalPhos. Next, optimization of the DNA:PEI ratio demonstrated that a 1:1 ratio (1.61 × 108 TU/mL) outperformed a 1:2 ratio (9.25 × 107 TU/mL) (Fig. 1D). Based on these results, PEI at a DNA:PEI ratio of 1:1 was selected for subsequent LV production. Finally, to optimize harvest timing, lentiviral vectors were collected at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-media exchange following transfection, with peak functional titers observed at 48 hours (1.31 × 106 TU/mL) compared to 24 hours (5.25 × 105 TU/mL) (p value = 0.0016) and 72 hours (1.20 × 105 TU/mL) (Fig. 1E). Accordingly, 48 hours post-media exchange following transfection was selected as the optimal harvest time for downstream GMP manufacturing.

Collectively, these process refinements provide a direct bridge between preclinical-scale development and GMP implementation, minimizing the need for process re-optimization and enabling streamlined validation of manufacturing procedures and associated quality controls for both packaging cell banks (MCB and WCB) and lentiviral vectors (LVC1). These findings support the robustness and scalability of the process for subsequent clinical-grade LV manufacturing.

Establishment and comprehensive qualification of GMP-grade HEK293T Lenti-X cell banks

First, a GMP-compliant manufacturing protocol was designed for HEK293T Lenti-X cells expansion and cryopreservation, as well as for the subsequent production of the lentiviral vector for producing CARTemis-1 [14] (LVC1) (Fig. 2). Cell culture and cryopreservation were performed in a cleanroom under aseptic conditions, whereby particles and microorganisms were monitored according to GMP Part IV [2]. HEK293T Lenti-X cells were expanded with DMEM-Gmax medium supplemented with 10% FBS and cryopreserved after two passages to create a MCB with ≥170 × 106 cells (the total number of live cells obtained was 352 × 106 (Table 1). Cells at this initial stage showed a normal fibroblastic morphology (Fig. 3A), with exponential growth (Fig. 3B) and cell viability ≥ 98% (Table 1). A unique MCB was generated for GMP process validation in the present study, representing a homogeneous, well-characterized and long-term starting material, and constituting an appropriate strategy for manufacturing applications.

For WCB generation, a MCB cryovial was thawed and cells were subcultured and further expanded for at least three passages; then cryopreserved to create a WCB (Fig. 2). The WCB manufacturing process was carried out three consecutive and independent times (three batches) in the cleanroom to validate the GMP process. Every WCB batch was composed of at least 17 cryotubes totaling ≥ 50 cryotubes for WCB. The three WCB batches showed exponential cell growth (Fig. 3B), and cell viability ≥ 94% (Table 1), with a total number of live cells per batch of 170 × 106, 267 × 106 and 310 × 106 for WCB 1, WCB 2 and WCB 3, respectively (Table 1).

A GMP-compliant documentation system and associated quality control framework were established to support validation of the cell banking process. Cell identity of the MCB was confirmed by STR DNA fingerprinting, analyzing a panel of genetic loci including amelogenin for sex determination. The STR profile obtained fully matched (100%) the reference profile provided by the commercial source (Takara), confirming the identity of the cells as female. Additionally, comparison with an extended panel of reference loci from ATCC® showed a 95% concordance, further supporting correct cell line authentication for GMP-compliant manufacturing (Table 1). All WCB batches produced maintained the identity based on DNA fingerprinting (Table 1). According to AEMPS recommendations, genetic stability of the producer cells must be analyzed and it was tested at or beyond the maximum number of cell doublings used for production according to Ph. Eur. 5.34. [17] In the three WCB batches, a large number of passages were performed in order to study the accumulated cell doublings (Fig. 3C). lain Doherty et al. [18] and Stacy et al. [19, 20] demonstrate the suitability of DNA fingerprinting for mutation detection as an even more powerful tool than cytogenetics. Furthermore, this test is also recommended for this purpose by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) of USA in one of its guidance documents [6]. Therefore, a DNA fingerprinting test was performed on HEK293T cells with more than 26 cell doublings accumulated and compared with the one used to identify the MCB cells (population cell doublings < 8), obtaining a 100% match, showing that no genetic modification was detected (Fig. 3D).

The commercial HEK293T Lenti-X cells were produced with porcine trypsin and therefore several porcine viruses may potentially contaminate the MCB. According to the section “Detection of porcine viral contaminants” in the United States Pharmacopoeia (USP) monograph < 1237> “Virology test methods” [4], some porcine viruses such as porcine parvovirus, transmissible gastroenteritis virus and porcine encephalitis may be present in raw materials of porcine origin, including trypsin. Thus, the MCB cells were analyzed for porcine viruses by RT-qPCR or qPCR at the Exopol, the reference laboratory in Spain for the diagnosis of animal diseases. Moreover, other porcine, bovine and human viruses can be detected by cytopathic effect in Vero lines [4]. For this reason, the cytopathic effect of MCB was analyzed in Vero line, in addition to the RD and MRC-5 lines, as a screening for human viruses. On the other hand, the AEMPS also recommended testing for endogenous retroviruses in MCB by detection of retroviral reverse transcriptase. This test was performed by ViruSure using fluorescence qPCR product-enhanced reverse transcriptase (F-PERT). Finally, specific viruses in the MCB and WCB, were also analyzed by RT-qPCR and qPCR: HIV, HBV, HCV, EBV, CMV (Table 1). All these tests were carried out in accordance with ICHQ5A [21] and AEMPS recommendations. No adventitious viruses (HIV, HBV, HCV, EBV and CMV) were observed in the MCB and in the three WCB produced (Table 1).

The stability of the cryopreserved WCB vials were tested 12 months after cryopreservation, with results showing 93.60% viability and 56.67% cell recovery after thawing. However, ongoing stability studies are being carried out to extend the shelf life of the cell banks (Fig. 3E). Mycoplasma, sterility and endotoxins tests were also performed for both MCB and WCBs as quality control tests for intermediate products, and results met the acceptance criteria (Table 1).

Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful establishment of a robust and GMP-compliant HEK293T Lenti-X cell banking platform, fulfilling all critical quality requirements for advanced therapy medicinal product (ATMP) manufacturing. The generated MCB and WCB exhibited consistent identity, genetic stability, high viability, and absence of microbial and viral contaminants, in accordance with AEMPS and international regulatory guidelines. Furthermore, the reproducibility of the banking process across independent WCB batches supports the reliability and scalability of the system. Together, these findings validate the suitability of the HEK293T Lenti-X cell banks as a qualified and secure starting material for GMP lentiviral vector production, providing a solid foundation for subsequent clinical-grade manufacturing of CAR T-cell therapies.

Plasmids were manufactured in-house and released for use according to predefined specifications for identity, concentration, purity, sterility and endotoxin levels (Supplementary Table S1). Functional stability of GMP-produced LVC1 lentiviral vectors

To support clinical application and define product shelf-life, the functional stability of GMP-produced lentiviral vectors (LVC1) was systematically evaluated under both long-term cryopreservation and post-thaw conditions.

The stability of cryopreserved LVC1 was assessed by measuring functional titers at defined time points following storage (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months). No significant decline in vector potency was observed, with titers of 6.25 × 108 TU/mL at 6 months and 6.24 × 108 TU/mL at 12 months, comparable to baseline values (6.23 × 108 TU/mL). Based on these results, a shelf-life of at least 12 months under cryogenic conditions was established. Ongoing stability studies are being conducted to further extend the defined shelf-life (Fig. 4C).

Post-thaw stability was also evaluated by monitoring functional titers at 1, 3, 5, and 48 hours at room temperature (20–25°C). LVC1 retained functional activity for up to 5 hours, with titers remaining within ±25% of baseline. In contrast, a reduction exceeding 25% was observed at 48 hours, indicating a limited stability window under non-cryogenic conditions (Fig. 4D).

In summary, cryopreserved LVC1 batches maintained stable functional titers over a 12-month period, with no significant decline compared to initial values, demonstrating robustness under standard storage conditions. Upon thawing, vector functionality remained preserved for up to 5 hours at room temperature (20–25°C), with titers remaining within ±25% of baseline. These findings support a defined shelf-life of at least 12 months under cryogenic storage and establish a clinically relevant post-thaw usability window, ensuring operational flexibility for CAR T-cell manufacturing workflows.

Discussion

In the present study, we report the successful establishment and validation of a GMP-compliant platform for the production of lentiviral vectors intended for academic CAR T-cell therapy. This platform integrates the generation of qualified HEK293T Lenti-X master and working cell banks, together with a fully defined manufacturing process and associated quality control framework, enabling the production of clinical-grade lentiviral vectors for CARTemis-1.

LVC1 vectors were produced under aseptic conditions and met all predefined release criteria required for their use as starting material in CARTemis-1 manufacturing for the treatment of relapsed multiple myeloma patients after allogeneic transplantation. The manufacturing and control processes were performed in compliance with European Pharmacopoeia standards, ICH Q5A guidelines, GMP Part II, and AEMPS recommendations, ensuring alignment with current regulatory requirements for advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs). A key strength of our platform lies in the implementation and validation of a comprehensive quality control strategy. Notably, several analytical approaches were developed and applied for the first time within our unit following AEMPS guidance. Among these, DNA fingerprinting (STR) was used to confirm HEK293T cell identity, demonstrating 100% concordance with the reference profile and stability across extended culture conditions (>26 population doublings). This approach provides a robust method for ensuring genetic identity and stability of packaging cells, which is critical for consistent vector production. In addition, extensive viral safety testing was performed, including the detection of adventitious agents by cytopathic effect assays and molecular assays (PCR/RT-PCR) for porcine, endogenous, and human viruses. These analyses, together with sterility and mycoplasma testing, confirmed the absence of contamination in both MCB and WCB batches, fulfilling essential safety requirements for ATMP manufacturing. Furthermore, stability studies demonstrated high post-thaw viability (>90%) of cryopreserved cell banks after 12 months, consistent with previously reported data from other GMP lentiviral production facilities [26]. In order to ensure long-term manufacturing sustainability, future MCB and WCB will be generated from larger starting cell numbers (500–1000 × 106 cells). Increasing the initial cell input is expected to support higher production yields and improved batch-to-batch consistency.

For LV production, comprehensive quality control testing is required to ensure their suitability as starting material for CAR T-cell manufacturing. This includes determination of functional titer prior to formulation, as well as assessment of sterility, mycoplasma, HCP residues, and residual DNA derived from both HEK293T cells and plasmids. Endotoxin testing is typically performed for informational purposes at this stage, given the subsequent downstream processing. Although RCL testing can be performed at the level of the final CAR T-cell product, its implementation at the LV stage is highly recommended to streamline release timelines and enhance overall process efficiency. In this study, all LVC1 batches met the predefined quality criteria, with no detectable bacterial or fungal contamination, absence of mycoplasma, endotoxin levels within acceptable limits, and no evidence of RCL.

Additional characterization parameters, including physical particle quantification, infectious-to-particle ratio, vector genome quantification, transgene integrity, and vector copy number assessment, may provide complementary information to further define LV quality. Although these analyses were not part of the predefined GMP release criteria for the validated LVC1 batches, exploratory physical characterization was performed on two validated batches using the Leprechaun nanoparticle-based platform. Full LV particles, defined as capsid-positive/RNA-positive particles, were quantified at 1.47 × 108 particles/mL for LVC1-2 and 5.76 × 107 particles/mL for LVC1-3 (Supplementary Table S2), resulting in infectious-to-particle ratios of 0.215 and 0.106, respectively (Supplementary Table S3). These preliminary findings suggest that a measurable fraction of structurally complete LV particles retains functional transducing activity, while also highlighting batch-to-batch variability in the relationship between physical particle content and functional potency. In the current manufacturing strategy, transgene functionality and VCN are assessed downstream in the final CAR T-cell product. The first CARTemis-1 products manufactured using the validated LVC1 batches showed CAR surface expression levels of 34% on average, and a mean VCN of approximately 3 copies per cell. These data support efficient gene transfer and functional CAR expression, with VCN values compatible with the safety and quality requirements established for the final CAR T-cell product. Nevertheless, dedicated LV-level assays for transgene integrity and additional molecular characterization will be considered in future process development studies.

Beyond vector potency and particle characterization, assessment of process-related impurities is also essential to define the quality and safety profile of GMP-produced LV batches. In this regard, to our knowledge, this study provides one of the first reports in a Spanish academic setting describing the development and validation of assays for the evaluation of residual impurities in GMP-produced lentiviral vectors. Our results showed the presence of ranges between 3 to 12.5 µg/mL of proteins from HEK293T and total DNA between 1.4 to 7.9 ng/µL. There is not enough evidence reported regarding detection limits for residual DNA and protein from packaging cells in LV, therefore, they were set in accordance with the WHO and the FDA references. Current bioprocess technologies cannot generate completely protein or DNA free materials when working with living cells, and improvements in analytical sensitivity will reveal trace in products that were previously considered “undetectable”. Although AEMPS requires both DNA and HCP testing in ATMPs, it does not define an official acceptance limit, instead, the recommendation is that manufacturers establish scientifically justified specifications. Only few publications report DNA or HCP concentrations in lentiviral vectors or CAR T products, as it is the case of the study by Soldi and colleagues [27], which describes mean HCP levels around 1 µg/ml. These values are comparable to ours, though they used a different commercial ELISA kit (Cygnus Technologies, Item #F650S), whose distinct antibody epitope coverage limits direct comparability. Reported residual DNA levels in lentiviral vector preparations range from 4.7 µg/mL [27] to 7.77 µg/mL [28], values that are lower than those observed in our study. Notably, these studies incorporate additional purification steps within automated or semi-automated manufacturing workflows, which are expected to enhance the removal of residual nucleic acids and may account for the differences observed. In contrast, our process is based on a manual, non-automated production platform, which may retain higher levels of residual DNA. Importantly, despite these differences, our residual DNA levels remain well below the safety threshold established by WHO and FDA guidelines (<10 ng per patient dose), supporting the suitability of the product for clinical application. In addition, our analytical strategy included the targeted quantification of specific genetic elements associated with potential safety risks, such as SV40LTA and E1A/E1B sequences derived from the HEK293T Lenti-X genome. These sequences, if present at significant levels, could raise safety concerns in the final product; however, only minimal concentrations were detected. Furthermore, plasmid-derived DNA was comprehensively assessed through detection of the ampicillin resistance gene, which is common to all plasmids used in lentiviral vector production. This approach provides a broader and more stringent evaluation compared to previous studies, which typically focus on a single sequence such as the VSV-G gene from one helper plasmid [27]. In this regard, is it important to keep increasing awareness of this matter and the standardization of techniques, the unification of measurement units for the results, and the transparency of the results become particularly relevant, with the aim of establishing accurate detection limits in the future that can redefine the criteria.

Functional titers of frozen LV batches ranged between 9.95 × 107 and 3.07 × 108 TU/mL, which fall within the broad range reported by other groups using adherent culture systems [27–34]. However, direct comparison across studies remains challenging due to significant differences in upstream and downstream processing parameters. In particular, variations in harvested volumes and concentration factors can substantially influence reported titers. In our process, lentiviral supernatants are concentrated approximately 300-fold following harvest, whereas reported concentration factors in the literature range from ~20-fold to >3000-fold. Therefore, although final titers appear comparable, the underlying production efficiencies and process yields are highly heterogeneous across platforms. In this context, an approach to increase LV functional titers and to enhance process consistency is the implementation of bioreactor-based expansion systems. Although scale-up represents a critical step in lentiviral vector manufacturing, the establishment of a robust manual, non-automated production process provides a solid foundation that facilitates subsequent scalability. In this context, scaling becomes more readily achievable, as key validation elements—particularly analytical method validations performed on comparable matrices—can be leveraged without the need for full revalidation. The implementation of bioreactor-based systems is widely recognized as a strategic approach for the automation of lentiviral vector production [35–38]. Such systems have the potential to enhance production efficiency, improve process consistency, and reduce manufacturing costs and timelines. These advantages are particularly relevant in the context of early-phase clinical trials, where increasing demand for GMP-grade lentiviral vectors necessitates scalable and economically sustainable manufacturing solutions. Currently, our platform at ViPro-IBiS-UPRC is undergoing validation of an automated GMP-compliant lentiviral vector manufacturing process based on bioreactor systems. This advancement is expected to complement the existing non-automated GMP production already certified by AEMPS, enabling the implementation of both manual and automated manufacturing strategies within the same facility. In this context, the integration of automated platforms is anticipated to enhance process scalability, consistency, and efficiency, ultimately enabling flexible and scalable manufacturing strategies to meet increasing clinical demand.

Importantly, the transferability of this platform should be interpreted in two complementary ways. First, the validated workflow described here provides a technical and regulatory framework that may serve as a reference for academic centers with established GMP infrastructure seeking to implement or strengthen LV manufacturing capabilities. This includes qualified cell banking, standardized LV production procedures, validated quality controls, aseptic process qualification, and regulatory interaction with the competent authority. Second, transferability does not necessarily require direct replication of the entire LV manufacturing process in every academic institution. Given that access to GMP-grade LV remains a major bottleneck for academic CAR T-cell development, ViPro-IBiS-UPRC may also function as a centralized academic LV manufacturing platform, providing qualified LV batches to support CAR T-cell production programs in centers without in-house viral vector manufacturing capacity. In parallel, ongoing process optimization and scale-up toward automated bioreactor-based GMP manufacturing are expected to further increase production capacity, consistency, and operational flexibility once fully optimized, validated, and approved by the relevant regulatory authorities. This centralized academic supply model may help reduce dependence on external commercial vector suppliers and support broader implementation of academic CAR T-cell therapies within public healthcare systems.

Non-viral gene transfer approaches are actively being investigated for CAR T-cell manufacturing, including plasmid delivery by electroporation [39–41](microfluidic mechanoporation or lipid nanoparticles [42], genome editing approaches such as CRISPR [43], and transposon/transposase platforms such as Sleeping Beauty or PiggyBac [44]. These strategies are attractive because they may reduce manufacturing costs and lessen the need for specialized viral vector production facilities, potentially broadening access to CAR-based therapies. In addition, some of these platforms may mitigate concerns classically associated with viral vectors, such as vector-related insertional mutagenesis. However, despite these advantages, electroporation-based approaches still present important limitations for routine clinical manufacturing. Electroporation frequently imposes substantial cellular stress, often resulting in reduced viability, increased apoptosis, and impaired proliferative capacity after gene transfer [40], which may negatively affect final cell yield and product fitness. Moreover, transfection performance is often highly dependent on cell activation status, nucleic acid format and size, and donor-specific cell properties, thereby increasing process variability and complicating standardization at GMP scale. When electroporation is used for mRNA delivery, CAR expression is transient, which may be advantageous for some applications but generally limits in vivo persistence and may require repeated administrations to maintain antitumor activity [40]. Conversely, when combined with integrating systems such as transposons, electroporation can provide more durable expression, but at the cost of additional challenges related to control of copy number, genomic integration profiling, and product characterization. Likewise, CRISPR-based strategies add further analytical and regulatory burdens, including the need to assess editing efficiency, off-target activity, chromosomal rearrangements, and long-term genomic stability. Therefore, although non-viral platforms are highly promising and may become increasingly relevant as the field matures, viral transduction currently remains the most robust and clinically validated approach for consistent large-scale manufacturing of CAR T-cell products.

In addition, in vivo CAR T cell therapy production has recently emerged [45–47], in which, unlike the classic ex vivo production method, the lentiviral vector is introduced directly into the patients´ body for in vivo transduction of its T lymphocytes. This has sparked emerging interest in LV production for this ex vivo CAR T cell production. In this case, LV release QCs must be even more restrictive to ensure the safety and specificity of the use of the lentiviral vectors in patients. To date, there are several clinical trials ongoing using this type of CAR-T therapy production (NCT06691685, NCT07349849, NCT07239323, NCT06097832, NCT07398963). Some of them have shown in congresses success in their efficacy on the first patients recruited, however the available data from monitoring of treated cases is still very short-term (only several months of follow-up). Therefore, additional studies and longer patient follow-up are required to verify the safety and efficacy of this type of production.

Conclusions

In summary, we present, to our knowledge, a comprehensive and GMP-compliant framework for lentiviral vector manufacturing and quality control implementation within an academic setting in Spain, aligned with AEMPS and international regulatory requirements. This work provides a practical and validated reference for the development of high-quality lentiviral vectors, supporting their use as starting material for CAR T-cell therapies.

The integration of GMP lentiviral vector production at ViPro-IBiS-UPRC with CAR T-cell manufacturing at UPRC-CTTC defines a robust end-to-end academic platform within the Spanish public healthcare system. Beyond supporting local CAR T-cell clinical programs, this model may also provide a centralized academic source of qualified GMP-grade LV batches for centers without in-house viral vector manufacturing capacity. This approach may help reduce dependence on external commercial vector suppliers, address a key manufacturing bottleneck in academic CAR T-cell development, and support broader clinical implementation while maintaining product quality and regulatory compliance. Moreover, its scalable potential allows to progressively expand manufacturing capacity and facilitate implementation across academic GMP settings while maintaining product quality and regulatory compliance. Ultimately, this approach positions academic GMP platforms as a critical driver for expanding equitable access to advanced therapies and accelerating their translation into routine clinical practice worldwide.
