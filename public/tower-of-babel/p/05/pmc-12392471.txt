Functional characterization of CsSTK in floral organ development and its positive regulation by CsBPC5 in tea plants
Authors: Dandan Pang, Chunlin Chen, Yiping Tian, Huibing Jiang, Shaochun Deng, Yue Liu, Linbo Chen, Yufei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs an important economic crop, the development of floral organs in tea plants plays an essential role in tea yield, quality, and varietal improvement. Previous studies have shown significant downregulation of the CsSTK gene in the pistil-deficient mutant ‘Yuncha Qirui’ (CRQS) during flower development, suggesting its involvement in floral organ and pistil development.ResultsThis study investigated CsSTK function and regulatory mechanisms, concurrently conducting a systematic identification of the BPC transcription factor family members in tea plants. The results showed that CsSTK is specifically expressed in tea plant flowers (particularly in pistils) and fruit tissues. Notably, its expression level in CRQS flower buds was less than 1% of that in normal tea plants, whereas it was significantly upregulated in the sterile-flower mutant ‘Yuncha Yirui’ (ZDH), which exhibits abnormal fused carpels. Heterologous overexpression of CsSTK was found to promote early flowering in Arabidopsis, indicating its involvement in flowering time regulation. Additionally, genome-wide analysis identified 12 BPC family members in tea plants, which were divided into three subclasses (Class Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅲ). Except for CsBPC6.4, all other CsBPCs showed higher expression levels in normal flower buds compared to fully bloomed flowers, consistent with the CsSTK expression pattern. Further studies demonstrated that CsBPC5 positively regulates CsSTK expression.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the key regulatory role of CsSTK in tea plant floral organ development and explores the molecular mechanism underlying the CsBPC5-CsSTK regulatory module. These findings provide important theoretical insights into the molecular basis of floral organ development in tea plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07266-3.

Introduction

The tea plant (Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze) is a globally significant economic crop, and its young shoots serve as the primary commercial organs. Processed tea products derived from these shoots have high economic value and cultural importance. As a perennial woody species, tea plants exhibit complex physiological and molecular interactions that influence floral organ development and tea quality/yield. The flowering process in tea plants encompasses critical stages such as floral bud differentiation, flowering regulation, and reproductive resource allocation. These developmental events not only directly influence the efficiency of sexual reproduction but also compete with vegetative growth (leaf development) for resources through “source-sink” competition mechanisms [1]. A previous study reported that debudding (removal of young floral buds) in autumn and winter increases spring tea yield by 13.8–70.6%, while defruiting (removing flowers) enhances yield by 2.4–25.9% compared to the control group (no flower removal) [2]. Consequently, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of floral development in tea plants holds substantial importance for tea production.

The ABCDE model of floral organ development, established through systematic analysis of homeotic gene mutants and provides a framework for understanding floral patterning [3–7]. This model implies that floral organ development is regulated by specific gene combinations. Sepals are controlled by class A and E genes, petals are determined by class A, B, and E genes, stamens depend on class B, C, and E genes, carpel development is modulated by class C and E genes, and ovule formation is regulated by class D, C, and E genes [8]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, class D genes include SEEDSTICK (STK), SHATTERPROOF1 (SHP1), and SHATTERPROOF2 (SHP2) [9]– [10]. Among these, AtSTK serves as a key class D gene with high expression in carpels and young siliques, specifically localized to placental tissues and ovule primordia during carpel development [11]. Functional analyses have shown that AtSTK participates in carpel and ovule morphogenesis, with ectopic expression capable of inducing sepal-to-carpel transformations accompanied by ovule formation [10]. Additionally, ectopic expression of AtSTK promotes early flowering in Arabidopsis, as well as phenotypes such as reduced petal, stamen, and silique size, as well as enlarged fruits [10, 12]. OsMADS13, the rice ortholog of AtSTK, is strictly expressed in ovules. The osmads13 mutant exhibits female sterility, with ovules transforming into carpel-like structures, and also plays a role in floral meristem determinacy [13]. Our preliminary studies revealed significantly downregulated STK expression in the flower buds of the pistil-deficient tea plant resource ‘Yuncha Qirui’ (CRQS), suggesting its potential involvement in regulating pistil development. However, the specific function and molecular regulatory mechanisms of the STK gene in tea plants (CsSTK) remain unclear.

BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BPC) transcription factors have been identified in diverse plant species [14]– [15]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, BPC genes are classified into three subfamilies based on sequence similarity: Class I (BPC1–BPC3), Class II (BPC4–BPC6), and Class III (BPC7) [16]. Aside from the pseudogene BPC5, all other BPC members exhibit broad but distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns [16]– [17]. Functional studies revealed BPC genes exhibit high redundancy in plant development, and combinations of multiple bpc mutants display significant developmental defects, indicating that this gene family plays a crucial role in plant growth and development [17]. Class I and Class II BPC proteins directly suppress STK expression in specific spatiotemporal contexts, thereby regulating flower, embryo, and seed development [18–20]. Nevertheless, the role of the BPC family and potential involvement in floral development in tea plants remains unknown.

This study investigated the function of CsSTK in tea plants, systematically analyzed the relationship between the BPC family and floral development, and identified CsBPC5 as a regulator of CsSTK expression. Our experimental results revealed that CsSTK plays a regulatory role in tea plant floral organ development (particularly in pistil formation), and its overexpression significantly accelerates flowering in Arabidopsis thaliana. Genome-wide analysis revealed 12 BPC family members in tea plants that exhibit broad but differential expression patterns across various tissues. Notably, further studies indicated that, in contrast to its Arabidopsis counterparts, CsBPC5 promotes rather than suppresses CsSTK expression, providing new insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying floral development in tea plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

This study utilized the tea plant cultivars ‘Yuncha 1’ (YC1) and ‘Yuncha Qirui’ (CRQS), along with wild-type tea plants (WT) as experimental materials. CRQS is a natural pistil-deficient mutant originating from Yunnan Province, China. All these materials were preserved in the National Large-Leaf Tea Germplasm Resource Garden established by the author’s research institution. Flower bud samples were collected in October 2022, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use. In addition, the plants Nicotiana benthamiana and Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia ecotype) were maintained in our laboratory.

RNA and DNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using the EASYspin Plus Complex Plant RNA Kit (RN5301, Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase) (KR116-01, TIANGEN BIOTECH (BEIJING) CO., LTD.) for downstream applications, including coding sequence (CDS) cloning and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. Genomic DNA was isolated using the Super Plant Genomic DNA Kit (Polysaccharides & Polyphenolics-rich) (DP360, TIANGEN BIOTECH (BEIJING) CO., LTD.).

Gene cloning and sequence analysis

The coding sequences of CsSTK and CsBPC5 were amplified from YC1 cDNA via PCR, while the promoter sequence of CsSTK was amplified from YC1 genomic DNA, following the methodology described by Pang et al. [21]. The obtained CsSTK promoter sequence was then analyzed and aligned using DNAman software.

Genetic transformation and plant cultivation

The coding region of CsSTK was cloned into the plant overexpression vector pBWA(V)BS via homologous recombination. Arabidopsis thaliana was transformed using the floral dip method. Transgenic positive T1 lines were obtained by antibiotic screening and PCR verification, and T3 generation lines were acquired via successive self-pollination for phenotypic analysis. All Arabidopsis thaliana and tobacco materials were cultivated in an artificial climate chamber under controlled conditions: a 16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod, temperature of 24 °C/22°C, light intensity of 6000 lx, and relative humidity of 75%.

Gene expression analysis

The expression levels of the samples were determined using the KAPA One-Step qRT-PCR Kit (KK4651, KAPA Biosystems). The tea plant GAPDH (XM_028237220.1) and Arabidopsis Actin (AT3G18780) were used as internal reference genes for tea and Arabidopsis samples, respectively, following the experimental procedure described by Pang et al. [21]. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak et al., 2001) [22], with primer sequences listed in Table S1. The expression levels (TPM) of CsSTK in different tissues of ‘Shuchazao’ were obtained from the TPIA database (TPIA2: an updated tea plant information archive for Camellia genomics) [23]. The transcriptome data of YC1, WT, and CRQS were derived from Liu et al. [24], while other transcriptome data were obtained from unpublished results in our laboratory.

Dual-luciferase (LUC) reporter assay

The promoter fragment of CsSTK and the coding region of CsBPC5 were cloned into the pGreenII 0800-LUC reporter vector and pGreenII-62-SK effector vector, respectively. The reporter and effector constructs were co-transformed into Arabidopsis or tobacco protoplasts via polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation, followed by incubation in darkness at 23 °C for 18–24 h, as previously described by Jeon et al. [25]. Following incubation, luciferase signals were measured using a VICTOR Nivo™ microplate reader (PerkinElmer). The expression strength of the reporter gene was quantified as the ratio of firefly luciferase (LUC) activity to Renilla luciferase (REN) activity (LUC/REN). Each experiment was performed with three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data were visualized using GraphPad Prism (version 8.2.1). Significant differences were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and all statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.

Results

Tissue-specific expression of CsSTK in tea plants

Analysis of public transcriptomes and our laboratory’s previous RNA-seq data revealed that CsSTK gene is expressed in specific tissues of the reproductive organs of tea plants. The gene was highly expressed in floral and fruit tissues, with transcript levels at the floral bud stage significantly exceeding those in fully bloomed flower stage (Fig. 1A, B). These findings suggest that CsSTK may play crucial regulatory roles in floral organ development and fruit formation in tea plants. Further research indicated markedly downregulated CsSTK expression in the pistil-deficient tea germplasm ‘Yuncha Qirui’ (CRQS). Transcriptomic data revealed that CsSTK expression in CRQS floral buds (FB) was less than 30% of that observed in normal tea plants (YC1_FB and WT_FB) (Fig. 1C). qPCR quantification further confirmed significantly reduced CsSTK expression in CRQS_FB compared to controls (Fig. 1D).

Additionally, comparative analysis between the completely sterile flower material ‘Yuncha Yirui’ (ZDH), which exhibits abnormal carpel fusion, continuous stamen differentiation, and defective anther development, and its fertile parents (FDDB and FX) showed significantly elevated transcriptional levels of CsSTK in ZDH_FFB (FFB, fully opened flower), reaching 14–68 times higher than in FDDB_FFB and FX_FFB (Fig. 1E). Tissue-specific expression analysis of floral organs demonstrated that CsSTK expression in the pistil was significantly higher than in other floral organs, including sepals, petals, pedicels, anthers, and stamens (Fig. 1F).

Collectively, these results suggest that CsSTK may be involved in tea plant floral organ formation by regulating pistil development. The findings provide evidence and deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying reproductive development in tea plants.

Overexpression of CsSTK promotes early flowering in Arabidopsis

The coding sequence (CDS) of CsSTK was cloned via PCR from the cDNA of tea cultivar ‘Yuncha 1’ (YC1). The open reading frame (ORF) was 696 bp in length, encoding 231 amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 1). Sequence alignment analysis revealed that CsSTK shares high conservation with homologous genes in Arabidopsis, tobacco, rice, and tomato, all containing the MADS-box and K-box domains, which are characteristic of the MADS-box gene family (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The CsSTK CDS was cloned into the plant overexpression vector pCAMBIA1300 via double-enzyme digestion and then transformed into Arabidopsis via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated floral dip transformation. Four independent transgenic lines (CsSTK_1, CsSTK_2, CsSTK_3, and CsSTK_4) were selected for further analysis. Genomic PCR confirmed CsSTK integration in transplastomic Arabidopsis lines but not wild-type (WT) plants (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated stable CsSTK expression in transgenic lines, while no detectable expression was found in the WT counterparts (Fig. 2A), confirming successful heterologous expression of CsSTK in Arabidopsis. Phenotypic observations revealed that 35 S::CsSTK transgenic lines exhibited significantly earlier bolting than WT (Fig. 2B). Transgenic lines bolted (reached 1 cm in height) 1–2 days earlier than WT, with CsSTK_1 and CsSTK_4 bolting 2 days earlier, and CsSTK_2 and CsSTK_3 bolting 1 day earlier (Fig. 2C). At the bolting stage, CsSTK_3 and CsSTK_4 produced significantly fewer rosette leaves than WT, whereas CsSTK_1 and CsSTK_2 showed no significant difference (Fig. 2D). Meanwhile, the first flower opening time in transgenic lines was 1–3 days earlier than in WT (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that overexpression of CsSTK promotes early bolting and flowering in Arabidopsis.

Identification and expression analysis of tea plant BPC family members

Through homologous alignment with Arabidopsis BPC family protein sequences, 12 BPC family members were identified in tea plants (Camellia sinensis), designated as CsBPC1 to CsBPC7.3 (Supplementary Table 2). Phylogenetic analysis classified them into three distinct subfamilies (Fig. 3): Class I (CsBPC1-3 clustering with AtBPC1-3), Class II (CsBPC4-CsBPC6.4 grouping with AtBPC4-6), and Class III (CsBPC7.1-7.3 clustering with AtBPC7). This classification implies evolutionary conservation of BPC proteins between tea plants and Arabidopsis. Tissue-specific expression analysis revealed that Class II members exhibited significantly higher expression levels compared to Class I and III (Fig. 4). Most genes were widely expressed across various tissues but displayed distinct tissue preferences. Specifically, CsBPC1 (Class I), CsBPC6.1 (Class II), and CsBPC7.1 (Class III) showed particularly high expression in old stems and roots. Except for CsBPC6.4, all CsBPC genes exhibited higher expression in flower buds than in fully opened flowers, a pattern consistent with CsSTK expression. CsBPC5 positively regulates CsSTK transcription

Previous studies have demonstrated that Arabidopsis BPC family genes are involved in the regulation of floral organ development by modulating AtSTK expression. Aside from CsBPC6.4, all CsBPC genes exhibited higher expression levels in flower buds compared to the fully opened flowers of fertile varieties (FDDB and FX), which was highly consistent with CsSTK expression. Notably, in sterile flowers (ZDH), the expression trends of CsBPCs were similar to those of CsSTK, suggesting potential regulatory roles of CsBPCs in CsSTK transcription.

To test this hypothesis, the coding sequence (CDS, 933 bp encoding 310 amino acids; Supplementary Figure 2) of CsBPC5, a Class II member, was cloned from tea cultivar YC1, and a 1500-bp promoter sequence upstream of the CsSTK translation start site (CsSTKpro) was isolated. In silico analysis using PlantPAN 4.0 identified 23 BPC family binding motifs within CsSTKpro, including seven high-confidence motifs (Similarity Score > 0.85; Supplementary Table 3). Dual-luciferase reporter assays were utilized in both Arabidopsis and tobacco protoplasts to validate the regulatory effect of CsBPC5 on CsSTK. Specifically, the 35S-driven CsBPC5 effector was co-transfected with the CsSTKpro-LUC reporter construct (Fig. 5B). The results indicated that CsBPC5 overexpression significantly enhanced LUC activity by 221.11% in Arabidopsis protoplasts and 245.45% in tobacco protoplasts compared to controls (Figs. 5C and 5D). These findings provide direct evidence that CsBPC5 activates CsSTK promoter activity, thereby positively regulating CsSTK expression.

Comparative analysis of CsBPC expression dynamics during floral development revealed distinct patterns between sterile (ZDH) and fertile (FDDB, FX) varieties. In fertile flowers (FDDB, FX), all genes except CsBPC6.4 showed significantly higher expression in buds compared to open flowers. In contrast, sterile flowers (ZDH) displayed similar expression levels between buds and opened flowers, with the opened ZDH flowers exhibiting substantially higher expression compared to their FDDB and FX counterparts. These results indicate that tea plant BPC family genes may play regulatory roles in floral development and potentially contribute to the formation of sterile phenotypes in ZDH.

Discussion

Floral organ development is a critical area in the biological research of tea plants (Camellia sinensis). Its regulatory mechanisms directly influence yield and quality formation and also provide essential guidance for hybridization breeding of tea plants [24]. Preliminary studies have suggested that the CsSTK gene may contribute to the formation of CRQS (carpel reduction or absence syndrome) [24]. Further expression analysis indicated that CsSTK is likely involved in the development of tea plant flowers and fruits, particularly in carpel formation (Fig. 1). Notably, heterologous overexpression of CsSTK in Arabidopsis thaliana significantly accelerated flowering in transgenic plants, which is consistent with the ectopic overexpression of the endogenous STK gene in Arabidopsis [10, 20].

Through genome-wide screening and identification, a total of 12 BPC family members were found in tea plants. Phylogenetic analysis classified these members into three groups, similar to BPCs in Arabidopsis. Class II contained the most members (6), while Classes I and III each contained 3 members. This phylogenetic clustering pattern was also consistent with observations in rice [26], cucumber [27], and camellia [28]. Tissue expression profiling revealed that CsBPCs generally exhibit broad expression patterns with relatively weak tissue specificity, potentially reflecting their involvement in multiple growth and developmental processes [17]. Nevertheless, certain expression differences were observed among different tissues. For example, CsBPC1 in Class I, CsBPC6.1 in Class II, and CsBPC7.1 in Class III are all highly expressed in old stems and roots. In Class II, all members except CsBPC6.4 showed higher expression in normal flower buds compared to fully bloomed flowers. Similar expression patterns were observed in Arabidopsis, suggesting functional redundancy of BPC genes while maintaining some degree of differentiation; notably, Class I genes in Arabidopsis can complement the mutant phenotypes of Class II genes, whereas Class II genes cannot rescue Class I mutant phenotypes [17].

The results of this study indicate that in tea plants, the expression levels of most CsBPC genes (except for CsBPC6.4) in flower buds were significantly higher than in fully bloomed flowers, which is consistent with the expression trend of CsSTK. Furthermore, CsBPC genes showed high expression in both flower buds and fully bloomed flowers of the ZDH cultivar, with higher expression in buds (FDDB_FFB and FX_FFB). These findings suggest that CsBPCs may participate in floral development, potentially through positive regulation of CsSTK expression. Dual-luciferase reporter assays further confirmed that CsBPC5 can positively regulate CsSTK expression.

Nonetheless, this regulatory pattern differs from findings in Arabidopsis, where both class I and class II members of the BPC family were shown to repress STK transcription [18–20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Arabidopsis BPCs likely suppress STK expression by recruiting TERMINAL FLOWER 2/LIKE HETEROCHROMATIN PROTEIN 1 (TFL2/LHP1) and mediating the establishment and maintenance of H3K27me3 repressive marks on DNA [20]. This confirmed H3K27me3 modification as essential for BPC-mediated STK repression. In Arabidopsis, H3K27me3 marks are enriched in genes with tissue-specific expression patterns; these results suggest that this epigenetic marker is developmentally regulated [29]. The differences in regulatory patterns may be attributed to the heterologous nature of the experimental materials and their non-floral tissue origin. Therefore, these findings should be validated using tea plant protoplasts or floral tissues to exclude potential species- and tissue-specific influences on the regulatory mechanism.

In certain plants (e.g., rice), BPC members act as positive regulators of developmental pathways, suggesting that CsBPC5-mediated activation of CsSTK may not represent an isolated mechanistic paradigm. For instance, the BPC family member OsGBP1 positively regulates the transcription of OsLFL1 and Ghd8, thereby inhibiting the Ehd1-Hd3a pathway and ultimately affecting flowering time [30]. This finding supports the hypothesis that CsBPC5 may participate in floral development by positively regulating CsSTK expression. Given the functional redundancy and uniqueness among BPC family members, future studies should systematically explore the functions of BPC family members in tea plants, focusing on their roles in floral development regulatory networks, to comprehensively investigate the developmental biological functions of this gene family. Moreover, epigenetic analyses such as H3K27me3 modification detection should be incorporated to clarify the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the regulatory effects of CsBPCs on CsSTK. Future research may reveal the reasons underlying the functional differences between tea plant and Arabidopsis BPCs.

Conclusions

This study reveals the critical role of the CsSTK gene in the development of tea plant floral organs. Its expression is significantly enriched in the pistil and closely associated with floral developmental stages, showing markedly higher transcript levels during the bud stage compared to the full-bloom stage. In the pistil-deficient material CRQS, CsSTK expression was significantly downregulated, whereas abnormally high expression was observed in the ZDH material concomitant with abnormal carpel development. This indicates that the CsSTK gene plays a key regulatory role in pistil development. Functional analysis demonstrated that CsSTK overexpression promotes early flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis. Furthermore, CsBPC5 was confirmed to directly activate the transcription of CsSTK by comprehensive analyzes of tea plant BPC family members, co-expression patterns with CsSTK, promoter cis-element identification, and dual-luciferase reporter assays. These findings preliminarily elucidate the molecular mechanism of the CsBPC5-CsSTK regulatory module in tea plant floral organ development, providing important clues for a deeper understanding of the genetic regulatory network underlying reproductive development in tea plants.
