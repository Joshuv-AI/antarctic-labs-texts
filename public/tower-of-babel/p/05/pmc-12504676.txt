An evolutionary game model of HEI and graduate aligning postgraduate expansion with sustainable development
Authors: Jiahui Huang, Gaoquan Zhou, Yang Zeng, Ya Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study employs an evolutionary game model to investigate the strategic dynamics between higher education institutions (HEIs) and bachelor’s degree graduates (Graduates) amid China’s postgraduate enrollment expansion and its concurrent employment challenges. We construct a model around these two stakeholders and propose two novel parameters to quantify their decision drivers: the Education Opportunity Cost Premium (EOCP) for graduates and the Return on an expansion policy (RREP) for HEIs. The findings reveal a bistable system with two potential equilibria: a “conservative coordination” (Not Expand, Employ) and a “progressive coordination” (Expand, Enroll). The system exhibits significant path dependency, indicating that the final outcome is highly sensitive to initial conditions. Graduates’ strategies are primarily driven by the EOCP, while institutional choices are governed by the RREP. This research elucidates how individually rational decisions can converge towards a collectively suboptimal outcome, posing a challenge to sustainable development. By analyzing the model’s sensitivity to key parameters, this study critically assesses the implications for educational equity and proposes a collaborative university-industry-government framework to better align postgraduate expansion with sustainable development goals.

Introduction

In pursuit of its national strategy to become an innovation-driven economy, the People’s Republic of China has initiated an unprecedented expansion of its higher education system. This phenomenon of educational expansion is not an isolated event, but reflects a broader global trend. Driven by the forces of globalization and neoliberalism, many higher education institutions (HEIs) worldwide face declining public funding, compelling them to adopt market-driven models1. The rise of “academic capitalism” has transformed HEIs into markets, whose pursuit of financial resources, reputation, and rankings often leads to significant enrollment expansion.

However, this rapid expansion has created a significant dilemma in China. On one hand, the challenge of securing suitable employment for highly educated graduates persists as a major societal concern. On the other hand, many key industries report critical labor shortages. This juxtaposition highlights a severe structural imbalance in the labor market.

The underlying cause of this societal issue transcends a simple supply–demand imbalance. It is more accurately characterized as a “Temporary mismatch”. While students’ educational choices are associated with long-term career outcomes, the enrollment expansion policies of HEIs are often driven by short-term incentives.

Concurrently, technological advancements are continually reshaping the demand for highly skilled professionals. The apparent surplus of graduates may therefore be a transient phenomenon, potentially mitigated by dynamic adjustments in the industrial structure.

This challenge is directly related to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) proposed by the United Nations Economic and Social Council (UNESC) in 20222. Achieving a balance among the 17 SDGs necessitates moving beyond single-system approaches3–5. To analyze the “temporary education mismatch” and inform strategies for sustainable higher education, we first define two core related concepts:

Sustainable Education (SDG 4): This goal emphasizes “quality education”, aiming to “ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all”6. In this context, sustainable education implies not only providing quality educational opportunities, but also ensuring that educational outcomes align with societal development needs. Thereby avoiding the misallocation of resources caused by a disconnect between education and the labor market7,8.

Sustainable Employment (SDG 8): This goal aims to “promote sustained, inclusive and sustainable economic growth, full and productive employment and decent work for all”9. We define sustainable employment as a state, where the labor force’s skills are highly aligned with job market demands, resulting in high productivity, individual job satisfaction, and overall economic stability10,11.

The current predicament in China’s higher education can be viewed as a temporary failure to achieve both “sustainable education” and “sustainable employment”. While previous literature has described this phenomenon and its negative consequences, such as degree devaluation12, a clear research gap remains.

Existing studies have primarily focused on describing the consequences of this mismatch, but have not adequately explained the underlying processes and mechanisms that cause it. Specifically, there has yet to develop a dynamic model to systematically analyze the complex interactions between HEIs and graduates as core stakeholders within the context of “academic capitalism” and temporary education mismatch.

To fill this gap, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the intrinsic mechanisms governing the sustainable development of higher education. By focusing on the interactions between HEIs and graduates, this paper investigates the following core questions:What are the key factors that drive the strategic choices of HEIs and graduates regarding enrollment expansion and further education?How do the interactions between these stakeholders lead to the “temporary education mismatch” in the education-employment system?What constitutes an ideal equilibrium state for the system, where educational supply and employment demand are sustainably aligned?

To address these questions, this study proposes an evolutionary game model to simulate the interactions between HEIs and graduates. By solving the model’s replicator dynamic equations, we derive the system’s Evolutionary Stable Strategy (ESS) and the conditions required for its emergence. Subsequently, numerical simulations using MATLAB are conducted to demonstrate the impact of initial conditions and critical parameters on the system’s evolutionary trajectory. Finally, this paper concludes with a summary of its findings and offers policy recommendations for promoting the sustainable development of higher education.

Materials and methods

Model selection and rationale

To analyze the dynamic evolution of postgraduate enrollment expansion and graduates’ decision-making, this study employs an evolutionary game model. This approach was chosen over classical game theory for several key reasons.

First, the core stakeholders—HEIs and graduates—exhibit bounded rationality rather than perfect rationality13. Their strategic choices are not made with complete, instantaneous knowledge, but are instead adaptive, evolving through continuous learning, imitation of successful strategies, and reliance on past experiences14,15. The evolutionary game framework is particularly well-suited to capture this dynamic adjustment process.

Second, the model is designed to investigate a complex and under-theorized social phenomenon. To elucidate the fundamental interaction logic and causal mechanisms driving the system towards a collectively suboptimal equilibrium, we adopt a simplified modeling approach. This involves focusing on the two primary stakeholders and intentionally omitting secondary variables that might obscure the core dynamics.

It is important to note that while incomplete information serves as a foundational premise of this model, it is treated as a background condition rather than an endogenous variable to be parameterized.

Temporary mismatch in education.

The current imbalance between the number of graduates and available high-skilled employment opportunities can lead to a temporary “devaluation” of academic qualifications12. Therefore, aligning educational supply with labor market demands is of critical importance for achieving a sustainable “closed-loop” system in higher education, thereby fostering synergy between SDG 4 (Quality Education) and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth).

Therefore, from a game-theoretic perspective, this implies that unrestricted educational expansion is suboptimal. The system’s objective should be to reach a stable and socially optimal equilibrium rather than simply maximizing enrollment.

Construction of evolutionary game model

Model assumption

The game involves two primary populations of stakeholders:Population 1: Higher Education Institutions (HEIs). Driven by incentives associated with “academic capitalism”, HEIs must decide whether to expand their postgraduate programs. Their strategic space is therefore defined as (Expand, Not Expand).Population 2: Bachelor’s Degree Graduates (Graduates). Upon graduation, individuals must choose between entering the labor market immediately or pursuing further education. Their strategic space is thus defined as (Enroll in Postgraduate Studies, Seek Employment).

The replicator dynamic equations

To analyze the dynamic evolution of strategies between HEIs and graduates, we employ the replicator dynamics framework, a cornerstone of evolutionary game theory. The replicator dynamics describe how the proportion of a population using a particular strategy changes over time, based on the difference between the strategy’s payoff and the average payoff of the population. This allows us to model the convergence process towards stable equilibrium points.

Numerical simulation and analysis

Simulation purpose and methodology

To visually validate and further explore the theoretical findings from the stability analysis, this section presents a series of numerical simulations. The primary purpose of these simulations is to intuitively illustrate the system’s dynamic evolutionary paths and demonstrate the impact of key parameters, rather than to make precise quantitative predictions.

Our model is based on deterministic replicator dynamics, meaning that for any given set of parameters and initial conditions, the system’s evolutionary trajectory and final equilibrium are uniquely determined. Consequently, our analysis focuses on visualizing the qualitative structural changes in the system’s dynamics, not on statistical testing of random processes.

To clearly isolate the influence of individual parameters, we primarily employ the one-at-a-time (OAT) sensitivity analysis method. This approach allows for an unambiguous illustration of how each core parameter independently affects the system’s evolution, establishing a direct correspondence with our theoretical derivations.

Scenario selection and parameter settings

Based on our theoretical analysis and the realities of contemporary higher education—characterized by growing demand for postgraduate degrees18 and complex interactions between state and market forces19. Our simulations will focus on the most complex and realistic scenario identified previously: the bistable case (Finding 4.2). This scenario is defined by the following conditions: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{2}+{\Delta C}_{2}-\left({C}_{2}{\prime}+{\Delta C}_{2}{\prime}\right)<{E}_{2}-{E}_{2}{\prime}<\left({C}_{2}+{\beta \Delta C}_{2}\right)-{C}_{2}{\prime}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta E}_{1}>{C}_{1}$$\end{document}.

This case, which exhibits path dependency and features two potential ESSs (Not Expand, Employ) and (Expand, Enroll), provides the richest context for examining the system’s sensitivity to parameter changes.

For the simulations, the time horizon is set from t = 0 to t = 10, with a step size of 0.01 to ensure smooth trajectory plotting. The specific baseline parameter values for the simulation will be outlined in the following section.

Discussion

This study’s evolutionary game model provides a stylized representation of the complex adaptive system governing the relationship between institutional expansion and graduate enrollment decisions. The findings—highlighting the existence of multiple equilibria, path dependency, and high sensitivity to key parameters—offer a robust theoretical lens for understanding the intrinsic dynamics of contemporary higher education. The system’s potential convergence to either a “progressive” (Expand, Enroll) or a “conservative” (Not Expand, Employ) equilibrium underscores a fundamental tension that can be further illuminated by examining the contextual factors shaping the model’s parameters.

HEI’s decision-making driven by the tension between market logic and public mission

The parameter RREP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{\Delta E}_{1}}{{C}_{1}}$$\end{document}), institutional Return on an expansion policy, is more than a simple economic calculation. It encapsulates the central conflict facing modern HEIs. On one hand, RREP reflects the logic of “academic capitalism”1, where HEIs operate as market-driven enterprises. To maximize a market-oriented RREP, a common strategy is to expand low-cost, high-demand “soft” disciplines (e.g., business, social sciences), which ensures short-term financial viability. This aligns with empirical findings that graduates from humanities and social sciences are more prone to overeducation and structural supply–demand imbalances20.

On the other hand, HEIs are traditionally viewed as public goods tasked with fulfilling national strategic missions21. High-cost, strategically vital “hard” disciplines (STEM, medicine) may offer a lower short-term market RREP but are indispensable for national innovation and long-term competitiveness.

This internal tension manifests differently across national contexts. In highly marketized systems like the United States, the RREP of private research universities is predominantly shaped by market indicators such as tuition revenue, alumni donations, and competitive research funding22. This often biases expansion towards “soft disciplines” that generate immediate cash flow. In contrast, in state-led systems like those in China or continental Europe, the RREP of public universities is significantly influenced by national strategy. Governments can directly shape the RREP through targeted funding for strategic “hard disciplines” (e.g., semiconductors, AI), effectively defining enrollment expansion as a political act aligned with national missions.

Our simulation results provide a stark illustration of this conflict. We found that if RREP ≤ 1 (i.e., expansion is unprofitable), the system inevitably collapses into a single, “conservative” equilibrium (Not Expand, Employ). This finding models a critical form of market failure in higher education: in the absence of external support (e.g., government subsidies) or non-market incentives (e.g., social reputation), pure market logic would compel HEIs to underinvest in high-cost, high-risk, yet strategically essential disciplines.

The model’s bistable outcome suggests that a system’s tendency towards short-term market-driven expansion or long-term strategic investment depends fundamentally on how society and government define and incentivize institutional “returns”. An RREP driven solely by market forces can lead to an unsustainable academic structure, creating a surplus of graduates in certain fields, that constitutes a significant waste of human capital. While ensuring institutional survival in the short term, this approach undermines the system’s long-term capacity to support sustainable socioeconomic development.

Therefore, a sustainable governance framework must employ policy tools, such as strategic funding and targeted subsidies, to actively regulate the RREP. The goal is not to eliminate market forces but to balance them with public mission, fostering a form of “strategic entrepreneurship”23. This ensures that HEIs can maintain financial stability while making crucial long-term investments that align with national sustainable development goals, such as technological innovation and the green transition.

The social construction of EOCP and graduate decision-making

The parameter EOCP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{2}-{E}_{2}{\prime}$$\end{document}), the Educational Opportunity Cost Premium, is not merely an objective economic calculation but a socially constructed value system that shapes graduates’ decision-making. A high EOCP often corresponds to fields like renewable energy or advanced technology, where postgraduate qualifications yield significantly higher long-term returns in terms of salary and career progression24. However, our model reveals that the EOCP’s primary mechanism is more subtle than a simple cost–benefit analysis.

The model demonstrates that the EOCP influences graduates’ strategies by fundamentally altering their risk perception in the face of uncertain institutional strategies. This mechanism is mathematically captured by the x-coordinate of the saddle point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\left({x}^{*},{y}^{*}\right)$$\end{document}, which is determined by the relative costs and benefits of choosing “Employ” versus “Enroll”, particularly when HEIs do not expand (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta >1$$\end{document}). When the EOCP is extremely high, the perceived long-term penalty for not obtaining a postgraduate degree becomes so significant that it overshadows the short-term risks of application failure, even when admission is highly competitive. In this state, pursuing further education is no longer a choice to maximize returns, but a perceived necessity to minimize future risk, a finding consistent with prospect theory’s emphasis on loss aversion25.

This dynamic is particularly pronounced in highly “credentialism” and rigidly stratified social environments. According to signaling theory26, educational credentials serve as primary signals of potential productivity to employers. In a context of “credential inflation”, a postgraduate degree transforms from an added advantage into a perceived prerequisite for entry into desirable segments of the labor market. This societal context artificially inflates the EOCP, compelling graduates into an educational “arms race”27. They are driven to invest in further education not necessarily to acquire specific skills, but to maintain a competitive edge in a saturated market.

This arms race, fueled by a socially constructed EOCP, poses a significant threat to sustainable development. It can lead to a systemic decoupling of the education system from the actual skill demands of the economy, resulting in a workforce that is simultaneously overeducated for some roles and underskilled for others28. As empirical studies have shown, while the labor market may absorb the influx of graduates, this often comes at the cost of declining and more dispersed wage returns for higher education, widening inequality29. This represents both a misallocation of societal resources and a waste of individual human capital, directly undermining the principles of SDG 4 (Quality Education) and SDG 8 (Decent Work).

Systemic pressures from globalization and neoliberalism

Viewing the model’s dynamics within a broader global context reveals a powerful alignment with the structural transformations in higher education over recent decades. The pervasive influence of globalization and neoliberal policies can be understood as a significant exogenous shock that has systematically altered the “rules of the game” for both HEIs and graduates. Specifically, these macro-level pressures have reshaped the two core parameters of our model: the institutional RREP and the graduate EOCP.

First, the neoliberal trend of declining public funding for higher education19 directly intensifies market pressures on HEIs. As state support diminishes, institutions are compelled to rely more heavily on tuition fees and other market-driven revenue streams. This systematically inflates the market-oriented components of the RREP, forcing even public universities to adopt behaviors consistent with the “academic capitalism” model, where revenue generation can overshadow the public mission30.

Second, neoliberal ideology promotes a narrative of individual responsibility, reframing education as a private investment rather than a public good. This ideological shift directly impacts the EOCP by transferring a greater share of the costs and risks of higher education onto students and their families. The stakes of making a “wrong” strategic choice—either by failing to enroll when degrees are inflating in value, or by enrolling when the market is saturated—are significantly heightened.

Our model provides a clear illustration of the potential consequences of these systemic pressures. As Sarpong and Adelekan argue31, these trends risk eroding the public mission of universities and exacerbating educational inequality. Our findings suggest a specific mechanism for this: a system dominated by a market-driven RREP and a high-risk EOCP is prone to converging towards a suboptimal “progressive coordination” equilibrium (Expand, Enroll). In this state, HEIs engage in unplanned, revenue-driven expansion, while graduates flock to postgraduate studies out of a defensive, risk-averse necessity. This outcome, while appearing “coordinated”, can lead to a systemic imbalance, simultaneously straining institutional resources and distorting the labor market’s talent structure.

Implications for sustainable development and equity

Our analysis reveals that the higher education system, under certain conditions, can settle into a bistable equilibrium. This state of path dependency, where the system can converge to either a “conservative” or a “progressive” equilibrium, presents both a challenge and an opportunity for governance. The existence of a saddle point and its sensitivity to parameters like EOCP and RREP identify the effective policy levers for intervention. By shaping the incentives for HEIs and graduates, governments and industry partners can influence the initial conditions and parameter values to steer the system away from suboptimal outcomes (e.g., excessive expansion) and towards a dynamic equilibrium that fosters the synergistic development of sustainable education and employment (as conceptualized in Fig. 7).Fig. 7A dynamic equilibrium fosters the synergistic development of sustainable education and employment.

However, any discussion of educational sustainability is incomplete without addressing equity, a cornerstone of the Sustainable Development Goals. While expanding access to higher education may appear democratic, the feedback loops identified in our model can paradoxically exacerbate social inequality. As postgraduate degrees become increasingly commonplace due to “credential inflation”, they can transform into new prerequisites for entry into elite professions. This creates formidable barriers for students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds who may lack the financial resources or cultural capital to afford the extended time and cost of further education31.

This process effectively raises the entry barrier for all, disproportionately harming those for whom a bachelor’s degree was once a vital engine of social mobility. Furthermore, the expansion of postgraduate education does not necessarily diminish the advantages of privileged students. Even as access to a certain level of education expands, privileged families leverage their resources to secure access to the most prestigious programs within that level32. If expansion is concentrated in expensive professional master’s programs or if elite resources remain siloed within a few top universities, then enrollment expansion may simply reinforce existing social stratification at a higher educational tier.

Therefore, policy interventions must move beyond a narrow focus on enrollment numbers. Policy implications for “HEI-industry-government” collaborative governance

The model’s findings on path dependency and its sensitivity to EOCP and RREP highlight the critical need for proactive, collaborative governance. A “HEI-industry-government” framework can create and deploy effective policy levers to steer the system towards a socially optimal and sustainable equilibrium.

Aligning graduate choices with national needs

The government and industry can collaboratively shape the EOCP to better inform and guide graduates’ decisions:

Policy Tool 1: Dynamic Talent Demand Information Platform. Establish a national platform that provides real-time forecasts of talent shortages in key strategic industries, detailed graduate employment data (by major and degree level), and skill-based profiles for specific positions that go beyond formal educational qualifications.

It can empowers graduates to make more informed decisions based on market signals. But it requires significant investment in data infrastructure; risks promoting an overly utilitarian approach to major selection; predictive information may have a time lag.

Policy Tool 2: Dual-Track System of Academic and Professional Credentials. Promote a system where advanced, industry-recognized professional certifications can serve as alternative or supplementary qualifications for both graduation and recruitment in applied disciplines. The government could subsidize the cost for students to obtain these certifications.

It creates more flexible pathways to high-skilled employment and better aligns training with industry needs. But it requires significant fiscal subsidies; necessitates reforms to university curricula and professional accreditation systems; could potentially devalue traditional postgraduate degrees if not implemented carefully.

Guiding institutional strategy by regulating the RREP

The RREP is a powerful lever for influencing institutional behavior. It underscores the government’s critical role in ensuring investment in strategic disciplines.

Policy Tool 3: Strategic Discipline Development Fund. Establish a performance-based fund to selectively increase the RREP for programs aligned with national strategic objectives (e.g., STEM, green technologies). Funding could follow a “base grant + performance bonus” model, with the bonus tied to indicators such as university-industry co-training bases, graduate employment rates in strategic sectors, and the economic impact of research commercialization.

It incentivizes HEIs to invest in high-cost, long-term disciplines that the market alone would underfund. But it represents a significant fiscal commitment; requires a fair and transparent performance evaluation system; risks creating disciplinary imbalances that could harm the comprehensive academic ecosystem.

Policy Tool 4: Differentiated Enrollment Quota System with Regional Coordination. Future research could explore the political and institutional barriers to implementing such a collaborative governance framework.

Conclusion

This study employed an evolutionary game model, supplemented by numerical simulations, to analyze the dynamic strategic interactions between higher education institutions and bachelor’s degree graduates in the context of postgraduate enrollment expansion. The research systematically investigated the conditions for stable equilibria and the system’s sensitivity to key parameters. The principal findings, theoretical contributions, limitations, and directions for future research are summarized below.

Principal findings

Bistability and Path Dependency: The higher education enrollment system is characterized by two potential stable states: a “progressive coordination” equilibrium (Expand, Enroll) and a “conservative coordination” equilibrium (Not Expand, Employ). The system’s convergence to either state is not predetermined but is highly path-dependent, hinging critically on the initial strategic proportions of the two stakeholders. This underscores the profound impact that initial policy guidance and social expectations can have on the long-term trajectory of the entire system.The Central Role of the EOCP: The Educational Opportunity Cost Premium (EOCP), which reflects the perceived net benefit of postgraduate education, acts as the primary driver of graduate decision-making. A high positive EOCP propels the system towards the “progressive” equilibrium, while a negative EOCP locks it into the “conservative” state. The EOCP functions through a powerful positive feedback loop, where shifts in graduate preferences are amplified by corresponding institutional responses, thereby steering the entire system’s evolution.RREP as the Institutional Trigger: The Return on an expansion policy (RREP) serves as the key determinant of institutional strategy. A critical threshold exists (RREP = 1), below which the “progressive” equilibrium collapses, making conservative strategies inevitable. Above this threshold, a higher RREP increases the likelihood of a “progressive” outcome by expanding its basin of attraction. This highlights the role of HEIs as “opportunity providers” whose internal cost–benefit logic fundamentally shapes the landscape of choices for graduates.

Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the literature by developing a parsimonious evolutionary game model that reveals the emergent, systemic dynamics—such as path dependency and bifurcations—arising from micro-level strategic interactions in higher education. It offers a novel theoretical perspective that moves beyond static descriptions of educational mismatch to explain the underlying mechanisms that generate it, providing a formal framework for analyzing the macro-level evolution of higher education systems.

Limitations and future research directions

While the model’s simplicity is a source of its analytical clarity, it also presents limitations that offer valuable avenues for future research.
