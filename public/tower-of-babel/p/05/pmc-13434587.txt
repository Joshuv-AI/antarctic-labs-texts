Distinct psychophysiological biomarkers and risk stratification models for irritable bowel syndrome with constipation (IBS-C) and diarrhea (IBS-D)
Authors: Xiaoqing Chen, Yujing Wang, Yanhong Hou, Lin Zhang, Kai Wu, Chao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIrritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent disorder of gut-brain interaction (DGBI) with a complex, multifactorial pathogenesis. Emerging evidence underscores the significant role of psychosocial factors; however, comprehensive models integrating multidimensional psychophysiological biomarkers to predict IBS subtypes often lack validation in this highly heterogeneous population. To evaluate the value of a psychophysiological multimodal approach in identifying predictive signatures for irritable bowel syndrome with constipation (IBS-C) and diarrhea (IBS-D). In this cross-sectional study, 80 IBS-C patients, 80 IBS-D patients, and 80 healthy controls were included. Assessments included eye-tracking (cognitive-emotional interaction), heart rate variability (autonomic function), psychological scales (SCL-90, SCSQ, EPQ), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Multivariable logistic regression and ROC analyses were utilized to identify predictors and evaluate internal model performance. Shared predictive factors for both subtypes included the SCL-90 hostility factor, EPQ neuroticism, negative coping, and global PSQI score (all P < 0.05). IBS-C was specifically associated with hostility and neuroticism, whereas IBS-D was distinctively predicted by prolonged prosaccade latency, reduced HRV medium-frequency power, and a lack of positive coping. The models demonstrated strong internal discrimination, with AUCs up to 0.983 for IBS-C and 0.967 for IBS-D. Psychosocial factors and sleep disturbances are robust shared predictive risk factors for IBS-C and IBS-D. Furthermore, multimodal profiling reveals subtype-specific predictive signatures: prominent emotional reactivity in IBS-C, and altered sensory-autonomic integration in IBS-D. Introduction

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent disorder of gut-brain interaction (DGBI) characterized by abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits. It is more prevalent among individuals with psychological comorbidities and young adult women than in the general population. The pathogenesis of IBS is best understood through a biopsychosocial model, wherein dysregulation of the gut-brain interaction plays a central role. Substantial evidence supports that this dysregulated communication involves multifactorial mechanisms, including visceral hypersensitivity, altered gut microbiota, and dysmotility1–4. According to the Rome IV diagnostic criteria, IBS is subclassified into constipation-predominant (IBS-C), diarrhea-predominant (IBS-D), mixed bowel habits (IBS-M), and unsubtyped (IBS-U) subtypes5.

Emerging research highlights the critical role of psychosocial factors, with anxiety, depression, neurotic personality traits, and sleep disturbances demonstrating significant correlations with symptom severity6–11. While various studies have reported on psychophysiological biomarkers within a biopsychosocial context in IBS, these findings often lack validation across this highly heterogeneous population. Consequently, there remains a critical need for robust, multidimensional predictive models that integrate psychological, physiological, and behavioral domains to better distinguish between IBS subtypes.

To address this limitation, our study innovatively combines eye-tracking technology (assessing cognitive-emotional interactions), heart rate variability (HRV, evaluating autonomic function), and standardized psychometric instruments. This multimodal approach aims to construct predictive risk stratification models for IBS-C and IBS-D, thereby identifying subtype-specific psychophysiological predictive signatures and providing a foundation for targeted interventions.

Methods

Study participants

This case-control study enrolled 80 IBS-C patients, 80 IBS-D patients, and 80 healthy controls. All participants were consecutively recruited from the Gastroenterology and Psychological Medicine Clinics at the Eighth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital between June 1, 2024, and June 1, 2025. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eighth Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Approval No. S-2025-023-01). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment, and the study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable guidelines and regulations.

Diagnostic Criteria: IBS-C Group Recurrent abdominal pain (≥ 1 day/week in the past 3 months) meeting ≥ 2 of the following criteria: (1) pain relief after defecation; (2) pain-associated decreased stool frequency; (3) pain-associated hard stools (Bristol Type 1–2).12 Constipation-dominant bowel habits (with ≥ 25% of stools classified as Bristol Type 1 or 2); symptom duration ≥ 6 months with an active phase in the last 3 months; and exclusion of organic pathologies such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), colorectal cancer, celiac disease, thyroid dysfunction, or drug-induced constipation (e.g., opioids, calcium channel blockers). IBS-D Group: Recurrent abdominal pain (≥ 1 day/week in the past 3 months) meeting ≥ 2 of the following criteria: (1) pain relief after defecation; (2) pain-associated increased stool frequency (> 3/day); (3) pain-associated loose stools (Bristol Type 6–7). Diarrhea-dominant bowel habits (with ≥ 25% of stools classified as Bristol Type 6 or 7); symptom duration ≥ 6 months with an active phase in the last 3 months; and exclusion of organic pathologies such as IBD, colorectal cancer, celiac disease, hyperthyroidism, infectious diarrhea (e.g., giardiasis), or bile acid malabsorption.

General Exclusion Criteria (All Groups): (1) Cardiopulmonary, hematological, endocrine, or genitourinary disorders; (2) Gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcers, liver diseases, or other gastrointestinal pathologies; (3) Psychiatric or neurological disorders; (4) Malignancies; (5) History of gastrointestinal surgery; (6) Pregnancy or lactation; (7) Use of gastrointestinal motility–affecting medications within 2 weeks prior to or during the study.

Healthy Controls: Healthy control participants had no symptoms of any disorders of gut–brain interaction (including IBS)5 and exhibited normal findings on esophagogastroduodenoscopy, abdominothoracic CT, electrocardiography, and laboratory tests (including blood, urine, and stool analyses, as well as liver and renal function tests).

Verification: All patients were diagnosed using the Rome IV criteria5 along with validated questionnaires and relevant clinical guidelines. Organic diseases were excluded via comprehensive examinations. All participants were right-handed.

Ethical Compliance: The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eighth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital (Approval No. S-2025-023-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Methods

Eye movement assessment

Rationale for Implementation: Patients with IBS frequently exhibit altered cognitive processing, hypervigilance, and executive function deficits linked to gut-brain axis dysregulation.13 Eye-tracking technology provides an objective, non-invasive method to assess these cognitive-emotional interactions and attentional biases14. We specifically implemented the prosaccade and antisaccade tasks because they are well-established paradigms for evaluating fundamental attention orientation (sensorimotor integration) and top-down inhibitory control—domains hypothesized to be impaired in functional gastrointestinal disorders.

Apparatus and Metrics: Oculomotor parameters were recorded using the Qing-yan Eye Tracker (Qing-yan Technology, China), equipped with high-resolution optical imaging and precision tracking algorithms at a sampling rate of ≥ 100 Hz. Participants performed standardized prosaccade and antisaccade tasks, with the following target metrics quantified: (1) Fixation duration; (2) Prosaccade latency; (3) Antisaccade latency; (4) Antisaccade error rate; (5) Error-correction rate; (6) Error-correction time.

Psychophysiological measurements

A comprehensive summary of the implemented psychophysiological tools, their target domains, and the rationale for their selection is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Heart Rate Variability (HRV): HRV was assessed using the Fuliye Autonomic Nervous System Analysis System (Beijing Fuliye Technology Co., Ltd.). Frequency-domain analysis of 5-minute resting recordings provided: Low frequency (LF: 0.04–0.15 Hz); Medium frequency (MF: 0.07–0.15 Hz); High frequency (HF: 0.15–0.40 Hz); Very high frequency (VHF: 0.40–1.00 Hz); Very low frequency (VLF: 0.003–0.04 Hz); and the LF/HF ratio. Justification for MF band: While standard HRV nomenclature primarily defines the LF and HF bands15, the implemented system additionally computes a Medium Frequency (MF) band (0.07–0.15 Hz). This sub-band isolates the upper spectrum of the classic LF range (centering around 0.1 Hz), which is often utilized to more specifically capture sympathetically-mediated baroreflex activity, conceptually separating it from the mixed vagal and sympathetic influences present in the broader LF spectrum16. However, given its specific partitioning, physiological interpretations linking the MF band to sympathetic activity in this study are framed cautiously as associative rather than definitive.

Psychological Instruments: All scales were administered by trained clinicians using standardized Mandarin versions: (1) Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90): A 90-item multidimensional symptom inventory adapted from Derogatis’ Hopkin’s Symptom Checklist, evaluating psychological distress across 9 domains including somatization, obsessive-compulsive tendencies, and interpersonal sensitivity. It has been widely validated and applied in Chinese populations to screen for psychopathology17. (2) Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ): A 20-item psychometric instrument widely used in China to measure two behavioral coping dimensions: Positive coping (Items 1–12) and Negative coping (Items 13–20)18. (3) Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ): The adult version of this psychometrically validated tool assessing core personality dimensions (neuroticism, extraversion, psychoticism) and a lie scale19. (4) Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI): A 19-item scale quantifying sleep quality over 1-month recall periods. The Chinese version was validated by Liu Xianchen et al., demonstrating high reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.8) for assessing sleep disturbances in clinical settings20.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Data Presentation and Group Comparisons: The Shapiro-Wilk test was utilized to assess data normality. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared among the three groups using One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test (when variance was homogeneous) or Welch’s ANOVA (when variance was heterogeneous). Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. For significant Kruskal-Wallis results, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. The Z-scores reported in the results represent the standardized test statistics derived from these adjusted pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the Chi-square test.

Predictive Modeling and Diagnostics: Variables demonstrating a potential association (P < 0.2) in univariate logistic regression were entered into multivariable binary logistic regression models to identify independent predictors for IBS subtypes. Prior to finalizing the multivariable models, key statistical assumptions were verified. Specifically, multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF < 5 indicating no significant collinearity. Multivariable logistic regression was executed using the glm function from the rms package (v6.4.0). Model calibration and goodness-of-fit were evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test via the ResourceSelection package (v0.3-5).

Discriminatory Power (ROC Analysis): Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted using the pROC package (v1.18.0) to evaluate model discriminatory performance. For domains comprising multiple variables (e.g., Eye-tracking, SCL-90, SCSQ, EPQ), the predicted probabilities generated from the domain-specific multivariable logistic regression models were utilized as the single composite test variable for ROC curve construction. Area Under the Curve (AUC) values with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated and reported.

Visualization: All graphical visualizations, including diagnostic plots, were generated utilizing the ggplot2 package (v3.4.4).

Comparative analysis of oculomotor metrics

Eye-tracking comparisons revealed significantly prolonged saccade latencies in both the IBS-C and IBS-D groups relative to healthy controls during prosaccade tasks (P < 0.01). In the antisaccade task, the IBS-C group exhibited a higher error rate than the IBS-D group (P < 0.05). No other oculomotor parameters differed among the groups, as shown in Fig. 1 and Table S2.

Statistical comparison of eye-tracking metrics across groups.

Note: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Heart rate variability across groups

Frequency-domain analysis of heart rate variability (HRV) revealed significant reductions in autonomic functional metrics among IBS patients compared to healthy controls. Specifically, low-frequency (LF) and medium-frequency (MF) power were significantly lower in the IBS-C group relative to controls (P < 0.05). Similarly, the IBS-D group exhibited significantly reduced MF, high-frequency (HF), and very-high-frequency (VHF) power compared to the control group (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed for very-low-frequency (VLF) power or the LF/HF ratio among the three groups. Overall, the absolute power of most HRV frequency bands (LF, MF, HF, and VHF) was highest in healthy controls and depressed in both IBS subtypes, as detailed in Fig. 2 and Table S3.

Heart rate variability across groups. (Note: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001 )

Comparison of SCL-90 scores

IBS-C exhibited significantly higher scores than controls on all nine dimensions—somatization, obsessive-compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism (P < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed in IBS-D, with all dimensions markedly elevated versus controls (P < 0.001). Although no statistical differences emerged between IBS-C and IBS-D, mean values were consistently highest in IBS-C, followed by IBS-D, and lowest in controls, as shown in Fig. 3 and Table S4.

Comparison of SCL-90 scores. (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001)

Comparison of SCSQ coping styles

IBS-C did not differ from controls in positive coping but scored significantly higher in negative coping (P < 0.01). IBS-D showed both markedly lower positive coping (P < 0.001) and higher negative coping (P < 0.001) than controls. Although no statistical differences were detected between IBS-C and IBS-D, positive coping was numerically lowest in IBS-D, intermediate in IBS-C, and highest in controls, whereas negative coping followed the reverse order, as shown in Fig. 4 and Table S5.

Comparison of SCSQ coping styles (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001 SCSQ = Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire).

Comparison of EPQ dimensions

IBS-C scored lower on extraversion and higher on neuroticism and psychoticism relative to controls (P < 0.001). A parallel pattern was observed in IBS-D: extraversion was reduced, whereas neuroticism and psychoticism were elevated, as shown in Fig. 5 and Table S6.

Comparison of EPQ dimensions (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

Comparison of PSQI scores

Both IBS-C and IBS-D groups presented significantly higher global PSQI scores than healthy controls, indicating poorer sleep quality. The ‘Use of sleep medication’ subscale was excluded from the analysis due to a lack of variance, as all control participants scored zero on this subscale. The remaining PSQI components are presented in Fig. 6 and Table S7.

Logistic regression analysis for IBS-C

Univariate screening identified significant associations with the following variable domains: (1) Eye-tracking: prolonged prosaccade latency, antisaccade latency, and error-correction time (P < 0.05). (2) SCL-90: all subscales demonstrated significance (P < 0.01). (3) Coping style: negative coping (P < 0.001). (4) EPQ: extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism, and the lie scale (P < 0.01). Cautionary note on model estimates: While variance inflation factor (VIF) analyses indicated no significant multicollinearity among these variables, the multivariable model yielded exceptionally high odds ratios with implausibly wide confidence intervals for SCL-90 hostility and SCSQ negative coping. This statistical pattern typically indicates data sparsity or quasi-complete separation for these specific extreme traits within our current sample. ROC curve analysis for IBS-C

To evaluate the internal discriminatory capacity of each modality for stratifying IBS-C patients from healthy controls, ROC analyses were conducted. Using the composite predictive scores derived from the respective domain-specific multivariable models as the single test variable, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were calculated. The models demonstrated robust internal discrimination: (1) Eye-tracking: AUC = 0.692 (95% CI: 0.584–0.799); (2) SCL-90: AUC = 0.952 (95% CI: 0.911–0.993); (3) SCSQ: AUC = 0.888 (95% CI: 0.799–0.977); (4) EPQ: AUC = 0.983 (95% CI: 0.957–1.000); (5) PSQI: AUC = 0.931 (95% CI: 0.850–1.000).

HRV parameters were excluded from this step as none achieved significance in the univariate screening. Importantly, these ROC models represent internal discrimination performance only. No external validation was performed in the present study, and clinical implementation of these predictive thresholds would require rigorous independent validation in separate cohorts, as shown in Fig. 7; Table 3.

Logistic regression and ROC analysis for IBS-D

Logistic regression analysis of IBS-D

Univariate analysis identified significant associations across multiple domains: (1) Eye-tracking: prolonged prosaccade latency (P < 0.001). (2) HRV: reduced LF, MF, and HF power (P < 0.05). (3) SCL-90: all subscales demonstrated significant elevation (P < 0.01). (4) SCSQ: reduced positive coping and elevated negative coping (P < 0.01). (5) EPQ: lower extraversion, higher neuroticism, psychoticism, and lie scale scores (P < 0.01). (6) PSQI: higher global score (P < 0.001).

In the multivariable binary logistic regression model, six independent predictors were identified for distinguishing IBS-D from healthy controls: (1) Eye-tracking prosaccade latency (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.07, P = 0.003); (2) HRV MF power (OR < 0.001, 95% CI: <0.001–0.396, P = 0.030); (3) SCL-90 hostility (OR = 257.34, 95% CI: 2.33–28407.09, P = 0.021); (4) SCSQ positive coping (OR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.03–0.67, P = 0.013) and negative coping (OR = 71.04, 95% CI: 6.79–742.88, P < 0.001); (5) EPQ neuroticism (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.01–1.38, P = 0.038); (6) PSQI total score (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.47–4.45, P < 0.001), as shown in Table 2.

Cautionary note on model estimates: Similar to the IBS-C model, while variance inflation factor (VIF) analyses ruled out substantial multicollinearity, the exceptionally large odds ratios and wide confidence intervals observed for SCL-90 hostility and negative coping strongly suggest sparse data or quasi-complete separation for these specific variables. Consequently, while the directional associations of these factors with IBS-D risk are robust, their absolute magnitudes (effect sizes) lack precision and should be interpreted with caution.

ROC curve analysis for IBS-D

To assess the internal discriminatory performance of the distinct psychophysiological domains for identifying IBS-D, ROC curve analyses were conducted. We utilized the composite predictive scores derived from the multivariable logistic regression models of each respective domain as the single test variable. The models demonstrated good to excellent internal discrimination: (1) Eye-tracking: AUC = 0.765 (95% CI: 0.659–0.871); (2) HRV: AUC = 0.767 (95% CI: 0.654–0.879); (3) SCL-90: AUC = 0.921 (95% CI: 0.857–0.985); (4) SCSQ: AUC = 0.909 (95% CI: 0.834–0.984); (5) EPQ: AUC = 0.969 (95% CI: 0.912–1.000); (6) PSQI: AUC = 0.967 (95% CI: 0.917–1.000).

Discussion

The present study utilized a comprehensive multidimensional psychophysiological approach to identify distinct biomarker profiles and construct risk stratification models for IBS-C and IBS-D. Our principal findings demonstrate that while psychological distress and sleep disturbances represent shared risk factors across both subtypes, IBS-C and IBS-D exhibit divergent psychophysiological signatures. Specifically, IBS-C is predominantly associated with pronounced hostility and neurotic personality traits. Conversely, IBS-D is characterized by a specific combination of impaired top-down inhibitory control (prolonged prosaccade latency), altered autonomic function (reduced HRV medium-frequency power), and a deficit in positive coping strategies. These findings underscore the heterogeneous nature of IBS and highlight the predictive utility of integrating behavioral, psychological, and autonomic metrics.

Shared Psychophysiological Risk Factors At the overarching level, our models indicate that SCL-90 hostility, EPQ neuroticism, negative coping styles, and poor sleep quality (PSQI) are robust shared predictors for both IBS subtypes. These findings align with the biopsychosocial model of functional gastrointestinal disorders, which posits that emotional dysregulation and psychological stress are intricately linked to gut-brain axis dysfunction21–23. The consistent predictive value of neuroticism is in accordance with the observations of Mayer et al.24 and Osadchiy et al.25, supporting the concept that trait-level emotional instability may predispose individuals to visceral hypersensitivity. Furthermore, the significant predictive role of poor sleep quality reinforces existing literature suggesting a bidirectional relationship between sleep disruptions and gastrointestinal symptom exacerbation26–28. Importantly, it must be noted that the multivariable models yielded exceptionally large odds ratios with wide confidence intervals for hostility and negative coping. While these variables reliably demonstrate a strong positive association with IBS risk, these extreme estimates likely reflect data sparsity or the skewed distribution of these specific extreme traits within our sample. Therefore, the absolute magnitude of these effect sizes should be interpreted conservatively, focusing on their qualitative predictive value rather than their precise quantitative estimates.

Subtype-Specific Signatures: IBS-C Beyond the shared psychological burden, the risk profile for IBS-C was heavily dominated by emotional processing dimensions. The prominent association with the SCL-90 hostility factor and EPQ neuroticism suggests that individuals with IBS-C may experience heightened emotional reactivity and aggressive tendencies. Rather than inferring a direct causal pathogenesis, we hypothesize that these specific personality traits and emotional states may exacerbate psychological stress, which, through complex gut-brain signaling pathways, could be associated with altered gut transit times and constipation-predominant symptoms.

Subtype-Specific Signatures: IBS-D In contrast, the predictive model for IBS-D revealed a distinct pattern involving sensorimotor and autonomic alterations. Notably, prolonged prosaccade latency was identified as an independent predictor. Previous neuroimaging and cognitive studies have suggested that such oculomotor delays may reflect alterations in fundamental attention orientation and top-down inhibitory control13,29–32. While our behavioral data are consistent with these observations, it is crucial to emphasize that we did not directly assess neural substrates; thus, attributing these eye-tracking alterations to specific dysfunctions in the anterior cingulate-insular attention control networks remains a speculative hypothesis requiring future neuroimaging validation33,34. Additionally, the IBS-D subtype was uniquely predicted by a reduction in HRV medium-frequency (MF) power. While the MF band conceptually captures elements of sympathetically-mediated baroreflex activity15,35–37, definitive physiological interpretations regarding isolated sympathetic enhancement must be approached with caution. The autonomic profile observed in IBS-D more broadly suggests an overall depression of cardiac autonomic regulation. Furthermore, the distinct absence of positive coping strategies emerged as a significant risk factor for IBS-D, distinguishing it from IBS-C and highlighting a potential behavioral target for subtype-specific management.

Limitations and Future Directions The interpretation of our findings is subject to several important limitations. First, the cross-sectional design inherently precludes the determination of causal relationships; the identified biomarkers represent predictive associations rather than proven mechanistic pathways. Second, the recruitment of participants from a single tertiary medical center introduces potential selection bias, possibly skewing the cohort towards patients with more severe symptoms or higher psychological comorbidity, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader community settings. Third, the reliance on self-report instruments (e.g., SCL-90, SCSQ, EPQ, PSQI) introduces inherent subjectivity and the potential for reporting bias. Fourth, our physiological assessments (particularly HRV) may have been influenced by unmeasured confounding factors, including daily caffeine intake, recent physical exertion, precise dietary habits, and the potential residual effects of psychotropic or gastrointestinal medications, which were not comprehensively controlled for in the present analysis. Finally, our study was restricted to the IBS-C and IBS-D subtypes; the exclusion of mixed-type (IBS-M) and unclassified (IBS-U) patients constrains the completeness of our proposed risk stratification framework.

Future prospective, multi-center cohort studies are imperative to validate these predictive models externally and establish temporal causality. Such studies should enforce rigorous control over lifestyle and pharmacological confounders and consider integrating multidimensional data, including neuroimaging and microbiome profiles, to comprehensively delineate the biological underpinnings of IBS subtype divergence.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that specific psychological traits (including hostility, neuroticism, and negative coping styles) and sleep disturbances are robust shared predictive factors associated with both IBS-C and IBS-D. Furthermore, our multimodal approach highlights subtype-specific predictive signatures: IBS-C is predominantly associated with heightened emotional reactivity, whereas IBS-D is uniquely predicted by a combination of delayed attention orientation, depressed autonomic regulation, and a distinct deficit in positive coping strategies. While the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, these findings underscore the psychophysiological heterogeneity of IBS subtypes and highlight the potential utility of integrating behavioral, psychological, and autonomic metrics for risk stratification. Clinically, these predictive models suggest that comprehensive patient evaluations incorporating mental health, coping styles, and sleep quality may be valuable. Future prospective and longitudinal studies are warranted to externally validate these profiles and to determine whether subtype-differentiated interventions—such as targeted emotion management for IBS-C and behavioral coping enhancement for IBS-D—can effectively improve patient outcomes.
