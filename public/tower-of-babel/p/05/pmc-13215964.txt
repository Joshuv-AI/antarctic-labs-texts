Two orthobunyaviruses: OYAV and EBIV co-opt the AhR-CYP1A1 axis to suppress type I interferon responses
Authors: Qianyun Hu, Fei Wang, Xiaoyu Wang, Xiaokui Li, Zhiming Yuan, Maohua Zhong, Han Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The type I interferon (IFN-I) system serves as a frontline defense against viral infection, yet how orthobunyaviruses counteract this pathway remains poorly defined. Here, we identify cytochrome P450 1A1 (CYP1A1) as a crucial host factor promoting infection by two emerging orthobunyaviruses—Oya virus (OYAV) and Ebinur Lake virus (EBIV). Transcriptomic and functional analyses demonstrate that CYP1A1 overexpression enhances viral RNA synthesis, whereas its CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout attenuates infection. Mechanistically, OYAV and EBIV activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), driving its nuclear translocation and subsequent upregulation of CYP1A1. Deficiency of CYP1A1 potentiates IFN-β production and interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression, while its overexpression suppressed antiviral signaling, revealing an immunomodulatory role that is distinct from its canonical metabolic function. Collectively, this work defines the AhR-CYP1A1 axis as a conserved immune-evasion module exploited by emerging orthobunyaviruses and highlights the innate immune pathway as a potential therapeutic target against these emerging threats.

Introduction

The genus Orthobunyavirus (family Peribunyaviridae, order Elliovirales) encompasses over 120 species of arthropod-borne viruses with a global distribution and significant zoonotic potential (de Souza et al., 2024; Kuhn et al., 2021; Peinado et al., 2022). These viruses pose a growing public health threat, capable of causing a spectrum of clinical diseases ranging from acute febrile illness and pediatric encephalitis to severe hemorrhagic fever and congenital anomalies. The recent expansion of Oropouche virus (OROV) in South America and its link to fetal microcephaly underscores the urgent need to decipher orthobunyaviral pathogenesis and immune evasion strategies (Ding et al., 2025; Dutuze et al., 2018; Harding et al., 2019; Sakkas et al., 2018). Climate change and globalization further amplify the risk of outbreaks from emerging orthobunyaviruses, including recently identified but neglected species such as Oya virus (OYAV) and Ebinur Lake virus (EBIV). OYAV, first isolated in Malaysia in 2000 and subsequently detected in febrile patients across Asia, belongs to the Simbu serogroup (Kono et al., 2002). EBIV, discovered in Culex mosquitoes in China in 2014, is a member of the Bunyamwera serogroup and has shown seroprevalence in exposed human populations (Liu et al., 2014). Together, these viruses exemplify the dual challenges of viral evolution and ecological shifts in driving pathogen emergence.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a ligand-activated transcription factor, plays a central role in sensing both environmental toxins (e.g., dioxins, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons) and endogenous metabolites (Vogel et al., 2020). In its canonical pathway, ligand binding triggers AhR translocation to the nucleus, where it dimerizes with the AhR nuclear translocator (ARNT) to drive expression of target genes, most notably cytochrome P450 1A1 (CYP1A1) (Dong and Perdew, 2020; Schiering et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020). Traditionally studied for its role in xenobiotic metabolism, in which it oxidizes hydrophobic compounds for detoxification or activation, the AhR-CYP1A1 axis is now recognized as a critical modulator of viral immune evasion. Multiple viruses, including HIV and influenza, hijack this pathway to suppress host defenses: for instance, nuclear AhR can bind to interferon regulatory factor (IRF) promoters to inhibit type I interferon (IFN-I) production, and HIV Vpr protein drives AhR activation to maintain viral latency (Gutiérrez-Vázquez and Quintana, 2018; Wang et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2019). Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting this axis, such as with AhR antagonists (e.g., CH223191) or natural ligands (e.g., indole-3-carbinol), are under active investigation.

The AhR-CYP1A1 axis may influence viral success through several interconnected mechanisms: (1) Direct transcriptional regulation: AhR can bind to specific response elements (e.g., xenobiotic response elements, XREs) within viral genomes or host dependency factors, potentially upregulating transcription in a manner favorable for viral replication (Frauenstein et al., 2015; Ohashi et al., 2018). (2) Metabolic reprogramming: as a key metabolic effector downstream of AhR, CYP1A1 activation can alter the cellular redox state (e.g., by generating reactive oxygen species) and reshape energy metabolism, thereby indirectly affecting the host transcriptional machinery and resource availability for viruses (Bahman et al., 2024; Stading et al., 2020). (3) Suppression of antiviral immunity: sustained AhR activation has been shown to broadly inhibit antiviral signaling pathways, particularly the IFN-I response. By dampening the transcription of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), this axis can weaken intrinsic cellular defenses, clearing a path for efficient viral transcription and replication (Gutiérrez-Vázquez and Quintana, 2018; Liu X. et al., 2024; Schiering et al., 2017; Yamada et al., 2016). In summary, through a combination of direct gene regulation, metabolic manipulation, and immune suppression, the AhR-CYP1A1 axis can foster a cellular microenvironment conducive to viral persistence.

Transcriptomic approaches have revolutionized the study of virus-host interactions by mapping dynamic transcriptional changes during infection (Kohler and Tisserant, 2014; Wang et al., 2009, 2022). In this study, we integrated transcriptomic profiling with functional validation to investigate the interplay between emerging orthobunyaviruses and host pathways. This approach identified CYP1A1 as a critical host factor supporting OYAV and EBIV infection. Further mechanistic dissection revealed that both viruses co-opt the AhR-CYP1A1 axis to suppress IFN-I production and ISG activation, thereby facilitating immune escape. Our findings not only expand the known repertoire of orthobunyavirus immune evasion tactics but also highlight the AhR-CYP1A1 axis as a promising host-directed therapeutic target. By bridging cutting-edge omics with classical virological assays, this work provides a framework for understanding the pathogenesis of emerging orthobunyaviruses and informs future antiviral development.

Following classification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we implemented a screening strategy focused exclusively on up-regulated candidates. This involved: (1) identifying common DEGs across different MOI infections within the same cell lines; (2) cross-referencing these with DEGs from identically treated cells of another type, and (3) selecting only DEGs with log2FC > 1 (Fig. 1C). This systematic pipeline identified 41 strongly upregulated host genes, 28 of which were successfully cloned (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S1). Transient transfection of these constructs into 293T cells followed by viral challenge (24 hpi) enabled functional screening. The first-round quantification of viral RNA copies (Supplementary Fig. S1A) identified nine candidates associated with viral loads 2.5-fold lower or 1.5-fold higher than those of the control group.

To validate these hits, we performed transient overexpression of these nine candidates in HeLa cells (PLD6 was excluded due to unsuccessful detection; Fig. 1D, left panel). Subsequent infection with OYAV (MOI = 0.1) or EBIV (MOI = 0.1) revealed that CYP1A1 overexpression markedly enhanced both OYAV and EBIV infection at 24 hpi (Fig. 1D, right panel). Notably, the other genes that significantly affected viral infection in 293T cells showed no effect when overexpressed in HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A vs Fig. 1D). This functional divergence likely reflects cell type-specific regulatory mechanisms. However, CYP1A1 overexpression consistently enhanced viral infection across both models. This conserved phenotype warrants further investigation of CYP1A1's role in viral infection.

CYP1A1 is an important host factor for OYAV and EBIV infection

To investigate the role of CYP1A1, we generated HeLa cell lines stably overexpressing CYP1A1 (Fig. 2A). Both viral RNA levels and progeny virus titers at 24 hpi were significantly higher in CYP1A1-overexpressing cells than in control cells for OYAV and EBIV (P < 0.001; Fig. 2A and B). Conversely, loss-of-function studies using CYP1A1-knockout HepG2 cells (Fig. 2C and D) showed a profound inhibition of viral replication, with viral RNA levels reduced by 10–20 fold and progeny virus titers decreased by 5–10 fold (P < 0.001). Viral infection was restored following reintroduction of wild-type CYP1A1 into the knockout cells (Fig. 2E and F), confirming the specificity of the phenotype. To further assess CYP1A1's antiviral relevance, we tested two low-toxicity pharmacological inhibitors—Ethinylestradiol (EE) and Alpha-Naphthoflavone (ANF)—that competitively inhibit its enzymatic activity (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Both compounds effectively suppressed OYAV and EBIV infection (Fig. 2H, Supplementary Fig. S2B). Together, these results demonstrate that CYP1A1 supports orthobunyavirus replication and that this function depends on its enzymatic activity.

Then, whether primary transcription was affected by CYP1A1 was assessed by measuring viral RNA levels at early time points post-infection in the presence or absence of cycloheximide (CHX). CHX-mediated inhibition of protein synthesis abolishes OYAV and EBIV secondary transcription and genome replication activity, which relies on newly synthesized nucleoprotein (NP) monomers, thereby fully inhibiting new viral genome (vRNA, negative-sense RNA) and the second-round positive-sense RNA (cRNA and mRNA) synthesis (Huang et al., 2011). In CHX-untreated cells, OYAV and EBIV RNA levels were insensitive to CYP1A1 at 3 hpi but decreased nearly 10 fold by 6 hpi (Fig. 3B, right panels). In contrast, in CHX-treated cells, OYAV and EBIV RNA levels remained insensitive to CYP1A1 at both 3 and 6 hpi (Fig. 3B, left panels). The results above suggested that CYP1A1 does not play a role in the primary transcription of OYAV and EBIV.

Given that this effect emerges as early as 3–6 hpi, it appears premature to attribute it to robust viral genome replication. With a viral life cycle of approximately 24 h, where genome replication, similar to that of other negative-sense RNA viruses, is delayed until 12–24 hpi. The observed effect is more likely attributable to an impact on secondary transcription. To test this hypothesis, we measured viral vRNA and positive-sense RNA at 6 hpi using RT-qPCR. Results showed no significant difference in vRNA production (Fig. 3C, left panel), but a significant difference in newly synthesized positive-sense RNA (Fig. 3C, right panel), indicating that CYP1A1 influences infection by modulating viral secondary transcription.

Furthermore, to rule out the possibility that differences in virus budding efficiency affected the results, we measured viral copies in the supernatant and corresponding cells at 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpi. Virus production in the supernatant was not detected until 24 hpi, but differences in viral replication within cells were evident as early as 6 hpi (Fig. 3D), indicating that the observed differences in viral load were not due to multiple rounds of viral infection.

OYAV and EBIV infection activates the AhR-CYP1A1 axis

CYP1A1 expression can be regulated through both the canonical AhR-dependent pathway and various non-canonical mechanisms involving transcription factors such as Nrf2, HIF-1α, CAR/PXR, or inflammatory cytokines (Bakleh and Al Haj Zen, 2025; Jung et al., 2010; Kyoreva et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2007; Tian et al., 2020). To determine whether the observed proviral role of CYP1A1 is linked to the AhR pathway, we first examined its expression during infection. In AhR-expressing cell lines, infection with OYAV or EBIV significantly increased both mRNA and protein levels of CYP1A1, while AhR expression itself remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S3A–E). Genetic knockdown of AhR in HepG2 cells abolished the virus-induced upregulation of CYP1A1 mRNA (Fig. 4A). Pharmacological inhibition of AhR with CH-223191 similarly suppressed basal and infection-induced CYP1A1 transcription in both HepG2 and HeLa cells (Fig. 4B), and effectively inhibited viral replication (Supplementary Fig. S3F). To explore how infection triggers AhR activation, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis on transcriptomic data from infected cells. Both viruses induced broad reprogramming of host metabolic pathways (Fig. 4C and D, Supplementary Fig. S3G and S3H). Notably, key enzymes in the tryptophan metabolism pathway (ALDH8A and TPH1) were significantly enriched. Since tryptophan metabolites are established endogenous AhR ligands (Dong and Perdew, 2020; Zelante et al., 2013), we hypothesized that OYAV and EBIV may alter tryptophan metabolism to generate ligands that activate AhR, leading to CYP1A1 induction.

We next directly assessed AhR activation by examining its subcellular localization. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that, although baseline levels of AhR were detectable in the nucleus under uninfected conditions, an increase in nuclear accumulation was observed in infected cells compared to uninfected cells, suggesting stimulus-induced nuclear translocation of the protein (Fig. 4E and F). Quantitative analysis within the same field showed a higher nuclear AhR signal in successfully infected cells than in uninfected neighboring cells (Fig. 4G). This was confirmed by Western blot analysis of cellular fractions: while cytoplasmic AhR levels were unchanged, nuclear AhR increased significantly following OYAV or EBIV infection (Fig. 4H). These results indicate that both viruses promote AhR nuclear translocation, thereby driving CYP1A1 transcription.

The AhR-CYP1A1 axis suppresses type I interferon responses

Previous studies have implicated the AhR-CYP1A1 axis in the regulation of interferon signaling (Ohashi et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2023; Yamada et al., 2016). To define its role in our system, we first examined the activation of core IFN pathway components. In CYP1A1-knockout (KO) HepG2 cells infected with EBIV, we observed robust and sustained phosphorylation of both TBK1 and STAT1 at 24 h post-infection, a response that was markedly attenuated in wild-type cells (Fig. 5A and B). Consistent with enhanced upstream signaling, RNA-seq analysis of EBIV-infected CYP1A1-KO versus wild-type cells revealed significant upregulation of 84 immune-related genes, including a focused enrichment of 11 genes directly linked to the type I IFN pathway (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. S4A). qRT-PCR validation confirmed that CYP1A1 deficiency led to significant induction of IFN-β and classic interferon-stimulated genes (IFIT3, IFIT5, IFI16, IFI44, IFITM1, OASL, OAS2, RIG-I, STAT2, IFIT1, CXCL10, and OAS1), which correlated with suppressed viral replication (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Fig. S4B). Conversely, CYP1A1 overexpression reduced the expression of these antiviral genes and increased viral yield (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

To determine whether the proviral effect of CYP1A1 is specifically mediated through modulation of the IFN-I response, we turned to Vero cells, which lack functional type I IFN signaling. In this IFN-deficient background, neither stable overexpression of CYP1A1 (Fig. 5E, left) nor its pharmacological inhibition with Ethinylestradiol or Alpha-Naphthoflavone (Fig. 5F) affected the replication of OYAV or EBIV (Fig. 5E, right). These results confirm that the proviral function of the AhR-CYP1A1 axis is strictly dependent on a functional host IFN-I pathway.

Based on these findings, we propose a model (Fig. 5G) wherein OYAV or EBIV, upon cellular entry, may activate AhR and induce its nuclear translocation by rearranging the tryptophan pathway. The nuclear translocation of AhR subsequently upregulates the expression of CYP1A1, which inhibits the phosphorylation of TBK1 and STAT through an unknown mechanism, thereby dampening the type I interferon response. By co-opting this host regulatory axis to evade innate immunity, these viruses establish an intracellular environment favorable for their replication.

CYP1A1 protein exhibits broad-spectrum activity

To determine whether the proviral role of CYP1A1 extends beyond orthobunyaviruses, we examined its effect on phylogenetically diverse viruses. We selected two additional members of the class Bunyaviricetes—Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV; Phenuiviridae) and severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV; Phenuiviridae)—as well as three more distantly related flaviviruses: Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), Zika virus (ZIKV), and Tembusu virus (TMUV) (all Flaviviridae). Depletion or pharmacological inhibition of CYP1A1 reduced infection by all five viruses, although the effect was notably weaker than that observed for OYAV and EBIV (Fig. 6A and B). Thus, while CYP1A1 can support the replication of diverse RNA viruses, its specific induction and co-option for interferon suppression may represent a distinct immune-evasion strategy evolved by orthobunyaviruses.

Discussion

This study identifies CYP1A1 as a critical host factor that facilitates OYAV and EBIV infection through an AhR-dependent mechanism, which was functionally validated by both gain- and loss-of-function experiments. Transcriptomic profiling revealed consistent upregulation of CYP1A1 during early infection. Crucially, AhR knockout or pharmacological inhibition abolished the viral induction of CYP1A1, while immunofluorescence and subcellular fractionation confirmed virus-promoted nuclear translocation of AhR—a prerequisite for its transcriptional activity. These findings establish the AhR-CYP1A1 axis as a conserved pathway exploited by two phylogenetically distinct orthobunyaviruses.

Extensive evidence highlights the proviral role of AhR in diverse viral infections, including hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), and influenza A virus (IAV) (Chatterjee et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023; Kashuba et al., 2006; Ohashi et al., 2018; Xu L. et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2016). While the AhR-CYP1A1 axis represents a relatively non-canonical immune-regulatory pathway compared with well-characterized systems such as RIG-I or cGAS-STING, its mechanisms are becoming increasingly delineated.

AhR not only responds to environmental toxins but is also involved in a variety of physiological processes, such as immune regulation, cell cycle control, and metabolism. These functions may be driven by endogenous ligands (e.g., tryptophan metabolites, dietary components, cytokines) or ligand-independent mechanisms, such as crosstalk with other signaling pathways, leading to a basal level of nuclear localization. Viruses may activate AhR through direct or indirect routes: certain viral proteins or nucleic acids can bind AhR directly (Hu et al., 2023; Kashuba et al., 2006; Ohashi et al., 2018; Xu L. et al., 2024), or alternatively, infection-induced inflammation or metabolites (e.g., kynurenine from tryptophan catabolism) may serve as endogenous ligands (Liu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2021; Xu L. et al., 2024). Our KEGG analysis revealed enrichment of tryptophan metabolism genes during OYAV/EBIV infection, suggesting that AhR activation likely occurs through indirect, metabolite-mediated signaling rather than direct viral NP and nonstructural (NSs) protein interaction (Supplementary Fig. S5). Future metabolomic and interactome studies will help clarify the precise activation mechanism employed by orthobunyaviruses.

Once activated, the AhR-ARNT complex binds xenobiotic response elements (XREs) in the CYP1A1 promoter, driving its expression. The AhR-CYP1A1 axis may influence viral replication through multiple mechanisms, including metabolic reprogramming (Munn et al., 2005), suppression of type I interferon (IFN-I) responses (Gutiérrez-Vázquez and Quintana, 2018; Wang et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2016), and facilitation of specific steps in the viral life cycle (Chatterjee et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023; Ohashi et al., 2018). IFN-I suppression by AhR involves several potential mechanisms: direct transcriptional interference at IFN promoters (Kimura et al., 2009; Nguyen et al., 2010); inhibition of IRF3/7 and NF-κB activity (Huai et al., 2014; Sondermann et al., 2023); modulation of STAT signaling (Liu X. et al., 2024; Sondermann et al., 2023); regulation of upstream adaptors such as MAVS, STING, and TBK1 (Ma et al., 2023; Martin et al., 2024); and altered function of IFN-producing immune cells (Jin et al., 2010).

Our data specifically link CYP1A1 to the suppression of IFN-I signaling. While we cannot exclude that CYP1A1-derived metabolites may exert direct antiviral or immunomodulatory effects (Stading et al., 2020), the loss of CYP1A1's proviral activity in IFN-deficient Vero cells strongly argues that its primary role here is mediated through interference with the host IFN-I pathway. This extends the functional paradigm of the AhR-CYP1A1 axis beyond lipid metabolism to include direct antagonism of innate antiviral immunity.

By shifting the focus from AhR to its downstream effector CYP1A1, our work provides novel insights into orthobunyaviral immune evasion. Previous studies have emphasized AhR's immunoregulatory roles, but we demonstrate that CYP1A1 itself can directly dampen IFN-I responses, highlighting a previously underappreciated layer of this host pathway. This not only advances our understanding of orthobunyavirus pathogenesis but also nominates CYP1A1 as a potential host-directed antiviral target.

Targeting host factors such as CYP1A1 aligns with emerging strategies for developing broad-spectrum, resistance-resistant antivirals. However, careful evaluation of on-target physiological effects will be essential for therapeutic development. Further studies should clarify the precise molecular interface between CYP1A1 activity and the IFN-I cascade, which may reveal additional regulatory nodes for antiviral intervention.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identifies CYP1A1 as a critical host factor that promotes OYAV and EBIV infection. Mechanistically, we reveal that these emerging orthobunyaviruses co-opt the AhR-CYP1A1 axis to suppress type I interferon responses, thereby facilitating viral replication. These findings provide novel insights into virus-host interactions and highlight a potential target for host-directed antiviral strategies.

Materials and Methods

Plasmids

The plasmids pMD2.G (#12259), pSPAX2 (#12260), lentiCRISPR V2 (#52961) were kindly provided by Professor Yanyi Wang from Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. pTRIP-Flag-RFP plasmid was provided by Professor Shengsong Xie from Huazhong Agricultural University.

The plasmids pMD2.G (#12259), pSPAX2 (#12260), lentiCRISPR V2 (#52961), and pTRIP-Flag-RFP were used for gene knockout or complementation in cells. The pTRIP-Flag-RFP plasmid was also employed for exogenous protein expression.

Cell culture

In this study, African green monkey kidney cells (Vero), baby hamster kidney (BHK-21) and Henrietta lacks (HeLa) are from the National Virus Resource Center (NVRC). human hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) and human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cell lines were kindly provided by Professor Yanyi Wang from the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. These cells were all used for subsequent experiments. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. They were grown in Dulbecco's minimal essential medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) (4.5 g/L d-glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Moregate, Australia) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA).

Transient transfection

HeLa cells were seeded in 12-well plates. When the cells reached 50%–60% confluence, transfection was performed. According to the manufacturer's instructions for Neofect transfection reagent (NEOFECT, China), an appropriate amount of transfection reagent and 1 μg of the overexpression plasmid (pTIRP-Flag-RFP-gene) were added to 100 μL of Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA) reduced serum medium. The mixture was gently pipetted and left undisturbed for 10 min before being added to the cells. Fluorescence was observed after 24 h to ensure transfection efficiency.

Virus propagation and titration

Oya virus (OYAV, strain SZC50), Ebinur Lake virus (EBIV, strain Cu20-XJ) were isolated and propagated in BHK-21 cells as previously described (Meng et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2020). Their titers were determined using the plaque formation assay, and the working stocks were found to be 3 × 106 plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL) and 3.3 × 107 PFU/mL, respectively.

JEV (strain HN-QH23-Ct-13) and TMUV (strain HN-TC23-Cu-03) were isolated from field mosquito samples by our group (Wu et al., 2024). RVFV (vaccine strain MP12) and ZIKV (FSS13025) was kindly provided by Prof. Chao Shan. SFTSV (strain JS-2020) was kindly provided by Prof. Zhenhua Zheng respectively.

Virus titration

Titration of the virus was measured by plaque assays. Briefly, confluent monolayers of BHK-21 cells in 12-well plates were infected with 100 mL serial dilutions of supernatant at 37 °C for 1 h. After removal of the inoculum, cells were overlaid with DMEM containing 2% FBS and 1% carboxymethyl cellulose (Sigma). After 3 days of incubation, the cells were stained with 1% crystal violet after being fixed with 4% formaldehyde. The plaques were counted and quantified, and the virus titers were expressed in PFU per milliliter.

Viral infection

The virus was diluted in medium containing 2% serum at an appropriate MOI, incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, washed twice with PBS to remove surface-adsorbed viruses, and then replaced with fresh medium containing 10% serum for continued cell culture. Between 0 and 12 h post-infection, the virus undergoes adsorption, transcription, and replication within the cells, while progeny virus production initiates after 12 h. Based on this lifecycle, appropriate time points were selected for validation experiments.

Infection dose assays and infection kinetics assays were performed on HepG2 and HeLa cells using OYAV, EBIV. The low MOI of 0.01 at 24 hpi and the medium MOI of 0.1 at 24 hpi were selected as time points for functional validation experiments. For investigating related mechanistic studies, an MOI of 1 at 24 hpi was chosen. Specific infection conditions can be found in the figure legends.

Primary viral RNA synthesis, secondary transcription and viral genome replication assays

To distinguish between primary viral transcription (de novo synthesis of viral RNA dependent on virion-delivered polymerases), secondary transcription (dependent on newly synthesized viral proteins) and replication, this study performed time-course experiments with cycloheximide (CHX, a well-known translation elongation inhibitor) treatment as previously described (Huang et al., 2011; Pinto et al., 2023). Cycloheximide (CHX) prevents the translation of newly synthesized NP protein in virus-infected cells, thereby blocking secondary transcription and replication. However, since the virus itself contains a small amount of RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP), CHX does not affect primary transcription.

For detection of primary viral transcription of the virus NP gene, non-target control (NTC) and gene knockout cells were pretreated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide (MCE, China) in complete medium for 1 h at 37 °C to block host protein synthesis. Cells were infected with EBIV or OYAV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 in CHX-containing medium for 3 h and 6 h.

For detection of viral secondary transcription and replication, cells were infected with OYAV and EBIV (MOI = 1) for 6 h. After that, cells were harvested for RNA extraction and reverse transcription for qPCR. Characterize the level of secondary transcription using virus-generated positive-sense RNA (cRNA and mRNA). For detecting viral positive-sense RNA, reverse transcription was performed with qOYAV/EBIV-R, amplified and quantified by qOYAV/EBIV-F/R. Characterize the level of replication using virus-generated vRNA. For detecting vRNA, reverse transcription was performed with qOYAV/EBIV-F, amplified and quantified by qOYAV/EBIV-F/R. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Lentivirus production and infection

HEK293T cells in 6-well plates were transfected with plasmids (Vector: pSPAX2: pMD2.G = 2.5:1.9:1.25, total amount of 5.65 mg) along with 3-fold volumes of TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Mirus, USA). After 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium. The supernatant was collected after 36–48 h and then filtered through a 0.22 μm nitrocellulose membrane (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates. When cells reached 50%–60% confluence, the packaged lentivirus was added with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma, USA). The cells were passaged every 48 h.

Knockout cell line generation

To generate knockout cells for specific genes, paired oligonucleotides of sgRNAs (Supplementary Table S1) were annealed and cloned into the Esp3I (NEB, USA) digested lentiCRISPR-V2 vector. Then, lentiviral production was carried out, followed by transduction into HepG2 cell lines. Two days later, cells were selected with puromycin (InvivoGen, USA) for an additional 6 days (1.5 mg/mL for HepG2 cells). Surviving cells were seeded in 96-well plates for limiting dilution and monoclonal isolation. CYP1A1 knockout was confirmed by Western blotting using an anti-CYP1A1 antibody (1:1000; Proteintech, China).

Complement cell line generation

To generate complement cell lines for specific genes and stably overexpress specific genes, a cDNA library was prepared from total RNA of HepG2 cells using the RNAiso extraction method (Takara, Japan) and reverse transcription (Vazyme, China). The coding regions of the target genes were amplified from the HepG2 cDNA library with a 15-nt homologous arm of the pTRIP-Flag-RFP vector. Next, the vectors were linked to the amplified coding regions via homologous recombination (Vazyme, China). Notably, one or two synonymous mutations were introduced into the coding region of CYP1A1 at the sgRNA-targeted PAM region. The constructed vectors were transfected into HEK293T cells for lentivirus production. Subsequently, the target gene knockout cell lines and HeLa cell lines were transduced with the packaged lentivirus for 4 days and then analyzed by flow cytometry to obtain the complemented cell lines and stable overexpressing cell lines. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA from HeLa and HepG2 cells (n = 3 biological replicates) were extracted using RNAiso (Takara, Japan). Libraries were prepared with NEBNext Ultra II Kit (NEB, USA) and sequenced on NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina, USA) in 150-bp paired-end mode (SeqHealth, Wuhan, China). Raw reads were aligned to hg38 using STAR (v2.7.10a), and differentially expressed genes identified with DESeq2 (|log2FC| > 1, FDR <0.05).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA was extracted from cell culture supernatant using an automated nucleic acid extraction system following the manufacturer's instructions (NanoMagBio, China). For RNA extraction from cells, the RNAiso extraction method (Takara, Japan) was employed.

Reverse transcription of 1 μg of RNA was performed using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting cDNA was non-specific, and primers were selected based on the detection requirements for subsequent qPCR. The two-step RT-qPCR method targets two distinct types of viral RNA. In brief, cDNA is specifically amplified using the 5′-qOYAV/EBIV-F/R primers listed in the Supplementary Table S1. The resulting cDNA is then subjected to real-time PCR using the same primers. Thus, this approach allows for the specific quantification of the viral genome (vRNA, negative-sense RNA) and the generated positive-sense RNA. The synthesis of cDNA was performed using 4 μL of extracted RNA in a PCR instrument with the HiScript IV 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative PCR was performed as previously described (Liu S. et al., 2024; Xu Z. et al., 2024). The threshold cycle (Ct) values of the target genes were normalized to that of the housekeeping gene GAPDH and presented as relative RNA levels. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Viral attachment and internalization assays

To quantitatively assess viral attachment efficiency and receptor-mediated endocytosis, we established a temperature-shift assay coupled with low-pH stripping, adapted from established protocols. This approach allows differentiation between surface-bound virions (4 °C attachment) and internalized particles (37 °C entry). For the viral binding assay, cells at 80%–90% confluence were incubated with EBIV or OYAV (MOI = 1) at 4 °C for 1 h. Following this incubation, cells were extensively washed with cold phosphate buffered saline (Gibco, USA) to remove unbound virus and then processed for RNA extraction. For the viral internalization assay, after the initial incubation with the virus at 4 °C, cells were shifted to 37 °C for an additional 1 h to allow virus internalization. To eliminate surface-bound virus, cells were treated with PBS at pH 3.0 and then lysed for RNA extraction.

To quantify the levels of viral RNA associated with binding and internalization, cells were harvested for RNA extraction. Reverse transcription was performed using Oligo dT primers, followed by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) with NP RNA-F/R and GAPDH-F/R primers. The RT-qPCR reactions were carried out according to standard protocols, and the results were analyzed to determine the relative levels of viral RNA. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure data reliability and reproducibility.

Western blotting

Cells were collected and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). The cell lysates were precipitated and separated by SDS-PAGE (GenScript, China), then transferred to a nitrocellulose filter membrane. Nitrocellulose filter membranes were rinsed briefly in PBS, blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 30 min and incubated with anti-AhR (1:2000; Proteintech, China); EBIV/OYAV nucleoprotein (NP) (1:200; custom-generated antibodies were used); anti-CYP1A1 (1:2000; Proteintech, China); anti-GAPDH (1:5000; Proteintech, China); anti-Lamin B1 (1:2000; Proteintech, China); anti-Flag (1:5000; Proteintech, China); anti-STAT1/p-STAT1 (1:2000; Selleck Chemicals, USA); anti-TBK1/p-TBK1 (1:2000; Abcam, USA) antibodies at room temperature for 2 h and visualized with horseradish peroxidase-coupled goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:2000; Proteintech, China). The results were imaged using an X-ray film imager.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining, 1.5 × 105 cells per well were seeded in a 12-well plate containing sterilized coverslips and incubated overnight. Cells were inoculated with EBIV at an MOI of 0.1 24 hpi or mock, cells were rinsed briefly in PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) at 4 °C for 20 min permeabilized with 0.3% Triton at 4 °C for 10 min, blocked with 1% BSA, and incubated with anti-AhR (1:200; Proteintech, China); EBIV nucleoprotein (NP) (1:200; custom-generated antibodies were used) or anti-CYP1A1 (1:200; Proteintech, China) antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Then, cells were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h (Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated goat anti-mouse, 1:1000; Invitrogen, USA; Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated goat anti-rabbit, 1:100; Proteintech, China). The cell nucleus was stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China). Images were captured using a Leica STELLARIS 8 FALCON DLS.

Inhibitor treatment of cell lines

Ethinylestradiol; Alpha-Naphthoflavone and CH223191 were purchased from MedChemExpress. HepG2 cells (1.5 × 105) and HeLa cells (1.5 × 105) were pretreated with 1 μM Ethinylestradiol; 10 μM Alpha-Naphthoflavone and 10 μM CH223191 for 1 h and then infected with the virus mentioned in the article.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Data analyses were performed with one way ANOVA and two-tailed t-test. Statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism software, version 10.0. All statistically analyzed pairwise comparisons are indicated with a bar and the P -value is represented by a symbol. (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.0001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.00001). P values of >0.05 were considered not significant and not annotated.
