Cholesterol biosynthesis as a drug-induced vulnerability in diffuse large B cell lymphoma insensitive to EZH2 inhibition
Authors: Rachele Niccolai, Camiel Göbel, Klevis Ndoj, Maaike Kreft, Hendrik J. Kuiken, Cor Lieftink, Ben Morris, Sietse D. Yska, Sebastian Hendrix, Bram van den Broek, Vincent Pappalardo, Marie José Kersten, Roderick L. Beijersbergen, Noam Zelcer, Fred van Leeuwen, Heinz Jacobs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•GCB-DLBCL insensitive to EZH2 inhibition become dependent on cholesterol synthesis.•This dependency relates to altered LDLR subcellular localization and LDL uptake.•EZH2 inhibition attenuates activation of SREBP2 targets.

Introduction

Diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is one of the most frequent and aggressive type of non-Hodgkin lymphomas [1]. While 60 % of patients diagnosed with follicular lymphoma (FL) or DLBCL are successfully treated by rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP) chemoimmunotherapy, a substantial 40 % is either refractory or relapses, identifying an unmet medical need [[2], [3], [4]]. Especially refractory or relapsed (R/R) DLBCLs often prove to be fatal, as only a small proportion of patients have durable response to either high-dose salvage chemotherapy and autologous stem cell transplantation, or chimeric antigen-receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. Other limitations relate to severe toxicity and high costs [[5], [6], [7]].

Germinal Center (GC) B cell-like DLBCL arises from and closely resemble the pro-proliferative differentiation state of GC B cells, wherein epigenetic alterations are pivotal in preventing the terminal differentiation into antibody secreting plasma cells [[8], [9]]. Notably, the histone H3K27 methyltransferase EZH2, the catalytic subunit of the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) responsible for histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3), is highly expressed in GCB cells [10] and plays a critical role in B cell differentiation, by silencing genes involved in cell cycle checkpoint control and terminal plasma cell differentiation [8,11]. The dependency of antigen activated B cells on EZH2 to support the pro-proliferative identity of GCB cells underlies in the failure of Ezh2-KO mice to establish GCs [11].

In recent years, epigenetic-targeted therapies have gained attention in cancer treatments, as the aberrant expression and activity of epigenetic modifiers often contribute to tumor development [12,13]. In follicular lymphoma (FL) and DLBCL, gain-of-function mutations in EZH2 are frequently found (22 %), resulting in the repression of cell cycle checkpoints and differentiation [14,15]. Accordingly, several inhibitors have been developed to block the methyltransferase activity of EZH2, including the FDA-approved EZH2 inhibitor (EZH2i) Tazemetostat. Despite its proven clinical efficacy, the overall response rate to EZH2i was 69 % in EZH2 gain-of-function and 35 % in EZH2 wild-type follicular lymphoma [16]. These clinical insights highlight a suboptimal treatment efficacy associated with single agent therapies, indicating the need to uncover novel vulnerabilities induced by EZH2i that may be exploited as therapeutic targets to overcome resistance or insensitivity to EZH2i.

Recently, our group and others have identified that the malignant behavior of GCB-DLBCL strongly depends on the activity of the epigenetic writers DOT1L and EZH2 [17,18]. Here, co-targeting of DOT1L and EZH2 prohibited the outgrowth of human GCB-DLBCL xenografts in vivo, highlighting the potential of combined epi‑drugging as an alternative treatment for patients with GCB-DLBCL [17]. While the majority of GCB-DLBCL respond to the combined inhibition of DOT1L and EZH2, a proportion of EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines remain non-responsive to this combination [17].

To uncover novel vulnerabilities capable of sensitizing the EZH2i-insensitive subset, we performed an unbiased EZH2i-anchored genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 drop-out screen. Here, we identified genes involved in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway to be essential for EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines upon EZH2i exposure. Interestingly, we identified a critical role for EZH2 in preserving both exogenous and endogenous cholesterol sources. EZH2 inhibition altered the cellular localization of low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), resulting in impaired uptake of cholesterol-containing LDL particles. Surprisingly, this reduced LDL uptake did not induce SREBP2-mediated activation of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. Despite this critical condition, the inefficacy of EZH2i to compromise cell viability indicated that cellular cholesterol availability remained sufficient. However, cell growth was compromised upon co-targeting of cholesterol biosynthesis and EZH2, in vitro and in vivo.

This study uncovers an EZH2i-induced metabolic dependency in EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL. Simultaneous targeting of the epigenome and cellular lipid metabolism exhibited a synergistic effect in GCB-DLBCLs insensitive to EZH2 inhibition. Given the efficacy of clinically approved cholesterol synthesis inhibitors, such as statins, this targetable vulnerability may ultimately contribute to improved EZH2i-based treatments for GCB-DLBCL patients and potentially other tumors.

Methods

Cell lines and reagents

GCB-DLBCL cell lines were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco Medium (Thermofisher scientific, cat. No. 12440053) supplemented with 8 % fetal calf serum (Capricon Scientific), 1 % glutamax (Gibco, 35050061) and penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. No. P4333). Cells were maintained at 37°C and 5 % CO2. GSK343 (HY-13500), YM-53601 (HY-100313A), Ro 48-8071

(HY-18630A), Lenalidomide (HY-A0003) and Tazemetostat (HY-13803), GSK126 (HY-13470) were obtained from MedChemExpress, Atorvastatin (S2077) was purchased from Selleckchem.

Dose response curves

A semi-automated seeding method was used to determine the dose-response curves and synergistic potential. 5 × 10^4 cells/ml were reseeded with growth medium every four days until day 12 using a robotics protocol (Hamilton CO-RE96 Platform). During each passage, GSK343 (GSK), YM-53601 (YM), Ro 48-8071 (RO), and Atorvastatin (AT) were added using a drug dispenser (HP Tecan digital dispenser). Cells were stained with DAPI to determine cell viability by Flow cytometry.

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen

Oci-Ly8 Cas9 cells were transduced with the lentiviral Brunello whole, human genome guide RNA library (Addgene, 73178) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.3, resulting in an average gRNA coverage of approximately 500-fold. Transduced cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for three days, after which reference samples (T0) of 40 million cells each were harvested, washed with PBS and stored as pellets at −20°C until DNA extraction. The remaining cells were split into two experimental arms (each represented by three replicates): 5 μM of GSK343 or DMSO vehicle control. An appropriate number of cells to maintain, on average, a 500-fold representation of the library were reseeded every two days with fresh medium containing GSK343 or DMSO. Cells were harvested after 8-10 population doublings, washed with PBS, and stored at −20°C as pellet until DNA extraction. Guide RNA sequences were isolated and amplified by two rounds of PCR as described by van der Noord and colleagues19. The products of the second PCR were pooled by combining 400 ng of each sample. This pool was sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq system with the High-Output Kit (75 bp, single read).

Dil-LDL uptake

Oci-Ly8 and SUDHL4 cell lines were treated with DMSO and GSK343 (5 μM) for six days. After Qive days, cells were washed with PBS and incubated overnight at 37°C in medium containing 10 % lipoprotein deficient serum and fresh inhibitors. The day after, cells were incubated at 37°C for one hour with 5 μg/mL Dil-LDL (Kalen Biomedical, cat. No. 770230-9). Cells were washed twice with PBS, and the cellular uptake of Dil-LDL was analyzed by Flow cytometry.

Rescue experiment – Water soluble cholesterol

Oci-Ly8 and SUDHL4 cell lines were treated for six days with 0.05 % (v/v) DMSO, GSK (5 μM), RO (35 nM and 100 nM), and combination of GSK and RO. At day 0 and day 3, 50 μg/mL of water soluble cholesterol (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. No. C4951) was added to the cells. After six days, cells were stained with DAPI to measure the number of viable cells by Flow cytometry.

Bioinformatic analysis CRISPR-Cas9 screen

The sequence count data were normalized using a relative total size factor. Statistical comparison of the conditions treated (GSK) versus untreated (DMSO) was performed using drugZ20. Log2fold changes were calculated based on the mean of each of the two conditions. The first criterion for hit selection was a drugZ FDR smaller than or equal to 0.1 in the treated/untreated comparison. In addition, log2fold change of treated/t0 and the difference of this value with log2 of untreated/t0 smaller or equal to −1 were considered.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Oci-Ly8 and SUDHL4 cell lines were treated with 5 μM GSK343 or DMSO. After five days, cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 20 hours at 37°C in either normal medium (IMDM) or medium containing 10 % lipoprotein deficient serum (LPDS) and fresh inhibitors. The day after, cells were lysed in RLT buffer (Qiagen). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the xGen polyA+ stranded RNA library kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Paired-end 54 bp sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000, on a SP Flowcell. FASTQ Qiles were produced by BCL Convert using demultiplexing. Sample distribution was based on indexing using IDT UDI indexes. Seqpurge was used for adapter trimming. RNA-seq data were aligned with STAR against reference genome GRCh38, and reads were counted and annotated per gene using GenSum (https://www.github.com/NKI-GCF/gensum) against Ensembl GRCh38.107. DESeq2 package was used to normalize read counts and to obtain the differential gene expression of RNA-seq samples. Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under the accession number PRJEB98828.

In vivo tumor control

The 6- to 8-week-old nonobese diabetic (NOD)–Scid IL2Rgnull (Jackson Laboratory) mice, 50 % males/females, were injected subcutaneously in the right flank with 5 × 106 Oci-Ly1 and LSS-KO Oci-Ly1 (LSS-KO) cells in 50 % Matrigel (Corning) in phosphate-buffered saline. After xenotransplantation, mice were randomized to untreated control or 150 mg/kg GSK126 (20 % captisol). Treatment started once the tumors reached 100 mm3. Compound was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) once daily. Mice were sacrificed when tumors reached 1500 mm3.

Additional procedures and methods are detailed in the supplementary methods.

Results

CRISPR-Cas9 screen identifies cholesterol synthesis as an EZH2i-induced dependency in EZH2i-insensitive Oci-Ly8 cell line

To evaluate the efficacy of the EZH2 inhibitor GSK343 (GSK) in GCB-DLBCLs, we treated a panel of ten established GCB-DLBCL cell lines for 12 days. The dose response curves revealed that six out of ten GCB-DLBCL cell lines were sensitive to GSK (SUDHL5, Oci-Ly7, WSU-NHL, SUDHL6, SUDHL10, WSU-DLCL2) and four out of ten were insensitive to EZH2i (Oci-Ly1, Oci-Ly8, Oci-Ly18, SUDHL4) as previously reported [17] (Fig. 1A). To identify novel vulnerabilities that are induced upon EZH2 inhibition, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen in the GSK-insensitive Oci-Ly8 cell line treated with GSK. We opted for Oci-Ly8 cells as this cell line was found insensitive to previously published single and combination treatments with EPZ-5676 (Pinometostat; DOT1Li) and GSK343 (EZH2i) [17], as well as Lenalidomide (IKAROSi) and Tazemetostat (EZH2i) [19] (Fig S1A).

Cells stably expressing Cas9 were transduced with viral particles encoding sgRNAs from the Brunello whole-genome library [20] and selected with puromycin for three days (T0). Cells were exposed for eight days to either DMSO as control or 5 μM of GSK, the lowest concentration required to maximally reduce histone H3K27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) (Fig S2A). Comparing GSK- with DMSO-treated cells, we identified 35 genes as potential hits (Fig S1B,C). To explore the functional relationship between these hits, we performed STRING clustering analysis, which identified three major clusters: cholesterol biosynthesis, protein N-linked glycosylation, and ER-associated protein degradation (ERAD) (Fig. 1C). Given the relevance of cholesterol in cancer development [21,22], and the availability of clinically established cholesterol lowering compounds, we sought to further investigate the relevance of this metabolic pathway in GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCLs.

To validate the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway-associated hits, two gRNAs were designed to knock-out (KO) ACAT2 and LSS. Introducing these gRNAs resulted in the complete absence of their corresponding protein expression in the Oci-Ly8 cell line (Fig. 1D). In addition, the LSS-deficient Oci-Ly8 cells displayed a drastic reduction in cell number when cultured in lipoprotein-deficient serum (LPDS), confirming their dependence on exogenous lipoprotein, in line with their inability to synthesize cholesterol (Fig S1D). Additionally, GSK-treated Oci-Ly8 cell line with independent KOs of ACAT2 and LSS showed a reduction in cell number, further confirming its dependency on cholesterol biosynthesis upon GSK exposure as a novel vulnerability (Fig. 1E). Of note, two additional EZH2 inhibitors, GSK126 and Tazemetostat, were effective in reducing the growth of LSS KO as compared to the parental LSS proficient cell line (Fig S1E,F). These data suggest that EZH2 inhibition in GCB-DLBCL renders GSK-insensitive cell lines dependent on cholesterol biosynthesis.

Loss of EZH2 activity renders EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCLs sensitive to cholesterol synthesis inhibition

To test the potential of combining EZH2i with cholesterol synthesis inhibitors, we treated four GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines (Oci-Ly1, Oci-Ly8, Oci-Ly18, SUDHL4) with GSK in combination with either YM-53601 (YM, FDFT1 inhibitor) or Ro 48-8071 (RO, LSS inhibitor), which inhibit the activity of two cholesterol enzymes identified as hits in the CRISPR-Cas9 screen. Additionally, cells were treated with the widely used and FDA-approved atorvastatin (AT, HMG-CoA inhibitor) in combination with GSK (Fig. 2A) [23]. To determine the dose of GSK required to maximally reduced H3K27me3 levels, cells were exposed to varying concentrations of GSK for six days. Across all four cell lines tested, 5 μM of GSK was chosen to maximally reduce H3K27me3 levels while minimally affecting cell growth and was applied in combination studies with cholesterol synthesis inhibitors (Fig S2A). To evaluate the potential synergistic effect between cholesterol inhibitors and EZH2i in the GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines, we compared the observed combination response (green) to the expected combination response (grey), which was calculated by multiplying the effect based on cell number of each single treatment. A given measurement was considered synergistic if the observed response was lower than the expected response (+10 % error) (Fig S2C). Overall, combination of EZH2i and cholesterol inhibitors showed synergy in all four GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines analyzed.

To determine whether the reduced cell numbers was associated with increased cell death, we analyzed to what extent the drug combinations promoted apoptosis. We treated Oci-Ly8, SUDHL4, Oci-Ly18 and Oci-Ly1 with GSK in combination with either AT or RO and measured the frequency of Annexin V-positive cells. After six days of treatment, the percentage of Annexin V-positive cells significantly increased in the combination treatments as compared to single treatments (Fig. 2C and S2D-F), highlighting the relevance of this metabolic pathway as an inducible vulnerability in GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCLs. To determine if the induced dependency on cholesterol synthesis generally applies to all GCB-DLBCLs, we also tested the impact of LSS inhibitor RO on four GSK-sensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines. In this subset, different concentrations of EZH2i were used to maximally reduce H3K27me3. For Oci-Ly7, SUDHL6 and SUDHL10 EZH2i concentrations were chosen as previously reported [17]. In contrast to the other cell lines, WSU-DLCL2 showed a bimodal distribution of H3K27me3 upon EZH2 inhibition, therefore we chose the concentration able to maximally increase the H3K27me3-negative population (Fig S2G). In contrast to the GSK-insensitive cell lines, addition of RO to GSK had only a minor impact regarding the induction of apoptosis (Fig. 2C, Fig S2H). These analyses identify two metabolically distinct subsets of GCB-DLBCLs, of which only the GSK-insensitive subset can be further sensitized by inhibition of cholesterol biosynthesis.

LSS-deficiency sensitizes GCB-DLBCL xenograft insensitive to EZH2 inhibition

To determine the sensitivity of EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL in vivo, we first determined the efficacy of LSS inhibitor Ro 48-8071 in combination with EZH2i in a GCB-DLBCL xenograft models (NOD scid gamma mice). We observed that daily intravenous injections of 20 mg/kg Ro 48-8071 caused necrosis in the injection site. Therefore, we opted for an alternative approach by employing a xenotransplantation model using isogenic LSS-knockout Oci-Ly1 cells in NOD scid gamma mice (Fig S3). Upon engraftment, mice were randomized in control untreated or treatment groups with the EZH2 inhibitor GSK126 and measured the tumor volume over time by caliper (Fig. 3A). Tumor volumes were normalized relative to baseline measurements and log-transformed to model the natural exponential growth. To assess differential temporal dynamics between groups, the model incorporated time effects, interaction between time and treatment/cell line as well as the interaction between the three. This model revealed that GSK126 treatment significantly delayed tumor growth selectively in mice bearing LSS-deficient Oci-Ly1 cells compared to LSS-proficient Oci-Ly1 cells (Fig. 3B). In line, GSK126 treatment was effective in increasing tumor survival of LSS-knockout Oci-Ly1 bearing mice as compared to Oci-Ly1 GSK126-treated mice (Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.042) (Fig. 3C). EZH2 inhibition impairs LDLR compartmentalization and reduces LDL uptake in GCB- DLBCL

While usually cells acquire cholesterol predominantly via LDLR-mediated LDL uptake they can also synthesize cholesterol through the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway [24,25]. To determine whether the observed EZH2i-induced dependency on cholesterol biosynthesis results from attenuated LDL uptake, we measured the cell surface occupancy of the receptor and observed a reduction of approximately 50 % upon GSK exposure (Fig. 4A). Consistent with reduced surface LDLR abundance, we observed a marked decrease in LDL uptake (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, despite the reduced surface deposition, total LDLR protein levels were significantly increased in Oci-Ly8 and SUDHL4 upon GSK exposure (Fig. 4C,D), without LDLR transcriptional changes (Fig S4A). The discrepancy between total LDLR protein abundance in GSK-treated cells and the reduced surface occupancy of the receptor points towards aberrant trafficking of LDLR. Normally, after endocytic internalization of an LDL particle, LDLR either undergoes rapid recycling to the plasma membrane or is delivered to lysosomes for degradation, where cholesterol content is released for cellular use [[26], [27], [28]]. To assess the cellular compartment in which LDLR is retained upon GSK exposure, we determined the co-localization of the receptor with established endocytic markers. Consistent with previous observation, the acquired images and quantifications confirmed the increased levels of intracellular LDLR upon GSK exposure in Oci-Ly8 (Fig. 4F). Quantification of LDLR colocalization with early endosomes (RAB5), late endosomes (CD63), and lysosomes (LAMP1) revealed increased localization of the receptor to a LAMP1-positive compartment, accompanied by reduced co-localization with earlier endosomal markers in Oci-Ly8 cell line (Fig. 4E,G; Fig S4B,C). Together, our findings suggest that EZH2 inhibition affects LDL uptake by restricting LDLR surface expression, likely due to altered LDLR trafficking. In turn, the impaired LDLR-mediated LDL uptake likely explains the EZH2i-induced dependency on endogenous cholesterol synthesis in GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCLs.

EZH2 inhibition attenuates activation of SREBP2 targets and co-inhibition of EZH2 and cholesterol biosynthesis can be rescued by exogenous cholesterol

Cholesterol biosynthesis and uptake are under the transcriptional control of the transcription factor sterol regulatory element binding protein 2 (SREBP2) [29,30], which activation is controlled by the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane cholesterol content [31]. Given the impaired LDL uptake in GSK-treated GCB-DLBCLs, we reasoned that cells would require enhanced activation of the SREBP2 pathway to promote cholesterol biosynthesis and fulfill their cholesterol requirements. To investigate this possibility, we performed a transcriptomic analysis on the EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines and compared the transcriptional landscape between cell lines cultured in complete medium (i.e. IMDM containing 10 % FCS) or in medium containing lipoprotein-deprived serum (LPDS) in the presence or absence of GSK. As expected, untreated cells cultured in LPDS medium (i.e. sterol deprivation) led to enhanced expression of established SREBP2 targets in both SUDHL4 and Oci-Ly8 cells (Fig. 5A,B). However, GSK treatment limited the activation of SREBP2-regulated target genes, irrespective of the medium used. Under conditions of low ER cholesterol levels, SREBP2 is transported from the ER to the Golgi, where it is proteolytically cleaved to release its transcriptional active domain into the nucleus to activate its target genes [[31], [32], [33]]. The inability to transcriptionally activate SREBP2 targets upon GSK treatment may result from impaired cleavage of the SREBP2 protein to its mature and active form. Therefore, we analyzed the SREBP2 cleavage status in Oci-Ly8 and SUDHL4 cultured in complete or sterol-deprived media (Fig. 5C,D). SUDHL4 cells cultured in LPDS exhibited an increased ratio between the mature and the precursor form of SREBP2 (lane 3) compared to complete medium (lane 1) (Fig. 5D). However, GSK exposure reduced the induction of SREBP2 cleavage under LPDS condition (lane 4) and only marginally under IMDM condition (lane 2) (Fig. 5D). A similar, yet less pronounced trend was observed in Oci-Ly8 cells (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that EZH2 inhibition limits complete activation of the SREBP2 pathway, likely at the level of cleavage as well as transcriptional activation.

The impaired LDL uptake and the limited SREBP2 target gene activation upon EZH2 inhibition, suggest that under normal conditions cholesterol supply suffices to maintain high proliferative phenotype of GCB-DLBCLs. However, pharmacological inhibition of cholesterol synthesis in combination with GSK severely impairs cell survival, most likely due to an insufficient cholesterol availability. To test this possibility, we provided cells with water-soluble cholesterol thereby bypassing the need for receptor-mediated LDL uptake and cholesterol synthesis. Supporting the idea that cholesterol availability is a limiting factor upon GSK treatment, this exogenous source of cholesterol rescued growth of both Oci-Ly8 and SUDHL4 upon combination treatment (RO+GSK), albeit not to the level of RO or GSK conditions, indicating that under this combined treatment other pathways are likely affecting cell numbers (Fig. 5E,F).

Together, our results demonstrate that EZH2 inhibition impairs LDL uptake and attenuates activation of cholesterol biosynthesis, rendering cells vulnerable to the inhibition of cholesterol synthesis. This EZH2i-induced vulnerability may provide a new strategy to combat GSK-insensitive GCB-DLBCLs.

Discussion

Approximately 40 % of DLBCL patients are refractory to or relapse upon R-CHOP therapy, highlighting a critical unmet medical need for alternative, innovative intervention strategies [2]. Given the oncogenic role of EZH2 in GCB-DLBCL, inhibitors of EZH2 are being used in clinical trials, yet with suboptimal efficacy as a single agent therapy [16]. In line with clinical data, GCB-DLBCL cell lines can be categorized into two subsets, EZH2i sensitive and insensitive [16]. To discover new targetable dependencies that overcome insensitivity to EZH2 inhibition, we performed an unbiased EZH2i-anchored genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 drop-out screen in a GCB-DLBCL cell line insensitive to EZH2i, as well as its combination with DOT1L inhibitor [17] or lenalidomide [19]. This unbiased approach revealed a novel dependency of EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines on cholesterol availability following EZH2i treatment.

Cholesterol is an essential building block of cellular membranes, and its contribution in tumorigenesis and cancer therapy is well established [21,[34], [35], [36]]. Cholesterol homeostasis is a balanced system between uptake, synthesis, efflux, and storage [37]. Cells can synthesize cholesterol via the mevalonate pathway [24,25], or acquire cholesterol via low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR)-mediated uptake [38]. Both cholesterol synthesis and uptake are transcriptionally controlled by the sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2), to ensure optimal cellular cholesterol levels [39,40]. Interestingly, we observed that EZH2i altered the cellular localization of LDLR, followed by an impaired uptake of cholesterol-containing LDL particles. However, in contrast to other studies in diffuse midline glioma, mesothelioma and HNSCC [22,41,42], this critical condition did not induce the transcriptional activation of the SREBP2-mediated cholesterol genes. Pandey et al. reported an upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis genes following EZH2 inhibition in mesothelioma [42]. Similarly, Xu et al. demonstrated that the cholesterol synthesis enzyme SQLE is directly regulated by PRC2 in HNSCC [22]. The failure to transcriptionally activate SREBP2 targets suggests a different mode of action underlying the inhibition of EZH2 in GCB-DLBCL. In this subset, the dependency on cholesterol-associated genes relates to impaired cholesterol uptake and failure to fully activate the cholesterol synthesis pathway upon EZH2i.

Despite reduced uptake, cell survival upon EZH2i exposure suggests a sufficient cholesterol supply in EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines. However, cellular cholesterol availability is critically reduced when cholesterol biosynthesis is pharmacologically inhibited. Accordingly, all the four identified EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL cell lines were responsive towards the combined inhibition of EZH2 and cholesterol synthesis, confirming and extending the results of the CRISPR-Cas9 screen and the relevance of this metabolic pathway in the absence of EZH2 activity. To support our in vitro findings, we determine the efficacy of targeting EZH2 and cholesterol synthesis in vivo. Statin family members are FDA-approved and widely used medications that lower cholesterol levels by inhibiting HMGCR, the key enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. While statins effectively reduce LDL-cholesterol in humans, their activity in murine models remain controversial [43,44]. As an alternative we used a genetic approach, by establishing an isogenic LSS-proficient and -deficient xenograft model. In contrast to LSS-proficient Oci-Ly1 cell line, inhibition of EZH2 in this xenograft setting reduced tumor growth of the cohort transplanted with the LSS-deficient Oci-Ly1 cell line. Further studies are required to assess the potential therapeutic efficacy of cholesterol synthesis inhibitors in vivo.

The LSS inhibitor Ro 48-8071 (RO), targets 2,3-oxidosqualene lanosterol cyclase (OSC, also known as LSS), an enzyme involved in one of the final steps of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. Inhibition of LSS has demonstrated efficacy in controlling tumor growth in different cancer types, including breast, prostate, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. Unlike inhibitors of earlier steps, LSS inhibition does not interfere with enzymes involved in other pathways, such as prenylation, which is essential for post-translation modifications of proteins [49]. Therefore, the use of Ro 48-8071 instead of the commonly used statins, may reduce off-target effects. Future comparative studies are required to address this alternative.

Interestingly, the observed increased colocalization of LDLR with LAMP1-positive vesicles upon EZH2i, suggests receptor accumulation in the lysosomal compartments, likely connected to defective maintenance of LDLR surface expression. Such accumulation may result from enhanced lysosomal targeting or impaired recycling to the plasma membrane. Disruption of lysosomal acidification may also impair LDL-LDLR dissociation, leading to receptor retention in LAMP1-positive compartments [50]. However, these impairments do not preclude other trafficking defects. In fact, ER stress, COPII transport alterations, or Golgi abnormalities, can compromised LDLR maturation and trafficking in the ER-Golgi compartment, also contributing to altered cellular LDLR distribution [[51], [52], [53], [54]]. In line, our CRISPR screen identified genes involved in ER-associated protein degradation and protein N-linked glycosylation, further suggesting a secretory trafficking defects upon EZH2i. Although the interconnections between these pathways remain unclear, future molecular and cellular studies are needed to elucidate their respective contribution in LDLR surface expression and function.

In conclusion and consistent with clinical data [16], a subset of GCB-DLBCL cell lines is insensitive to EZH2 inhibition. Based on an EZH2i-anchored genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen, we demonstrated that this EZH2i-insensitivity induces a strong dependency on cholesterol availability. This dependency relates to a perturbation in LDL uptake and transcriptional activation of genes essential for cholesterol biosynthesis. These insights could be of relevance to improve targeting of EZH2i-insensitive GCB-DLBCL. The same principle may apply to other B cell malignancies, particularly follicular lymphomas insensitive to EZH2 inhibition.
