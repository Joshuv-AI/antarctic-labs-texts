Association of nephrology referral timing with long-term survival and peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis in patients initiating peritoneal dialysis: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Keisuke Okamoto, Masaru Matsui, Masahiro Eriguchi, Ken-ichi Samejima, Kazuhiko Tsuruya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe timing of nephrology referral is an important determinant of outcomes in chronic kidney disease, and observational studies have examined its association with outcomes among patients initiating peritoneal dialysis (PD). We investigated whether early referral (ER) was associated with long-term survival and PD-associated peritonitis (PD peritonitis).MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of 105 Japanese patients who initiated PD between 2007 and 2014. ER was defined as a nephrology consultation ≥ 90 days before PD initiation, and late referral (LR) as < 90 days. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, assessed by Cox regression. The secondary outcomes were time to first PD peritonitis (Fine–Gray model) and PD peritonitis incidence rates (episodes per patient-year), compared in adjusted analyses. Follow-up continued until death, transplantation, or the end of 2020.ResultsOf 105 patients (median age, 58 years; 71.4% male), 81 were in the ER group and 24 were in the LR group. During a median follow-up of 7.3 years, 42 patients (40.0%) died. ER was associated with lower mortality risk (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.43; 95% confidence interval, 0.22–0.82). PD peritonitis occurred in 54 patients (51.4%). ER was not significantly associated with time to first PD peritonitis (adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio, 0.60; 95% confidence interval, 0.33–1.07) or PD peritonitis incidence (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 0.55; 95% confidence interval, 0.29–1.05).ConclusionER before PD initiation was associated with significantly improved long-term survival. Associations between ER and PD peritonitis outcomes did not reach statistical significance. These findings underscore the importance of timely nephrology referral in patients considered for PD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04956-4.Keywords: Peritoneal dialysis, Nephrology referral, Mortality, Peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis

Introduction

The timing of referral to nephrology services is a key predictor of outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) who are preparing to start kidney replacement therapy [1, 2]. Among kidney replacement therapy options, peritoneal dialysis (PD) offers several advantages over in-center hemodialysis (HD), including the possibility of home-based treatment, better preservation of residual kidney function, and higher treatment satisfaction—all of which can contribute to an improved quality of life [3]. However, the timing of nephrology referral can significantly influence clinical outcomes. Early referral (ER) has been linked to higher rates of PD uptake, reduced use of temporary vascular access, and lower early mortality [2]. Although definitions of ER vary, most studies use a threshold of at least 90 days before dialysis initiation [2].

Although ER is known to improve clinical outcomes in patients with CKD, relatively few studies have examined its specific impact on those starting PD. One observational study reported lower mortality among PD patients who received ER, but its median follow-up period was relatively short—only 3 years [1]. More recent data from the United States Renal Data System indicate improved survival among PD patients, highlighting the need to assess the long-term effects of referral timing on survival [4].

Most prior research has focused on survival outcomes, yet PD-associated peritonitis (PD peritonitis) remains a major complication of PD. It is linked to increased risks of transition to in-center HD and higher mortality [5]. Factors such as older age and lower use of automated PD have been associated with PD peritonitis [5, 6], but evidence regarding the relationship between nephrology referral timing and the incidence of PD peritonitis remains limited.

In this study, we examined the association between the timing of nephrology referral and long-term survival, as well as the incidence of PD peritonitis, in patients initiating PD.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This single-center, retrospective cohort study included consecutive Japanese adult patients (aged ≥ 20 years) who initiated PD at Nara Medical University Hospital between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2014. Patients who discontinued PD because of recovery of kidney function were excluded. Patients were followed from the initiation of PD until death, kidney transplantation, loss to follow-up, or December 31, 2020, whichever occurred first.

Data collection and measurements

The baseline patient characteristics collected at the time of dialysis initiation included age, sex, body mass index, primary kidney disease, whether dialysis initiation was planned or unplanned, the number of days between nephrology referral and dialysis initiation, urine output, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [7], and laboratory data. Planned dialysis initiation was defined as beginning dialysis with a PD catheter, while unplanned initiation referred to starting HD with a temporary vascular catheter (central venous catheter), followed by transition to PD within 30 days. We assessed urgent-start PD, defined as initiating PD within 14 days of PD catheter insertion without interim hemodialysis; no patients in this cohort met this definition. The variables used to calculate the CCI are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Laboratory parameters included hemoglobin, serum albumin, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), serum potassium, corrected serum calcium, and intact parathyroid hormone.

In Japan, a unique dialysis modality known as combined PD and HD (PD + HD therapy) is sometimes used during the transition from PD to full HD [8, 9]. This therapy typically involves PD 5 to 6 days per week with an additional HD session once weekly. It is designed to enhance solute clearance and ultrafiltration beyond what PD alone provides, while preserving the lifestyle flexibility and quality-of-life benefits associated with PD. In this retrospective study, we reviewed medical records to identify transitions to PD + HD therapy, recording the date of the first HD session added to PD during follow-up. We then report the total number and proportion of patients who received PD + HD therapy. In addition, PD-related variables such as dialysis adequacy and peritoneal transport parameters were collected for descriptive modality-stratified analyses. In our clinical practice, the first peritoneal equilibration test was generally performed approximately 6–12 months after PD initiation.

Exposures, major confounders, and outcomes

The main exposure variable in this study was the timing of nephrology referral, which classified patients into ER and late referral (LR) groups. ER was defined as a nephrology consultation occurring 90 days or more before the initiation of PD, while LR was defined as a consultation occurring within 90 days prior to PD initiation.

Analyses were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, and CCI, all of which have been previously associated with mortality among PD patients [10–12]. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Follow-up continued until death, kidney transplantation, loss to follow-up, or 31 December 2020, whichever occurred first. The secondary outcomes were the time to the first episode of PD peritonitis and PD peritonitis incidence rates, expressed as episodes per patient-year.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics are summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. The Mann–Whitney U test and Pearson’s χ2 test were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

To evaluate the association between mortality and the timing of nephrology referral, univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). For all time-to-event analyses, time zero was the date of PD initiation (index date). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Patients who were lost to follow-up were censored at the date of their last known contact. Patients who underwent kidney transplantation were censored at the date of transplantation. The dependent variable in the Cox regression analysis was all-cause mortality during the observation period. Four independent variables were included in the multivariable model: referral group (ER vs. LR), age, sex, and CCI. For secondary outcomes, a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to analyze the time to the first episode of PD peritonitis, accounting for competing risks such as transition to HD, kidney transplantation, or death prior to PD peritonitis. The number of PD peritonitis episodes per patient was analyzed as count data. We first fitted a Poisson regression model with the log of follow-up time (person-years) as an offset to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Overdispersion was assessed using the ratio of the Pearson chi-square statistic to the residual degrees of freedom. When the dispersion parameter exceeded 2.0, indicating that the variance substantially exceeded the mean, a negative binomial regression model was used instead of the Poisson model. Both univariable and multivariable analyses were performed, and multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, and CCI. A subgroup analysis examined whether the association between nephrology referral timing and mortality differed by age, sex, or CCI. All adjustment covariates (age, sex, and CCI) had complete data; therefore, no imputation or exclusion due to missingness was required for the multivariable analyses.

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the findings. The first substituted unplanned versus planned dialysis initiation for the CCI in the multivariable Cox regression model. The second excluded patients with exceptionally long time at risk for PD peritonitis (above the 95th percentile) and re-estimated PD peritonitis incidence using the negative binomial regression model. The third excluded patients who died within 6 months of PD initiation and re-estimated the association between referral timing and mortality using the Cox model. Additional exploratory analyses were performed to assess whether treatment category modified the associations of selected variables with outcomes and to examine these associations separately within the PD-alone and PD + HD subgroups. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P values of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient flow

Between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2014, a total of 106 patients initiated PD. One patient was excluded according to the predefined criterion—discontinuation of PD because of kidney function recovery—leaving 105 patients for the final analysis. These patients were categorized into two groups based on the timing of nephrology referral: 81 patients in the ER group, who received a nephrology consultation ≥ 90 days before PD initiation, and 24 patients in the LR group, who received a consultation < 90 days before initiation. The patient flow chart is presented in Fig. 1. Of the 105 patients included in the study, 9 (8.6%) were lost to follow-up during the observation period and were censored at their last known contact. During follow-up, 4 patients underwent kidney transplantation (ER: 2/81; LR: 2/24).

Baseline patient characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study population. The median age was 58 years, and 71.4% of patients were men. Diabetes mellitus was the most common cause of end-stage kidney disease, accounting for 44.8% of cases. Compared with the LR group, patients in the ER group had a lower frequency of unplanned dialysis initiation (9.9% vs. 58.3%), greater use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (91.4% vs. 37.5%), higher serum albumin levels (3.8 vs. 3.3 g/dL), and more frequent use of automated PD (55.6% vs. 29.2%) at the start of dialysis. Based on the definition of unplanned initiation, HD via a central venous catheter at dialysis initiation occurred in 22 patients overall (ER: 8/81; LR: 14/24). Among patients with unplanned initiation (n = 22), the median interval from HD initiation to PD initiation was 17 days (range, 2–27). There were no significant differences between the groups in age, sex, body mass index, urine output, CCI, hemoglobin, eGFR, corrected serum calcium, or serum phosphorus levels. eGFR at PD initiation was similar between groups (ER: 5.4 vs. LR: 5.8 mL/min/1.73 m²; P = 0.5). Primary outcome

The median observation period was 7.3 years (IQR, 5.6–9.5). During this time, 42 of the 105 patients (40.0%) in the overall cohort died, including 27 of 81 patients (33.3%) in the ER group and 15 of 24 patients (62.5%) in the LR group. The Kaplan–Meier survival curves are shown in Fig. 2A. Although the ER group tended to have higher survival probabilities, the difference between groups was not statistically significant (log-rank test, P = 0.06). In the univariable Cox regression analysis, ER was not significantly associated with mortality, although the point estimate suggested a lower risk (HR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.29–1.04; P = 0.07). However, after adjustment for age, sex, and CCI, ER was significantly associated with reduced all-cause mortality (adjusted HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.22–0.82; P = 0.01). The parameter estimates for all variables in the multivariable Cox regression model are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Adjusted survival curves based on the multivariable model are shown in Fig. 2B. In a supplementary analysis additionally adjusting for treatment category (PD + HD vs. PD alone), the association between early versus late referral and all-cause mortality remained significant (adjusted HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.22–0.84; P = 0.01) (Supplementary Table 6A). In exploratory interaction analyses, no statistically significant interaction was observed between treatment category and age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index, D/P creatinine, or urine output for all-cause mortality (Supplementary Table 7). In exploratory subgroup-specific analyses within the PD-alone and PD + HD groups, baseline eGFR was associated with all-cause mortality in the PD-alone subgroup, whereas no consistent pattern was observed across treatment subgroups (Supplementary Table 8 A). In additional modality-stratified analyses, early referral was significantly associated with lower all-cause mortality only in the PD-alone subgroup; the association was not statistically significant in the PD + HD subgroup (Supplementary Table 9 A).

Unadjusted and adjusted survival curves. A: Unadjusted survival curves for each group, generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. B: Adjusted survival curves for each group, derived from the multivariable Cox regression analysis. Adjustment variables included age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index

During follow-up, 45 of the 105 patients (42.9%) underwent combined PD + HD therapy, including 35 of 81 ER patients (43.2%) and 10 of 24 LR patients (41.7%), with no significant difference in proportions between the groups. The median duration of PD therapy—including time spent on PD + HD therapy—was 3.7 years (IQR, 2.1–5.8) for the overall cohort, 3.7 years (IQR, 2.3–5.8) in the ER group, and 2.9 years (IQR, 1.2–6.0) in the LR group. The difference in PD duration between the groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.4). Among patients who received combined PD + HD therapy, the median time from PD initiation to the first HD session added to PD was 3.44 years in the ER group and 4.61 years in the LR group (P = 0.33).

Secondary outcomes

Time to first episode of PD peritonitis

During the follow-up period, 54 of the 105 patients (51.4%) experienced at least one episode of PD peritonitis—38 of 81 patients (46.9%) in the ER group and 16 of 24 patients (66.7%) in the LR group.

The cumulative incidence of PD peritonitis was analyzed using a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model, treating transition to HD, kidney transplantation, and death prior to PD peritonitis as competing risks. In the univariable analysis using a Fine–Gray model for the cumulative incidence of first PD peritonitis, ER was not significantly associated with time to first PD peritonitis (subdistribution HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.35–1.09; P = 0.1).

In the multivariable analysis adjusted for age, sex, and CCI using a Fine–Gray model for the cumulative incidence of first PD peritonitis, ER was not significantly associated with time to first PD peritonitis (adjusted subdistribution HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.33–1.07; P = 0.08). In a supplementary analysis additionally adjusting for treatment category (PD + HD vs. PD alone), neither early referral (adjusted subdistribution HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.33–1.11; P = 0.1) nor treatment category (adjusted subdistribution HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.40–1.22; P = 0.2) was significantly associated with time to first PD peritonitis (Supplementary Table 6B). In exploratory subgroup-specific analyses within the PD-alone and PD + HD groups, baseline eGFR was associated with time to first PD peritonitis in the PD + HD subgroup, whereas no consistent pattern was observed across treatment subgroups (Supplementary Table 8B). In additional modality-stratified analyses, the association between early referral and time to first PD peritonitis was not statistically significant in either subgroup (Supplementary Table 9B).

Among the 54 patients who developed PD peritonitis, the median time to the first episode was 1.6 years (IQR, 0.5–3.9). When stratified by referral group, the median time was 1.7 years (IQR, 0.6–3.4) in the ER group and 1.1 years (IQR, 0.5–4.5) in the LR group. This analysis, limited to patients who experienced PD peritonitis, showed no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.8). Unadjusted and adjusted cumulative incidence curves generated from the Fine–Gray model are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The PD peritonitis incidence rate, defined as the number of episodes per patient-year, was a median of 0.13 episodes per patient-year (IQR, 0.00–0.47) in the entire cohort, 0.00 (IQR, 0.00–0.41) in the ER group, and 0.18 (IQR, 0.00–0.92) in the LR group.

In the univariable Poisson model, overdispersion was observed (dispersion = 2.73); therefore, a negative binomial regression model was used for analysis. In the negative binomial model, ER was not significantly associated with PD peritonitis incidence (IRR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.28–1.07, P = 0.06). Similarly, in the multivariable model adjusted for age, sex, and CCI, overdispersion was also present (dispersion = 2.57), and the negative binomial model was applied. The association remained not statistically significant after adjustment for age, sex, and CCI (adjusted IRR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.29–1.05, P = 0.05). In a supplementary analysis additionally adjusting for treatment category (PD + HD vs. PD alone), early referral was significantly associated with a lower PD peritonitis incidence rate (adjusted IRR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.29–0.97; P = 0.03) (Supplementary Table 6 C). The parameter estimates for the main multivariable model are provided in Supplementary Table 3. In exploratory subgroup-specific analyses within the PD-alone and PD + HD groups, age was associated with PD peritonitis incidence in the PD-alone subgroup, whereas no consistent pattern was observed across treatment subgroups (Supplementary Table 8 C). In additional modality-stratified analyses, the association between early referral and PD peritonitis incidence was not statistically significant in either subgroup (Supplementary Table 9 C).

Overall, Gram-positive bacteria were the predominant pathogens (43 of 84 episodes, 51.2%), with Staphylococcus aureus responsible for 13 episodes (15.5%). There were no significant differences between the ER and LR groups in pathogen distribution (P = 0.7) or device use (P = 0.4).

Subgroup analyses

As shown in Fig. 3, subgroup analyses revealed no statistically significant interactions for age, sex, or CCI.

Forest plot of adjusted hazard ratios in subgroup analyses. The positions of the squares represent adjusted hazard ratios, and the horizontal lines indicate the corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Hazard ratios were adjusted for age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index

Sensitivity analyses

In the first sensitivity analysis, a multivariable Cox regression model was performed, substituting dialysis initiation status (unplanned vs. planned) for the CCI as a covariate. The adjusted HR for all-cause mortality in the ER group was 0.44 (95% CI, 0.22–0.86; P = 0.02), indicating a significant survival benefit associated with ER. This finding was consistent with the primary analysis.

In the second sensitivity analysis, we assessed whether exceptionally long time at risk for PD peritonitis influenced the PD peritonitis incidence estimates. We excluded patients with time at risk for PD peritonitis above the 95th percentile (> 6.78 years; n = 6) and re-estimated PD peritonitis incidence using a negative binomial regression model adjusted for age, sex, and CCI. The results were similar to the primary analysis (adjusted IRR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.27–1.07; P = 0.06).

In the third sensitivity analysis, we assessed early mortality within 6 months of PD initiation. Death within 6 months occurred in 2 of 24 patients (8.3%) in the LR group and 1 of 81 patients (1.2%) in the ER group (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.13). After excluding these early deaths (n = 3), ER remained associated with lower all-cause mortality (adjusted HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.24–0.97; P = 0.04).

Discussion

In this single-center cohort study of patients initiating PD, we found that ER, defined as consultation at least 90 days before PD initiation, was associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality after adjustment for age, sex, and CCI. Regarding infectious complications, ER was not significantly associated with PD peritonitis incidence, and no significant difference was observed in the time to the first PD peritonitis episode when competing risks were considered.

The survival advantage associated with ER in this study aligns with previous findings. In a single-center study from Hong Kong, patients referred more than 3 months before PD initiation showed significantly lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality during a median follow-up of 3 years [1]. Similarly, a large Brazilian national cohort demonstrated improved survival among patients who received at least 90 days of pre-dialysis care, although follow-up in that study did not exceed 5 years [13]. By contrast, our study followed patients for a median of 7.3 years, with some observed for up to 14 years, thereby extending prior evidence and indicating that the benefit of ER persists over prolonged treatment periods.

For PD peritonitis outcomes, the timing of nephrology referral did not significantly affect the time to the first episode, consistent with findings from a recent cohort study [14]. Nonetheless, our competing-risk analysis did not reach statistical significance, and the evidence remains inconclusive.

The analysis of PD peritonitis incidence rates adds context to the interpretation of these findings. To date, few studies have examined whether the timing of nephrology referral influences the frequency of PD peritonitis. When interpreting these findings, it is important to consider established guideline benchmarks. The International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis recommends a target PD peritonitis incidence rate of no more than 0.40 episodes per patient-year [15], whereas Japanese guidelines report lower national averages of approximately 0.21–0.24 episodes per patient-year [16]. The overall incidence rate in our cohort was similarly low, aligning more closely with the Japanese benchmark. Within this context, the association between ER and PD peritonitis incidence did not reach statistical significance, and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Several mechanisms may explain the association between ER and lower mortality. Patients in the ER group showed indicators of better preparation at PD initiation, including fewer unplanned starts, higher serum albumin, more frequent use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, and greater adoption of automated PD. These factors likely reflect increased opportunity for modality education, nutritional optimization, anemia management, and timely catheter placement [17, 18], all of which have been linked to improved long-term outcomes. Notably, the similar proportion of patients who underwent PD + HD therapy during follow-up suggests that the association between ER and lower mortality was unlikely to be fully explained by differences in treatment category during follow-up.

Interpretation of the PD peritonitis results should take into account the distinction between analytic measures. The Fine–Gray model evaluates the risk of the first event under competing risks, whereas the negative binomial model captures the total number of recurrent events over time. ER did not significantly delay the occurrence of the first episode, and the association with PD peritonitis incidence did not reach statistical significance. Because causative organisms and device use were similar between groups, any potential association—if present—might relate to processes of care (e.g., structured training, reinforcement of aseptic technique, and closer follow-up) rather than microbiological factors [19–21].

The present findings reinforce the role of ER as a quality target in CKD care. Referral at least 90 days before the anticipated start of dialysis, as recommended in clinical guidelines [18], allows patients to begin PD in a more prepared state—an especially important consideration for those pursuing home-based therapies. In primary care, standardized referral triggers based on eGFR thresholds [18] or predicted kidney failure risk (for example, using the Kidney Failure Risk Equation) [22] could facilitate timely referral. Within nephrology clinics, structured pre-dialysis pathways that include nutrition evaluation, anemia management, vaccination, catheter planning, and practical PD training may translate early engagement into lower infection rates and improved survival [17, 18]. Because the association between ER and lower mortality persisted in sensitivity analyses, these findings suggest the potential system-level value of promoting early referral. Timely referral may also reduce the frequency of urgent starts and their associated costs, while supporting patient preferences for home-based dialysis.

Subgroup analyses were broadly consistent with the main findings, as the association between ER and lower mortality was similar across categories of age, sex, and CCI, with no significant interactions observed. Although the study was not powered for definitive subgroup comparisons, the absence of effect modification suggests that the advantages of ER apply broadly across patient characteristics.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of findings to similar care settings. Second, the LR group was relatively small, increasing the risk of type II error and reducing precision, particularly in the PD peritonitis subanalyses. Third, although we adjusted for major covariates and conducted sensitivity analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Unmeasured factors—such as socioeconomic status, health literacy, caregiver support, and the quality of PD training—may have influenced both referral timing and outcomes. Fourth, some misclassification of referral timing may have occurred if patients received earlier nephrology consultations outside our institution. Fifth, although PD peritonitis events and microbiological data were carefully reviewed, variations in practices such as exit-site care or prophylactic protocols were not captured and may have contributed to the observed differences. Finally, the Japanese clinical context, where PD + HD combination therapy is available during transition, may limit the applicability of these findings to other healthcare systems.

Future research should recognize that randomized comparisons of ER versus LR are not feasible because delaying nephrology referral for research purposes would be unethical. Instead, large observational cohorts and registry-based studies are needed to generate more precise estimates and enhance generalizability. Further work should also identify which specific elements of ER—such as patient education, timely catheter planning, or structured pre-dialysis care—most strongly influence outcomes.

In conclusion, this single-center cohort study with up to 14 years of follow-up demonstrated that early nephrology referral before PD initiation was associated with reduced all-cause mortality. Associations between ER and PD peritonitis outcomes did not reach statistical significance. These findings support timely nephrology referral as a potentially important component of pre-dialysis care for patients considering PD.
