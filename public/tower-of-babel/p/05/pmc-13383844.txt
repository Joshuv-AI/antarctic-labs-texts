From weak adsorption to selective detection: functionalized interfaces for SERS glucose sensing
Authors: Ha Anh Nguyen, Ngo Thi Loan, Dao Thi Nguyet Nga, Dang Thi Hanh Trang, Vu Ngoc Phan, Anh-Tuan Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) offers molecularly specific, highly sensitive, and potentially miniaturized analysis for glucose sensing, but glucose remains an intrinsically difficult SERS target on bare noble-metal surfaces. As a neutral, highly hydrated, weakly adsorbing aliphatic polyol, glucose does not readily populate plasmonic hotspots or form a stable, selective, and spectroscopically productive interfacial state. Consequently, the central limitation in SERS glucose sensing is not simply weak Raman scattering, but the mismatch between glucose chemistry and the interfacial requirements of SERS detection. This review examines functionalized interfaces as the main route for overcoming that mismatch. We first discuss the SERS principles most relevant to glucose sensing, including electromagnetic and chemical enhancement, distance dependence, adsorption probability, molecular orientation, and matrix competition. We then analyze boronic-acid-centered interfaces, which currently represent the most developed practical strategy because they combine reversible cis-diol recognition, compact surface attachment, and adaptable readout designs. Alternative recognition routes, including biomolecular and molecularly imprinted interfaces, are also assessed as complementary strategies that offer different forms of selectivity but introduce additional challenges related to distance, stability, transport, or interfacial coupling. Across these interface classes, recent progress shows that analytical performance depends not only on receptor chemistry, but also on hotspot proximity, signal-conversion logic, sample handling, and matrix tolerance. By framing SERS glucose sensing as an interfacial problem, this review identifies the design priorities needed to move from weak adsorption toward selective, reproducible, and analytically credible glucose detection.

Introduction

Glucose is one of the most important small-molecule targets in analytical and clinical chemistry because it remains central to diabetes diagnosis, metabolic control, and long-term risk management. Even as diabetes care has expanded to include glycated biomarkers, continuous monitoring systems, and digital decision support, glucose itself still anchors many diagnostic and therapeutic decisions.1 Its importance is further amplified by the scale of diabetes worldwide and by the continuing demand for measurements that are rapid, reliable, and adaptable to decentralized settings. At the same time, the analytical context of glucose sensing has broadened beyond conventional blood analysis. Glucose is increasingly pursued in interstitial fluid and other minimally or non-invasively accessible biofluids, which are attractive for wearable, portable, or application-specific monitoring.2–10 Within SERS specifically, this shift has motivated biointerface-compatible formats such as microneedle-assisted intradermal sensing and flexible plasmonic hydrogel patches, where sampling geometry, interfacial stability, and signal generation must be considered together.11,12 However, these opportunities come with added analytical difficulty because matrix composition, dilution, transport lag, and sample-specific interferents can all affect the relationship between measured signal and physiologically meaningful glucose levels.2–9 Thus, glucose sensing remains an active problem in analytical design rather than a solved measurement task.

Within this landscape, surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) is an attractive platform because it combines molecular fingerprinting with high sensitivity and the possibility of rapid, miniaturized analysis. However, glucose is a particularly demanding analyte for SERS sensing. As a neutral, highly hydrated, aliphatic polyol, glucose lacks the strong surface affinity that many more SERS-responsive analytes exhibit on Au or Ag. It does not contain the strongly adsorbing functional groups that readily localize molecules at plasmonic hot spots, and its stabilization in aqueous solution further disfavors spontaneous accumulation at the metal interface. This poor interfacial affinity is accompanied by a modest Raman response and by spectral congestion in regions where baseline fluctuation, substrate variability, and band overlap can complicate assignment and quantitation.13–15 For that reason, glucose detection is not difficult simply because its signal is weak; it is difficult because the molecules are not naturally positioned where SERS enhancement is most effective. This distinction is crucial, because it means that the main analytical challenge lies not only in signal amplification but in controlling interfacial access to the sensing region.

That challenge becomes even more pronounced in realistic media. A bare plasmonic substrate may appear highly responsive in simplified buffer systems, yet perform poorly in biological or chemically crowded environments where other species can access the surface more efficiently than glucose. Proteins such as albumin, or small thiol-containing molecules such as glutathione, may compete for interfacial access, alter local adsorption equilibria, or dominate the surface environment in ways that reduce glucose-specific response. Under such conditions, stronger electromagnetic enhancement alone does not guarantee analytically meaningful glucose detection. The key issue is whether glucose can be brought selectively and reproducibly into the SERS-active region while maintaining signal credibility in the presence of competing adsorption and matrix variability.13,14 Accordingly, the central limitation of SERS glucose sensing is not the absence of hotspots, but the mismatch between glucose's molecular behavior and the physicochemical requirements of hotspot-based detection.

Functionalized interfaces have emerged as the principal way to overcome this mismatch. In the context of SERS glucose sensing, the interface should not be viewed as a passive coating placed on top of a plasmonic substrate, but as the chemical layer that determines whether glucose can be enriched, recognized, confined, transformed, or transduced into a measurable response. Such interfaces may increase the local concentration of glucose near the sensing surface, impose more favorable orientation, suppress nonspecific adsorption, modulate hydration and transport, or convert recognition into a more robust spectral output. From this perspective, the field has progressed not simply by making nanostructures more enhancing, but by making interfaces more chemically informative. This is why receptor-functionalized surfaces, polymeric and hydrogel interphases, porous or hybrid confinement layers, and reporter-assisted architectures have become so important in the glucose-SERS literature.16–21 Representative studies have shown that meaningful gains in sensitivity, selectivity, and matrix tolerance arise when capture chemistry, confinement, and plasmonic coupling are engineered together rather than optimized separately.22–24 In this sense, the route from weak adsorption to selective detection is fundamentally an interfacial one.

This review is organized around the premise that the SERS glucose sensing is primarily an interfacial problem. Although many studies in this area are discussed in terms of substrate composition, enhancement efficiency, or detection limit, these descriptors alone do not fully explain why some platforms achieve more credible glucose detection than others. Here, functionalized interfaces are treated as the central link between glucose chemistry and SERS readout, because they govern analyte access to plasmonic hotspots, recognition selectivity, molecular positioning, and signal conversion. This perspective allows boronic-acid-centered, biomolecular, and molecularly imprinted (MIP) systems to be compared not only as different material classes, but as different ways of solving the same interfacial challenge. By emphasizing the relationship between recognition chemistry, hotspot proximity, transduction strategy, and matrix tolerance, the review aims to clarify how functionalization has shaped the development of glucose SERS and which design principles are most important for future analytically reliable platforms. SERS principles relevant to glucose sensing

Raman fingerprinting and the analytical value of SERS

Raman spectroscopy is attractive for chemical sensing because it provides molecularly specific vibrational fingerprints with relatively narrow bands, allowing structurally similar analytes to be distinguished without fluorescent labels. For bioanalytical targets, this spectral specificity is particularly valuable because the analytical challenge is often not merely detecting the presence of a molecule, but resolving it from a chemically crowded background. SERS retains this fingerprinting capability while greatly increasing sensitivity by coupling the Raman process to plasmonic nanostructures, most commonly Au or Ag, that amplify the optical field experienced by molecules located at or near the surface.15,25,26 In this sense, SERS is not simply a more sensitive version of Raman spectroscopy; it is an interfacial vibrational technique whose analytical performance is governed by both spectroscopy and surface chemistry.15,25,27

That distinction is especially important for small metabolites such as glucose. Glucose has a well-defined Raman spectrum, but the existence of Raman-active vibrations does not guarantee strong analytical performance under SERS conditions. The SERS signal depends on whether glucose can access the enhancing surface, reside within a highly amplified near-field region, and adopt an adsorption geometry that yields an observable spectral response. Recent reviews therefore emphasize that the usefulness of SERS in biosensing arises from the combination of molecular specificity, nanoscale field localization, and controllable surface interactions rather than from signal enhancement alone.15,25,28,29 For glucose sensing, this means that the analytical value of SERS is realized only when substrate design and interface engineering work together.

Electromagnetic and chemical enhancement

The dominant contribution to SERS is usually electromagnetic (EM) enhancement. When incident light excites localized surface plasmon resonances in a nanostructured noble metal, the optical field can become highly concentrated at sharp tips, interparticle junctions, nanoscale crevices, or other high-curvature features. Molecules located in these regions (hot spots) experience a much larger local field during both excitation and scattering, leading to substantial signal amplification.25–27,29 Because this enhancement is strongly localized, substrate geometry, nanogap size, metal composition, excitation wavelength, and local dielectric environment all influence analytical performance. A substrate may therefore exhibit strong nominal plasmonic activity while still performing poorly for a given analyte if the analyte is not effectively delivered to the relevant hotspots.25,27–29

Chemical enhancement (CM) is smaller in magnitude but often decisive for small molecules. It arises from short-range electronic interactions between the adsorbate and the surface, including charge-transfer pathways, adsorption-induced changes in polarizability, and modifications of the Raman tensor that depend on the local bonding configuration.27,28 Recent theoretical work has shown that this contribution is highly sensitive to adsorption site, molecular orientation, surface composition, and excitation energy, and thus cannot be treated as a single constant correction to the EM effect.27 For strongly adsorbing analytes, chemical enhancement can substantially reshape both intensity and band pattern. For glucose, however, the problem is that bare Au and Ag surfaces do not naturally provide an especially favorable adsorption environment. As a result, glucose often benefits less from the chemical contribution than molecules containing groups with stronger affinity for noble-metal surfaces.

These two EM and CM mechanisms should therefore be viewed as coupled rather than separate, and in practical SERS substrates this coupling is strongly shaped by material architecture. Plasmonic metals such as Ag and Au mainly provide the field-generating component by supporting localized surface plasmon resonances and creating intense near fields at rough surfaces, sharp tips, interparticle junctions, and nanogaps.25,26,29 In composite or hybrid substrates, semiconductor or oxide domains can complement the plasmonic metal by promoting charge transfer, adsorption, catalytic transduction, molecular enrichment, diffusion control, antifouling behavior, or structural stabilization.26,30,31 For glucose sensing, however, these material-level functions are not sufficient unless they are connected to an interface that can selectively bring glucose, or a glucose-responsive reporter, into the SERS-active region. Therefore, the central design problem is not only how to build a stronger enhancing material, but how to couple that material with a functional interface that converts weak glucose adsorption into productive recognition, positioning, and signal transduction. This is why interface design becomes central to SERS glucose sensing from the very beginning of the discussion, not only at the application stage.

Distance, adsorption, and orientation effects

SERS is intrinsically a near-field phenomenon. Enhancement decays sharply with distance from the metal surface, so the first adsorbed molecular layer and the first few nanometers above it dominate the observed response.25–29 This distance dependence has direct design consequences. Recognition layers, protective shells, hydrogels, or porous coatings may improve capture and selectivity, but they must do so without displacing the relevant molecular vibration too far from the plasmonic surface. If the interfacial architecture becomes too thick or too poorly organized, affinity may increase while signal decreases. Quantitative SERS reviews have repeatedly identified this balance between capture efficiency and near-field accessibility as a central design problem.28,29

Adsorption probability is equally important. A substrate can only enhance the Raman signal of molecules that actually reach and remain in the enhanced region during acquisition. In real systems, this access is shaped by competitive adsorption from solvent molecules, residual capping ligands, salts, buffers, and matrix components. Surface chemistry is therefore dynamic rather than static, and the population of hotspot sites may be dominated by species other than the target analyte.27,28 This issue becomes more severe in biological media, where proteins and other abundant components can attenuate or distort target signals. Recent experimental work has shown that proteins affect different classes of SERS readout in different ways, reinforcing the need to consider matrix-dependent interfacial competition explicitly rather than assuming that target binding alone controls the spectrum.32

Orientation adds a further layer of selectivity. The relative geometry between a molecule and the plasmonic surface influences which vibrational modes are preferentially enhanced, because the effective Raman response depends on how the induced polarizability change is projected onto the local field. Thus, adsorption through different functional groups or at different angles can alter both intensity distribution and apparent spectral signature.27,28 For glucose, this is a major complication. Although the molecule contains multiple hydroxyl groups, these do not automatically confer strong, well-defined adsorption on bare noble metals. Glucose is highly hydrated, conformationally flexible, and only weakly surface-active relative to many competing species. Studies on weakly adsorbing analytes more broadly have shown that successful SERS detection often requires explicit surface modification to promote interfacial access and retention.33 In other words, for glucose the challenge is not only low intrinsic enhancement, but low probability of forming a stable, favorable, and spectroscopically productive interfacial state.

Why bare-surface SERS usually fails for glucose

In practical terms, these interfacial constraints make direct glucose detection on unmodified Au or Ag difficult to reproduce. Bare noble-metal nanostructures may generate intense hotspots, but glucose is a small, neutral, highly solvated molecule that does not strongly or selectively populate those sites. Any measurable signal obtained under idealized conditions can therefore be highly sensitive to substrate morphology, aggregation state, drying history, local concentration, or co-adsorbed species. Even when glucose bands are observable, reproducibility and selectivity often remain limited because the bare interface does little to control competitive adsorption, binding geometry, or distance from the enhancing surface.25,27–29,33

Recent glucose-oriented studies illustrate both the promise and the limitations of this bare-surface route. Label-free strategies based on self-assembled interfacial control can improve glucose access on carefully engineered plasmonic substrates, but the analytical outcome still depends strongly on how effectively the surface organizes the molecule near the hotspot region.34 More broadly, current SERS glucose research has shifted toward architectures in which the interface is asked to do more than expose a metal surface. It must help create a chemically meaningful sensing boundary, where weak glucose–surface interactions can be redirected into more controlled recognition, positioning, and spectral response. This role is most clearly developed in boronic-acid-centered interfaces and is further explored through biomolecular and MIP recognition strategies.17,35–37

This shift is conceptually important because glucose sensing no longer depends on the expectation that a weakly adsorbing molecule will spontaneously generate a strong and selective signal on bare metal. Instead, the interface actively restructures the measurement problem by introducing chemical recognition, improving molecular positioning, and converting weak glucose–surface interactions into a more readable SERS response. Boronic-acid-centered systems illustrate this logic most clearly because reversible cis-diol binding can increase local glucose affinity while preserving relatively compact interfacial architectures.17 Biomolecular and MIP interfaces extend the same principle through different recognition mechanisms, respectively relying on biological function or template-defined binding cavities.38,39 The broader message is that the core obstacle in glucose SERS is interfacial rather than vibrational: glucose does possess Raman information, but bare noble-metal surfaces usually do not position the molecule to exploit the SERS mechanism effectively. This is why the remainder of the review focuses on functionalized interfaces as the main route by which affinity, selectivity, and reliable transduction become possible in SERS glucose sensing.

Boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS interfaces

Because bare Au and Ag surfaces do not capture glucose with sufficient efficiency or selectivity, most successful glucose SERS strategies begin by introducing an interfacial recognition layer. Among the available options, boronic-acid-based chemistry has become the most widely used synthetic route because it offers a practical balance between reversible glucose affinity, molecular compactness, and straightforward integration onto plasmonic materials. This combination is especially important in SERS, where the recognition element must not only bind the analyte but also keep it close enough to the enhanced electromagnetic field for efficient signal generation. Accordingly, this section examines how boronic-acid-centered interfaces have been designed for glucose SERS, what performance variables govern their behavior, and why their apparent success must still be judged against persistent limitations in selectivity, matrix tolerance, and mechanistic clarity.

Why boronic acids became central in glucose SERS sensing

Boronic-acid chemistry became central in glucose SERS sensing because it addresses the core mismatch between glucose and bare plasmonic substrates in a way that is chemically simple and structurally compatible with the SERS mechanism. Glucose is highly hydrophilic, small, and only weakly adsorbing on unmodified Au or Ag, so even though it possesses Raman-active vibrational modes, it is not naturally positioned for efficient surface-enhanced detection. Boronic acids offered a practical synthetic route to overcome this interfacial problem because they can reversibly interact with cis-diol-containing molecules, including glucose, thereby increasing the probability that glucose is retained at or near the metal interface.23,40,41 This does not mean that boronic acids solve selectivity in any absolute sense. Their saccharide recognition is conditional and medium-dependent, and discrimination among glucose and other diol-containing species is often incomplete. Even so, for SERS purposes, boronic acids became attractive not because they are perfect receptors, but because they offer a workable compromise between affinity, molecular compactness, chemical tunability, and ease of immobilization. Compared with bulkier recognition layers, a small boronic-acid ligand can improve local glucose capture without placing an excessively thick barrier between the analyte and the plasmonic field. That balance is especially important in SERS, where even chemically meaningful recognition can become analytically useless if the captured molecule is held too far from the enhancing surface.

This same balance explains why boronic acids fit SERS particularly well at the level of interface design. In many glucose SERS platforms, the question is not only whether glucose can be recognized, but whether it can be recognized within the highly distance-dependent near-field region where enhancement is strongest. Boronic-acid ligands, especially thiolated aromatic boronic acids such as 4-mercaptophenylboronic acid (4-MPBA), are well suited to this requirement because they can be grafted directly onto Au or Ag surfaces through established surface chemistries while maintaining a thin functional layer.23,41,42 This creates a compact recognition interface in which the receptor is not an outer add-on but part of the immediate SERS-active boundary. In practice, that structural economy matters as much as the binding event itself. A thicker polymeric or biomolecular recognition shell may improve affinity in principle, however, it also risks moving the bound analyte outside the most intense enhancement zone. Boronic-acid interfaces therefore gained traction because they can be integrated as monolayers or near-monolayer-scale modifiers on nanorods, nanoparticle assemblies, fibers, or other plasmonic supports, allowing glucose binding to occur with relatively little distance penalty. At the same time, these systems retain synthetic flexibility: ligand density, spacer design, local polarity, and aromatic scaffold structure can all be tuned without abandoning the thin-layer logic that SERS demands. Still, this apparent simplicity should not be overstated. Performance remains strongly influenced by pH, local hydration, competitive saccharides, and the steric accessibility of the boronic-acid sites, so reports of “specific” glucose recognition must be interpreted carefully unless comparison against realistic interferents is convincing.

A further reason boronic acids became dominant is that they do more than capture glucose: they also participate in transduction. Rather than treating recognition and Raman reporting as separate layers, several boronic-acid-based platforms exploit spectral changes in the interfacial ligand itself, or in a closely linked reporter, after glucose binding.23,24,42–44 This dual function is highly advantageous for glucose, whose own Raman signal is comparatively weak and difficult to measure selectively in complex media. In such cases, the boronic-acid interface becomes not merely a capture motif but a chemically responsive interfacial element that helps convert recognition into a more readable SERS output. That design logic helps explain why boronic-acid systems have remained dominant across otherwise diverse glucose SERS formats. Their central role in the field is therefore best understood not as a claim of ideal saccharide selectivity, but as the result of a practical convergence: boronic acids are small enough to preserve near-field access, easy enough to graft onto plasmonic materials, versatile enough to support multiple readout schemes, and strong enough in diol recognition to outperform bare-metal capture under many conditions. For this reason, boronic-acid chemistry became the leading synthetic recognition route in glucose SERS, even while its limitations in selectivity and medium dependence continued to motivate comparison with biomolecular and imprinted alternatives.

Main boronic-acid interface designs in glucose SERS

Once boronic acids were adopted as compact synthetic recognition elements for glucose, their implementation in SERS followed a limited number of recurring architectural patterns. Boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS interfaces can be grouped into four main design categories: compact surface-grafted boronic-acid layers (Fig. 2A), boronic-acid-functionalized nanoparticle or assembled substrates (Fig. 2B), confined or transport-assisted boronic-acid formats (Fig. 2C, 4A and B), and hybrid or distributed-function boronic-acid architectures (Fig. 4C). These categories differ in whether boronic-acid recognition is implemented directly at the plasmonic boundary, embedded within a hotspot-rich nanostructured assembly, incorporated into a confined or device-integrated sample-delivery format, or combined with additional enrichment and signal-readout modules. The simplest and most direct of these is the surface-grafted boronic-acid layer, in which thiolated aromatic boronic acids are immobilized directly on an Au or Ag surface so that glucose recognition occurs immediately at the plasmonic boundary. In this category, the main design variable is the interfacial chemistry of the modified surface itself, rather than the construction of a more elaborate nanostructure. This approach is attractive because it keeps the recognition event as close as possible to the enhancing surface, thereby minimizing the distance penalty that often limits SERS efficiency. Early linker-centered strategies and later ligand-mode sensing studies both reflect this logic: the boronic-acid moiety is not placed in a secondary coating or remote capture layer, but integrated into the surface chemistry itself.23,40,44 Such monolayer-like interfaces are therefore the clearest realization of boronic-acid glucose SERS as an interfacial recognition problem. Their main strength lies in structural economy. The receptor is thin, chemically well defined, and directly anchored to the metal, which helps preserve analyte access to the near field. However, that same simplicity imposes constraints. Surface density is not automatically beneficial, since densely packed ligands can reduce steric accessibility or alter local hydration; relatively smooth or only mildly structured modified surfaces may also offer fewer effective hotspots than more elaborate nanostructured systems; and the chemical recognition itself remains only partially selective toward glucose relative to other cis-diol-containing species. Thus, surface-grafted boronic-acid layers provide the most mechanistically transparent interface type, but not always the strongest overall SERS platform.

A second major category consists of boronic-acid-functionalized nanoparticles (NPs) and colloidal assemblies, in which the recognition chemistry is introduced onto plasmonic nanostructures that are themselves a major part of the sensing strategy. Here, the design objective is not merely to create a chemically functionalized surface for glucose capture, but to embed that recognition layer within a nanoparticle-based architecture that already provides high hotspot density and large accessible interfacial area. As a result, boronic-acid ligands are often incorporated into particle-coated supports, nanorod assemblies, or core–shell nanoparticle systems that combine recognition with deliberate electromagnetic coupling.40,42 This class of interface is important because it shifts the analytical emphasis from surface functionalization alone to recognition within a hotspot-engineered plasmonic structure. In principle, such designs improve both the probability of glucose capture and the probability that the bound glucose or ligand-mediated response will be detected. In practice, however, they also introduce greater structural variability. Once boronic-acid recognition is distributed across nanoparticle assemblies, analytical performance becomes more sensitive to particle spacing, aggregation state, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and the stability of the assembled substrate in non-ideal media. The recognition layer may remain chemically compact, but the platform as a whole is no longer minimal. For this reason, nanoparticle-based boronic-acid interfaces are often analytically stronger than simple surface-grafted layers, but mechanistically less clean: improvements in sensitivity can arise from better affinity, better hotspot formation, or both, and the relative contribution of each is not always easy to isolate.

A third design family includes confined or transport-assisted boronic-acid platforms, such as capillary-based, optofluidic, fiber-based, paper-based, or textile-integrated systems. These formats emerged from a practical recognition problem: even when boronic acids improve interfacial affinity, glucose still has to be delivered efficiently to the active surface, especially at low concentration or in matrices where mass transfer is limited. In these systems, boronic-acid recognition is incorporated into a geometry that helps guide, confine, or wick the sample through a SERS-active region rather than relying solely on passive diffusion to a static substrate. Optofluidic in-fiber composites functionalized with 4-mercaptophenylboronic acid, boronic-acid-modified capillary capture surfaces, and Janus textile platforms for sweat analysis all exemplify this logic.41,43,45 What distinguishes this category is not a fundamentally different recognition chemistry, but a different spatial implementation of that chemistry. The boronic-acid layer is still the recognition element, however, it is embedded in a microenvironment that improves analyte-interface contact by controlling how the sample approaches the surface. This is an important design shift in glucose SERS because it acknowledges that weak adsorption cannot be solved by ligand chemistry alone. At the same time, the literature base here is still relatively limited, and many such systems remain proof-of-concept rather than mature format classes. Paper- or textile-based designs, for example, may add sampling convenience or portability, but their true analytical value depends on whether the boronic-acid interface remains stable, accessible, and reproducible under realistic operating conditions. Thus, these formats should be understood primarily as recognition formats with built-in delivery assistance, not yet as evidence that transport engineering has fully solved the underlying selectivity or robustness problem.

The most recent and structurally complex category comprises hybrid boronic-acid architectures with added functional layers, in which boronic-acid recognition remains present but is no longer the sole contributor to performance. Reporter-linked systems, sandwich-type assemblies, and magnetic- or polymer-assisted platforms all fall into this broader class when boronic-acid binding still provides the primary glucose-recognition step. In such designs, boronic acids may be used to capture glucose and simultaneously organize reporter-bearing particles, bridge a capture substrate with a SERS tag, or cooperate with auxiliary materials that enhance substrate loading or hotspot generation.24,46 These architectures are scientifically important because they reveal the limits of purely minimalist boronic-acid interfaces. Once direct glucose capture on a simple modified surface proves insufficient for signal clarity or matrix tolerance, additional layers are introduced to strengthen readout, increase contact probability, or improve recovery from complex samples. From a classification standpoint, these systems still belong in a boronic-acid-centered subsection when the glucose-recognition step depends fundamentally on boronic-acid chemistry. However, they also mark the point at which the mechanistic role of boronic binding becomes harder to separate from platform-level engineering. A high analytical response in a sandwich or multi-hotspot system may reflect not only successful boronic-acid recognition, but also reporter amplification, structural confinement, magnetic enrichment, or improved hotspot coupling. For that reason, hybrid boronic-acid architectures should be treated as an expanded design class rather than as direct evidence that boronic-acid recognition alone has become highly selective or sufficient. Their emergence shows how the field has extended boronic-acid chemistry into more elaborate sensing constructs, but it also underscores that interface complexity tends to increase when simple recognition layers are not enough.

Considered as a whole, these four design types show that boronic-acid glucose SERS has developed less as a single platform and more as a family of interfacial strategies built around one shared recognition motif. Surface-grafted monolayers represent the most direct and conceptually pure implementation; nanoparticle and assembled substrates embed the same chemistry into hotspot-rich nanostructures; confined or transport-assisted formats adapt boronic recognition to analyte-delivery constraints; and hybrid architectures extend it into multicomponent sensing systems where recognition is increasingly coupled to other functions. This architectural progression helps explain why boronic acids remained central even as individual platform designs diversified: the chemistry is sufficiently compact and versatile to be translated across very different SERS formats without losing its core recognition role. At the same time, the more elaborate the interface becomes, the less appropriate it is to attribute performance solely to boronic-acid binding. This tension between recognition simplicity and architectural complexity leads directly to the next question, namely which design variables actually control performance across these boronic-acid interface types.

Key design variables controlling performance in boronic-acid glucose SERS interfaces

A first variable is the chemical speciation of the boronic-acid receptor at the interface. In glucose SERS systems, boronic acid should not be treated as a chemically fixed surface label. Its recognition behavior depends on the local equilibrium between the neutral trigonal boronic-acid form and the tetrahedral boronate form, and that equilibrium is affected by pH, hydration, ionic composition, and the immediate nanoscale environment surrounding the surface-bound ligand.17 Because the receptor is immobilized rather than freely dissolved, its apparent binding behavior is determined not only by intrinsic boronic-acid/diol chemistry but also by how the metal-supported interfacial phase alters protonation state, local polarity, and vibrational response. Experimental SERS studies support this interpretation. Sun et al. used boronic-acid-functionalized aligned Ag nanorod arrays for glucose sensing, while Pham et al. showed that the SERS behavior of 4-MPBA on GO@SiO2@Ag substrates changed with pH, indicating that the receptor layer itself is chemically environment-sensitive rather than analytically invariant.47,48 Accordingly, pH in these systems is not merely a measurement condition. It is a mechanistic control parameter that determines whether the immobilized boronic-acid layer is in a binding-competent state and whether glucose complexation produces a usable spectral perturbation.

A second variable is surface density and steric accessibility. Here, the central issue is not chemical speciation, but how many receptor sites remain physically available to glucose once the monolayer is formed. The literature does not support a monotonic “more ligand is better” model. Increasing boronic-acid loading changes lateral packing, exposed headgroup area, and the steric openness of the interface. Once the surface becomes too crowded, glucose access can be restricted even if the nominal number of receptor sites increases. This point is especially clear in the planar work of Kong et al., where boronic-acid-mediated binding and secondary complex formation had to take place directly on the SERS-active substrate; under those conditions, an analytically useful interface had to remain accessible rather than simply receptor-rich.22 A comparable trend was reported by Pham et al., who found that the SERS intensity of GO@SiO2@Ag NPs@MPBA reached a maximum at an intermediate 4-MPBA concentration rather than rising continuously with further modifier addition.48 This means that the relevant design parameter is not absolute loading, but effective receptor availability. An optimal boronic-acid layer must be dense enough to support reliable glucose capture, but open enough to permit diffusion, interfacial hydration, and local rearrangement during reversible diol binding.

A third variable is the distance between the recognition event and the plasmonic near field. This is a distinct issue from steric accessibility. Even if glucose can reach the receptor and form a complex efficiently, the resulting event is analytically weak unless the chemically perturbed bonds or reporter modes lie within the region of strong field enhancement. Because SERS enhancement decays rapidly away from the metal surface, boronic-acid glucose platforms are usually built around short, directly anchored ligands rather than thick interlayers or remote recognition films.13,17 This is why boronic-acid chemistry is so often implemented through compact aromatic thiols on Ag or Au/Ag nanostructures. In Pham et al., 4-MPBA was immobilized directly on Au@Ag shell assemblies so that glucose-responsive chemistry remained field-coupled.42 In Chen et al., quantitative glucose analysis was achieved on a functionalized nanorod-assembled plasmonic substrate, again preserving short spatial separation between the recognition layer and the enhancing surface.40 In Zhang et al., the sandwich architecture was designed so that glucose-mediated assembly produced a SERS-active configuration rather than a remote capture event, allowing the analytical signal to arise from a strongly enhanced local geometry.24 Thus, this variable is fundamentally spatial: successful boronic-acid glucose SERS requires not only binding, but binding that occurs close enough to the hotspot to be spectroscopically amplified.

A fourth variable is how binding is converted into analytical selectivity in complex media. This is separate from the preceding questions of speciation, packing, and distance. Boronic acids provide reversible cis-diol recognition, but they do not by themselves guarantee glucose-exclusive analytical discrimination; practical selectivity therefore depends on the design of the transduction scheme and on how the interface behaves in the presence of competing matrix components.13,17 In direct-readout systems, selectivity relies on whether glucose binding produces a spectral change that is sufficiently characteristic and reproducible to be distinguished from baseline drift, interfacial heterogeneity, and chemically related interferents. In other systems, the assay is deliberately engineered so that selectivity is produced downstream of initial binding. Pham et al. used glucose oxidase/H2O2-mediated conversion chemistry because direct signal changes at physiological pH were not strong enough for reliable quantitative analysis.42 Zhang et al. improved analytical discrimination through a sandwich configuration and silent-region reporter readout, while Yu et al. extended the concept into a Janus textile platform for sweat monitoring, where signal quality depends not only on receptor chemistry but also on the integrated transport and substrate design.24,43 The implication is that boronic-acid affinity should not be equated with assay selectivity. In practice, selectivity emerges from the combined performance of receptor chemistry, readout logic, and matrix management.

These interdependent factors define a clear design framework for boronic-acid glucose SERS. The receptor must first exist in a chemically binding-competent state; it must then be packed densely enough to support capture without becoming sterically inaccessible; the recognition event must occur within the effective plasmonic near field; and the resulting perturbation must be translated into a selective analytical signal under realistic conditions. Framed this way, the performance of boronic-acid glucose SERS is not determined by receptor identity alone, but by how chemical speciation, steric organization, spatial positioning, and transduction strategy are co-optimized at a single interface.

Boronic-acid-centered interfaces as the dominant functional strategy

Among the functional strategies developed for glucose SERS sensing, boronic-acid-centered interfaces remain the most mature because they offer a practical balance between molecular compactness, surface compatibility, reversible cis-diol recognition, and adaptability in readout design. Recent reviews still identify boronic-acid chemistry as the central practical route in glucose SERS, not because it has solved the selectivity problem in an absolute sense, but because it provides the most versatile chemical handle for converting glucose binding into an interfacial spectroscopic event.13 The key point, however, is that boronic-acid-centered sensing is no longer a single design type. Its development has proceeded along four increasingly distinct directions: first, the construction of more controlled direct-recognition interfaces; second, the engineering of stronger and cleaner transduction pathways; third, the adaptation of these platforms to realistic matrices and device formats; and fourth, the emergence of hybrid architectures in which recognition is only one module within a larger sensing system. These four directions clarify how recent progress has occurred at different levels of interface design.

From compact boronic-acid layers to better-controlled direct-recognition interfaces

The earliest boronic-acid glucose SERS studies established a principle that still defines this branch of the field: if glucose is to be sensed at all, the recognition layer must be kept compact and sufficiently coupled to the SERS-active surface that binding changes can be translated into a measurable interfacial response. Kong et al. showed this clearly in their planar alkyne-functionalized boronic-acid substrate for glucose sensing in biological media (Fig. 2A).22 What made that study foundational was not only the demonstration of sensing itself, but the recognition that boronic-acid chemistry had to be organized as a true interfacial layer rather than treated as a generic receptor coating. In other words, the problem was already being framed spatially: glucose binding needed to occur within a distance regime and geometric environment compatible with efficient SERS generation.

In this context, the distinction between compact and controlled boronic-acid interfaces is architectural rather than purely chemical. Compact interfaces place the boronic-acid receptor directly at the plasmonic boundary, whereas controlled interfaces organize the same recognition chemistry within nanoparticle assemblies, confined geometries, or other structured environments that regulate analyte access, hotspot proximity, and signal generation.

Against this background, recent boronic-acid-centered platforms can be viewed as a shift from compact recognition layers to more controlled recognition environments. In compact planar interfaces, the boronic-acid receptor is placed directly on the plasmonic surface, so glucose recognition depends mainly on whether the analyte can reach and bind near the metal surface. In more controlled architectures, the receptor chemistry remains MPBA or 4-MPBA, but the platform is designed to control where that recognition occurs relative to nanoparticle assemblies, sample-transport pathways, and plasmonic hotspots. Gao et al. moved beyond a relatively simple planar recognition layer by embedding 4-MPBA in an optofluidic in-fiber composite containing graphene oxide (GO) and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for cerebrospinal-fluid glucose sensing (Fig. 2C).41 The value of that design lies in how it organizes recognition, mass transport, and plasmonic enhancement in a single confined geometry. Pham et al. pursued a parallel objective through an MPBA-immobilized Au–Ag core–shell assembled silica substrate (Fig. 2B).42 Although that system still relies partly on glucose oxidase/H2O2-mediated chemistry and therefore does not represent a fully direct boronic-acid-only readout, it is still relevant here because it reflects the same broader move toward a more structured and stable glucose-responsive interface. The common thread in these studies is therefore not new receptor chemistry, but better spatial control over where and how the glucose-responsive boronic-acid layer operates.

Seen from this perspective, the direct-recognition branch of boronic-acid glucose SERS has evolved from proof that compact functionalized surfaces can work into a more demanding effort to engineer the recognition layer as a reproducible nanoscale microenvironment. The emphasis here is on interfacial discipline: controlling proximity to hotspots, constraining local geometry, and preserving access of glucose to the active layer without losing signal efficiency. This distinction separates advances in interfacial organization from later developments in signal encoding. At this stage, the central issue is not how the signal is encoded, but how the glucose-responsive boronic-acid layer is physically organized at the sensing boundary.

From foundational interfacial readout to increasingly engineered transduction

If the first direction concerns how the recognition layer is positioned, the second concerns how the resulting signal is read. Here, the history is better described as a progression from relatively simple interface-coupled readout to increasingly engineered transduction, rather than as a shift from “direct glucose detection” to “indirect detection”. In fact, the boronic-acid route was interfacial from the beginning. One of the clearest early examples is the transition-metal carbonyl probe assay reported by Kong et al., which already treated glucose binding as a trigger for a designed reporter response rather than relying on the native Raman signature of glucose itself (Fig. 3A).22 That study was conceptually important because it made clear that successful boronic-acid glucose SERS did not depend on glucose becoming a strong Raman scatterer; it depended on building a more favorable readout around the recognition event.

Later work refined this logic by making the interface itself increasingly signal-bearing. Yang et al. showed that glucose could be sensed through surface-enhanced Raman mode constraining, which effectively shifted analytical attention from glucose alone to the glucose-conditioned vibrational state of the boronic-acid-modified surface (Fig. 3B).23 More recent studies then expanded this principle in two complementary ways. Zhang et al. used boronic-acid-mediated recognition in a sandwich assay coupled to AEPO reporting near 1977 cm−1 in the Raman-silent region (Figure 3C),24 while Si et al. designed a boronic-acid-functionalized capillary platform using nitrile-coded SERS tags with a built-in band at 2222 cm−1 for urine glucose sensing (Fig. 3D).49 In both cases, the real advance lies in spectral engineering: the readout is moved into a cleaner and more analytically manageable signal channel.

A different but related refinement appears in the 2026 work of Ingilala et al., where 3-thienylboronic acid-Ag nanoclusters were used so that glucose binding was read through aromatic ring-stretching changes of the linker itself.44 This does not move away from engineered transduction; rather, it pursues it through a more compact strategy. Instead of adding an external reporter, the interface is designed so that the boronic-acid-derived linker becomes the reporter. This is why reporter-assisted silent-region systems and linker-mode systems belong together in the same conceptual lane: both are responses to the same limitation of native glucose SERS. Their difference is only in where the transduction burden is placed on an added signal element or on the recognition layer itself. Framed this way, the progression is coherent: early boronic-acid studies established interfacial readout, later work made that readout cleaner, and the newest designs are experimenting with different ways to balance signal clarity against architectural simplicity.

From proof-of-concept sensing to matrix-aware and portable formats

A third and separate direction concerns not the recognition layer or the transduction mechanism in isolation, but the adaptation of boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS to realistic matrices and usable formats. This matters because even a well-designed interfacial readout remains analytically limited if the overall platform cannot manage sample delivery, confinement, or interference in a meaningful way. Recent work therefore increasingly treats matrix context as a design input rather than as a final validation step. In this respect, the field is moving from purely conceptual sensing demonstrations toward platforms that begin to acknowledge the practical conditions under which glucose would actually be measured.

The more recent literature illustrates this in several distinct sample contexts. Gao et al. built their 4-MPBA/GO/Ag system in an optofluidic in-fiber format for cerebrospinal-fluid glucose sensing, making confined transport part of the sensing concept (Fig. 4A).41 Yu et al. then pushed the logic further by integrating glucose and lactate sensing into a Janus textile platform aimed at sweat analysis and portable wellness monitoring (Fig. 4B).43 These studies do not all solve the same practical problem, but they do share one direction of travel: the boronic-acid interface is no longer treated as a static chemical layer on a conventional lab substrate, but as part of a device format that must negotiate sample contact, fluid handling, and matrix background together with recognition and signal generation.

Matrix-aware design is therefore distinct from transduction design. A platform may use silent-region tags or linker-mode readout and still remain analytically fragile if the sample format is poorly matched to the sensing surface. Conversely, a well-chosen device format can improve usability even when the recognition chemistry and signal mechanism are not radically new. The main contribution of this branch of the literature is therefore architectural adaptation to sample context: urine, cerebrospinal fluid, or sweat are not just test media, but environments that reshape how the boronic-acid-centered interface must be implemented.

From functionalized interfaces to distributed-function hybrid architectures

The most recent direction is the move toward hybrid systems in which boronic-acid chemistry no longer bears the full sensing burden alone. This is conceptually different from matrix-aware formatting. In matrix-aware platforms, the main question is how to adapt the sensing surface to sample conditions; in hybrid architectures, the main question is how to divide sensing function across multiple modules so that recognition, enrichment, and signal generation no longer need to be performed by a single interface. This is an important shift because it acknowledges a practical truth that the earlier literature sometimes left implicit: for a target such as glucose, the demands of capture, localization, and readout are often too great to be handled optimally by one compact receptor-modified plasmonic surface.

The 2025 system reported by Sun et al. captures this transition well. By combining boric-acid-affinity magnetic materials with multi-hotspot SERS substrates, the platform distributes analytical work between modules that can be tuned for different roles (Fig. 4C).46 Affinity magnetic materials can assist capture or preconcentration, while the SERS substrate can be optimized more specifically for hotspot generation and optical output. In this type of design, boronic-acid chemistry remains central, but now as the recognition anchor within a broader coordinated architecture. That is analytically significant because it helps explain why boronic-acid-centered sensing remains dominant despite the intrinsic limitations of boronic-acid/glucose chemistry. Its real strength is adaptability: boronic-acid recognition can be retained while the surrounding platform becomes more elaborate and more performance-oriented.

At the same time, hybridization changes how the literature should be interpreted. Once signal enhancement, analyte enrichment, and recognition are distributed across multiple components, improved analytical performance can no longer be attributed to the boronic-acid interface alone. Gains may arise from better capture efficiency, stronger hotspot density, cleaner signal routing, or better coordination between modules. This does not weaken the value of such systems; it simply means that the field has moved from asking how to optimize a boronic-acid-functionalized surface to asking how to build a complete sensing architecture around boronic-acid recognition. This distinction marks system integration as a separate level of design. It is about system integration, not merely recognition, not merely transduction, and not merely sample format.

Seen in this sequence, the recent boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS literature now reads as a progression across four clearer levels of design. First, the recognition layer was made more spatially controlled at the plasmonic boundary. Second, the readout was made more deliberately engineered through reporter-based, silent-region, or linker-mode transduction. Third, the resulting platforms were adapted to more realistic matrices and portable device formats. Fourth, the whole sensing problem began to be redistributed across hybrid architectures in which boronic-acid recognition is only one coordinated function among several. Framed in this way, the recent boronic-acid-centered literature becomes easier to interpret because each stage answers a different question: where binding occurs, how it is read, under what sample conditions it operates, and how much of the sensing burden is assigned to the boronic-acid interface itself. That, in turn, explains why boronic-acid-centered interfaces remain the dominant practical strategy in glucose SERS sensing while still continuing to diversify in form.

Critical assessment of boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS interfaces

Viewed as a whole, boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS has become the dominant synthetic strategy not because boronic acids solve glucose recognition completely, but because they offer an unusually practical balance between interfacial compactness, surface compatibility, and chemically interpretable response. Compared with bulkier biorecognition elements, arylboronic-acid motifs are small enough to be incorporated into self-assembled or nanoparticle-supported plasmonic interfaces without necessarily displacing the recognition event too far from the field-intensive region, and they can be coupled to Au or Ag through established surface chemistries.17,22,40,50,51

In practical terms, this has made boronic-acid recognition unusually portable across substrate formats. It has been implemented on planar surfaces, colloidal nanoparticle assemblies, nanorod-based plasmonic substrates, capillary and fiber platforms, and more recently wearable or enrichment-assisted systems.22,24,40,41,43,46,49,50 A second advantage is that the boronic-acid-diol interaction is reversible and chemically tunable, which allows the sensing layer to function not only as a capture interface but also as a spectroscopically responsive linker layer or as part of a sandwich-type recognition architecture.22,24,44 Together, these features explain why boronic-acid chemistry has remained the most adaptable route for glucose-oriented SERS interface design.

At the same time, however, this practical success should not be mistaken for complete molecular selectivity. Boronic acids do not recognize glucose with absolute exclusivity; rather, they interact more generally with cis-diol-containing compounds, and the apparent response can depend strongly on receptor structure, local surface organization, and medium conditions such as pH and ionic composition.17,51–53 In a SERS context, this means that improved analytical behavior often reflects a combination of factors rather than a purely recognition-driven gain. Boronic-acid-functionalized interfaces can certainly improve glucose responsiveness, but they do not automatically eliminate the broader selectivity constraints inherent to diol-binding chemistry.24,40,49

This distinction matters because the strongest performance gains in boronic-acid-based SERS do not all arise from the same mechanism. In some systems, the key advance is the creation of a cleaner reporter window, such as alkyne or nitrile readout, so that glucose binding is inferred through a more isolated spectral change from the interfacial layer itself.22,24,49 In other systems, the dominant improvement appears to come from better plasmonic organization or hotspot formation, as in nanorod assemblies or structured wearable substrates, where the boronic-acid layer is only one component of the overall performance gain.40,43 Still other designs benefit from fluidic or enrichment-assisted handling that increases local analyte access to the sensing zone.41,46 More recent linker-mode approaches likewise reinforce the same general lesson: the measurable signal is often generated by the functionalized interface responding to glucose, not by direct observation of glucose alone.44

A second persistent limitation is environmental sensitivity. Because boronic-acid-diol complexation is condition-dependent, performance obtained in simplified aqueous systems does not automatically translate to more complex media. Urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and sweat demonstrations represent important progress precisely because they begin to test boronic-acid SERS under chemically crowded conditions, where ionic composition, competing species, and matrix-dependent surface effects become much more consequential.41,43,49 Even so, it often remains difficult to disentangle true recognition robustness from the stabilizing influence of reporter design, substrate architecture, or signal-normalization strategy.24,46,49

When magnetic enrichment, wearable transport layers, secondary capture steps, or spectrally encoded reporter schemes are added, the analytical response can improve substantially, but the contribution of boronic-acid recognition alone becomes harder to isolate.24,43,46,49 That does not diminish the value of these platforms; rather, it clarifies how the field has evolved. The most effective recent boronic-acid-based glucose SERS systems are no longer simple receptor-coated metal surfaces. They are integrated sensing interfaces in which recognition chemistry, plasmonic architecture, signal transduction, and sample handling are deliberately co-designed. In that sense, boronic-acid-centered glucose SERS should be viewed not as a solved problem of molecular selectivity, but as the most mature current example of how functionalized interfaces can make an intrinsically weak SERS target analytically accessible.

Biomolecular and imprinting-assisted recognition interfaces

Beyond simple boronic-acid-modified plasmonic interfaces, several alternative or hybrid recognition strategies have been explored for SERS glucose sensing, including enzyme-centered systems, aptamer-mediated recognition, and molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP)-assisted interfaces. These approaches do not always eliminate boronic-acid chemistry entirely; in some cases, especially MIP-based platforms, boronic-acid functional groups are retained as part of the imprinting or signal-transduction layer. Their relevance lies in how they redistribute the roles of recognition, interfacial positioning, and spectral readout.

Biomolecular interfaces

Biomolecular interfaces improve glucose selectivity by introducing a recognition element whose behavior is more specific than bare-surface adsorption or simple synthetic affinity. Their core principle is therefore recognition by biological molecular function rather than by direct glucose-metal interaction. In enzyme-centered systems, glucose is identified through selective catalytic conversion, and the SERS signal usually reports the chemical consequences of that conversion rather than intact glucose itself. In aptamer-based systems, recognition is instead based on selective binding and a binding-induced structural or positional change, which can then be converted into a SERS response through reporter displacement, distance modulation, or altered hotspot access. In both cases, the main attraction is higher molecular specificity, whereas the main interfacial penalty is that the recognition event often becomes more structurally complex and less directly coupled to the near-field region required for efficient SERS. This helps explain why biomolecular glucose recognition remains scientifically important, but has not generated a literature base as extensive or as structurally coherent as that of boronic-acid-centered systems.13

Within glucose SERS, the most established biomolecular route is the enzyme-centered one, because it provides a chemically direct way to convert poor glucose adsorbability into a more SERS-readable event. Here the recognition principle is catalytic transduction: glucose is first recognized by an enzyme, most commonly glucose oxidase, and the analytical signal is generated from a downstream chemical consequence of that recognition. Accordingly, the Raman-observed species is often not glucose itself, but a reporter, product, or local chemical change generated by the enzyme-mediated process. The study by Qi et al. illustrates this logic clearly. Their glucose oxidase-based SERS probe demonstrated that biochemical glucose recognition can indeed be coupled to a plasmonic Raman interface.54 Nevertheless, the analytical significance of such work lies precisely in the fact that enzyme-based specificity does not solve the weak intrinsic glucose–surface interaction directly; rather, it bypasses that weakness by converting glucose recognition into a more SERS-compatible chemical event. The benefit is improved selectivity. The cost is that the sensing logic becomes less purely interfacial and more dependent on reaction coupling.

Later enzyme-centered studies reinforced the same principle while making the interface architecture more elaborate. A representative example is the MPBA-immobilized Au–Ag core–shell assembled silica platform discussed in Section 3.4.2, in which the SERS-active assembly is coupled with glucose oxidase/H2O2-mediated chemistry to generate a glucose-responsive signal (Fig. 2B).42 In another approach, Hu et al. reported SERS-active AuNPs with enzyme-mimicking activities for measuring glucose and lactate in living tissues, showing that catalytic function and plasmonic enhancement can be integrated into a single nanosystem (Fig. 5A).38 Wang et al. later used hydrogel microspheres embedded with Ag nanobipyramids for pretreatment-free glucose detection in biological samples, illustrating how microstructured materials can be designed to couple catalytic specificity with better local optical control and sample handling.55 These studies are important because they show that biomolecular glucose SERS has moved beyond simple proof-of-concept reaction schemes toward more deliberate interface engineering. At the same time, they also make the limitation of the route more visible: much of the observed performance arises from the combined architecture of catalyst, reporter environment, and plasmonic scaffold rather than from glucose recognition alone. In other words, the enzyme route strengthens specificity, but usually by increasing the complexity of the transduction pathway.

Aptamer-based glucose SERS rests on a different principle. Instead of catalytic conversion, it relies on selective binding followed by a binding-induced structural change that can be optically transduced. In principle, this makes aptamers especially attractive for SERS because a conformational shift, probe displacement, or change in reporter proximity can produce a large spectral effect even when the target itself is a weak Raman scatterer. Aptamers are also more programmable than proteins and potentially more compatible with nanoscale optical architectures. This broader logic is already well established in the small-molecule aptamer-SERS literature, which has expanded substantially in recent years.56 For glucose specifically, however, the literature remains limited, so the principle is clearer than the evidential base.

Recent work has begun to test whether aptamer-based SERS logic can be extended to glucose. Wang et al. reported a SERS aptasensor built from Au bipyramids, a thiolated aptamer labeled with methylene blue (MB), and a PEG blocking layer to reduce nonspecific adsorption (Fig. 5B).57 In this design, glucose binding induces a conformational change in the MB-labeled aptamer, altering the distance between the MB reporter and the SERS-active surface and thereby changing the MB reporter signal. The analytical readout therefore does not come from the intrinsic Raman signature of glucose itself, but from an aptamer-mediated recognition-to-transduction process. This is an important conceptual shift because it shows that glucose selectivity can, in principle, be introduced through a programmable recognition element that is decoupled from bare glucose adsorption on the metal surface. At the same time, the study is better read as an early proof of feasibility than as evidence of a mature glucose-SERS platform. Although selective and sensitive detection was demonstrated and the method was validated in saliva, broader questions of small-molecule aptamer robustness, matrix tolerance, and reproducibility across clinically realistic conditions remain open. Its value therefore lies in demonstrating that selective binding and purposeful recognition-to-readout coupling can be combined in a diabetes-related SERS format, while the current literature remains too sparse to support strong conclusions about superiority over enzyme-centered or boronic-acid-centered systems.

Viewed from this perspective, biomolecular interfaces clarify an important point for glucose SERS. Higher specificity is not achieved here by making glucose intrinsically more Raman-active, but by coupling glucose recognition to a biological function that produces a more readable optical consequence. The essential design question therefore becomes twofold: first, whether the biological recognition event is sufficiently selective; and second, whether that event is translated into a Raman signal without losing too much proximity to the hotspot. This is why biomolecular interfaces remain instructive even though they have not emerged as a compact, broadly adopted replacement for boronic-acid-centered surface chemistry. They reveal that recognition and readout are separable design problems, and that improving the former does not automatically resolve the latter.

Molecularly imprinted interfaces

Molecularly imprinted interfaces follow a different recognition principle from both boronic-acid systems and biomolecular ones. Instead of relying on reversible covalent affinity or biological function, they attempt to encode target selectivity into a synthetic cavity formed in the presence of a template. After template removal, rebinding is favored because the cavity retains a complementary size, shape, and local functional environment. The recognition principle is therefore template-defined structural complementarity. For glucose SERS, however, imprinting alone is not enough. The imprinted region must also remain thin, permeable, and sufficiently close to the plasmonic surface that rebinding occurs within an optically useful near field. This is why molecular imprinting is attractive in principle for glucose sensing but difficult in practice for SERS. More broadly, the MIP-SERS literature has already shown that imprinting and plasmonic readout are not inherently incompatible, provided that the imprinted layer is kept structurally accessible to the electromagnetic field.58

This SERS-specific constraint is particularly important for glucose. Glucose is small, highly hydrophilic, and structurally similar to other saccharides, so a nominally matching cavity does not by itself guarantee useful selectivity. In addition, a cavity that is chemically well defined may still be analytically ineffective if diffusion is slow, if rebinding occurs too far from the metal surface, or if the polymer framework partially screens the recognition event from the hotspot. The central design problem in glucose-imprinted SERS is therefore not only whether a selective cavity can be formed, but whether that cavity can function without imposing an excessive distance or transport penalty. This is the key difference between imprinting as a chemical concept and imprinting as a practical SERS interface.

The broader MIP-SERS literature provides many useful precedents for how this principle can succeed or fail. Systems reported for analytes such as melamine, bisphenol A, phthalate plasticizers, and tryptamine show that selective rebinding can indeed be combined with strong plasmonic enhancement when the imprinted layer is integrated carefully with the metallic substrate.59–65 At the same time, these examples also show why MIP-SERS performance is often overinterpreted. Improved analytical figures of merit may arise not only from better cavity selectivity, but also from substrate nanostructure, local enrichment, transport control, or reporter configuration. Thus, even in successful imprinted systems, recognition chemistry and platform engineering are not easily separable.

That caution becomes even more important for glucose-specific MIP-SERS. The recent urinary glucose platform reported by Liu et al. is significant because it provides one of the few glucose-oriented examples in which molecular imprinting is coupled directly with a SERS substrate for analysis in a urine matrix. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, the strategy involves construction of a three-dimensional plasmonic substrate, surface functionalization with 4-MPBA, glucose-templated formation of an imprinted layer, and subsequent template removal to generate glucose-recognition cavities near the SERS-active interface.39 In this sense, the platform is MIP-based, because glucose recognition is assisted by template-generated cavities formed during the imprinting process. At the same time, it should not be framed as evidence that a purely imprinting-based glucose-SERS solution has already matured. The recognition layer is constructed with 4-MPBA as the functional monomer, and the principal analytical bands used for readout are assigned to 4-MPBA-related vibrations that change upon glucose complexation. The platform is therefore better understood as a boronic-acid-assisted MIP-SERS interface rather than as an imprinting strategy independent of boronic-acid chemistry. Within that hybrid framework, this approach stands out because it combines glucose-oriented imprinting, selective interfacial design, a three-dimensional plasmonic substrate, and model-assisted spectral analysis in a urine-oriented sensing workflow. Its main value lies in showing that integrated glucose-imprinting strategies are becoming experimentally credible, not that imprinting alone has already solved the broader problems of selectivity, robustness, and interfacial-readout coupling that continue to limit the field.

Compared with biomolecular interfaces, MIP systems offer a more synthetic and potentially more stable route to glucose recognition. Compared with boronic-acid-centered interfaces, they promise stronger structural selectivity in principle. But their practical success still depends on a demanding combination of factors: the cavity must be chemically meaningful, spatially accessible, optically proximate, and compatible with realistic sample transport. For that reason, molecular imprinting should presently be treated as a strategically important but still developing route in glucose SERS. It is conceptually attractive because it seeks to build selectivity directly into the interface itself, but it remains less established than boronic-acid systems and less experimentally mature than enzyme-centered biomolecular sensing if judged strictly by the current glucose-specific SERS evidence base.

Boronic-acid systems, biomolecular interfaces, and MIP interfaces therefore operate through different recognition principles, respectively based on reversible affinity chemistry, biological function, and template-generated structural complementarity. However, in all three cases, analytical success depends on more than recognition alone. It also depends on whether the recognition event occurs close enough to the hotspot, whether it is translated into a clear Raman signal, and whether the surrounding platform supports transport, enrichment, and matrix tolerance. For this reason, apparent improvements in glucose SERS should not be attributed too quickly to receptor chemistry alone.

Comparative assessment and design priorities for functionalized glucose SERS interfaces

The preceding sections show that functionalization is not a minor refinement in glucose SERS, but the central strategy that makes glucose analytically accessible at all. Bare noble-metal surfaces provide strong plasmonic enhancement, but glucose itself adsorbs weakly, lacks intrinsically favored surface chemistry, and is readily outcompeted in complex media. Functionalized interfaces address that mismatch in different ways: some improve affinity, some impose stronger molecular discrimination, and some translate otherwise weak recognition events into more readable Raman outputs. A useful comparison therefore asks not which interface is universally best, but which type of interfacial design solves which part of the glucose-sensing problem most effectively.

Comparative assessment of current interface strategies

The most informative way to compare functionalized SERS interfaces for glucose is not simply by reported detection limit, but by asking what kind of molecular control each strategy contributes at the plasmonic surface. On that basis, boronic-acid interfaces, biomolecular interfaces, and MIP interfaces solve different parts of the glucose-sensing problem and should not be treated as analytically interchangeable.

Boronic-acid-centered interfaces are presently the most mature and practically adaptable family in glucose SERS. Their main advantage is structural compactness: arylboronic acids such as 4-MPBA or related thiolated boronic-acid ligands can be anchored directly to Au or Ag while keeping the glucose-binding event close to the near field, allowing recognition, interfacial positioning, and spectral readout to be integrated within a thin surface layer.22,40,50 At the same time, boronic-acid recognition is fundamentally based on reversible cis-diol binding rather than uniquely glucose-exclusive lock-and-key recognition, so analytical improvements should generally be interpreted as enhanced affinity and more useful interfacial capture within a specific surface environment rather than absolute molecular exclusivity.17,66 This is precisely why boronic-acid platforms have evolved in multiple directions, including direct interfacial binding schemes, silent-region reporter strategies, aggregation-based turn-on formats, and more elaborate signal-conversion designs.22,40,50

Biomolecular interfaces occupy a different position. Enzymes, glucose-binding proteins, and aptamer-type receptors offer, in principle, stronger chemical discrimination than boronic-acid motifs, especially when the goal is to distinguish glucose from structurally related interferents under controlled physiological conditions.67 In SERS, enzyme-based systems have shown that glucose recognition can be converted into a measurable Raman response through enzyme-mediated chemistry rather than simple direct adsorption.54,68 Aptamer-based SERS transduction has likewise emerged as a route for diabetes-related biomarker analysis, although glucose-specific SERS applications remain less developed than boronic-acid systems.57 That greater recognition strength, however, does not automatically translate into superior SERS performance. Biomolecular layers are often thicker, softer, and more structurally delicate than small boronic-acid monolayers, which means that the recognition event may occur farther from the hotspot or may require a secondary transduction step before it becomes spectroscopically readable. As a result, biomolecular glucose SERS systems more often depend on enzyme-mediated chemistry, reporter generation, or programmed signal conversion than on direct glucose observation at the metal surface.54,57,68

MIP interfaces represent a third design logic. Their appeal lies in robustness, synthetic tunability, and the possibility of constructing shape- and functionality-biased cavities without using fragile biological components. In principle, MIPs are well matched to harsh or contaminated analytical environments, but their success in SERS depends strongly on whether the imprinted recognition event is actually expressed within an optically useful distance from the plasmonic surface. Thin and accessible imprinting layers, controlled shell thickness, and direct coupling between rebinding and the SERS-active region are repeatedly identified as decisive factors in the broader MIP-SERS literature.58,69 Much of that foundation still comes from non-glucose targets, while glucose-specific MIP-SERS remains relatively sparse. The recent urinary glucose study by Liu et al. showed that MIP selectivity, three-dimensional SERS structuring, and data-driven interpretation can now begin to converge in a glucose-specific format.39

From a design perspective, these three families should be read as complementary rather than competing in a simple winner-takes-all sense. Boronic-acid interfaces currently provide the best practical balance among compactness, surface compatibility, reversible affinity, and flexible signal-design options.17,22,40,42,46,50 Biomolecular interfaces remain the clearest route when the central objective is stronger chemical discrimination, but they still face distance, stability, and implementation penalties in SERS.54,57,67,68 MIP interfaces are attractive for robustness and engineered selectivity, but their glucose-specific SERS development is still at an earlier stage and remains more constrained by shell architecture and transport than the boronic-acid literature.39,58,69Table 2 summarizes these cross-platform differences in terms of recognition basis, SERS coupling mode, analytical strengths and weaknesses, practical stability, current literature maturity, and their most appropriate role in the field.

Analytical performance depends on more than recognition

The present glucose SERS literature is easiest to interpret when performance is separated into at least three layers: recognition chemistry, interfacial positioning near the hotspot, and signal-conversion logic. This separation matters because a platform may show excellent analytical output without proving that its receptor is intrinsically the strongest recognition element. In many successful systems, the final signal reflects not only glucose capture, but also reporter placement, nanoparticle coupling, local field intensity, confinement geometry, or preconcentration effects. Reported performance should therefore be read as the outcome of an integrated interface, not as a pure measure of receptor chemistry alone.

The first layer is recognition chemistry. Boronic-acid ligands improve the probability that glucose is captured or at least chemically engaged at the surface; biomolecular receptors seek stronger molecular discrimination through enzyme specificity, protein binding, or aptamer recognition; and MIPs attempt to encode selectivity into synthetic cavities.17,67,69 These are chemically distinct mechanisms, and they should be judged on their own terms. A receptor that improves capture probability but relies on a secondary reporter is doing something different from one that generates a direct spectral consequence of binding, and both differ again from a receptor that mainly enriches glucose near the surface while hotspot engineering provides most of the signal gain.

The second layer is interfacial positioning relative to the hotspot. SERS remains exceptionally sensitive to distance, so it is not enough for a receptor to bind glucose in a chemically meaningful way; the recognition event must also occur within an optically useful near-field region. This is where compact boronic-acid monolayers have had a persistent practical advantage, whereas thicker biomolecular or polymeric layers can incur a distance penalty unless the plasmonic architecture is deliberately adjusted around them.22,69 Much of the recent literature addresses exactly this issue through engineered assemblies that reorganize where recognition occurs relative to the field. MPBA-immobilized Au–Ag core–shell assembled silica nanostructures were used to couple boronic-acid recognition with a more controlled plasmonic scaffold.42 An optofluidic in-fiber system combining 4-MPBA, graphene oxide, and AgNPs was used for cerebrospinal-fluid glucose detection, integrating recognition with guided transport and spatially organized enhancement.41 A sandwich-type assay was used to separate capture and tag generation while keeping the readout in a designed SERS-active configuration.24 Capillary confinement and a built-in nitrile reporter were introduced for interference-reduced urine analysis.49 A Janus textile platform extended this integration logic into sweat analysis.43 Boric-acid affinity magnetic enrichment was further combined with a multi-hotspot SERS substrate to improve sensitivity through coordinated preconcentration and field engineering rather than receptor chemistry alone.46

The third layer is signal-conversion logic. In many glucose SERS assays, the most readable signal is not a native glucose band but an indirect spectral consequence of glucose binding: a reporter entering a hotspot, a silent-region tag changing intensity, receptor-mediated aggregation, or a sandwich event that makes glucose analytically visible through an engineered Raman label.24,40,49 This is not a weakness in itself; in fact, it is often the reason glucose becomes measurable at all. But it does mean that receptor performance and total platform performance should not be conflated. A chemically modest receptor can appear analytically excellent when paired with highly efficient field localization and a clean reporter strategy, whereas a chemically sophisticated receptor can underperform if the recognition event is kept too far from the hotspot or if spectral readout is poorly designed.

The three-layer framework also clarifies why different functionalization strategies lead to different sensing behavior. In boronic-acid systems, the recognition layer is usually placed directly at the plasmonic boundary, so glucose binding, molecular localization, and SERS readout are spatially compact. This compact arrangement is attractive, but it makes the response strongly dependent on surface density, pH, competing cis-diols, and the efficiency of reporter- or peak-shift-based signal transduction.22–24,49 In biomolecular systems, the layers are more separated. Enzyme-centered platforms use a biological or enzyme-mimicking recognition layer to generate catalytic chemical changes, whereas aptamer-based platforms use binding-induced conformational or distance changes to modulate a reporter signal.38,55,57 In MIP-based systems, the recognition layer is defined by template-derived cavities that help pre-organize glucose-accessible sites near the enhancing interface, although current glucose examples often remain hybrid because boronic-acid chemistry or model-assisted spectral interpretation is still needed to support selective readout.39 Thus, the practical difference among these strategies lies in how the recognition layer, localization layer, and signal-transduction layer are connected at the SERS-active surface.

This layered view reinforces the central point that functionalization is not a surface decoration step, but the mechanism by which glucose sensing becomes possible in SERS. A useful interface must recognize glucose or a glucose-responsive event, place that event within a field-enhanced region, and convert it into a spectral change that remains interpretable in the intended sample matrix. Progress in the field should therefore be judged not only by stronger Raman intensity, but by how coherently these layers are integrated. This leads directly to the question of benchmarking, because reported sensitivity is meaningful only when considered together with selectivity, matrix complexity, reproducibility, and validation design.

Design priorities for the next phase of glucose SERS interfaces

The next phase of glucose SERS development should not ask only which receptor binds glucose more strongly, but which interface best coordinates recognition chemistry, hotspot access, readout clarity, and matrix tolerance in a single design. That broader criterion changes how each interface family should be optimized. For boronic-acid systems, the priority is no longer merely to demonstrate glucose binding, but to improve chemical discrimination without sacrificing compactness. This likely means more deliberate control over the boronic-acid microenvironment, pH response, mixed-monolayer organization, and ratiometric or silent-region readout strategies that make reversible cis-diol binding more analytically selective in complex media.17,66 For biomolecular systems, the central challenge is to preserve their recognition advantage while reducing distance penalties and structural fragility at the SERS interface.57,67 For MIP systems, the priority is to engineer thinner, more permeable, and more spatially controlled imprinting layers so that rebinding occurs where the plasmonic field can actually report it.39,58,69

A second priority is better benchmarking. Future studies should distinguish more explicitly between recognition selectivity, total assay selectivity, and total platform sensitivity. Controls should be designed to show whether improved performance arises from stronger receptor chemistry, from a better reporter mechanism, from more favorable hotspot formation, or simply from improved sample handling. In practice, that means more use of receptor-free controls, nonbinding analog controls, matched plasmonic scaffolds across receptor types, and clearer discussion of what the measured Raman signal physically represents. This is especially important for glucose because the field has advanced precisely by moving away from bare-surface glucose detection and toward engineered transduction schemes. Without such controls, the literature risks over-crediting the receptor when the decisive improvement may instead come from geometry, confinement, or reporter design.14

A third priority is real-matrix validation that is mechanistically informative rather than merely demonstrative. The strongest glucose SERS studies increasingly test urine, sweat, or cerebrospinal fluid, but future work should use those experiments not only to prove applicability, but also to identify which part of the interface fails first under realistic conditions. For boronic-acid platforms, that may mean quantifying susceptibility to competing cis-diol species and ionic-background effects.17,46 For biomolecular platforms, it may mean testing structural stability, nonspecific adsorption, and signal drift under prolonged exposure.54,57 For MIP platforms, it may mean separating the influence of imprint fidelity from diffusion and shell-thickness constraints.39,58,69 Such experiments would make interfacial design principles much more transferable across platforms.

The benchmarking criteria should also reflect the type of recognition interface being tested. For boronic-acid-based platforms, represented by surface-bound, reporter-assisted, peak-shift, and silent-region glucose-SERS designs, the key validation issue is whether the response remains glucose-selective under realistic pH and in the presence of competing cis-diol species, rather than only in simple buffer systems.23,24,47,49 For biomolecular platforms, including enzyme-centered and aptamer-mediated formats, benchmarking should include stability of the recognition layer, immobilization reproducibility, nonspecific adsorption controls, and performance after exposure to biological matrices.38,55,57 For MIP-based platforms, the most important controls are different: comparison between imprinted and non-imprinted substrates, evidence of template removal, rebinding selectivity, and matrix-dependent recovery should be reported.39 These differences mean that a single LOD value is not sufficient for cross-platform comparison; the reported sensitivity must be interpreted together with the controls used to support selective recognition and stable signal generation.

Overall, future glucose-SERS studies would be easier to compare if they reported a common minimum validation set: calibration range, LOD calculation method, selectivity panel, matrix composition, recovery, replicate structure, substrate-to-substrate variability, storage stability, and the specific controls used to verify the recognition mechanism. Such reporting would make it clearer whether a platform is only SERS-active under optimized conditions or genuinely suitable for glucose sensing in the intended sample environment.

Conclusions

Glucose is an intrinsically difficult SERS target on bare noble-metal surfaces, not because it lacks Raman-active vibrations, but because it is poorly matched to the interfacial conditions required for strong, selective, and reproducible signal generation. The central obstacle is therefore not vibrational invisibility, but weak and nonselective access to the plasmonic interface. Functionalized interfaces have accordingly become the defining strategy in glucose SERS detection, not because they create glucose information de novo, but because they increase the likelihood that glucose can be positioned within a chemically and optically favorable environment for detection.

Within this framework, boronic-acid-centered interfaces have emerged as the most developed practical route, largely because they offer an effective compromise among compactness, surface compatibility, reversible affinity, and adaptability across different SERS formats. Their success, however, should be interpreted realistically. In most cases, boronic-acid chemistry improves glucose capture probability more reliably than it guarantees absolute glucose exclusivity, and many of the strongest recent platforms derive their performance from the combined effects of recognition, reporter design, hotspot engineering, and sample-handling architecture. Biomolecular and MIP interfaces remain scientifically important because they broaden the design space and clarify what boronic-acid systems do not fully resolve, particularly with respect to specificity and alternative recognition logic, but they are still less mature as broadly validated glucose-SERS strategies.

Ultimately, the literature shows that effective glucose SERS detection depends on more than recognition alone. It requires the deliberate integration of molecular capture, near-field proximity, readable signal conversion, matrix compatibility, and reproducible interface construction. The most meaningful future advances are therefore likely to come not from simply adding complexity, but from designing functionalized interfaces that are more compact, more interpretable, and more rigorously validated. In that sense, progress in glucose SERS detection will depend less on identifying a single perfect receptor than on learning how to engineer the interface so that glucose can finally behave as a tractable SERS analyte.
