Trend analysis and forecasting of energy humanization in Ghana
Authors: Joseph Kwasi Asafo, Omowumi Iledare, Eric Amoo Bondzie, Shafic Suleman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnergy is a key source of economic growth because many production and consumption activities involve energy as a basic input hence central to the operation of any modern economy. The study constructs an energy humanization index and forecasts the various dimensions of energy humanization in Ghana by 2030 using the minimum-maximum normalization approach and assigning equal weight to each of the different indicators and dimensions. The forecast ascertains the achievability of SDG 7. Exponential smoothing was also adopted for forecasting future values. The study employed a dataset spanning from 1980 to 2021. The analysis revealed a positive trend in energy humanization, particularly within the dimension of energy usage. However, the energy security dimension presents a mixed picture. While some aspects, such as energy availability, have consistently improved, others like energy acceptability, affordability, and technology application have declined over time. This decline is concerning because of the importance of these dimensions in a comprehensive energy humanization framework. Without significant interventions, these crucial aspects are expected to worsen, potentially undermining the overall progress in energy humanization. The study recommends the implementation of carbon capture by IPPs, the promotion of electric vehicles, and investment in smart technologies in Ghana. Also, the Ministry of Finance must be circumspect in imposing more levies on petroleum products and electricity tariff although it is an inelastic good.Keywords: Energy humanization, Energy security, Energy usage, Carbon emissions, Energy consumption, SDG7

Introduction

Energy plays a crucial role in driving economic expansion, as numerous production and consumption activities heavily rely on it as a fundamental input [1]. Looking at it from a physical perspective, the utilization of energy propels economic productivity and industrial growth, serving as a core component in the functioning of contemporary economies [2]. In recent times, governments and energy policymakers around the world have increasingly recognized the significance of Sustainable Energy. Securing an uninterrupted and dependable energy provision is critical to enhance the well-being of individuals, elevate quality of life, and realize sustainable environmental and economic development goals on a global scale. Consequently, policymakers are making significant efforts to understand and pursue net-zero targets. Moreover, the “Agenda 2030 for Sustainable Development” explicitly emphasizes the urgent need for action, aiming to humanize energy by 2030.

The history of energy begins with early humans using fire for cooking, heating, and lighting. This discovery was significant for our species' evolution [3,4]. Over time, humans developed more advanced energy harnessing methods. Waterpower was used for grinding grain as early as 200 BCE, with the first water mills appearing in the Middle East. Wind power was also utilized in ancient times for sailboats and windmills, becoming a vital source of power in Europe by the 12th century [3]. The Industrial Revolution in the 18th and 19th centuries transformed energy utilization. Coal became the primary source for steam engines in transportation and manufacturing, leading to a significant increase in demand. The discovery of oil and natural gas in the 20th century further fuelled energy demand, making them the dominant sources for most of the century [3,4]. Nuclear energy emerged in the mid-20th century, but safety concerns and high costs limited its adoption [3]. In recent years, renewable energy sources such as solar, wind, hydro, and geothermal energy have gained more attention. These sources are environmentally sustainable and emit fewer greenhouse gases than fossil fuels. As nations strive to reduce carbon emissions and transition to sustainable energy, the use of renewable sources is expected to increase in the coming years [3].

The significance of energy in a nation's daily functioning has made securing its supply a geopolitical priority. This concern arose notably after the oil crisis of the 1970s, triggered by the first oil crisis starting in October 1973 [5,6]. This crisis highlighted the vulnerability of industrialized economies to oil price fluctuations. The Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) imposed an oil embargo on several countries, leveraging oil for political goals—a strategy known as the “oil weapon”. Consequently, international oil prices surged from US$ 3 to US$ 12 per barrel. In response to such supply interruptions, the International Energy Agency (IEA) was founded in 1974. The IEA became a trusted source of global energy market information and evolved into a leading international body for enhancing energy security, aiding member and non-member countries in policymaking [6].

Following significant occurrences like the September 11 attacks in 2001, Hurricane Katrina in 2005, and the Russia-Ukraine gas dispute in 2005–2006, the idea of risks expanded beyond conventional disputes between or within states. These events introduced new dimensions of risk, encompassing man-made and natural disasters, along with threats from terrorists or non-state actors engaging in cyberattacks [7]. Many industrialized nations today view energy security as a subject of national security [8]. The concept of energy security and its goals may, however, differ due to the differences in natural resources, political systems, economic well-being, ideologies, and geographical locations among various countries [6].

The conflict between Russia and Ukraine began in February 2014, sparked by disagreements over Crimea and Donbas, further escalation in in February2022, resulted in many governments and businesses globally imposing significant economic sanctions on the Russian government [9]. Russia is a major exporter of oil, gas, and coal in the energy markets in particular. The battle appears to have boosted global stock prices, notably in the renewable energy industries [10]. Given that the Russian Federation is the principal supplier of natural gas and the second-largest exporter of oil, the situation in Ukraine has also affected the supply of fossil fuels and the wider market.

The Rising energy prices may force many developing countries out of energy markets, putting the most vulnerable people at risk. Such a situation is already having an impact on hard-won gains in energy access and poverty reduction, and progress has already been interrupted as a result of the pandemic. This situation is exacerbated by the food and financial difficulties experienced in these nations due to the conflict in Ukraine and the pandemic, placing significant social and economic pressure on governments. A potential “fuel scramble” in which only the highest-paying countries gain access would be devastating for a global system based on trust and proportionality.

The concept of “Energy humanization” is relatively new and largely used by the World Energy Council during the November 2021 World Energy Week and during their 25th World Energy Congress at St. Petersburg in October 2022. According to Dr Wilkinson, Secretary General of the World Energy Council (WEC), many individuals lack awareness of their energy use patterns, costs associated with energy usage, the expenses involved in transitioning to alternative sources, or their own involvement in this process, in discussions about energy. Hence the need to study the concept of “energy humanization, which brings together the concept of energy security and energy usage. Whilst energy security in this context captures energy affordability, technology application, availability, and acceptability, the concept of energy usage will capture energy use and its patterns.

A systematic review conducted by Gasser [6], concluded that energy security has so many different aspects, indicator-based approaches are especially well suited for nation comparisons since the resulting indices are easy to understand and are useful instruments for policymakers’ communication. The result also found that most studies focused on countries in North America, Europe, and Asia and even if the concept of Africa comes into the conversation, it is largely how its energy resources can help the advanced world secure their energy need. The views of Gasser [6] is confirmed by the works of Abdullah et al. (2020), Azzuni and Breyer [11], Augutis et al. [12] and Radovanovic, Filipovic and Pavlovic [13] whose work on the measurement of energy security largely focused on developed countries.

The energy security for people living in Sub-Saharan Africa is being threatened by foreign countries’ efforts to strengthen their oil security. Also, the studies listed above stressed the fact that, since socioeconomic characteristics of a region, cultural practices, the pattern of region energy consumption, the expenses involved in transitioning to alternative sources differ from region to region and even sometimes country-to-country measurements of energy security for policy implementation should be region or country specific depending on the level of policy formulation whether regional or country level policy formulation. This further re-echoed the need for a study that considers the examination of energy security at the country level.

Ghana's energy sector is facing significant challenges that require a focused study on energy humanization. Despite being one of the more stable and rapidly growing economies in Sub-Saharan Africa, Ghana is struggling with issues of energy access and reliability. These challenges include the following: Energy Access - a significant portion of the population, particularly in rural areas, lacks access to reliable electricity. This disparity hampers economic development and quality of life for the rural folks; Energy Reliability - frequent power outages and load shedding disrupt daily activities and economic productivity. This inconsistency in energy supply affects both urban and rural areas; Energy Costs - high energy costs burden households and businesses, limiting economic growth and exacerbating poverty. The expenses involved in transitioning to alternative energy sources are also a significant concern; Environmental Impact – Ghana's reliance on fossil fuels contributes to greenhouse gas emissions, posing environmental and health risks. Transitioning to renewable energy sources is critical for sustainable development.

Why is it important to analyze and forecast energy humanization in Ghana? Analyzing and forecasting energy humanization in Ghana is necessary for several reasons: policy formulation - understanding energy humanization helps policymakers design targeted interventions to improve energy access, reliability, and affordability. It also aids in developing strategies for transitioning to renewable energy sources; economic development - reliable and affordable energy is vital for economic activities. By addressing energy humanization, Ghana can enhance its economic productivity and achieve sustainable growth; and social equity - ensuring equitable access to energy reduces disparities between urban and rural areas, improving overall quality of life. It also addresses issues of energy poverty.

In a nutshell, since the previous studies did not consider the concept of energy humanization in their analysis, a concept that seeks to balance the issue of energy security and energy usage. According to the Secretary General of the WEC, if individuals are aware of their energy use patterns, costs associated with energy usage, the expenses involved in transitioning to alternative sources, the importance and benefits of transitioning, it will enable us to tackle the issue of climate change from energy consumers perspective the main contributors to the emissions much effective and efficient. Therefore, there is a need for a study that will develop a framework for measuring and assessing energy humanization, a gap the current study seeks to fill. This study empirically developed a conceptual framework for analysing and estimating energy humanization based on the ideas from the November 2021 World Energy Week and the 25th World Energy Congress at St. Petersburg in October 2022 and further tested the framework using data from Ghana. To achieve this, the study estimates the various dimensions of the energy humanization index measured using energy security and energy usage within the context of Ghana and forecasts the level of various dimensions of energy humanization up to 2030 to assess the plausibility of achieving Sustainable Goal 7 in Ghana.

The subsequent sections are structured as follows; section two provides a review of existing theoretical and empirical literature and section three presents a description of the data, sample and research methodology. Section four presents the empirical results while section five finally concludes the paper.

Literature review

A general review of relevant literature and the theoretical framework on energy, energy humanization, and energy usage is undertaken here to situate the study and adequately highlight its relevance.

Theoretical framework

The theories considered in this study include the rebound effect theory, energy justice theory, and the 4-A theory of energy security. The rebound effect theory, also known as the Jevons paradox, suggests that improvements in energy efficiency can paradoxically lead to increased energy consumption due to heightened demand for energy-intensive goods and services [14]. This is attributed to decreased energy costs, prompting consumers and firms to use more energy-intensive products and services, offsetting any energy savings. While energy efficiency remains crucial for reducing energy use and greenhouse gas emissions, the rebound effect underscores the need for policies that encourage sustainable behaviour.

Energy justice theory emphasizes the equitable distribution of affordable, reliable, and clean energy, prioritizing marginalized groups' needs and involvement in decision-making processes. It highlights the unequal distribution of energy resources and stresses procedural justice in energy decision-making [15,16]. Finally, the 4-A theory of energy security proposed by the Institute of Energy Economics, Japan, focuses on four key dimensions: availability, applicability, affordability, and acceptability.

By energy availability, we mean a stable and reliable supply of energy resources to meet demand. With applicability, we mean the capacity to apply modern technology to guarantee efficient use of the existing oil and gas reserves as well as electricity. By affordability, we mean ensuring energy prices remain affordable for households, businesses, and industries. With acceptability, the study refers to how well-accepted energy production and use are on a social and environmental basis. It offers a comprehensive framework for addressing energy security challenges, emphasizing a balanced approach considering economic, social, environmental, and political factors to ensure a sustainable and secure energy system [17].

Empirical review

A systematic review was conducted by Gasser [6] to look at studies that focused on indicators for energy security to compare national performances. The study discovered around 102 studies, after removing all duplicates 63 of them were utilized in the systematic review. The study concluded that since energy security has so many different aspects, indicator-based approaches are especially well suited for nation comparisons since the resulting indices are easy to understand and are useful instruments for policymakers’ communication. The majority of research, according to the findings, were conducted mostly in North America, Europe, and Asia. Especially Africa and most of South America lack official records.

Azzuni and Breyer [11] using 15 energy sector indicators to estimate the level of energy security of the selected countries. Among the countries that were used for the study, energy security was at its highest levels in the United States and Germany, and its lowest in Turkmenistan and the Central African Republic. The study concluded that there are several avenues and choices, not just one, for advancing energy security. Nations must exchange knowledge about what works best in their local settings to put these strategies into practice and increase energy security.

Augutis et al. [12] analysed the Baltic States’ performance in terms of their level of energy security using indicators. The study takes into consideration the significant changes the energy industry of the Baltic States underwent between 2008 and 2016. The level of energy security is evaluated using the index-based methodology. Employing an indicator framework that considers the technical, economic, geopolitical, and socio-political aspects of energy security. The results showed that Lithuania has less energy security than Latvia and Estonia. The major cause of these variations is that Lithuania imports a lot of electricity whereas Estonia and Latvia have substantial domestic energy resources like oil shale and hydropower, respectively. Hence, they perceive that each country needs to look at their energy security from their perspective.

The primary objective of Radovanovic et al. [13] is to create a fresh energy security indicator that prioritizes long-term sustainability. The study applied this indicator to a set of 28 European Union nations from 1990 to 2012 to evaluate the impact of six different indicators on energy security. The United Kingdom, France, Germany, and Italy, the four EU countries with the strongest economy, had substantially less volatility in energy security for 23 years than other countries.

Abdullah et al. (2020) evaluate Pakistan's performance in terms of energy security based on an analysis of its energy security index from 1991 to 2018. The study emphasizes the ideas of “availability,” “affordability,” “technology,” “government,” and “environment”. From a pool of twenty-two indicators, the Energy Security Indicators for Pakistan (ESIP) were selected. These indicators were assigned weights through principal component analysis and then normalized using the z-score approach. The research utilizing this index showcased a decline in Pakistan's energy security performance from 1991 to 1999, followed by an improvement until 2018.

Gajdzik et al. [18] focused on the “Our Energy” cooperative in Poland and highlighted the transformative potential of renewable energy sources (RESs). The study demonstrated that energy cooperatives not only offer economic benefits to their members, such as stable energy prices and a share in energy production profits, but also promote environmental sustainability by reducing carbon footprints. The study emphasizes the role of cooperatives in localizing energy production and consumption. This, in turn, strengthens community resilience and energy independence. The study also identified key challenges, including financial barriers, technical limitations, and regulatory hurdles, which can hinder the growth and effectiveness of these initiatives. Additionally, the integration of innovative technologies and stakeholder engagement are frequently discussed as critical factors for the success and scalability of energy cooperatives.

Dudka et al. [19] discuss the role and challenges of renewable energy cooperatives (RECs) in promoting an equitable and inclusive energy paradigm. The study notes that while RECs primarily serve the mutual interests of their members by providing energy services, they also face the challenge of balancing economic viability with broader social missions, such as addressing energy poverty. The study conducted surveys with 5402 members of two RECs, Ecopower in Belgium and Enostra in Italy, as well as 20 semi-structured interviews. The findings reveal mixed sentiments among members regarding the adoption of general-interest missions, indicating a possible divergence between members’ views and those of the cooperative boards. Additionally, the study highlights significant differences between the two RECs, which can be attributed to their unique operational contexts and stages of development.

The study conducted by Gajdzik et al. [18] explores the energy-saving behaviors of household energy prosumers who utilize photovoltaic panels and heat pumps. The study sheds light on the increasing significance of prosumers in terms of achieving energy independence and cost reduction in the economy. Through the utilization of the CAWI method, data from 326 Polish prosumer households were collected. The findings indicate that prosumers exhibit advanced ecological behaviors, with more than half of the respondents actively implementing pro-ecological practices within their homes. The energy independence of prosumers contributes to economic stability and decreases reliance on traditional energy sources. The study emphasizes the overall inclination of prosumers to engage in environmentally friendly behavior and suggests the promotion of prosumer activities and the adoption of pro-ecological energy practices. It is anticipated that such initiatives will lead to a reduction in energy consumption, an increase in ecological awareness, and the cultivation of a sense of environmental responsibility.

Alemzero, Sun, Mohsin, Iqbal, Nadeem, and Vo (2021) using data from a sample of 28 African nations from the years 2000–2018, this study creates a composite index of the continent's energy security and then assesses its effects and trends using principal composite analysis (PCA) on a set of 13 variables. The main findings point to a propensity toward energy insecurity among the studied nations, which is confirmed by their high per capita emissions and preponderance of reliance on energy imports. These findings confirm the depressing situation that exists across Africa.

In the context of Ghana, Acquah et al. (2015) was the only study that attempted to measure Ghana's energy security using the composite energy security index (CESI) developed by Sovacool [16]. In this study, an assessment was conducted to gauge Ghana's energy security status relative to 34 other nations using PCA. The availability, cost, technology development, and efficiency, environmental sustainability, and regulatory and governance elements were among the indicators that were taken into account and categorized. Among the countries evaluated, Tunisia, Libya, Algeria, Egypt, and Morocco exhibited the weakest performance, while Brunei, Japan, New Zealand, the United States, and the EU demonstrated the most robust results. Notably, some of the top-performing African nations were DR Congo, Cameroun, Angola, Tanzania, and Zambia. Ghana ranked 17th in this comparative analysis. However, it is important to note that the study fell short in comprehensively explaining the variables constituting the diverse dimensions of energy security as intended, encompassing availability, affordability, technology development and efficiency, environmental sustainability, as well as regulatory and governance categories, along with the specifics of how these variables were assessed. The study also failed to explain and indicate the procedure employed in the data normalization before employing PCA in aggregating the data. Therefore, there is a need for a new study that will estimate the various dimensions of energy security in the context of Ghana and also forecast the level of various dimensions of energy security up to 2030 to assess if sustainable goal 7 could be achieved by Ghana.

These theories and empirical reviews, as presented in Section 2.1, 2.2 respectively, offer a comprehensive framework for addressing energy humanization. The framework emphasizes a balanced approach that simultaneously considers energy security and energy usage. Its objective is to establish a sustainable and secure energy system that caters to the diverse needs of society. In this study, a conceptual framework has been developed to integrate insights from the rebound effect theory, energy justice theory, and the 4-A theory of energy security. This framework is utilized to analyze and estimate energy humanization in Ghana.

The framework takes into account potential rebound effects by incorporating indicators that measure not only energy efficiency improvements but also energy usage and energy-intensity. Drawing from energy justice theory, the framework also includes measures of equitable access to energy and the distribution of energy resources. This ensures that the framework addresses the needs of all societal groups, particularly the most vulnerable. The 4-A theory of energy security is employed to evaluate the availability, applicability, affordability, and acceptability of energy resources in Ghana. By integrating these dimensions, the framework provides a holistic assessment of energy humanization that simultaneously considers energy security and energy usage (consumption).The empirical development of the framework involved several key steps. Based on the aforementioned theories and empirical reviews, the key dimensions of energy humanization were determined to be energy security and energy usage. Specific indicators were carefully selected to measure each dimension. For example, energy availability, as a sub-dimension of energy security, was assessed using indicators such as energy imports, installed electricity capacity, total energy production from natural gas, total energy production from petroleum and other liquids, dry natural gas production, natural gas reserves, crude oil including lease condensate reserves, and access to electricity. Data was then collected from various sources, as indicated in Table 1. This data provided valuable insights into the current state of energy humanization in Ghana. Data and methodology

In this section, we outline the data, variables, and methodologies employed to achieve the goals of the study. The variables selected to measure the different dimension of energy humanization in this study are guided by the need to comprehensively measure and understand the multifaceted concept of energy humanization. These chosen variables reflect the key dimensions identified in the conceptual framework and are crucial for capturing the specific energy challenges and opportunities in Ghana.

Data and variable description

Data on the variables were sourced from World Development Indicators (WDI), the Energy Commission of Ghana, the International Energy Agency (IEA), and World Governance Indicators. The research utilized yearly data spanning from 1980 to 2021. The variables used in the study and their sources are presented in Table 1 where it captures the variables used in the estimation of energy humanization and its dimensions.

Estimation techniques

We estimate the various dimensions of energy humanization which is basically a composite index for energy security and energy usage. To estimate energy security, a dimension of energy humanization, we employ the different sub-dimensions of energy security where the study adapts the “4-A's” theory of energy security (availability, accessibility, affordability, and acceptability) established by the APERC (2007) since it was able to address contemporary issues in the definition of energy security. The study further modifies the Four A's to include technology application as suggested by Tongsopit et al. [20] and Yao and Chang [21] hence taking into account the crucial 4-A factors of resource availability (AV), technological application (AP), social acceptability (AC), and affordability of energy resources (AF), with sub-indicators in each to assess Ghana's energy security.

In an attempt to estimate total energy usage as a dimension of energy humanization, the study employs total electricity consumption and total petroleum product consumption as a measure of total energy usage. The framework for energy humanization estimation is represented in Fig. 1. To estimate the various sub-dimensions of energy security, several parameters or indicators are assigned to each dimension as shown in Fig. 1. The study further aims at forecasting energy humanization up to 2030 to ascertain if SDG 7 could be achieved. The study initially uses autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) method which combines autoregressive (AR), moving average (MA) and differencing (I) components to forecast the different dimensions of energy humanization and overall energy humanization up to 2030. If the data showed signs of seasonality a more complex seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA) model would have been employed to capture the seasonal patterns in the data.

The study adopted the exponential smoothing techniques since none of the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) lags nor the autocorrelation function (ACF) lags are significant. It is practically impossible to use ARIMA since the data do not have strong autocorrelation patterns. The results of the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) and the autocorrelation function (ACF) are presented in the appendices Figs. 27, 28 and 29. Exponential Smoothing techniques are good forecasting substitutes in such circumstances [[24], [25], [26]]. Simple exponential smoothing, Holt's linear exponential smoothing, and Holt-Winters seasonal exponential smoothing are examples of exponential smoothing techniques that aim to capture time series patterns without primarily depending on autocorrelation. These techniques deal with data that contains patterns, seasonality, and even a small amount of noise.

Exponential smoothing is a technique that involves continuously updating a forecast based on the latest information about estimated data. Essentially, this approach assigns exponentially decreasing weights to older observations, placing more importance on recent data when making forecasts. There are various approaches to exponential smoothing. In this paper, we forecast the energy humanization and its dimensions using three different exponential smoothing methods, which are Simple exponential smoothing, Holt-Winters Additive Seasonal, and Holt-Winters Multiplicative Seasonal exponential smoothing briefly described below. The choice between the different exponential smoothing forecasting methods is based on the model with the smallest Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).

Results

Estimation of energy humanization index

To estimate energy humanization, we employed the conceptual framework shown in Fig. 1. Energy humanization has two main dimensions: energy security and energy usage (energy consumption). The energy security dimension of energy humanization also has four sub-dimensions: energy availability, technology application, energy acceptability, and affordability. Energy usage (energy consumption) can also be divided into two sub-dimensions: net electricity consumption and petroleum and other liquid consumption. Before starting the analysis, we first examine the descriptive statistics of the variables used in the estimation. After the descriptive statistics, this section will estimate the various dimensions of energy security, energy usage, and the energy humanization index.

Descriptive statistics of the variables used in the estimation of energy humanization index

Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics for the variables used. As shown in Table 2, CO2 intensity, CO2 emissions from liquid fuel consumption, combustible renewables and waste, energy-related methane emissions, and share of renewable energy in total energy consumption served as measures of the energy acceptability dimension of energy security (environmental sustainability). The average values of these indicators are 1.005 kg per kg of oil equivalent energy use, 13.398 percent of total fuel combustion, 101.778 percent of total, 49.328 percent of total energy, 36.421 percent of total methane, and 41.36 percent of total energy consumption. An increase in the values of CO2 intensity, CO2 emissions from liquid fuel consumption, and energy-related methane emissions may indicate a decrease in energy acceptability, hence lower energy security and energy humanization, while an increase in combustible renewables and waste and the share of renewable energy in total energy consumption indicates an increase in energy acceptability, hence an improvement in energy security and energy humanization. Discussion of the results

The energy acceptability index of Ghana has experienced a significant decline over time. There are multiple factors that contribute to this decline: a shift from hydroelectric to thermal power generation, resulting in higher emissions and environmental impacts; an increase in automobile usage, leading to higher emissions and environmental degradation; the growth of industries and the proliferation of energy-consuming factories and machinery; and the surge in electronic gadget usage, which has increased overall energy consumption. To improve the energy acceptability index, Ghana should prioritize renewable energy sources, enhance energy efficiency, and reduce dependency on fossil fuels. Additionally, it is crucial to address the environmental impacts of energy consumption in transportation and industry in order to reverse this downward trend. This result contradict the works of Azzuni and Breyer(2020) who argued that energy acceptability is improving in Africa.

Ghana's technology application index reveals a significant decline from 1980 to 2002, followed by stabilization facilitated by government policies. Several key factors contribute to this stabilization, including the implementation of policies such as the National Energy Efficiency and Conservation Policy (NEECP), the establishment of energy efficiency standards, the retrofitting of public buildings, and the promotion of renewable energy sources. To maintain this positive trend, it is crucial to enhance investment in renewable energy, with a specific focus on solar, wind, and other renewable sources. Additionally, it is imperative to reinforce energy efficiency policies, modernize infrastructure to support the adoption of new technologies, and raise public awareness through educational initiatives that emphasize the benefits of energy efficiency and renewable energy. Overall, sustained efforts are necessary to enhance Ghana's technology application index and ensure a sustainable energy future. Confirmed by the of Azzuni and Breyer [11] who argued that energy technology application is improving overtime.

Ghana's energy affordability index has experienced fluctuations due to economic conditions, regulatory changes, and external factors. Since 2000, energy affordability has declined due to reduced subsidies, increased taxes, and rising global oil prices [27]. The years 2013–2014 were particularly challenging due to supply issues. These challenges can be reevaluating subsidy policies and considering targeted subsidies to improve affordability for vulnerable populations. Additionally, implementing measures to mitigate the impact of global oil price fluctuations and addressing electricity supply challenges to reduce reliance on costly backup power are crucial steps. Furthermore, increasing investment in renewable energy to decrease dependence on imported petroleum and lower overall energy costs would be beneficial. These measures have the potential to improve Ghana's energy affordability index and support more stable and equitable energy access.

Ghana has made significant strides in enhancing the energy availability aspect of energy security in recent decades, aligning with broader patterns observed in developing nations. This improvement can be attributed to key factors such as increased electricity installed capacity and diversified energy production through the utilization of natural gas and renewables. Gitelman et al. [28] emphasize that an increase in installed capacity and diversifying production contribute to bolstering system resilience. However, despite these advancements, challenges like financial limitations, fuel supply issues, and occasional power outages (known as “dumsor”) continue to persist[29]. To further enhance energy security, promote greater electricity access, and supporting sustainable development, Ghana's investments in renewable energy and rural electrification initiatives, such as the Rural Electrification Project (REP), play a crucial role (IRENA, 2022).

The relative improvement in Ghana's energy security index from 2010 to 2021 can be attributed to several key factors. The discovery and exploitation of crude oil and natural gas have significantly increased energy availability, reducing dependence on hydropower and enhancing energy security [30,31]. Government policies that promote diversification of electricity generation through the introduction of thermal power and the involvement of the private sector have further increased generation capacity, aligning with the findings of Sovacool [32] regarding the benefits of diversified energy sources. Additionally, the expansion of the grid and rural electrification programs have enhanced access to electricity, supporting the Canton [33] emphasis on infrastructure development for energy security. However, this improvement, primarily driven by energy availability, highlights the need to address other aspects of energy security, such as affordability, acceptability, and technological advancement, in order to achieve a more balanced and sustainable energy security framework [34].

The creation of the energy humanization index in this study, which combines the energy security index and energy usage index, reflects a comprehensive approach to evaluating the impact of energy on human development. The analysis shows that while the energy usage index has consistently increased, the energy security index declined until 2011. However, positive trends have emerged since then, likely due to increased energy availability as a result discovery of oil and gas [31]. This shift emphasizes the significant role of energy availability in enhancing energy security. The study also explores different weighting schemes and finds that varying the emphasis on energy security and usage does not significantly affect the overall trend of the energy humanization index. This demonstrates the robustness of the composite index. The findings suggest that a balanced focus on both energy security and usage is crucial for achieving sustainable energy humanization.

Conclusion and Recommendations

The analysis showed a positive trend in the energy humanization index, specifically in energy usage, which has been steadily increasing. However, the energy security dimension presents a mixed picture. While some aspects, such as energy availability, have consistently improved, others like energy acceptability, affordability, and technology application have declined over time. This decline is concerning because of the importance of these dimensions in a comprehensive energy humanization framework. Without significant interventions, these crucial aspects are expected to worsen, potentially undermining the overall progress in energy humanization. This trend highlights the need for targeted policies and investments to improve energy acceptability, affordability, and technological advancements, in order to ensure a balanced and sustainable energy security landscape. The continuous increase in energy availability, driven by factors such as the discovery of crude oil and natural gas and the diversification of energy sources, provides a solid foundation. However, comprehensive improvements across all dimensions of energy security and energy humanization are necessary for long-term sustainability and resilience.

Energy Commission together with the Ministry of Energy should propose and implement the policy of carbon capture which will then force the independent power producers (IPPs) to adopt this technology in power generation process and also encourage the adoption of electric vehicles once we can guarantee stable electricity and charging points. Since energy acceptability component of energy security is expected to decrease into the future possibly due to CO2 emissions and CO2 intensity largely from the energy sector in-terms of power generation and the transport sector. The Ministry of Finance must be circumspect in imposing more levies on petroleum products and electricity tariff although it is an inelastic good an increase in the price will result in a decrease in affordability, energy security, energy humanization and growth. This must be considered because energy affordability a dimension of energy security is expected to fluctuate much in the future and a decrease in energy affordability will result in energy humanization and growth.

The Energy Commission, Ministry of Energy together Ministry of Finance must commit resources to finance research and adaptation of smart technologies, especially in the transmission and distribution of electricity, energy efficiency technologies, and public education on the need to use standard and energy efficient equipment's and gadgets which will help promote technology application in the energy sector. The Energy Commission, Ministry of Energy, National Petroleum Authority, Public Utility, and Regulatory Commission should deal with energy security and energy usage simultaneously (Energy Humanization) as opposed to treating them separately to promote growth and development.
