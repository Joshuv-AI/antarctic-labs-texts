Advances in Understanding Calcium Sensitization in Gastrointestinal Smooth Muscles: Pathways, Interstitial Cells, and Disease
Authors: Brian A Perrino, Ji Yeon Lee, Sang Don Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContractile activity of gastrointestinal smooth muscles is triggered by increased intracellular Ca2+, which activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) and leads to phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chain (RLC), enabling contraction. Myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP) dephosphorylates RLC for relaxation. The balance between MLCK and MLCP determines the relationship between calcium levels and contractile force. Shifting the balance between MLCK and MLCP in favor of MLCK by inhibiting MLCP allows MLCK to phosphorylate more RLC and generate increased force at any given Ca2+ concentration. Thus, when MLCP is inhibited, the myofilaments are more sensitive to calcium, allowing greater force at any given Ca2+ concentration. Major mechanisms for calcium sensitization in GI smooth muscle include protein kinase C-mediated phosphorylation of protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 14A (CPI-17) and Rho-associated coiledcoil kinase 2 (ROCK2)-mediated phosphorylation of myosin phosphatase targeting subunit 1. Calcium sensitization is crucial for sphincter tone, sustained contraction, peristalsis, energy conservation, and coordinated motility. Smooth muscle cells, interstitial cells of Cajal, and platelet-derived growth factor alpha positive (PDGFRα+) cells form the SIP syncytium, which regulates excitability and calcium sensitization. This review updates our current understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of calcium sensitization with the recent findings from genetically engineered mice with deficiencies in protein kinase C and ROCK2 calcium sensitization pathways, and the involvement of interstitial cells in regulating GI smooth muscle cell excitability. Dysregulated calcium sensitization pathways in motility disorders are summarized, to potentially aid in the development of new pharmacological strategies to prevent and treat GI motility disorders.Keywords: Interstitial cells of Cajal, Knockout mice, Phosphorylation, Signal transduction, Smooth muscle

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal (GI) motility depends on the coordinated contraction and relaxation of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) within the tunica muscularis, a process regulated by complex interactions among SMCs, enteric motor neurons, and interstitial cell populations. Contraction is initiated through excitation–contraction coupling, whereby increases in intracellular Ca2+ activate calmodulin-dependent myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), resulting in phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain (RLC) of myosin and the initiation of cross-bridge cycling.1-3 However, it is now well established that the magnitude and duration of GI smooth muscle contraction cannot be explained solely by changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentration. Instead, mechanisms that regulate the Ca2+ sensitivity of the contractile apparatus are critical for modulating contractile force as necessary for proper motor function.4,5

Calcium sensitization refers to the enhancement of force generation at a given intracellular Ca2+ concentration, primarily through preservation of myosin RLC phosphorylation by inhibition of myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP). MLCP plays a central role in determining the phosphorylation state of myosin RLC, thus the balance between MLCK and MLCP activities is a key determinant of smooth muscle contractility.6,7 Regulatory pathways that inhibit MLCP, including protein kinase C (PKC)-dependent phosphorylation of protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 14A (CPI-17) and RhoA/Rho-associated coiled-coil kinase 2 (ROCK)-mediated phosphorylation of the myosin phosphatase targeting subunit (MYPT1), have emerged as major mechanisms underlying Ca2+ sensitization in GI smooth muscles.8-11 These pathways enable sustained contractile responses, contribute to sphincter tone, and support the generation of coordinated motility patterns.12,13

Although the biochemical mechanisms underlying MLCP regulation have been extensively studied, their physiological roles in intact GI tissues have proven to be more complex than initially appreciated. Emerging evidence suggests that the relative contributions of PKC/CPI-17 and ROCK/MYPT1 pathways differ depending on muscle phenotype, anatomical location, and mode of stimulation. 9,11,14 Studies using pharmacological approaches have traditionally supported a prominent role for ROCK-mediated MYPT1 phosphorylation in Ca2+ sensitization.15,16 However, more recent investigations, including those employing genetically engineered mouse models, indicate that constitutive MLCP inhibition and basal tone may be more strongly associated with MYPT1 phosphorylation at Thr694, while physiological activation of Ca2+ sensitization during neurotransmission may preferentially involve CPI-17 phosphorylation. 14,17 These findings highlight the need to distinguish between mechanisms derived from pharmacological stimulation and those that operate under physiological conditions.

A key factor contributing to this complexity is the organization of GI smooth muscle within the SIP syncytium, a functional network composed of SMCs, interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC), and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α–positive (PDGFRα+) cells.18,19 These cell types are electrically coupled and act in concert to regulate excitability, integrate enteric neural inputs, and coordinate contractile responses. ICC serve as pacemaker cells and intermediaries for excitatory neurotransmission, whereas PDGFRα+ cells mediate inhibitory signaling.18,19 The anatomical and functional relationships within the SIP syncytium restrict neurotransmitter access to specific cellular targets and shape downstream signaling pathways. As a result, the activation of Ca2+ sensitization mechanisms in SMCs depends on the mode of stimulation and the cellular context in which signaling occurs. Recent studies have begun to reveal that physiological activation of GI smooth muscle via enteric neurotransmission may differ from responses elicited by exogenous agonists. Neural stimulation appears to preferentially activate PKC-dependent CPI-17 phosphorylation, whereas bath-applied agonists can engage both PKC- and ROCK-dependent pathways, reflecting broader receptor activation across multiple cell types within the SIP syncytium.14,20 These observations underscore the importance of interpreting experimental findings within the context of physiological signaling and highlight potential limitations of pharmacological approaches in defining the mechanisms of Ca2+ sensitization.

Dysregulation of Ca2+ sensitization pathways has been implicated in a variety of GI motility disorders, including gastroparesis, achalasia, and inflammatory bowel disease.21-24 Alterations in the expression or phosphorylation of CPI-17, MYPT1, and associated signaling proteins have been linked to impaired contractility and abnormal motility patterns, suggesting that targeting components of the Ca2+ sensitization machinery may represent a promising therapeutic strategy.23-25 Despite its central importance, relatively few recent reviews have focused specifically on calcium sensitization in GI smooth muscle, instead focusing on how these incorporate sensitization mechanisms within broader discussions of Ca2+ signaling, electromechanical coupling, and integrated motor control. 26,27 While this reflects the increasingly comprehensive view of GI motility, it also underscores the need for a focused reappraisal of calcium sensitization within this expanded conceptual framework.

In this review, we seek to provide an updated synthesis of the mechanisms regulating Ca2+ sensitization in GI smooth muscle, with particular emphasis on the roles of MLCP regulation, interstitial cells within the SIP syncytium, cytoskeletal dynamics, and insights gained from genetically engineered mouse models. We summarize the current understanding of calcium sensitization mechanisms in Gl smooth muscles and examine their role in the regulation of motility under physiological and pathophysiological conditions. Emphasis is placed on the integration of classical MLCP regulatory pathways with emerging concepts in ion channel function, intercellular signaling, and neuromuscular coordination. We also examine the distinctions between physiological and pharmacological activation of sensitization pathways and discuss how dysregulation of these processes contributes to GI motility disorders. By integrating molecular, cellular, and systems-level perspectives, this review aims to emphasize Ca2+ sensitization as an integral component of the broader regulatory networks governing gastrointestinal motor function.

ROLE OF MYOSIN LIGHT CHAIN KINASE IN SMOOTH MUSCLE CONTRACTION

Normal GI smooth muscle motility patterns are essential for maintaining homeostasis and the adaptive responses to stresses imposed by pathological disorders. A network of signaling pathways centered on the molecular motor myosin as well as membrane proteins associated with calcium handling and cell adhesion regulate myosin phosphorylation for cross-bridge cycling and GI smooth muscle contractile responses.5,28-30 G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling pathways, voltage-dependent Ca2+ influx, or release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, increase the smooth muscle cell cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration to activate the Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent MLCK (Fig. 1).1,3 Activated MLCK phosphorylates the RLC of smooth muscle myosin, allowing myosin to form cross bridges and bind to actin filaments to generate force.2,3,31,32 MLCK is the only kinase that phosphorylates RLC Ser19 (S19) physiologically in different kinds of smooth muscles.1,33,34 MLCK activation is also modulated by phosphorylation that reduces its activity, to fine tune the RLC phosphorylation response.35,36 MLCK is inactivated by the decrease in cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations that dissociates calmodulin from MLCK. RLC S19 is dephosphorylated by MLCP, initiating relaxation by returning myosin to an inhibited state (Fig. 1).6 Thus, the ratio of MLCK/MLCP activities is a major determinant of the extent of RLC phosphorylation and contractile force.4 In contrast to striated muscle, the force and shortening velocities of smooth muscles are regulated functions of cross-bridge phosphorylation.12

Gq12/13 stimulates guanosine 5′-triphosphate (GTP) binding to RhoA, which binds to and activates Rho-associated coiled-coil kinase (ROCK). Ca2+ and DAG also bind to and activate PKC, which phosphorylates CPI-17 to further inhibit MLCP. ROLE OF MYOSIN LIGHT CHAIN PHOSPHATASE IN SMOOTH MUSCLE CONTRACTION

Although both Ca2+ influx and myosin RLC phosphorylation are required for smooth muscle contraction, it is now well established that regulation of MLCP activity, independent of the Ca2+-dependent activation of MLCK, is an important factor in determining the extent of RLC phosphorylation and contractile force.5,13 Shifting the ratio of MLCK/MLCP activities in favor of MLCK activity by inhibiting MLCP allows MLCK to phosphorylate more myosin RLC at any given Ca2+ concentration. This means that the contractile proteins are essentially more sensitive to calcium, leading to increased muscle contraction for a given calcium level. Thus, myofilament calcium sensitization in smooth muscles refers to the phenomenon that greater force is generated when MLCP activity is inhibited at any given concentration of Ca2+ which activates MLCK, or the muscle can generate greater force at lower intracellular calcium concentrations.6,37,38

The MLCP holoenzyme is composed of 3 subunits: the catalytic type 1 phosphatase subunit (PP1cβ; Human gene, PPP1CB; Mouse gene, Ppp1cβ), a regulatory subunit (myosin phosphatase targeting subunit 1 [MYPT1]; Protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 12A; Human gene, PPP1R12A; Mouse gene, Ppp1r12a) and a 20 kDa subunit of unknown function (Fig. 1).39,40 MLCP dephosphorylation of RLC (Myosin-RLC; Human gene MYL9; Mouse gene, Myl9) is involved in various biological processes involving different myosin II isoforms, including smooth muscle contraction, cell division, cell migration, morphogenesis and other developmental processes.7 PP1cβ activity is increased by binding to MYPT1, and inhibited by MYPT1 phosphorylation.41-43 MYPT1 also binds myosin and/or other proteins, thus serving as a scaffolding protein.41,42

Two different signaling pathways primarily inhibit MLCP activity and increase RLC phosphorylation in the absence of additional cytoplasmic Ca2+ (Fig. 1).8,13,44 Phosphorylation of MYPT1 inhibits MLCP activity and contributes to smooth muscle Ca2+ sensitization. 17,41-43 In most smooth muscles, MYPT1 exhibits high constitutive phosphorylation of MYPT1 at Thr694 (T694, mouse sequence numbering; Thr696, T696 in human MYPT1) which inhibits MLCP activity, but is not extensively increased in response to contractile agonists.9-11,15,17,20,45-47 Activation of GPCRs by contractile agonists has clearly been shown to increase MYPT1 phosphorylation at Thr852 (T852, mouse sequence numbering; Thr853, T853 in human MYPT1) by ROCK2, and increase the contractile responses of smooth muscles, suggesting that MYPT1 phosphorylation at T852 inhibits MLCP activity.14,48-52 Ca2+ sensitization in smooth muscles is also mediated by CPI-17 (protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 14A, human gene PPP1R14A; mouse gene Ppp1r14a), which is converted into a potent inhibitor of MLCP by phosphorylation of Thr38 (T38) by PKC.53-58

ROLE OF MYOSIN PHOSPHATASE TARGETING SUBUNIT 1 PHOSPHORYLATION IN SMOOTH MUSCLE CONTRACTION

A large number of studies over the past several decades have been conducted to improve our understanding of the physiological functions of MYPT1 phosphorylation at T694 and T852 in smooth muscles. The constitutive phosphorylation of MYPT1 T694 now appears to be a primary mechanism for regulating force maintenance of smooth muscle, rather than phosphorylation of MYPT1 T852.17,20 Studies using a reconstituted recombinant MLCP enzyme show that MLCP activity is almost completely blocked when MYPT1 is phosphorylated at T694.59,60 The phospho-T694 residue docks directly at the active site of PP1, causing autoinhibition.59,60 The majority of reports demonstrate that T694 phosphorylation is constitutively high in resting smooth muscle tissues and is barely elevated by any contractile agonist stimulus that activates ROCK2, or reduced by any relaxant stimuli.8,14,44,49,61-63 MYPT1 T694 phosphorylation is not reduced by ROCK2 or PKC inhibitors that affect MYPT1 T852 or CPI-17 phosphorylation.14,17,48,49,64,65 Thus, it appears that MYPT1 phosphorylation at T694 is not responsible for ROCK2- or PKC-mediated Ca2+ sensitization of smooth muscle. The kinase(s) involved in the constitutive phosphorylation of MYPT1 T694 are still unidentified. AMPK, ZIPK, ILK or p21-activated kinase can phosphorylate MYPT1 T694 in vitro, but whether they phosphorylate MYPT1 T694 in vivo is still unknown.66-68

The phosphorylation of MYPT1 at T852 has been a major focus of studies investigating the regulation of smooth muscle calcium sensitization because it has been consistently observed that T852 phosphorylation is increased by contractile agonists that increase ROCK2 activity.5,6,14,17,37,38,49,64,69 However, most of the studies investigating the effects of MYPT1 phosphorylation at T852 in smooth muscles in vivo has been determined primarily with pharmacological approaches using ROCK2 inhibitors. These pharmacological perturbations affecting MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation associated with diminution of force responses have formed a fundamental model of signaling mechanisms for MLCP inhibition and force maintenance by MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation in a variety of smooth muscles.8,9,44,51,62 Despite these observations, the experimental evidence about MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation and its functional roles are conflicting.10,59,60,70 Some reports propose that the regulatory mechanism of phosphorylated T852 is via direct inhibition of PP1c activity, while others suggest that T852 phosphorylation triggers dissociation of the MLCP complex from myosin filaments.10,43,70-72 Using chicken gizzard MYPT1 mutants containing T850 with nonphosphorylatable T695A, or T695 with nonphosphorylatable T850A (chicken isoform numbering), Feng et al59 showed that the T850A mutant almost completely inhibited MLCP activity in vitro, suggesting that phospho-T695, not phospho-T850, is the inhibitory site for MLCP activity. Similarly, Khasnis et al60 developed a novel procedure to express MLCP reconstituted in mammalian cells and showed MYPT1 T694 phosphorylation inhibited MLCP activity whereas T852 phosphorylation did not. Khromov et al31 demonstrated in vitro that T852 phosphorylation displayed 30-fold less inhibitory activity towards MLCP than T694; while Murányi et al70 concluded that T852 phosphorylation regulated MLCP activity equivalently to T694.

Investigations of ROCK2-mediated Ca2+ sensitization were significantly advanced by transgenic approaches to rule out off-target effects where pharmacological inhibitors may act non-selectively on other protein kinases or proteins in different signaling pathways that regulate contractile responses. To further investigate the regulation of MLCP activity in vivo, Chen et al17 established 2 mouse lines with Ala substitution of MYPT1 T694 or T852. Isolated ileum and bladder smooth muscles from T852A mice displayed no significant changes in RLC phosphorylation or force responses, but force was still inhibited by a ROCK2 inhibitor. In contrast, smooth muscles containing the T694A mutation showed a significant reduction in force along with reduced RLC phosphorylation. The contractile responses of T694A mutant smooth muscles were also independent of ROCK2 activation. These findings suggest that phosphorylation of MYPT1 T694, but not T852, appears to be the primary mechanism contributing to the constitutive inhibition of MLCP activity, preservation of basal RLC phosphorylation levels, and force maintenance in vivo in smooth muscles.

Evidence supporting a role for MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation in contributing to enhanced RLC phosphorylation in intact tissues depends mostly on results obtained with pharmacological inhibitors of ROCK2. ROCK2 inhibitors clearly reduce MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation, and inhibit GI smooth muscle contractions and reduce basal tone. However, ROCK2 is a multifunctional Ser/Thr protein kinase that phosphorylates several substrates, and other potential physiological substrates and processes affecting smooth muscle contraction have been identified. Thus, pharmacological inhibition of ROCK2 would inhibit the phosphorylation of other proteins by ROCK2 that promote contractile responses. For example, ROCK2 appears to be involved in agonist activation of Ca2+ entry distinct from voltage or store operated channels.73,74 ROCK2 also affects actin dynamics in multiple signaling modules, including in particular the phosphorylation of LIMK, which phosphorylates and inactivates cofilin’s ability to sever F-actin.75,76 The resulting increase in actin polymerization is associated with enhanced force development through membrane adhesions linking the actomyosin motor complex with the extracellular matrix.77 Treating smooth muscles with ROCK2 inhibitors inhibits F-actin polymerization and contraction.78,79 Thus, even selective pharmacological inhibition of ROCK2 is not selective for inhibiting MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation, as the phosphorylation of multiple proteins affecting smooth muscle contraction are also inhibited. Finally, the secreted protein Mfge8 was described as a novel gastric braking protein in 2016 by Khalifeh et al80 MFG-E8 decreases the force of antral contractions and curtails gastric emptying. MFG-E8 binds to α8integrin on gastric SMCs and inhibits ROCK2 phosphorylation of MYPT1 at T852, resulting in RLC dephosphorylation and muscle relaxation. Lack of MFG-E8 results in a 50% increase in the rate of gastric emptying, demonstrating the importance of this pathway in regulating gastric contractions, but is probably due to inhibition of phosphorylation of other proteins by ROCK2 that would otherwise promote contractile responses.

ROLE OF PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 1 REGULATORY SUBUNIT 14A IN SMOOTH MUSCLE CONTRACTION

In GI smooth muscles, inhibition of MLCP in response to pharmacological agonist-induced Ca2+ sensitization, and Ca2+ sensitization induced by membrane depolarization-induced Ca2+ influx may primarily be due to phosphorylation of CPI-17 at T38 by PKC.11,56,81 CPI-17 was first identified in pig aorta smooth muscle homogenates as an endogenous MLCP inhibitor protein that exhibits elevated potency upon phosphorylation at T38 by PKCα and PKCδ.56,63,65,81-85 Unlike MYPT1, CPI-17 expression is highly restricted to SMCs and neurons.86-88 When CPI-17 is phosphorylated, the phospho-T38 selectively docks at the active site of PP1c associated with MYPT1, with a Kd value in the sub nM range, causing a potent inhibition.55 Accumulated evidence shows that CPI-17 regulates MLCP activity in smooth muscles.9 The extent of Ca2+ sensitization is directly correlated with CPI-17 expression in smooth muscle tissues.88,89 CPI-17 expression is also linked to individual smooth muscle contractile characteristics. CPI-17 expression is higher in tonic muscles and lower in phasic muscles, such as urinary bladder and vas deferens.88 CPI-17 expression also positively correlates with the extent of phorbol ester-induced Ca2+ sensitization of force.88 CPI-17 is not expressed in farm chicken smooth muscles, but the levels of RhoA, ROCK2, PKC, and MYPT1 are comparable to those in rabbit and pigeon smooth muscles.89 Stimulation with endothelin-1, phenylephrine, or phorbol ester, induces a minimal agonist-induced Ca2+ sensitization of contraction in chicken arterial smooth muscle, but in rabbit and pigeon arteries that do express CPI-17, these agonists induce a robust contraction.89 The recent development of CPI-17 knockout (KO) and T38A mice has confirmed the importance of MLCP inhibition by CPI-17 phosphorylation at T38 by PKC for the regulation of vascular tone and blood pressure, and that CPI-17 expression is positively correlated with the extent of phorbol ester-induced Ca2+ sensitization of force.58,90 Phorbol ester-induced aorta contraction is completely abolished in CPI-17 KO mice, and severely diminished in T38A KO mice, demonstrating that CPI-17 phosphorylation at T38 mediates PKC-mediated Ca2+ sensitization.58 However, studies using CPI-17 KO mice and T38A mice have not been carried out in GI smooth muscles.

BASAL VS CA2+-STIMULATED PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 1 REGULATORY SUBUNIT 14A PHOSPHORYLATION

In GI smooth muscles, CPI-17 is basally phosphorylated in unstimulated muscles, although the levels vary between sphincter, tonic, or phasic smooth muscles.91 CPI-17 phosphorylation is increased by K+ depolarization or muscarinic receptor stimulation in GI smooth muscles.16,49,92-95 In contrast to MYPT1 phosphorylation at T852, the use of pharmacological inhibitors or activators of PKC has clearly demonstrated the functional role of CPI-17 phosphorylation at T38 in GI smooth muscle Ca2+ sensitization. Although PKC phosphorylates pT38, the roles of the different PKC isozymes involved in T38 phosphorylation is still unclear. PKC is a family of conventional Ca2+-dependent α, β, and γ cPKCs, novel Ca2+-independent δ, ɛ, θ, and η nPKCs, and atypical ξ, and ι/λ aPKCs.96 The cPKCs are activated by Ca2+, diacylglycerol, and phosphatidylserine. 96 The nPKCs do not bind Ca2+, and are activated by diacylglycerol and phosphatidylserine, but not Ca2+.96 The cPKCs α, βI, βII, and γ; and the nPKC δ are all highly expressed in GI smooth muscle tissues, suggesting that CPI-17 can be phosphorylated by Ca2+-dependent and Ca2+-independent PKCs.94,95,97-101 This could explain some of the conflicting reports describing the effects of pharmacological inhibition of PKC on basal CPI-17 phosphorylation and basal tone.102 Efforts aimed at developing specific nPKC inhibitors, along with the specific cPKC inhibitors, may make it possible to determine the contributions and roles of cPKCs and nPKCS to basal and agonist-mediated CPI-17 phosphorylation in different GI smooth muscles with different functions.103 Sphincters are generally characterized by high, sustained tone, in contrast to the stomach and intestines which are characterized by rhythmic, propulsive contractions to provide mixing and motility of material within their lumens. To specifically target agonist-or Ca2+-induced CPI-17 phosphorylation without inhibiting basal CPI-17 phosphorylation could potentially provide novel therapeutic strategies for improving GI motility without adversely affecting GI sphincter function.

CA2+ SENSITIZATION IN GASTROINTESTINAL SPHINCTERS

Several groups have extensively studied calcium sensitization pathways in feline and human lower esophageal sphincter (LES).16,104-108 However, the data regarding the regulation of LES spontaneous tone and agonist-evoked contractions by calcium sensitization mechanisms are conflicting, and the relative contributions of ROCK2 and PKC-mediated pathways are still unclear. A study by Hillemeier et al109 showed that the broad-spectrum cPKC and nPKC inhibitors H-7 and calphostin c reduce spontaneous resting tone in feline LES muscle strips. In addition, U73122, an inhibitor of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate-specific phospholipase C, and D609, an inhibitor of phosphatidylcholine-specific phospholipase C, reduced diacylglycerol formation and LES tone in a concentration-dependent manner. These data suggest that resting LES tone is associated with elevated diacylglycerol levels and PKC activity. However, it should be noted that the effects of Rho kinase inhibitors on the cat LES were not examined. In contrast, a report by Sims et al16 showed that in human LES muscles, the Rho-kinase blockers Y-27632, and HA-1077, caused concentration-dependent reductions of tension in spontaneously contracted LES muscles. These ROCK2 inhibitors also reduced intrinsic nerve-evoked and carbachol (CCh)-evoked contraction. Calphostin C and chelerythrine reduced CCh-evoked contractions to a lesser extent. Neither PKC inhibitor tested caused a statistically significant change in the resting tension of LES smooth muscle. These findings suggest that in human LES muscles, calcium sensitization by ROCK2 is more important for maintaining resting tone and agonist-induced increases in tone than PKC.16 Neither MYPT1 or CPI-17 phosphorylation were investigated in these studies. In murine gastric fundus and proximal colon muscles, but not gastric antrum muscles, we found that the broad-spectrum PKC inhibitor, Gf109203x, reduces CPI-17 phosphorylation below the basal level.49 We subsequently found that either Gf109203x and Go6976, a selective inhibitor of cPKCs, inhibited CCh- or cholinergic nerve-stimulated increase in T38 phosphorylation, but only Gf109203x reduced T38 phosphorylation below basal levels in the murine gastric fundus.14 Similarly, basal CPI-17 phosphorylation and tone in the monkey LES is unaffected by the cPKC inhibitor Go6976, but is reduced by the broad-spectrum PKC inhibitor Gf109273X.110 These findings are consistent with a nPKC contributing to LES tone. These findings suggest that a nPKC maintains basal CPI-17 phosphorylation, and that a cPKC mediates agonist-evoked increases in CPI-17 T38 phosphorylation.

Calcium sensitization pathways in rat and human internal anal sphincter (IAS) have been extensively studied by Rattan and coworkers. 111,112 These studies revealed significantly greater expression levels of RhoA, and ROCK2, and greater T694 phosphorylation levels in the tone-generating IAS than in predominantly phasic rectum smooth muscle. ROCK2 inhibitors abolished MYPT1 T694 phosphorylation and were more potent antagonists of contraction in the IAS than in other phasic muscles. However, the effects of ROCK2 inhibitors on MYPT1 T853 were not examined. Rat and human IAS and rectum CPI-17 levels, and constitutive T38 phosphorylation were also found to be greater in the IAS than in rectum. However, due to the limited ability of PKC inhibitors to reduce IAS tone, the authors concluded that tone in the IAS is primarily due to calcium sensitization via the ROCK2/MYPT1 pathway.112 The ROCK2/MYPT1 calcium sensitization pathway in the monkey IAS and rectum were examined by Keef and co-workers.110 Their results mostly agree with those of Rattan et al110 in that T694 and T852 phosphorylation and ROCK2 expression levels are greater in the IAS than in rectum. However, little difference in the potency of ROCK2 inhibitors between muscles was found. Likewise, little difference between the potency of ROCK2 inhibitors in the phasically active mouse colon versus the tone-generating fundus has been found.49 Together, these data suggest that some degree of constitutive myofilament sensitization is likely to be present in both phasic and tone-generating muscles.102

PHYSIOLOGICAL VS PHARMACOLOGICAL STIMULATION OF CA2+ SENSITIZATION VIA MYOSIN LIGHT CHAIN PHOSPHATASE INHIBITION

Physiological activation, in contrast to pharmacological stimulation, of smooth muscle contraction elicits different phosphorylation responses for MYPT1 and CPI-17. Cholinergic neurostimulation results in increased CPI-17 phosphorylation, but not MYPT1, in gastric fundus or urinary bladder smooth muscles (Fig. 2).14,113 However, pharmacological stimulation of muscarinic receptors with bath applied muscarinic agonists increased MYPT1 and CPI-17 phosphorylation in both types of smooth muscles (Fig. 3).14,113 The agonist-evoked increase in T852 phosphorylation observed in these 2 smooth muscle tissues may be due to pharmacologically intense activation of signaling modules not observed physiologically, due to exposure of the agonist to a larger population of muscarinic receptors. In contrast, the myogenic response in arterial resistance vessels is associated with increased MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation and actin polymerization, but no CPI-17 phosphorylation.114,115 These responses are diminished with ROCK2 inhibition, implicating a ROCK2-dependent contribution to the myogenic response by phosphorylation of MYPT1 T852 and/or cofilin. A subsequent study confirmed the importance of ROCK2 in cofilin phosphorylation and enhanced actin polymerization in the myogenic response.115

Thus, during pharmacologically-mediated contraction, the ratio of MLCK activity to MLCP activity is even higher, and contraction is further augmented because both the PKC- and ROCK-mediated calcium sensitization pathways are activated. Many studies investigating the role of Ca2+ sensitization in response of GI smooth muscles to muscarinic stimulation have not acknowledged the physiological fact that acetylcholine (ACh) released from neurons does not necessarily elicit responses in all post-junctional cells in the SIP syncytium (Fig. 2).18,19 Ca2+ sensitization pathways of GI smooth muscle contraction have typically been studied by immersing muscles in solutions containing contractile agonists, such as CCh (Fig. 3). However, stimulating muscles by bath-applied agonists is not equivalent to neurotransmitter release because different post-junctional receptors are activated in response to these different modes of stimulation (Fig. 3).14,64,113 We previously found that a bath-applied cholinergic agonist activates Ca2+ sensitization mechanisms in murine gastric fundus smooth muscles that are different than those of cholinergic neurotransmission. 14 Electrical field stimulation (EFS) of cholinergic enteric motor neurons only increased CPI-17 phosphorylation, while bath-applied CCh increased both CPI-17 and MYPT1 phosphorylation. We then used the cholinesterase inhibitor neostigmine to strengthen the cholinergic signal during EFS of cholinergic enteric motor neurons. With neostigmine present, both CPI-17 and MYPT1 phosphorylation were increased by EFS. These results support the conclusion that the increase in MYPT1 T852 phosphorylation is the result of a stronger cholinergic signal due to exposure of the agonist to a wider population of muscarinic receptors, that may not typically occur physiologically. Further support for this conclusion is provided from our results using fundus muscles of W/Wv mice, which lack intramuscular ICC (ICC-IM). In fundus muscles of W/Wv mice, EFS alone of cholinergic enteric motor neurons was able to increase both CPI-17 and MYPT1 phosphorylation. These data suggest that ICC-IM and cholinesterase activity restrict ACh accessibility to a select population of muscarinic receptors, possibly only those expressed by ICC-IM. Cholinergic responsiveness was altered by cholinesterase inhibition or by the absence of ICC-IM, by allowing ACh released from nerve terminals to reach muscarinic M3 receptors on SMCs and enable the activation of ROCK2-dependent Ca2+ sensitization pathways and MYPT1 phosphorylation that are not normally activated when cholinesterases are active and ICC are present. Thus, it appears neural stimulation of contraction evokes GI smooth muscle Ca2+ sensitization primarily by increasing CPI-17 phosphorylation. The anatomical arrangement of GI enteric motor neurons, ICC-IM, and SMCs within the SIP syncytium may account for these physiological responses. However, these findings have only been reported for murine gastric fundus muscles.

THE SIP SYNCYTIUM: GENERATION OF CA2+ ACTION POTENTIALS IN SMOOTH MUSCLE CELLS AND CA2+ SENSITIZATION

SMCs provide the forces necessary for GI motility, but interstitial cells, electrically coupled to SMCs, generate the pacemaker activity that underlies major motor patterns, tune SMC excitability, and transduce inputs from enteric motor neurons that relax or contract GI smooth muscles.18 The interstitial cells that regulate SMCs are ICCs and PDGFRα+ cells.19 Together these cells form the SIP syncytium.13,19 ICC and PDGFRα+ cells express signature Ca2+-dependent conductances: ICC express Ca2+-activated Cl- channels, encoded by Ano1, that generate inward current, and PDGFRα+ cells express Ca2+-activated K+ channels, encoded by Kcnn3, that generate outward current.26 ICC and PDGFRα+ cells are intermixed with smooth muscle fibers in networks within the circular muscle layer (and in some regions in the longitudinal muscle layer also). ICC-IM and PDGFRα+ cells are closely associated with the varicosities of enteric motor neurons and express receptors, second-messenger pathways, and ion channels that facilitate the functional responses to enteric motor neurotransmitters.116,117 ICC and PDGFRα+ cells are electrically coupled to SMCs via gap junctions, so responses elicited in the interstitial cells can conduct to SMCs and regulate the excitability of the musculature.

Cholinergic excitatory neurotransmission activates muscarinic M3receptors on ICC-IM via specialized synapses between the enteric nerve varicosities and ICC-IM, activating Gq and phospholipase Cβ and generating inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3).118-120 IP3 releases Ca2+ from intracellular stores into the cytoplasm, causing a transient rise in intracellular Ca2+. These Ca2+ transients couple to activation of ANO1 Cl- channels, generating inward currents that elicit a transient depolarization of the ICC-IM that conducts to SMCs via gap junctions, causing depolarization of the SMCs.18,19,26 Depolarization of SMCs elicits action potentials that occur by activation of L-type voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels.120,121 Ca2+ entry into SMCs through L-type (dihydropyridine-sensitive) Ca2+ channels during action potentials is substantial, as indicated by the rapid upstroke velocities of action potentials (up to approximately 20 V/s).119 The opening of L-type Ca2+ channels causes an immediate and considerable increase in the cytoplasmic concentration of Ca2+ and Ca2+ binding to calmodulin, which then binds to and activates MLCK, and is the major mechanism through which GI SMCs achieve excitation-contraction coupling by enabling myosin RLC phosphorylation and myosin binding to actin myofilaments for cross-bridge cycling.119 Thus, the increase in the cytoplasmic concentration of Ca2+ that occurs by the depolarization of L-type Ca2+ channels appears to be the major mechanism by which GI SMCs achieve excitation-contraction coupling, and appears to be the primary physiological mechanism by which GI SMCs contract. The increase in T852 phosphorylation observed in GI smooth muscle tissues by bath applied CCh, as well as the increase in T852 phosphorylation by EFS in the presence of a cholinesterase inhibitor likely represents pharmacologically-evoked intense activation of Ca2+ sensitization pathways not observed physiologically. The data showing that excitatory motor neuron neurotransmission only increases PKC-dependent CPI-17 phosphorylation supports this mechanism of excitation-contraction coupling in GI smooth muscles and supports the conclusion that neural stimulation of GI smooth muscle contraction evokes Ca2+ sensitization primarily by increasing CPI-17 phosphorylation (Fig. 2).

ACCURATE QUANTITATION OF PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 1 REGULATORY SUBUNIT 12A, PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 1 REGULATORY SUBUNIT 14A, AND MYOSIN REGULATORY LIGHT CHAIN PHOSPHORYLATION

The primary requirement for measuring in vivo levels of protein phosphorylation in cells and tissues is to preserve protein phosphorylation during homogenization of the cells or tissues for analysis. However, a common problem in assessing in vivo protein phosphorylation is the occurrence of nonspecific dephosphorylation of phosphorylated proteins during sample preparation.122,123 Studies of the regulation of MLCP activity in vivo by protein kinases require accurate quantitative measurements of the phosphorylated substrates; in particular MYPT1, CPI-17, as well as the substrate for MLCK and MLCP, myosin RLC. There are important considerations for measuring the phosphorylation of proteins, including tissue fixation methods, the extraction of phosphoproteins, and the methods utilized for measuring the extent of phosphorylation of specific phosphorylated sites.124-126 Important considerations for preparing tissue for analysis include: minimizing dissection trauma or other disturbances; freezing or fixing the tissue rapidly enough to prevent changes from occurring during transfer of the tissue to the freezing or fixing medium, preventing alterations during storage and during manipulation before tissue lysis; and avoiding alteration of phosphoproteins by kinases and phosphatases in the lysis buffer and during tissue lysis.127 Freezing tissue rapidly enough is critical to prevent artificial changes due to the uncontrolled activities of protein kinases and phosphatases. However, smooth muscle tissues are particularly challenging, due to the high expression levels of both MLCK and MLCP. Tissue sample handling and processing, storage, as well as buffer formulations are all significant variables in the measurement of MYPT1, CPI-17, and RLC phosphorylation. Rapidly placing the frozen tissue into ice-cold lysis buffer before it can thaw and allow enzymatic activities to recover is just as important as rapidly freezing the tissues to stop enzymatic activity. In tissues homogenized directly in SDS sample buffer without phosphatase inhibitors and sufficient tissue dilution, RLC can be dephosphorylated, resulting in low baseline tissue RLC phosphorylation values.128,129 Therefore, in addition to containing protease inhibitors, smooth muscle tissues must be homogenized in buffers containing sufficient concentrations of phosphatase inhibitors.122,123 A typical lysis buffer designed to inhibit phosphatase activities contains sodium fluoride, sodium orthovanadate, sodium pyrophosphate, and β-glycerophosphate.122,123 In addition, prior to freezing, the smooth muscle tissue should be submerged into an ice-cold solution of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) dissolved in acetone.67,125,129,130 The acetone permeabilizes the tissue, allowing TCA to rapidly penetrate the tissue. TCA is a strong protein precipitant and denaturant, which rapidly inactivates cellular phosphatases, proteases, and kinases. Utilization of trichloroacetic acid to fix proteins in a phosphorylated state is a powerful tool for accurate quantification of protein phosphorylation. Fixing cells or tissues in 10% TCA in acetone has also been shown to preserve protein phosphorylation for detection by immunofluorescence microscopy.131,132 Recently, RNA later has been shown to preserve proteins and protein phosphorylation for proteomic studies, but smooth muscle tissues have not been tested.133

DISEASE

Alterations to Ca2+ sensitization mechanisms have been reported in certain models of common GI motility disorders. Gastroparesis is characterized by abnormal gastric function resulting in delayed gastric emptying in the absence of mechanical or physical obstruction.134 Gastroparesis is a common complication of diabetes, adversely impacting quality of life with symptoms of abdominal discomfort, nausea, and vomiting.22 The pathogenesis of this disorder is not well understood, but an innate immune dysregulation and injury to the ICC and other components of the enteric nervous system through paracrine and oxidative stress mediators are likely central to the pathogenesis of gastroparesis.135-137 Smooth muscle atrophy, degeneration, and fibrosis have been observed in severe diabetes.136 Investigations have reported alterations to Ca2+ sensitization mechanisms in some animal models of gastroparesis. The ob/ob leptin-deficient mouse model of obesity and type 2 diabetes develops delayed gastric emptying, providing an animal model for investigating how gastric smooth muscle dysfunction may contribute to the pathophysiology of diabetic gastroparesis. 138 Using this model of obesity and diabetes, we found that ROCK2 expression in gastric antrum smooth muscle was decreased compared to age-matched controls.48 Basal and agonist-evoked MYPT1 and RLC phosphorylation, but not CPI-17 phosphorylation, were also reduced compared to age-matched controls.48 These findings suggest that decreased ROCK2 phosphorylation in gastric antrum smooth muscles could potentially contribute to the antral dysmotility of diabetic gastroparesis, but gastric emptying was not measured.

Streptozotocin induced type 1 diabetes leads to gastric motor abnormalities and altered gastric emptying in rats.139 Reports show that streptozotocin induced diabetes results in reduced activities of phospholipase C, PKC, and MLCK activities, and a decrease in the contractility of gastric smooth muscles, due to reduced Ca2+ signaling, although CPI-17 phosphorylation was not investigated. 140,141 Diabetes occurs spontaneously in 90% of partially inbred BB/W diabetic rats.142 This animal model resembles human insulin-dependent diabetes and has been used to investigate the mechanism of diabetic gastroparesis.143-145 Six months after induction of diabetes, CCh-induced responses of gastric smooth muscles were significantly reduced in diabetic rats compared to controls.146 In addition, there was also impaired PKC activation and translocation from the cytosol to the plasma membrane in gastric muscles from diabetic BB/W rats. These observations indicate that functional myogenic impairment occurred in diabetic BB/W rats, resulting from altered intracellular signal transduction involving abnormal IP3 production and PKC translocation, although CPI-17 phosphorylation was not investigated.146

Ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease are chronic inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs). It is well known that IBD is associated with dysmotility of the gastrointestinal tract that can lead to diarrhea, constipation or both.147 In patients with IBD and in experimental colitis animal models, it has been demonstrated that spontaneous contractions and agonist induced contractions of isolated colonic smooth muscles are decreased by functional alternations to the smooth muscle, such as impaired Ca2+ mobilization, enhancement of ATP-sensitive K+ channels, and/or dysfunction of the enteric nervous system.148,149 However, there are also a few reports about the influence of intestinal inflammation on smooth muscle contractile systems. In the mouse model of dextran sodium sulphate (DSS)-induced ulcerative colitis it was found that CCh-induced contractions of colon smooth muscles were reduced, while the expression of muscarinic M3 receptors, RhoA, ROCK1, ROCK2, and MYPT1 in smooth muscles was not changed.24 However, CPI-17 expression was significantly decreased in DSS-treated mouse colon. These results suggest that the suppression of CCh-induced contraction in mice with colitis may be at least partially attributable to the increased activity of myosin phosphatase by the downregulation of CPI-17 expression. In the TNBS-induced colitis model in rats, CCh-induced contractions of colon smooth muscles were reduced, and PKC expression and CPI-17 phosphorylation were reduced.21 These findings suggest that Ca2+-sensitization in rat colon involves a PKC-dependent pathway, and that the reduced CCh-induced contraction in colitis was due to inflammation-induced changes in Ca2+-sensitization involving a defect in the PKC pathway. IL-10 KO mice develop spontaneous and chronic colitis accompanied by immune cell infiltration, submucosal fibrosis, and thickening of the muscularis externa.23 α-smooth muscle actin protein expression in colon smooth muscles did not change, but CPI-17 protein was significantly decreased compared with healthy wild-type controls. Colon smooth muscle contractile force and RLC phosphorylation induced by a muscarinic agonist were reduced in the KO mice. In addition, in colonic smooth muscle tissues from patients with ulcerative colitis, CPI-17 protein expression was decreased compared with healthy control subjects.23 These findings suggest that CPI-17 downregulation may contribute to the decreased motor function in chronic inflammatory bowel diseases. Th1 and Th2 cytokines cause hypocontractility and hypercontractility of inflamed intestinal smooth muscle.150-152 Th1 cytokines downregulate CPI-17 and L-type Ca2+ channels and upregulate regulators of G protein signaling 4, which contributes to the hypocontractility of inflamed intestinal smooth muscle.152

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

This review emphasizes the importance of smooth muscle calcium sensitization to GI motility, and highlights the value of genetically engineered mouse models with targeted deficiencies in the PKC and ROCK2 pathways for more clearly defining the physiological roles of CPI-17 and MYPT1 phosphorylation in regulating MLCP activity and smooth muscle contractile responses, especially in physiological versus pharmacological contexts. The evidence supporting the role of ICCs, as part of the SIP syncytium, in regulating the activation of calcium sensitization pathways within SMCs is discussed. This review summarizes how dysregulation of calcium sensitization pathways may contribute to GI motility disorders. Future studies should expand on these models to dissect the specific roles of these proteins in sphincter function and GI motility, and the impact of their dysregulation in GI motility and sphincter disorders. Translating findings from animal models and cellular studies into clinical research to identify new drug targets and treatment strategies for GI motility disorders could yield potentially beneficial results.
