Adverse Event Profiles of Acetaminophen and Ibuprofen in Infants: A Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System Database Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Yaqing Bao, Hai-Feng Lv, Xing Li, Bi-Hua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aims to analyze adverse events (AEs) associated with the use of acetaminophen and ibuprofen in infants based on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database. The study also seeks to evaluate the safety differences between these 2 drugs to provide scientific evidence for clinical practice.Materials and Methods:This study utilized the FAERS database and employed 3 statistical methods: Proportional Reporting Ratio, Reporting Odds Ratio, and Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network to identify AEs signals associated with acetaminophen and ibuprofen. The positive AEs signals were classified according to the System Organ Class (SOC) to assess the relationship between Preferred Term signals and their corresponding SOCs.Results:The total number of reported AEs associated with acetaminophen and ibuprofen was 2102 and 691, respectively. Further comparison showed that acetaminophen was associated with significantly higher incidences of hepatobiliary disorders (P < .001, OR: 2.61, 95% CI [1.48, 4.59]) and nervous system disorders (P = .009, OR: 3.14, 95% CI [1.27, 7.71]) compared to ibuprofen. However, acetaminophen had a significantly lower risk of “general disorders and administration site conditions” (P < .001, OR: 0.13, 95% CI [0.07, 0.27]) and “renal and urinary disorders” (P < .001, OR: 0.19, 95% CI [0.09, 0.41]) compared to ibuprofen.Conclusion:Through a systematic analysis of the FAERS database, this study highlights significant safety differences between acetaminophen and ibuprofen. The findings offer direct guidance for clinicians in selecting safer antipyretic medications, supporting optimized fever management practices and minimizing drug-related AEs in infants.

Infants’ immature hepatic and renal function increases their vulnerability to adverse drug events. However, current pharmacovigilance studies primarily focus on adult and older pediatric populations, while safety data specifically for infants remain substantially limited.

Introduction

Infant fever is one of the most common symptoms in pediatric practice, often caused by infections, immune responses, or other diseases.1 Acetaminophen and ibuprofen are the first-line medications for treating infant fever due to their efficacy in reducing body temperature and alleviating discomfort.2 However, despite their widespread clinical use, the potential adverse effects and safety concerns of these medications require significant attention.

For instance, in 2013, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a safety alert regarding severe and potentially fatal skin reactions associated with acetaminophen.3 There have been reports of acute kidney injury following the administration of appropriate doses of ibuprofen.4 Infants’ unique physiological characteristics (e.g., immature liver and kidney function, weaker drug metabolism capacity) make their responses to drugs notably different from those of adults, potentially leading to a higher risk of adverse reactions.

Clinical guidelines for ibuprofen use in infants aged 3-6 months remain inconsistent. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against its use in infants under 6 months, while other regions permit it for infants ≥3 months and ≥5 kg.5,6 This discrepancy stems from limited data on nephrotoxicity risks in young infants, as few studies have specifically assessed this population.5,7-10 Current infant antipyretic safety research lacks prospective and systematic studies, while delayed drug labeling updates and insufficient metabolism data contribute to long-term undetected risks.

The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) contains a vast collection of global adverse event (AE) reports.11 These data not only enable researchers to identify potential drug safety concerns but also help explore associated risk factors, thereby providing more precise guidance for clinical practice. Although some studies have investigated the safety of antipyretic use in children, there remains a significant gap in dedicated AE research focusing specifically on infants.

To address this gap, this study aims to analyze the FAERS database to evaluate the characteristics of AEs and potential risk factors associated with antipyretic use in infants. The findings will provide scientific evidence to help clinicians and parents optimize fever management strategies for this vulnerable population.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

This study utilized the FAERS database, a comprehensive repository of drug-related AE reports from global sources, which serves as a critical resource for pharmacovigilance research. This research used publicly available database and complied with applicable regulatory standards and institutional policies, which did not mandate ethics committee approval or written participant consent under the specified legal framework.

Study Design

Data Extraction and Deduplication

Data from the FAERS database spanning January 2014-September 2024 were extracted for analysis. To eliminate duplicate reports, the study adhered to FDA guidelines: for cases with identical CASEID (the unique identifier for FAERS cases), the report with the most recent FDA_DT (the date the FDA received the case) was retained. If both CASEID and FDA_DT were identical, the report with the higher PRIMARYID (the unique identifier for FAERS reports) was selected.

Drug and Population Selection

The study focused on 2 target medications: acetaminophen (paracetamol) and ibuprofen, specifically analyzing their use in infants aged 0-1 year. For acetaminophen, both generic and brand names were considered, including (1) Generic names: APAP, paracetamol, acetaminophen; (2) Brand names: Acephen, Acamol, Anacin-3, Bufferin, Calpol, Calonal, Crocin, Disprol, Dolo 650, Dymadon, Datril, Excedrin, Midol, Panadol, Panado, Pacimol, Tylenol. For ibuprofen, the following were included (1) Generic name: ibuprofen; (2) Brand names: Advil, Brufen, Ibumetin, Ibugesic, Motrin, Merrin, Nurofen, Nuprin, Rufen, Rafen, Salprofen. In the FAERS database, cases were selected where the “patient_age” and “patient_age_unit” fields indicated an age in days ≤ 365 or months (≤ 12) or 1 year.

Adverse Event Classification

This age group was chosen because AEs in this population have not been extensively investigated in prior studies. In this study, AE reports were included if the target drug was identified as the primary suspect by the reporter. The AEs were classified and described using the preferred terms (PTs) and system organ classes (SOCs) from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 27.0). As the data source is publicly available, ethical approval was not required.

Signal Detection Methods

Signal detection in pharmacovigilance commonly employs the Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), and Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN) methods. In this study, these 3 signal detection methods were applied to identify AE signals associated with the target drugs. The PRR compares the proportion of a specific AE reported for the target drug to the proportion of the same AE reported for all other drugs in the database. The ROR estimates the odds of a specific AE being reported with the target drug compared to other drugs. The BCPNN uses Bayesian statistics to compute the Information Component (IC), which measures the strength of the drug-AE association. A signal was classified as a potential AE signal if all 3 methods yielded positive results.12 A positive signal was strictly defined as meeting all of the following criteria: (1) ROR: number of reports ≥ 3, and the lower limit of the 95% CI > 1; (2) PRR: > 2 with χ2 > 4, and number of reports > 3; (3) BCPNN: the lower limit of 95% CI, of IC (IC025) > 0.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed descriptively, with categorical variables expressed as frequencies/percentages. All statistical tests for group differences were performed exclusively on categorical variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests (for expected cell counts <5), with a significance threshold of P < .05 (2-tailed). All analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1 (R Core Team; Vienna, Austria) and Microsoft Office Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation; WA, USA).

Results

Demographic Characteristics of Adverse Events Reports

Between January 1, 2014, and September 30, 2024, a total of 16 637 264 AE reports were identified in the FAERS database. After removing duplicates, 14 357 295 reports remained. Among these, 2868 reports were associated with infants aged 0-1 year. Ultimately, 1370 infants treated with acetaminophen (n = 1019) or ibuprofen (n = 351) were included in the analysis. The total number of reports for the studied drugs was 2102 for acetaminophen and 691 for ibuprofen. A detailed workflow of the data processing is illustrated in Figure 1. The majority of reports for both acetaminophen and ibuprofen originated from the United States, accounting for 72.9% and 51.6% of reports, respectively. Distribution of Preferred Terms in Medication Error

This study conducted a systematic analysis of medication errors associated with the use of acetaminophen and ibuprofen in infants aged 0-1 year, based on data from the FAERS database. A total of 17 types of medication errors were identified and categorized. The results revealed that the most common type of medication error was “incorrect dose administered,” which occurred in 243 cases, accounting for 32.27% of all reported medication errors. This was followed by “product use issue” with 113 cases (15.01%), and “accidental exposure to product” with 86 cases (11.42%). The remaining types of medication errors occurred at lower frequencies. Mining and Analysis of Preferred Term-Level Adverse Event Signals

In this study, AE signals were analyzed using the ROR, PRR, and BCPNN. After excluding signals related to product quality issues, medication errors, “pregnancy, prenatal, and perinatal conditions,” “various congenital, familial, and genetic disorders,” drug indications, and signals clearly unrelated to the drugs, a total of 42 positive AE signals for acetaminophen and 31 positive AE signals for ibuprofen were identified.

Subsequently, all positive signals at the PT level were analyzed, with a focus on the top 20 most frequently detected signals in terms of signal strength (Table 2). The 3 most frequently reported AEs associated with acetaminophen were vomiting (n = 59), acute liver failure (n = 33), and skin discoloration (n = 13). The top 3 AE signals based on ROR, PRR, and IC values were acute liver failure (ROR = 33.33), toxic epidermal necrolysis (ROR = 15.21), and hepatic encephalopathy (ROR = 15.03). For ibuprofen, the 3 most frequently reported AE signals were hypothermia (n = 40), vomiting (n = 18), and diarrhea (n = 15). The top 3 AE signals based on ROR, PRR, and IC values were duodenal ulcer hemorrhage (ROR = 124.64), hypothermia (ROR = 71.87), and throat irritation (ROR = 66.67).

Stratified Analysis of Adverse Event Reports by System Organ Class

The AE signals for acetaminophen and ibuprofen were classified by SOC using MedDRA v27.0. The specific numbers and proportions are detailed in Table 3. Acetaminophen-associated AEs involved 13 SOCs, primarily gastrointestinal disorders (29.80%), hepatobiliary disorders (17.77%), and liver-related investigations (8.02%), including serious conditions like acute hepatic failure and fulminant hepatitis. Ibuprofen-related AEs spanned 11 SOCs, with higher rates of gastrointestinal disorders (23.87%), “general disorders and administration site conditions” (18.02%), and renal/urinary disorders (12.16%), such as tubulointerstitial nephritis and acute kidney injury.

Comparative analysis revealed distinct safety profiles. While gastrointestinal, skin, and immune-related AEs were similar between drugs (P > .05), acetaminophen showed significantly higher incidence of hepatobiliary disorders (17.77% vs. 7.66%; P < .001) and nervous system disorder rates (8.02% vs. 2.70%; P = .015). Conversely, ibuprofen had more “general disorders and administration site conditions” (18.02% vs. 2.87%; P < .001) and renal complications (12.16% vs. 2.58%; P < .001). Risk assessment further highlighted these differences: acetaminophen exhibited stronger liver-related signals (e.g., acute hepatic failure, ROR = 33.33), while ibuprofen showed higher renal risks (e.g., tubulointerstitial nephritis, ROR = 56.79).

Notably, acetaminophen was uniquely associated with “several blood and lymphatic system disorders” (e.g., coagulopathy) and “metabolism and nutrition disorders” (e.g., metabolic acidosis), whereas ibuprofen had exclusive links to respiratory disorders and procedural complications. These findings underscore acetaminophen’s greater hepatotoxicity risks vs. ibuprofen’s propensity for renal and systemic adverse effects, guiding clinical risk-benefit evaluations.

Discussion

Study on the safety differences between acetaminophen and ibuprofen in infants based on systematic pharmacovigilance data is still lacking, although they are commonly used as antipyretic drugs in pediatrics.13 Leveraging the FAERS database, this study presents the first detailed analysis of AEs in infants (0-1 year). Key findings include (1) Medication errors: prevalent antipyretic-related errors with dosage were identified, highlighting the need for improved dosing accuracy and administration protocols. (2) AE signals: disproportionality analysis revealed distinct safety profiles—acetaminophen was associated with hepatobiliary and nervous system disorders, whereas ibuprofen correlated with renal/urinary and general disorders. These findings address critical knowledge gaps and emphasize the importance of vigilant prescribing practices. By delineating organ-specific risks, the study provides actionable insights to enhance infant medication safety.

This study identified 756 medication error cases (27.07% of total AEs), with incorrect dosing being most prevalent. Common pediatric errors included double dosing, improper intervals, and measurement confusion, while multi-product acetaminophen use accounted for <1% of cases in children under 6.14,15 Liquid formulations proved optimal for precise weight-based dosing in infants,16 with strawberry-flavored ibuprofen syringes showing better acceptability than orange-flavored acetaminophen dosing cups.17 Given infants’ small dose requirements, oral syringes are strongly recommended over error-prone cups to improve administration accuracy and safety in this vulnerable population.

Ibuprofen’s dual COX-1/COX-2 inhibition contrasts with acetaminophen’s selective COX-2 action.18 Acetaminophen has a relatively narrow therapeutic index, meaning that overdosing can lead to severe hepatocyte damage.16 As shown in Table 3, infants exhibit higher rates of hepatobiliary AEs with acetaminophen than with ibuprofen—a finding consistent with trends reported by Wang et al19 in populations under 18 years. While generally safe at therapeutic doses, acetaminophen overdose is well-documented to induce acute liver injury.20,21 The primary mechanism involves formation of the toxic metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine and subsequent glutathione depletion. Emerging evidence suggests additional roles for ZBTB22 expression dysregulation and Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathway accumulation.22,23 Notably, even within recommended dosages, prolonged exposure may exacerbate subclinical liver damage, occasionally progressing to hepatic failure.24-26 Consistent with the literature, hepatobiliary side effects are more frequently reported with acetaminophen than with ibuprofen (5.67% vs. 1.36%, P < .001).27 In this study, infants receiving acetaminophen showed significantly higher hepatobiliary disorder incidence vs. the ibuprofen group (P < .001, OR: 2.61, 95%CI [1.48, 4.59]), including more frequent liver function abnormalities. However, incomplete dosing data in FAERS reports precluded detailed dose-dependent hepatotoxicity analysis.

Compared to acetaminophen, ibuprofen poses a greater risk of acute kidney injury, particularly in dehydrated patients. This is because ibuprofen reduces the protective effects of prostaglandins on renal perfusion and filtration processes.28 Valentina Pelliccia et al27 reported that ibuprofen was associated with 0.8% more renal AEs than acetaminophen (2.25% vs. 1.38%, P < .001). However, a systematic analysis including 2 randomized studies and 27 374 participants, some of whom were dehydrated children, found no evidence of a higher likelihood of renal impairment in children using ibuprofen compared to acetaminophen.13 In this study, ibuprofen was associated with a significantly higher number of positive AE signals related to renal and urinary disorders compared to acetaminophen (P < .001, OR: 0.19, 95% CI [0.09, 0.41]). These findings highlight the need for careful consideration of renal risks when prescribing ibuprofen, especially in vulnerable infant populations.

Currently, there is limited research comparing the AEs related to nervous system disorders between acetaminophen and ibuprofen in infants. However, a study based on the Italian spontaneous reporting database analyzed adverse reactions to acetaminophen and ibuprofen in pediatric populations. The results showed significant differences in the incidence of psychiatric symptoms (15.7% vs. 6.8%) and neurological symptoms (12.4% vs. 6.4%) between acetaminophen and ibuprofen users (P < .001).27 Consistent with these findings, this study demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of nervous system disorders in infants treated with acetaminophen compared to those treated with ibuprofen (P = .009, OR: 3.14, 95% CI [1.27, 7.71]). These results align with previous studies in pediatric populations, suggesting that acetaminophen may pose a greater risk of nervous system-related AEs in infants. There are also studies indicating that exposure of fetuses to acetaminophen in utero can lead to neurological related diseases in children, but the results are not yet clear.29,30

This study identified a noteworthy incidence of acetaminophen-associated mortality (46 cases, 4.5%), with 39 cases occurring during the neonatal period. This finding suggests that the unique physiological status of neonates may significantly influence drug safety. However, establishing a causal relationship between mortality and medication presents substantial challenges:31,32 causal pathway analysis confirmed only 8 cases were directly attributable to overdose, while for most cases, it remained difficult to distinguish whether death resulted from drug toxicity or underlying comorbidities. This attribution difficulty constitutes a core limitation of the study and may be further compounded by indication bias, potentially overestimating the actual drug risk and confounding the true association between medication and adverse outcomes.

Data incompleteness represents another critical limitation. Due to the retrospective study design, only 15.90% of paracetamol cases and 19.94% of ibuprofen cases contained complete dosing regimens and weight information, precluding systematic evaluation of the associations between dosage, diagnosis, administration frequency, and AE risks. Notably, missing data may introduce selection bias, particularly when documentation gaps correlate with specific clinical characteristics (e.g., disease severity). Furthermore, inadequate adjustment for potential confounders (e.g., underlying illness severity, concomitant medications, indication for use) could influence the results. Of particular concern is that clinicians may select medications based on infants’ baseline health status (e.g., hepatic/renal function)—this indication bias cannot be fully corrected through conventional statistical methods.

This FAERS-based study has important limitations inherent to spontaneous reporting systems, including reporting incompleteness and uncertain drug-AE causality. Potential misclassifications in infant case identification may include over-inclusion, where reports state “age 1 year” for children actually older than 12 months, and under-inclusion, due to records listing only gestational age, unit errors, or missing age data. Detected signals represent statistical associations rather than proven causal relationships. Importantly, FAERS data cannot determine population-level AE incidence due to underreporting. Future research should validate these signals through rigorous observational studies or clinical trials to establish real-world epidemiological characteristics, while mechanistic studies should explore potential causal pathways. This approach will ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the AEs and their relationships with the medications in question.

This study utilizing the FAERS database characterizes distinct AE profiles for acetaminophen and ibuprofen in infants. Medication errors, particularly dosing inaccuracies, constituted a significant AE subset, emphasizing the need for standardized drug administration education. The AEs spanned multiple organ systems, with distinct patterns observed between the 2 drugs: Acetaminophen demonstrated a stronger association with severe hepatotoxicity and neurological complications in infants, whereas ibuprofen was more frequently linked to acute kidney injury. These findings highlight the need for careful risk stratification when selecting antipyretic therapies for pediatric patients.

Take-Home Messages

1. Risk is drug-specific: The choice between acetaminophen and ibuprofen for infant fever management should not be arbitrary. Clinicians must be aware of distinct AE profiles, with acetaminophen carrying a higher risk of liver and neurological events, and ibuprofen posing a greater risk for kidney injury.
