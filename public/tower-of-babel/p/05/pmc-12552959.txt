Hospital wastewater microbiome & resistome: An overlooked one health crisis driving superbugs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)
Authors: Hemayet Hossain, Shihab Ahmed
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The global health community is standing at the precipice of a silent crisis antimicrobial resistance (AMR) that is evolving faster than our countermeasures. While extensive discussions have been directed toward antimicrobial misuse in humans and livestock, a largely neglected but formidable contributor to the AMR epidemic is wastewater, particularly hospital wastewater. This overlooked reservoir is now emerging as one of the most potent sources of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) bacteria, along with their resistance and virulence genes, thereby fueling a slow but inevitable pandemic.

Hospital wastewater is often discharged untreated or inadequately treated into rivers, canals, drainage systems, and municipal sewage in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Unlike domestic wastewater, hospital effluents are enriched with high loads of antibiotics, disinfectants, resistant pathogens, and heavy metals that exert selective pressure [1]. This unique ecosystem promotes the proliferation and persistence of superbugs while accelerating horizontal gene transfer (HGT). Mobile genetic elements (Plasmids, Transposons, Integrons, Bacteriophages (phages), Insertion sequences) in hospital wastewater harbor resistance and virulence genes, accelerating HGT (Fig. 1) [1,2]. Resistance determinants such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs), carbapenemases, and colistin resistance (mcr) genes are frequently mobilized, transforming commensal or non-virulent microbes into highly pathogenic, drug-resistant strains (Fig. 1) [3,4].

Drivers and transmission pathways of superbugs (MDR, XDR, PDR) from hospital wastewater in LMICs through resistome and horizontal gene transfer within the One Health triad.

Hospital wastewater harbors a complex microbiome enriched with clinically significant pathogens (Fig. 1), many of which fall under the WHO's global priority pathogen list (PPL). These include Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterococcus faecium, and Staphylococcus aureus [1,4]. Such bacteria persist and thrive under selective pressure from residual antibiotics, disinfectants, and heavy metals. This environment fosters both the survival and horizontal transmission of resistance and virulence genes, making hospital wastewater a dangerous reservoir and dissemination hub for superbugs.

The implications of this environmental dissemination are profound. Once released, resistant bacteria and genes spread rapidly across interconnected ecosystems humans, animals, agriculture, and natural environments creating an inseparable One Health crisis (Fig. 1). Farm animals consuming contaminated water, aquatic organisms in polluted rivers, and communities dependent on these water sources all become part of the resistome network. This ecological transmission is particularly concerning in LMICs where poor sanitation, limited surveillance, inadequate wastewater treatment, and high antibiotic consumption converge [1,4,5]. The result is an increased likelihood of resistant infections, therapeutic failures, and elevated mortality rates, especially in vulnerable populations.

A recent surge of studies has documented alarming evidence: rivers downstream of hospital discharges carry a diverse pool of resistance genes; untreated municipal effluents contain carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae; and plasmids harboring virulence and resistance traits circulate between environmental and clinical isolates. These findings underscore that wastewater is not merely a waste by-product but a dynamic incubator and dissemination hub for AMR.

If this crisis remains unchecked, LMICs are at risk of facing devastating public health and economic consequences. The threat extends beyond national boundaries, as resistant genes and pathogens do not respect borders. Given the globalized nature of trade, food supply chains, and travel, wastewater-associated superbugs represent a transboundary hazard that could trigger the next slow pandemic. To mitigate this emerging crisis, we propose the following strategic actions under a One Health framework: (A) Mandatory Treatment of Hospital Wastewater: Establish regulatory frameworks requiring hospitals to install effective wastewater treatment systems (e.g., advanced oxidation, membrane bioreactors, UV disinfection) before discharge. (B) AMR Surveillance in Wastewater: Integrate wastewater monitoring into national AMR surveillance programs to detect emerging resistance patterns early. (C) Antimicrobial Stewardship: Reduce antibiotic discharge at the source by implementing strict stewardship policies within healthcare facilities. (D) One Health Collaboration: Develop intersectoral task forces linking public health, veterinary, agricultural, and environmental authorities for coordinated AMR control. (E) Capacity Building in LMICs: Strengthen infrastructure, laboratory capacity, and policies for wastewater management and AMR detection, supported by international collaborations and funding mechanisms. (F) Public Awareness and Risk Communication: Engage communities about the risks of using untreated water and encourage safe water practices.

In conclusion, hospital wastewater represents a critical but under-addressed frontier in the global fight against AMR. Without urgent intervention, LMICs may witness a catastrophic amplification of resistant pathogens, jeopardizing decades of medical progress. Addressing wastewater resistomes through a holistic One Health approach is no longer optional it is imperative for safeguarding global health security.
