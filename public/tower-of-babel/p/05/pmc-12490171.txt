StOSD1/UVI4 is crucial for accurate mitotic progression and the maintenance of leaf morphology in potato
Authors: Meiping Sun, Jiabao Lu, Lei Wu, Bo Wang, Jun Han, Yang Li, Hao Yang, Qianghua Xie, Zitong Wang, Lu Yang, Lingkai Zhang, Getu Chao, Hong Zhang, Yaqingqing Niu, Zhiyong Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate cell cycle progression is essential for plant growth and development. UVI4(UV-B-insensitive 4) and its homologue OSD1(Omission of the Second Division) function as negative regulators of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) and are critical for cell cycle regulation in plants. In potato, UVI4 and OSD1 have a single homologue, StOSD1/UVI4, and the role of one copy of this gene in cell cycle regulation is largely unknown.ResultsWe showed that mutation of StOSD1/UVI4 in potato resulted in a shorter plant height and a variegated leaf phenotype characterized by mosaic areas with dark green and light green colours. The mutant plants presented altered chloroplast distribution and decreased fluorescence signal in palisade cells within the light-green sectors, accompanied by a significant reduction in total chlorophyll content. Transcriptome analysis revealed the downregulation of genes associated with chlorophyll biosynthesis in the mutant. Paraffin sectioning revealed increased cell volume in the light green regions. Ploidy analysis via 5S rDNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrated that the StOSD1/UVI4 mutation induced endomitosis in the palisade cells in the light green sectors. Further ploidy determination in leaf cells via 26S rDNA and 5S rDNA FISH revealed that the StOSD1/UVI4 mutation led to a replication pattern combining incomplete replication with endomitosis. These findings indicate that the StOSD1/UVI4 mutation impairs chloroplast distribution and function in potato and triggers endomitosis. Additionally, the number of nonglandular trichomes on the leaf epidermis decreased, and the second division at meiosis was skipped in the StOSD1/UVI4 mutant.ConclusionOur findings establish a novel link between endomitosis and chlorophyll accumulation in plants. Additionally, we reveal roles for StOSD1/UVI4 in plant morphology (influencing trichome density) and meiosis (ensuring the second division). Collectively, these findings underscore a new role for StOSD1/UVI4 in regulating mitosis, plant morphology, and leaf development in potato.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07318-8.Keywords: StOSD1/UVI4, Endomitosis, Leaf variegation, Palisade tissue cells, Potato

Background

The precise coordination of cell proliferation and expansion is fundamental to the growth of multicellular organisms [1]. The former phenomenon is associated with the ongoing progression of the mitotic cell cycle, whereas the latter is partly achieved through a variation of the cell cycle that results in elevated cellular ploidy, leading to endopolyploidy [2, 3]. Endopolyploid cells are generated via endoreplication, which involves two strategies: the endocycle and endomitosis [4]. The endocycle occurs without completion of mitosis, resulting in polytene chromosomes and increased DNA ploidy. In contrast, endomitosis involves entry into but not completion of mitosis, leading to a single polyploid nucleus [5]. Plant cell cycle transitions are regulated by CDK-cyclin complexes: CDKA;1/CYCD governs G1/S progression, whereas CDKB/CYCB-CYCA2 assemblies gatekeep the G2/M transition [6–8]. The ubiquitin‒proteasome system (UPS) degrades key cell cycle regulators, with SCF (Skp1, Cullin, F-box protein) and APC/C (anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome) E3 ligases playing crucial roles [9–11]. APC/C, the largest E3 ligase, is particularly important in cell division [12, 13].

In Arabidopsis thaliana, OSD1 (Omission of the Second Division) and UVI4 (UV-B-insensitive 4) are negative regulators of APC/C. Genetic studies have revealed that osd1 mutants present significantly enlarged cotyledon epidermal cells, accompanied by elevated ploidy levels [2]. Notably, OSD1 plays a dual regulatory role in meiotic progression: its absence not only eliminates the second meiotic division but also, when combined with CYCA1;2/TAM (TARDY ASYNCHRONOUS MEIOSIS), disrupts the transition from prophase to meiosis I during male gametogenesis [14, 15]. Parallel studies on UVI4 have demonstrated its critical function in maintaining mitotic-endocycle balance, with the uvi4 mutant displaying hyperploidization in leaves/trichomes and root meristem contraction [16, 17]. Intriguingly, this mutant also exhibited increased UV-B stress tolerance, suggesting potential regulatory interplay between genes involved in cell cycle regulation and environmental adaptation. Both proteins physically interact with APC/C components to modulate cell cycle transitions [2, 18]. Notably, transgenic overexpression of either OSD1 or UVI4 results in increased pathogen resistance [19], highlighting the broader roles of these genes in plant stress responses beyond canonical cell cycle regulation.

While the functional architecture of OSD1 and UVI4 has been mapped in Arabidopsis, its operational logic in polyploid crop species remains unknown. In potato, AtUVI4 and AtOSD1 have a single homologue [20], StOSD1/UVI4, and the role of one copy of this gene in cell cycle regulation is largely unknown. To systematically investigate its pleiotropic functions, we generated CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout mutants in tetraploid potato. Our mutants presented disrupted palisade cell morphogenesis with variegated leaves, endomitosis-driven ploidy escalation accompanied by nuclear hypertrophy, chloroplast distribution anomalies linked to chlorophyll biosynthesis downregulation, and trichome reduction and meiotic aberrations. These findings establish StOSD1/UVI4 as a multifunctional coordinator of cell cycle progression, ploidy homeostasis, and organ development in potato. These tissue-specific phenotypes reveal novel interplay between endomitotic regulation and the photosynthetic machinery, providing critical insights for engineering polyploidy-associated traits in crops.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The potato (Solanum tuberosum L. cv. Atlantic) was used for genetic transformation to develop transgenic lines, which also served as a wild-type (WT) control. Atlantic was gifted by the Inner Mongolia Potato Virus-free Seed Potato Breeding Center, China. The plants were cultured in vitro in Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with either 3% sucrose for seedling development or 8% sucrose for tuber production. The pH level was adjusted to a range of 5.7–5.9. The plants were cultivated in an artificial climate chamber under a photoperiod of 16 h at 23 °C during the day and 8 h at 21 °C at night, with a light intensity of 2800 lx [21]. The propagated seedlings were subsequently transplanted in a greenhouse and grown under natural lighting conditions at Inner Mongolia University.

Phylogenetic analysis

The protein sequences of AtOSD1 (AT3G57860) and AtUVI4 (AT2G42260) were retrieved from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR), followed by BLAST homology searches across four genomic database: the Solanaceae Genomics Resource database (https://spuddb.uga.edu/), the Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/), the Solanaceae Genomics Network database (https://solgenomics.net/), and the NCBI database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Protein alignments were performed using Clustal X2, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA11 [20, 22]. Phylogenetic tree images were generated using the Interactive Tree Of Life (ITOL) tool (https://itol.embl.de/). Homologous sequence alignment was performed using genedoc and MEGA11 software [20, 22].

Plasmid construction

The coding sequence was analysed to identify potential CRISPR targets using CCTop (http://cctop.cos.uni-heidelberg.de). Two 20-bp target regions (Target 1: 5′-ACATCTACAGTCGTCGGCGG-3′; Target 2: 5′-GCTTGGTATCCAAGAACTCC-3′), each positioned immediately upstream of a 5′-NGG-3′ protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) in exon 1, were selected. These guide sequences were synthesised and, in conjunction with a Cestrum yellow leaf curling virus (CmYLCV) promoter and a gRNA scaffold defined by BbsI sites, were cloned and inserted into the standard vector pUC57 from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Construct validity was confirmed through BbsI restriction digest analysis and bidirectional Sanger sequencing. The verified cassette was subsequently subcloned and inserted into the BsaI sites of the plant-optimised CRISPR/Cas9 vector pYLCRISPR/Cas9P35S-N (Addgene plasmid #66191), which contains (i) a cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter-driven Cas9 expression cassette and (ii) a kanamycin resistance marker for prokaryotic and eukaryotic selection.

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation

The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, carrying the binary vector pYLCRISPR/Cas9P35S-N-StOSD1/UVI4, was used to transform potato explants via the method described by Bhaskar, with minor modifications [23]. In brief, internodes measuring 1 cm in length were excised from four-week-old in vitro-generated plants and immediately transferred to Petri dishes containing 35 ml of MS liquid medium. The excised explants were initially cultured in the dark on zig-type medium for two days. Subsequently, 35 ml of Agrobacterium culture (OD 600 = 0.5) was centrifuged at 6500 rpm for 5 min and then combined with 35 ml of MS medium to infect the explants for 15 min. Next, the explants were cocultured on zig-type medium on sterilized filter paper for a span of 4–5 days. After being rinsed with sterilized water, the explants were dried and subsequently placed on zig-type medium supplemented with 40 mg/L cefotaxime, 28.48 mg/L Timentin or 50 mg/L kanamycin for screening and further culture.

Molecular analysis

Transgenic potato lines derived from the CRISPR/Cas9 construct were initially screened for the kanamycin resistance marker. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted on genomic DNA extracted from these transgenic lines. The primers used for positive identification were as follows: forward primer, 5ʹ-CTCGAGAGGCGCGCCAATGAT-3ʹ; reverse primer, 5ʹ-GGCGCGCCGTAGTGCTCG-3ʹ. The presence of a specific 1132 bp PCR product was confirmed in the transgenic plants. The PCR protocol adhered to the manufacturer’s guidelines, encompassing an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of thermal denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The transgenic lines were further evaluated for gene editing using Sanger sequencing. The primers used for sequencing were as follows: forward primer, 5ʹ- ATGGCCGAAGGAAGAGACAGATT-3ʹ; reverse primer, 5ʹ- GCGCATTGACATTAAAGTTTTAA-3ʹ.

Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM)

For confocal microscopy, young leaves were mounted on slides in water and examined under a confocal microscope [24]. Confocal fluorescence images were obtained via a Nikon AX upright Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) system, using a 499–551 nm laser line to visualize the green signal, a 571–625 nm laser line to visualize the red signal and a 663–738 nm laser line to visualize the far-red signal.

Paraffin sectioning

Rectangular leaf tissue samples measuring 5 cm by 5 cm were excised from leaves with pathological symptoms via a sterile razor blade and subsequently placed into a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube containing 1.5 ml of FAA (Formalin-Aceto-Alcohol). These samples were then shipped to MISP Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for preparation processes, which included paraffin embedding, sectioning, mounting on positively charged microscope slides, deparaffinization, and Safranin O/Fast green staining.

Leaf chlorophyll content measurement

Third leaves from 4-week-old plants were excised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at −80℃, and utilised (0.1 g fresh weight) for total chlorophyll extraction. The leaf pieces were then ground to a fine powder by using a mortar and pestle, and the chlorophyll was extracted with 1 ml of 80% acetone [25]. All of the extracts were subsequently diluted 10-fold (200 µl each), transferred to a microplate, and analysed by using a SpectraMax i3 microplate reader [26]. Nucleus Preparation and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Leaves measuring 3 to 4 cm in length and roots measuring 1 cm were collected and then immersed in a mixture of glacial acetic acid and water (3:1, v/v) for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were stored at −20 °C. Slides were subsequently prepared via the enzyme maceration method described by Kato et al. [28]. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was conducted in accordance with the protocol outlined by Kato et al. [28], with minor modifications, as suggested by Xiong et al. [29]. After hybridization and washing, a drop of Vectashield mounting medium with DAPI (H-1200; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) was applied, and the cells were sealed with a 24–350 mm cover glass. FISH images were captured using a Zeiss Imager M2 microscope equipped with an Axiocam 506 mono camera (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). These samples were processed using Zeiss’s proprietary software (ZEN software). Images were edited using Adobe Photoshop CS, in which cropping, resizing, and contrast adjustments were applied uniformly to the entire image.

FISH of paraffin sections

A subset of the samples that were not dewaxed were returned for further experimentation. The slides were immersed in xylene twice for 5 min each time. The samples were rehydrated by immersion in various concentrations of ethanol. A 5-minute immersion in 100% ethanol was first performed, followed by 10-minute immersions in 95% ethanol, 85% ethanol, 75% ethanol, and distilled water to wash the xylene [30, 31]. The samples were then treated with 30% sodium bisulfite at 43 °C for 15 to 30 min, followed by digestion with 0.25 mg/ml protease K at 37 °C for 1 h. They were subsequently washed once with 1x PBS and once with DEPC-treated water before being dried and prepared for hybridization. The slides were placed in an aluminium box with 2x SSC-soaked filter paper, denatured in a 100 °C water bath for 3 min, and then precooled on ice for 2 min. FISH was conducted following the protocol outlined by Kato et al. [28].

Trichome morphology and density

For each species under study, three leaves from the apical region of compound leaves situated at the second or third node were harvested from 60-day-old plants cultivated in the greenhouse. The trichome morphology and density were assessed via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Tabletop Microscope S2300701, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a Bruker energy spectrometer (XFlash6100). A Quorum cryogenic transfer system (PP3010T), Quorum point dryer (K850) and IXRF ion sputterer (MSP-2 S) were used for tissue desiccation and fixation. Images were captured at 13 kV to reduce surface charging of the trichomes [32].

RNA sequencing

Plants exhibiting robust growth under greenhouse conditions were selected for RNA sequencing. Following total RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation and sequencing procedures were performed in collaboration with Shanghai Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), where library analysis was subsequently conducted using the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing platform.

Identification and annotation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

For differential gene expression analysis and functional annotation, the potato reference genome and annotation files were downloaded from Ensembl Plants. Trimmomatic was used to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads, and the remaining reads were selected as clean reads. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and raw read counts per gene were quantified using featureCounts [33]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened according to the criteria of a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2Fold change| >1 using the DESeq R package v1.20.0 (Robinson et al., 2010). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed via the clusterProfiler R package [34–36] to elucidate the biological pathways of interest.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) validation

Total RNA was extracted from the plant samples using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed with a reverse transcription system (PrimeScript™ II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit; Takara Beijing, China), with Tubulin used as an endogenous reference control. qRT‒PCR amplification was carried out on a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System with SYBR Green chemistry (qPCR Master Mix; Takara Beijing, China). All primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Relative quantification of target gene expression was performed via the comparative 2^(-ΔΔCt) method [36].

Results

Identification and characterization of the StOSD1/UVI4 gene in potato

We identified StOSD1/UVI4 homologues in Solanaceae species through an online BLAST search (www.solgenomics.net) using the amino acid sequences encoded by the Arabidopsis OSD1 (AT3G57860) and UVI4 (AT2G42260) genes. Unlike other plant species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana, Brassica rapa, Oryza sativa and Zea mays, which have two UVI4 homologue genes, potato and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) have only one UVI4 homologue gene. However, tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) and petunia (Petunia hybrida) had four and three homologues, respectively, in the draft genome (Fig. 1A). The potato homologue SoltuDM.10G027010.1 was named StOSD1/UVI4. An earlier study reported that both AtUVI4 and AtOSD1 have three conserved motifs, GxENbox, D-box and MR-tail, which are also present in the potato homologue StOSD1/UVI4 (Fig. 1B).

The StOSD1/UVI4 mutant exhibited leaf variegation in potato

Two target sequences were designed for genome editing within the first exon of the StOSD1/UVI4 gene in potato (2n = 4x = 48) (Fig. 2A). Sixty-five transgenic lines containing CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids were generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Sequencing analysis revealed two lines (D64 and Z3) carrying biallelic mutations in all four alleles of StOSD1/UVI4 (Fig. 2A). Notably, the D64 and Z3 mutants presented a leaf mosaic phenotype with irregular dark green (DG) and light green (LG) sectors (Fig. 2B), a feature absent in WT plants. The Z3 mutants exhibited a significant reduction in plant height (43.8 ± 5.6 cm) compared with wild-type plants (WT: 67.0 ± 4.7 cm), while D64 plants (60.6 ± 4.7 cm) showed no statistically significant difference from WT (Fig. 2C and Table S2). Pigment analysis revealed that total chlorophyll levels were reduced by 10.83% (2.265 mg g⁻¹ FW) in D64 and 23.78% (1.936 mg g⁻¹ FW) in Z3 compared with those in WT (2.540 mg g⁻¹ FW). The chlorophyll a content showed more drastic decreases of 13.17% (1.351 mg g⁻¹ FW) and 29.37% (1.099 mg g⁻¹ FW) for D64 and Z3, respectively, compared with WT (1.556 mg g⁻¹ FW). Chlorophyll b levels were similar in WT, D64 and Z3 (Fig. 2D and Table S3). This reduction in chlorophyll may impair the light-harvesting capacity of the photosystems, leading to decreased photosynthetic efficiency and restricted plant growth in stosd1/uvi4.

Mutation of the StOSD1/UVI4 gene alters the chloroplast distribution pattern and fluorescence intensity within the palisade tissue of the LG region

To further investigate the phenotypic changes caused by the disruption of StOSD1/UVI4, we analysed the chloroplast distribution and autofluorescence intensity in the leaf cells of WT, D64, and Z3 using CLSM. Bright-field microscopy revealed that WT cells maintained a uniform spatial distribution with smooth contours (Fig. 3A a), whereas the cells of both the D64 and Z3 mutants presented distinct protrusion structures in their LG regions (Fig. 3A f, k). Chloroplast autofluorescence imaging in the far-red region (663–738 nm) and green region (499–551 nm) further revealed that chloroplasts in WT exhibited a homogeneous distribution pattern with consistent fluorescence signal across mesophyll cells (Fig. 3A b, c). In contrast, both the D64 (Fig. 3A g, h) and Z3 mutants (Fig. 3A l, m) showed weaker fluorescence signals within the LG regions. In WT plants, chloroplast autofluorescence was not detected within the red spectral range (571–625 nm) (Fig. 3A d). However, both the D64 mutant (Fig. 3A i) and the Z3 mutant (Fig. 3A n) displayed localized fluorescence intensification at the LG-DG boundaries within the same spectral range. High-magnification imaging revealed chloroplast aggregation in Z3 LG regions compared with their DG counterparts (Fig. 3B), with low-magnification panoramic views confirming systemic fluorescence attenuation across LG sectors (Fig. 3C), indicating potential functional impairment of chloroplasts.

Mutations in the StOSD1/UVI4 gene stimulate the enlargement of palisade tissue cells

To investigate the cellular basis of the changes in leaf morphology, we conducted comparative analyses of 4-week-old leaf sections via Safranin O/Fast green staining. In the WT plants, complete epidermal and mesophyll cells were clearly observed (Fig. 4A). However, in both the D64 and Z3 mutants, we detected significantly enlarged palisade tissue cells, whereas the epidermal cells and spongy mesophyll cells remained unchanged (Fig. 4A). This observation suggests that the StOSD1/UVI4 mutation specifically affects leaf palisade tissue development. To investigate whether the enlargement of palisade cells resulted from structural damage to the overlying epidermal cells, we subsequently performed field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) analysis on the integrity of epidermal cells in the LG region. The FESEM images confirmed that the epidermal ultrastructure remained intact (Fig. 4B). This finding effectively ruled out the possibility that epidermal damage was the cause of the enlargement of palisade cells. Previous studies in Arabidopsis have demonstrated that OSD1/UVI4 can trigger endoreduplication, which in turn leads to cellular hypertrophy in epidermal cells [2, 16]. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that mutations in StOSD1/UVI4 might induce a similar endoreduplication process in potato palisade tissue. To test this hypothesis, we performed 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining. The staining results revealed a significant increase in nuclear size in the abnormal palisade cells (Fig. 4C). To further investigate whether nuclear enlargement in palisade cells results from ploidy variation, we performed FISH with 5S rDNA probes on paraffin-embedded leaf sections. The 5S rDNA locus, which is located specifically on chromosome 1 in potato, produces four distinct hybridization signals in tetraploid nuclei during interphase, thereby serving as a reliable cytogenetic marker for ploidy determination. FISH analysis revealed more than four discrete 5S rDNA foci (Fig. 4D). These findings collectively demonstrate that impaired StOSD1/UVI4 function mediates palisade cell expansion by promoting endoreduplication.

StOSD1/UVI4 gene mutation increases ploidy leavel by endomitosis

We performed ploidy analysis on third-leaf cells of WT, D64, and Z3 plants using dual-probe FISH with 5S rDNA and 26S rDNA markers. In WT nuclei, both 26S rDNA (localized to the nucleolus organizer region on chromosome 2) and 5S rDNA loci consistently displayed four distinct hybridization foci during interphase (Fig. 5A), corresponding to normal tetraploid (4n) organization. The nuclei of the D64 and Z3 mutants exhibited variable ploidy levels and increased nuclear volumes (Fig. 5B-F). Notably, the 5S rDNA signals exceeded the 26S rDNA counts across all of the ploidy states (Fig. 5B-F). Discrete 5S rDNA and 26S rDNA signal proliferation in interphase nuclei can distinguish endomitosis from the endocycle. Endomitosis increases ploidy via full chromosomal segregation, thereby proportionally increasing the number of signal loci, whereas the endocycle retains original signal numbers but demonstrates enhanced fluorescence intensity via S-phase reiteration without segregation. Based on these distinct cytogenetic signatures, we propose that mutations in StOSD1/UVI4 induce endomitosis. Statistical analysis of nuclei with varying ploidy levels revealed a positive correlation between phenotype and increased ploidy in cells. WT plants maintained ploidy stability, with only 0.65% of the leaf nuclei exhibiting nontetraploid states (Table S4 and Fig. 5G). The D64 mutants demonstrated ploidy variation, wherein 3.72% of the nuclei exhibited abnormalities (Table S4 and Fig. 5G). Z3 mutants demonstrated ploidy dysregulation, with 14.77% of the nuclei exhibiting abnormalities (Table S4 and Fig. 5G). D64 roots presented 6.18% nontetraploid cells (Fig. 5H and Table S4), whereas Z3 and WT roots maintained strict tetraploidy. The combined evidence from both leaves and roots confirmed that StOSD1/UVI4 mutations drive genomic instability through endomitotic nuclear reorganization rather than the endocycle.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals the molecular determinants of leaf variegation in StOSD1/UVI4 mutants

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying leaf variegation, we performed comparative transcriptomic profiling between WT and Z3 mutant plants. A total of 6,706 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 3,548 upregulated and 3,158 downregulated genes (Table S5). DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with the criteria false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and a log2-fold change (FC) > 1 or < −1. In the volcano plot, 10 significantly upregulated genes and 10 significantly downregulated genes are highlighted (Fig. 6A). The heatmap displays the 50 most significant DEGs among all the DEGs (Fig. 6B). The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI SRA under accession number PRJNA1242038. Transcriptome reliability was confirmed through qRT‒PCR validation of three randomly selected upregulated genes (Soltu.DM.08G019590, Soltu.DM.08G029560, and Soltu.DM.08G020030) and three downregulated genes (Soltu.DM.10G002270, Soltu.DM.03G034520, and Soltu.DM.09G008210), showing strong correlation between sequencing and experimental data (Fig. 6E). Notably, the gene encoding protochlorophyllide reductase (Soltu.DM.10G002270), involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis, was downregulated. This enzyme is critical for converting protochlorophyllide to chlorophyll [37]. Additionally, the expression of the NPH3 protein-encoding gene (Soltu.DM.03G034520), which is part of the phototropin signalling system, was downregulated. In Arabidopsis, NPH3 regulates leaf orientation and flattening, optimizing light perception and promoting plant growth [38]. Among the 210 KEGG pathways analysed, “Metabolism” was the most enriched, with 106 pathways (Fig. 6C and Table S6). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the significant involvement of the ‘porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism’ pathway (KEGG ID: 00860), which is essential for porphyrin and chlorophyll biosynthesis and critical for photosynthesis and cellular functions. The critical genes included the protochlorophyllide reductase gene (Soltu.DM.10G002270), which downregulated glutamate-1-semialdehyde aminotransferase (Soltu.DM.04G005660) and chlorophyllase (Soltu.DM.09G024990), and upregulated staygreen (Soltu.DM.12G007750, Soltu.DM.12G007710) (Fig. 6D). This coordinated expression pattern suggests concurrent suppression of chlorophyll biosynthesis (through glutamate-1-semialdehyde aminotransferase and protochlorophyllide reductase gene downregulation) and delayed degradation (via staygreen upregulation and chlorophyllase downregulation). We hypothesize that StOSD1/UVI4 mutation disrupts the spatiotemporal coordination of chloroplast biogenesis and senescence programs, with this dual regulatory failure establishing the metabolic basis for variegation.

The number of nonglandular trichomes was reduced in the StOSD1/UVI4 mutant

While mutations in the UVI4 gene in Arabidopsis are known to increase trichome branching, whether the orthologous StOSD1/UVI4 gene in potato shares analogous functions remains unknown. To investigate this, we employed FESEM to analyse trichome morphology in WT, Z3 and D64. Following the trichome classification criteria for potato established by Cho et al. [32], four distinct trichome types were identified on both adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces: nonglandular type II, nonglandular type V, glandular type VI, and glandular type VII (Fig. 7A, B). Notably, unlike the Arabidopsis uvi4 mutants, neither Z3 nor D64 exhibited increased trichome branching. However, statistical analysis demonstrated a reduction in nonglandular trichome densities (types II and V) on both the leaf surfaces of the Z3 and D64 mutants compared with those of the WT plants (Fig. 7C, D; Table 1; Table S7). These findings suggest that StOSD1/UVI4 regulates trichome density rather than branching complexity in potato.

StOSD1/UVI4 mutation may causes the reproductive cells to skip the second meiotic division

In Arabidopsis, OSD1 plays a critical role in regulating the progression of the second meiotic division [14, 15]. However, the functional conservation of its orthologue StOSD1/UVI4 in potato remains uncharacterized. To address this issue, we conducted comparative cytological analysis of meiosis in WT and D64 mutant lines using DAPI-based chromosome staining. Our observations revealed that WT plants completed all stages of meiosis with normal chromosomal segregation (Fig. 8A-H), whereas the D64 mutants exhibited incomplete meiotic progression (Fig. 8I-N). These findings suggest that StOSD1/UVI4 may function as a key regulator enabling the bypass of meiosis II in potato, analogous to its characterized role in Arabidopsis.

Discussion

Evolutionary divergence and functional specialization

The presence of a single StOSD1/UVI4 homologue in potato and tomato contrasts sharply with the duplicated paralogues observed in Arabidopsis, Brassica, and monocots, indicating distinct evolutionary trajectories within the Solanaceae family. While tetraploid tobacco and diploid petunia retain multiple homologues, the singular StOSD1/UVI4 copy in potato suggests functional specialization in polyploid species, where precise spatiotemporal regulation of endoreduplication is essential for coordinating cell expansion with organ morphogenesis. In Arabidopsis, overexpression of UVI4 or OSD1 suppresses endoreduplication, resulting in dwarfed plants with reduced lateral shoots, whereas knockout lines exhibit hyperendoreduplication without significant alterations in plant size under standard growth conditions [2, 16, 17, 19]. Strikingly, knockout of StOSD1/UVI4 in potato not only induces endoreduplication but also causes leaf variegation and growth impairment—a phenotypic divergence not observed in Arabidopsis. In Arabidopsis, UVI4 and OSD1 redundantly control endoreduplication in epidermal and trichome cells, primarily affecting cell branching and differentiation [2, 10]. However, our study revealed tissue-specific functional divergence in potato: StOSD1/UVI4 exclusively regulates endoreduplication in palisade mesophyll cells (Figs. 4 and 5). This spatial restriction contrasts sharply with that of Arabidopsis and suggests that Solanaceae plants may have repurposed OSD1/UVI4 homologues to optimize photosynthetic tissue architecture. StOSD1/UVI4 may play dual regulatory roles in both mitotic cell cycle control and meiotic progression—a hypothesis corroborated by the meiotic arrest phenotype observed in D64 mutants.

Palisade cell endomitosis and leaf variegation

Our findings establish a previously unrecognized causal relationship between endomitotic polyploidization and chloroplast functional impairment. Notably, while the DG and LG sectors share identical genotypes, the variegated phenotype manifests exclusively in palisade cells undergoing endomitosis (Fig. 4A). This cell type-specific manifestation demonstrates that leaf colour variation arises autonomously in endomitotic cells rather than through tissue-wide signalling—a phenomenon distinct from classic variegation mechanisms [39–42]. Intriguingly, despite generating cells of varying ploidy levels, all endomitotic derivatives consistently exhibited attenuated chloroplast fluorescence in LG regions (Fig. 3A-C). This paradoxical uniformity in chloroplast dysfunction across different polyploid states suggests ploidy-independent regulation of organelle homeostasis, a phenomenon requiring further investigation through single-cell transcriptomic approaches. The observed chloroplast aggregation and fluorescence attenuation in the mutant LG sectors (Fig. 3A-C) correlate with the transcriptional downregulation of chlorophyll biosynthesis genes, particularly protochlorophyllide oxidoreductase (Fig. 6A). This dual impairment, which combines structural chloroplast mislocalization and metabolic pigment deficiency, creates a synergistic effect that likely compromises photosynthetic efficiency, consistent with the dwarf phenotype observed in Z3 mutants (Fig. 2C).

Endomitosis in tetraploid potato leaves

In Arabidopsis thaliana, several key cell cycle regulators are known to interact with UVI4 and OSD1, including CPR5 (AT5G64930), CCS52A1 (AT4G22910), CCS52A2 (AT4G11920), CCS52B (AT5G13840), CDC20.1 (AT4G33270), CDC20.5 (AT5G27570), APC10 (AT2G18290), CYCB1;2 (AT5G06150) and CYCB2;3 (AT1G15570) [19, 43]. Via homology analysis, we identified the potato orthologues of the following genes: Soltu.DM.04G022880 (CPR5), Soltu.DM.08G026740 (CCS52A1/A2), Soltu.DM.06G008810 (CCS52B), Soltu.DM.06G028180 (CDC20.1/5), Soltu.DM.02G006480 (APC10), Soltu.01G005930 (CYCB1;2) and Soltu.DM.03G035060 (CYCB2;3). Transcriptome analysis revealed that the expression of these identified orthologues was downregulated in the StOSD1/UVI4 mutant (Table S5). Given that UVI4/OSD1 functions as a regulator of the APC/C complex, the coordinated downregulation of these core APC/C components and targets suggests potential perturbations in APC/C activity and cell cycle progression in potato under the investigated conditions. However, as our current analysis was conducted at the transcriptional level, it does not provide direct evidence regarding how these proteins functionally interact within the potato APC/C complex. Future studies employing protein-protein interaction assays and functional characterisation will be essential for elucidating the precise mechanisms through which these cell cycle regulators interact and are modulated in potato.

Cytogenetic analysis revealed that specific endopolyploid cell types in tetraploid potato exhibit noncanonical replication patterns characterized by multiple rounds of chromosomal duplication events rather than a single round of endomitosis, potentially due to incomplete replication or other events in combination with endomitosis. Incomplete replication, wherein genetic material undergoes partial duplication, may result in compromised genomic integrity during DNA synthesis [44–46]. This phenomenon may account for the pronounced disparity in replication fidelity observed between the 5S and 26S rDNA loci (Fig. 5B-F), with structural constraints associated with heterochromatic regions likely contributing to this differential efficiency [44]. While previous studies have relied on flow cytometry to assess ploidy, this method has some limitations and may misidentify incomplete replication. In contrast, FISH provides a more precise and intuitive approach for determining the ploidy level of cells. We hypothesize that StOSD1/UVI4 mutations increase the likelihood of incomplete replication, leading to aneuploid nuclei after complete division. Some aneuploid nuclei may further replicate via endomitosis, creating nuclei with varying ploidy levels. These atypical replication patterns provide novel insights into endopolyploidization mechanisms, offering a revised framework for understanding genome plasticity in agriculturally important polyploid crop species.

Conclusion and future perspectives

This study, through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout analysis, identified StOSD1/UVI4 as a multifunctional gene in potato that governs palisade cell endomitosis and genome stability. The mutants presented leaf variegation, reduced chlorophyll levels, and ploidy escalation via endomitosis, which were correlated with impaired photosynthesis. Unlike Arabidopsis homologues that regulate epidermal endoreduplication, StOSD1/UVI4 uniquely controls mesophyll-specific polyploidization, reflecting functional divergence in Solanaceae. The results of transcriptomic profiling revealed that the decrease in chlorophyll biosynthesis and phototropin signalling in the StOSD1/UVI4 mutant may induce these phenotypes. However, cytological evidence revealed the dual role of this gene in mitosis and meiosis. Future studies should elucidate the mechanistic crosstalk between nuclear polyploidization and chloroplast dysfunction, dissect tissue-specific regulatory networks via single-cell omics, and explore StOSD1/UVI4’s potential in optimizing photosynthesis or stress resistance through controlled ploidy manipulation. Additionally, its conserved role in meiotic progression warrants investigation for the development of potato breeding strategies, bridging fundamental insights with agricultural innovation.
