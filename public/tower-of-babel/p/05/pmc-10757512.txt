High Mortality With COVID‐19 Acute Pericarditis
Authors: Dale S Adler
Published: 2023

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Li and Shi, and their coauthors used the National Inpatient Sample 2020 database and analyzed 211 619 patients with a primary diagnosis of COVID‐19. 1 They found that the incidence of acute pericarditis was 0.46% overall, with a lower incidence during the second half of the year, attributed to the initiation of dexamethasone for patients with COVID‐19 with respiratory compromise. They compared the 980 patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis to the patients with COVID‐19 without acute pericarditis, and found a striking difference in mortality (21.4% versus 11%, P<0.001). After matching that balanced age, younger patients might be more likely to have a vigorous, exuberant immune response; anemia that may be a marker for chronic disease; chronic kidney disease and cancer that reflect immune issues and diminished reserve; lower income, higher Medicaid, urban teaching hospital care site, and factors that can reflect access impediments to care; the patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis still had a much greater mortality rate (21.3% versus 11.1%, P<0.001).

The patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis compared with the patients with COVID‐19 without acute pericarditis had a greater incidence of clinical cardiac involvement, with a higher incidence of cardiogenic shock, heart failure, ventricular arrhythmia, and cardiac arrest. Not surprisingly, they also had a higher average length of stay. What is notable is that patients with acute pericarditis had a higher incidence of acute kidney injury but not a higher incidence of acute respiratory failure.

The patients with acute pericarditis presumably had at least 2 of the 4 hallmark signs of pericarditis, pleuritic chest discomfort, pericardial rub, characteristic electrocardiographic changes, and new or worsening pericardial effusion. However, a breakdown of the characteristics of the acute pericarditis population was not available. Troponin or NT‐ProBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) values that could help establish the extent of cardiac involvement or supply demand imbalance were not available, nor were some of the standard measures of inflammatory response, including white blood cell count, lymphocyte depletion, and C‐reactive protein.

Based on the comparison between the patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis with the patients with COVID‐19 without acute pericarditis, and based on the small drop in incidence of acute pericarditis following the introduction of dexamethasone, there is the supposition that the patients with acute pericarditis had a greater inflammatory response that involved the heart and that also involved the kidneys. A reasonable hypothesis is that clinical visceral pericardial involvement is related to generalized inflammation combined with myocardial inflammation that abuts the pericardium, and the combination of these influences, especially the intense myocardial involvement, are the basis for the much greater overall mortality. The clinical pericarditis is an indicator of the fury of both types of attacks on the pericardium.

Wisely, the authors also compared patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis with patients without COVID‐19 but with acute pericarditis in the same inpatient database. There was a matching for known demographic and clinical characteristics that have pegged worse outcome in patients with COVID‐19 from the earliest days of 2020: age, male sex, Black race, Hispanic ethnicity, urban residence, lower income, diabetes, and obesity.

There was a marked difference in mortality between COVID‐19 acute pericarditis and non‐COVID‐19 acute pericarditis (21.7% versus 7.3%, P<0.01). The 7.3% mortality rate for non‐COVID‐19 acute pericarditis seems high and bespeaks the overall illness in the hospitalized patient data set. Not surprisingly, the patients with COVID‐19 had a greater incidence of acute respiratory failure, because COVID‐19 is a disease that often starts in the respiratory epithelium. The patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis had a much greater incidence of acute kidney injury, suggesting that the acute kidney injury was not attributable just to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs or contrast, but to a heightened inflammatory component as well.

What is highly interesting is that unlike in the propensity‐matched comparison between patients with COVID‐19 with and without acute pericarditis, the increased mortality rate in the comparison between COVID‐19 acute pericarditis and non‐COVID‐19 acute pericarditis was not associated with greater cardiac morbidities, such as acute congestive heart failure or cardiogenic shock or ventricular arrhythmia. There was a higher incidence of cardiac arrest in the patients with COVID‐19, but arrest in a group with much higher acute respiratory failure may have been a right ventricular strain or global metabolic issue rather than a marker of more myocardial damage.

Based on the careful propensity‐matched comparison of non‐COVID‐19 acute pericarditis versus the COVID‐19 acute pericarditis group, there appears to be a suggestion, again, that inflammation is the driving mechanism, as evidenced by the high incidence of acute kidney injury, with the longtime knowledge that the glomeruli and interstitial tissue are important integrators of systemic inflammation. However, the devastation from the inflammatory effects does not seem to be attributable only to myocardial involvement with secondary or perimyocardial involvement. The added respiratory component in patients with COVID‐19 of course plays a role. Is there a possibility that the pericarditis in the patients with COVID‐19 arises from a different route and a different group of inflammatory mediators than the pericarditis in patients without COVID‐19? Does a different route or mechanism for attack on the pericardium, perhaps combined with different inflammatory markers, also contribute to the nearly 3‐fold mortality increment that occurs in patients with COVID‐19 who have, versus those who do not have, clinically manifest acute pericarditis? In the presence of COVID‐19, does acute pericarditis sum inflammation that is in the myocardium, and especially the subepicardium, in addition to inflammation that gets to the pericardium via a different route? Are the inflammatory mediators necessarily the same from the 2 different routes? Is the assault on the pericardium from >1 direction a marker for a greater system inflammatory response that may account for the 3‐fold mortality differences in patients with COVID‐19 and acute pericarditis, whether compared with other patients with COVID‐19 or with other acute pericarditis patients?

A question that can be raised is why report on a study involving patients with COVID‐19 in 2020. There can be an argument that the world has moved on from a time when there were no vaccines (until the end of the 2020), and when there was virtually no innate immunity from prior exposures. By early 2021, α variants took over from ancestral variants, and by the latter half of 2021, the δ variants were rampant. By early 2022, Omicron was becoming dominant, starting with 1.1.529 and evolving through multiple BA variations, and then to BQ 1.1 and to XBB variations. 2 Each variant seemed more contagious but less virulent, and a bivalent booster, released before human efficacy trials, seemed to afford 64% protection against COVID‐19 hospitalizations and death. 3

Some of the dreaded vascular effects of COVID‐19, including an association with myocardial infarction and stroke, have been mitigated by vaccines. 4 Lay and scientific community attention has turned to the origins of COVID‐19, asking whether it was accidentally transmitted from a bat in the Wuhan, China seafood wholesale market or from an accidental escape from the Wuhan Institute of Virology, or from a more nefarious source. 5 , 6 There remains interest in which states and countries had the most judicious masking and return to work and school policies. There is concern over future effectiveness of nirmatrelvir–ritonavir (Paxlovid), which was associated with a 70% reduction in hospitalization in patients >65 years old 7 that targets NSP5 (nonstructural protein 5) in the spike protein, and whether the target will continue to remain conserved. 8

Serious effects of COVID‐19 are not gone. At the end of 2020, there had been 1.5 million COVID‐19 deaths and 80 million infections. 9 By the summer of 2023, there were >6.9 million World Health Organization‐reported deaths and more than three‐quarters of a billion cases (https://covid19.who.int/). Long COVID‐19, or postacute sequala of COVID‐19, is a major focus, with recognized important effects at 6 months and longer that include double the expected pulmonary symptoms and 20% to 40% increments in neurologic and mental health complaints, in addition to an increased incidence of diabetes. 10 Subsumed in the neurologic category are the large numbers of patients with fatigue and dysautonomia, including postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome; included in the mental health complaints are the patients with cognitive symptoms, depression and anxiety (there has been documentation of increased neural inflammation measured with a positron emission tomography index for gliosis 11 ). Diabetes is not the only metabolic alteration that is unfavorably affected. There is a lingering dyslipidemia that may turn out to be important, as was noted 12 years after SARS‐CoV, the predecessor to SARS‐CoV‐2. 12

As Li and Shi and their coauthors point out, analysis of 2020 patients and the focus on acute pericarditis may offer a window into COVID‐19 manifestations, acute and chronic, and offer insight into pericarditis. Initial studies of COVID‐19 mortality focused on the catastrophic outcomes in older patients with underlying cardiovascular disease and cardiovascular risk factors who were not able to tolerate the profound COVID‐19 respiratory burden and the secondary infections. There was an early awareness that cardiac involvement, primarily or secondarily, manifested by troponin elevation, was highly important. Additionally, there was an awareness that markers of systemic inflammation, white blood cell count, absolute lymphocyte count, platelets, and albumin, could help to predict outcome. 13 Progressively, there was an awareness and concern that ACE2 (angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2) expression in cardiac tissue as well as in the respiratory epithelium might account for the 11.8% of patients, in one series, who presented with only cardiac symptoms. 14

Our present understanding represents an evolution from concern about binding of the virus in the cardiovascular system to an understanding that cardiovascular and systemic effects are related to inflammation with positive feedback loops that overwhelm counter regulatory mechanisms in the endothelium resting monolayer. 15 The result of the lifting of counter regulatory mechanisms to inflammation leads to vascular permeability, vasoconstriction, increased oxidative stress, fibrosis, and clotting. The mediators include TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor‐α), IL (interleukin)‐1α and β, in addition to IL‐2, Il‐6, IL‐7, IL‐17, monocyte chemo attractant protein‐1, macrophage inflammatory protein 1‐α, and many other factors. The auto amplification also causes an imbalance of type 1 and type 2 helper cells. 9

There is now a recognition that troponin elevations and the associated increment in mortality are related to myocardial inflammation, with a macrophage infiltration and often a lymphocytic infiltration and microvascular thrombosis, in addition to demand ischemia, with a lower frequency of inflammation‐mediated plaque instability, stress cardiomyopathy, or direct viral myocarditis. Focal pericarditis has been seen in up to 19% of autopsy patients with COVID‐19 and generally not in the setting of myocyte injury. 16

The study by Li and Shi suggests that when there is clinically manifest acute pericarditis in a patient with COVID‐19, whether there is more myocardial involvement or not, there is a level of inflammation that is associated with a striking mortality difference. The study forces a further elucidation of the causes of pericarditis in COVID‐19, and makes us ask why clinical pericardial involvement, not even with tamponade, might be associated with such high mortality.

The visceral pericardium has a blood supply from the internal thoracic arteries, off the subclavian arteries, and from the bronchial arteries, off the thoracic aorta. The visceral pericardium has postganglionic sympathetic innervation and preganglionic parasympathetic innervation. The sensory neurons are carried within the phrenic nerve. 17 , 18 There is also lymphatic drainage of the pericardium. System inflammation with endothelial changes in the pericardial vasculature can inflame the visceral pericardium that then becomes even more vascular, and thus even more inflamed. Alterations of the parasympathetic ganglia may be part of the sinus tachycardia and even atrial arrhythmia that are often seen in acute pericarditis. The parietal pericardium, largely fibrous and avascular, can serve to trap the inflammation in the pericardial sack. A rub and pleuritic discomfort and an effusion ensue. If visceral pericardial inflammation is severe enough, there can be a myopericarditis, because the changes in the visceral pericardium affect the epicardium, with resultant electrocardiographic changes, alongside the expected pain, rub, and effusion. Myopericarditis is somewhat distinct from perimyocarditis, in which visceral pericardial inflammation is secondary to epicardial inflammation. The high mortality rates associated with COVID‐19 acute pericarditis may reflect both processes, with greater myocardial involvement than is seen in the patients with COVID‐19 without acute pericarditis, and greater, direct pericardial involvement than is seen in non‐COVID‐19 acute pericarditis. The higher mortality in COVID‐19 acute pericarditis compared with non‐COVID‐19 acute pericarditis is partly respiratory mediated. Acute pericarditis may also sum myocardial involvement and direct pericardial vascular involvement. For there to be direct, pericardial vascular involvement, there may have to be inflammation in the blood supply to the pericardium, perhaps starting with inflammation in the aorta and its branches, perhaps derived from the bronchial arteries being affected by respiratory involvement. There may have to be specific inflammatory markers that derive from direct effects on the pericardial vasculature. The renal involvement may reflect a similar issue. Whereas as many as 19% of COVID‐19 autopsies show patchy pericardial involvement, the patients with clinically manifest acute pericarditis would be expected to have much greater pericardial inflammation and perhaps greater endothelial alterations in the blood supply to the visceral pericardium. The large vessel inflammation, with a different array of inflammatory mediators, may be part of the explanation for the much higher mortality and morbidity (length of stay) associated with the acute pericarditis in patients with COVID‐19.

Non‐COVID‐19 pericarditis has a high recurrence rate mitigated by colchicine 19 and by IL‐1α and IL‐1β antagonists, anakinra and rilonacept. 9 , 20 There is a great deal of interest in sorting out precise mechanisms of inflammation in COVID‐19 and even COVID‐19 vaccines with assessments of agents that affect T cells, TNF‐α, and monocytes and macrophages, 21 and the elucidation of the expression of an antibody to the regulation of IL‐1Ra (part of the explanation for vaccine myocarditis). 22

A focus on past and present patients with COVID‐19 acute pericarditis, with deeper phenotyping and elucidation of vessels involved and inflammatory markers, may provide more insight into this group's high mortality, COVID‐19 morbidity in general, perhaps the alteration of the parasympathetic nervous system seen in long COVID‐19, and the mechanisms of inflammation in non‐COVID‐19 pericarditis.
