Clinical outcomes and mechanical evaluation of patient-specific mandibular reconstruction following tumor resection: a retrospective study with finite element analysis
Authors: Jiwon Do, Jeong Joon Han, Jong-Ho Lee, Ik-Jae Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMandibular defects most frequently result from ablative oncologic surgery and pose major functional and esthetic challenges. While vascularized osseous free flaps remain the gold-standard treatment, patient-specific implants (PSIs) fabricated by three-dimensional (3D) printing are an increasingly popular alternative. PSIs provide superior anatomical conformity and improved cosmetic outcomes, yet their long-term mechanical reliability and fatigue resistance remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate both the biomechanical performance and clinical outcomes of 3D-printed PSIs for mandibular reconstruction.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed nine patients who underwent mandibular reconstruction with PSIs at Seoul National University Dental Hospital. Clinical outcomes and complications were reviewed at final follow-up (mean 36.1 ± 17.3 months; range, 5.5–63.9 months). Reconstruction plates were categorized into two types: Non-Filling Contour Plate (NFCP) and Defect-Filling Mesh Plate (DFMP). Finite element analysis (FEA) was performed on representative cases to assess stress distribution and fatigue behavior under physiological loading.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 36.1 months, two fractures occurred (22%): one NFCP at 34 months and one DFMP at 20 months, with both exhibiting segmental defects. FEA showed deformation of 2.01 mm in NFCP, 2.20 mm in DFMP, and 1.45 mm in modified DFMP. Peak stress was 683.3 MPa for NFCP, 378.2 MPa for DFMP and 498.8 MPa for modified DFMP, with DFMP showing mesh stress (156.6 MPa) exceeding local strength (68.2 MPa). NFCP fatigue analysis predicted ~ 184,000 cycles (≈ 3 months at maximal load), equivalent to 6–21 months under physiologic loading. Modified DFMP reduced mesh stress to 41.3 MPa.ConclusionsPSIs showed favorable clinical outcomes with excellent anatomical fit. Introduction

Mandibular defects most frequently occur after ablative surgery in the oral cavity, although they may also arise from trauma, infection, or congenital anomalies [1, 2]. Among these causes, oncologic resection represents the predominant etiology in adults and poses a major reconstructive challenge [3]. Resection of malignant tumors not only compromises mastication, speech, and airway stability, but also results in significant esthetic deformities, which together have a profound impact on patient quality of life [4, 5].

In recent decades, vascularized osseous free flaps—particularly fibula, iliac crest, and scapular flaps—have become the gold standard for mandibular reconstruction, providing reliable bone stock and long-term functional outcomes. However, these procedures are not without limitations. Donor-site morbidity, prolonged operative time, and perioperative risks often preclude their use in elderly or medically compromised patients. Moreover, in cases of extensive tumor-related resection, flap availability and geometry may not adequately restore mandibular continuity [6, 7].

As an alternative or adjunct, metallic reconstruction plates have remained a mainstay in oncologic mandibular repair. Conventional stock plates are widely available and allow immediate restoration of mandibular continuity, but intraoperative bending is required to adapt them to individual anatomy. This process is time-consuming, may compromise esthetic outcomes due to contour mismatch, and has been shown to weaken the mechanical integrity of the plate, thereby increasing the risk of complications such as fatigue fracture, screw loosening, and plate exposure under repetitive masticatory loading [8–11].

The advent of three-dimensional (3D) printing has enabled the development of patient-specific implants (PSIs), which are designed to precisely fit resection defects. In this process, high-resolution preoperative imaging data, typically obtained using computed tomography (CT), are converted into three-dimensional digital models of the mandible. Based on these models, virtual surgical planning is conducted to simulate tumor resection and reconstruct the anticipated defect. Subsequently, a patient-specific implant is designed to precisely conform to the planned post-resection mandibular contour and is fabricated using additive manufacturing techniques, such as stereolithography or selective laser melting [12, 13]. Particularly in oncologic cases where defects are large and geometrically complex, PSIs offer superior anatomical conformity, reduced operative time, and improved cosmetic outcomes [14, 15]. Despite these advantages, the mechanical reliability and fatigue resistance of 3D-printed PSIs have not been fully elucidated. In particular, data on long-term outcomes after tumor resection and the relationship between design features and implant survival remain limited.

In this study, we report our clinical experience with PSIs in the repair of mandibular defects following oncologic and other ablative surgeries. We evaluated clinical outcomes and complications, and used finite element analysis (FEA) to investigate stress distribution and fatigue behavior of representative designs. This combined clinical and biomechanical approach aimed to identify design limitations and propose strategies to improve long-term mechanical reliability in oncologic mandibular reconstruction.

Patients and methods

Patient presentation

This retrospective study included all patients who underwent mandibular reconstruction using patient-specific reconstruction plates in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Seoul National University Dental Hospital. All surgeries were performed between October 2018 and January 2020. During this period, patient-specific reconstruction plates were applied selectively based on reconstructive requirements, and a total of nine patients underwent mandibular reconstruction using these implants. Clinical and radiographic follow-up was conducted from June 2020 to October 2024 to evaluate postoperative outcomes and complications. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Dental Hospital (IRB No. CDE19001), and all procedures were performed in accordance with institutional ethical standards, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Plate design classification

All reconstruction plates were manufactured using 3D printing with a Ti-6Al-4 V alloy. All reconstruction plates were manufactured using 3D printing with a Ti-6Al-4 V alloy. The production process consisted of preoperative CT data acquisition, three-dimensional image segmentation and modeling using Mimics software (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium; version 19.0), virtual surgical planning, and patient-specific plate design. The finalized design was fabricated using additive manufacturing techniques, followed by post-processing procedures including heat treatment, surface finishing, and sterilization. The overall production time from image acquisition to delivery of the final implant was approximately 2–3 weeks, depending on the complexity of the defect and the manufacturing schedule. The implant designs were categorized into two types based on their structural characteristics and bone-contact interface. The Non-Filling Contour Plate (NFCP) is a design that leaves the defect space unfilled and instead closely conforms to the outer surface of the residual mandible. The Defect-Filling Mesh Plate (DFMP) is a design in which the bone defect zone is filled with a lightweight mesh structure that is integrated into the plate to occupy the resection void. The modified DFMP is a design in which the mesh structure is repositioned away from the bone surface to minimize local stress and enhance mechanical reliability (Fig. 1).

Finite element model

Finite element modeling and analysis were performed with the assistance of a professional engineer with expertise in biomedical finite element simulations. The collaboration focused on accurately reproducing physiologic oral loading conditions and mandibular biomechanics to ensure realistic mechanical interpretation of the reconstruction plate behavior. Patient-specific mandibular geometries were reconstructed from preoperative CT images and converted into stereolithography format. FEA was conducted using Ansys 2024 R1 (ANSYS, Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA). The mandible was modeled as a single solid body with a uniform cortical bone thickness of 1.5 mm. The 3D-printed reconstruction plates were modeled based on the computer-aided design files used in clinical fabrication. Screws were simplified as solid cylinders with a diameter of 2.3 mm and length of 10 mm, and threads were not modeled. All bone–plate and screw–plate interactions were defined using linear contact conditions.

Given the complex curved geometry of the mandible and implant structures, meshing was performed using SOLID187, a 10-node quadratic tetrahedral element in Ansys. For accurate stress resolution, the maximum element size for the implant plate and screws was set to 0.5 mm, while a coarser mesh size of 1.0 mm was applied to the condyle and mandibular bone. The final mesh density varied by case: NFCP consisted of 1,849,954 nodes and 1,293,601 elements, while DFMP and modified DFMP contained 2,570,470 nodes and 1,785,655 elements.

Loading and boundary conditions

Physiological occlusal loading was simulated by applying a total bite force of 403.7 N, uniformly distributed across the occlusal surfaces of the mandibular dentition. In addition to bite force, masticatory muscle forces were also applied to the mandible to replicate physiological loading conditions.

Muscle force data were adopted from a published finite element modeling study [16] and applied symmetrically on both sides of the mandible (Fig. 1). The following total muscle forces were used: 378 N for the lateral pterygoid muscles (LP), 191.4 N for the medial pterygoid muscles (MP), 528.6 N for the temporalis muscles (T), 340 N for the masseter muscles (M), and 155.9 N for the openers (O). These forces were divided and assigned to anatomically accurate insertion points of each muscle group, according to anatomical landmarks derived from the patient-specific mandibular models. To simulate muscle contraction during mastication, the forces were applied in vector directions representing the anatomical line of action for each muscle. For NFCP, where M and MP were surgically resected, the force vectors from M and MP were excluded in accordance with clinical findings. The posterior aspect of the mandible and both condylar regions were constrained in all degrees of freedom to represent fixed boundary conditions during clenching.

Material properties and analysis parameters

Material properties for bone and implant structures were defined according to values reported in the literature [17, 18]. The cortical bone was modeled with a young’s modulus of 16,300 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.31, while cancellous bone was assigned a young’s modulus of 960 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.30. The implant plate and screw fabricated from Ti-6Al-4 V alloy were modeled with a young’s modulus of 120,000 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.33. The implant mesh was assigned a young’s modulus of 2,100 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.33 (Table 1).

To evaluate mechanical reliability, two types of simulations were conducted. The first was static structural analysis, which assessed deformation and von Mises stress distribution under maximum bite force loading. The second was fatigue life analysis, which estimated the number of cycles to failure under repetitive masticatory loading using a stress–life (S–N) curve for Ti-6Al-4 V, with the applied cyclic force ranging from 0 N to 403.7 N. Due to a lack of validated fatigue data for meshed components, fatigue analysis was not performed for modified DFMP designs.

Results

Clinical outcomes

A total of 9 patients (4 males, 5 females) were included in the study, with a mean age of 68.9 years (range, 60–79 years). The mean follow-up period was 36.1 ± 17.3 months (range, 5.5–63.9 months) (Table 2). Patients presented with various types of mandibular defects, including marginal defects (n = 3), segmental defects involving the mandibular body (n = 2), body and ramus (n = 3), and condyle (n = 1). Reconstructive procedures were performed either as a primary insertion (n = 3) or as a secondary insertion (n = 6) following previous ablative surgery (Fig. 2).

Finite element analysis

The ultimate strength of solid Ti-6Al-4 V was set at 951 MPa, whereas the ultimate strength in meshed regions of the plate was reduced to 68.2 MPa to account for the structural weakening caused by perforation. The quantitative results of deformation, stress distribution, and fatigue analysis for all three designs are summarized in Table 3. NFCP showed a slightly reduced deformation of 2.01 mm. In DFMP, the maximum deformation was 2.20 mm, which was observed at the lower aspect of the mandible where occlusal forces were applied. In modified DFMP, the maximum deformation was further reduced to 1.45 mm (Fig. 4). In terms of ultimate strength and stress distribution, NFCP exhibited a high peak stress of 683.3 MPa, though this remained below the ultimate strength of Ti-6Al-4 V (951 MPa). The maximum von Mises stress in DFMP reached 378.2 MPa, which was well below the ultimate strength of solid Ti-6Al-4 V (951 MPa). However, in the meshed region of the DFMP, the stress was 156.6 MPa, exceeding the estimated ultimate strength of the meshed titanium (68.2 MPa). In modified DFMP, although the overall maximum stress in the plate increased to 498.8 MPa, it remained within the yield limits for Ti-6Al-4 V (951 MPa); importantly, the stress in the meshed region was significantly reduced to 41.3 MPa, well below the ultimate strength (Fig. 5). Regarding fatigue behavior under cyclic loading, fatigue analysis for NFCP using repeated masticatory loading of 403.7 N estimated a fatigue life of approximately 184,470 cycles (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the clinical outcomes and mechanical behavior of patient-specific mandibular reconstruction plates using retrospective data and finite element analysis. Clinically, plate fracture occurred in two patients (one NFCP and one DFMP) at 20 and 34 months postoperatively, and early screw loosening was observed in one DFMP case. Finite element analysis demonstrated that NFCP showed high peak stress, DFMP generated excessive stress within the meshed region, and the modified DFMP reduced mesh-related stress while exhibiting the lowest overall deformation, suggesting improved mechanical performance.

The popularity of PSIs for mandibular reconstruction has increased rapidly due to their superior anatomical conformity and potential to improve both functional and esthetic outcomes. Previous studies have primarily emphasized surgical feasibility and esthetic restoration [14, 15, 19, 20], but long-term mechanical reliability has remained underexplored, particularly in oncologic patients who often require wide segmental resection [21].

In this study, we evaluated both clinical outcomes and mechanical reliability of patient-specific reconstruction plates. Clinically, most patients healed uneventfully, confirming that PSIs can be feasible in complex oncologic cases. However, plate fractures occurred in both DFMP and NFCP designs, indicating that implant failure may arise from distinct mechanical mechanisms. Importantly, FEA closely reflected these outcomes: DFMPs exhibited stress concentrations at the mesh–plate junction that exceeded the ultimate strength, while NFCPs showed peak stress localized at the anterior border of the mandibular angle.

In NFCPs, fatigue analysis predicted a lifespan of 184,000 cycles, equivalent to roughly three months under maximal loading. Taking into account that actual masticatory forces are usually lower and variable, the realistic fatigue life could be extended to 6–21 months [22, 23]. This finding underscores that cumulative fatigue, rather than acute overload, may drive long-term failure. Interestingly, NFCP fracture was clinically observed at 34 months postoperatively—later than the predicted fatigue life. This discrepancy likely reflects patient-specific factors such as reduced masticatory loading due to limited dentition and dietary modification, highlighting how in vivo conditions can prolong implant survival beyond computational estimates [24–26]. Although fatigue life analysis in FEA cannot perfectly replicate the complex and variable real-world environment, incorporating cyclic loading evaluation remains essential for assessing the long-term stability of reconstruction plates.

For DFMP and modified DFMP, fatigue life could not be meaningfully assessed. When a full masticatory load of 403.7 N was applied, immediate fracture occurred at the weakened mesh region, precluding cyclic fatigue testing. This suggests that meshed structures may be intrinsically vulnerable to early failure under maximal loading conditions. Moreover, this highlights the limitations of our analysis. The use of uniform peak loads in FEA may not replicate the dynamic and submaximal forces encountered during daily mastication. Therefore, while static stress evaluation provided valuable insights, direct extrapolation of fatigue lifespan to clinical settings should be approached with caution.

Compared with previous studies that reported fracture rates of up to 8–30% in conventional stock plates [11, 27, 28], the complication rate in our series (2 of 9 patients, 22%) was within a similar range. This suggests that while PSIs offer clear advantages in anatomical adaptation, anatomical fit alone does not ensure mechanical durability. The modified DFMP design, which relocated the mesh away from the bone surface, markedly reduced stress concentration and improved safety margins, demonstrating how relatively small design modifications can yield meaningful mechanical benefits.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and small sample size limit the generalizability of the clinical findings. Although all patients who underwent mandibular reconstruction using patient-specific implants during the study period were included, the cohort remains limited due to the relatively recent and selective clinical adoption of PSIs. Second, the follow-up duration, while sufficient to capture mid-term mechanical complications, may not fully reflect long-term implant performance beyond several years. To address and mitigate these limitations, we developed a high-fidelity, patient-specific FEA model that enables detailed biomechanical evaluation under physiologically relevant loading conditions, thereby providing complementary evidence to support and reinforce our clinical findings.

In summary, our study achieved its purpose by evaluating the clinical performance of patient specific reconstruction plates after oncologic mandibular resection, identifying mechanical failure modes of both DFMP and NFCP, and demonstrating that design optimization, particularly repositioning meshed structures, can improve mechanical reliability. Most importantly, our findings highlight fatigue life assessment as an essential step in preclinical evaluation of PSIs for long-term stability. Future work should incorporate larger cohorts and patient-specific loading conditions to establish robust design guidelines for long-term implant survival in oncologic reconstruction.

Conclusions

Patient-specific 3D-printed reconstruction plates demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes and excellent anatomical fit in mandibular reconstruction. Nonetheless, observed plate fractures highlight the need for careful mechanical design, particularly in load-bearing regions. FEA effectively predicted stress concentrations and guided design refinement. Ensuring adequate fatigue strength remains important for long-term implant durability. Future efforts should focus on optimizing geometry, improving material performance, and accounting for patient-specific functional loading conditions.
