Myoblast paracrine factors induce catabolic remodelling in electrically stimulated myotubes
Authors: Magda A. Lesinski, Michael J. Kamal, Gianni Parise
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle plasticity is strongly influenced by a multitude of cell populations within the muscle niche. Satellite cells, or resident muscle stem cells, play an essential role in skeletal muscle remodelling after injury where they differentiate into myoblasts and directly donate myonuclei to damaged fibres thereby promoting protein synthesis. Satellite cells have also been shown to act in a paracrine fashion to influence the infiltration of immune cells and other cell types into the skeletal muscle environment to advance regeneration. However, in many degenerative muscular conditions, satellite cell activity is impaired. Moreover, their paracrine mediated signalling directly to muscle fibres remains poorly understood, highlighting a promising therapeutic target for addressing satellite cell dysfunction. The purpose of this project was two-fold: (1) develop an in vitro model of myoblast (MBL) intercellular communication following myotube damage and (2) to determine if MBL proximity alone is adequate for improving tissue repair and reducing cellular stress during recovery. C2C12-derived myotubes were exposed to 1 h of electrical pulse stimulation (EPS). Immediately following EPS, porous cell inserts containing either myoblasts (MBL+) or no cells (MBL–) were introduced to the damaged myotubes. We employed quantitative protein, mRNA, and morphological analyses of myotubes to characterize the phenotypic response to myoblast signalling. EPS produced Z line sarcomeric disorganization and creatine kinase release from the myotubes, which was mitigated in the MBL + groups (p < 0.05). A significant main effect of MBL exposure was observed in molecular indicators of muscle repair and metabolism; MBL + myotubes had greater hspa1a gene expression, calpain 3 protein and gene expression, BNIP3 and t-ULK protein expression as compared to MBL– myotubes when recovering from EPS (p < 0.05). Myotube diameter decreased significantly in MBL + myotubes post-EPS (p < 0.05). Myoblasts participate in intercellular crosstalk directly to damaged myotubes, and this signalling may increase catabolic processes through the upregulation of contraction-mediated protease activity and autophagic protein content. This research is the first to identify an early, non-fusogenic response mechanism of muscle-resident myoblasts to skeletal muscle in response to damaging stimuli.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21126-y.

Background

Satellite cells (SCs) are a lineage of multipotent muscle progenitor cells (MPCs) that reside between the basal lamina and sarcolemma of muscle fibres1. They remain in a state of quiescence until activated by environmental stressors, after which they proliferate, differentiate into myoblasts, and migrate to sites of damage in response to cytokine release from immune cells and the damaged muscle itself2. While the established primary role of SCs is to facilitate myofibre regeneration through fusion events and nuclear donation, there is evidence to suggest that SCs also participate in non-fusogenic signalling1. Paracrine communication has been observed both in vitro and in vivo between MPCs and other skeletal muscle resident cells through several signalling molecules3–5. However, our understanding of the complete secretome of SCs and other MPCs is limited. Under basal conditions, C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes share secretory molecules classified under biological processes such as vesicle-mediated transport and common molecular functions include cytoskeletal protein binding, unfolded protein binding, and calcium ion-binding6. Furthermore, in vitro myotubes stimulated or treated with exercise mimetics release hundreds of proteins generally involved in stress responses, cytoskeletal remodelling, and catabolism6. While these data provide a comprehensive understanding of the secretory profile of single cell populations, research into the dynamic nature of extracellular signalling when cells are in more complex, multi-cell type environments, remains to be addressed.

Of late, studies have identified a role for MPCs in extracellular signalling to adjacent, namely fibrogenic, cells in response to hypertrophic stimuli. In these conditions, MPCs release micro-RNA responsible for inhibiting collagen biosynthesis, where the lack of MPCs consequently results in excess collagen deposition in the skeletal muscle microenvironment by fibrogenic cells4. Furthermore, lineage tracing of fusion-incompetent MPCs co-cultured with myotubes demonstrated an increase of MPC-derived markers within myotubes during load-induced hypertrophy, indicating the uptake of MPC-originating content that participate in extracellular matrix remodelling4.

As MPCs have robust fusogenic activity in conditions of skeletal muscle growth, regeneration, and hypertrophy, models that have eliminated this cell population or inhibited its activity have shown severe impairments in these processes7–9. Further, with increasing age, there is a decline in MPC content and activity that has been correlated with sarcopenia10. It has been proposed that aging-associated increases in fibrosis may “incarcerate” these cells and prohibit their migration to sites of damage and perhaps impair their potential for anti-fibrotic signalling4,10. As such, insight into the paracrine signalling potential of myogenic progenitor cells may elucidate therapeutic targets for conditions where the migratory and fusogenic capacities of these cell types are impaired.

The objective of this study was to develop a model of in vitro myoblast signalling to damaged myotubes, investigate the potential role of their secretory factors, and determine if fusion-independent mechanisms broadly influence recovery and cellular stress in vitro. We sought to characterize the morphological and physiological adaptations associated with electrical pulse stimulation (EPS)-induced myotube contraction and damage, and the concomitant effects of myoblast paracrine signalling thereafter.

Results

EPS induces structural and molecular alterations to myotubes which May be attenuated by the presence of myoblasts

The aim of this project was to develop an in vitro model of muscle progenitor cell paracrine signalling and investigate its effects on skeletal muscle remodelling and repair in response to contraction-induced damage. Fully differentiated C2C12 myotubes were subjected to electrical pulse stimulation (EPS) for 1 h as previously described11. Using transmission electron microscopy, we confirmed the chosen protocol was sufficient to elicit myotube damage as indicated by disorganization of sarcomeres. Immediately after EPS, sarcomeres displayed obvious signs of contraction-mediated stress through Z line streaming, which was absent in the NS condition (Fig. 1A). Next, we assessed EPS-induced changes in the expression of several proteins relating to cytoskeletal remodelling such as desmin and β-dystroglycan as well as key molecules involved in muscle protein turnover such as Unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase (ULK1), calpain 3, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and 4e-binding protein 1 (4EBP1) (Fig. 1B-C). Immediately after the stimulation protocol, we noted a significant decrease in desmin and p-ULK1ser555 as well as an increase in total ULK1 and total 4EBP1 protein content.

To elucidate the fusion-independent roles of muscle progenitor cells in response to skeletal muscle damage, transwell inserts containing adhered myoblasts (MBL+) or no cells (MBL–) were introduced to myotubes immediately following EPS (Fig. 1D). Creatine kinase (CK) is a cytoplasmic enzyme that resides closely to the M-line of sarcomeres under resting conditions. Upon insults to skeletal muscle in vivo and in vitro, CK is often leaked extracellularly due to enhanced membrane permeability12–14. As such, we investigated enzymatic activity of CK in conditioned media of stimulated myotubes with and without exposure to MBL. Consistent with previous literature, CK activity robustly increased by 3.3- and 4.4-fold in the media of myotubes 8- and 12-hours following EPS, respectively, relative to 1-hour post-EPS (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, exposure to MBL significantly reduced the EPS-induced leakage of CK up to 12-hours post stimulation. Next, we assessed desmin and β-dystroglycan expression due to their integral role in sarcomeric stability, noting that their deficiency has been linked to increased susceptibility to contraction-induced injury and sarcolemmal fragility15–17. Desmin protein expression did not change over the 24 h period post-EPS while β-dystroglycan protein content progressively decreased independent of MBL exposure as indicated by a main effect of time (Fig. 1F-H).

Anabolic stress responses May be upregulated with MBL exposure post-EPS

Intracellular stress responses associated with contraction are important modulators of homeostasis within skeletal muscle. In particular, heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), a molecular chaperone, has previously been reported to increase in response to intramuscular heat and reactive oxygen species accumulation following exercise to assist with appropriate protein folding and/or unfolding18–21. Hspa1a mRNA expression demonstrated a main effect of both time and MBL exposure, while protein expression of HSP70 was not influenced by MBL (Fig. 2A-C). The role of HSP70 in anabolic processes has yet to be fully elucidated but has been implicated in mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) inhibition through Rheb degradation22. As such, we sought to assess the expression and activation of mTOR as well as the downstream effector 4EBP1 after EPS due to its roles in regulating protein synthesis. The total and phosphorylated states of both mTOR and 4EBP1 remained unchanged post-EPS suggesting that molecular alterations following EPS that would influence muscle protein synthesis are independent of mTORC1 signalling (Fig. 2B, D-E).

Catabolic processes are upregulated with MBL exposure post-EPS

Given that EPS did not alter mTOR signalling, we next assessed the catabolic response to EPS. Regulation of muscle protein breakdown is crucial in skeletal muscle adaptations as it allows for the clearance of dysfunctional proteins and organelles through autophagic processes and cytoskeletal remodelling through proteolytic enzyme activity. Total ULK1 protein content was altered as a function of time and MBL exposure (Fig. 3A-B). While other markers of autophagy (lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1; LAMP1, LAMP2, and microtubule-associated protein 1 A/1B-light chain 3; LC3) and mitophagy (parkin) were unchanged in response to MBL exposure (Fig. 3C-E), the mitophagy-associated protein BNIP3 was significantly upregulated over time, with robust increases in MBL + myotubes in comparison to the MBL– controls (Fig. 3F). Further, a main effect of MBL exposure was observed with Capn3 mRNA content, suggesting that MBL may increase calpain 3 transcription (Fig. 3G). However, calpain 3 protein content did not change (Fig. 3H). Calpain 3 localization was assessed within the myotube using immunofluorescence, as it is predominantly present at full length in in vivo myofibrillar fractions, while nuclear fractions contain a minority of total Calpain 3 but a higher relative proportion of its autolyzed state23. Nevertheless, there were no observed changes in localization or fluorescent intensity within myotubular cytosol or nuclei. (Fig. 3I).

Myotube morphology is altered with MBL exposure post-EPS

As anabolic and catabolic signalling were affected by EPS and MBL exposure, the direct morphological consequences on myotube development were assessed. At both 12- and 24-hours post-EPS, MBL + myotubes were 13% and 8% smaller respectively than MBL– myotubes and MBL + myotubes contained a higher frequency of smaller myotubes (Fig. 4A-B). However, there were no observed changes to myotube surface area or fusion index (Fig. 4C-D).

Discussion

The aim of the current investigation was to broadly identify the impact of myoblast (MBL) signalling on damaged muscle cells independent of fusion-mediated processes. Herein, we present evidence suggesting that this EPS protocol was able to simulate contraction-mediated muscle damage as identified by sarcomeric disorganization and increases in extracellular creatine kinase (CK) concentrations. The presence of myoblasts was able to regulate these processes by reducing CK levels and increasing protease and autophagic responses, while decreasing myotube size post-EPS compared to MBL– myotubes. Together, these data suggest that paracrine factors from myoblasts enhanced catabolism in adjacent EPS-stimulated myotubes (Fig. 4E).

The skeletal muscle niche is a complex environment that contains a multitude of cell types, including immune cell populations, fibro-adipogenic progenitor cells, satellite cells, and of course skeletal muscle fibres1. The highly plastic nature of skeletal muscle allows it to rapidly respond and adapt to various stimuli, such as muscle contraction and damage. These adaptations are tightly regulated through many mechanisms, which often rely on paracrine signalling. One such example are myokines (cytokines released from skeletal muscle), which have been implicated in immune cell infiltration into the skeletal muscle niche to promote pro-inflammatory clearance of necrotic tissue24. This ultimately leads to satellite cell activation and later differentiation to promote skeletal muscle repair through fusion-dependent mechanisms.

Exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD) is characterized by the ultrastructural changes in muscle tissue that is often associated with reduced muscle strength, increased muscle soreness and the secretion of myofibrillar enzymes. EIMD is a consequence of lengthening–eccentric or lengthened isometric contractions rather than concentric contractions, often leading to sarcomeric disorganization due to the increased mechanical strain25. The EPS protocol used in this study was adapted from Orfanos and colleagues (2016), who utilized EPS to develop an in vitro ‘damage’ protocol. They identified the formation of sarcomeric lesions on myotubes in vitro that were observable as quickly as 1-hour with continuous EPS and continued to grow thereafter11. In the present study, Z line streaming was evident after administration of this same protocol, as visualized through transmission electron microscopy. However, myotube stimulation with EPS represents neither eccentric nor complete concentric contractions, as myotubes do not stretch and cannot fully shorten when adhered to an inflexible substrate11. As such, the adaptations seen herein are difficult to attribute to a particular exercise modality and may more closely resemble the consequences of an isometric contraction26. Despite this, the ultrastructural alterations suggest the induction of a phenotype similar to that seen in EIMD11.

The sarcomeric disorganization seen with muscle damage is observable in vivo immediately post-exercise and persists throughout recovery. As seen previously, sarcomeric damage has been localized at lesion sites where the sarcomeres lose contractile capacity11. Desmin is the chief intermediate filament that aligns with the Z band and is a primary proteolytic target during cytoskeletal remodelling with damage27. Eccentric exercise in vivo has shown increases in desmin protein content at lesion sites, however these expression changes have been noted at 2–8 days post-exercise, with no acute desmin response17. In conditions of disuse atrophy in vivo, it has been observed that initial loss of desmin is required for subsequent myofibril disassembly, suggesting that the immediate decrease observed in the current study may be associated with the initial disruption in the molecular scaffolding required for remodelling.

Skeletal muscle damage increases membrane permeability, which occurs in concert with sarcomeric disorganization, as this structure is tightly associated with anchoring structures that connect to the extracellular matrix28,29. In the present study, membrane permeability was augmented in electrically stimulated myotubes as evidenced by the increased release of cytosolic creatine kinase. It is well established that, in vivo, conditions of muscle damage release CK into circulation, while in vitro myotubes also release CK throughout development and following damage12–14. Interestingly, in this investigation, the introduction of myoblasts in transwell inserts significantly reduced CK activity in the conditioned media. This suggests that factors secreted by myoblasts may acutely improve myofibrillar integrity in response to EPS, independent of desmin and β-dystrogylcan protein content. Calcium ionophore-induced myotube damage has been shown to increase CK release into cell culture media, which highlights a direct correlation between intracellular calcium concentrations and membrane permeability14. As such, calcium-dependent responses to damage may be influenced by EPS and become targets for myoblast-associated paracrine signalling.

Muscle contraction is reliant on calcium influx and efflux from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to the cytosol. Contraction-induced damage can often lead to excessive cytosolic calcium concentrations. This increase in cytosolic calcium activates calpain 3, a calcium-dependent protease, which plays a critical role in sarcomeric degradation in EIMD and its increased activation has been associated with atrophy in conditions of disuse and denervation30,31. Its protease activity acts to degrade sarcomeric proteins such as titin and desmin under conditions of remodelling and regeneration27,32,33. Calpain 3 has also been implicated in several repair-associated processes such as the dysferlin membrane repair complex where it is responsible for degrading the cytoskeleton at sites of damage to allow the fusion of intracellular vesicles to induce patch repair34,35. In this investigation, calpain 3 gene expression did change over time and with myoblast exposure, however protein content did not. Calpain 3 activity is intricately regulated, as it is thought that dissociation from titin within the sarcomere is required to expose the IS1 domain of calpain 3, allowing for autolysis and activation36,37. As such, calpain 3 transcription may be regulated by myoblast exposure in response to EPS damage; however, the lack of protein content changes may be due to the limitations of available antibodies that target al.l isoforms of the peptide and not exclusively the active, proteolyzed state.

The observed increases in BNIP3 protein expression in comparison to MBL– counterparts provided further evidence for increased catabolism in response to MBL exposure. The mitophagy regulator BNIP3 acts both in a pro-apoptotic and pro-survival fashion, where it permeabilizes the outer mitochondrial membrane to promote its degradation. This is crucial for the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, which is commonly seen in conditions such as advanced aging and metabolic disease38. Interestingly, whole-body knockdown of BNIP3 has been shown to not only influence mitophagy, but also the dysregulation of pathways involved in membrane and cytoskeletal integrity38. Further, BNIP3 has been shown to be activated as a consequence of AKT-mTOR pathway inhibition in disease conditions of muscle atrophy, including late-onset Pompe disease and its overexpression linked to myotube atrophy in vitro39. Interestingly, myoblasts have been documented to release myostatin which is implicated in muscle atrophy and proteolysis by directly inhibiting this same pathway40,41. We also observed increased ULK1 protein content in response to MBL exposure, a typical response in similar catabolic conditions and exercise to promote autophagic signalling42. Interestingly, this aligns with proteomic data from the secretome of myotubes treated with compounds that upregulate exercise-associated pathways6. However, despite the influence of both catabolic and anabolic processes on muscle size, neither the master regulator of muscle protein synthesis, mTOR, nor its inhibition of the translation initiation inhibitor 4EBP1 responded to this EPS protocol or myoblast exposure. These findings suggest that myoblasts are involved in communicating pro-catabolic signalling to differentiated myotubes, particularly targeting mitophagic processes.

In contrast, the increase in HSP70 gene expression was altered in response to EPS and MBL exposure. HSP70, a chaperone protein that tightly regulates protein folding and unfolding, is highly reactive to conditions of increased reactive oxygen species, heat, and changes to pH, all of which are consequences of both exercise and damage21,43. Models of disuse atrophy have implicated HSP70 in inhibiting FOXO3a, thereby reducing the expression of ubiquitin ligases that are responsible for targeting proteins for degradation44. However, the exact relationship between HSP70 and these proteins is unclear. HSP70 has also been implicated in stabilizing lysosomal membranes during autophagy in non-muscle tissue and has been classified as a cachexin, indicating a role in regulating catabolism as well44,45. Further, it has been implicated in mTORC1 inhibition through Rheb targeting for degradation22. As such, the role of HSP70 in skeletal muscle mass regulation is complex and requires further assessment. Lastly, a reduction in average myotube diameter, a higher frequency of smaller myotubes but no observable changes in MHCI surface area when cultures were exposed to MBL may indicate delays in development or increases in catabolism that may not be observable within larger, more developed myotubes. The data presented herein are suggestive of an increase in catabolic processes with MBL exposure post-EPS. While these secreted paracrine factors may be directly involved in catabolic signalling, it is also possible that they instead trigger alternative molecular pathways that ultimately promote catabolism.

While the purpose of this work was to model physiologically relevant muscle damage, the ability of EPS to properly mimic different in vivo conditions, including aerobic and resistance exercise, has been widely debated46. Orfanos et al., (2016) demonstrated structural adaptations to myotubes in response to the EPS protocol used within this investigation, however our molecular and morphological data do not compare11,26. This may be due to limitations with the study design; the substitution of differentiation media to growth media was necessary to ensure that transwell-plated myoblasts did not differentiate, however this may also have influenced myotube development. Furthermore, the presence of undifferentiated myoblasts within the myotube population may conceal a more significant influence of exogenous myoblasts. Future work could benefit from utilizing pharmacological interventions to produce more severe myotube damage, such as mitochondrial uncouplers and calcium ionophores to help characterize these changes in supraphysiological conditions. However, limitations include the targeting of specific molecular pathways to induce damage as opposed to biomechanical influences, thereby failing to recapitulate EIMD.

Previous work has identified a significant impact of myogenic progenitor cell signalling on fibrogenic cell function and extracellular matrix-associated protein regulation. While those findings are crucial to understanding the regulatory signalling in the maintenance of the skeletal muscle environment, none have focused solely on the interactions between muscle progenitor cells and skeletal muscle tissue itself during contraction-mediated damage. Alongside further characterizing the impact of paracrine factors in the muscle microenvironment, the present study provides novel insights into the biological processes influenced by the myogenic progenitor cell secretome. This work has identified a potential early myoblast response mechanism to muscle damage, in which they may facilitate the breakdown of cellular components to promote subsequent hypertrophy and fusion-mediated regeneration.

Methods

General cell culture

C2C12 myoblasts (American Type Culture Collection, Cedarlane) were cultured in growth medium (GM) containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) High Glucose (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 11965092) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Invitrogen; 12483020), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen; 15140122). Cells were allowed to adhere and grow to 60% confluency within a 37˚C incubator at 5% CO2 prior to passaging. Following a 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) wash, cells were trypsinized (Invitrogen; 15090046) for 3 min at 37˚C, then neutralized with one volume GM and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min at 20˚C. The supernatant was then replaced with fresh GM and cells were plated at 200,000 cells/well in 6-well plates. For myoblast differentiation, cultures were allowed to grow to 100% confluency in GM, after which the media was changed to differentiation medium (DM) composed of DMEM supplemented with 2% Horse Serum (Invitrogen; 16050122) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. DM was changed every 48 h for 6 days prior to experimentation.

Transwell and electrical pulse stimulation experiments

Myoblasts were plated at 20,000 cells per transwell insert sized to fit 6-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 140660) and incubated with GM for twelve hours prior to day 6 of myotube differentiation. On the day of experimentation, EPS was administered to the differentiated myotubes with the C-Pace EM (IonOptix) at 23 V with 15 Hz pulse for 5 s and 5 Hz pulse for 5 s, separated by a 5 s break as previously described to induce myotube damage11. After 1 h of EPS, electrodes were removed and the transwell inserts were immediately added for 1, 3, 8, 12, or 24 h to allow for intercellular signalling without fusion. The 0-hr time point was collected immediately following the EPS protocol. Control groups underwent the same intervention either without stimulation and/or using transwell inserts only containing GM and no myoblasts. This study design ensures myotubes are harvested after a differentiation period of no less than 6 days (0-hr time point) and no more than 7 days (24-hr time point).

Immunofluorescence and analysis

Coverslips were adhered to the base of the 6-well plates prior to myoblast plating and differentiation. Myotubes were washed 3 times with 1x PBS prior to a 10-minute fixation period with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich; P6148-500G). Myotubes were again washed with 1x PBS and permeabilized for 15 min with 1% BSA (BioShop Canada; ALB007.500), 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich; X100-1 L) in 1x PBS. The permeabilization solution was removed and coverslips were washed for 3 × 5 min with 1x PBS prior to a 1-hour block in 1x PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (1xPBST; Thermo Fisher Scientific; BP337500) and 5% goat serum (Sigma-Aldrich; G9023-10ML). Myotubes were incubated overnight at 4˚C with the primary antibody (Table 1), diluted appropriately in a 1% BSA in 1x PBST solution. The next day, myotubes were washed in 1X PBS and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibody (Table 1). 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:50,000) was applied prior to mounting slides using fluorescent mounting medium (Dako; S3023). Images were captured using the Nikon Eclipse Ti2 at 10x magnification. Nikon Imaging Software thresholding analysis was used to determine myotubular and myonuclear-specific calpain 3 expression and myosin heavy chain type I (MHCI) positive myotubes. For each sample, the diameter of ~ 300 myotubes was evaluated by averaging the length of 5 lines drawn across the width of each myotube. Bifurcations were considered new myotubes. MHCI thresholding was used to identify myotube surface area and MHCI cells. Fusion index was assessed as the number nuclei within fused myotubes as a fraction of the total number of nuclei. Differentiation index was assessed as the number of MHCI + myonuclei as a fraction of the total number of nuclei.

Protein isolation and Western blotting

Myotubes were quickly washed with ice-cold 1x PBS. RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich; R0278-500ML) containing phosphatase (Sigma-Aldrich; P5726-1ML) and protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A32965) was added to myotubes and immediately flash frozen in a liquid nitrogen bath prior to storage at −80 °C to allow for the processing of all samples together. After thawing on ice, samples were sonicated, and then further homogenization using the FastPrep-24 (MP Biomedicals). Samples were then centrifuged at 14,000 x g for 15 min and the supernatant was transferred to new 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes for protein quantification using a standard bicinchoninic protein (BCA; Thermo Scientific cat: 23225). Protein lysates were diluted to 1 µg/µl and 10 µg of each sample was loaded into individual lanes of a 4–15% Criterion TGX precast protein gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories; 5678085). Following gel electrophoresis, proteins were transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane using a Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Stain-free membranes were then imaged for band visualization. Membranes were then washed 3x times with 1x tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and placed in a blocking solution (5% BSA in 1x TBST) for 1 h prior to overnight incubation at 4 °C with the relevant primary antibody (Table 1). Blots were washed in 1x TBST prior to a 2-hour incubation at room temperature in the appropriate diluted secondary antibody. Luminol-based ECL reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories; 1705062) was applied for band visualization with the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. All images were quantified using Image Lab software, version 6.1.0 (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Bands were normalized to total protein obtained from stain-free images.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR

After the EPS and unstimulated protocols were completed, myotubes were washed with ice cold 1x PBS thrice for 5 min each. Samples were collected and processed as suggested by the E.Z.N.A. Total RNA Kit I (VWR; R6834-02). Isolated RNA was stored at −80 °C until further processing. RNA content was quantified using the Nanodrop Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher; ND2000CLAPTOP). For cDNA synthesis, High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 4368814) was used according to manufacturer’s instructions. PCR reactions using Taqman assay probes were performed with Taqman Master Mix (Fisher Scientific; 44–445-57) and the relevant FAM assay mix. PCR reactions using primers were performed with GoTaq Master Mix (Promega; A6002) and 5µM of forward and reverse primers. All assays and primers used are listed in Table 2.

Creatine kinase assay

Conditioned media collected from the transwell experiments were centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min to pellet cellular debris, after which the supernatant was transferred to ne microcentrifuge tubes and stored at −80 °C. Creatine kinase activity in the cell culture media was assessed using the colorimetric Creatine Kinase Activity Assay Kit (Abcam; ab155091) as per manufacturer instructions47.

Transmission electron microscopy

Myoblasts were plated and differentiated as previously described under ‘Transwell Experiments’. After the EPS and unstimulated protocols were completed, myotubes were washed with 1x PBS thrice for 5 min each. The monolayers were then fixed in 2% Glutaraldehyde (2% v/v) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer pH 7.4 and processed at the Canadian Centre for Electron Microscopy (CCEM) at McMaster University. Briefly, the samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 1 h and then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Final dehydration was done in two changes of 100% propylene oxide. The samples were slowly infiltrated with Spurr’s resin through a graded series with rotation of the samples in between solution changes. The tissue samples were transferred to embedding moulds filled with fresh 100% Spurr’s resin and polymerized overnight in a 60 C oven. Thin sections were cut on a Leica UCT ultramicrotome and picked up onto Cu grids. The sections on the grids were post-stained with Uranyless and Lead Citrate stains (Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA) and then viewed in a JEOL JEM 1200 EX TEMSCAN transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Peabody, MA, USA). Images were acquired with an AMT 4-megapixel digital camera (Advanced Microscopy Techniques, Woburn, MA).

Statistics

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the main effects of time and MBL exposure. When applicable, a Tukey post hoc test was employed to determine group specific differences. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare changes between MBL + and MBL– groups, when appropriate. All results are expressed as the mean ± SEM. p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
