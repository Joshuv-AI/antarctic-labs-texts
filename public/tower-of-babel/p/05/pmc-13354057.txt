Gender disparities in psychological distress and sexual dysfunction among infertile couples: a cross-sectional study in Bangladesh
Authors: Mohammad Shamsul Ahsan, Ahsan Aziz Sarkar, Andrea Sansone, Samina Akter, Md Al-Amin Khan, Leuza Mubassara, Rezwan Karim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose To compare rates of depression, anxiety, stress, and sexual dysfunction between men and women with infertility.Methods This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Departments of Reproductive Endocrinology of two tertiary hospitals in Bangladesh. Participants were randomly selected following a confirmed diagnosis of infertility by consultants. The Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale-21, International Index of Erectile Function, Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool, and Female Sexual Function Index-6 were used for assessment.Results A total of 658 participants (320 men and 338 women) were included. The mean ages of men and women participants were 33.77 ± 6.55 and 28.57 ± 5.34 years, respectively. Infertile women reported significantly higher severity and proportions of depression (67.2% vs. 31.9%), anxiety (75.1% vs. 32.8%), and stress (69.2% vs. 37.8%) compared with infertile men (P < .001 for all). Conversely, sexual dysfunction was more prevalent among infertile men (37.5%) than women (13.9%, P < .001). Multivariate analysis revealed that, among men, sexual dysfunction was a significant predictor of depression, anxiety, and stress, whereas among women, longer duration of infertility treatment was independently associated with these psychological distresses.Discussion These findings highlight the gendered psychosocial impact of infertility within a sociocultural context where parenthood is highly valued. Routine screening and integrated management of mental health and sexual dysfunction may improve overall patient well-being and treatment outcomes. However, the findings should be interpreted considering the cross-sectional and hospital-based design, which limits causal inference and generalizability.

Introduction

Infertility is a profound reproductive and sexual health challenge, increasingly recognized for its multifaceted impact on psychological well-being and sexual function. Globally, it affects about 1.8% of men and 3.7% of women, with the highest rates reported in East and South Asia and Eastern Europe.1 In Bangladesh, a South Asian country and the eighth most populous nation in the world, infertility affects approximately 4% of the population. This burden is compounded by high rates of sexually transmitted and reproductive tract infections, unsafe abortions, obstetric complications, and suboptimal health service systems.2 Beyond its biomedical dimensions, infertility represents a deeply distressing psychosocial experience that challenges personal identity, marital stability, and social belonging.

In many cultures, including those of South Asia, a pronatalist culture is central to social identity and familial continuity. Parenthood is viewed as a marker of success and social worth, and children are perceived as symbols of accomplishment, economic security, and support in old age.3 Consequently, individuals and couples facing infertility often experience intense social pressure and stigma that evoke feelings of shame, guilt, and inadequacy.4 Such pressures not only heighten psychological distress but also contribute to marital discord, family conflict, and, in some cases, divorce or second marriage.5 As fertility rates decline even in developing countries, the psychosocial consequences of infertility are becoming a growing public health concern.

The emotional burden of infertility frequently manifests as depression, anxiety, and stress. This distress is not merely a reaction to social stigma but may also interfere with biological processes that affect fertility outcomes through hormonal dysregulation, impaired semen parameters, and ovulatory dysfunction.4,6 The complex relation between infertility and sexual health is inherently bidirectional. While infertility-related distress can precipitate sexual dysfunction, pre-existing sexual disorders such as erectile dysfunction (ED) or premature ejaculation (PE) may also hinder conception. Furthermore, the medicalization of intimacy often required during infertility treatments through scheduled intercourse can transform sexual activity from a source of pleasure into a stressful, goal-oriented task, thereby exacerbating sexual morbidity.7,8

Despite this, most research has remained compartmentalized, focusing either on psychological distress or sexual health, often through a single-gender lens. Comparative evidence that integrates these domains, particularly in the socio-cultural context of lower-middle-income countries like Bangladesh, remains sparse. Against this background, the present study was designed to compare the prevalence and severity of depression, anxiety, stress, and sexual dysfunction between infertile men and women in a hospital-based sample, and to identify the sociodemographic, clinical, and reproductive factors associated with these outcomes. This study hypothesized that infertile women would report higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress than infertile men, whereas sexual dysfunction would be more prevalent among infertile men. The present study was guided by the following research questions: Are there significant differences in the rates of depression, anxiety, stress, and sexual dysfunction between infertile men and infertile women? What demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics are associated with depression, anxiety, stress, and sexual dysfunction among infertile men and infertile women separately? By integrating psychological and sexual dimensions within a single analytical framework, this study seeks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of infertility’s impact and to inform gender-sensitive approaches to its management.

Methods

Study design and setting

This hospital-based, cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2024 and June 2025 at the Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility departments of two tertiary-level referral hospitals in Bangladesh - Bangladesh Medical University (BMU) and Bangladesh Institute of Research and Rehabilitation in Diabetes, Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders (BIRDEM).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received Institutional Review Board approval prior to participant recruitment and data collection. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. Given the sensitive nature of sexual health data, interviews were conducted in private consulting rooms to ensure confidentiality and minimize social desirability bias. All data were anonymized using unique identification codes.

Sample size and sampling strategy

The sample size was determined using a single-population proportion formula. Assuming a 50% prevalence of sexual dysfunction, a 99% confidence level (𝑍 = 2.576), and a 5% margin of error, the required sample size was 664. A systematic random sampling technique was employed. With an estimated clinic flow of 9600 patients, a sampling interval of 𝑘 = 14 was established. If the selected individual was unavailable, the immediate next eligible patient was approached. A final cohort of 658 participants (320 men and 338 women) was successfully recruited.

Participants

Infertility was confirmed by consultants based on standard clinical criteria. Eligible participants were aged ≥18 years. To avoid data clustering, only one partner from a couple was enrolled. Individuals with a documented history of psychiatric disorders or sexual dysfunction prior to their infertility diagnosis were excluded to isolate the impact of the infertility experience itself.

Measures

A semi-structured questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic, reproductive, and clinical information. Psychological distress was assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21), and sexual function was measured using gender-specific validated instruments: the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-6) and Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT) for men, and the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-6) for women. All instruments used were Bangla-validated versions.

DASS-21: The DASS-21 is a widely used self-report instrument designed to measure symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress.9 It consists of 21 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0-3), with higher scores indicating greater severity. The Bangla-validated version demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.85 for each subscale) and an acceptable three-factor model fit.10

PEDT: The PEDT is a five-item self-report tool that assesses ejaculatory control, frequency of PE episodes, stimulation threshold, distress, and interpersonal difficulty.11 Scores ≤8 indicate no PE, 9-10 suggest probable PE, and ≥ 11 indicate definite PE. The Bangla-validated version retained the original single-factor structure and demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.827).12

IIEF-6: The IIEF-6 is a six-item version of the IIEF that specifically measures erectile function, the core domain of men sexual health.13 Each item is rated from 0 to 5, with total scores ranging from 1 to 30. The severity classification is as follows: no ED (26-30), mild (22-25), mild to moderate (17-21), moderate (11-16), and severe (6-10). The Bangla version, validated by Parvez et al. demonstrated high reliability.14

FSFI-6: The FSFI-6 is a brief, six-item version of the FSFI-19 used to screen female sexual dysfunction across desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain domains.15 Items are rated on a 0-5 scale, with lower total scores indicating greater dysfunction. The Bangla version showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.887) and high diagnostic accuracy, with a cutoff score of 19 yielding a sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 100%.16

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were computed to summarize the sociodemographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics of infertile males and females. Prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress was determined using established DASS-21 severity cut-offs, with caseness defined as scores above the normal range (ie, ≥10 for depression, ≥8 for anxiety, and ≥ 15 for stress). For regression analyses, DASS-21 subscale scores were treated as continuous variables to preserve variability and statistical power. Group differences between infertile men and women were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables. To explore factors associated with psychological distress (depression, anxiety, and stress), univariate linear regression analyses were initially performed. Variables showing significant associations (P < .05) were subsequently included in multivariate linear regression models to identify independent predictors. Similarly, univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine factors related to sexual dysfunction followed by multivariate logistic regression to determine independent predictors. All analyses were stratified by sex and performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 658 patients with infertility (320 men and 338 women) were enrolled. The mean age of participants was 31.1 years (SD = 6.5, range 18-65), with men being older (M = 33.7, SD = 6.5, range 21-65) than women (M = 28.5, SD = 5.3, range 18-40). The mean age at marriage was 26.9 years (SD = 5.6) for men and 20.8 years (SD = 4.8) for women. The overall mean duration of marriage was 7.4 years (SD = 4.9), and the mean duration of infertility was 5.0 years (SD = 4.1) for men and 5.2 years (SD = 4.3) for women. The mean duration of infertility treatment was 3.2 years (SD = 4.1). Demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

A comparison of psychological distress and sexual dysfunction between infertile men and women revealed significant gender differences across all domains (Table 3). Depression, anxiety, and stress were significantly more prevalent among infertile women than men (67.2% vs. 31.9%; 75.1% vs. 32.8%; and 69.2% vs. 37.8%, respectively; all P < .001). In contrast, sexual dysfunction was more common among infertile men than women. Approximately 37.5% of men reported sexual dysfunction compared with only 13.9% of women (χ2 = 48.32, P < .001). Compared with secondary infertility, primary infertility was associated with significantly higher rates of depression (55.9% vs. 38.7%; P < .001), anxiety (50.3% vs. 36.0%; P < .001), and sexual dysfunction (28.9% vs. 18.7%; P = .004), while no significant difference was observed in stress category (47.6% vs. 43.1%; P = .276).

Multivariate regression analyses identified distinct psychosocial and clinical predictors of psychological distress and sexual dysfunction among infertile men and women (Table 4). Among men, higher partner educational attainment was associated with significantly lower depression (B = −1.64, P = .003) and stress (B = −0.89, P = .021) scores. The presence of sexual dysfunction strongly predicted greater depressive (B = −3.89, P < .001), anxiety (B = −1.41, P = .018), and stress symptoms (B = −2.90, P = .001) in men. In contrast, among women, longer duration of infertility treatment was consistently associated with increased depression (B = 0.42, P = .006), anxiety (B = 0.43, P = .027), and stress (B = 0.50, P = .008). Additionally, those with secondary infertility reported significantly lower stress levels compared with those with primary infertility (B = −2.94, P = .003).

Regarding sexual dysfunction, logistic regression revealed that in men, higher depressive symptoms independently predicted greater odds of sexual dysfunction (OR = 1.08, 95% CI = 1.03-1.14, P = <.001). Among women, a longer duration of marriage was a significant predictor of sexual dysfunction (OR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.01-1.12, P = .017). No other variables remained significant after adjustment.

Discussion

This study is among the few that directly compare psychological distress and sexual dysfunction between infertile men and women, highlighting both the high prevalence and gender-specific patterns of these challenges. Overall, infertile women exhibited significantly higher rates of depression, anxiety, and stress, whereas sexual dysfunction was more pronounced among men. Distinct psychosocial and clinical correlates were observed across genders. These findings align with a comparative study from Iran, which reported 82.5% of women exhibiting depressive symptoms, 92.5% anxiety, and 100% stress, compared with 72.5%, 77.5%, and 75% among infertile men, respectively.17 Similarly, a systematic review of 366 articles over the past decade confirmed that women consistently report higher levels of psychological distress than men in infertility contexts.18 In low- and middle-income countries such as Bangladesh, sociocultural norms strongly emphasize motherhood as a central role for women. Infertility can therefore lead to feelings of inadequacy, social stigma, and identity disruption, amplifying psychological distress. The prolonged and uncertain treatment process, repeated unsuccessful attempts, and social isolation disproportionately affect women, often resulting in internalized failure, heightened anxiety, and depressive symptoms.19

In contrast, sexual dysfunction was more prevalent among infertile men, reflecting both physiological and psychosocial mechanisms. ED and PE are common contributors to male infertility, as documented in a large Chinese study reporting 19% prevalence of PE and 18% ED among infertile men.20 In addition, underlying medical conditions such as low testosterone, varicocele, and genitourinary infections further exacerbate sexual dysfunction, which can directly impair fertility.21 ED and PE can co-exist in the same individual, and it is common for men to develop one condition as a consequence of the other. For instance, men with PE may attempt to reduce their arousal to delay ejaculation, which inadvertently increases their risk of developing ED. Conversely, men with ED might try to increase their arousal to sustain an erection, thereby raising their risk of developing PE.22 In women, sexual dysfunction prevalence was relatively low in this sample (13.9%), lower than rates reported in other hospital-based studies in Bangladesh (56%), possibly reflecting differences in population, infertility etiology, or reporting bias.23 Interestingly the frequency of sexual intercourse increased after the diagnosis of infertility among female infertile patients and their sexual dysfunction is less than other studies. Given their higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress compared with men, sexual activity for women often shifts from an act of intimacy to a means of achieving pregnancy—where love-making becomes baby-making. Similar patterns have also been reported in previous studies.8

Gender-specific predictors of psychological distress and sexual dysfunction were evident in this study. Among men, sexual dysfunction emerged as the strongest predictor of depression, anxiety, and stress. Sexual difficulties can threaten masculine identity, sexual self-esteem, and social roles, often causing substantial emotional distress.24 In women, longer duration of infertility treatment was consistently associated with higher depression, anxiety, and stress. Repeated cycles of hope and disappointment, prolonged treatment procedures, physical side effects, and disruptions in life plans may cumulatively heighten emotional exhaustion, anxiety about the future, and diminished self-esteem.25 Women with secondary infertility exhibited lower stress than those with primary infertility, suggesting that prior childbirth may buffer psychosocial strain. Other predictors highlight the role of social context in modulating distress. Among men, higher partner educational attainment was associated with lower depression and stress scores, likely reflecting enhanced emotional support, communication, and relational stability. A partner’s education may serve as a buffer against infertility-related distress, particularly given men’s vulnerability to relational and performance expectations. In contrast, women’s distress may be shaped by biological, social, and cultural pressures that are not necessarily mitigated by partner education alone.

Logistic regression analyses further revealed gender-specific determinants of sexual dysfunction. In men, higher depressive symptoms independently predicted sexual dysfunction, consistent with evidence that male sexual function is closely intertwined with psychological well-being.26 For women, the duration of infertility treatment was the primary predictor, indicating that repeated cycles of hope and disappointment, prolonged treatment procedures, physical side effects, and disruptions in life plans may cumulatively heighten emotional exhaustion, anxiety about the future, and diminished self-esteem.25

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the large sample size and the use of gender-specific, validated instruments to compare psychological and sexual health within the same sociocultural framework. However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, causal inferences cannot be made, and the observed relationships should be interpreted as associations only rather than cause–effect relationships. The reliance on self-reported measures in a conservative society may introduce reporting bias. Data on relevant medical comorbidities (eg, diabetes, hypertension) and medication use (eg, antidepressants), which may influence sexual function, were not collected in this study; therefore, we were unable to adjust for these potential confounders or perform sensitivity analyses, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Additionally, data regarding the specific etiology of infertility (male factor, female factor, or combined causes) were not collected, which limited the ability to perform etiological stratification and may restrict the clinical interpretability of the findings. As a hospital-based study in tertiary centers, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the rural infertile population. Although validated gender-specific instruments were used, the operational definitions of sexual dysfunction were not fully equivalent between men and women. Male sexual dysfunction was assessed primarily through ED and PE domains (IIEF-6 and PEDT), while the FSFI-6 captured a broader multidimensional construct in women. Other relevant male sexual health domains, such as hypoactive sexual desire disorder and delayed ejaculation, were not assessed. Therefore, comparisons between genders should be interpreted as not strict equivalent measures of sexual dysfunction.

Conclusion

Infertility in Bangladesh is associated with a substantial psychological burden, manifesting differently across genders. Women face higher psychological morbidity driven by treatment duration, while men struggle with sexual dysfunction that directly impacts their mental well-being. These findings underscore the need for a multidisciplinary approach in infertility clinics. Integrating routine mental health screening and sexual medicine into standard care is essential to move beyond purely biomedical outcomes and improve the overall quality of life for infertile couples.
