Retrospective analysis of healing outcomes and associated factors in patients with pressure ulcers
Authors: Jong-Ho Kim, Jin-Woo Kim, Minju Ha, Kyoung Min Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pressure ulcers (PUs) are serious complications among immobilized or chronically ill patients, often resulting in prolonged hospitalization, morbidity, and increased healthcare costs. Despite therapeutic advances, predictors of long-term healing remain unclear. This study aimed to identify factors associated with 1-year healing outcomes in patients with advanced PUs. This retrospective cohort study included adult patients diagnosed with PUs of grade 2 or higher between January 2020 and January 2025. Clinical, laboratory, and wound-related variables were obtained from electronic records, including demographic data, comorbidities, nutritional indicators, ulcer features (stage, location, size, infection, and bone exposure), and treatment modalities such as negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) and surgical debridement. The primary outcome measure was complete wound healing within 12 months. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified independent predictors of healing. A total of 240 patients (mean age, 61.5 ± 10.6 years; 69.6% male) were included in the final analysis. Complete healing at 12 months was achieved in 48.8% of the patients. In the multivariate logistic regression model, NPWT, hospital-based treatment, lower initial ulcer stage, and absence of paralysis were independently associated with complete healing, with all predictors reaching statistical significance (all P < .01). NPWT, hospital-based management, lower initial ulcer stage, and absence of paralysis were strongly associated with improved 1-year healing outcomes in patients with advanced PUs. These findings highlight the importance of early, structured wound care and suggest that both the treatment setting and patient mobility status play critical roles in long-term recovery.

1. Introduction

Pressure ulcers (PUs), also known as pressure injuries or decubitus ulcers, are localized areas of tissue damage that develop when sustained pressure, shear, or friction impairs blood flow to the skin and underlying tissues.[1] They are common and serious complications among immobilized or chronically ill patients, particularly those with neurological impairment, malnutrition, or prolonged hospitalization.[2,3] The prevalence of PUs differs widely between countries and healthcare systems, reflecting variations in patient populations, clinical practices, and reporting standards, and contributes substantially to patient suffering, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs.[4] In severe cases, they can lead to systemic infection, sepsis, or even mortality, making them a major indicator of healthcare quality and patient safety.[5,6]

Over the past decade, various preventive and therapeutic strategies have been developed, including pressure-relieving surfaces, nutritional support, advanced wound dressings, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), and surgical reconstruction.[7,8] Despite these advances, the long-term healing outcomes of PUs remain unsatisfactory. Healing is influenced by a complex interplay of systemic and local factors, such as nutritional status, infection, tissue perfusion, and the patient’s overall condition.[1,2,9]

Because most PUs are considered preventable, the management of risk factors has been the primary focus of many previous studies.[3,10] Although prevention remains the most cost-effective strategy from a healthcare perspective, the treatment of patients who have already developed ulcers is equally important. However, few studies have comprehensively analyzed the long-term healing process or identified prognostic factors that influence recovery after ulcer onset.[11,12] Furthermore, most existing reports have been limited to selected patient populations, making it difficult to generalize their findings to broader clinical settings.[11] Investigating factors that promote healing or hinder recovery after ulcer formation is essential for improving clinical outcomes and reducing the unavoidable social and economic burden associated with PUs. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate 1-year healing outcomes and identify independent factors associated with complete recovery in patients with grade 2 or higher PUs.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and patient selection

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort analysis conducted at the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. The medical records of adult patients diagnosed with PUs of grade 2 or higher between January 2020 and January 2025 were reviewed. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B-2512-1013-102), and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years or older and had been clinically diagnosed with a PU of Grade 2 or higher, according to the classification system of the National Pressure–Ulcer Advisory Panel. Patients were excluded if their medical records were incomplete, if they had <12 months of follow-up data, if they died or were transferred before the 1-year evaluation, or if the ulcer was caused by non-pressure-related etiologies such as vascular, neuropathic, or malignant wounds. Because this study was retrospective in nature, a prior sample size or power calculation was not feasible. Instead, the study cohort consisted of all eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria and had available clinical and photographic records in the electronic medical records during the predefined study period. The patient selection process is summarized in Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R734.

2.2. Data collection and variables

Demographic and clinical variables collected included age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease), initial pressure–ulcer stage, initial ulcer size (cm2), infection status, dressing type, surgical debridement, hemoglobin level, albumin level, C-reactive protein level, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. Infection status was defined as the presence of a clinically diagnosed wound infection requiring systemic antibiotic therapy, based on the treating physician’s assessment and documented medical records. Paralysis data were also collected, including paraplegia and tetraplegia, which were categorized collectively as paralysis. Treatment-related variables included ulcer location, treatment location (hospital vs. home), bone exposure, and osteomyelitis status. Hospital-based treatment was defined as having received wound management during inpatient hospitalization. The primary outcome was complete wound healing within 12 months, defined as full epithelialization without a residual open area. Wounds that did not achieve full epithelialization were classified as incomplete healing. Healing outcomes were assessed based on clinical records and photographic documentation when available. Clinical evaluations were based on physical examination and review of wound photographs documented in the electronic medical records. Although standardized photography protocols were not uniformly applied, wound healing was routinely assessed by treating physicians during scheduled outpatient visits or inpatient care. This study was conducted using a complete-case analysis approach. As the analyzed variables were routinely collected during scheduled follow-up visits, the proportion of patients excluded due to missing data was minimal, and no imputation was performed.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk). For univariate analysis, categorical variables were compared between the complete-healing and non-healing groups using the chi-square test. For continuous variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were analyzed using the independent-samples t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. All tests were 2-tailed, and a P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes for categorical variables were reported as odds ratios (ORs) or relative risks, as appropriate. For the multivariate analysis, a logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of complete healing at 1 year. Variables that demonstrated P < .1 in the univariate analysis were entered as candidate predictors for the multivariate model. The dependent variable was 1-year complete healing (binary: healed = 1, not healed = 0). Categorical predictors were coded as binary indicators (1 for presence or favorable condition and 0 for absence or unfavorable condition). For each independent variable, ORs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

To address potential confounding, multivariate logistic regression was performed including clinically relevant covariates identified in the univariate analysis. Advanced causal inference methods, such as propensity score matching, were considered but not applied due to concerns about overfitting and reduced statistical power given the limited number of outcome events. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

3. Results

In total 240 patients were included in the final analysis. A detailed flow diagram of patient screening and inclusion is presented Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R734. Among them, 117 patients (48.8%) achieved complete healing within 1 year, whereas 123 patients (51.2%) did not. The mean age of the study population was 61.5 ± 10.6 years (range, 32–93 years), and the cohort consisted of 167 males (69.6%) and 73 females (30.4%). The distribution of the initial ulcer stages was as follows: stage 2 (n = 62, 25.8%), stage 3 (n = 75, 31.3%), and stage 4 (n = 103, 42.9%). All 4 variables that were significant in the univariate analysis were statistically significant predictors of complete healing in the multivariate logistic regression model (Table 3). NPWT was the strongest independent predictor, with an adjusted OR of 8.005 (95% CI: 3.321–19.298; p = P < .01). Hospital-based treatment was also associated with higher odds of healing (adjusted OR: 4.457; 95% CI: 2.367–8.392; P < .01). A higher initial ulcer stage was inversely associated with healing (adjusted OR: 0.378 per stage increase; 95% CI: 0.252–0.567; P < .01). Finally, absence of paralysis remained a significant positive predictor of healing (adjusted OR: 2.684; 95% CI: 1.442–4.997; P < .01). Overall, these findings indicate that the use of NPWT, treatment in a hospital setting, lower initial ulcer stage, and absence of paralysis significantly increased the likelihood of achieving complete healing within 1 year.

4. Discussion

This study retrospectively analyzed 1-year healing outcomes and associated clinical factors in 240 patients with grade 2 or higher PUs treated at a tertiary hospital. The overall complete-healing rate was 48.8%, indicating that long-term recovery remains suboptimal despite the use of modern wound-care techniques. This study identified 4 independent predictors of complete healing within 1 year; hospital-based treatment, lower initial ulcer stage, absence of paralysis, and use of NPWT. These findings highlight the substantial impact of both treatment and patient-related factors on long-term wound recovery and provide clinically meaningful insights for the management of advanced PUs.

The beneficial effect of facility-based care is consistent with previous reports demonstrating improved healing in patients treated in specialized nursing or rehabilitation centers.[13] Continuous professional wound assessment, appropriate pressure off-loading, nutritional support, and adherence to standardized dressing protocols may contribute to superior outcomes compared with home-based management.[14,15] In contrast, patients cared for at home often experience irregular dressing changes, suboptimal nutrition, and limited monitoring of infection or deterioration, all of which can delay granulation and epithelialization. The present findings reinforce the importance of structured wound-care environments and suggest that transferring patients with advanced ulcers to facility-based programs may improve long-term healing. However, hospital-based treatment is not always feasible or appropriate for every patient, because financial constraints or environmental circumstances may limit access to facility care. Although not fully captured in the present dataset, less severe PUs can often be effectively managed at home when caregivers receive adequate education and support.[16,17] Moreover, although this could not be fully documented due to limitations in the medical records, many patients who received home-based care with regular visits from specialized wound-care nurses achieved excellent healing outcomes. While structured hospital-based management demonstrated significantly superior healing in this study, the large number of patients who require home-based care highlights the need for improved caregiver education and stronger institutional support to optimize wound outcomes.

Initial ulcer stage demonstrated an inverse relationship with healing. Patients with higher-stage ulcers had significantly lower odds of achieving complete closure, consistent with prior studies reporting poor outcomes in stage 4 ulcers due to deep tissue destruction, bone involvement, and impaired local perfusion.[18,19] In our study, although the initial ulcer size was measured for all patients, ulcer size itself was not a significant predictor of healing. This suggests that, in PUs, depth and structural involvement – particularly exposure of bone or muscle with compromised tissue viability – are more critical determinants of healing potential than the surface area alone. These findings underscore the importance of early detection and aggressive intervention to prevent ulcers from progressing to deeper and more complex stages that significantly reduce the likelihood of complete recovery.

The finding that paralysis independently predicted poor healing underscores the critical role of neurological impairment and immobility in ulcer persistence.[2,20,21] In this study, paraplegia and tetraplegia were analyzed collectively as “paralysis,” which may not fully reflect differences in severity among neurologically impaired patients. Nevertheless, paralysis itself clearly exerts a detrimental effect on wound recovery. Unlike other patients who retain some degree of voluntary movement and can occasionally off-load pressure, individuals with paralysis lack the ability to reposition themselves, leading to prolonged pressure, tissue ischemia, and delayed healing.[21] Patients with spinal cord injury or severe neurological deficits also experience reduced muscle tone, impaired blood flow, sensory loss, and higher risk of contamination due to incontinence, all of which further hinder recovery.[2] These findings highlight the importance of specialized preventive strategies – including frequent turning schedules, advanced support surfaces, and close nursing supervision – for this high-risk population. In the long term, technologies such as biofeedback-based pressure-relief alarms or assistive devices that facilitate automatic or supported repositioning may help reduce the substantial social and healthcare burden associated with PUs in patients with paralysis.[22,23]

The positive association between the use of NPWT and healing aligns with extensive evidence supporting its ability to promote granulation tissue formation, remove exudate, and reduce bacterial load.[24,25] Several meta-analyses have demonstrated that NPWT shortens healing time and lowers recurrence rates in chronic wounds.[26-28] At our institution, NPWT is actively employed in patients who are deemed suitable for its use – typically those who can be adequately managed in a hospital setting or those receiving reliable home-based care without active infection. Because NPWT was applied selectively to patients considered likely to benefit, the possibility of treatment-selection bias cannot be excluded. However, even after accounting for these considerations, NPWT remained a significant predictor after adjusting for ulcer severity and infection, suggesting a meaningful independent therapeutic effect. Clinically, we have also observed that even some patients with advanced stage 4 PUs achieved complete healing within 6 months when NPWT was applied appropriately as part of their treatment regimen.[28] These findings supports the early use of NPWT, particularly for deep or exudative wounds, to optimize wound-bed preparation and enhance the likelihood of successful healing. Nevertheless, the observed association between NPWT and improved healing outcomes should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered evidence of a direct causal effect.

In this study, several variables traditionally regarded as important – such as nutritional indicators (albumin and hemoglobin), inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate), and comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease – were not identified as independent predictors of healing.[29,30] One possible explanation is that laboratory values were collected only at the initial assessment, without a longitudinal follow-up during the entire healing period. As nutritional and inflammatory parameters can fluctuate substantially over time, relying solely on baseline values may have limited the ability to detect their true impact on long-term wound recovery. Nonetheless, these findings may also reflect that although such systemic factors influence overall wound physiology, their direct effect on ulcer healing is less pronounced than that of more immediate, wound-specific determinants. Systematic pressure off-loading, structured repositioning schedules in hospital settings, and the use of NPWT in higher-stage ulcers likely exert stronger and more immediate effects on tissue recovery than baseline laboratory abnormalities alone.[9] With a larger sample size and longer observational periods, future studies may detect the significance of factors such as diabetes, advanced age, and hypoalbuminemia – variables that have been associated with delayed healing in previous research.[30,31] Continued prospective, long-term investigations are therefore necessary to further clarify the relative contributions of these systemic factors to pressure–ulcer healing.

Despite these findings, this study had several limitations. First, the retrospective study design introduced potential selection and information bias. Because the study population was defined retrospectively based on available clinical and photographic documentation, the sample size was determined by data availability rather than by pre-study statistical considerations. Therefore, the possibility of insufficient power to detect smaller effect sizes – particularly for variables that were not significant in the univariate analysis – cannot be excluded. Besides, patients referred to a tertiary hospital such as ours often represent more severe or treatment-resistant cases, which may result in a study population with a higher baseline severity than the general pressure–ulcer population. Furthermore, treatment decisions – including the application of NPWT – were made at the discretion of the treating physicians rather than randomization, introducing the possibility of confounding by indication. In particular, NPWT may have been preferentially applied to wounds perceived to have greater healing potential or to patients deemed more suitable for intensive wound care. Although multivariate adjustment was performed, the possibility of treatment-selection bias cannot be excluded. Moreover, because healing assessments rely partly on photographic documentation, variability in image quality and recording intervals may also have influenced the outcome classification. In addition, the frequency and methodology of wound assessments were not standardized across all patients, which may have introduced assessment bias in the evaluation of healing outcomes. Likewise, because the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, the institutional practice patterns and resource availability may limit the generalizability of these findings to other clinical settings. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized wound-assessment protocols and controlled treatment allocation are needed to validate these results and reduce potential confounding factors.

In conclusion, NPWT, hospital-based care, lower initial ulcer stage, and absence of paralysis were strong independent predictors of 1-year healing in advanced PUs. These findings highlight the importance of early, structured wound management and appropriate use of NPWT for deep or complex ulcers. Because many patients still require home-based care, improved caregiver education and expanded access to professional wound-care support are essential to optimize outcomes. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the role of systemic factors and to refine individualized treatment strategies for this challenging population.
