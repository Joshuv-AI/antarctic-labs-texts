Ballistic and drop-weight impact response of SS304 metal mesh embedded woven flax/hemp fiber hybrid composites
Authors: R. Elayaraja, G. Rajamurugan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research looks over the way hybrid natural fiber composites reinforced with woven SS304 wire mesh and flax-hemp yarns behave under low and high speed impacts. The hand lay-up approach was used to create hybrid laminates with alkali-treated flax fibers serving as the main reinforcement and epoxy resin serving as the matrix. The interlaminar strength and energy absorption capability of the stainless-steel mesh were enhanced by weaving it with hemp and flax strands. S1 (Flax/SS304/Flax) and S2 (Flax/SS304/Flax with woven hemp-flax yarns) are the two stacking sequences that were developed. Drop heights of 0.5 m and 1 m were used for low-velocity impact examinations, while a gas-gun apparatus was used for high-velocity ballistic evaluations. Based on the results, the woven hybrid structure (S2) outperformed the non-woven design (S1) in impact and ballistic performance, with energy absorption rates up to 25% higher and specific energy absorption rates 20% higher. The incorporation of SS304 mesh enhanced delamination resistance and localized load redistribution, while the utilization of hemp fibers permitted ductile deformation and delayed fracture development. Metal mesh and natural fibers worked together to improve crashworthiness and damage tolerance through a controlled, gradual failure mechanism. The results show that hybrid composites made of wire mesh and natural fibers can be a viable, lightweight alternative to traditional structural and ballistic protection materials.

Introduction

It is anticipated that the global market for armored vehicles will continue to expand at a faster rate until at least the year 2025. This market is characterized by strong growth. Further, there is a widespread requirement, all over the world, to improve the armored vehicles that are already in existence. More than two thousand automobiles in Greece have reached the end of their life cycle and are in need of being upgraded1. Due to the increasing calibre of projectiles (> 7.62 mm) and the growing need to reduce overall armour weight, steel has increasingly been regarded as a less preferred material for military vehicle armour applications2–5. These materials make it possible to provide the highest possible level of ballistic protection while simultaneously minimizing the amount of mass, weight, and production costs6,7. Furthermore, new numerical simulations and modeling techniques have been developed and confirmed with real ballistic experiments either for fiber composites armour systems or for laminated composites armour systems. These techniques have the potential to contribute to the reduction of the weight of protection systems as well as the costs associated with their ballistic tests8,9. It has been demonstrated that projectiles can be stopped by materials that are sufficiently thick regardless of whether they are single or combined10.

A lot of research has demonstrated that natural fiber reinforced polymers (NFRPs) made with flax fibers are stronger than those made with hemp fibers11–13. Flax fiber NFRPs, especially when they are not woven, have also shown promise for usage in structural applications in the building industry14,15. As expected, woven flax fiber NFRPs are stronger than hemp-based systems and work better than nonwoven ones16,17. Unidirectional (UD) flax composites work well when the load is applied in the same direction as the fibers. However, their efficiency compared to woven flax composites relies on the direction of the load. Flax fibers are great for unidirectional reinforcement because of their natural structure and strong tensile strength. Figure 1 shows that the overview illustrating the wide applicability and structural versatility of flax fibers used as reinforcement in composite materials18–20.

Hybrid composites, which mix natural and synthetic fibers, are made to promote sustainability while still providing good mechanical performance21. For this aim, glass and carbon fibers are typically mixed with natural fibers. To keep them biodegradable and recyclable, though, hybrids can also be made using different kinds of natural fibers or different varieties of the same fiber family. The ability of composites to withstand impacts is crucial due to their widespread use in applications that prioritize safety. Since the 1960 s, protective gear like body armor and military armor has made use of high-strength fibers that can endure more impact22. In the automotive and aerospace sectors, where composites are frequently subjected to impact damage, they play a crucial role. The exteriors of land and airborne high-speed vehicles frequently make use of composites. These buildings need to be shatterproof even when hit quite hard. The reliability of composites relies on their meticulous design, extensive testing, and inspection for damage processes. We learn a lot about the materials performance in use via high-velocity impact tests and numerical simulations23,24.

The crucial relevance of impact resistance and lightweight properties for effective protection has prompted the substantial study of ballistic materials for defense and military applications. Personal body armor is a common use for Kevlar fiber composites because of their high impact resistance and excellent strength-to-weight ratio25,26. A study conducted by Sarkar et al. examined the ballistic performance of hybrid composites composed of carbon and glass fibers27. Various carbon/glass hybrid constructions were tested with projectile velocities ranging from 400 to 500 m/s. Based on their findings, the hybrid composite that used the G-C-G-C-G stacking sequence had the best ballistic performance. Ballistic experiment on dry-stacked flax fibers by Delcourt et al. similarly28 shown that synthetic fibers with better mechanical properties have a wider range of uses than natural fibers like flax. However, combining natural and synthetic fibers is a more economical way to boost performance.

Many high-profile studies have investigated how natural fiber-reinforced polymer composites can be improved. For instance, the mechanical and impact performance of NFRP laminates can be significantly enhanced through biomimetic layup sequencing, stacking optimization, and hybridization strategies. Chew et al. demonstrated biomimicry inspired layup designs to improve mechanical response29. Liu et al. further confirmed that hybridization and layup design markedly enhance the impact resistance of NFRP laminates30. Alternatively, high-performance resin systems can be used to improve natural fiber-reinforced polymer composites. Thermoset setting polymers like phenolic resin are polycondensates. It is made by reacting phenols (e.g., phenol, cresol, or xylenol) with aldehydes (e.g., formaldehyde) in a controlled environment with acidic or basic catalysts and a certain temperature31. There is a wide range of applications for phenolic resins, from classic building materials to state-of-the-art aeronautical components. Their great chemical resistance to solvents, high dimensional stability, strong resistance to heat and flame, and exceptional mechanical strength are the reasons behind their popularity32–34. Epoxy resins are versatile, but their high stiffness, brittleness, and strong cross-linking make them a poor choice in several situations35. Particularly for parts of the structure that can easily crack, this is the case. In an effort to address these limitations, researchers have explored the prospect of enhancing the durability and adaptability of epoxy matrices by mixing them with bio-based phenolic resins36. The mechanical and thermal properties of composites are significantly impacted by the mix of different fibers and polymer matrices. A comprehensive review of the existing literature revealed no research that utilized a bio-phenolic/epoxy blended matrix technology to produce hybrid composites reinforced with flax fabric (FF) and carbon/kevlar (CK) fibers.

A new material design including flax fibers and stainless steel 304 wire mesh is presented in this research. Laminates were made using the hand lay-up technique with epoxy resin as the matrix, and the mesh was reinforced with hemp and flax threads to form a unique hybrid. This unique blend overcomes the drawbacks of conventional natural fiber composites by combining the renewable and lightweight qualities of natural fibers with the toughness and malleability of stainless steel. There is great promise for low-velocity impact and ballistic applications due to the hybrid system’s improved impact energy absorption, damage tolerance, and durability.

Material selection and methods

Selection of materials

This study examined the impact performance of hybrid composites with and without additional wire mesh reinforcement by incorporating SSWM between layers of flax fiber fabric (292 GSM). The SS304 wire mesh had a wire diameter of 0.45 mm, an aperture size of 2.5 mm × 2.5 mm. The flax and hemp yarns had linear densities of 210 tex and 180 tex37, respectively, with a twist level of 250–300 TPM. Their average tensile strengths were approximately 550 MPa (flax) and 420 MPa (hemp), as provided by the supplier. In a composite system, the matrix phase is essential for a number of reasons, including connecting the reinforcing fibers, protecting them from environmental deterioration, and improving the transverse mechanical performance of the laminate through uniform stress transfer across the fibers. Because of its affordability, extensive use, and great interfacial compatibility with natural fibers, epoxy resin was used as the matrix in this work. Go Green Products of Chennai supplied the epoxy resin (LY556), hardener (HY951), flax yarns, and hemp yarns used in the fabrication process. The chosen epoxy systems were chosen because of their compliance with the processing procedures and their shown suitability for natural fiber composites. Alkali treatment of natural fibers

An alkaline treatment using a NaOH solution was applied to all natural fibers in order to remove surface impurities and enhance interfacial interaction with the matrix. Woven plant fiber sheets were cut to 500 mm × 500 mm according to the specifications needed for the lamination. For 4 h at room temperature, these samples were submerged in a solution of 5% NaOH. Sodium hydroxide pellets from VIT, Vellore were used to make the solution, with a fabric-to-solution ratio of 1:2039,40. Once the specified soaking time had elapsed, the materials were extracted and washed in a distilled water rinse until the wash solution achieved a neutral pH, which can range from 6.8 to 7.2. The next step was to shade-dry the treated fibers for 1 day.

Fabrication of hybrid composite

The hybrid composite laminates were fabricated using the hand lay-up technique, a cost-effective and widely adopted method suitable for small-scale experimental studies due to its ability to control fiber placement, stacking sequence, and resin impregnation. Epoxy resin LY556 and hardener HY951 were used as the matrix system and mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Epoxy was selected over other thermosetting polymers because of its superior mechanical properties, dimensional stability, and minimal curing shrinkage. All reinforcement materials were prepared prior to fabrication to ensure good interfacial bonding and dimensional accuracy. The flax fibers were oven-dried at 60 °C for two hours to remove residual moisture and surface impurities. The SS304 woven wire mesh was incorporated as an intermediate reinforcement layer due to its high strength, ductility, and energy absorption capability. To enhance interlaminar strength and crack-arresting behavior, flax and hemp yarns were hand-woven onto the mesh using a simple weaving technique. Two laminate configurations were developed: S1 with a flax/SS304/flax stacking sequence, and S2 with additional interwoven flax–hemp yarns across the SS304 mesh within the same sequence (Fig. 2). These configurations were designed to examine the influence of yarn weaving on mechanical and ballistic performance. The laminates were fabricated on a 300 mm × 300 mm glass mold to achieve an overall thickness of 3 ± 0.5 mm. The mold surface was cleaned with acetone and coated with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a release agent. The epoxy mixture was applied using brushes and rollers to ensure complete impregnation and removal of entrapped air. Layers were assembled according to the prescribed stacking sequence, followed by the application of light roller pressure to improve consolidation and remove excess resin. The laminates were cured at room temperature (25–30 °C) for 24 h. After curing, the composite plates were demolded and cut to size using a diamond cutter. Visual inspection confirmed uniform surface quality with no significant voids, delamination, or resin-rich regions. Thickness variations remained within acceptable limits for hand lay-up processing, and specimen preparation followed the relevant standards: ASTM D7136 for low-velocity impact testing and ASTM D8101 for ballistic testing. To maintain consistent laminate quality, the fiber weight fraction method was employed throughout fabrication. All dry reinforcements were weighed prior to lay-up, and the epoxy content was regulated based on the target fiber to resin ratio. After curing, the fiber weight fraction was determined and converted to fiber volume fraction using the known densities of the constituent materials. The resulting laminates exhibited a consistent fiber volume fraction of 38–41%, confirming effective resin control, good consolidation, and reproducibility of the fabrication process.

Material characterization

Low velocity impact test

The low-velocity impact (LVI) test was conducted at the Vellore Institute of Technology in Chennai, Tamilnadu, India (Micro Mech Instrument, UNV-1) (Fig. 3a). Figure 3(b) shows the setup for the data acquisition system, with the major parts highlighted. Low-velocity impact testing was conducted in accordance with ASTM D7136. Sandwich composite specimens measuring 90 × 90 × 4 mm were tested using a 9.5 kg drop weight impactor. The indenter fell from heights of 0.5 m and 1 m. The LVI device can handle a drop height of up to 1.5 m, however the tests were limited to 1 m to protect both the equipment and the people who used it. The impactor was composed of high-carbide steel and was cylindrical in shape. It was 0.105 m tall and 0.013 m wide. The purpose of the LVI test was to see how well the weaving and non-weaving wire mesh structure absorbed energy when it was hit. Although the actual contact duration during low-velocity impact occurs within a few milliseconds, the instrumented drop-weight system records force and energy responses over an extended acquisition window to capture rebound behavior and post-impact oscillations. Accordingly, the time axis in the presented load-time and energy-time curves is shown in milliseconds as recorded by the data acquisition system. Figure 3(c) shows pictures of the test samples before the impact, and Figs. 4 and 5 shows the impact (front) face and impact (back) face weaving and non-weaving wire mesh construction specimens.

Results and discussion

Low velocity impact test

Composite laminates ability to withstand abrupt dynamic loading is often assessed using low-velocity impact testing. This work used drop weight indentation at 0.5 m and 1 m heights on flax/SS304 mesh hybrid composites that had two stacking sequences (S1 and S2). After testing, specimens that were struck impact (front) face and impact (back) face showed different indentation morphologies, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5. Whether stacking sequence design and hybrid reinforcement improve impact tolerance can be inferred from the variance in indentation size, crack start, and delamination zones43. Here, metallic reinforcement (SS304 mesh) was used in conjunction with natural fibers (flax and more hemp yarn) to form hybrid composites. Delaying fracture propagation and minimizing localized failure were two goals of this hybridization, which attempted to increase structural integrity44. The findings of the tests proved that the impact energy and the loading direction in relation to the fiber layup are the two most important factors influencing impact performance.

The effect of a 0.5 m drop weight on indentation

Both the impact (front) face and impact (back) face samples showed limited damage at the lower impact energy level (0.5 m drop height). While impact (front) face specimens had indentation sizes of 7.053 mm (S1) and 6.834 mm (S2), impact (back) face specimens had smaller indentation marks that were less distinct. Samples taken in a plane show circular indentations, which mean there was localized plastic deformation but no fiber fracture. An important function of the SS304 mesh reinforcement was to prevent the propagation of delamination by arresting cracks at the fiber-matrix contact. Adding metallic mesh increased indentation resistance by spreading the load over more surface area, as shown in earlier studies43.

The positive impact of hemp yarn reinforcement is demonstrated by the decreased indentation diameter in S2 when contrasted with S1. The composite system benefited from the addition of hemp fibers, which impart more ductility than flax due to their higher elongation at break. Because of this, the structure was able to undergo mild deformation instead of brittle fracture when subjected to impact force. Thakaret al. 202445 found that a balance between stiffness and toughness is achieved through the hybridization of natural fibers, which is in line with these observations. Residual deformation observed after impact provides insight into the permanent damage sustained by the laminate. In the present study, the S1 laminates exhibited greater residual indentation and back-face deformation, indicating dominant brittle fracture and limited elastic recovery. In contrast, the S2 laminates showed reduced residual deformation due to energy dissipation through yarn stretching, controlled delamination, and plastic deformation of the SS304 mesh. This behavior confirms the improved damage tolerance and post-impact integrity of the woven hybrid architecture.

Two separate composite samples, S1 and S2, were tested at a 0.5-meter drop height to determine their low-velocity drop weight impact response. Examining the load-time and energy-time curves for every specimen allowed us to gauge the mechanical behavior. At about 35 milliseconds, the load reaches its peak of 767 N, as shown in Fig. 8a, the load-time curve for specimen S1. The elastic deformation of the material and the beginning of damage initiation are correlated with this initial peak. When the load suddenly drops after the peak, it means that there been a major failure, like fiber breakage or matrix cracking. Afterwards, there are minor peaks, one of which is at 471 N, which indicates that the impactor and specimen came into additional contact as a result of rebound and localized material failure. The absorbed energy curve for S1 starts with a peak absorption of 1.34 J (Fig. 8b), then there is a secondary absorption of 0.93 J, and finally, there are two little events of energy dissipation, measuring 0.91 J and 0.42 J, respectively.

In contrast, specimen S2 has a far superior impact resistance profile. Figure 9a depicts the load-time curve, which demonstrates that the specimen has a maximum load capacity of 771 N before it begins to fail. This improved performance is illustrated by the absorbed energy curve for S2, which reaches a high of 2.38 J (Fig. 9b). There is a clear failure trend compared to S1, with more energy absorption in the early phases, as indicated by an extra peak of 0.92 J following the first energy peak. Both energy graphs displayed continuous oscillations at the end of the test, indicating that energy was being constantly lost.

At a drop height of half a meter, the load-time and energy-time curves for specimens S1 and S2 show quite different characteristics, which help to explain how they withstand impacts. For low-velocity impacts, S2 larger peak load and much higher absorbed energy are crucial signs of its improved crashworthiness and structural integrity.

Before any long-term harm happens, the specimen undergoes elastic deformation to withstand the impact load; this is the first elastic zone of the curves. Catastrophic failure modes in composite materials, including delamination, fiber fracture, and matrix cracking, are characterized by abrupt and substantial decreases in load after the first peak. This progressive failure process, where various damage mechanisms are activated as the impact energy is diffused through the specimen, is suggested by the existence of several load drops46.

With a peak load of 771 N, S2 is more capable of withstanding the applied force than S1 (767 N). Although there is a noticeable improvement in the load-bearing capacity, the most revealing indicator of S2 better performance is its peak absorbed energy of 2.38 J, which is approximately 78% greater than S1 1.34 J. One way to evaluate a material’s impact resistance and structural integrity is by looking at its energy absorption. The large disparity in absorbed energy implies that, prior to a catastrophic fracture occurrence, S2 releases a larger quantity of energy via non-catastrophic damage modes including matrix cracking and delamination.

Even though the drop height is consistent and the load profiles appear to be same, the test findings for S1 and S2 are different because of minute variances in their production methods or material composition. It would be possible to improve the load transmission and energy absorption of S2 by adjusting its curing temperature, pressure, fiber alignment, or interfacial bond strength. An important measure of crashworthiness is a composite’s capacity to gradually absorb and disperse energy through controlled failure processes, as opposed to a single catastrophic failure.

The effect of a 1 m drop weight on indentation

Extensive damage was noted in both impact (front) face and impact (back) face specimens at the greater impact energy (1 m drop height). Indentation diameters became larger for impact (front) face samples, reaching 10.987 mm (S1) and 9.901 mm (S2). Occasionally, tiny fissures expanded outside the indentation zone and the spherical imprint became more noticeable.

It was much worse for the impact (back) face specimens. Delamination zones were found to be quite large, reaching up to 20.190 mm for S1 and 18.882 mm for S2. Damaged areas included cracked resin, broken fibers, and localized debonding around metallic mesh. It is clear from the widespread impact (back) face damage that the stress transfer efficiency is controlled by the angle of contact with respect to the fiber layup. There is a lot of delamination and increased interlaminar shear stresses when the fibers aren’t parallel to the impact direction, which happens when the impact is angled. Such off-axis impacts weaken laminated composites structural integrity because they create huge subsurface delamination zones, as pointed out earlier by Greenhalgh and Hiley47. After testing, the hybrid composite specimens exhibited distinct damage morphologies on the impact (front) face and the back (non-impact) face, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5. The observed differences in damage morphology arise from bending and interlaminar stress distribution rather than from different impact direction.

Because of its more robust internal structure possibly due to a higher fiber volume fraction, stronger fiber-matrix adhesion, or an optimized stacking sequence S2 is able to tolerate the applied force better, as indicated by its higher peak load. As energy absorption is a direct indicator of a material’s ability to resist damage propagation and maintain structural integrity after an impact event48, the large increase in peak absorbed energy for S2 (from 2.30 J to 3.42 J) further validates this observation. The mechanical qualities that are produced can be greatly affected by variables like the curing temperature, pressure, or even the quality of the fibers that are impregnated with resin. A larger peak load and more energy absorption before significant damage could result, for example, from better fiber alignment or a stronger interfacial binding in S2.

Effect of stacking sequence on impact resistance

The resistance to indentation damage is significantly different between the S1 and S2 stacking sequences. Although the indentation zones at 1 m were bigger than those at 0.5 m for both laminates, the S2 series consistently exhibited smaller indentation diameters and less delamination spread. Because hemp yarn improved energy dissipation and interfacial bonding with the epoxy matrix, it was added to the S2 laminate, which is reason it improved.

The SS304 mesh also served as an energy barrier, preventing cracks from spreading beyond the surface area. The synergistic reinforcement effect occurred when the ductile hemp yarn and metallic fibers worked together to strengthen the composite, mitigating the effects of each component’s relative weakness. All of this lines up with what musthaq ahamed et al49. found: that hybrid laminates made of both natural and synthetic fibers absorbed more energy than single-fiber systems.

The hybrid composites showed different failure processes as a function of impact energy and direction, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5. Localized resin failure at the indentation zone and matrix cracking were the most common forms of damage at the lower drop height of 0.5 m. Fiber breakage and pull-out became more noticeable as the impact energy increased to 1 m, particularly in impact (back) face specimens. The flax fibers became visible voids when they broke and separated from the matrix. Consistent with the results of Kageyama et al.50, who found that delamination was the main damage mechanism in low-velocity impacts, delamination emerged as the most significant failure mode at greater drop heights, especially in impact (back) face structures. Also, the embedded SS304 mesh helped with the confinement effect by limiting the spread of cracks and acting as a localized stiffener, but it did sometimes cause stress concentrations when subjected to greater loads. All things considered, our findings point to a damage progression from low-energy localized resin cracking to high-energy widespread delamination and fiber breakage.

The larger indentation and delamination area observed as the drop height increases suggest that the composites are absorbing more energy. The smaller damage zones continuously seen in S2 as compared to S1 suggest that the impact energy is distributed more efficiently in S2. Crucial to the dissipation of energy through micro-fibril deformation and delaying catastrophic fracture was the role of hemp yarn reinforcement in S2. Hybrid natural fiber composites reinforced with glass or basalt fibers have also shown similar tendencies, with further reinforcing improving impact performance by increasing toughness and stiffness18.

Figure 12 compares the energy absorption and specific energy absorption of S1 and S2 laminates, highlighting the superior impact performance of the woven hybrid configuration. The findings indicated that both EA and SEA values gained with the increase in impact height, attributed to the greater kinetic energy transferred to the laminate during the drop-weight impact. The S1 configuration (Flax/SS304/Flax) absorbed 6.10 J and 6.39 J of energy at drop heights of 0.5 m and 1 m, respectively. A SEA value of 141.86 J/kg or 148.6 J/kg would have been appropriate here. Flax, stainless steel, flax, and woven hemp yarns, the S2 configuration, showed significant improvement. It measured 7.42 J of absorbed energy and 7.49 J of SEA at the same drop heights, and 172.55 J/kg and 174.18 J/kg, respectively. This indicates that the woven hybrid structure is superior at handling collisions, as it can absorb 20–25% more energy than S1.

Due to improved interfacial adhesion and stress transfer, S2 laminates have a superior capacity to absorb energy. The mechanical interlocking of the fiber layers and the epoxy matrix was extremely strong when hemp and flax strands were woven together over the SS304 mesh. This strengthened the interfacial shear strength, which in turn ensured a more uniform distribution of stress and postponed the onset of interfacial debonding in response to impact loading. The result is a load transfer technique that is more controlled and slower, which improves the composite’s energy dissipation significantly.

While flax fibers were more brittle, hemp strands were ductile and failed gradually as well. Multiple localized failure modes, such as fiber pull-out, micro-fibrillation, and sliding at the interface, were observed in the hybrid S2 laminate during the impact event. These measures enabled the laminate to dissipate higher levels of impact energy prior to complete failure. Findings from studies conducted by Thakare et al. (2024) and Musthaq Ahamed et al. (2025) demonstrate that using natural fibers in hybridization enhances post-impact strength and reduces the likelihood of matrix failure by dispersing failure patterns45,49.

The use of the SS304 wire mesh significantly improved the laminate’s energy absorption capabilities. With its load redistribution layer function, the metal mesh restricted the damage region, prevented cracks from propagating, and allowed for localized plastic deformation. The laminate was more impact-resistant because it prevented cracks and bridged stress simultaneously. Metallic mesh layers, according to Elayaraja and Rajamurugan (2024), improve the energy absorption capabilities, breaking resistance, and stiffness of natural fiber composites by preventing delamination mechanically51–55.

Compared to unidirectional natural fiber laminates, this material was less anisotropic due to the multi-directional reinforcement given by the woven flax-hemp yarn structure. The yarn weaving made the effective fiber volume fraction higher and the resin-rich portions smaller. This made the matrix–fiber contact denser and stronger. This microstructural densification made it less likely for the matrix to shatter and made the composite better able to handle repeated or off-axis impacts. The hybrid architecture thus enabled both impact (front) face and impact (back) face energy transfer during impact events, ensuring enhanced stability under dynamic loading.

Thermo-viscoelastic energy dissipation is another reason why the SEA in S2 is higher. When a quick impact load hits, some of the energy that is absorbed turns into heat through viscoelastic deformation and friction at the fiber/matrix border. The hybrid composite has a multi-phase structure that gave it more frictional contacts for internal damping. This made the energy dissipation rates higher. This internal friction mechanism accounts for the persistent oscillations noted in the energy–time curves post-peak load, indicating progressive energy dissipation and postponed structural breakdown.

A progressive damage model can be established to correlate the low-velocity impact (LVI) response with the observed ballistic performance of the hybrid laminates. Under LVI loading, damage initiates primarily in the epoxy matrix as localized micro-cracking, followed by interlaminar delamination and limited fiber fracture. These mechanisms govern subsequent ballistic resistance through strain-wave attenuation and progressive energy dissipation56,57. The superior impact resistance of the S2 laminate is attributed to the higher ductility of hemp yarns, which enable crack bridging, delayed delamination, and enhanced energy absorption through fiber stretching and pull-out58. The SS304 wire mesh acts as a stress-redistribution layer, attenuating strain waves and confining delamination through localized elastic–plastic deformation59–61.

The impact and ballistic response of hybrid flax hemp yarn/SS304 laminates is governed by a sequence of progressive damage mechanisms including (i) localized matrix cracking, (ii) interlaminar delamination growth, (iii) yarn stretching and pull-out with frictional dissipation, and (iv) plastic deformation/bending of SS304 wire mesh. The SS304 layer acts as a crack-arresting and stress redistribution barrier by attenuating bending-induced interlaminar shear stresses and confining delamination. In the woven hybrid architecture (S2), interwoven yarns increase mechanical interlocking and improve stress transfer continuity across plies, thereby delaying catastrophic through-thickness fracture. This results in higher absorbed energy and lower damage volume. These mechanisms align with recent mechanistic interpretations reported for laminated composite protective structures and impact-induced damage evolution62.

At the fabric level, energy dissipation is strongly controlled by yarn stretching, transverse wave propagation, yarn bending, and pull-out friction. Woven para-aramid fabric studies confirm that transverse wave propagation and yarn pull-out contribute significantly to ballistic efficiency63. Although natural fibers exhibit lower strength than aramids, similar mechanisms operate in flax/hemp yarn structures, with ductile hemp promoting greater strain accommodation and delaying rupture.

Hybrid systems do not always yield superior ballistic efficiency unless hybrid configurations are optimized. The superior behavior of S2 over S1 is attributed to synergistic stiffness-ductility coupling, interlaminar reinforcement continuity, and frictional energy dissipation. This is consistent with seminal hybrid fabric system studies emphasizing configuration-driven ballistic efficiency rather than material addition alone64.

Ballistic test

Table 2 shows the results of the experimental HVI test. The damage mechanisms seen show that the two laminates absorb energy in different ways. In S1, the failure was mostly brittle, with fiber breakage and matrix cracking happening mostly around the point of contact. In contrast, S2 failure mechanism was slower and more involved cracking in the matrix, delamination between laminations, pulling out of fibers, and localized bending of the metal mesh. These mechanisms together lose more kinetic energy through plastic deformation, interfacial shear, and frictional sliding. This is consistent with the higher measured absorbed energy (68.4 J) shown in Fig. 13. The ballistic response of the hybrid laminates can be quantitatively interpreted by correlating perforation mode, residual velocity, absorbed energy, and damage volume. High-speed imaging and post-impact morphology indicate that the S1 laminate predominantly undergoes mixed-mode perforation characterized by shear plugging and brittle fiber fracture, leading to rapid energy release and extensive localized damage. In contrast, the S2 laminate exhibits a more progressive perforation mechanism involving yarn stretching, mesh bending, controlled delamination, and delayed fiber rupture. The lower residual velocity measured for S2 corresponds to higher absorbed kinetic energy, which is dissipated through plastic deformation, interfacial friction, and progressive damage evolution. This enhanced energy dissipation results in reduced damage volume, smaller delamination zones, and lower mass loss compared to S1. The observed ballistic damage morphology therefore directly reflects the synergistic role of ductile hemp yarns and SS304 wire mesh in stress redistribution, strain-wave attenuation, and delamination confinement, confirming the effectiveness of the hybrid reinforcement architecture.

The results show that combining hemp yarn with SS304 mesh greatly increases the energy dissipation ability and ballistic tolerance of natural fiber composites. The synergistic reinforcing effect in S2 makes it easier to turn the kinetic energy of a projectile into deformation and internal fracture work. This improves protection performance, making it ideal for lightweight ballistic and impact-resistant uses.

The S1 laminate predominantly demonstrated a brittle failure mode, consistent with the standard behavior of non-woven fiber composites under high-velocity impacts. The damage was mostly near where the projectile hit, and it was caused by fibers breaking, the matrix splitting, and localized delamination. After the hit, observations Fig. 15 showed a deeper dent on the impact surface and a lot of damage on the back face. Cracks appeared in the matrix, layers separated, fibers were pulled out, and the metallic mesh was plastically deformed in certain areas68. Through a combination of sliding, shearing, and deforming the metal reinforcement, these processes accelerated the laminate’s energy loss. This made the material more impact-resistant and improved stress distribution. Damage to the impact face was minimal, with delamination zones measuring 13.229 mm and 15.835 mm (Fig. 15c). Delamination zones of 17.871 mm and 22.871 mm were visible on the back face (Fig. 15d), which demonstrated how hemp and flax yarns prevented cracks from propagating and prevented the material from entirely breaking through. As soon as the projectile made contact with both laminates, extensive damage began to occur. Because the resin matrix and the composite layers had differing bending stiffnesses, delamination resulted69. The matrix cracks were propagated by transverse shear stresses, and when the impact load increased, the residual tensile strength was reduced by bending stresses, which ultimately resulted in fiber breakage and partial penetration. The fibers snapped at a specific point in the damage progression. Because they prevent fracture propagation, distribute load more uniformly, and postpone catastrophic failure, the woven reinforcing structure and metallic confinement of the S2 design improve its ballistic performance.

The observed differences in impact and ballistic performance between the S1 and S2 laminates can be further understood by examining the underlying hybrid composite mechanisms governing stress transfer and damage evolution. The impact and ballistic behavior of hybrid flax-hemp fiber composites reinforced with SS304 wire mesh can be rationalized by considering fundamental mechanisms governing hybrid fiber composites, namely filament distribution, matrix flow, interfacial adhesion, and consolidation kinetics. In hybrid laminates, the spatial arrangement and continuity of reinforcing filaments critically control stress transfer efficiency and damage evolution under dynamic loading conditions. Uniform filament distribution, particularly when achieved through yarn weaving and fiber-metal interlocking, promotes effective load sharing among constituents and mitigates localized stress concentrations that initiate premature failure23,24. Matrix flow behavior during fabrication directly influences void formation, resin-rich regions, and interlaminar shear strength. Inadequate resin flow or poor wet-out around metallic reinforcements can create stress concentrators that accelerate matrix cracking and delamination under impact loading23,46. In the present study, the woven flax-hemp yarn architecture employed in the S2 laminate enhances resin penetration around the SS304 mesh, leading to improved consolidation and reduced interfacial discontinuities compared to the non-woven S1 configuration. Interfacial adhesion between natural fibers, polymer matrix, and metallic reinforcement governs the efficiency of load transfer and the activation of energy-dissipating mechanisms. Alkali treatment of flax and hemp fibers increases surface roughness and exposes reactive hydroxyl groups, thereby improving fiber-matrix bonding and interfacial shear strength18,39. Simultaneously, the SS304 wire mesh acts as a mechanically compatible reinforcement that bridges adjacent plies, redistributes stresses, and suppresses interlaminar crack propagation, consistent with observations reported for hybrid fiber-metal laminate systems44,51. Consolidation kinetics further dictate damage resistance by controlling fiber packing density, laminate density, and effective fiber volume fraction. Improved consolidation in woven hybrid systems has been shown to promote progressive damage mechanisms and enhanced impact energy absorption under both low-velocity and ballistic loading22,25. These fundamental mechanisms collectively explain the superior performance of the S2 laminate.

The scope of the present investigation is deliberately confined to a comparative assessment of the S1 and S2 laminate configurations in order to clearly identify the influence of stainless steel wire mesh incorporation and yarn weaving architecture on impact and ballistic behavior. By keeping the flax fiber reinforcement and epoxy matrix system constant, the study isolates the effects of hybrid reinforcement without introducing additional material or processing variables. While the inclusion of a flax-only laminate would offer a useful reference for quantifying the absolute contribution of the metallic mesh, such a baseline comparison is beyond the scope of the current work and is proposed for future studies to further benchmark the performance of the hybrid laminates.

Conclusion

Natural fiber composites can have their impact resistance and energy absorption enhanced by reinforcing them using flax and hemp yarns in conjunction with stainless steel 304 wire mesh. The woven hybrid laminate (S2) showed better performance than the non-woven configuration (S1) in a stacking sequence comparison, with an increase in specific energy absorption of 141.86 J/kg to 174.18 J/kg and an increase in absorbed energy of about 23–25%. The metallic mesh controlled delamination and dispersed isolated forces, while the ductile hemp fibers slowed the damage process.

The combination of the natural fibers and the metal reinforcement improved load transmission, reduced crack propagation, and strengthened the structure against both slow and fast impacts. S2 was more resilient and effective at stopping projectiles due to its 19.4% higher kinetic energy absorption compared to S1, according to ballistic tests. Lightweight, environmentally friendly laminates that can resist impacts and damage are created by combining weaving techniques with metallic confinement in natural fiber composites, according to this research. These laminates are promising for application in vehicles, aircraft, and the military.

Future scope

This study results suggest a number of interesting avenues for future research and practical use in industry. There is room for improvement in the material and structural optimization of the hybrid flax-hemp/SS304 composite system, which has shown promise as a sustainable, lightweight, and damage-tolerant material. To better understand strain evolution, delamination propagation, and interfacial damage under changing strain rates, future research should center on multi-scale modeling of impact dynamics. This can be achieved by combining finite-element simulations with digital picture correlation.

Improved accuracy in composite armor applications will be achieved through predictive design and the connection of experimental ballistic data with numerical simulations made possible by such modeling. It is also possible to produce certain energy absorption and stiffness properties by optimizing the weave design, which includes yarn density, orientation, and hybridization ratio. Improving environmental sustainability and post-impact recovery should be the goal of investigating the effects of alternative matrix systems like bio-epoxy, phenolic, or toughened thermoplastic resins, as well as different metal meshes like aluminum, titanium, or copper. Graphene, nanoclay, or SiO₂ nanoparticles are examples of nanofillers that could be included into the epoxy phase in future studies. This would improve thermal stability and interfacial adhesion, leading to longer composite durability even in harsh environments. The fabrication method must be scaled up to accommodate large-format ballistic panels and structural vehicle components in order to validate it in the industrial setting. To evaluate operational dependability in hot and outdoor conditions, long-term durability tests including fatigue, thermal aging, water absorption, and ultraviolet degradation are required. Experimental results, when combined with data-driven optimization frameworks and AI-assisted material design, could speed up the development of next-gen hybrid composites that are more eco-efficient and have better impact performance. The creation of flax/hemp-metal mesh hybrid composites is a major step toward making lightweight armor, aircraft fairings, and automobile crash-energy absorbers using materials other than glass or Kevlar. These hybrid laminates have the potential to establish a new category of bio-metallic composite materials for environmentally friendly high-performance engineering uses if the fiber design, surface modification, and process scalability are optimized correctly. Future research should prioritize long-term durability by conducting comprehensive testing on fatigue, thermal aging, and ultraviolet (UV) degradation to ensure these materials can withstand harsh environments over extended periods. Environmental aging remains a significant concern, necessitating detailed investigations into moisture absorption and its subsequent impact on the fiber-matrix interface, which is essential for any practical outdoor engineering application.
