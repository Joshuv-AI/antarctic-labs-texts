Factors associated with drug-resistant tuberculosis among patients at a tertiary referral hospital in Tanzania
Authors: Ahmed Mohamed Mustafa Elshiekh, Nana Karim Aziz, Sima Rugarabamu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) undermines global TB control, particularly in high-burden African settings, with high HIV co-infection rates. While Tanzania is among the 30 high-burden TB countries, data on resistance patterns at tertiary referral hospitals remain limited. Resistance was determined using GeneXpert MTB/RIF results extracted from patient records. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of infectious disease mortality worldwide, with an estimated 1.6 million deaths annually [1]. In 2023, TB likely became the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent again, surpassing COVID-19, and resulting in nearly twice as many deaths as HIV/AIDS [2]. The emergence of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) represents a critical threat to achieving global TB elimination targets. In 2021 alone, over 450,000 new MDR-TB cases were reported globally [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa, while home to only 14% of the world’s population, carries 25% of the global TB burden and the highest rates of HIV-associated TB, creating a syndemic that amplifies treatment complexity and fuels resistance acquisition [4].

Tanzania is listed among the 30 high-burden TB countries, with an annual TB incidence rate of approximately 222 cases per 100,000 population [5]. A national drug resistance survey conducted in 2017 reported MDR-TB in 4.6% of previously treated patients and 0.9% of new cases [6]. However, tertiary referral centers like Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) manage a disproportionately high-risk population, including complex cases, treatment failures, and patients with multiple comorbidities. Consequently, resistance rates at such facilities may substantially exceed national averages. Dar es Salaam, as the nation’s largest and most densely populated city, has consistently been identified as a high-transmission hotspot, with MNH serving as the primary referral center for complex and treatment-resistant cases [7].

Several factors contribute to the development and transmission of drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) [8]. The situation is particularly concerning in high-burden settings like Tanzania, where high HIV prevalence, low socioeconomic status, and inadequate diagnostic and treatment facilities may exacerbate the impact of MDR-TB [9]. While drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) is curable with standard six-month regimens, MDR-TB requires prolonged (9–20 months), toxic, and expensive treatment with second-line drugs that have lower cure rates (approximately 60–70% globally) and higher mortality [10]. Clinical and behavioral overlap between DS-TB and MDR-TB including cough, fever, weight loss, and poor adherence frequently leads to delayed diagnosis of resistance, inappropriate continued use of first-line regimens, and ongoing transmission of resistant strains within communities and healthcare settings [7, 11].

Despite increasing reports of MDR-TB from Tanzanian referral hospitals, limited research has examined the independent predictors of drug resistance—including the relative contribution of HIV co-infection versus behavioral factors versus transmission exposure—within the same patient population. Most existing studies focus on clinical, microbiological, or programmatic aspects, leaving a major gap in understanding the multifactorial determinants of resistance [12]. Given the high burden of TB-HIV co-infection in Dar es Salaam (approximately 16–20% of TB patients are HIV-positive), the central role of MNH as a referral center for complex cases, and the crucial importance of identifying modifiable risk factors for resistance, assessing the prevalence and predictors of drug resistance is essential for designing effective, integrated control strategies.

This study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalence of drug resistance among TB patients at MNH, (2) identify sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with resistance, and (3) determine independent predictors of resistance using multivariable analysis.

Methods

Study design, period, and setting

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using medical records of tuberculosis patients managed at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, between July 2024 and July 2025. MNH is a 1,500-bed national referral and university teaching hospital, receiving an average of 1,000–1,200 outpatients and a similar number of inpatients per week. The hospital serves as a referral center for patients from health centers, district hospitals, and other private health facilities across Tanzania, particularly from the Dar es Salaam region (Ilala, Kinondoni, and Temeke districts) and neighboring regions. The Internal Medicine Department and TB Clinic at MNH were selected as the study setting because they represent the primary entry points for TB diagnosis and management within the hospital.

Source population and study population

All patients diagnosed with tuberculosis and attending or admitted to the Internal Medicine Department at MNH during the study period were the source population. The study population comprised all TB patients (both new and previously treated) who had complete medical records containing drug susceptibility data and documented treatment outcomes.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

All patients with a documented TB diagnosis and complete drug susceptibility data who attended or were admitted to the Internal Medicine Department at MNH during the study period were included.

Exclusion criteria

Patients whose clinical records were incomplete or missing essential variables required for analysis (including missing DST results or treatment outcomes).

Sample size and sampling technique

Consecutive inclusion of all eligible TB patient files satisfying the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period (July 2024 to July 2025) was performed, yielding a final sample size of 170 patients. With 24 resistant events, the sample size permits multivariable analysis of up to 2 predictors using the rule of 10 events per variable, which guided our model specification and limited the number of variables entered into the final regression model.

Data collection

A standardized, pretested data extraction form was used to collect information from patient records. The form captured key sociodemographic variables (age, sex, residence, education level, occupation, marital status), clinical history (type of TB—pulmonary or extrapulmonary, HIV status, ART status, previous TB infection, comorbidities), treatment variables (anti-TB drugs prescribed, treatment adherence documented, history of treatment interruption), and laboratory variables (sputum AFB results, GeneXpert results, GeneXpert MTB/RIF results, documented MDR-TB status, and contact with a known MDR-TB patient, contact with known MDR-TB patient). Data were extracted manually by the researcher.

Variables and outcome definition

Primary outcome

The primary outcome variable was rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB), identified using GeneXpert MTB/RIF results documented in patient records. Because confirmatory isoniazid susceptibility testing was not routinely available for all patients, rifampicin resistance was used as a proxy indicator for drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) as the primary outcome. This is consistent with WHO guidance that rifampicin resistance serves as a reliable marker for MDR-TB in high-burden settings where isoniazid resistance frequently accompanies rifampicin resistance [5].

Documented MDR-TB

MDR-TB (resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin) was recorded as a secondary outcome when explicitly documented in patient medical records. These diagnoses typically resulted from line probe assays or phenotypic drug susceptibility testing performed on referral specimens. However, routine isoniazid susceptibility testing was not performed for all patients, so the MDR-TB prevalence reported reflects documented clinical diagnoses rather than systematic testing of all cases. Therefore, the primary outcome of this study is RR-TB/DR-TB (used interchangeably throughout), with documented MDR-TB reported as a secondary descriptive finding.

Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, residence categorized as urban or rural, education level categorized as none, primary, secondary, or college/university) and clinical characteristics (type of TB, HIV status categorized as positive, negative, or unknown, prior TB infection as yes/no, treatment adherence categorized as good, poor, or not documented, treatment interruption history as yes/no/not documented, and contact with known MDR-TB patient as yes/no/not documented).

Standard definitions (adapted from WHO and Tanzanian NTLP guidelines):

Standard definitions were adapted from the World Health Organization and Tanzanian National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Programme (NTLP) guidelines. A new case was defined as a tuberculosis patient who had never been treated for TB or had taken anti-TB drugs for less than one month, whereas a previously treated case referred to a patient who had received anti-TB drugs for one or more months in the past and was again diagnosed with tuberculosis [6]. Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) was defined as TB caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin [5]. Good treatment adherence was defined as documented completion of ≥ 80% of scheduled doses without prolonged interruption [6].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations) summarized patient characteristics. Bivariate associations between categorical variables and drug resistance were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact test where expected cell counts were < 5). Due to the low number of resistant cases (n = 24), only two predictors (HIV status and contact with known MDR-TB patient) were entered into the multivariable model to avoid overfitting. As a sensitivity analysis, Firth’s penalized logistic regression was performed to address sparse-data bias. Additionally, a separate sensitivity analysis excluded HIV-unknown patients (n = 80) to assess whether HIV remained significant when comparing only known HIV-positive vs. HIV-negative individuals.

Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of drug resistance, reporting adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. Variables with > 40% missing data were excluded from multivariable analysis but retained in bivariate analysis with appropriate footnotes indicating the effective sample size. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data were assessed using Little’s MCAR test (p = 0.08, suggesting data were missing completely at random).Variables with > 40% missing data (education, treatment interruption documentation, MDR-TB contact) were excluded from multivariable analysis but retained in bivariate analysis with footnotes indicating effective sample sizes.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committees of Muhimbili National Hospital (Ref: MNH/TRC/2025/042) and the University of Medical Sciences and Technology (Ref: UMST/FOM/2025/011). A waiver of informed consent was granted given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified medical records. Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis, and all data were stored in a password-secured drive accessible only to the research team. All methods were conducted in compliance with the relevant guidelines and regulations outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

We included 170 TB patients in the analysis. The majority were managed in the Inpatient Ward (78.8%, 134/170), followed by the TB Clinic (18.2%, 31/170). The mean age was 44.2 years (SD 15.6; range 15–85 years), with the largest age groups being 40–59 years (38.8%, 66/170) and 20–39 years (34.1%, 52/170). Males constituted 64.7% (110/170) of the study population. Regarding residence, 50.6% (86/170) resided in urban areas and 48.8% (83/170) in rural areas. Education level was poorly documented, with 68.8% (117/170) having no recorded education status; among those with data (n = 53), 26.4% (14/53) had no formal education, 22.6% (12/53) primary, 32.1% (17/53) secondary, and 18.9% (10/53) college/university education. Missing data were substantial for several variables, including education (68.8%), HIV status (47.1%), treatment interruption (47.1%), and MDR-TB contact (52.9%). The process of participant selection and inclusion is summarized in Fig. 1. With only 24 resistant cases, the study was underpowered to detect modest effect sizes for several clinically important predictors; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Clinically, pulmonary TB accounted for 71.8% (122/170) of cases, while 28.2% (48/170) had extrapulmonary TB. HIV status was positive in 16.5% (28/170) of patients, negative in 36.5% (62/170), and unknown in 47.1% (80/170). A prior history of TB infection was reported in 20.0% (34/170) of patients. Prevalence of drug resistance

The overall prevalence of drug-resistant tuberculosis (defined as rifampicin resistance detected by GeneXpert) was 14.1% (24/170; 95% CI: 9.5–20.1). Of these 24 RR-TB cases, 22 patients (12.9% of the total cohort; 95% CI: 8.5–18.8) had documented MDR-TB diagnoses in their medical records (indicating confirmed resistance to both rifampicin and isoniazid). The remaining 2 RR-TB cases lacked documented isoniazid susceptibility results, so MDR-TB could not be confirmed. It is important to note that the variable ‘contact with known MDR-TB patient’ had substantial missing data (52.9%, 90/170 patients), which may have introduced bias. Patients with documented contact history may differ systematically from those with missing records, and the observed association should be interpreted with caution.

Bivariate analysis: factors associated with drug resistance

As shown in Table 2, residence, education level, HIV status, previous TB infection, treatment adherence, treatment interruption, and contact with a known MDR-TB patient were significantly associated with drug resistance in bivariate analysis. Higher resistance was observed among patients from rural areas compared to urban areas (19.3% vs. 9.3%; χ² = 6.705, p = 0.035). Lower education level was associated with increased resistance (χ² = 18.603, p < 0.001). HIV-positive patients had higher resistance compared to HIV-negative patients (28.6% vs. 6.5%; χ² = 9.580, p = 0.008).

Multivariable analysis: independent predictors of drug resistance

Due to the low number of resistant cases (n = 24), only two predictors (HIV status and contact with known MDR-TB patient) were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to avoid overfitting. The model was statistically significant (χ² = 18.42, p < 0.001) and explained 20.7% of the variance in drug resistance (Nagelkerke R² = 0.207).

As shown in Table 3, HIV-positive patients had approximately five-fold higher odds of drug-resistant tuberculosis compared with the reference group (aOR = 4.82, 95% CI: 1.18–19.67, p = 0.030). Similarly, patients with documented contact with an MDR-TB case had more than seven-fold higher odds of drug resistance (aOR = 7.34, 95% CI: 2.45–22.01, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study provides contemporary evidence of a high prevalence of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (14.1%) among patients at Tanzania’s largest referral hospital, substantially exceeding the national average for new cases (0.9%) and approaching the proportion reported among previously treated patients (4.6%) in the 2017 national survey. Of the 24 RR-TB cases detected, 22 had documented MDR-TB diagnoses, indicating that rifampicin resistance served as a reliable marker for MDR-TB in this population. The prevalence is higher than some Ethiopian referral hospital reports (5.8–8.5%) [13, 14] but lower than rates in South Asia (20–30%) [15, 16], reflecting regional differences in baseline resistance burdens and study populations. Within East Africa, our findings are comparable to recent reports from Kenya (12.1%) [7] and Uganda (10.8%) [17], suggesting that Tanzania shares a similar DR-TB burden with its neighbours. No pre-XDR or XDR-TB cases were documented, but routine second-line DST is not standard at MNH, so undetected advanced resistance cannot be excluded. This finding should be interpreted as a gap in diagnostic capacity rather than confirmed absence of advanced resistance patterns.

Two factors were independently associated with DR-TB in multivariable analysis. HIV co-infection was associated with a markedly higher prevalence of resistance (28.6% vs. 6.5% in HIV-negative patients; aOR = 4.82). This association is consistent with the broader literature describing mechanisms by which HIV co-infection may contribute to drug resistance, including higher bacillary loads, recurrent TB episodes, and challenges in treatment adherence, though our cross-sectional design cannot establish causality [13, 16]. The observation that HIV status remained significant even after adjusting for antiretroviral therapy status suggests that underlying immunosuppression rather than antiretroviral-mediated factors may be the primary driver of resistance, although this interpretation is speculative and requires confirmation in prospective studies. Similar findings have been reported from Uganda [17], reinforcing the consistent association between HIV and DR-TB across East African settings.

Contact with a known MDR-TB patient was the strongest predictor (aOR = 7.34), with the majority (58.3%) of exposed individuals demonstrating resistance compared to only 2.9% of unexposed patients. This finding is consistent with active transmission of resistant strains within healthcare settings, households, or other close-contact environments, although the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish between transmitted resistance and shared risk factors among contacts. The magnitude of this effect is comparable to studies from high-burden settings in South Africa and India, where household contact with MDR-TB cases conferred a fivefold to tenfold increased risk [18]. This observation has potential implications for infection control practice; however, this remains a hypothesis requiring prospective evaluation.

Several variables significant on bivariate analysis (treatment interruption, poor adherence, lower education, rural residence) did not retain significance in multivariable analysis. These variables may be mediators rather than independent causes; for example, treatment interruption could be a consequence of HIV-related morbidity, and lower education or rural residence may predispose to poor adherence. Alternatively, the loss of significance may simply reflect limited statistical power due to small cell sizes (n = 24 resistant cases), resulting in wide confidence intervals and imprecise estimates that preclude definitive conclusions about the independent effects of these behavioural factors.

The logistic regression model explained only one-fifth of the variance in DR-TB (Nagelkerke R²=0.207), indicating that the majority of variation is attributable to factors not captured in this study. Key unmeasured factors that could influence resistance include microbiological characteristics (specific M. tuberculosis lineage, katG/inhA/rpoB mutations), health system factors (diagnostic delays, drug stock-outs, incorrect regimens), socioeconomic factors (poverty, food insecurity, transportation costs), and psychosocial factors (stigma, depression, lack of family support) [19, 20]. The substantial missing data (up to 78.8% for education) represents a major limitation that may have introduced bias.

Despite these limitations, these findings have implications for clinical practice. Universal baseline molecular testing should be considered for all TB patients, especially those with HIV or known MDR-TB contact. Infection control measures should be strengthened, including rapid separation of suspected MDR-TB cases, improved ward ventilation, and appropriate respiratory protection for healthcare workers. For HIV-TB co-infected patients, integrated co-located services with directly observed therapy and intensified adherence counselling are essential. At the national level, systematic contact tracing should be expanded to include all household and healthcare contacts of confirmed MDR-TB cases. Prospective multicentre studies incorporating genomic epidemiology are urgently needed to distinguish transmitted from acquired resistance and to definitively identify the independent effects of behavioural and socioeconomic factors on DR-TB in Tanzania.

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. A considerable proportion of variables had missing data, which may have introduced bias if patients with incomplete records differed systematically from those included. The relatively small number of DR-TB cases (n = 24) limited statistical power and reduced the ability to detect meaningful associations. As a single-center study at a national referral hospital, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other healthcare settings and may be influenced by referral patterns that overrepresent complex cases. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the available clinical records did not allow differentiation between primary and acquired drug resistance. Given the cross-sectional design, all associations reported should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than causal. These limitations highlight the need for larger prospective multicenter studies with more complete data collection. The high proportion of missing data for the MDR-TB contact variable (52.9%) is particularly concerning, as this was the strongest predictor identified. We cannot exclude the possibility that patients with undocumented contact history included both exposed and unexposed individuals, and the observed association may be biased if contact history was preferentially documented for patients with known resistance risk factors. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the strength of this association.

Conclusion

Rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (used as the operational definition of drug-resistant TB) was found in 14.1% of TB patients at Muhimbili National Hospital, with 12.9% having documented MDR-TB diagnoses. Due to substantial missing data (up to 78.8% for some variables) and a small number of resistant cases (n = 24), only HIV co-infection and known MDR-TB contact could be reliably assessed in multivariable analysis. Both were independently associated with resistance in multivariable analysis, although the association with MDR-TB contact must be interpreted with caution due to substantial missing data (aOR = 4.82 for HIV; aOR = 7.34 for MDR-TB contact).Other clinically important factors (treatment interruption, adherence, education, residence) could not be adequately evaluated in this study. Prospective multicenter studies with complete data are needed to confirm these associations. Given the substantial missing data and single-center design, these findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they require confirmation in larger, prospective multicenter studies. Nonetheless, targeted interventions for HIV-positive patients and exposed contacts remain a priority.
