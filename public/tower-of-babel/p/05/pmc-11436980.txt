Predicting the potential geographical distribution of Zingiber striolatum Diels (Zingiberaceae), a medicine food homology plant in China
Authors: Bin Huang, Shuai Chen, Lei Xu, Heng Jiang, Xun Chen, Hongping He, Ting Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractZingiber striolatum Diels is a unique medicine food homology plant native to China. In recent years, due to severe habitat destruction, studying the impact of climate change on the distribution of wild resources is of great significance for the ecological conservation and artificial cultivation of Z. striolatum. This study collected 141 valid species distribution records, and 37 environmental variables, and projected two future climate scenarios (SSP126 and SSP585) for two periods (2050s and 2090s). By employing Pearson analysis, Maximum Entropy Model (MaxEnt), and Geographic Information System (ArcGIS), we predicted the potential suitable habitats for Z. striolatum under present and future climates, as well as identified the dominant environmental variables influencing its distribution. The results indicated that the MaxEnt model performed well (AUC > 0.9) with high accuracy and reliability. The dominant environmental factors included Precipitation of driest quarter (39.0 ~ 473.8 mm), Precipitation of wettest quarter (593.2 ~ 1269.4 mm), Temperature annual range (9.8 ~ 28.6℃), and Mean diurnal range (6.5 ~ 9.6℃). The highly suitable areas for Z. striolatum were mainly distributed in western and southern Yunnan, northern and western Guangxi, Guangdong, Fujian, and central Hainan. Under future climate change, the centroid of the total suitable area for Z. striolatum is projected to shift towards the southwest (Yungui Plateau) at higher elevations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73202-4.Keywords: MaxEnt, ArcGIS, Zingiber striolatum, Medicine food homology plant, Environmental variable, Potential geographical distributionSubject terms: Plant sciences, Ecology

Introduction

Zingiber striolatum Diels is a perennial herbaceous plant in the ginger genus of the Zingiberaceae family, and it is a medicine food homology plant endemic to China. According to the “Flora of China,” Z. striolatum is mainly distributed in provinces such as Hunan, Hubei, Anhui, Guizhou, Yunnan, Guangxi, and Sichuan, growing in shady areas or along streams at altitudes of 300 ~ 1900 m1. Although Z. striolatum is widely distributed in China, it is relatively less cultivated, with wild populations being predominant2. As a unique wild plant resource in China, Z. striolatum holds significant economic, culinary, and medicinal value. Z. striolatum is a natural, pesticide-free health vegetable, with its edible parts mainly being the flower buds and roots3. It commands a relatively high price among vegetables, offering good economic returns3. Studies have shown that the tender flower head and mature stem and leaf parts of Z. striolatum contain a wide range of essential amino acids for humans, with levels generally higher than common cereals4. Z. striolatum also contains abundant proteins, dietary fiber, and polysaccharides, making it nutritionally valuable5. Z. striolatum has high medicinal value. In the Ming Dynasty, Li Shizhen’s “Compendium of Materia Medica” recorded that Z. striolatum has the effects of promoting blood circulation, resolving phlegm, relieving cough, and alleviating pain and swelling6. Modern research has also confirmed that Z. striolatum has pharmacological effects of anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and antimicrobial properties, and may have potential therapeutic effects on metabolic syndrome (obesity and type-2 diabetes)7. Yang found a certain amount of vitamin C, total phenols, and total flavonoids in Z. striolatum, confirming that they are the main antioxidants and can effectively scavenge free radicals8. Tian investigated the chemical composition of the volatile oil from Z. striolatum roots and its in vitro antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects9. The results showed that it had weak activity in clearing DPPH and ABTS free radicals, but had significant inhibitory effects on Clostridium perfringens, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans, and could significantly inhibit the proliferation of human leukemia (K562), lung cancer (A549), and prostate cancer (PC-3) cell lines. Subsequently, they also found that the volatile oils from the flowers, leaves, and stems of Z. striolatum had varying degrees of inhibitory effects on the proliferation of K562 cells, A549 cells, and PC-3 cells10. These studies indicate the potential application value of Z. striolatum in the food, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical industries.

With the continuous progress of human civilization and the ongoing development of industrial technology, many issues such as population growth, energy consumption, environmental pollution, and climate change are posing crises and challenges globally. Among these, climate change is one of the most severe problems faced by the world in the 21st century. Over the past century, the average surface temperature in China has risen by 0.99 °C, particularly in the last 60 years, where the rate of temperature increase has been almost twice the global average11. Climate change affects the biodiversity of organisms and is closely related to the distribution and survival of species. Climate change has had a significant impact on the phenology, abundance, and distribution of some plants, and may even lead to a decrease in the number of certain species or their extinction12–14. As global average temperatures continue to rise in the future, precipitation patterns and other bioclimatic factors will change. Crop plants are greatly affected by climate change, as changes in temperature and precipitation can affect the total yield of crops and the length of the potential growing season15,16. Due to the economic, edible, and medicinal importance of Z. striolatum, in recent years, uncontrolled harvesting and utilization have led to severe destruction of its wild resources and habitats, with resources in its original geographical distribution area nearing depletion and the distribution of wild resources nationwide gradually shrinking. Meanwhile, climate change has exacerbated the ongoing deterioration of the global ecological environment, causing Z. striolatum to lose its most suitable habitats, resulting in a sharp annual decline in its wild resource numbers17. Therefore, scientifically analyzing the relationship between species geographical distribution and climate change, and implementing rational and effective species protection measures, is of great significance for maintaining biodiversity and reducing the risks brought about by global climate warming.

Species distribution models (SDMs) established by ecological niche models and future climate models are widely used to predict and analyze the potential habitat of species under climate change18. Initially, the relationship between present species distribution and environmental factors is established using SDMs, and then this model is driven by future climate change scenarios to predict species response curves and presence probabilities19. Various ecological niche models, such as generalized linear models (GLM), maximum entropy model (MaxEnt), ecological niche factor analysis (ENFA), and genetic algorithm for rule-set production (GARP), have been developed20–23. Different models require different data and algorithms during the species distribution simulation process, leading to potential biases in the computed results24. Among these models, the MaxEnt model stands out as it evaluates species’ ecological niches by incorporating species’ geographic distribution information and environmental factors, projecting them onto the landscape. This model can visually display species’ presence probabilities, and potential habitats, making it the most widely applied and effective model25. The combination of the MaxEnt model and the BCC-CSM2-MR model is particularly suitable for predicting the potential habitats of Chinese species under present and future climate change, with high predictive accuracy26.

In the early study of the geographical distribution and suitable habitats of Z. striolatum, Huang et al. used the MaxEnt model to obtain the potential distribution of this species17. During the process of selecting environmental variables, the authors included 19 bioclimatic variables for model construction. In the construction of the MaxEnt model, the authors used 75% of species occurrence points as the training dataset and the remaining 25% as the test dataset, with other parameters set as default. In analyzing and evaluating the model results, the authors assessed the accuracy and reliability of the model using the area under the curve (AUC) value and measured the importance of environmental variables using the Jackknife method. Although the above modeling method successfully predicted the geographical distribution and suitable habitats of Z. striolatum, there are still some limitations to this approach. We have provided a detailed comparison and analysis in the “Discussion” section of this paper, building upon and supplementing previous research in our study.

In this study, based on distribution information and environmental variable data, the ecological suitability of Z. striolatum was simulated using the ArcGIS system and the MaxEnt model. The future distribution of Z. striolatum was predicted under two different scenarios (SSP126 and SSP585) at different periods using the BCC-CSM2-MR model. The main objectives of this study are threefold: (1) analyze the important environmental variables influencing the distribution of Z. striolatum; (2) predict the potential geographic distribution of Z. striolatum in China during the present period; and (3) predict changes in the distribution area of Z. striolatum under the influence of future climate change. The results of this study can provide a scientific basis for developing rational ecological conservation and cultivation strategies for Z. striolatum.

Suitable habitat range

The environmental variables response curve allows for the evaluation of the relationship between the probability of occurrence and environmental factors. In this research, we utilized the MaxEnt model to investigate the ideal range of environmental factors influencing the distribution of Z. striolatum and to establish its adaptive threshold. According to the importance of environmental factors, we only present the response curves and suitable ranges of the four dominant environmental factors. Data for other environmental factors can be found in Supplementary Material 2. Based on Fig. 4; Table 3, it can be inferred that using a probability of occurrence greater than 0.5 as the reference standard, the optimal ranges for Z. striolatum corresponding to Bio16, Bio7, and Bio2 are 593.2 ~ 1269.4 mm, 9.8 ~ 28.6 °C, and 6.5 ~ 9.6 °C, respectively. Additionally, it is important to note that when Bio17 is between 39.0 ~ 473.8 mm, the probability of occurrence of Z. striolatum is above 0.5, and in a continuous growth state; beyond 473.8 mm, the probability of occurrence remains stable at around 0.77. Therefore, the optimal range for Z. striolatum corresponding to Bio17 is 39.0 ~ 473.8 mm. Moreover, environmental factors such as Aspect, S_caco3, Slope, and Bio10 also have significant impacts on the potentially suitable habitat of Z. striolatum. In summary, in terms of precipitation, Z. striolatum is suitable for distribution in the humid regions with a tropical and subtropical monsoon climate in southern China; in terms of temperature, Z. striolatum is not cold-tolerant and is suitable for distribution in moderate regions.

Potential distribution under present climate

The results of the MaxEnt model were used in ArcGIS 10.8 to predict the potential distribution area of Z. striolatum in China. Figure 5 illustrates the findings of this analysis. The map displays various color-coded zones: blue indicates unsuitable areas, green indicates generally suitable areas, yellow indicates moderately suitable areas, and red indicates highly suitable areas. The distribution map clearly shows that the most favorable regions for the growth of Z. striolatum are primarily situated south of the Yangtze River. It is noteworthy that the high suitability areas for Z. striolatum are mainly distributed in western and southern Yunnan, northern and western Guangxi, Guangdong, Fujian, and central Hainan, covering a total area of 40.57 × 104 km2, accounting for 4.22% of China’s land area, and these areas closely match the geographical distribution information of Z. striolatum collected in this study. The moderately suitable areas for Z. striolatum are mainly located in central Yunnan, southern Guangxi, southwestern Guizhou, central Hunan, and central Jiangxi, covering a total area of 67.45 × 104 km2, accounting for 7.01% of China’s land area. The generally suitable areas are primarily found in northern Yunnan, Sichuan, northern Guizhou, Chongqing, western Hubei, western Hunan, southern Anhui, Zhejiang, Taiwan, and western Hainan, covering a total area of 78.36 × 104 km2, accounting for 8.15% of China’s mainland area. The total area of suitable areas is 186.38 × 104 km2, representing 19.38% of China’s land area.

Potential distribution under future climate

In the SSP126 scenario, from 2041 to 2060, the highly suitable areas are mainly distributed in most parts of Yunnan, Guangxi, southwestern Guizhou, southern Hunan, western Guangdong, and most parts of Hainan, covering an area of 52.78 × 104 km2, accounting for 5.49% of China’s land area. The moderately suitable areas are mainly distributed in northeastern Yunnan, central and northern Guizhou, central and northern Hunan, southwestern Hubei, southern Guangxi, northern and eastern Guangdong, and western and southern Jiangxi, covering an area of 57.20 × 104 km2, accounting for 5.95% of China’s land area. The total suitable area is 180.82 × 104 km2, accounting for 18.80% of China’s land area. From 2081 to 2100, the highly suitability areas are mainly distributed in areas outside of central and southern Yunnan, Guangxi, northern and central Guangdong, and central Sichuan, covering an area of 55.28 × 104 km2, accounting for 5.75% of China’s land area. The moderately suitable areas are mainly distributed in Guizhou, Hunan, southern Guangxi, southwestern and eastern Guangdong, Hainan, northern Taiwan, southeastern Chongqing, and southwestern Hubei, covering an area of 70.70 × 104 km2, accounting for 7.35% of China’s land area. The total suitable area is 192.92 × 104 km2, accounting for 20.06% of China’s land area. Comparing the highly suitable areas, moderately suitable areas, and total suitable areas under the present climate conditions, we found that in the SSP126 scenario, from 2041 to 2060, the area of highly suitable areas has slightly increased in a small range, while the area of moderately suitable areas has locally decreased, leading to a reduction in the total suitable area. By the period from 2081 to 2100, due to the warming of the ecological environment of Z. striolatum, the highly suitable areas, moderately suitable areas, and total suitable areas show a trend of growth.

In the SSP585 scenario, from 2041 to 2060, the highly suitable areas are mainly distributed in most parts of Yunnan, central Sichuan, most parts of Guangxi, southwest and southeast Guizhou, southwest and southern Hunan, and central-northern Guangdong, covering area of 57.02 × 104 km2, accounting for 5.93% of China’s land area; the moderately suitable areas are mainly distributed in central Guizhou, central and northern Hunan, southwest Hubei, southern Guangxi, eastern Guangdong, western and southern Jiangxi, and Hainan, covering an area of 65.73 × 104 km2, accounting for 6.83% of China’s land area; the total area of suitable areas is 196.71 × 104 km2, accounting for 20.45% of China’s land area. From 2081 to 2100, the highly suitable areas are mainly distributed in most parts of Yunnan, southwest and southeast Guizhou, western and northern Guangxi, southwest and southern Hunan, central-northern Guangdong, and central Sichuan, covering an area of 54.43 × 104 km2, accounting for 5.66% of China’s land area; the moderately suitable areas are mainly distributed in central Guizhou, central and western Hunan, south-central Guangxi, central-northern Hainan, southwest Hubei, western and southern Jiangxi, southwest Fujian, and central-southern Zhejiang, covering an area of 59.38 × 104 km2, accounting for 6.17% of China’s land area; the total area of suitable areas is 184.73 × 104 km2, accounting for 19.20% of China’s land area. Comparing the highly suitable areas, moderately suitable areas, and total suitable areas under the present climate conditions with those under the SSP585 scenario, we found that from 2041 to 2060, although the area of moderately suitable areas has locally decreased, the area of highly suitable areas has significantly expanded, resulting in an overall increase in the total suitable area. However, from 2081 to 2100, due to the gradual increase in greenhouse gas emissions, the areas of highly suitable, moderately suitable, and total suitable areas show a decreasing trend. Discussion

The MaxEnt model is one of the most commonly used niche models in ecology, with good predictive ability. It has been widely applied to predict the potential distribution of crops and medicinal plants27–29, and its predictive accuracy is superior to other niche models under equivalent conditions30. In this study, the MaxEnt model was used to predict the potential suitable habitats of the medicine food homology plant Z. striolatum in China. The results showed that the model’s AUC value was greater than 0.9, indicating accurate results and high reliability31. In a study on the dominant environmental factors affecting the distribution of Z. striolatum, Huang found through MaxEnt modeling that the Precipitation of driest month (Bio14), Precipitation of warmest quarter (Bio18), and Min temperature of coldest month (Bio6) were the dominant environmental factors17. However, the results of this study showed that the dominant environmental factors affecting the distribution of Z. striolatum were Precipitation of driest quarter (Bio17), Precipitation of wettest quarter (Bio16), Temperature annual range (Bio7), Mean diurnal range (Bio2), with a cumulative contribution rate exceeding 85%. The reasons for the differences in research results may be (1) differences in distribution data and environmental factor selection. This study ensured that there was only one occurrence data within 5 km and conducted correlation analysis and selection of the environmental factors used to avoid overfitting of data during calculation32, which could affect the prediction results, while previous studies did not screen environmental factors. (2) Different types and quantities of environmental factors used. In this study, 37 environmental variables were included, and ultimately 15 environmental factors were selected for modeling analysis. Different modeling with environmental factors may lead to different simulation results33. (3) Model parameter settings. The settings of parameters such as Max number of background points, Maximum number of iterations, and the number of cross-validations have a significant impact on the model’s running results, and the default settings of the software may not be the optimal parameters34. In this study, Max number of background points was set to 10,000, the Maximum number of iterations was set to 106, and 10 cross-validations were conducted. Through 106 times of machine learning and computations, and the presentation of the average value after 10 cross-validations, the reliability of the model predictions could be further improved.

According to the “Flora of China”, Z. striolatum is mainly distributed in provinces such as Hunan, Hubei, Anhui, Guizhou, Yunnan, Guangxi, and Sichuan in China1. Model predictions show that the suitable habitat of Z. striolatum is mainly concentrated in regions south of the Yangtze River in China, including the Yangtze River Delta, Central China, Southwest China, and the southern coastal areas. It is worth noting that under current climate conditions, the predicted highly suitable areas are mainly concentrated in South China, including Yunnan, Guangxi, Guangdong, Guizhou, Hunan, Fujian, and other regions. This prediction result is largely consistent with the records in the “Flora of China”. These regions belong to a typical subtropical monsoon climate, characterized by hot and rainy summers, and mild and humid winters. The species richness in this region is relatively high, making it particularly suitable for plants in the Zingiberaceae family. It is worth noting that China is one of the countries with the most diverse distribution of Zingiberaceae, and researchers have discovered a wide variety of plants in the Zingiber genus35. Among the provinces in China with the most diverse species distribution, Yunnan stands out, with a particularly rich distribution of Zingiberaceae. Yunnan Province is mainly composed of mountainous plateaus, with tropical rainforests, tropical seasonal moist forests, tropical montane evergreen broadleaf forests, and tropical monsoon forests. The altitude varies significantly, with distinct differences, and the southern part is covered by large areas of primary forests, which can block a large amount of cold air from the north in winter36, providing a favorable environment and climate for the growth of Zingiberaceae. Precipitation, temperature, and other climatic factors are important determinants of the potential geographic distribution of species, and different species in different regions respond differently to global climate change37. The results of this study indicate that temperature (Bio7: Temperature annual range, Bio2: Mean diurnal range) and precipitation (Bio17: Precipitation of driest quarter, Bio16: Precipitation of wettest quarter) are the dominant environmental factors influencing the distribution of Z. striolatum. This is primarily because Z. striolatum thrives in tropical and subtropical regions where the climate is warm and humid, providing optimal temperature and precipitation conditions for its growth. It is worth noting that we found the Substratesoil clay content (s_clay) contributed more than 1% to the model, but with a small gain value, and it was ultimately discarded. Previous studies have shown that plants of the Zingiberaceae family prefer to grow in moist and well-drained soils, such as sandy loam, clay loam, red loam, and red soil, mainly due to the rich microbial environment in these soils that promote plant growth38,39. The plant growth environment is highly complex and influenced by multiple factors such as light, altitude, soil, topography, temperature, precipitation, and human disturbances. Therefore, in future research, it is necessary to comprehensively consider multiple factors affecting species distribution models and continuously improve research results to provide a more scientifically informed basis for the ecological research and conservation of Z. striolatum.

Different Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) explain varying levels of future climate change40,41. SSP126 represents the most optimistic scenario for future greenhouse gas emissions (limiting global warming to below 2 °C), while SSP585 represents the worst-case scenario for greenhouse gas emissions by the year 2100 42. This study selected two scenarios (SSP126 and SSP585) for two periods (2050s and 2090s) to predict the future geographic distribution of Z. striolatum. The results show that under the SSP126-2050s scenario, the area of highly suitable habitat increased by 30.08%, while the area of moderately suitable habitat decreased by 15.20%, resulting in a total decrease of 2.99% in suitable habitat area. Under the SSP126-2090s scenario, the area of highly suitable habitat increased by 36.25%, the area of moderately suitable habitat increased by 4.82%, and the total suitable habitat area increased by 3.51%. These results indicate that by the end of this century, with improving climate conditions, the suitable habitat for Z. striolatum has been somewhat enhanced, especially with a significant expansion of highly suitable areas, and a gradual transition of unsuitable areas to suitable ones. In the SSP585-2050s scenario, the area of highly suitable habitat increased by 40.52%, while the area of moderately suitable habitat decreased by 2.56%. The total area of suitable habitat increased by 5.53%. In the SSP585-2090s scenario, the area of highly suitable habitat increased by 34.14%, but the area of moderately suitable habitat decreased by 11.97%. The total area of suitable habitat decreased by 0.89%. Despite continuous greenhouse gas emissions, the total area of suitable habitat did not show a significant decrease, mainly due to the gradual transition of generally suitable and moderately suitable habitats to highly suitable habitats. Combining the forecast results of two scenarios (SSP126 and SSP585), we can easily observe a commonality between them, which is the increase in the area of highly suitable habitat, with an average growth area exceeding 35%. From Fig. 6, it is evident that the increased area of highly suitable habitat is mainly concentrated in Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi, especially in Yunnan where the increase is most pronounced. The explanation for these results may be attributed to the following reasons: firstly, with the worsening climate change, the concentration of greenhouse gas emissions continues to increase, leading to an increase in precipitation in tropical and mid-latitude regions43. Z. striolatum, as a plant that thrives in warm and humid environments, is likely to migrate towards these regions with abundant precipitation. Secondly, Yunnan is predominantly mountainous, exhibiting significant variations in climate (including tropical rainforest, tropical seasonal moist forest, tropical montane evergreen broadleaf forest, and tropical monsoon forest) and altitude (76.4 ~ 6740 m), providing multidimensional and diverse environments for the growth of Zingiberaceae plants. Southern Yunnan has the largest area covered by primary forests, shielded from cold northern air in winter by the Hengduan Mountains and situated at low latitudes, forming a tropical humid climate36, which provides favorable conditions for the growth and reproduction of Zingiberaceae plants. Lastly, as the tolerance of each species to the range of climate change is limited, many plants need to move more than 1 km annually to adapt to climate change44. It is evident that under future climate change, the centroid position of the overall suitable habitat is consistently shifting towards the southwest direction (mainly concentrated in the Yungui Plateau region), with an average migration distance close to 200 km, further validating the observed results under future climate conditions. We also found that under both future scenarios (SSP126 and SSP585), the suitable habitat range of Z. striolatum has expanded to some extent, with this expansion primarily concentrated at the junction of the three southwestern provinces, particularly in areas such as Luoping and Qiubei in Yunnan, southwestern Qiannan in Guizhou, and Xilin in Guangxi. This suggests that these regions could serve as alternative areas for the artificial cultivation of Z. striolatum in the future.

Z. striolatum is an important medicine food homology plant in China. Due to its narrow distribution range and low root yield, artificial cultivation is difficult, so the species mainly exists in the wild2,45. In recent years, due to deteriorating natural environments and intensified human activities, the natural distribution range of Z. striolatum has been shrinking annually, leading to the depletion of its wild resources. To prevent the depletion of wild resources of Z. striolatum, appropriate conservation measures should be taken. First, uncontrolled excavation should be avoided. Secondly, artificial cultivation is an important means to achieve sustainable utilization of its resources. By planting in highly suitable areas for Z. striolatum, it can rapidly propagate and avoid the crisis of extinction. However, it is important to prioritize considering local precipitation and temperature, two dominant environmental factors, in artificial cultivation. Local precipitation conditions should be given priority, especially in areas with abundant rainfall. Furthermore, attention should be paid to local temperatures, with a preference for areas where the temperature difference is close to the range of the research results. Lastly, basic research on the growth and development dynamics of Z. striolatum should be strengthened. Corresponding seedling breeding bases should be established in highly suitable areas, actively conducting surveys, collection, preservation, and breeding of wild germplasm resources. In addition, relevant management departments should strengthen supervision in its suitable areas to consolidate existing achievements, ultimately achieving comprehensive protection and sustainable utilization of resources of Z. striolatum.

Materials and methods

Collection and processing of species geographic distribution data

We assembled geographical distribution data of 147 samples from three databases: the Chinese Virtual Herbarium (CVH, http://www.cvh.ac.cn (accessed on 23 February 2024)), the National Specimen Information Infrastructure (NSII, http://www.nsii.org.cn/2017/home.php (accessed on 25 February 2024)), and the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, https://www.gbif.org/ (accessed on 28 February 2024)). All samples were unique and had accurate latitude and longitude information (Supplementary Material 1). The latitude and longitude information of all samples was compiled into a data text file (CSV format) and imported into ArcGIS (version 10.8; ESRI, Redlands, USA). Utilize the " Spatially Rarefy Occurence Data for SDMs " function in the “SDM Toolbox” to filter out samples that are less than 5 km apart to reduce spatial autocorrelation46. As shown in Fig. 8, a total of 141 sample information points were selected for subsequent modeling.

The distribution information of Z. striolatum.

Selection and processing of environmental variables

The environmental variables related to the habitat of Z. striolatum selected for this study include bioclimatic variables, soil factors, and topographic factors. Bioclimatic variable data were downloaded from the WorldClim database (http://www.worldclim.org/ (accessed on 28 April 2022)) at a resolution of 2.5 min, encompassing 19 bioclimatic variables for present (1970–2000) and future climate scenarios. The BCC-CSM2-MR model, known for its strong performance in simulating Chinese climate, was chosen for projecting future climate changes47. Two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) scenarios were selected from the BCC-CSM2-MR model: a low carbon emission scenario (SSP126) and a high carbon emission scenario (SSP585). SSPs are widely used for predicting global social and climate scenarios, providing good results in explaining future uncertainties48. SSP585 represents a high-forcing scenario where the population is highest, technological innovation is low, energy improvements are slow, and energy demand remains high for an extended period, while SSP126 represents a low-forcing scenario49. Each SSP scenario was further divided into two time periods: 2050s (2041–2060) and 2090s (2081–2100)42. Soil variables and topographic variables were obtained from the Harmonized World Soil Database (https://www.fao.org/soils-portal/data-hub/soil-maps-and-databases/harmonized-world-soil-database-v12/en/ (accessed on 15 October 2022)) and the WorldClim database (http://www.worldclim.org/ (accessed on 18 October 2022)). All the environmental variables mentioned above contain global data, which we clipped to the borders of China using ArcGIS (version 10.8). Based on the geographical distribution information of Z. striolatum and the iterative calculation results of MaxEnt 3.4.4 software using present environmental variables, environmental variables with contribution rates greater than 0 were selected. Subsequently, the “sampling” function in ArcGIS software was used to extract the values of the variables obtained from the previous step. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis of the environmental variables was conducted using SPSS (version 26; IBM, New York, USA) software. Environmental variables with absolute correlation coefficients greater than 0.8 and small contribution rates were removed to eliminate the negative effects of collinearity among environmental variables on model50. The selected environmental variables were used for the next modeling step.

Modeling and Data Processing

This study utilized 141 geographic distribution records of Z. striolatum and 15 environmental variables imported into MaxEnt 3.4.4 software for modeling. The parameters were set as follows: 75% of occurrence data as a training dataset, and 25% as a test dataset. The Max number of background points was set to 10,000, the maximum number of iterations was set to 1,000,000, and 10-fold cross-validation was performed. The accuracy of the MaxEnt model can be evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with values ranging from 0 to 1. The AUC value greater than 0.9 indicates high accuracy51. The contribution and importance of environmental factors were determined through a jackknife test, and the optimal range reflected by the response curve of the environmental factors was identified52. The ASCII files generated from the MaxEnt prediction analysis represented the probability of Z. striolatum occurrence, denoted as P, with values ranging from 0 to 1 53. A threshold of P ≥ 0.5 was set to determine the optimal suitable range of important environmental factors, with the peak of the probability of occurrence serving as the adaptive threshold for these factors42. The ASCII files were imported into ArcGIS (version 10.8) and converted into raster files. The Reclassify function in the raster calculator of the Spatial Analyst Tools was used to merge the predictions. The resulting suitability index was categorized into four classes using Jenks’ natural breaks method: non-suitable habitat (0 ~ 0.1), low suitable habitat (0.1 ~ 0.3), moderately suitable habitat (0.3 ~ 0.5), and highly suitable habitat (0.5 ~ 1)54. Finally, the attribute table of the reclassified files was used to calculate the ratio of the grid numbers for each class to the total number of grids, thereby estimating the potential habitat area for Z. striolatum.

Centroid migration

The centroid of the suitable habitat of Z. striolatum in different scenarios at different periods was calculated using the “Mean Center” function in the spatial statistics module of ArcGIS (version 10.8). This analysis clarified the centroid position and changes in the suitable habitat of Z. striolatum under different climate change conditions, enabling further analysis of centroid migration trends55.

Conclusions

This study used Pearson analysis, Geographic Information System (ArcGIS), and Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) model to predict the suitable habitat of Z. striolatum under present climate and future climate scenarios (SSP126 and SSP585) for two periods (2050s and 2090s). Additionally, this study conducted an in-depth analysis of the dominant environmental variables influencing the distribution of Z. striolatum. The research indicated that Precipitation of driest quarter, Precipitation of wettest quarter, Temperature annual range, and Mean diurnal range are the dominant environmental factors affecting the potential distribution of Z. striolatum, with suitable ranges of 39.0 ~ 473.8 mm, 593.2 ~ 1269.4 mm, 9.8 ~ 28.6 °C, and 6.5 ~ 9.6 °C, respectively. Under present climate conditions, the potential geographic distribution of Z. striolatum is mainly located in western and southern Yunnan, northern and western Guangxi, Guangdong, Fujian, and central Hainan, covering a total area of 40.57 × 104 km2. The total suitable habitat area of Z. striolatum does not show significant increase or decrease under future climate change scenarios, but its centroid is projected to shift towards the southwest (Yungui Plateau) at higher altitudes. In the future, three actions can be taken to protect Z. striolatum. Firstly, government agencies should enhance supervision and management to prevent the unrestricted excavation of wild resources. Secondly, wild resources of this species should be introduced and cultivated artificially. Lastly, there should be an emphasis on basic research, actively conducting investigations, collection, preservation, and breeding of resources of this species. The findings of this study can provide a scientific basis for the development of rational ecological conservation and cultivation strategies for Z. striolatum, a medicine food homology plant.
