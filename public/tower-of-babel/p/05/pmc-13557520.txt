From traumatic necrotizing fasciitis to central nervous system involvement: Expanding the clinical spectrum of Apophysomyces variabilis
Authors: S Kataria, N Vyas, SK Kumar, A Bairathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpecies belonging to the genus Apophysomyces are thermotolerant filamentous fungi within the order Mucorales and family Mucoraceae. These organisms have increasingly been recognized as clinically significant pathogens due to their ability to cause severe, often fatal invasive infections. While Apophysomyces species classically cause post-traumatic necrotizing soft-tissue infections in immunocompetent individuals, central nervous system involvement is rare and typically reported in immunocompromised hosts. This case series uniquely juxtaposes two distinct clinical presentations of Apophysomyces infection: post-traumatic necrotizing fasciitis and neurological infection in a patient with cancer. These cases underscore the broad clinical spectrum of the disease and highlight the diagnostic challenges associated with Apophysomyces infections. The definitive diagnosis of these infections continues to rely primarily on fungal culture and histopathology, with molecular methods increasingly used to complement conventional approaches. Early recognition, timely microbiological diagnosis, and prompt antifungal therapy combined with surgical management are critical.

Introduction

Apophysomyces species are thermotolerant fungi of the order Mucorales that have emerged as important causes of mucormycosis in tropical and subtropical regions.[12] Reports describing central nervous system (CNS) involvement by Apophysomyces, particularly mimicking neoplasms, remain exceedingly limited.[3] This case series uniquely juxtaposes two distinct clinical presentations: post-trauma necrotizing fasciitis and intracranial mucormycosis mimicking glioma, underscoring the broad clinical spectrum and diagnostic challenges associated with Apophysomyces variabilis (A. variabilis), a rare but clinically significant pathogen.

Case Series

Case 1

A 58-year-old woman presented with a skin ulcer over the right forearm. One month earlier, she had sustained a crush injury after falling into a water tank, with a concrete slab falling on her forearm. She underwent open reduction and internal fixation with Titanium Elastic Nailing System (TENS) for forearm fractures, followed by surgical debridement. Initial investigations revealed leucocytosis (17.8 × 103/µL), anemia (hemoglobin: 9.7 g/dL), hyponatremia (132.7 mmol/L), and hypokalemia (3.0 mmol/L).

Despite repeated surgical interventions over 2 months, the wound progressively worsened, developing blackish discoloration of the margins and subcutaneous tissue devitalization, suggestive of necrotizing soft-tissue infection. Empirical levofloxacin (500 mg; 12 hourly) and linezolid (600 mg; 12 hourly) were initiated. Aerobic bacterial culture of a pus specimen showed no growth; however, KOH microscopy demonstrated thin hyaline septate hyphae with acute-angle branching, prompting empirical antifungal therapy.

Fungal culture on Sabouraud’s dextrose agar (SDA) incubated at 25°C yielded rapid growth within 7 days, producing dense, white, cottony, and fluffy colonies that filled the entire slant on both tubes. The colony surface appeared uniformly white, consistent with the macroscopic morphology seen with Mucorales species in clinical mycology cultures. Lactophenol cotton blue examination demonstrated broad pauci-septate hyphae with characteristic funnel-shaped apophyses and pear-shaped sporangia suggestive of Apophysomyces species [Figure 1a and b]. As MALDI-TOF (matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight) failed to identify the isolate, it was referred to the National Culture Collection of Pathogenic Fungi, Chandigarh, where it was identified phenotypically as Apophysomyces variabilis (accession number IL6368).

Treatment was escalated to liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) along with topical antifungal therapy for 3 weeks. At the 1-month follow-up, the patient showed marked clinical improvement.

Case 2

A 52-year-old woman presented with a 2-day history of progressive forgetfulness and loss of insight, associated with severe headache for 4–5 days. She had a history of vertigo and recently diagnosed hypertension on treatment. There was no history of fever, seizures, trauma, diabetes/other immunocompromising conditions, and the patient was, therefore, considered immunocompetent at the time of presentation based on the available clinical information. On examination, she was conscious and oriented (Glasgow Coma Score [GCS]: 15/15) with no focal neurological deficits.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (15.2 × 103/µL) with neutrophilia (91.1%), suggestive of an infectious/inflammatory process, along with thrombocytopenia (141000/µL). Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a large intra-axial peripheral enhancing cystic space-occupying lesion in the left temporal lobe with perilesional edema and mass effect. A provisional diagnosis of left temporal cystic glioma was made, and anticonvulsants (phenytoin 300 mg at bedtime and levetiracetam 500 mg; 12 hourly) along with dexamethasone (4 mg; 8 hourly). The patient subsequently underwent left temporal craniotomy with excision of the lesion and duroplasty.

Intraoperatively, the lesion was grayish-white, soft, friable, and moderately vascular. Histopathology demonstrated broad pauci-septate hyphae with right-angle branching, angioinvasion, vascular thrombosis, and tissue necrosis, consistent with mucormycosis [Figure 1d]. Direct Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) examination was negative; however, culture on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) yielded A. variabilis after one week, identified by slide culture morphology [Figure 1c].

The patient received liposomal amphotericin B (200 mg/day for 2 days, followed by 300 mg/day for 3 weeks) with monitoring of renal function and electrolytes. Postoperative imaging showed a reduction in lesion size without progression. She was discharged in stable condition and remained neurologically well with clinical and radiological improvement at 1-month follow-up. No definite portal of entry or antecedent trauma could be identified. However, based on the known epidemiology of A. variabilis, infection is presumed to occur via environmental acquisition, most commonly through traumatic inoculation, although no definite history of trauma or portal of entry was identified in this case.

Discussion

Apophysomyces variabilis is an increasingly recognized cause of mucormycosis in immunocompetent individuals, usually following traumatic inoculation of contaminated soil. Most reported infections are cutaneous or soft-tissue infections that may progress to necrotizing fasciitis because of the organism’s angioinvasive nature, causing vascular thrombosis, tissue necrosis, and dissemination.[45] In contrast, our series demonstrates a broader clinical spectrum, including CNS involvement and radiologically deceptive presentations. Although cerebral disease is rare, its occurrence highlights the potential for dissemination beyond the primary site. The absence of an identifiable portal of entry in patients with isolated CNS disease, both in our series and previous reports, suggests unrecognized traumatic inoculation/hematogenous spread. These findings indicate that Apophysomyces is not solely a post-traumatic cutaneous pathogen but a potentially aggressive invasive mold capable of causing deep-seated disease in otherwise healthy hosts.

An important observation was that invasive Apophysomyces infection may closely mimic neoplastic lesions on neuroimaging, presenting as enhancing mass lesions with surrounding edema and necrosis. Consequently, fungal infection should be considered in the differential diagnosis of atypical intracranial mass lesions, particularly in patients from endemic regions/in those with a history of trauma, environmental exposure/rapidly progressive disease.

Diagnosis of A. variabilis remains challenging. Culture-based identification is often difficult because the organism may fail to sporulate on routine media and require specialized media, higher incubation temperatures, or prolonged incubation.[6] Histopathological examination and direct microscopy provide rapid presumptive evidence of mucormycosis, demonstrating broad, pauci-septate hyphae with irregular branching, unlike the narrow, septate hyphae with acute-angle branching characteristic of Aspergillus species [Table 1]. However, altered hyphal morphology may lead to misinterpretation, as observed in Case 1, making fungal culture an important confirmatory tool. MALDI-TOF MS failed to identify our isolate, likely reflecting the limited representation of Apophysomyces species in current databases. In addition, reliable serological assays for Mucorales are lacking. These limitations highlight the need to integrate clinical, radiological, histopathological, culture, and molecular findings for accurate diagnosis [Table 2].[78910]

Our findings emphasize three key clinical messages: Apophysomyces infection should not be regarded solely as a cutaneous pathogen; radiological appearances may be misleading and necessitate tissue diagnosis; and early multidisciplinary management incorporating surgery, mycological confirmation, and prompt antifungal therapy remains crucial for optimizing outcomes.

This study has certain limitations. Antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed, precluding assessment of isolate-specific susceptibility patterns and optimization of targeted antifungal therapy. Additionally, the follow-up period was limited to one month, precluding evaluation of long-term outcomes, including recurrence and delayed complications. Nevertheless, given the rarity of A. variabilis infection, particularly with CNS involvement, our series adds to the limited literature and highlights important diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

In conclusion, Apophysomyces variabilis is an emerging cause of mucormycosis in immunocompetent individuals and may present with unusual manifestations, including soft-tissue infections and intracranial lesions. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for this pathogen even in immunocompetent hosts, as early recognition and definitive microbiological diagnosis are crucial for optimal outcomes.
