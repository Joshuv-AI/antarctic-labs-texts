Management of sliding suction loss during keratorefractive lenticule extraction performed with VisuMax 800 femtosecond laser using CIRCLE software: a case report

Background

Keratorefractive lenticule extraction (KLEx) has gained popularity as a flapless refractive procedure. One of the most critical intraoperative complications is suction loss, which occurs in approximately 0.2% to 4.4% of cases [1]. The VisuMax 800 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) features a faster laser frequency, reducing the procedure time to approximately 10 s and theoretically lowering the risk of suction loss [2]. However, as suggested by a report indicating that approximately 35% of suction loss incidents during KLEx with the VisuMax 500 occurred after the first 10 s,3 the risk of suction loss remains a significant concern even with the faster VisuMax 800.

Standard manufacturer protocols for managing intraoperative suction loss are well-documented, and adherence to these guidelines has been shown to yield favorable clinical outcomes across various stages of the procedure [3, 4]. However, a more complex scenario arises when the globe moves significantly without immediate and complete detachment from the contact glass. In such instances, the laser may continue to cut as the eye shifts, leading to a partially or entirely decentered and irregular lenticule interface.

This phenomenon, which occurs when the eye moves while still partially engaged with the cone, particularly during laser delivery, can be defined as sliding suction loss. Unlike a clean break in suction that triggers an immediate laser halt, sliding suction loss results in a decentered lamellar plane. This poses a significant risk of irregular or double cuts if immediate re-docking and laser application are attempted. In this report, we describe a case of sliding suction loss during the cap cut stage, which was successfully managed the following day by utilizing the CIRCLE software.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male presented for refractive surgery. Preoperative manifest refraction was − 4.25 diopters (D) sphere and − 0.25 D cylinder at axis 90° in the left eye. KLEx surgery was initiated using the VisuMax 800. The lenticule cut, lenticule side cut, and the majority of the cap cut (approximately 5.5 mm in diameter) proceeded without issue (Fig. 1A). During the peripheral portion of the cap cut, however, the patient experienced a sudden and forceful Bell’s phenomenon, causing the eye to rotate superiorly. Notably, suction was not lost immediately upon the initiation of the eye movement. Instead, the suction remained partially engaged as the globe shifted, leading to a sliding suction loss. This resulted in the peripheral cap cut being completed in a decentered, inferior position relative to the intended visual axis (Fig. 1B). Following the completion of this decentered cap, the system proceeded to the cap-side cut (incision making). Suction loss finally occurred when the incision was 48.5% complete (Fig. 1C).

The clinical situation posed a significant challenge that could not be safely addressed by the standard re-docking protocol. If the procedure had been resumed using the manufacturer’s automated protocol, the system would have attempted to complete only the remaining portion of the incision. However, because the previously created cap was already decentered inferiorly, a centered re-docking would have resulted in a new incision that failed to intersect with the existing cap margin, rendering lenticule extraction through the incision impossible.

Conversely, re-initiating the entire procedure from the cap cut stage to ensure alignment was also deemed high-risk; performing a second cap cut over the existing one would have likely created an irregular lamellar plane or a double cut, leading to permanent corneal irregularities. Given these risks, the procedure was aborted. To resolve the irregular interface and achieve successful lenticule extraction without compromising corneal integrity, a rescue procedure was scheduled for the following day using the CIRCLE software.

During this secondary procedure, the junction diameter was configured to 5.5 mm, with a vertical offset of ± 5 μm (Fig. 2A). To ensure surgical continuity, the cap diameter and incision parameters were set identically to the original KLEx settings: a 7.4 mm cap diameter and a 2.0 mm incision at the 135-degree position (Fig. 2B). Following the laser application, a spatula was used to dissect the upper and lower planes of the lenticule, including the junction cut. The dissection proceeded without any complications or resistance, allowing for the complete extraction of the lenticule and the successful completion of the KLEx procedure.

On the first postoperative day following the rescue procedure, the patient’s uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) was − 0.04 logMAR, and the manifest refraction was + 0.25 D sphere. By the one-month follow-up, the UDVA remained stable at -0.04 logMAR, with a manifest refraction of 0 sphere − 0.25 cylinder at axis 170°. Slit-lamp examination showed a clear cornea with a well-positioned interface and no specific abnormalities. Furthermore, dual Scheimpflug tomography confirmed a well-centered treatment area without irregular astigmatism (Fig. 3).

Discussion and conclusions

While the faster laser frequency of the VisuMax 800 reduces the suction time to approximately 10 s, intraoperative suction loss remains a significant concern. Generally, when suction loss occurs, surgeons follow established protocols, identical to those used with the VisuMax 500, which vary depending on the stage of the procedure. If managed appropriately according to these guidelines, favorable refractive outcomes can still be achieved in most standard cases [1].

However, this case presented a unique challenge that the standard manufacturer protocol could not safely address: sliding suction loss. Unlike a complete and immediate detachment, where the laser stops instantly, a sliding suction loss occurs when the globe shifts while suction is partially maintained. In our case, this led to the completion of a decentered peripheral cap cut.

If we had followed the standard protocol by simply re-docking and resuming the treatment, the system would have attempted to complete the remaining portion of the incision based on a newly centered position. Because the existing cap was already decentered, this new centered incision would have been physically separated from the cap margin, making it impossible to identify the interface or extract the lenticule [1]. Furthermore, re-initiating the cap cut from the beginning was not a viable option, as it posed a high risk of creating a double cut or an irregular lamellar plane. Such complications can lead to irregular astigmatism, making any subsequent surgical intervention extremely difficult [5].

To navigate this technical dilemma, we utilized the CIRCLE software. Originally, CIRCLE was developed as a dedicated enhancement tool for the VisuMax system, designed to convert a KLEx cap into a LASIK-like flap for treating myopic regression or residual refractive errors [6]. However, its utility as a therapeutic “rescue” tool for intraoperative KLEx complications has recently emerged in the literature [5, 7].

Sachdev et al. [5] reported a case where CIRCLE was used to manage suction loss specifically during the cap side cut stage. Furthermore, Ganesh et al. [7] described the use of CIRCLE to manage retained lenticule fragments and false-plane dissections. While these studies established the feasibility of CIRCLE for KLEx rescue, our case is unique in its etiology. To our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the use of CIRCLE software specifically for rescuing a KLEx procedure following a sliding suction loss that resulted in a completed but decentered cap cut.

By configuring the junction diameter to 5.5 mm with a minimal depth of 5 μm, we were able to create a precise bridge to the existing decentered lamellar plane. This allowed us to bypass the misaligned incision and the decentered peripheral cap edge from the initial attempt. The successful extraction of the lenticule without resistance and the excellent postoperative visual recovery confirm that the CIRCLE software is not just an enhancement tool, but a critical rescue strategy for complex, non-standard suction loss scenarios.

It is crucial to emphasize that the application of this CIRCLE rescue strategy is strictly limited by the specific surgical stage in which the complication occurs. In this case, because the sliding suction loss took place during the peripheral cap cut, the deeper corneal structural integrity remained uncompromised. However, if such incomplete suction loss with globe movement happens during the lenticule posterior surface cut, ongoing laser scanning would inevitably create irregular, off-axis incisions in the deep stroma. This could result in severe lamellar irregularities where a CIRCLE rescue would be completely contraindicated. Thus, this approach should not be generalized as a routine procedure for all suction loss scenarios. Instead, it must be defined as an individualized rescue measure indicated solely under specific circumstances after careful evaluation.

In conclusion, “sliding suction loss” is a distinct challenge even with the high-speed VisuMax 800, where a decentered but completed cap makes standard re-docking protocols risky due to potential double cuts. This case demonstrates that the CIRCLE software is a highly effective rescue strategy, enabling successful lenticule extraction by bridging misaligned lamellar planes. Our findings establish CIRCLE as an essential tool for managing complex intraoperative complications, extending its utility beyond simple refractive enhancement.
