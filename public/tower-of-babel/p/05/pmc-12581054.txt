Obstetric Neonatal Interest Teaching (ONIT): multi-disciplinary learning in perinatal medicine
Authors: Evangelia Myttaraki, Carolina Zorro, Ghalia Ashoor, Anne Greenough
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterprofessional education is essential in perinatal medicine, where diverse professionals must collaborate to meet the urgent and complex needs of perinatal care. To address this, King’s College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust launched ‘Obstetric Neonatal Interest Teaching (ONIT)’, a trainee-led regional study day. The event engaged more than 150 participants across specialties, featuring expert talks, parental perspectives and interactive simulations. Faculty from two major London Trusts ensured breadth and inclusivity. Feedback demonstrated excellent organisation and high satisfaction with content and relevance. ONIT highlights the value of trainee-led multi-disciplinary learning in enhancing teamwork, knowledge and maternal–neonatal outcomes.Keywords: Neonatology, Child Health, Maternity, Women's health

Introduction

Interprofessional education in healthcare brings together professionals from different backgrounds to learn from each other and work with families and communities to deliver high-quality care.1 This requires open communication, respect for diverse perspectives, understanding of each discipline and regular collaboration. Such teamwork strengthens health systems and improves outcomes across specialties.2

In perinatal medicine, multi-disciplinary learning is especially important due to the large number of professionals involved, the urgent nature of the field and the dual needs of mother and baby. These challenges make collaborative learning vital in this sector.3

This year, King’s College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust launched ‘Obstetric Neonatal Interest Teaching (ONIT)’, a trainee-led regional study day. ONIT promotes collaborative learning in perinatal care, enhances knowledge, strengthens teamwork, bridges prenatal and postnatal care and highlights the role of multi-disciplinary practice in improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.4

Methods

ONIT was open to perinatal care professionals of all levels, including obstetric, neonatal and paediatric doctors and trainees, fetal medicine specialists, anaesthetists, midwives and nurses. The full-day event featured three sessions led by a 25-member multi-disciplinary faculty from King’s College Hospital and Guy’s & St Thomas’ Hospital in London. Representation across specialties ensured relevance for the whole perinatal team, with promotion through trainee networks, departmental meetings and word-of-mouth helping attract a broad, motivated audience.

The agenda followed the perinatal journey from pre conception to post delivery, combining short talks with parents’ perspectives shared in collaboration with Bliss charity. Interactive staged simulations on antenatal counselling, the use of LifeStart in preterm deliveries and the stabilisation and immediate management of gastroschisis further enhanced learning and audience engagement (figure 1).

A feedback link was shared with all participants at the end of the day. A certificate of attendance was provided.

Results

A total of more than 150 participants attended ONIT. The overall organisation rating was 98.7%. The overall likelihood of reattending a similar event in the future was 98.6%. Based on the Likert scale, all three sessions were ranked as 4.89, 4.91 and 4.89 out of 5 for the quality of the talks provided.

ONIT was very well received among the perinatal community. All participants mentioned that the ONIT content was very useful to their clinical role, they would highly recommend this to their colleagues and they would definitely reattend a similar event in the future (figure 2) .

Discussion

ONIT showcased the value of trainee-led, multi-disciplinary education in perinatal care. Designed to foster cross-specialty learning and teamwork, it featured a diverse faculty from two major London Trusts, modelling collaborative practice and reinforcing the importance of interprofessional care (Figure 3).

Take home messages.

The programme was carefully structured to fit into a standard working day, balancing a wide range of topics with smooth transitions to maintain engagement. Content was tailored to a mixed audience, focusing on shared goals and communication rather than specialty silos. The trainee-led format also offered leadership opportunities and highlighted the role of junior clinicians in driving innovation.

While the broad agenda maximised inclusivity, it limited time for in-depth discussion. Questions and answers sessions were brief, though informal exchanges occurred during breaks. Some participants may benefit from follow-up events with more specialty-specific content, particularly those seeking more advanced knowledge.

Conclusion

ONIT’s success is proof that multi-disciplinary team learning is highly desired and achievable in perinatal medicine. The feedback demonstrated how well-planned, inclusive and trainee-led educational events can enhance learning in complex clinical environments. We recommend and encourage the implementation of ONIT in other hospital affiliations and networks in order to provide opportunities for interprofessional education, communication and collaboration among specialists and build stronger perinatal teams.
