Chronic lung infection by Achromobacter xylosoxidans in a patient with cystic fibrosis successfully treated with cefiderocol as a pre and post-lung transplant regimen
Authors: Susana Ramos-Oliveira, Rodrigo Alonso-Moralejo, Virginia Pérez, Carlos Andrés Quezada, Alicia de Pablo, Irene Muñoz-Gallego, José Tiago Silva, José María Aguado, Francisco López-Medrano
Published: 2025

Sir,

Cystic fibrosis (CF) patients’ life expectancy has consistently increased in the last decade [1]. However, it remains a life-shortening disease due to deterioration of lung function caused by acute respiratory infections and chronic lung inflammation [1–3]. The main bacterial pathogens causing respiratory infections in CF patients are, in order of prevalence, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Haemophilus influenzae, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia and Achromobacter spp. [3].

Kerem et al. [3] analyzed the clinical outcomes of Achromobacter spp. infection in 2,093 CF patients who had never undergone lung transplantation: compared with patients without Achromobacter spp. infection, a higher prevalence of severe disease, i.e., lower forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) percent of predicted was observed, as well as lower body mass index and higher treatment burden. These results prove a link to disease severity, but it remains unclear whether the infection is causally related to clinical worsening.

Therapeutic options for Achromobacter spp. infections are limited due to its several intrinsic resistance mechanisms, biofilm-production capacity, and ability to acquire other resistance mechanisms [2,4,5].

We report the case of a CF patient chronically infected with Achromobacter xylosoxidans who received cefiderocol in a peri-lung transplant regimen, with clinical and microbiological success.

Clinical case

Thirty-six-year-old woman with CF diagnosed at 6 months of age, with chronic colonization with various bacteria since early childhood, the most persistent being methicillin-susceptible S. aureus and A. xylosoxidans. Treatment of respiratory exacerbations was challenging due to the patient’s allergies to multiple beta-lactams, including imipenem.

A. xylosoxidans was initially susceptible to multiple antibiotics, but over the years because of numerous antimicrobial treatments, it became pandrug-resistant (PDR) to all tested antibiotics - cotrimoxazole, minocycline, aminoglycosides, quinolones, and all beta-lactams, including imipenem, ceftolozane/tazobactam and ceftazidime/avibactam. After confirming in-vitro cefiderocol susceptibility and discussing the case with the Allergy Department, the patient received three courses of treatment (2 g every 8 hours) for 14 to 21 days, in combination with minocycline, with or without levofloxacin. Following these treatment courses, clinical and radiological improvement was observed (Figure 1A, B and C), and subsequent isolates regained imipenem susceptibility, indicating the eradication of PDR A. xylosoxidans.

Due to severe respiratory insufficiency, a multidisciplinary decision to proceed with a bilateral lung transplant was made, and cefiderocol was requested (on compassionate use) to ensure immediate postoperative treatment as a preemptive strategy. Thirty-two months after the initial course of cefiderocol, the patient underwent a bilateral lung transplant; A. xylosoxidans was the only relevant pathogen isolated in the explanted lungs, showing susceptibility to imipenem. As planned, the patient received cefiderocol (2 g every 8 hours), linezolid (due to previous S. aureus infection), and inhaled colistin for four weeks postoperatively. Immunosuppression was initiated with basiliximab for induction, followed by tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone. Follow-up bronchoscopy did not grow pathogenic bacteria, and hospital discharge occurred one month after transplant as planned.

Two years post-transplant, the patient remains off oxygen therapy and performs daily activities without dyspnea, reporting only occasional episodes of cough, mainly non-productive. Sputum cultures are consistently negative for Achromobacter spp. or other pathogenic bacteria (Figure 2), lung function remains stable with a mean forced vital capacity of 3.445 liters and FEV1 of 3.125 liters, and there is no evidence of relevant radiological lung infiltrates (Figure 1D).

Lung transplantation is considered in selected patients with CF when all other therapeutic options have proven insufficient; however, infection at the time of transplant increases the risk of postoperative infectious complications, especially due to the intensive immunosuppression required in the early stages, and is associated with a high likelihood of early re-colonization [6].

The challenge of successfully eradicating persistent lung infections in CF patients, combined with the increase in the prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacilli, emphasizes the need to optimize antibiotic strategies for this particular population. Cefiderocol is a novel siderophore cephalosporin with broad-spectrum activity, including against MDR Gram-negative bacilli.

Clinical evidence on the use of cefiderocol in CF patients or lung transplant recipients is scarce, relying mostly on case reports of compassionate use [4,5]. Warner et al. [4] reported eight CF patients infected with extensively MDR A. xylosoxidans treated with cefiderocol; of note, six patients received it as a peri-lung transplant regimen. Although most patients accomplished a clinical response, microbiological clearance occurred in a single patient, who received concomitant meropenem-vaborbactam and bacteriophage therapy. More recently, Persaud et al. [5] reported treatment with cefiderocol in fifteen lung transplant recipients, of whom only one was infected with Achromobacter spp., and achieved microbiological success after 42 perioperative days of cefiderocol.

In our patient, compassionate use of cefiderocol successfully treated pulmonary exacerbations caused by PDR A. xylosoxidans, as evidenced by clinical and radiological improvements. Eradication of this PDR strain enabled the patient’s survival while on the transplant waiting list, ultimately leading to a successful bilateral transplant. Our experience suggests that early intensive treatment with cefiderocol after lung transplant may be an effective and safe option in the peri-transplant setting, even in well-established chronic infections.
