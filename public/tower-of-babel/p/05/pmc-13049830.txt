Porphyromonas gingivalis-derived outer membrane vesicles travel from oral to distant organs via blood circulation in a time-dependent manner
Authors: Congcong Lv, Kaikai Shi, Zixin Guo, Pingchan Luo, Zhe Wu, Pei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis is closely related to a variety of systemic diseases. Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is one of the key pathogens of periodontitis. The influence of P. gingivalis outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) on systemic diseases have become an emerging research topic. However, the kinetic distribution of P. gingivalis OMVs in vivo is still unknown.MethodsKinetic biodistribution of P. gingivalis OMVs in mice with or without periodontitis was analyzed by Near-infrared fluorescence imaging. Custom Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs were developed and verified for quantitative analysis of various organs. Systemic and liver inflammatory indexes of mice were explored by ELISA and blood routine tests.ResultsOur results showed that P. gingivalis OMVs mainly distributed to different systemic organs as time-dependent manner through blood circulation. The accumulation in liver and lung was particularly obvious at 6 h after administration, but lung signals was absent at 24 h. The distribution of P. gingivalis OMVs was associated with the destruction of periodontal tissue. Spatially, P. gingivalis OMVs were detected in the intercellular matrix and cytoplasm of target cells, promoting systemic and local inflammation.ConclusionThese findings advance our understanding of the distribution route of P. gingivalis OMVs in systemic, providing insights for developing new therapeutic strategies for patientis with periodontitis and systemic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07979-3.Keywords: Outer membrane vesicles, Periodontitis, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Kinetic biodistribution, Blood circulation

Background

Periodontal disease is a chronic infectious illness connected to a diverse range of complex bacterial species. It lead to the degradation of periodontal tissues, including the gums, periodontal ligaments, alveolar bone, and cementum [1]. Gingivitis represents the earliest phase of periodontal disease and may advance into periodontitis. This later stage typically triggers gingival bleeding, tooth mobility and even tooth loss. Many studies have indicated a strong relationship between periodontal disease and various systemic conditions, which include, but are not restricted to, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [2], rheumatoid arthritis [3], Alzheimer’s disease [4], and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [5, 6].

P. gingivalis is regarded as the principal pathogen behind chronic periodontitis. Together with Tannerella forsythia and Treponema denticola, it constitutes the “red complex,” a group of bacteria that undermines the host innate immune response and amplifies inflammatory reactions. The major virulence factors of P. gingivalis are gingipains, a class of cysteine proteinases including arginine-gingipains A and B (RgpA and RgpB), along with lysine-gingipain (Kgp) [7]. These enzymes can provide multiple modes of cell death, stimulate inflammation, and promote alveolar bone resorption. As periodontal disease advances, inflammatory tissue damage increases the production of gingival crevicular fluid. This fluid carries broken-down collagen and heme compounds into the gingival sulcus [8]. Some bacteria preferentially consume these molecules, disrupting the ecological balance of the microbial community within the gingival sulcus. Also, species beneficial to oral health are less competitive under these altered microenvironment, further shifting the composition of the microbiota and intensifying inflammation, thereby driving the development of periodontitis [7]. Notably, it has been suggested that oral pathogenic bacteria may translocate to the intestine via swallowing and influence systemic health by modifying the gut microbial environment, though the precise mechanisms remain unclear [9, 10].

Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are spherical, bilayer nanostructures with diameters typically between 50 and 250 nm. They are released by gram-negative bacteria [11]. These vesicles consist of proteins, lipopolysaccharides, phospholipids, DNA, and periplasmic constituents, all encapsulated within an outer membrane [12]. It is well established that OMVs produced by P. gingivalis significantly contribute to the development of periodontitis [13]. Their functions include aiding in stress adaptation, acquiring nutrients, and mediating interactions among bacteria and host cells [14].

In some respects, the systemic effects of P. gingivalis OMVs could be greater than those of the bacterium itself [15]. Numerous constituents within these vesicles act as virulence factors, promoting host cell damage, immune evasion, cellular invasion, and antibiotic resistance. Furthermore, the virulence factors encapsulated within P. gingivalis OMVs offer several advantages, such as protecting against proteolysis and degradation, facilitating long-distance transport, and coordinating secretion with other bacterial effectors [16]. Nonetheless, the dynamic distribution of P. gingivalis OMVs in each organ is still unclear. Further research is needed to investigate how P. gingivalis OMVs affect systemic inflammation and contribute to the pathogenesis of systemic diseases.

This research aims to investigate whether the P. gingivalis OMVs distributes from oral site to whole body. In an effort to examine the biodistribution kinetics of P. gingivalis OMVs in various organs in vivo and its pathological effect on liver, we administered a single injection of P. gingivalis OMVs either into the periodontal membrane or via intragastric administration (Scheme 1). Additionally, the quantity of P. gingivalis OMVs was quantified with the help of a specific anti-P. gingivalis OMV antibody. Additionally, we investigated the systemic and liver inflammation induced by P. gingivalis OMVs over time to better understand their long-distance delivery route and the impact they have on liver function.

Materials and methods

Animals

Wild-type female (C57BL/6J) mice and SKH1-E hairless mice (Strain #2022–0301) were purchased from Zhuhai Baitry Tong biological Technology CO., LTD. All mice were maintained within a specific-pathogen-free facility. SKH1-E hairless mice were used to observe Cy7-Labeled P.gingivalis OMVs on IVIS spectrum and C57BL/6J mice were used in other experiments. The animal protocol (Number IACUC-MIS20230041) was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University. All animal studies adhered to the relevant ethical guidelines for vertebrate research.

Mice were euthanized by placing them into a closed chamber prefilled with carbon dioxide (CO2). This process ensured loss of consciousness quickly followed by death, minimizing pain and suffering. Every effort was made to ensure that animals were completely unconscious prior to cessation of respiration.

Periodontitis was induced by ligation as described previously [17]. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized by isoflurane and fixed on animal-specific fixed plates in the prone position. The oral cavity was disinfected with 75% ethanol, and a 4–0 silk thread was ligated around the left upper second molar and fixed with a surgical knot. All the knots were placed on the palatal side. After the ligation was completed, the mouse’s tongue was slightly pulled out of the mouth and placed in a warmer place until the mouse woke up. The mice were fed normal chow and had free access to drinking water. If the silk thread was loose or the thread knot was incomplete, the mouse was excluded from the experiment.

Mice were randomly grouped namely PBS group, periodontal membrane injection (PMI) group and intragastrical (IG) group with or without periodontitis (PD/Non-PD).

Preparation of P. gingivalis OMVs

P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 was cultured under anaerobic conditions (10% CO2, 10% H2, 80% N2) on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar medium enriched with 5% defibrinated sheep blood, 5 µg/mL hemin, 0.1% vitamin K1, and 0.5 mg/mL yeast extract. Bacterial cell–free outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) were purified from P. gingivalis via ultracentrifugation, as outlined in the accompanying schematic diagram (Fig. S1a) [18]. OMVs were isolated from P. gingivalis by exobacteria OMV isolation kit for gram negative bacteria (System Biosciences Co., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Relevant reagents detected by test paper were neutral liquids. Thirty milliliter of bacterial liquid was collected and centrifuged twice at 5000 × g for 20 min at 4℃. The supernatant was collected and passed through 0.45-µm-pore-size and 0.22-µm-pore-size filters successively. The crude P. gingivalis OMVs were concentrated by ultracentrifugation (150,000 × g, 4℃, 3 h) using Optima XE-100 and a Type 45 Ti rotor, then purified by iodixanol-based density gradient ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 4℃, 18 h) using an SW 40 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). After washing the column three times with binding buffer, the P. gingivalis OMVs were eluted and collected with the OMV elution buffer, and stored at −80℃. The concentration was determined by BCA protein analysis kit (BestBio. CO., China) before use.

Identification of P. gingivalis OMVs

The extracted P. gingivalis OMVs were collected and the diameter and particle number were measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Particle Metrix Co., Germany). P. gingivalis OMVs were fixed and observed with a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan). Proteomics analysis of P. gingivalis OMVs were detected by nanoLC-MS/MS (Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Western blotting was used to detected the protein expression of Kgp and Rgp.

In vivo imaging of Cy7-labeled P. gingivalis OMVs

Cy7-labeled OMVs were prepared as follows. P. gingivalis OMVs (30 µg of total protein) were incubated with 5 µM of Cy®7 Mono NHS Ester (Amersham Biosciences) for 2 h at 37 °C. Cy7-labeled samples were isolated using ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 3 h at 4 °C. Cy7-labeled OMVs were treated in SKH1-E hairless mice according to the experiment needs. In vivo fluorescence imaging of mice was performed on IVIS spectrum (Caliper Life Sciences, Hopkinton, MA) at 0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h. Detection was carried out immediately after injection of Cy7-labeled P. gingivalis OMVs near NIR imaging equipment, and this time point was defined as 0 h. After 6 h or 24 h, various organs were harvested to observe the Cy7 fluorescence. Radiant efficiency was analyzed using Live Image 3.1 software [19].

Periodontal membrane injection: 3 µL of PBS or Cy7-labeled P. gingivalis OMVs (30 µg of total protein) were injected into buccal periodontal ligament of maxillary second molar by 31G × 1 mL disposable syringe.

Production of custom Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs

Seven-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to three experimental groups: 15 µg P. gingivalis OMVs, 30 µg P. gingivalis OMVs, and a combined group receiving 15 µg Kgp (gingipain) plus 15 µg P. gingivalis OMVs. The purified OMVs were emulsified in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA; Sigma-Aldrich) and administered via intraperitoneal injection for primary immunization. Subsequent booster immunizations with identical doses were performed on days 21 and 42. On day 49, blood was collected, and serum was isolated to purify antibodies [20]. The serum was dialyzed against 20 mM sodium phosphate (PH 7.0) overnight at 4 °C, and loaded onto a HiTrap protein G affinity column (GE Healthcare) in 20 mM sodium phosphate (PH 7.0). The antibodies were eluted with 100 mM glycine–HCl (PH 2.7), and immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris–HCl (PH 9.0). The purified antibody was dialyzed against PBS overnight at 4 °C. Antibody biotinylation was performed using EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with termination by 1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.0). To remove excess biotin and ethanolamine, the biotinylated antibody was dialyzed against PBS overnight at 4 °C. The biotinylation was validated by using a streptavidin-peroxidase conjugate (Roche Applied Science) antibody that was able to bind the biotinylated antibody. Liver from P. gingivalis-infected mice (6 h post-infection) were processed for protein extraction, and antibody specificity was validated via Western blot. Gray value was quantified using ImageJ software and statistically analyzed.

Quantification of P. gingivalis OMVs associated antigenic protein using custom Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs

Ocular blood and organs of treated mice were obtained at 0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h. The organs were grinded under liquid nitrogen and serum was obtained by centrifugation. After protein extraction, double antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to determine the protein accumulation of P. gingivalis OMVs in various organs. After adding protein samples into the micropores coated with custom Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs, standards and HRP-labeled antibodies were used. Substrate TMB was used to develop color. Finally, the absorbance (OD value) was measured by enzyme-labeled instrument at the wavelength of 450 nm.

Blood routine examination, AST, ALT, IL-6 and TNF-α levels

Rectal temperature was detected in each group. Ocular blood was collected from mice after treatment for 0, 3, 6, 12, 24 h with P. gingivalis OMVs. For leukocytes and platelets counting, blood was incubated with 1% HCl or 1% ammonium oxalate, respectively, and the number of cells was counted using light microscopy. Serum was acquired by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C. The concentrations of aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine transaminase (ALT) were measured by blood biochemical profile analysis. The IL-6 and TNF-α present in the serum was analyzed by an ELISA kit (R&D Systems).

Histopathology

The organs and maxilla were immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, after which the maxilla underwent decalcification in 15% EDTA for one month. The samples were then embedded in paraffin and sliced into 4 µm sections. These sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%). Subsequently, the slides were utilized for various staining procedures including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Masson’s trichrome staining and a commercial TRAP staining kit (Sigma, 387A-1KT) were employed accordingly.

For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, antigen retrieval was performed using a specific unmasking solution (Vector Laboratories Inc., Burlingame, CA), followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100. After blocking with normal serum, endogenous peroxidase activity was suppressed by treating the sections with BLOXALL reagent (Vector Laboratories Inc., Burlingame, CA). The sections were then subjected to sequential incubation with either custom anti-P. gingivalis OMVs or an anti-MPO antibody (Abcam, USA) at dilutions previously determined to be optimal. Finally, color development was carried out using appropriate secondary antibodies and a DAB substrate kit.

The tissue sections were digitized using a Pannoramic Slice Scanner and examined with CaseViewer 2.4 software. Target regions within the tissues were identified for imaging at 200 × magnification. Initially, two trained investigators (CCL and KKS) independently examined and quantified the target cells across all acquired images. A third researcher (PY) then reviewed the cell counts to resolve any discrepancies and establish consensus. Subsequently, the numbers of inflammatory cells, osteoclasts, and MPO-positive cells were quantified per slide, and the corresponding tissue areas were calculated using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Statistical analysis

The data collected were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests were used to assess the normality of the studied parameters. Variables that do not follow normal distribution were expressed as median and were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test to compare intergroup differences. Categorical variables were expressed as number or percentage and were analyzed using the chi-square post hoc test to compare between each group. Association between measured variables was performed using the Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A p-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of P. gingivalis OMVs

The particles isolated from P. gingivalis were spherical closed lipid bilayer vesicles, which conformed to the typical structure of P. gingivalis OMVs (Fig. S1b). P. gingivalis OMVs diameters ranged between 90 and 160 nm, with the highest peak at approximately 136.6 nm (Fig. S1c). The gingipains (Kgp, RgpA, RgpB), which were anchored to outer membrane, were identified in P. gingivalis OMVs (Fig. S1d). Kgp and Rgp were detected by western blot analysis. Compared to the same protein concentration of P. gingivalis OMVs, the results showed that the contents of Kgp and Rgp in P. gingivalis OMVs were higher (Fig. S1e). We next used ImageJ software to compare protein expression of Kgp and Rgp contained in P. gingivalis supernatant and P. gingivalis OMVs by gray values (Fig. S1f). The results showed that the contents of Kgp and Rgp in P. gingivalis OMVs were 3–5 × higher at the same protein concentration, which is similar with the study of Seyama M et al. [21].

Establishment and verification of mouse PD model

In order to investigate whether the periodontal condition would affect the biodistribution of P. gingivalis OMVs in vivo, we constructed periodontitis model in C57BL/6J mice. H&E staining could find that the periodontal biological width decreases and the number of inflammatory cells in gingival tissue increases (Fig. S2a and S2b). Masson staining showed that gingival fibers in alveolar septum and gingival tissue of periodontitis model mice were seriously damaged (Fig. S2c). Trap staining of periodontal tissue proved that the number of osteoclasts in alveolar bone of periodontitis model was significantly higher than that of healthy mice, and alveolar bone absorption could be seen (Fig. S2d and S2e). Compared with healthy mice, the results of MPO immunohistochemical staining in periodontal tissue confirmed that the accumulation of myeloperoxidase in lysosomal granules of granulocytes and some monocytes in periodontal tissue of chronic periodontitis model mice increased significantly, which proved that there was oxidative stress reaction (Fig. S2f and S2g). These results showed that this animal model successfully simulated a series of pathological changes in chronic periodontitis, such as inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar bone destruction, gingival connective tissue damage, oxidative stress response.

Compared with IG group, the temperature changes in PMI group showed a trend of rising first and then falling. The rectal temperature at 3 h was obviously higher than that at 0 h, and reached the peak at 6 h, then began to decrease at 12 h, and returned to the normal level at 24 h. The results of blood routine tests showed that there was no significant difference between blood leukocytes and platelets in Non-PD IG group. Compared with Non-PD IG group, blood leukocytes and platelets in PD IG group increased significantly within 24 h. Compared with the Non-PD IG group, the Non-PD PMI group showed a fluctuating trend that the level of blood platelets and leukocytes began to rise at 3 h, peaked at 6 h, decreased at 12 h, and returned to normal at 24 h. Compared with Non-PD PMI, the levels of blood platelets and leukocytes in PD PMI group quickly reached the peak at 3 h, and then gradually decreased at 6, 12 and 24 h (Fig. 5b). Discussion

Recently, exosomes have gradually become a hot research direction [23]. And we showed that compared with P. gingivalis, P. gingivalis OMVs not only could transport long-distance through blood circulation in vivo, but also has stronger ability to carry virulence factors, especially Kgp and Rgp [21]. The major virulence factors of P. gingivalis are gingipains, a class of cysteine proteinases including arginine-gingipains A and B (RgpA and RgpB), along with lysine-gingipain (Kgp) [7]. These enzymes can provide multiple modes of cell death, stimulate inflammation, and promote alveolar bone resorption. In this study, we extracted and purified OMVs from cultured P. gingivalis in vitro, and identified the characteristics by NTA, nanoLC-MS/MS and WB. Our results showed the diameters ranged at 90–160 nm, and the specific protein (Rgp, RgpB and Kgp) of P gingivalis were all detected on the particles, indicating the characterization of P gingivalis OMVs. Similar to our study, Bonnington, K. E et al. demonstrated that OMVs can further enrich the pathogenic factors in P. gingivalis, and played a more significant role in the destruction and invasion of host cells. Moreover, the virulence factors encapsulated in P. gingivalis OMVs had many advantages, including preventing proteolysis and enhancing long-distance transportation [16].

Among them, the oral-intestinal-liver axis or blood long-distance transportation route has gradually become the key point in the discussion of exosomes of periodontal pathogens and the pathogenesis of systemic diseases [9, 24]. In previous studies, it was found that there were different opinions on the way of forming systemic distribution of exosomes of periodontal pathogens. Acharya, C et al. and Wang, T et al. believed that most exosomes of periodontal pathogens entered the intestine after oral administration, which led to the increase of mesenteric permeability by disturbing the microenvironment of intestinal microorganisms, and then made the exosomes of periodontal pathogens enter the liver from the intestine to form a long-distance transmission function [25–27]. On the contrary, Zhang et al. and Aguayo, S et al. held different views that most exosomes of periodontal pathogens entered the systemic circulation through oral periodontal pocket capillaries, and some entered the blood circulation through intestinal capillaries or portal vein to form the dynamic distribution of various organs in the whole body [15, 28]. This made the systemic distribution of exosomes of periodontal pathogens a problem to be solved. In our study, we designed PMI group and IG group to simulate the capillary route of periodontal pocket and oral route into intestine. The results showed that P. gingivalis OMVs in PMI group formed a long-distance distribution effect after 6 h, while P. gingivalis OMVs in IG group only stayed in the stomach. Our results showed that the OMVs of periodontal pathogens cannot form systemic distribution through the oral-intestinal-liver axis, and we are prone to support the point of long-distance transportation through blood circulation.

There is emerging evidence on the important role of the oral microbiome in the respiratory infection. Indigenous oral microbes in dental plaques, periodontal pockets or saliva could be inhaled into the lower respiratory tract to cause or aggravate respiratory infections and diseases such as aspiration pneumonia and COPD [29, 30]. These microbial species of oral origin can migrate to the lungs via micro‐aspiration, and may contribute to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia, and even increased severity of COVID‐19 [31]. Although the micro‐aspiration route may be the potential route for P. gingivalis OMVs to enter the lungs, the establishment of the relevant respiratory model is not yet mature, and further research is needed in the future.

Subsequently, our study found that P. gingivalis OMVs in PMI group were mainly observed in liver, lung, stomach, kidney, spleen and small intestine after 6 h. Moreover, the results of fluorescence intensity at 6 h was significantly higher than 24 h. After 24 h, P. gingivalis OMVs signals existed only in liver, stomach and kidney. These results were similar to those described in Liang Na et al. and S.C. Kim et al.’ s research, in which the drug was mainly distributed in liver, kidney, lung, spleen and other tissues at the initial stage after it entered the blood circulation through intravenous administration, but only existed in liver and kidney after 24 h [32, 33]. The main reason was that the metabolism and elimination of drugs relied mainly on the liver and kidney [34]. In the liver, the drug was absorbed into the liver cells by ingesting transporters or passive diffusion through the hepatobiliary duct, and then transferred from the liver cells to bile or portal vein blood by efflux of transporters. In the kidney, drugs were metabolized and eliminated by glomerular filtration, renal tubular secretion and reabsorption [35, 36]. Similar to epidemiological results [37], after 6 h, our study found a lot of vascularization and bleeding in the lung section, indicating that P. gingivalis OMVs may cause severe acute inflammatory reaction in the lung in a short time. Due to the discrepancy of the accumulation and retention time of P. gingivalis OMVs in different organs, we speculated that periodontitis was potentially related to many systemic diseases, but it may be more closely related to chronic diseases of liver, stomach, kidney, and acute diseases of lung and spleen.

Interestingly, the NIR imaging results of our study showed that the accumulation rank of P. gingivalis OMVs in various organs between the Non-PD PMI group and the PD PMI group after 6 h was different, but that was the same after 24 h. In order to explore the possible reasons, we referred to the studies of Mayr et al. [38] and Donis-González et al. [39]. The studies showed that it is necessary to consider the heterogeneity of samples. The NIR error may also be related to the angle of the instrument, the size of the acquisition surface, electronics and working principle, as well as the position and size of the detector. Therefore, we have a high probability of suspecting that the bias of NIR imaging leads to different ranking results between the two groups.

Subsequently, to verify this conjecture and accurately quantify the accumulation of P. gingivalis OMVs associated antigenic protein in various organs at different time periods, we designed and customized an Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs. In the production process, in order to obtain the best custom antibody and meet the purpose of specific application in mice, we extracted and purified the custom antibody from the sera of three groups of mice with antigen immune reaction after different treatments. We found that mice treated with 30 µg of P. gingivalis OMVs could produce antibodies with high specificity. To accurately quantify P. gingivalis OMVs associated antigenic protein, we innovated a method of treating mice with P. gingivalis OMVs and Kgp to induce antigen immune reaction. Our results showed that the custom antibody purified by this method had higher specificity, therefore we finally chose this protocol to customize Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs.

We further confirmed the accumulation of P. gingivalis OMVs associated antigenic protein in various organs after 6 h using custom Ab-P. gingivalis OMVs by ELISA. The results showed that the accumulation rank of P. gingivalis OMVs associated antigenic protein in Non-PD PMI group and PD PMI group were consistent, which was contradictory to results of NIR. Owing to unauthentic result of NIR technology, we suggest that specific antibodies should be applied or customized in the related researches of systemic distribution of OMVs associated antigenic protein, which can greatly reduce the error and be more convincing.

Because the OMVs of periodontal pathogens transported to the corresponding tissues or cells cannot be fully localized in NIR imaging, we applied IHC and IFC to solve this problem. Our results confirmed that P. gingivalis OMVs can enter the tissues and cells of liver, lung, spleen and kidney. On the one hand, recent reports have elucidated how OMV-transported factors manipulate the host cell trafficking machinery [40]. For example, M. catarrhalis OMVs acted as immunomodulatory molecules, delivering the outer-membrane-bound superantigen Moraxella immunoglobulin D-binding protein (MID) into B cells [41]. M. catarrhalis OMVs were internalized via receptor clustering in lipid rafts, and they activated the B cells via OMV-associated DNA, which induced Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) signaling [42]. On the other hand, the effects of exosomes of periodontal pathogens entering different locations of cells are different. The results of Juodeikis, R et al. [43] showed that most of P. gingivalis OMVs entered the cytoplasm and interstitial space of related tissues and cells. OMVs trigger inflammation via compartmentalized receptor signaling: extracellular LPS engages TLR4 to activate NF-κB, while peptidoglycan released into the cytosol is recognized by NOD1, activating AP-1. Both pathways converge to induce a robust inflammatory cytokine response [44]. According to the above literatures, we conclude that P. gingivalis OMVs may remain in cytoplasm and intercellular space through endocytosis after long-distance transmission, and played the role in promoting the production of inflammatory factors and regulating related signal pathways.

Periodontal pathogen exosomes may distribute to systemic organs through blood circulation, so we were concerned about whether they can trigger systemic effects and liver damage. In PMI group, not only rectal temperature, blood leukocytes and platelets, but also serum ALT, AST, IL-6 and TNF-α within 24 h after injection of P. gingivalis OMVs, reaching the peak at 3 h or 6 h, and returning to normal after 24 h. We speculated that the rapid changes of rectal temperature, white blood cells and platelets may be related to systemic acute inflammatory reaction [45–47]. ALT and AST are most closely associated with fat accumulation in the liver and is most commonly found in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes. Serum ALT and AST has historically been utilized as a measure of inflammation for liver cell damage in conditions including cirrhosis, hepatitis, liver cancer, and alcoholic liver disease [48–50]. The fluctuation trend of AST/ALT in the study of Hyun Young Kim et al. represented that long-term and low-dose periodontal pathogenic vesicles led to the occurrence and development of NAFLD [44]. Moreover, other studies have shown that NAFLD progression can lead to subsequent cirrhotic liver disease and end-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [51]. More importantly, interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a member of the pro-inflammatory cytokine family, induces the expression of a variety of proteins responsible for acute inflammation, and plays an important role in the proliferation and differentiation of cells in humans [52]. IL-6 directly affected endothelial cells by inducing endothelial cell adhesion molecule expression and inhibiting endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activation [53]. Being one of the most important pro-inflammatory cytokines, TNF-α participated in vasodilatation and edema formation, and leukocyte adhesion to epithelium through expression of adhesion molecules. Also, it regulated blood coagulation, contributes to oxidative stress in sites of inflammation, and indirectly induces fever [54]. The present study found an association between arterial stiffness and the TNF signaling system. TNF-α can affect the mechanical properties of arteries by increasing the stiffness of vascular endothelial cells [55], promoting proliferation [56], and stimulating collagen deposition by fibroblasts [57]. The study of Miriam Cortez-Cooper et al. showed TNF-α and IL-6 may be better indicators of early vascular changes that are associated with cardiovascular disease [58]. A series of results showed that P. gingivalis OMVs can not only be transported to all organs of the whole body in a long distance, but also caused acute inflammatory reactions and adverse effects on blood vessels of the whole body in the process.

Recently, we determined the correlation between periodontitis and NAFLD and role of P. gingivalis OMVs in NAFLD. We noticed that continuous exposure to low-dose P. gingivalis OMVs could promote hepatic injure and steatosis in NAFLD mice induced by high-fat-diet. Moreover, Seyama et al. [21] demonstrated that P. gingivalis OMVs could cause changes in glucose metabolisms in the liver and promotes the development of diabetes mellitus. Yang et al. [59] indicated that P. gingivalis OMVs could promote calcification of vascular smooth muscle cells in a concentration-dependent manner, which is a hallmark of atherosclerosis. Larsen et al. [60] found that 78 citrullinated proteins of P. gingivalis OMVs was associated with rheumatoid arthritis. Singhrao et al. [61] confirmed that P. gingivalis OMVs-derived LPS could activate glial cells, induce brain inflammation, and correlate with Alzheimer’s disease. In the future, we will further solve the damage to other organs caused by continuous exposure to chronic retention or clearance of P. gingivalis OMVs and potential cumulative toxicity.

Notably, we found that the retention time of P. gingivalis OMVs in the oral cavity of mice without periodontitis was longer than that of mice with periodontitis. We reckon it is due to the “accumulation effect”. As Diaz et al. found [62], compared with non-periodontitis patients, the amount of subgingival bacteria in periodontitis patients increased by three times, indicating that the periodontal pocket of non-periodontitis patients has less ability to store bacteria and their products. Moreover, non-periodontitis patients had no telangiectasia caused by destruction of epithelial or underlying connective tissue [63]. Healthy periodontal epithelial or underlying connective tissue can delay the distribution rate of periodontal pathogens OMVs to the whole body and cause the accumulation in periodontal tissue, which is we called “accumulation effect”. “Accumulation effect” probably become a biological barrier for OMVs of periodontal pathogens to affect systemic diseases. However, “Accumulation effect” is attenuated in inflammatory periodontal tissue, which would promote the spread of P. gingivalis OMVs. In other words, the spread of P. gingivalis OMVs associated with the destruction of periodontal tissue.

The limited sample size and the metabolism mode of P. gingivalis OMVs were the limitations in this study. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study found that P. gingivalis OMVs mainly distribute to organs in the whole body as time-dependent manner through blood circulation rather oral-intestinal-liver axis, and their accumulation in liver and lung is particularly obvious (Fig. 6). Then, they were transported to the intercellular and cytoplasm of target cells to promote systemic and local inflammation. We also found that the “accumulation effect” of P. gingivalis OMVs in healthy periodontal tissue. Inflammatory periodontal tissue would weaken this effect. Additionally, due to the error of NIR technology, application of specific antibodies is strongly recommended for quantification of OMVs. Our results provide clinical reference and instructions for these systemic diseases from the perspective of the action pathway of P. gingivalis OMVs, suggesting that we should pay more attention to examine the periodontal condition during the treatment of systemic diseases, so as to improve the corresponding treatment effect.
