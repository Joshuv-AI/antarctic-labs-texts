QnAs with Ulrich R. Christensen
Authors: Sandeep Ravindran
Published: 2024

Ulrich Christensen has made major contributions to the scientific understanding of the internal dynamics of Earth and other planets in the Solar System. He has used theory and numerical simulations to study slow convective motions in Earth’s mantle and uncovered the role of solid–solid phase transitions in mantle convection and in the generation of Earth’s magnetic field. More recently, he has studied the dynamics of jet streams in the gas planets and the interaction of jet streams with the planetary magnetic field. In his Inaugural Article (IA), he describes recent work on jet streams on Jupiter (1). Now an emeritus scientist at the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research in Gottingen, Germany, Christensen was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2021.

How did you become interested in studying the winds on Jupiter?

I have been interested in this question for the past 20 years. After my PhD, I was focused more on how convective flow in the Earth's mantle drives the continents. Later on, in the 1990s, I changed to studying flow in real planetary fluids like iron in the Earth’s core and also the gas layers of the big gas planets. I worked on how to simulate them, but, at that time, it wasn't known how deep the winds on gas planets may reach; the emphasis was more on understanding what drives them and why there are a fair number of jets with alternating directions.

In the past decades, there have been disagreements about how deep these winds go into the interior of gas planets. Some people assumed that they were rather shallow, and there was another theory claiming that they may actually extend rather deep into the planet. This is an old question that has been more or less decided by the measurements of the Juno mission by NASA, which approached Jupiter very closely and was able to measure the perturbations of Jupiter's gravity field. This showed rather conclusively that these winds must be quite deep, so a few thousand kilometers or maybe 5% of Jupiter's radius. The Juno mission also suggested that the wind velocity at a certain depth must drop off rather sharply below 2,500 or 3,000 kilometers. So, I got interested in the question of what is really the physical mechanism that makes the wind to continue more or less with a constant velocity to a certain depth but then decrease in a rather narrow depth interval.

What did you find?

Basically, the answer we came up with is that you need both stable stratification—when the density structure is such that it prevents overturning circulation—plus the electromagnetic effect. Because the electrical conductivity at the surface of Jupiter is negligible, it's an insulator, but it rises kind of exponentially and, at a certain depth, you may call it a semiconductor.

We also know that Jupiter has a strong magnetic field, which is generated deeper down by a dynamo. So, the electromagnetic effects due to the existence of a magnetic field plus the electrical conductivity is one important ingredient. However, this alone wouldn't work—that’s something I published a couple of years ago. So you really need this combination of the stable stratification and electrical conductivity. There has always been kind of the idea that stable stratification may exist in the gas planets, but it was suggested for Saturn and not necessarily for Jupiter. So for Jupiter, it was a bit more hypothetical. There's a little bit of independent evidence of stable stratification on Jupiter, but it’s rather scant. This stable layering should exist more or less at a depth where it is needed in a way to get rid of the wind velocity. So far, I cannot come up with any other plausible mechanism for explaining what has been observed or deduced from the gravitational perturbations. Rather, there must be stable stratification plus these electromagnetic effects.

What were some other major findings reported in your IA?

One conclusion of the paper is that if you look at the velocity of the surface winds, they are strong at low latitudes, and they are still strong but slower as you move toward the poles. At least one outcome was that our simple theory predicts the same thing, but that the kind of dissipation or resisting force on the wind is weaker at low latitudes than it is at high latitude, which in a way can be at least part of an explanation for why the winds are strongest at low latitudes. It is related to the geometry of the magnetic field and the vector of gravity and rotational axis and how they align.

Another very important finding of this paper is that a fairly simple theory can explain more or less everything we see in the numerical simulation. For fluid dynamics, you look at different forces acting on a fluid, and there are quite a number of forces that are potentially important, like viscosity. But viscosity is very low in the gas planets. So, I said, let's ignore viscosity entirely. And then there's the Coriolis force due to the rotation of the planet; this is important. Then, there are electromagnetic forces that may also be important.

It's possible to come up with a fairly simple theory, which is, I think, the main new point about the paper. My student Paula, the co-author of the paper, had done similar simulations before, and I asked her to do these dedicated simulations to compare with the simplified theory and see whether it really fits almost quantitatively, and it turned out it does. So the charm of this theory is that you can come up with a single nondimensional parameter that controls how these winds drop off this fast. We call it the MAC number from Magnetic, Archimedean, and Coriolis, which are the primary forces that govern basically what happens.

What follow-up experiments are you planning?

We would certainly like to look at other gas planets, such as Saturn, which also has very similar patterns. The theory is nice if it fits one planet, but it makes it more credible if it applies to more than one planet.

What were some of the challenges in doing this study?

The challenge was, I think, basically to figure out this theory and to get a grasp and understanding of what's really important and how to treat it. It's partly a mathematical problem of how to scale the equations so that, at the end, a very simple result drops out. And it took me quite a while to develop an understanding from the 3D numerical simulations. We already had an idea of what was going to happen, but these are very complex numerical simulations that take all the forces into account, even forces that in a planetary context are not important, like viscosity. It turned out the results of the 3D simulations agreed very nicely in quite a number of aspects with the very simple theory that eliminates viscosity altogether.
