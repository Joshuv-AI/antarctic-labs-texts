Establishment of a Flow‐Through System for the Macrophyte Growth Inhibition Test (OECD 239) Including Photosynthetic Activity Measurement to Determine Early Effects
Authors: Joëlle Lousberg, Jost Hanfland, Lena Alix Kosak, Sebastian Eilebrecht, Benedikt Ringbeck, Karsten Schlich
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaintaining constant exposure concentrations during ecotoxicological studies while testing rapidly degradable substances is a challenge. To achieve stable concentrations during exposure, flow‐through systems are used. To assess the impact of substances on higher aquatic plants, the 14‐day macrophyte water–sediment Myriophyllum spicatum growth inhibition test (Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development [OECD, 2014a] test guideline 239) only includes a static or a semistatic test design. The main aim of our study was to investigate the applicability of a flow‐through system for M. spicatum. The standard OECD test design was miniaturized, and a flow‐through system with spill‐over was developed to achieve stable exposure concentrations of a rapidly degrading substance. The main endpoints were total shoot length and fresh and dry weight. Photosynthetic activity was used as an endpoint for the identification of early effects using the noninvasive Image‐Producing Pulse Amplitude Modulation (IMAGING‐PAM) procedure. Atorvastatin (AV; fast degrading) and bentazone (BT; photosynthesis inhibitor) were used as model substances to observe differences of the effect concentration depending on the test design. At higher exposure levels of AV, stronger necrosis combined with lower effect concentrations was observed in the flow‐through test compared with the semistatic test, indicating the applicability of the flow‐through test for evaluating degradable substances. The test with BT demonstrated a concentration‐dependent decrease in the photosynthetic yield (Y(II)) from day 3 onward even before macroscopically visible changes occurred. Our results show that the flow‐through system in the macrophyte growth inhibition test (OECD test guideline 239; 2014a) is a suitable alternative when one is testing rapidly degradable substances such as AV. In addition, we showed that photosynthetic yield can serve as a supplementary endpoint, when one is testing substances with photosynthesis inhibition as a mode of action. INTRODUCTION

Each year, numerous chemicals, pharmaceuticals, plant protection products, and biocides are registered for sale, for which a wide range of regulations must be considered (Tilman et al., 2002; Yin et al., 2017). For ecotoxicity testing in freshwater aquatic systems, model species such as Raphidocelis subcapitata or Desmodesmus subspicatus for algae, Daphnia magna as a representative of invertebrates, and the zebrafish Danio rerio are the most commonly used organisms. However, depending on the regulatory purpose, there are also tests with the monocotyledons Lemna minor or Lemna gibba and the aquatic dicotyledon Myriophyllum spicatum as primary producers, which are considered representative species of aquatic macrophytes. These and other model organisms are used for the comprehensive environmental risk assessment of new substances (Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development [OECD], 2014b, 2022).

The risk assessment is based on test guidelines issued by the OECD, whose test guideline 239 (2014a) describes how to determine substance‐related effects on M. spicatum in a sediment–water system. The test design and the endpoints (total shoot length, fresh weight, and dry weight) are highly standardized. At the present time, OECD test guideline 239 describes only static (no water renewal) or semistatic (with water renewal) test designs, which can be chosen depending on the stability of the respective test substance concentrations. However, various compounds exist that show a loss of substance, for example, by degradation of more than 20% of the nominal test concentration within hours or days; therefore OECD test guideline 239 (2014a) recommends analysis of sediment to determine the extent to which the test substance adsorbs to sediment. Sediment analysis, however, allows one only to determine substances that partition into the sediment phase and is thus not suitable for rapidly degrading substances. A flow‐through system presents an alternative to obtain higher stability, accurately reflecting the continuous exposure of substances in the environment and thus allowing a more realistic risk assessment. Many test guidelines already allow the use of flow‐through conditions in addition to static or semistatic test designs (see OECD, 2006, 2012, 2013).

The OECD test guideline 239 (2014a) considers three endpoints, the total shoot length, fresh weight, and dry weight. However, the latter two endpoints in particular can only be obtained by addition of further test vessels, if measurements at an earlier time point are required within a test. A method of measuring photosynthetic activity could be used to assess an impact on photosynthesis and plant health as an early warning indicator. The Image‐Producing Pulse Amplitude Modulation (IMAGING‐PAM) fluorometry method is a technique commonly employed to assess the influence on photosynthesis (Escher et al., 2006). Information on photosynthetic activity can be derived from the induction and quenching of chlorophyll fluorescence induced by short pulses of light, measured with an optical detector (Schreiber, 2004). Several parameters are recorded and can be used as reliable indicators for stress conditions inactivating the photosystem II or inducing sustained quenching (Murchie & Lawson, 2013). A widely used parameter is the effective photosystem II (PSII) quantum yield (Y(II)), which expresses the fraction of total incoming light energy going to photochemistry (Oláh et al., 2021). The Y(II) thus corresponds with the photosynthetic rate.

The aim of our study was to establish a flow‐through setup following most of the recommendations of OECD test guideline 239, to be able to test rapidly degrading test substances and to improve their risk assessment. A test design was chosen that followed the recommendations of OECD test guideline 239, paragraph 32—with one shoot/pot/test vessel. Appropriate test vessels were used to achieve sufficient space between plant shoot and the water surface. In addition, the nutrient concentration of the test media was adapted to yield comparable growth rates with a static or semistatic test design, which was necessary due to the continuous addition of fresh media in the flow‐through system. After establishment of the flow‐through setup, we tested its suitability using the chemically stable PSII inhibitor bentazone (BT; Herbicide Resistant Action Committee, 2020), a well‐known pesticide, and the rapidly degradable pharmaceutical atorvastatin (AV). In addition, the photosynthetic yield was determined using the IMAGING‐PAM fluorometry method to determine its suitability as an additional early warning endpoint. We used BT as the positive control and AV as the negative control.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Water–sediment M. spicatum growth inhibition test

The overall test setup followed the recommendations of OECD (2014a) test guideline 239. Briefly, M. spicatum shoots were held in sterile culture before being transferred into Smart and Barko medium for an acclimatization phase of up to 2 weeks. The Smart and Barko medium was prepared as described in OECD test guideline 239 and consisted of nutrients such as calcium chloride and magnesium sulfate but also carbon sources as sodium hydrogen carbonate and potassium hydrogen carbonate. The pH was adjusted to 7.9. Seven days prior to test initiation, shoots were cut to 6 ± 1 cm and weighed before being planted as quadruplicates into 700 g of sediment. The sediment used was prepared as described in OECD test guideline 239 with peat, kaolin clay, quartz sand, calcium carbonate, and aqueous nutrient medium containing ammonium chloride and sodium phosphate. The 7‐day establishment phase was performed under test conditions, at 20 ± 2 °C, pH 7.5–8, and a light intensity of 140 ± 20 µmol m−² s−1 (16:8‐h light:dark). At day 0, the plants were transferred into 2‐L glass beakers containing 1.7 L of the respective substance concentration in Smart and Barko medium. Lengths of shoots and lateral branches were recorded at days 0, 7, and 14, giving the total shoot length (TSL) of each plant. The fresh weight and the dry weight of representative plants were measured at test initiation and at test termination, for the test plants. For determination of dry weight, the plants were dried at 60 °C for 48 h. The growth rates (GR) of the three main parameters total shoot length, fresh weight, and dry weight were used to calculate effect concentration values.

In the static test there was no media renewal. If a semistatic test was performed, the growth medium was renewed after 7 days of exposure. Analytical samples were taken at days 0 and 14 in a static test design and at days 0 and 7 before medium renewal in a semistatic test design (see section Chemical Analysis).

Modifications of OECD test guideline 239

In general, the test design followed the recommendations of OECD test guideline 239. The overall steps in developing a suitable test system and validating its suitability are shown in Figure 1.

As a first step, we followed paragraph 32 of the guideline: “Alternative test designs of one shoot per pot per test vessel are acceptable provided that replication is adjusted as required to achieve the required validity criteria” (OECD, 2014a). To save resources in substance amount, space, sediment, and test plants, the test setup was miniaturized. This modification was also required as preparation for the flow‐through setup and to measure the plants in the IMAGING‐PAM instrument. The test setup was modified by potting one plant in 100 g of sediment instead of four plants in 700 g of sediment. Ten to 12 replicates containing one shoot each were used. At day 0, each replicate was transferred to a 500‐mL glass measuring cylinder filled to the top with the respective solution of the test substance or medium, to accord with the minimum of 12 cm height of the water column. The endpoints were measured as just described, except the time points for measuring the total shoot length were adjusted by measuring at days 3, 7, 10, and 14 instead of days 7 and 14 so as to be able to also use the data for the establishment of a short‐term test for analyses of effects on the transcriptomic level published by Hanfland et al. (2024). The suitability of the miniaturized test was confirmed based on the effects of 3,5‐dichlorophenol (DCP) on total shoot length; DCP is used as a reference substance in OECD test guideline 239. For this, both setups (2 L vs. 500 mL) were performed at the same time with plants of the same preculture. In this test, total shoot length and fresh weight were determined, because the international ring test outlined in the OECD test guideline 239 provides data only for these endpoints.

In the second step, we hypothesized that plant growth would be significantly increased if we provided new Smart and Barko medium constantly in a flow‐through system compared with a static or semistatic test design in which fresh medium is used only at the start of exposure. Therefore, we adapted the concentrations of calcium chloride, magnesium sulfate, sodium hydrogen carbonate, and potassium hydrogen carbonate provided in the Smart and Barko medium; the nutrients ammonium and phosphate, which were provided within the sediment, remained stable. A pretest with a static control and two controls in the flow‐through system (one with the same and one with only one‐fourth of the nutrient concentration) was performed with 10 replicates/treatment; total shoot length and fresh weight were determined after 14 days of exposure to the differently prepared growth media.

For the design of the flow‐through system, a spill‐over setup was chosen by which the respective solutions were continuously stirred in stainless steel vessels and pumped into the test vessels of the miniaturized test design, using 4‐, 8‐ and 12‐channel peristaltic pumps (IsmatecTM, Cole‐Parmer). Polytetrafluoroethylene tubes were used due to their material properties and their transparent color. Tubes were attached to the pump‐tubes by Y‐couplers and placed at the bottom of the test vessels to avoid turbulence in the water column, leading to a disturbance of the sediment layer, for example, and were replaced after each test. Furthermore, this ensured exchange of the test solution, because the excessive medium spilled over the edge of the test vessels. Using a collection tray, the substance was removed from the solution through activated carbon. For BT, the medium in the stainless‐steel vessels was renewed every 48 h, and for AV it was exchanged every 24 h. Before starting the test, the pumps were calibrated at least three times for 10 min and, if necessary, adjusted the day before initiation of the test and additionally at day 7. The flow rate was set to 125 mL h−1. After each test, all tubes were exchanged to avoid any cross‐contamination of the test substances.

Test substances

The reference substance DCP (CAS no. 591‐35‐5, purity 97%) was obtained from Sigma Aldrich, AV calcium (CAS no. 134523‐03‐8, purity > 95%) was purchased from abcr, and the PSII inhibitor BT (CAS no. 25057‐89‐0, purity > 97%) was obtained from TCI. All test solutions and dilutions were prepared on the same day in Smart and Barko medium, prepared according to OECD test guideline 239. For DCP, the three test concentrations (2.5, 5.0, and 10 mg L−1) were prepared the day prior to the test start and stirred for 24 h. Bentazone was solubilized by ultrasonication for 15 min followed by 1 h of stirring; for AV, 25 min of ultrasonication were followed by 2 h of stirring. For the static and semistatic tests, test solutions with BT (0.8, 4.0, and 20 mg L−1) and AV (0.03, 0.30, and 3.00 mg L−1) were prepared as a dilution series of the highest test concentration. The test solutions for the flow‐through system were diluted from freshly prepared stock solutions at concentrations of 450 mg L−1 for BT and 32 mg L−1 for AV. The calcium salt of AV was used to perform the tests, but all concentrations and effective concentration (ECx) values refer to the active substance.

Chemical analysis

The test substances BT and AV were analyzed by ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) by taking samples from the static and flow‐through tests. Samples were stabilized by adding 5 mL of the respective aqueous sample to 1 mL acetonitrile (ACN). During the development of the flow‐through test system, samples for the measurement of BT and AV were taken and analyzed. For BT, samples from the highest concentration (20 mg L−1) were taken for the static test at days 0 and 14. For AV, in the semistatic test samples were taken from the lowest and highest concentrations (0.03 and 3.00 mg L−1) at test initiation, after 1‐ and 2 days of exposure, and at the end of the first 7‐day exposure interval. During the establishment of the flow‐through system, but before exposure of the plants, samples from the highest test concentration were taken from freshly prepared test solution after 0, 2, and 24 h and after a further change of the stock solution after 0 and 6 h to evaluate the stability of AV levels at the highest test concentration before starting the actual test. At the 14‐day exposure period, samples of the three test concentrations were taken at test initiation, after 2, 6, and 24 h, and after the preparation of new stock solutions at day 5 again after 0, 2, and 6 h. Samples for BT were stored at ≤−18 °C until analysis and diluted with a mixture of test medium and ACN of 5 + 1 (v + v) to fit in the calibration range, where necessary. The stability of the samples was confirmed in advance. The samples from tests with AV were measured immediately to avoid degradation. For both AV and BT, the limits of detection and limits of quantification were 1 and 5 µg L−1, respectively, and the calibration ranges were 1–100 µg L−1 (in the pure aqueous portion). A detailed description of the analytical procedure, as well as the chemicals and materials used, can be found in the Supporting Information.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed with ToxRat® Professional Ver. 3.3.0. Data were analyzed toward outlier replicates using the raw data of TSL, fresh weight, and dry weight and the nonparametric outlier test after Hampel (p = 0.05). Significant outliers were excluded from further analysis. The statistical evaluation of tests with DCP and AV were performed using a linear Probit analysis with maximum‐likelihood regression. A statistically significant concentration–response curve was obtained if the F test resulted in p(F) < 0.05. The regression led to EC10, EC20, or EC50 values and also provided confidence intervals according to Fieller's theorem. The steepness of the relation was given by a Litchfield and Wilcoxon value lower than 10. For BT, test results were analyzed using the default settings of ToxRat® with a 3‐parameter normal cumulative distribution function and nonlinear regression without weighting but using Levenberg–Marquardt optimization (500 cycles).

Photosynthetic activity measurement using IMAGING‐PAM

The pulse‐amplitude‐modulation (PAM) chlorophyll fluorometer called IMAGING‐PAM MAXI (M‐Series, ©Heinz Walz; software: ImagingWinGigE) was used. Prior to the measurements, the instrument was adjusted for the purpose of the study (Supporting Information, Figure A). Briefly, the photosynthetic active radiation was set to 185 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for actinic light (450 nm, 300‐W LED array), and the total time of measuring was 315 s. Three replicates of each BT and AV test were measured by placing one cylinder with the plant at the respective time in the IMAGING‐PAM device and immediately starting the measurement. The area of interest (AOI) was adjusted for every plant and was set to the shoot apical meristem. After a first saturation pulse to determine maximum and minimum chlorophyll‐a fluorescence (FM and F0), 40 s of dark‐period were started, before applying a saturation pulse every 20 s (setting given in the Supporting Information, Figure A). The effective quantum yield of energy conversion at PSII reaction centers (Y(II)) was then determined by measuring the momentary fluorescence (F) and the maximum fluorescence under actinic light conditions (F′M), leading to Equation (1) as described by Genty et al. (1989). (1) Y(II)moment=F′M−FF′M

The Y(II) values of each concentration were determined by averaging the values from saturation pulse 4 to 15, resulting in stable Y(II). The inhibition in photosynthetic efficiency was determined by comparing the Y(II) of the treated plants with the Y(II) of the respective control plants (Equation (2)). RESULTS

Miniaturization of OECD test guideline 239

For the establishment of the utilized single‐plant glass vessels for flow‐through and PAM measurement purposes, a validation was conducted by comparing EC values for DCP for the modified setup with the standard OECD test guideline 239 setup. There were no statistically significant differences between the approaches (Table 1 and Supporting Information, Table 1). The statistical evaluation of all endpoints resulted in comparable EC50 values for total shoot length with overlapping confidence intervals, while the fresh weight data differed by a factor of approximately two, which was considered biological variability. These results were the basis for establishing the flow‐through setup.

Establishment of a flow‐through setup

The pretest on the optimal nutrient concentrations in terms of magnesium and calcium in Smart and Barko growth medium proved the hypothesis that a constant influx of standard Smart and Barko growth medium would strongly influence the plant growth in a flow‐through system compared with the standard static system without medium renewal over the 14‐day exposure period. A comparison among the static control, the flow‐through with the same nutrient concentration, and a control with only one‐fourth of the nutrient concentration showed significantly higher growth of the plants in the flow‐through system with the standard nutrient concentration in Smart and Barko medium. The differences when using one‐fourth of the initial nutrient concentration for the flow‐through setup were smaller and less significant for the measure growth rate of fresh weight (GR‐fresh wt) and were not significant for the endpoint growth rate of total shoot length (GR‐TSL; Table 2 and Supporting Information, Table 2). We therefore continued using one‐fourth of the nutrient concentration when comparing both static and flow‐through systems using the model substances BT and AV.

As shown in Table 3, the comparison of biological endpoints obtained from both approaches revealed the exact same EC50 value of 2.6 mg L−1 for growth rate of dry weight (GR‐dry wt). This was also the most sensitive endpoint. Measurement of photosynthetic activity

BT

The PSII inhibitor BT induced severe effects on photosynthetic activity by decreasing the Y(II) to 0% at test end. Already at day 3, a concentration‐dependent inhibition of photosynthetic activity was observed in both static and flow‐through test setups. Figure 2 shows the decrease in Y(II) of plants exposed to the highest concentration using the color scale of the IMAGING‐PAM. A slight recovery was observed between days 3 and 7, before the Y(II) decreased toward day 14, resulting in 11% to 0% of the control values. The effective Y(II) of the control batch was constant until day 14 (Figure 3).

AV

The evaluation of all Y(II) of both the semistatic and the flow‐through setups resulted in no observable inhibition in photosynthetic activity induced by the treatment. In the semistatic test design, the measured values fluctuated over the entire test duration and were always in a range comparable to that of the control values. In the flow‐through test, at the two lower test concentrations the measured Y(II) values were comparable to those of the control treatment throughout the test. At the highest test concentration of 3.00 mg L−1, an inhibition of Y(II) was found from day 10 to day 14 in the flow‐through test (Figure 4). However, the plants in the flow‐through test had a lower Y(II) on day 14 compared with the other measurement days, which was then again at the same level as the control values. As a consequence of the increased effects in the flow‐through system, the triplicate of 3.00 mg L−1 could not be measured accurately. These plants showed severe necrosis at the plant heads, which led to unreliable measurements due to the difficulties in selecting an AOI, from day 7 on.

Three plants/concentration were measured with Image‐Producing Pulse Amplitude Modulation at days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 of the tests. Saturation pulses were applied every 20 s, and the Y(II) obtained by the ImagingWinGigE software was further evaluated as a relevant endpoint. The first three values were excluded to only use stable Y(II). Each of the data points represents the averaged Y(II) of all three plants of the respective exposure concentration, including their variances. The values of the treated plants were normalized to the control values (set equal to 100%).

DISCUSSION

Miniaturization of OECD test guideline 239

Both test setups fulfilled the validity criteria requiring the doubling of the shoot length and fresh weight and a coefficient of variation below 35% for fresh weight in the control treatment (OECD, 2014a). This indicated that miniaturization did not influence the growth of the macrophytes. The calculated EC50 values for TSL and fresh weight were in line with the listed EC50 of between 4.7 and 6.1 mg L−1 obtained in the ring test in the OECD test guideline 239 and in the study by Mohr et al. (2013). The statistical comparison of the GRs of both approaches showed no significant differences at any concentration, leading us to conclude that the test setups were comparable, and that the miniaturized test can be used to obtain reliable EC values.

Establishment of a flow‐through setup

When one is performing a test under flow‐through conditions, different approaches can be used to obtain the appropriate concentrations. The different test concentrations can be set by preparing one stock solution that is diluted with test medium at different volume ratios (Meinertz et al., 2008). Another option is to prepare individual stock solutions at different concentrations and directly pump these solutions to the vessels, without further mixing with test medium. The latter approach was used in the present study and also by Lammer et al. (2009). Stainless‐steel vessels with stirrers were used to ensure homogeneity and no adsorption of the substances to the walls. Because neither BT nor AV are volatile (Li et al., 2008; National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2022), this option was considered suitable. No algae growth was visible in the tubes, and due to their transparent appearance, they did not shield the plants from light and did thus not interfere with growth. In addition, the results of the chemical analysis showed no concentration decrease caused by adsorption to the material used for the flow‐through system or degradation during the flow. The overall setup for the preparation of test media was therefore considered suitable. Considering the results of the pretest with different nutrient concentrations in Smart and Barko medium, we concluded that one‐fourth of the OECD test guideline 239 nutrient values in Smart and Barko medium were adequate for our purposes. However, it must be kept in mind that the fresh weight was significantly higher in the flow‐through setup compared with the static control. The results with BT, for example, which are discussed in the following paragraph, also indicate that the additional nutrient supply via the Smart and Barko medium may have an effect on the observed endpoint and the determined effect concentrations. Various mechanisms are discussed in this context, and future tests with even lower nutrient concentrations will give us more insights into an even more adequate nutrient level.

BT

The high stability of BT we observed over the test period is supported by previous findings of Mouvet et al. (1997). Subsequently, similar effects were expected after exposure for both the static and the flow‐through test. A clear comparability was found for GR‐dry weight, GR‐TSL, and GR‐fresh weight in terms of the EC10 values. The EC50 values in the flow‐through system were higher, and therefore plants were less sensitive in that system. Because the nutrient concentration was adapted, this effect cannot be explained by a continuous addition of nutrients and correspondingly healthier plants. Both tests fulfilled the validity criteria stated in OECD test guideline 239. Therefore, plants appeared to be healthy in both tests, and test results should be comparable.

Unfortunately, no published data for M. spicatum were found, but our results for the PSII inhibitor BT were in a comparable range with other studies using L. minor, reporting EC50 values of 2.56 and 2.94 mg L−1 (Cedergreen & Streibig, 2005; Loll et al., 2022; Munkegaard et al., 2008). Additionally, a study conducted with the marine diatom Skeletonema costatum reported an EC50 value of 24.0 mg L−1 (Macedo et al., 2008). The EC50 values for the green algae Raphidocelis subcapitata of 13.6 mg L−1 from Cedergreen and Streibig (2005) underlined the sensitivity of M. spicatum to this herbicide compared with other aquatic primary producers. We must consider that although L. minor, S. costatum, and R. subcapitata as aquatic primary producers are affected by the same mechanism of action as M. spicatum (inhibition of photosynthesis), effect concentrations can differ as a result of different exposure scenarios due to the presence of sediment in the test system with M. spicatum. Therefore, in terms of species sensitivity, the results indicated that M. spicatum is as sensitive to BT as Lemnaceae and is therefore relevant for an environmental risk assessment including sediment in the system. Even though both approaches had the exact same most sensitive endpoint, the freshly weighed plants in the flow‐through approach were heavier in the control and at all three concentrations compared with the plants in the static test system. This could possibly be explained by a more distinctive protection mechanism in the plants in the flow‐through setting, because they were continuously exposed to freshly prepared test substance. Farooq et al. (2012) found that plants use osmotic regulation to lower the intracellular concentration of a nondesired compound. Because the dry weight was the same, but the fresh weight was considerably higher in the plants in the flow‐through setup, this difference may have been due to increased water retention. In addition, the better overall growth of total shoot length and fresh weight might partly be explained by the already observed differences caused by the constant medium renewal and therefore higher availability of nutrients.

AV

Chemical analysis of AV revealed degradation up to 35% after 7 days in the semistatic approach, whereas in the flow‐through test AV appeared to be stable in the settings we used. The degradation observed in the semistatic test was less than expected based on literature values reporting half‐lives of AV in surface water solutions of 0.6 to 2.4 h (Lam & Mabury, 2005) and rapid degradation of 90% reported by Loll (2021) for the L. minor growth inhibition test according to OECD (2006) test guideline 221. However, the chosen plants were different (dicotyledon vs. monocotyledon), different test media were used, the Lemna test is sediment free, and illumination periods in the test developed in our study were shorter than in the L. minor test (16:8 h vs. 24 h). Stable concentrations of AV at each time point were measured in the flow‐through approach. This was not only the case throughout the test, but also in the pretest phase without any test organisms (data not shown). In addition to the chemical analysis, the biological endpoints also differed between both approaches, with the flow‐through system being more sensitive. The EC10 values for both approaches were comparable. The EC50 values for the TSL showed for the flow‐through approach to be precise within a range of 3.69 to 9.78 mg L−1, even when only three concentrations were tested. In a corresponding study that focused on the establishment of a short‐term test for the toxicogenomic assessment of ecotoxic modes of action in M. spicatum, effects of AV at the transcriptomic level at the same concentration range were found that were in good agreement with the results of the standard OECD test guideline 239 test (Hanfland et al., 2024). Further data available on the effect of AV on M. spicatum or other Myriophyllum species are rather limited. Nonetheless, results from tests with L. gibba are available. The EC50 value determined in our flow‐through test using M. spicatum was more than 15 times higher than the EC50 value recorded in L. gibba by Brain et al. (2004; 0.24 mg L−1) or Loll et al. (2022; 0.27 mg L−1). The EC10 values showed the same difference, that is, 0.35 to 0.51 mg L−1 obtained in our study compared with 0.026 mg L−1 in the L. gibba test from Brain et al. (2006). Based on these findings, L. gibba appears to be more sensitive to AV. The results from the tests with the degrading pharmaceutical AV, that is, stable substance concentrations in the flow‐through approach with corresponding stronger effects reveal that the flow‐through system is suitable for testing rapidly degrading substances.

Photosynthetic activity measurement

The IMAGING‐PAM instrument was adjusted specifically for measuring aquatic macrophytes in a 500‐mL cylinder. Thus the camera settings and the light intensity were optimized. However, in the previous literature different approaches were reported for assessing the intensity of the actinic light and the duration between the saturation pulses (Escher et al., 2006; Muller et al., 2008; Oláh et al., 2021; Schreiber et al., 2011). Most of the reported methods were based on green algae, diatoms, or Lemnaceae. One study by Schreiber et al. (2011) used M. aquaticum in six‐well plates with sediment. The endpoint was the Y(II) measured in the same way as in our study. Oláh et al. (2021) evaluated 29 publications using the PAM method and assessing different endpoints; approximately half of the studies used Y(II). In addition, after different endpoints were ranked based on their sensitivity to nickel, chrome(VI), and natrium chloride, the most sensitive endpoint was found to be the electron transport rate, followed by the Y(II). An average Y(II) of 0.1 was found for the control plants throughout all tests. This value went along with the findings of Fang et al. (2015), but was approximately five times lower than the values reported by Muller et al. (2008) and Schreiber et al. (2011). However, it must be considered that the latter study used algae suspensions in well‐plates, whereas Fang et al. (2015) worked with rice plants. In conclusion, the endpoint Y(II) seems to be a reliable and sensitive parameter for detecting effects on photosynthesis.

BT

The results of the test with the herbicide and PSII inhibitor BT revealed an early concentration‐dependent effect on the treated plants. The IMAGING‐PAM results at day 0, measured within 1 h after exposure, described a fast decrease in Y(II) and were supported by findings from Fang et al. (2015), reporting a strong decrease in Y(II) in rice plants after several hours of treatment with 500 mg L−1 BT. This rapid action of the herbicide underlines the necessity of measuring the Y(II) directly after exposing the plants at day 0. This also explains the measured start values below the control values, at least at the two highest test concentrations, which were measured 1 h after test initiation. Already at day 3, decreases of 40%, 96%, and 100% at the respective concentrations revealed a strong inhibition of photosynthetic activity. This concentration‐dependent decrease was observed until day 10, and all plants showed more than 90% inhibition of Y(II) at day 14. It must be noted that the high exposure concentrations led to complete inhibition from day 3 onward, whereas the lower concentrations had a stronger action toward the end of the test.

By comparing growth rates of the TSL, we used the effects on Y(II) to determine whether IMAGING‐PAM would be suitable to reveal early effects. The static approach showed a steeper decrease in growth rate of the TSL for the treated plants than the flow‐through setup. However, this difference was not seen in the Y(II) testing, in which <10% photosynthetic efficiency after 14 days was found for both approaches compared with the control. Moreover, a decrease to 0% was already seen at day 3. For the TSL from day 3, a maximum inhibition of 15% was observed across all exposure concentrations, and no concentration–response relationship was found. The growth rate increased during the testing period until the regulatory relevant response at day 14. The increase at the lowest exposure concentration from day 3 to day 7 aligned with a steeper increase in the growth rate of the plants.

Summing up, the IMAGING‐PAM method detected strong effects on photosynthetic efficiency as of day 3 of the test, much earlier than the onset of macroscopically detectable effects, which were only statistically significant for all concentrations as of day 10. This finding of greater sensitivity than the conventional endpoints was previously reported by Macedo et al. (2008), who found two times lower EC50 values using Skeletonema costatum with FV/FM as the endpoint compared with growth rate. Knauert et al. (2010) also found an early‐stage significant inhibition of photosynthetic efficiency of up to 60% at day 2 of a M. spicatum test for the herbicides isoproturone, atrazine, and diurone. In addition, Schreiber et al. (2011) reported a four times more sensitive response of M. aquaticum to atrazine using the IMAGING‐PAM method instead of the endpoint growth rate.

AV

No previous studies have reported an effect of AV on the photosynthesis of plants, so it is unclear whether AV causes photosynthetic inhibition. To our knowledge, our study is the first to record AV data using IMAGING‐PAM. Already from day 3, and onward, a trend was observed, with treated plants showing higher Y(II) than the control. Most treated plants remained within ±20% of the control value until the end of the test. A similar trend was observed for the standard endpoints. The flow‐through setup resulted in more stable effects, which were also recorded in IMAGING‐PAM. However, the recording of photosynthetic activity remained a challenge because of the feathery leaf shape of M. spicatum.

CONCLUSIONS

The flow‐through system offers a valid alternative for testing rapidly degradable substances, among others, in a water sediment test system with the aquatic plant M. spicatum according to OECD (2024a) test guideline 239. The data for (semi‐)static and flow‐through setups were comparable, and stable exposure concentration represents a more realistic scenario for continuously released substances. Further research addressing the appropriate composition of the nutrient concentrations in the Smart and Barko medium is needed to better determine the composition of a semistatic test. In addition, a review of the method within the framework of an international ring test would be a possibility to establish the flow‐through system for rapidly biodegradable substances or substances with other properties for future use within OECD test guideline 239.

The IMAGING‐PAM technique was identified as a functional tool to assess photosynthetic activity in the macrophyte growth inhibition test after completing OECD test guideline 239. The advantages of this noninvasive in vivo method are apparent: detection of early effects on photosynthesis without needing to remove the plants from their test environment, its sensitivity, and its ability to show the mechanism of action. Having a tool that is able to determine changes in photosynthesis much earlier than the occurrence of visible effects would be enormously useful for the ecotoxicological risk assessment of aquatic plants.
