Maternal Bacillus probiotic regulates offspring growth and immunity via spleen IGF-1/mTOR and FOXO1/IL-10 pathways
Authors: Tahany Saleh Aldayel, Heba M A Abdelrazek, Nadia A El-Fahla, Noran M Tawfik, Dalia A Eltamany, Dalia W Zeidan, Sherine Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdministering Bacilli clausii as probiotics during the maternal period may enhance microbial balance and positively impact offspring growth and immune health. This study evaluated the influence of Bacillus given to pregnant mice on the growth and immune development of their male albino offspring at 28 days old. Twenty-four Swiss albino mice (16 females and 8 males) were mated and assigned to four groups. Sixteen pregnant mice were divided into four groups (n = 4/group). The control group received a standard diet with distilled water, while the other three groups received daily doses of Bacilli (1.25 mL at 1 × 10^9 CFU) at different pregnancy stages: the second group on gestational day (GD) 0, the third on GD 8, and the fourth on GD 16. At 28 days after birth, male offspring were weighed, and blood samples were collected. The EDTA samples were used for hematological profiles, while serum samples were analyzed for growth hormone (GH), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and immunoglobulins (IgA and IgG). Spleens were weighed and analyzed for mTOR using qPCR, ELISA, and histological analysis and immunohistochemical staining for IGF-1 and FOXO1 markers. The GD0 group showed significant increases in body weight, spleen weight, spleen somatic index (SSI), GH, IGF-1, IL-10, IgA, and IgG levels, as well as mTOR protein and transcript levels, while TNF-α and IFN-γ levels decreased. Macroscopic analysis revealed a well-structured white pulp containing aggregated lymphocytes. Elevated levels of IGF-1 expression were observed, while FOXO1 expression was decreased. These findings indicate that early maternal probiotic supplementation improves lymphocyte activity and facilitates the remodeling of the splenic immune system in offspring and thus represents an innovative approach to immune programming during early life.Keywords: Maternal probiotic, Immunomodulation, Cytokines, Immunoglobulins, Histology, mTOR qPCR, mTOR protein, IGF-1, FOXO1Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology

Introduction

A balanced diet during pregnancy influences the growth of offspring, the development of the immune system, and the establishment of a healthy microbiota1. Colostrum from the mother during pregnancy and breastfeeding passes on immunological molecules to the offspring, which makes them stronger2. The colostrum and milk from the mother can provide passive immunity to the suckling animal. These fluids contain numerous active antibodies that help develop active immunity3. The fetal immune system tolerates maternal antigens and develops in response to environmental factors. Inadequate maternal nutrition can influence the newborn’s gut microbiota, increasing the risk of immunological disorders and inflammatory diseases in the newborn4.

Probiotics are live beneficial microbes that, when consumed in sufficient amounts, are beneficial to the host. They enhance intestinal barrier function, modulate immune responses, and produce essential nutrients and support health. Probiotics can trigger the immunoglobulin IgA secretion5, and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines6. Certain beneficial bacteria play a crucial role in stimulating B cells to produce immunoglobulin A (IgA), which is vital for intestinal immunity7. Supplementation with Bacillus sp., Lactobacillus casei, Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Streptococcus thermophilus could activate IgA-producing cells, strengthen the gut barrier, and lower infection risk8. Their germinated spores survive in the intestinal tract, interacting with lymphocytes and macrophages to promote immune activation and maintain gut immune balance9. Research suggests that probiotics during pregnancy can enhance birth outcomes by supporting maternal gut health and reducing inflammation, leading to better birth weight7.

Probiotics influence cytokine activity; they can upregulate levels of IL-10, an important anti-inflammatory cytokine that limits pro-inflammatory mediators like TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-12, and IFN-γ. This effect is crucial for preventing and managing inflammatory digestive disorders linked to immune dysfunction8,10. Some probiotics not only modulate the immune system but also produce antimicrobial substances that inhibit harmful bacteria, limit IgE-mediated responses, and reduce inflammation8. In this regard, beneficial microbes release a variety of metabolic products, including vitamins, organic acids, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), amino acids, enzymes, bacteriocins, and polysaccharides. According to Yeşilyurt, et al.11, SCFAs can suppress the NF-κB signal pathway in the gut epithelium, leading to the elevation of anti-inflammatory cytokine production like IL-10. While suppressing the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-812. The effectiveness of these benefits can vary based on the strain, concentration, and route of administration, making careful selection and standardization of probiotic strains vital for consistent health results13.

Bacillus clausii (B. clausii, Bacillus) is a well-known bacterial strain that supports gut health with antibacterial and immunomodulatory properties. It is often used to treat intestinal microbiota disorders, especially diarrhea. Studies explored its effectiveness in restoring microbial balance and enhancing the intestinal barrier14,15. B. clausii UBBC07, developed by Unique Biotech Ltd. in India and registered with the Microbial Type Culture Collection (MTCC 5472), is recognized for its temperature stability, robustness, spore-forming ability, and non-pathogenic properties16. In addition, the World Gastroenterology Organization recommends administering B. clausii daily for a few days or three months, at a dosage of about 2 × 10⁹ CFU for adults, children, and breastfeeding or pregnant mothers17. Controlled trials have reported no toxicological issues or negative reactions18,19. Research shows that B. clausii may improve immune regulation by lowering pro-inflammatory cytokines and boosting anti-inflammatory mediators like IL-10, suggesting its potential in managing urinary tract infections safely in pediatric care20.

Biological homeostasis relies on balancing cell development, proliferation, and death, influenced by growth factors, hormones, and nutrients. The mammalian Target of Rapamycin (mTOR) kinase network is crucial for regulating energy supply and protein synthesis while managing metabolism, immune regulation, and cell survival21. It operates through two complexes: mTORC1, which promotes anabolic pathways in nutrient-rich conditions, and mTORC2, which aids in cytoskeletal organization and survival signaling. Activation starts with phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), which phosphorylates AKT at threonine 308, followed by a second phosphorylation at serine 473 mediated by mTORC222,23. This dual phosphorylation is vital for full AKT activity, influencing several downstream targets, including transcription factors FOXO1 and FOXO3a, which regulate cell death, oxidative stress resistance, immune function, and metabolic balance24.

FOXO transcription factors in mammals regulate cell proliferation and lifespan by activating specific genes, while mTORC1 controls cell growth mainly by increasing protein synthesis25. FOXO1 in the nuclei of resting T lymphocytes actively maintains genes for immunological inactivity and stability26,27. When the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is activated, AKT phosphorylates FOXO1, causing its exclusion from the nucleus and leading to T cell proliferation28. This links mTOR signaling to immune activation and metabolic adaptability, highlighting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway as a key mediator between nutrition, growth signals, and cell proliferation29. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis is activated when insulin growth factor-1 (IGF-1) binds to its receptor IGF-1R. IGF-1 is a polypeptide hormone that is produced primarily by the hepatocytes under the control of growth hormone (GH)30. Most tissues express this pathway, which stimulates cell division, differentiation, growth, and metabolism. IGF-1 is a viable hormone required for maintaining and regulating tissue homeostasis and modulating immune responses.

Therefore, this research was conducted to examine the influence of maternal Bacillus supplementation throughout different phases of pregnancy on the postnatal growth and immunological development of male albino mice offspring. In addition, the study focused on the IGF-1/mTOR and FOXO1/IL-10 signaling pathways, measuring IGF-1 and FOXO1 expression in spleen tissue by immunohistochemistry and examining mTOR gene expression and protein levels using qPCR and ELISA. Hematological indices, as well as serum cytokines and immunoglobulins, were also assessed, along with a histological study of the spleen to cover the pathway. This technique provides novel insights into how the timing of early maternal probiotic intake may affect fetal growth and immunological regulation.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment protocol

Mice were acquired from the Laboratory Animal House at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Suez Canal University, Ismailia, Egypt. They were housed at a temperature of 24 °C ± 2 °C, under natural daylight cycles, and had ad libitum access to food and water. A stress-free environment was maintained to ensure proper hygiene at the Department of Zoology, Faculty of Sciences, Suez Canal University, Ismailia, Egypt. Before the experiment, the mice underwent a one-week acclimatization period, and all protocols followed the ethical guidelines set by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Suez Canal University, Egypt (SCU-VET-AREC-R-2025022).

Ethics approval: The animal study was reviewed and received approval from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Suez Canal University, Egypt (Approval No. SCU-VET-AREC-R-2025022; approval date: 15 July 2025). Accordance: All experimental procedures were conducted according to institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. ARRIVE: The study has been conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

For mating, two female mice in proestrus were placed with one male mouse overnight. The mouse was six weeks old and had an average weight of 26 g. The day a vaginal plug was observed and designated as gestational day zero (GD0). 16 pregnant mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 4 pregnant females/group), with each pregnant mouse housed individually. The control group received a control diet misted with distilled water, while three probiotic-supplemented groups were administered Bacilli 1.25 mL (1 × 10^9 CFU), misted with the basal diet as a commercial probiotic at different stages of pregnancy. This dose is consistent with previously published studies demonstrating the safety and immunomodulatory efficacy of B. clausii in rodent models31,32. The pregnant mice in the second group received Bacilli starting from GD0 (early trimester), the third group received it from GD8 (second trimester), and the fourth group received Bacilli at the late stage of pregnancy (third trimester, GD16).

The commercial probiotic Enterogermina® (Sanofi, Turkey) contains B. clausii at a concentration of 1.25 mL (1 × 10⁹ CFU). Each day, a fresh suspension was prepared and thoroughly mixed with a small amount of crushed standard pellet diet to ensure even distribution. The mixture was briefly air-dried at room temperature, a standard procedure in Bacillus probiotic feed preparation to ensure spore viability, uniform distribution, and prevent moisture accumulation as described by Cutting33 and Li, et al.34, before being given to pregnant females prior to their daily feed to ensure complete intake. This probiotic-supplemented diet was administered once daily during different gestational stages, starting on days 1, 8, or 16, depending on the group, and continued until birth.

Blood and tissue collection

At the end of 28 days after birth, the male offspring were weighed and anesthetized using isoflurane. This age was chosen because 28 days aligns with the weaning and early juvenile stage in mice, when the immune function matures significantly35. The induction concentration was set at 3–4%, and maintenance was at 1.5–2% in oxygen, delivered through a calibrated vaporizer (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the loss of the righting reflex and the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex before sample collection. Blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous sinus using a sterile heparinised glass microcapillary tube under isoflurane anaesthesia. About 0.4–0.5 mL of blood was collected from each mouse, which is within safety limits and sufficient for haematological and biochemical analyses. The blood was then transferred into ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) and plain tubes. The sera were separated by allowing the plain tubes to clot and then centrifuging them at 3000 rpm. Sera were kept at -70 °C for further biochemical analysis of growth hormone (GH), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), cytokine concentrations, and immunoglobulins. EDTA-coated tubes were implemented for hematological profile assessment. The spleens were dissected out for various analyses (n = 6 male offspring/group/analysis). Tiny spleen samples were preserved in RNAlater® solution and stored at -80 °C for mTOR gene expression analysis using qPCR, while another set of specimens was stored frozen for evaluating mTOR protein levels via ELISA. Additional tissue sections were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histological examination and immunohistochemical staining (IHC) for IGF-1 and FOXO1 markers.

After the procedures, mice were euthanized using CO₂ inhalation through a gradual fill method (20% chamber volume displacement rate/minute), followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, in accordance with the guidelines from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Suez Canal University, Egypt.

Hematological indices

The red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin concentration (Hb), hematocrit (HT), total leukocytic count (TLC), differential leukocyte count (neutrophil, lymphocyte, eosinophil, monocyte, and basophil), and platelet count were all measured using whole blood following the method of Patel, et al.36. The automated blood cell analyzer CELL-DYN® 3700 System (Abbott, USA) was used to conduct the analyses.

GH and IGF-1

Serum GH (6 samples/group) levels were analyzed according to Kopchick, et al.37 mechanism following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse GH ELISA kit (CUSABIO Company, USA, Catalog No.: CSB-E07343m, Detection range: 3.12 pg/mL-200 pg/mL and sensitivity: less than 0.78 pg/mL).

According to Baxter and Martin38 mechanism of analysis, serum IGF-1 (6 samples/group) levels were analyzed following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse IGF-1 ELISA kit (Catalog No. CSB-E04581m, Detection range: 0.156–10 ng/mL and sensitivity: less than 0.151 ng/mL, CUSABIO Company, USA).

Cytokine profile

The levels of serum TNF-α (6 samples/group) were examined in accordance with the Farney, et al.39 method following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse TNF-α ELISA kit (Catalog No. CSB-E13254Fh, detection range: pg/mL–5000 pg/mL and sensitivity: less than 125 pg/mL, CUSABIO Company, USA).

To determine the serum IFN-γ levels, 6 samples/group were analyzed according to Schreiber40 method following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse IFN-γ ELISA kit (Catalog No. EL0026Mo, Detection range: 23.44–1500 pg/mL, and sensitivity: less than 0.83 pg/mL, SunLong Biotech Co., LTD, China).

The IL-10 concentration in serum was determined by Hillyer and Woodward41. Methods following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse IL-10 ELISA kit (Catalog No. CSB-E04594m, detection range: 62.5–4000 pg/mL, and sensitivity: less than 15.6 pg/mL, CUSABIO Company, USA).

Immunoglobulins

Six samples in each group were used for serum IgA analysis according to Thang, et al.42 method, following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse IgA ELISA kit (Catalog No. EM1149-CM, detection range: 1.563–100 ng/mL and sensitivity: less than 0.938 ng/mL, FineTest® company, China).

Serum IgG (6 samples/group) levels were analyzed according to Thang, et al.42 method following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse IgG ELISA kit (Catalog No. EM0210, Detection range: 1.563–100 ng/mL and sensitivity: less than 0.938 ng/mL, FineTest® company, China).

mTOR gene expression

Spleen tissue from all groups of mice was collected for mTOR gene expression analysis. Total RNA extraction was conducted using the ABT Total RNA kit (Catalog No. ABT002, Applied Biotechnology, Egypt) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted mRNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with the ABT 2 × RT Mix Oligo kit (Catalog No. AMP11, Applied Biotechnology, Egypt). mRNA transcript level was assessed using the Applied Biosystems StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System. GAPDH is utilized as the housekeeping gene for data normalization. The primer sequences for the mTOR and the housekeeping gene are listed in Table 1. Thermal cycling and fluorescence detection were assessed using the ABT 2 × qPCR Mix (SYBR) high ROX mix (Catalog No. AMP04, Applied Biotechnology, Egypt). The thermal cycling conditions comprise 40 cycles, each consisting of 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 15 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 55 °C, and finalized by 30 s at 72 °C. The melting curves were analyzed to confirm the specificity of the amplification reaction. All reactions were carried out in triplicate for the target primer sequences. The data were evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCt method43.

mTOR protein

Mouse mTOR (serine/threonine-protein kinase mTOR) protein concentration in splenic tissues was determined by Lin, et al.44 method following the protocol of the manufacturer-specific mouse mTOR ELISA kit (Catalog No. EM0251, Detection range: 0.156–10 ng/mL and sensitivity: less than 0.094 ng/mL, FineTest® Company, China).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Spleen tissues (n = 6 male offspring/group) from all groups were washed with physiological saline (0.9% NaCl), fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. After 48 h of fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through ascending grades of ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Thin sections, with a thickness of 4–5 µm, were prepared using a microtome and then mounted on glass slides. The sections were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin and counterstained with eosin. A histological evaluation was performed under a light microscope to assess structural and pathological changes, following the described methodology by Cardiff, et al.45.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for IGF-1 and FOXO 1

Immunohistochemistry was performed based on specific antigen–antibody binding in tissue sections, where primary antibodies recognize target proteins, and an HRP-DAB chromogenic reaction produces a brown precipitate at the antigen sites. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated with graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave for 15 min. After cooling and rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature.

For IGF-1 detection, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit polyclonal anti-IGF1Rβ antibody (ABclonal, MA, USA, Cat. No. A0243; observed molecular weight: 90–130 kDa), following the protocol described by Brady, et al.46. For FOXO 1 detection, a rabbit polyclonal anti-FOXO1 antibody (ABclonal, MA, USA; Cat. No. A17978; observed molecular weight: ~ 80 kDa) was used, in accordance with the method outlined by Huang and Tindall47. After primary antibody incubation, sections were washed with PBS and treated with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Color development was done using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). The slides were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, dehydrated in ethanol and xylene, and mounted with a permanent medium. Stained sections were evaluated under a light microscope for positive immunoreactivity. This method enables the visualization of IGF-1 and FOXO1 expression patterns within spleen tissue, relying on the localization of the brown DAB reaction product.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were reported as mean ± standard error (SE). The obtained data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using Bartlett’s test. A one-way ANOVA test was used for the comparison between different groups. The post-hoc Tukey correction was then performed to compare the means of the groups. Statistical significance was set for a p-value less than 0.05. The statistical tests were completed using GraphPad Prism Software (version 8).

The GD8 and GD16 groups also had significantly higher values (p < 0.0001), with changes of 11.82%, 57.89%, and 22.50% for GD8 and 5.15%, 26.32%, and 16.30% for GD16 (Table 2).

Despite this, variations across groups were detected throughout the development of the white pulp. As depicted in Fig. 5A, the white pulp of the control spleen was small, including irregular primary lymphoid follicles and low lymphocyte aggregation. Discussion

Probiotics are generally considered a safe supplement for rebalancing gut flora and are commonly included in the diet to reduce inflammation, improve digestion, avoid illnesses, and promote growth and development48–50. This recent study explored the influence of Bacillus supplementation at different gestation periods on the splenic immune parameters observed in Swiss albino mice offspring.

Growth-related hormones, such as IGFs and GH, dimerize their receptors to control growth and metabolic homeostasis51. The pituitary gland releases GH, which binds with growth hormone receptors (GHRs), causing the liver to produce IGF-1. Then, IGF-1 hormone promotes tissue growth and inhibits further GH release through negative feedback52. The study demonstrated that the GD0 group exhibited increased final body weight, spleen weight, and SSI, as well as elevated circulating GH levels. These were consistent with Bernardeau and Vernoux53 investigation, which stated that probiotics play a key role in promoting animal growth and modulating hormonal metabolism. In addition, administration of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strains caused growth enhancement in mice via increased GH sensitivity and elevated IGF-154. The activation of the GH/IGF-1 signaling pathway may have contributed to the enhanced growth, observed as increased body weight gain following probiotic administration55. In addition, giving Bacillus subtilis bacteria during gestation has been associated with increased values of body weight in offspring pigs and mice via activation of growth hormones56. Previous studies confirmed that maternal probiotic intake during pregnancy enhanced the anti-inflammatory responses and activated immune function, while reducing metabolic disorders in both animal mothers and their offspring57,58.

The increase in the values of Hb, MCV, and MCH relative to the control values was detected in this study in the GD0 group. The observed elevation in Hb concentration was in line with the study of Shehzadi et al., which reported a significant increase in Hb concentration after oral administration of Lactobacillus bacteria in rats. Lymphocytes are key immune cells involved directly in pathogen defense59,60. The recent study was in alignment with a previous study, which revealed no differences in leukocyte count among Wistar rats administered with various probiotic combinations61.

The innate immunity triggered by exposure to foreign microorganisms and their byproducts stimulates the release of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-10, and IFN-γ, which enhance both adaptive and innate immune responses62. The present levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ were decreased in the Bacilli-administered groups, GD0, GD8, and GD16, consistent with prior research indicating that probiotics lower toll-like receptor activation and innate immune responses in the gut63,64, underscoring a reduction in the production of cytokines and acute-phase proteins63. Correspondingly, probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have been shown to modulate immune function by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, while enhancing lymphocyte activity65.

Beneficial microbes provide the host with SCFAs after supplementation, which can regulate the immune cell function and stimulate the anti-inflammatory response via lowering the inflammatory mediator, TNF-α66. Herein, the obtained TNF-α data confirmed that the daily supplementation of B. clausii is associated with an anti-inflammatory response of offspring. These findings are consistent with the study, which demonstrated that Bacillus-supplemented diets caused an elevation in IgM concentrations in both serum and ileum tissues when reducing TNF-α levels in the ileum of piglet offspring67. Similarly, previous research detected that beneficial bacteria might strengthen intestinal health by supporting lymphocyte activation and antibody production68. Furthermore, probiotic supplementation during pregnancy reduced TNF-α levels in the ileum of piglets at 95 days postnatal3.

The circulatory immunoglobulins, such as IgA, IgG, and IgM, are reliable indicators of humoral immunity. These antibodies are synthesized and produced by B lymphocytes and are involved in fighting pathogens69. Immunoglobulin A, IgA, is the main defense against harmful microbes and is vital for maintaining mucosal barrier function and immune integrity70. The intestinal bacteria have been shown to stimulate IgA production, promoting gut immune homeostasis71. In this study, IgA and IgG levels were elevated in the GD0, GD8, and GD16 groups, which is consistent with the known immunomodulatory effects of probiotics, where cytokine-driven activation of helper T cells enhances IgG antibody synthesis61.

Bacillus supplementation was associated with increased circulating GH, which may have contributed to elevated mTOR protein synthesis and mRNA transcript levels. GH has been documented to stimulate protein synthesis via activation of the mTOR signalling axis72. Both GH and mTORC1 are key cellular growth regulators, indicating a relationship between GH upregulation and mTORC1 activation. GH mediates its effects through PI3 kinase and PKB/Akt signaling, ultimately activating mTOR72.

mTORC2 phosphorylates Akt at Ser473, which then phosphorylates and inactivates the transcription factor FOXO1, leading to its exclusion from the nucleus73. When the Akt/mTOR pathway is suppressed, FOXO1 inhibition causes the promotion of T cell proliferation and cytokine production, including IL-10, an important immunoregulatory cytokine74. IL-10 signals through its receptor (IL-10R) to activate JAK1 and STAT3, supporting an anti-inflammatory response and further stimulating mTOR pathway75. These pathways offer a theoretical framework that may help explain the observed increase in mTOR expression, IL-10 levels and IGF-1 activity following Bacillus administration.

The spleen is a viable immunological organ that filters blood, removes old RBCs, and triggers adaptive immune responses. Its white pulp contains areas such as PALS, T lymphocytes aggregated, B-cell follicles, and germinal centers, all of which are essential for presenting antigens and producing antibodies76. Evaluating the spleen architecture by histological staining helps assess variations in the lymphoid tissue organization and development, while IHC reveals protein localization, and immune pathway activity77,78.

Recent studies have focused on the maternal feeding role in immunity, indicating that probiotic supplementation during pregnancy may enhance splenic development and immune programming in offspring79,80. Maternal consumption of Lactobacillus brevis KB290 for 14 days led to the upregulation of genes related to antigen presentation and NK cell activity in the spleen81. Supplementation of Lactobacillus rhamnosus at a dose of 2 × 108 CFU/day during pregnancy caused the development and proliferation of splenic dendritic cells and increased IL-10 production82. In addition, maternal supplementation with four strains of Lactobacillus administered daily significantly modified gene expression in the spleens of their offspring and increased lymphocytes-related immune activity80.

In line with previous findings, the current study revealed that maternal Bacillus supplementation improves lymphocyte aggregation and spleen tissue organization, especially in the GD0 group. This was evidenced by enlarged secondary lymphoid follicles, characterized by a central artery and a thick PALS. The germinal center was surrounded by macrophage-like cells, as observed in H and E staining. The IHC analysis confirmed these histological findings, demonstrating upregulated IGF-1 expression in follicular zones and reduced FOXO1 localization in active immune regions. Both markers are related to immune cell activation and germinal center activity83–85.

The variations detected in this study include an activated mTOR pathway, increased circulating GH and IGF-1, higher serum IL-10, and immunoglobulins (IgA and IgG), and lower TNF levels, suggesting that Bacillus supplementation modulates metabolic and immunological pathways in spleen tissue. IHC analysis indicated altered expression of IGF-1 and FOXO-1 that corresponded with serum hormone GH and cytokine levels. The increase in IGF-1 expression aligns with elevated circulating IGF-1 and GH, supporting the involvement of growth-related pathways86,87.

In contrast to IGF-1, FOXO1 localization in splenic immune cells was decreased, which in consistent with its regulation by AKT/mTOR pathway88. The upregulation of IGF-1 and mTOR levels observed in this study supports the possibility that the PI3K/AKT pathway was more active following Bacilli administration. This increased activity would likely diminish FOXO1’s nuclear function, which may influence immune cell proliferation and survival27,88–90. The data obtained from the tissue were correlated with the increase in mTOR expression. This indicates the critical role of GH, IGF-1, FOXO1, and mTOR pathways that participate in the immune health of the spleen. Early supplementation with Bacilli during pregnancy may influence these pathways and improve the immune function of offspring, promoting overall health.

Limitation

A limitation of this study is that it evaluated offspring only at day 28 postnatal age, during the early immune maturation period in mice. Evaluating additional ages would improve understanding of how maternal Bacillus supplementation affects immune and endocrine pathways during different offspring maturation stages. Future research should include various developmental time points to expand on these findings.

Conclusion

The study declared that prenatal supplementation with Bacillus probiotics enhances the immune function of offspring by improving spleen immunity. Histological examinations showed well-developed white pulp and increased lymphocyte proliferation. This supplementation led to higher levels of GH, IGF-1, and anti-inflammatory IL-10, while reducing inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ, via activation of the IGF-1/mTOR pathway. IHC findings confirmed these results, exploring increased IGF-1 and reduced FOXO1 expression in the spleen. These changes likely improve antibody responses, promote immune cell growth, and enhance overall immune activation and anti-inflammatory responses. This underscores the significance of the activation of the IGF-1/mTOR pathway in augmenting immune function and reflects the effective role of early supplementation of maternal Bacilli in the regulation of immune function of animal offspring, presenting a promising, cost-effective, and safe approach to promote early-life immunity. Further studies are needed to explore detailed insights via detecting phosphorylated AKT and FOXO1 in tissue samples, along with analyzing genes normally controlled by FOXO1.
