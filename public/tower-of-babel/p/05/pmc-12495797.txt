Assessment of human resource for eye health services in Southern Ethiopia
Authors: Zelalem Mehari, Ashiyana Nariani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman resources related to eye health are critical to significant reductions in blindness and low vision on a global and regional scale. Blindness and visual impairment affect the quality of life, poverty rates, and employment and educational opportunities of people with visual impairment. This study aimed to assess the availability and distribution of human resources for eye care to determine gaps in eliminating preventable and treatable blindness.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design. Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire distributed electronically via the KoboToolbox platform. The survey targeted all government, private, and NGO/Mission eye care facilities across the two regions. The respondents were primarily heads or coordinators of eye care units; in cases of nonresponse, senior staff members were invited to complete the questionnaire. The tool gathered information on the type, number, and distribution of eye care professionals, as well as the services provided at each facility.ResultOut of the 77 public hospitals and 642 public health centers, only 39 (5.4%) provided eye care services. A total of 48 facilities were provided with eye care services, each of which served 388,765 people in the two regions. Thirty-nine (81.3%) were government owned, 5 (10.4%) were private for profit, and 4 (8.3%) were NGO/Mission owned. In these facilities, a total of 217 eye care professionals were practicing: 30 were ophthalmologists, 10 were cataract surgeons, 93 were optometrists, 62 were ophthalmic nurses, 6 were ophthalmic officers, one was a low vision specialist, and 15 were optical technicians. The only low vision specialist identified was an optometrist with additional training in low vision care. The mean number of cataract operations performed per unit per year was 860 (range: 30–2,800). The CSR of the SNNPR and Sidama regions was 1086 operations/million population/year in 2022. Among all the eye care centers in the study area, 50% provided cataract surgery services, 19% provided glaucoma surgery services, and 90% provided refraction services. Only one facility had panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) and intravitreal injection services. However, none of the facilities provided an in vitro retinal surgery service.ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that the current human resource capacity for eye care in southern Ethiopia remains below the recommended thresholds outlined by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the IAPB Africa Strategic Plan. Furthermore, the distribution of eye care professionals is uneven, with the majority living in urban centers, whereas many rural areas remain critically underserved. These disparities suggest the need for more targeted workforce planning and support for rural deployment. However, these conclusions should be interpreted cautiously, given the study’s reliance on self-reported data and the absence of a standardized urban‒rural classification. Addressing the quantitative and geographic gaps in human resources for eye health will be essential for improving equitable access to services in the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12960-025-01007-1.

Background

The WHO Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health: Workforce 2030 underscores healthcare workers’ pivotal role in ensuring quality care delivery and the functionality of healthcare systems [1, 2]. As we transition from the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), the imperative of a well-prepared health workforce becomes increasingly apparent [3, 4]. The health-related SDGs underscore the critical need for a robust workforce to achieve ambitious targets, particularly in low- and middle-income countries with disproportionately high disease burdens [5].

The Lancet Global Health Commission on Global Eye Health has underscored the urgent need for comprehensive eye care beyond 2020, as millions suffer from preventable or treatable vision impairment [6]. Despite significant progress in global eye care efforts, human resources for eye health (HReH) in many regions, particularly sub-Saharan Africa, need to meet established targets, contributing to disparities in eye care services [7, 8]. Challenges such as inadequate workforce distribution, quality concerns, and limited career structures persist, hindering the effectiveness of eye health programs and exacerbating the burden of preventable blindness [8].

Recognizing the urgency of addressing these challenges, global initiatives such as VISION 2020 prioritize HReH development as a central goal, aiming to align workforce size and composition with population needs [9–11]. However, despite concerted efforts to strengthen HReH, many countries still struggle to meet established targets, particularly in rural and underserved areas [12–14]. The Global Action Plan (GAP) and VISION 2020 stress the importance of accessible, well-trained, and supported HReH in achieving equitable eye care and reducing the burden of preventable blindness [15].

Ethiopia, with a population exceeding 110 million, faces significant eye health challenges, with high prevalence rates of blindness and visual impairment [16, 17]. While the country has made strides in expanding its eye care workforce and infrastructure, challenges such as workforce maldistribution, quality concerns, and underprioritization of eye health persist [17, 18]. Despite the launch of national eye care initiatives, comprehensive assessments still need to be conducted to track HReH progress within the framework of global initiatives, such as VISION 2020 [18].

This study addresses this gap by providing policymakers and stakeholders with valuable insights into the current status and challenges of HReH in Ethiopia. By understanding specific needs and barriers, targeted interventions can be developed to strengthen eye health services and improve outcomes across the country, ultimately contributing to the global effort to eliminate avoidable blindness and vision impairment.

Methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR) and the Sidama Region of Ethiopia. According to population projections by the Ethiopian Statistical Service, these two regions had a combined estimated population of approximately 18.76 million during the study period [16]. The regions were selected because of their demographic significance, diversity, and representation of varying levels of healthcare infrastructure within the country.

This study was carried out between September 2022 and April 2023. Importantly, following the completion of data collection, administrative restructuring in Ethiopia led to the creation of three additional regions. However, this study’s analysis and reporting are based on the administrative boundaries in effect during the 2022–23 fiscal year.

Health facilities that participated in this study

A total of 48 health facilities, including 23 primary, 24 secondary, and one tertiary eye care facility, were included in this study. These facilities were selected from the public and private sectors, with the majority (81.3%) being government owned. Health facilities providing eye care services were identified on the basis of information obtained from regional health authorities.

Study design

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design to assess human resources for eye health in public, NGO/Mission, and private institutions across the two regions.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire designed to capture information on the number, type, and distribution of human resources for eye health, as well as service availability across facilities. The questionnaire was adapted from the World Health Organization (WHO) Health System Framework [19], which conceptualizes health systems around six core building blocks: service delivery, health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership/governance. As this is a widely accepted and authoritative framework, the tool was not formally validated but was used to structure descriptive data collection in alignment with WHO guidance.

This survey was distributed electronically through the KoboToolbox platform to 48 heads or coordinators of eye care units. Of these, 40 responded. For the 8 nonresponding facilities, the questionnaire was completed by more senior staff members, resulting in a 100% facility-level response rate. We followed up by telephone or conducted face-to-face visits to assist with and ensure completion of the questionnaire. The tool collected data on workforce composition, service delivery capacity, and reported challenges.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 22 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize workforce characteristics, service activities, and infrastructure. Human resources for eye health (HReH) workforce ratios were compared with VISION 2020 targets and other research findings. Summary tables, graphs, and charts were utilized for descriptive purposes.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Edinburgh Medical School Ethics Committee and regional health research ethics committees. Informed consent was obtained from all the respondents prior to their participation in the survey. A consent statement was included at the beginning of the questionnaire, which explained the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality assurances, and contact details for any questions or concerns. The participants confirmed their agreement by clicking "OK" to proceed, which served as documented informed consent. This procedure was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board.

According to the 2007 population census projection, the total population residing in the two regions was 18,758,776, with 14,111,104 in SNNPR and 4,647,672 in the Sidama Region. Twenty-nine (59%) of the eye care facilities in the study area were supported by international organizations such as the Himalayan Cataract Project (HCP), Orbis International, Christian Blind Mission (CBM), and Light for the World (LFW), which play critical roles in trachoma prevention and treatment programs, cataract surgeries, school screenings, glasses distribution programs, and capacity building and human resource development.

Human resources for eye health (HReH)

During the study period, 217 ophthalmic professionals were employed in the two regions, 33% of whom were female. These included 30 ophthalmologists, 10 cataract surgeons, 93 optometrists, 6 ophthalmic officers, 62 ophthalmic nurses, 1 low vision specialist, and 6 persons with short-term training as optical technicians. Additionally, more than 200 integrated eye care workers (IECWs), trained mainly for trachomatous trichiasis (TT) surgery and ophthalmic patient screening and referrals at health centers, practice in these two regions.

The distribution of human resources varied, with 83% employed in government establishments, 9% in private for-profit companies, and 8% in NGOs/Missions. Ophthalmologists

There were 30 ophthalmologists in the two regions, 83.3% working in government establishments, 10.7% working in private for-profit facilities, and 7.1% working in NGO/Mission facilities. Among the 30 ophthalmologists, 2 (6.6%) were subspecialists during the study period. The ophthalmologist-to-population ratio was 1:625,292, meeting 40% of the VISION 2020 target of 1:250,000.

Cataract surgeons (CSs)

The study revealed only 10 cataract surgeons in the two regions, with 70% employed in government establishments, 20% in private for-profit centers, and 10% in NGO/Mission eye care centers.

Optometrists

There were 93 optometrists in the two regions, 86% working in government establishments, 8.6% working in private for-profit facilities, and 5.4% working in NGO/Mission facilities.

Ophthalmic nurses

Among ophthalmic nurses, 92% worked in government establishments, 1.6% worked in private for-profit facilities, and 6.4% worked in NGO/Mission facilities.

Ophthalmic officers

This study identified 6 ophthalmic officers, all of whom were working in government establishments.

Workforce gap analysis

Generally, the eye care workforce in the southern region of Ethiopia appears to be inadequate and far beyond numerical shortfalls from World Health Organization (WHO) standards or/and the IAPB Africa strategic plan. The ratios of ophthalmologists to the population and allied ophthalmic professionals were 1:625,292 and 1:223,318, respectively. The current ophthalmologist-to-population ratio in the region lags behind the WHO standards by more than 250%, whereas the allied ophthalmic professional-to-population ratio lags behind the WHO standards by more than 280%. However, the optometrist-to-population ratio is in line with the IAPB Africa Strategic Plan, with a ratio of 1:201,707; the optometrist-to-population ratio exceeds the IAPB Africa strategic plan of 1:250,000 (Table 2). The distribution of ophthalmic professionals across the study area was markedly uneven, with a significant concentration in urban centers and regional capitals. Hawassa reported the highest number of professionals (71), followed by Butajira (16), Arba Minch (15), Wolaita (14), and Hossana (13). In contrast, several remote and rural districts were served by only one professional—or, in some cases, none at all. Output of available HReH

The average number of ophthalmic patients seen at the base health facilities ranged from 20 to 5,800 per month. During outreach activities, the number of patients screened per outreach/campaign ranged from 20 to approximately 7,000. The higher figures represent cumulative estimates across multiple outreach sites within a single high-volume cataract campaign, typically organized to deliver around 1,000 surgical interventions. These numbers were reported retrospectively by facility managers/coordinators and should be interpreted with caution due to the potential lack of detailed documentation for nonsurgical cases. Outreach services were provided by 21 (44%) eye care facilities, and those services included cataract surgery, trachomatous trichiasis (TT) surgery, and refraction. Seven primary eye care centers had an outreach program for TT surgery and screened and referred cataract patients and other patients to secondary or tertiary eye care centers. Thirteen secondary and one tertiary eye care center had outreach programs for cataract surgery and TT surgery. Almost all outreach services were delivered free of charge to reduce the barriers related to direct and indirect costs for eye care services.

Cataract surgery in southern Ethiopia was performed in base/center and outreach clinics to address the service to the rural population, which has poorer access to hospital services than the urban population does. In 2022, 20,636 cataract surgeries were performed in the two regions, of which 10,444 (50.6%) were performed at the base/center and 10,192 (49.4%) at the outreach clinic. The mean number of cataract operations performed per unit was 860 (range 30–2,800). The CSR of the SNNPR and Sidama regions was 1,086 surgeries/million population/year in 2022.

The facilities had constant schedules for outreach/campaign cataract surgery programs that were supported by international NGOs and reached needlessly blind people in the most remote and underserved areas of the regions, and they performed 200–1500 cataract surgeries per a single outreach/campaign program.

Half of the facilities (24) performed cataract surgeries, 19% offered glaucoma surgery services, and 90% provided refraction services. Only one facility offered panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) and intravitreal injection services for retinal cases. However, none of the facilities provided in vitro retinal surgery services. Among the facilities that provided cataract surgery data, 16 (67%) had a cataract surgery outcome monitoring tool/system to monitor the results of cataract surgery, and 58% (14) had inpatient ward services. Laser and anti-VGF services for advanced diabetic eye disease were offered in only one private center. Only 11 eye care facilities had spectacle production services. Of the 39 public eye care facilities, only 4 facilities had optical workshops. Only 9 (19%) eye care service providers or facilities had pediatric retinoscopy refraction (cycloplegic) services.

Discussion

The ophthalmic workforce in Ethiopia encompasses a diverse range of professionals, including ophthalmologists, optometrists, nonphysician cataract surgeons, ophthalmic nurses, ophthalmic officers, and integrated eye care workers (IECWs). Over the past decade, there has been a notable increase in the number of ophthalmologists and optometrists, which is attributable to expanded training initiatives. However, training programs for nonphysician cataract surgeons and ophthalmic officers have experienced disruptions, contributing to disparities within the workforce. Additionally, Ethiopia lacks training programs for other essential ophthalmology cadres, such as opticians, orthoptists, and eye photographers.

A significant challenge within the Ethiopian ophthalmic workforce is the high mobility of professionals, particularly ophthalmologists, who tend to concentrate in urban centers and capitals. This concentration exacerbates disparities in access to eye care services, particularly in remote areas where the need is often greatest. Unfortunately, there is a general lack of motivation among eye care professionals to work in underserved regions. Despite a few isolated initiatives, structured retention and motivation programs are mainly absent, further hindering efforts to address geographic disparities in eye care provision.

Enhancing the efficiency of Ethiopia’s eye care system hinges on the effectiveness of its ophthalmic professionals and their strategic deployment across the country. A crucial aspect involves ensuring the production and retention of adequately skilled eye care professionals to meet the evolving demands of the population.

While there have been notable improvements in access to ophthalmology services in recent decades, especially compared with previous years, a significant challenge persists in the geographical distribution of secondary and tertiary ophthalmic facilities. These facilities are predominantly situated in urban areas and large cities, which poses substantial barriers for rural patients, who often need more travel and lodging expenses. To mitigate this disparity, integrated outreach eye care services, encompassing treatments for cataracts, trachoma, and refractive errors, present a promising solution. By providing essential eye care services directly to communities, particularly those in remote and hard-to-reach areas, these initiatives can significantly increase accessibility and reduce the burden of travel costs. Furthermore, ongoing outreach and campaign efforts by nonprofit organizations and government agencies provided free of charge in targeted regions are vital in extending eye care services to underserved populations.

Human resources constitute indispensable assets in eye care programs to mitigate preventable blindness and warrant meticulous examination. The pivotal role of sufficient human resources in preventing avoidable blindness, particularly in addressing conditions such as cataracts, has been extensively documented and underscored in the literature [11, 20]. As populations continue to expand and age, the demand for ophthalmic services is poised to increase accordingly. Consequently, devising a comprehensive plan that considers the expanding market and the aging demographic is imperative to address this ever-growing challenge effectively.

Our study aimed to delineate the various types of human resources for eye health (HReH) within the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples (SNNP) and Sidama regions, examining their implications for providing eye care services. In line with guidelines set forth by the World Health Organization (WHO), which recommend staffing ratios of 4 ophthalmologists, 4 cataract surgeons, 4 optometrists, and 10 ophthalmic nurses per million population, our investigation revealed that, akin to regions in Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, and other sub-Saharan African countries, southern Ethiopia had not largely met these suggested HReH targets by 2022/23, except optometrists [21–23].

Despite this overall alignment with WHO recommendations, our survey revealed notable deficiencies in certain aspects of staffing and management within eye care facilities. Specifically, only 9 out of the facilities (18.8%) reported employing designated managers or coordinators to oversee eye care services. Furthermore, our findings highlighted significant challenges related to the allocation and placement of ophthalmic staff, particularly concerning high-level professionals or subspecialists. These bottlenecks underscore the need for targeted interventions to optimize staffing configurations and enhance the effectiveness of eye care delivery in the region.

The survey revealed a need for more human resources and an uneven distribution of eye care staff, with a notable concentration in urban health facilities. This pattern mirrors findings from other developing countries, such as South Africa, Nigeria, and Ghana. This is often attributed to low socioeconomic status and limited career development opportunities for eye care professionals. Consequently, this disparity adversely impacts the use of eye care services in underserved areas [21–23].

Remarkably, only 5.4% of all public health facilities in the two regions offered eye care services. Despite Ethiopia’s considerable health infrastructure, which includes approximately 17,699 health posts, 3,777 health centers, and 367 functional public hospitals, the integration of eye care services remains limited. In our study area, comprising 77 public hospitals and 642 public health centers across the two regions, only 39 health facilities provided eye care services. This stark reality underscores the urgent need for better-integrating eye care services into the existing healthcare system. To address this gap, organizations such as the Himalayan Cataract Project (HCP), Cure Blindness, and Novartis have recently partnered with the Ethiopian Ministry of Health to implement integrated people-centered eye care initiatives.

This study revealed notable gender disparities in the use of human resources in the eye health category. Among the 217 individuals comprising the workforce, only 83 (38%) of the ophthalmic professionals practicing in the two regions were women. This gender distribution resembles findings from India, where 42% of health workers were female [24], and South Africa, where 41.6% of medical doctors were women [25]. In contrast, previous studies conducted across 57 and 104 countries indicated a greater proportion of women employed in the health and social sectors, with an estimated 67% of health workers in the 104 countries being women [26, 27]. Data from Labour Force Surveys (LFSs) across 57 countries corroborate this trend, showcasing an increasing representation of women in the medical sector over time [28]. Notably, the feminization of medicine is significant, given its historical status as a male-dominated profession [29].

Our findings align with the 2017 Gender Gap Report, which projected that some countries would require 100 years to close gender gaps, with the most substantial disparities observed in the economy and health sectors [30]. Gender equality remains a persistent challenge in various labor markets, including healthcare. Several factors contribute to this gender gap, including unequal career opportunities, gender-neutral institutional policies, recruitment discrimination, gender-biased staffing, heightened risk of violence against women workers, and substandard working conditions. Addressing these multifaceted challenges is crucial for fostering gender equity within the healthcare workforce [31–33].

The lack of attention to eye care services and resource allocation ultimately results in the unavailability of services for patients within a country. In Ethiopia, eye health is not prioritized, as highlighted by various individuals [34]. This neglect has profound implications, particularly in underserved areas where the scarcity of human resources for eye health prevents early detection and treatment of vision issues such as cataracts, glaucoma, refractive errors, and diabetic retinopathy. Outreach programs play a crucial role in addressing these gaps by enabling the early identification and referral of such cases before irreversible damage occurs. Our survey revealed that 43.8% of facilities in the two regions offered such outreach services, primarily focusing on cataract and trachomatous trichiasis (TT) surgeries, which are often supported by NGOs. This model mirrors successful community eye care initiatives in India [34] and collaborative projects such as the Hilton Cataract Initiative in Nigeria [35]. However, the necessity of these outreach programs underscores the limited accessibility of quality eye care services in rural areas, exacerbating preventable blindness rates [36].

Nearly half (10,192) of the cataract surgeries in our study area were conducted through outreach or campaign programs predominantly supported by NGOs. However, overreliance on such external support undermines the sustainability of health programs. To address this challenge, it is imperative to integrate eye care services into government budgets and public health policies, ensuring sustainable funding, staffing, and oversight. By establishing sustainably funded and staffed eye care services, we can increase the resilience and longevity of eye health initiatives, ultimately reducing preventable blindness and improving access to quality care for all individuals [37].

In 2022, a total of 20,636 cataract surgeries were performed, with 51% conducted within base units and 49% during outreach campaigns. The mean number of cataract operations per unit was 430, which is consistent with research from 12 years prior. Notably, more surgeries (74.5%) were carried out at base eye units than did previous studies, indicating a shift in service delivery. Despite reaching only half of the Vision 2020 target of 2000 surgeries per million people, the regions demonstrated significant improvement in cataract surgical rates (CSRs) compared with past surveys and national averages [37, 38]. However, the high number of surgeries performed during outreach campaigns, although beneficial in reaching underserved populations, may pose challenges in sustaining CSR levels in the long term. Additionally, disparities in refraction services were observed, with services available in all secondary eye care units (SECUs) but limited to primary eye care units (PECUs), contradicting the expected national standard. Addressing these discrepancies is crucial to ensure equitable access to comprehensive eye care services across all levels of care.

In addressing refractive errors, which constitute the third major cause of blindness or the second major cause of low vision in the country [17], the government has a more robust workforce than other ophthalmic professionals do, except for opticians and low vision specialists. However, refractive services, particularly spectacle production and distribution, have yet to receive significant attention from the government or NGOs, similar to cataracts and trachoma in eye care programs. Our survey revealed that only twenty-three percent of facilities offered spectacle production services. Despite the simplicity of correcting refractive errors with spectacles, there remains a high prevalence of uncorrected refractive errors due to cost and accessibility barriers to refraction and spectacle dispensing services. With few public optical dispensing facilities in rural areas, production and dispensing are handled primarily by private optical shops or eye care facilities, posing accessibility challenges for rural communities. To address this, comprehensive services such as refraction and spectacle dispensing should be provided at the primary level of eye care, where they are most accessible to the community. Meeting VISION 2020 targets requires equipping and supplying manufacturing workshops, especially in public facilities, where spectacles are significantly more affordable than private establishments are [39, 40].

Additionally, only 14 (29%) eye care facilities had inpatient wards, with a total of 243 beds dedicated to ophthalmic patients. On average, 10 to 200 ophthalmic patients are admitted monthly, resulting in an inpatient bed-to-population ratio of 1:78,393. This falls below the VISION 2020 goal of one bed for every 20,000 people and Zambia’s ratio of 2.98:20,000 [40]. Addressing this shortfall in inpatient capacity is essential to ensure timely and adequate care for ophthalmic patients and align with international targets for eye health.

The findings of this study underscore the pressing need for effective strategies to address the shortfall in human resources for eye health in Ethiopia. To mitigate this challenge and improve access to eye care services, various strategies can be considered:1. Training and capacity building: Expanding training programs for eye care professionals, including ophthalmologists, optometrists, and ophthalmic nurses, is paramount. Initiatives aimed at increasing the number of graduates and enhancing their skills through continuous education and training should be prioritized. Collaborations with academic institutions and professional organizations can facilitate the development and implementation of comprehensive training curricula tailored to the needs of the Ethiopian context.2. Retention and motivation: Retaining skilled eye care professionals, particularly in underserved areas, is essential for ensuring equitable access to services. Incentive programs, such as financial bonuses, career advancement opportunities, and improved working conditions, can help retain personnel in remote and rural settings. Additionally, implementing structured retention and motivation programs, including mentorship and professional development initiatives, can increase job satisfaction and reduce attrition rates among eye care professionals.3. Task shifting and delegation: Exploring opportunities for task shifting and delegation of certain eye care responsibilities to lower-level cadres and community health workers can help alleviate workforce shortages. By expanding the scope of practice for allied health professionals and empowering community health workers to perform specific tasks, such as screening and basic eye examinations, the burden on specialized personnel can be reduced. However, careful consideration of training, supervision, and regulatory frameworks is essential to ensure safe and effective task shifting practices.4. Public‒private partnerships: Leveraging partnerships between government agencies, nonprofit organizations, and private sector entities can augment efforts to address human resource gaps in eye health. Collaborative initiatives, such as public‒private partnerships for workforce development and capacity building, can mobilize resources, expertise, and infrastructure to strengthen the eye care workforce. By fostering collaboration and resource sharing across sectors, these partnerships have the potential to enhance the sustainability and impact of eye care initiatives in Ethiopia.5. Digital health innovations: Leveraging digital technologies such as teleophthalmology and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted screening tools presents a promising strategy for expanding access to specialist eye care, particularly in underserved and remote regions. Teleophthalmology enables the remote diagnosis and management of eye conditions through digital imaging and communication platforms, reducing the need for patient travel while supporting primary-level care providers. AI-powered diagnostic tools can assist in the early detection of common eye diseases such as diabetic retinopathy and cataracts, enhancing service efficiency and coverage. Although infrastructural limitations and digital literacy remain barriers in some areas, pilot projects and strategic investment in digital eye care could complement human resource development efforts and strengthen service delivery in hard-to-reach communities.By implementing a multifaceted approach that encompasses these strategies, Ethiopia can work toward building a robust and resilient human resource base for eye health. However, successful implementation will require concerted efforts from government stakeholders, civil society organizations, academics, and the private sector to address systemic challenges and ensure the equitable provision of eye care services across the country.

Digital health innovations: Leveraging digital technologies such as teleophthalmology and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted screening tools presents a promising strategy for expanding access to specialist eye care, particularly in underserved and remote regions. Limitations

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, data were collected primarily through a structured, self-administered questionnaire completed by facility heads and senior eye care professionals. While this approach enables broad coverage across regions, it carries the inherent risk of self-reporting and recall bias, particularly regarding material resources, outreach service outputs, and monthly patient volumes. Independent verification of reported figures was not feasible owing to time and logistical constraints.

Second, although the study aimed to analyze the geographic distribution of human resources, we were not able to systematically classify facilities as urban or rural using standardized criteria. Instead, we relied on facility addresses and known service catchment areas to infer patterns of urban–rural disparity. These findings should be interpreted with some caution.

Third, the questionnaire was adapted from the WHO Health System Framework and used primarily to structure descriptive data collection; it was not formally validated as a measurement tool. While the WHO framework offers a strong conceptual foundation, the lack of formal validation is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Finally, while the study provides a regional overview, its cross-sectional design offers only a snapshot in time and does not account for seasonal variations or ongoing workforce changes. Despite these limitations, the findings offer valuable insights into workforce distribution and system gaps in eye health service delivery and can inform regional planning and policy development.
