Time to euthyroidism and its predictors among thyrotoxicosis patients on antithyroid drugs at tertiary hospitals, Western Oromia, Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Bokasa Negeri, Emiru Merdassa, Meseret Belete Fite, Tesfaye Shibiru, Firomsa Bekele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThyrotoxicosis is characterized by excessive action of thyroid hormones in tissues, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. Anti-thyroid drugs are the primary treatment for thyrotoxicosis; however, the time required to achieve euthyroid status varies among patients. The aim of this study was to determine the time to euthyroidism and its predictors among thyrotoxicosis patients on anti-thyroid drugs at tertiary hospitals in Nekemte city.MethodsA five-year hospital-based retrospective cohort study (from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2024) was conducted among patients treated with anti-thyroid drugs at Wollega University and Nekemte Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals. Data were extracted using a checklist, entered into Epi Data 4.6, cleaned, exported, and analyzed in STATA 14.1. Descriptive statistics (mean, median, incidence, interquartile range) were used to summarize the data. A Kaplan-Meier curve was used to estimate survival probabilities and compare between groups. The log-rank test was utilized to assess the existence of differences in survival probability. Bi-variable Cox regression analysis (with P ≤ 0.25) was performed to identify candidate predictors. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using a global test. Subsequently, in suba multivariable Cox regression analysis was conducted to determine the independent predictors of time to euthyroidism, with variables considered statistically significant at p-values < 0.05.ResultsIn this study, the median time to euthyroidism was 10.8 months. The incidence rate of achieving euthyroidism among thyrotoxicosis patients was 67.91 per 1000 person-months. The following factors were significantly associated with time to euthyroidism: Graves’ disease (AHR = 2.50; 95% CI: 1.23–5.07), WHO goiter grade 3 (AHR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.55–2.54), overt hyperthyroidism (AHR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.04–2.10), congestive heart failure (AHR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.35–0.91), and renal disease (AHR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.21–0.79).ConclusionThe time to euthyroidism in this study was comparatively long; however, the rate of normalization at the end of follow-up was relatively high. The study found that Graves’ disease, overt hyperthyroidism, WHO grade 3 goiter, congestive heart failure, and renal disease were significant predictors of time to euthyroidism in patients treated with anti-thyroid drugs. These findings emphasize the importance of prioritizing the management of these factors to improve euthyroidism outcomes in thyrotoxicosis patients.Keywords: Anti-thyroid drug, Euthyroidism, Nekemte, Predictors, Thyrotoxicosis

Introduction

Thyrotoxicosis is a clinical condition caused by excessively high thyroid hormone action in tissues, primarily due to elevated thyroid hormone levels [1, 2]. Affected individuals experience symptoms such as weakness, exhaustion, palpitations, dysphagia, anxiety, flushing, tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and elevated pulse pressure and shortness of breath [3]. This common endocrine metabolic disorder, which affects 0.2–1.2% of the general population [4], is categorized as either overt or subclinical thyrotoxicosis [2]. In the United States, the overall prevalence is approximately 1.2%, comprising 0.5% overt and 0.7% subclinical cases [2, 5]. Thyrotoxicosis most frequently results from Graves’ disease (GD). In populations with sufficient iodine, it accounts for 70–80% of cases; in areas with insufficient iodine, it accounts for about 50% [5]. Other etiologies include toxic nodular goiter, toxic multinodular goiter (TMNG), thyroiditis, and exogenous thyrotoxicosis (iatrogenic, factitious, or iodine-induced) [6]. In Africa, thyrotoxicosis remains a significant public health concern, often arising from the treatment of persistent iodine deficiency. The studies from the continent report prevalence rates ranging from 13% to 75%, highlighting substantial regional variation [7].

Thyrotoxicosis is also a common thyroid condition across the African continent, particularly in sub-Saharan countries [8]. Thyrotoxicosis has a prevalence of 66%, and Graves’ disease is the primary etiology, responsible for roughly 34.0% of cases [9]. The overall prevalence of thyroid disorders is estimated at 1.2%, with Graves’ disease identified as the leading cause of thyrotoxicosis [8]. Prolonged time to achieve euthyroid state can lead to severe consequences, including serious cardiovascular complications such as myocardial infarction and heart failure, as well as psychological distress marked by anxiety and fatigue [10, 11]. For hospitalized patients, failure to promptly treat thyrotoxicosis substantially increases the risk of life-threatening thyroid storm [12, 13]. Thyrotoxicosis raises the risk of thyroid storm and multi-organ failure involving the heart, kidneys, and liver [12–15]. The mortality rate from thyroid storm can exceed 10%, highlighting the critical importance of timely diagnosis and treatment [10].

Thyrotoxicosis significantly increases healthcare costs due to prolonged hospital stays and the need for intensive medical management [10, 16]. Patients with hyperthyroidism experience longer hospitalizations and higher overall costs compared to euthyroidism patients, reflecting a substantial burden on healthcare systems [10, 17]. This burden extends beyond physical health, adversely affecting patients’ quality of life, and emotional instability [18].

In managing thyrotoxicosis, achieving euthyroidism is essential to stabilize metabolism and relieve socio-demographic symptoms of hormone excess [19]. Restoring euthyroidism is vital in the management of thyroid disorders. Studies indicate that achieving a serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level within the normal reference range is essential for effectively managing thyrotoxicosis and improving patient quality of life [20]. Furthermore, within the euthyroidism range, higher TSH and lower free thyroxine (FT4) concentrations are associated with a reduced risk of adverse outcomes, including mortality, especially among older adults. This evidence underscores that maintaining euthyroidism is crucial for mitigating health risks and enhancing overall well-being [21].

The management of thyrotoxicosis in Ethiopia faces several challenges related to treatment options and drug availability [2]. Standard treatments include radioactive iodine, surgery, and thionamide antithyroid drugs (ATDs) such as propylthiouracil (PTU), carbimazole (CBZ), and methimazole (MMI) [22]. Treatment outcomes may be impacted by the initial choice of ATD [23]. Internationally, MMI is the preferred ATD over PTU and is recommended for all patients, with exceptions made for the first trimester of pregnancy and for those who experience side effects from MMI [2, 23, 24]. However, Ethiopia’s national formulary and treatment guidelines currently do not include MMI for thyrotoxicosis. Consequently, clinicians must rely predominantly on PTU, which is less effective, acts more slowly to achieve euthyroidism, and has a higher risk of side effects. This limitation contributes to prolonged treatment durations and suboptimal clinical outcomes for patients [25, 26].

Studies indicate that only 50–70% of patients treated with anti-thyroid medications achieve a successful outcome [5]. Despite the widespread use of (ATDs) propylthiouracil, a notable proportion of patients do not achieve euthyroidism within the expected timeframe [27, 28]. The rate of achieving euthyroidism varies significantly, with approximately 30–75% of patients reaching this state within six months to one year of treatment [6]. In Ethiopia, about half of patients achieve euthyroidism within nine months of treatment [29]. To date, limited data exist on the time to achieve euthyroidism among thyrotoxicosis patients in Ethiopia. As many studies are limited by small sample sizes and lack longitudinal data, hindering generalization across different demographics area. This study aims to determine the median time to euthyroidism and to identify predictors influencing euthyroid status in thyrotoxicosis patients at tertiary hospitals in Nekemte city. This understanding is crucial to hinder the factors that affect treatment care, as prolonged time to euthyroidism imposes significant burdens on patients and society.

Methods

Study setting, design and period

Five years retrospective cohort study design was conducted from January 1, 2020 to December 31, 2024, at two tertiary hospitals found in Nekemte City, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia. Nekemte city was found at 328 km west of Addis Ababa, capital city of Ethiopia.

Population

The source population includes all adult thyrotoxicosis patients who receive treatment with antithyroid drugs and attend the medical outpatient department (OPD), surgical OPD, or chronic ambulatory clinic at tertiary hospitals in Nekemte city. The study population, on the other hand, consists of all thyrotoxicosis patients who are selected during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

Patients with incomplete medical records such as: missing key laboratory results for FT3 (free triiodothyronine), FT4, and TSH; patients who took PTU for less than four weeks; patients who undergone thyroidectomy; pregnant and breastfeeding women were excluded.

Study variables and outcome endpoints

Time to euthyroidism in months was the primary outcome. It is the time duration measured from the initiation of anti-thyroid drug therapy until the patient achieves a stable euthyroid state from the laboratory result. The independent variables includes socio demographics and life style characteristic factors such as age, sex, residency and smoking; clinical characteristic factors such as etiology, comorbidity and WHO Goiter Size (0, 1, 2, 3); baseline TSH levels; baseline FT4 levels (free thyroxin); baseline T3 levels (triiodothyronine) and drug factors such as initial PTU dose, treatment modality (PTU only or Beta blocker and PTU) and drug side effect.

Operational definitions

Thyrotoxicosis

A clinical condition resulting from excessive levels of thyroid hormones in the body or TSH level < 0.4 mIU/L) [2].

Euthyroidism (event)

is normalization of thyroid function tests (TSH and/or FT4) according to the laboratory reference ranges used in the participating hospitals, when FT4, T3 and/or TSH are within the normal range:- (TSH, (0.4–5 mIU/L), FT4, (10.4–19.6pmol/l) and FT3, (4-5.3 pmol/l) [22, 30, 31].

Censored

Those subjects who were discharged against medical advice, discharged with significant problems, death other than thyrotoxicosis (accident or any cause not related to thyrotoxicosis), referred to other health facilities were considered as censored [32].

Overt hyperthyroidism

A clinical condition characterized by suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels below the normal reference range accompanied by elevated free thyroxine (FT4) and/or free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels.

Subclinical hyperthyroidism

A state where TSH levels are below the normal reference range, but free thyroid hormone levels (FT4 and FT3) remain within normal limits.

WHO goiter size

Grade 0, 1, 2, and 3 goiter sizes were classified based on WHO guidelines. Grade 0: When the neck is stretched, the goiter is not perceptible or visible. Grade 1: The goiter is detected on palpation and/or visible when the neck is extended. Grade 2: Goiter is visible when neck is in the normal position. Grade 3: Large goiter visible from distance [29, 33–35]

Sampling technique

From these two institutions, there were 1585 adult thyrotoxicosis patients registered and had follow-up. To select the 672 participants estimated for this study, a list of thyrotoxicosis patients was prepared as a sampling frame, and the number of participants selected from each institution was determined through proportional allocation. Then the final sample was selected using a systematic random sampling process from a list of patients, 1 through N1 and N2 at both institutions. The first card was chosen by simple random sample from the 1st to 2nd list of frame prepared by considering values of k. Then every kth interval (k = NT/n, k = 2) and the other cards were chosen to obtain a total sample size, which is proportionally allocated for both hospitals. Over the two months cohort duration 672 patients were followed. Out of these, 651patients were and 21 patients had incomplete medical charts. For final analysis, 651 patients were considered (Fig. 1).

Data collection tools and procedure

Data were collected through medical record reviews using a structured checklist adapted from different literatures [7, 26, 29, 30], which, contained three parts, such as socio-demographic items, clinical-related items, medication related items. Data were collected through retrospectively following of patient status over time using structured checklist. In this study setting, diagnosis of thyrotoxicosis was primarily based on clinical evaluation combined with biochemical thyroid function tests by physician note, because advanced diagnostic modalities such as radioactive iodine scintigraphy were not routinely available. Radioactive iodine scintigraphy and radioactive iodine therapy were not available at both hospitals. Therefore, radioactive iodine scintigraphy not performed for all patients. Graves’ disease was diagnosed based on typical clinical features (diffuse goiter, signs and symptoms of thyrotoxicosis) along with biochemical evidence of suppressed TSH and elevated FT4. Toxic multinodular goiter and toxic adenoma were identified based on clinical documentation indicating multinodular enlargement or solitary nodular thyroid disease where available. Destructive thyroiditis was excluded based on clinical course and physician documentation suggesting transient thyrotoxicosis without sustained antithyroid drug therapy. The laboratories values for subsequent TSH, FT4, and FT3 were followed-up until outcome or censoring. When the patient was confirmed euthyroid; the exact date was recorded using two consecutive laboratory results of both TSH and FT4 levels. Patients who don’t reach euthyroid at the end of the study date, who died, transferred out, defaulted, or lost to follow-up were extracted as censored data. Based on baseline laboratory (FT4, FT3, and TSH) captured, thyrotoxicosis was categorized as overt hyperthyroidism and subclinical. Other variables such as; socio-demographic characteristics, etiology (Graves, toxic MNG, toxic adenoma), date of diagnosis, treatment details (initial ATD dose, treatment modalities, side effects, thyrotoxicosis complications & comorbidities), and WHO grade of goiter size were captured as they were documented in the patient chart.

Data quality assurance

Before actual data collection, training was given for four data collectors (two BSc Nurses, two pharmacists) on contents of the data extraction checklist and how to abstract data from patient medical records and health facility logbooks for two days prior to the data collection period by the principal investigator and one experienced supervisor with a master’s degree in public health. Furthermore, construct validity and internal consistency reliability was checked by Pearson product-moment correlation and Cronbach alpha, respectively. Following that, the questionnaire was pre-tested for consistency on 5% (34 medical records) of the sample size in Ambo Tertiary Hospital before data collection. Finally, the supervisor checked the collected data for completeness and any irregularities in the fulfilled checklist.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were coded and entered into Epi-data version 4.6 and exported to STATA version 14.1 for further data processing such as cleaning then exported for analysis data. The data was described using descriptive statistics such as mean with standard deviations (SD) for age, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) was used to describe time variable. Kaplan-Meier curve was employed to estimate the overall time to euthyroidism, and a log-rank test was used to compare the observed difference in survival time between significantly associated categorical variables. Then bi-variable Cox regression analysis was performed to select variables that have a p < 0.25 were considered for multi-variable Cox regression model analysis. Before running the multi-variable model, Cox proportional hazard assumptions were checked, using global test, then Schoenfeld residual test to identify which variable violates PH assumption. As a result, no violation of Proportional hazard (PH) assumption detected (Prob.> chi2 = 0.3267). A Multivariable Cox regression model was used to declare predictors of euthyroidism at P-value < 0.05 with corresponding AHR and 95% CI.

Results

Socio-demographics and lifestyle related characteristics of the participants

Over the study period, 672 patients were followed at tertiary hospitals in Nekemte city, with data successfully extracted from 651 records, yielding a response rate of 96.875%. Among the 21 excluded records, six pertained to pregnant patients due to incomplete data. In terms of socio- demographic characteristics, 93.5% of participants under 40 years achieved a euthyroidism state, compared to 91.5% of those aged 40 and above. Sex distribution showed that 92.5% of females and 90.5% of males attained euthyroidism status. Geographic analysis indicated that 90% of rural participants and 94.9% of urban residents achieved euthyroidism states. Notably, 93.1% of never smokers were in the euthyroidism state, while only 53.8% of past smokers achieved this condition (Table 2).

Clinical Related characteristics of the participants

In this cohort study examining the etiology of thyrotoxicosis, three primary categories were identified: Graves’ disease (95.8% achieving euthyroid state), toxic multinodular goiter (91.4%), and toxic adenoma (52.9%). Notably, 92.6% of hyperthyroid patients without complications attained a euthyroidism compared to 87.2% of those with complications. Cardiac complications status showed that 92.8% achieved euthyroidism without cardiac complications, while compared with 82.8% with the complication. Thyroid storm patients presented a notable disparity, with only 64.7% achieving euthyroid status. Severity of hyperthyroidism also influenced outcomes, with 92.9% of overt hyperthyroidism patients achieving euthyroid compared to 83.7% of subclinical cases. Furthermore, patients’ WHO goiter grades (Grade 0 to Grade 3) exhibited high euthyroid achievement rates, ranging from 89.9% to 94.4%. The duration of symptoms indicated that 93.2% of patients with symptoms lasting less than two years reached euthyroid status, while this figure decreased to 89.3% for those symptomatic for over two years. Among patients without comorbidities, 93.3% achieved euthyroid, contrasting with 90.4% in those with comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, renal diseases, malaria, and hypertension, all showing variable outcomes in attaining euthyroid states (Table 3).

Time to euthyroidism of study participants

The median time to euthyroidism was 10.80 months, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 5.9 months. Whereas the shortest and longest time to euthyroidism were 1.33 month and 52.7 months, respectively. The overall rate of being euthyroidism among thyrotoxicosis patient enrolled on ATDs was 67.91 per 1000 person-months (95 CI: 62.69–73.56) with the total person-time 8849.8 person-months.

The Kaplan–Meier survival estimate of time to euthyroidism showed a progressive decline in the probability of remaining hyperthyroid throughout the follow-up period. During the first six months of treatment, the probability of remaining hyperthyroid decreased only slightly from 1.0 to approximately 0.99, indicating that very few patients achieved euthyroidism during the early treatment phase. Between 6 and 12 months, the curve declined sharply to about 0.40, showing that nearly 60% of patients achieved euthyroidism within the first year of therapy. This period represented the phase of most rapid treatment response, suggesting that the majority of patients responded effectively to anti-thyroid drugs during this time. Between 12 and 18 months, the probability continued to decline but at a slower rate, reaching about 0.19, corresponding to an 81% cumulative recovery rate. After 18 months, the curve flattened progressively, with the probability of remaining hyperthyroid dropping only slightly to 0.10 by 24 months and below 0.05 by 30 months. This plateau pattern indicates that few new recoveries occurred beyond two years of treatment, implying that most patients who would achieve euthyroidism had already done so. By the end of the 53-month follow-up period, the survival probability approached zero, suggesting that nearly all patients had attained euthyroidism (Fig. 2).

Proportional hazard assumption test

To identify predictors significantly associated with the time to euthyroidism, the Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed. A key assumption of this model is that the hazard ratios are proportional over time, meaning the effect of covariates on the hazard is constant throughout the study period. In this study, Cox proportional hazard assumptions were checked, using global test, then Schoenfeld residual test to identify which variable violates PH assumption. As a result, no violation of PH assumption detected according to global test (Prob.> chi2 = 0.3267). Additionally, the model’s adequacy was evaluated using the Cox-Snell residuals, which confirmed a good fit to the data. These diagnostic tests support the validity of the Cox regression model in this analysis (Fig. 4).

Predictors of time to euthyroidism of participants

In bi-variables cox regression analysis, variables such as: age, tobacco use, residence, etiology thyrotoxicosis, complications of hyperthyroidism, cardiac complications, thyroid storm, thyrotoxicosis classifications, WHO goiter size(0, 1, 2, 3), and rash, liver toxicity, presence of comorbidity, CHF, renal disease and hypertension found to be candidates for multivariable cox regression analysis at P-value ≤ 0.25. In the multivariable cox regression analysis, five predictors were found to be significantly associated with time to euthyroidism among patients with thyrotoxicosis at P-value < 0.05. These included WHO goiter size, etiology of thyrotoxicosis, thyrotoxicosis classification (overt and subclinical), CHF, renal disease. According to this study the etiology of thyrotoxicosis showed a strong association, where patients diagnosed with Graves’ disease achieved euthyroidism around 2.5 times more rapidly than those with toxic adenoma (AHR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.23–5.07, P = 0.011). Similarly, the classification of thyrotoxicosis was a significant predictor; patients with overt hyperthyroidism were 1.48 times more likely to reach euthyroidism earlier compared to those with subclinical disease (AHR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.04–2.10, P = 0.028).

Regarding goiter size, increasing WHO goiter grade was associated with a higher rate of likelihood of achieving euthyroidism patients with grade 2 and grade 3 goiter size attained euthyroidism more quickly than those with grade 0 (AHR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.04–1.71, P = 0.023; and AHR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.55–2.53, P < 0.001, respectively).In contrast, the presence of congestive heart failure (CHF) and renal disease significantly delayed the attainment of euthyroidism. Patients with CHF had a 44% lower rate of chance to euthyroidism (AHR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.35–0.91, P = 0.019) while compared with those has no CHF, similarly thyrotoxicosis patients with renal disease were 60% less likely to achieve euthyroidism compared to those without renal problems (AHR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.21–0.79, P = 0.008) (Table 5).

Discussions

As this study aimed to determine time to euthyroidism and its predictors among thyrotoxicosis patients treated with antithyroid drugs, it found that the median time to reach euthyroid status is 10.8 months, while the incidence of achieving euthyroid status is 67.91 person-time months per 1000, with a total of 8849.8 person-months.

In the current study the median time to be euthyroid is higher than study conducted at St. Paul’s Addis Ababa (7 month) [34], northern Ethiopia (9 months) [29], but study from Gondar reported the median time to euthyroidism as 11.13 months [26]. The discrepancy of time to euthyroidism in the current study and previous study is due to geographical, sociocultural and time of study conducted between the current and previous study.

Regarding the incidence rate of time to euthyroid, this study is nearly similar to the one reported from St. Paul’s Addis Ababa 68.4 per 1000 person-months [34]. This similarity could be due to comparable patient profiles means patient characteristics, treatment approaches, and follow-up periods among these settings.

Also, the present study identified several predictors that significantly influenced the time to achieve euthyroid among patients with thyrotoxicosis. The finding that patients with Graves’ disease attained euthyroid more rapidly than those with toxic adenoma aligns with existing evidence suggesting that autoimmune hyperthyroidism tends to respond favorably to antithyroid medications due to the reversible nature of thyroid stimulation by thyroid-stimulating antibodies rather than permanent nodular tissue activity [36–38].

In contrast, toxic adenoma and toxic multinodular goiter often involve autonomous hormone-producing nodules, which respond less effectively to medical therapy and may require longer treatment durations or definitive interventions such as radioiodine therapy or surgery [38]. According to this study the association between overt hyperthyroidism and higher to be euthyroid may be explained by the fact that overt cases are clinically apparent, leading to earlier diagnosis and more aggressive therapeutic management compared to subclinical disease, which is often monitored conservatively [2]. This contradicts with studies from Ethiopia and Edinburgh reporting that severely elevated FT4 predicts prolonged hyperthyroidism [24, 26]. However, a Swedish study did not find FT4 or FT3 to be significant prognostic factors, possibly due to differing etiologies and treatment protocols [39]. Similarly, patients with larger goiter sizes (WHO grade 2 and 3) were found to be normalize thyroid function more rapidly. But it is contrasts with study conducted at South Tigray, Ethiopia [29]. The possible reason was, visible large goiters trigger earlier detection, aggressive dosing, and monitoring unlike standardized protocols. Although this may seem counterintuitive, it might be attributed to better treatment responsiveness in patients’ classic clinical guidance and reviews explain that very large goiters or severe hyperthyroidism are often managed differently and possible explanations for this observation include differences in local treatment protocols, earlier clinical detection and more aggressive dosing in patients with visibly large goiters, measurement or classification differences, or residual confounding [29, 40]. Furthermore, current study revealed that the presence of congestive heart failure (CHF) and renal disease were associated with delayed attainment of euthyroid. These comorbidities may interfere with thyroid hormone metabolism and clearance, alter drug pharmacokinetics, and necessitate cautious dose adjustments that prolong therapeutic response [41, 42]. Additionally, systemic illnesses such as CHF and renal disease may reflect more severe metabolic derangements, thereby extending the duration required for biochemical stabilization.

The presence of CHF was identified in the current study as a significant predictor prolonging the time to euthyroidism. This is consistent with the finding from India [43]. This is due to the fact that the systemic effects of heart failure impair thyroid hormone metabolism and clearance, resulting in prolonged disease duration before euthyroidism status is achieved, systemic hypo perfusion and reduced clearance of thyroid hormones and antithyroid drugs [44, 45]. Also, regarding renal disease, this was identified in the current study as a significant predictor of prolonging the time to euthyroidism. This is similar to the finding of the United State of America [46]. This study finding is supported by study conducted at Harrisburg, USA [47] China [48].The possible reason could be shared mechanisms include reduced antithyroid drug clearance, and impaired renal clearance delays ATD metabolism.

Limitation of study

As the strengths, the study involved a larger sample size than other studies conducted in our country, which enhances its representativeness, and it was multi-center, providing greater generalizability to the findings. Nevertheless, this study would like to acknowledge limitations such as the study design relied on existing medical records, which might miss important variables. Treatment intensity may partially reflect disease severity and that residual confounding related to medication dosing cannot be fully excluded in a retrospective study design. Being retrospective in nature, this cannot address adherence to ATDs, which might have an effect on time to euthyroidism. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate remission or relapse rates.

Conclusion

In this study, although it took longer than expected to achieve euthyroidism, the majority of the patients attained euthyroidism at the end of ten months of the study follow-up. Time to achieve euthyroid among thyrotoxicosis patients on antithyroid drugs are influenced by Graves’ disease, overt hyperthyroidism, larger goiter sizes (grade 2 and 3), and having comorbidities such as CHF and renal disease. The findings highlight the importance of individualized management strategies in patients with thyrotoxicosis, considering both disease-specific and patient-related factors that influence treatment outcomes.
