Synchronous bone and adrenal extramedullary plasmacytoma: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Malek Bouhani, Rabeb Barred, Mohamed Amine Bouida, Marwa Somai, Aya Khemir, Olfa Jaidane, Tarek Ben Dhiab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdrenal extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) is an exceptionally rare plasma cell neoplasm with fewer than 20 documented cases worldwide. The clinical presentation, optimal management strategies, and long-term outcomes remain poorly defined.Case presentationWe report the case of a 51-year-old woman with synchronous bone and adrenal EMP, without multiple myeloma. The initial presentation involved pathological humeral fracture following minor trauma, with the subsequent discovery of a 40 mm left adrenal mass on staging CT. Emergency orthopedic fixation and bone biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of EMP. Immunohistochemical findings showed CD138, MUM1, and kappa light-chain restriction positivity, with negative lambda staining. After declining initial surgical intervention, the patient was lost to follow-up for two years, during which the adrenal lesion enlarged to 70 mm. Comprehensive staging including bone marrow biopsy, FDG-PET/CT, and laboratory workup confirmed absence of multiple myeloma features according to International Myeloma Working Group criteria. Complete laparoscopic left adrenalectomy was successfully performed, with uncomplicated recovery. Histopathological examination revealed a 75 × 55 × 30 mm encapsulated mass with characteristic plasma cell morphology and extensive hemorrhagic foci. The Ki-67 proliferation index was < 10% and Congo red staining was positive. Adjuvant lumbo-aortic radiotherapy (45 Gy) was administered. The patient achieved complete clinical and radiological responses at three-month follow-up.ConclusionsSynchronous EMP without multiple myeloma is an exceptionally rare entity that requires comprehensive systemic evaluation and multidisciplinary management. Contemporary management favors laparoscopic resection combined with adjuvant radiotherapy for optimal local control. Excellent survival outcomes support aggressive therapy, although lifelong surveillance remains essential given the potential progression to systemic disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04039-y.Keywords: Extramedullary plasmacytoma, Adrenal gland, Synchronous tumors, Laparoscopic adrenalectomy, Plasma cell neoplasm, Surgical oncology

Introduction

Plasmacytomas arise from clonal proliferation of plasma cells and represent part of the spectrum of plasma cell dyscrasias, which encompasses a heterogeneous group of progressively more severe monoclonal gammopathies, ranging from benign monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) to malignant multiple myeloma (MM). Plasma cell dyscrasias are characterized by the expansion of monoclonal bone marrow plasma cells that produce monoclonal immunoglobulins, with the clinical spectrum including MGUS, smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM), multiple myeloma, plasmacytomas, and systemic AL amyloidosis [1]. Systemic disease criteria for plasma cell dyscrasias are defined by the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) and include the following.

MGUS: Serum monoclonal protein < 3 g/dL, bone marrow clonal plasma cells < 10%, and absence of myeloma-defining events [1]

MGUS: Serum monoclonal protein < 3 g/dL, bone marrow clonal plasma cells < 10%, and absence of myeloma-defining events [1]

SMM: Serum monoclonal protein ≥ 3 g/dL and/or bone marrow clonal plasma cells 10–60%, with absence of myeloma-defining events [1]

SMM: Serum monoclonal protein ≥ 3 g/dL and/or bone marrow clonal plasma cells 10–60%, with absence of myeloma-defining events [1]

Multiple Myeloma: Presence of myeloma-defining events (CRAB features: hypercalcemia, renal dysfunction, anemia, bone lesions) or validated biomarkers (≥ 60% bone marrow plasma cells, involved/uninvolved free light chain ratio ≥ 100, or > 1 focal lesion on MRI) [1]

Multiple Myeloma: Presence of myeloma-defining events (CRAB features: hypercalcemia, renal dysfunction, anemia, bone lesions) or validated biomarkers (≥ 60% bone marrow plasma cells, involved/uninvolved free light chain ratio ≥ 100, or > 1 focal lesion on MRI) [1]

Extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) is defined as a solitary, localized accumulation of neoplastic plasma cells occurring outside the bone marrow, with bone marrow plasma cell infiltration below 10% (typically < 5%), and without evidence of systemic plasma cell dyscrasias. According to the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria, the diagnosis of EMP requires: (1) a biopsy-confirmed solitary lesion composed of clonal plasma cells; (2) a normal bone marrow aspirate and biopsy showing no clonal plasma cells; (3) a normal skeletal survey or advanced imaging excluding additional bone lesions; and (4) absence of CRAB features (hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, Anemia, Bone lesions) [2, 3]. EMPs are rare, representing only 3–5% of all plasma cell neoplasms [4].

They most often arise in the head and neck regions, with approximately 80–90% of cases involving the upper respiratory tract (e.g., nasal cavity, sinuses, or oropharynx) or other head/neck sites [5]. Less commonly, EMPs have been reported in other organs, such as the gastrointestinal tract [6], thyroid [7], skin [8], and others [9–12]. Adrenal gland involvement in plasmacytoma is exceptionally rare and often misdiagnosed. In the past two decades, a few additional cases have emerged, but fewer than 15 cases of solitary adrenal plasmacytomas (AEMP) without multiple myeloma have been described in the literature [13–27] (Table 1).

Unlike all previously reported AEMP, which have occurred as solitary tumors or with only locoregional lymphatic involvement, this is the first reported instance of truly synchronous extramedullary plasmacytomas arising at anatomically distant sites (bone and adrenal) without any evidence of multiple myeloma. Our patient presented with humeral plasmacytoma and a concurrent adrenal lesion; however, bone marrow evaluation and systemic workup revealed no plasma cell dyscrasia. Dual extramedullary presentations at the initial diagnosis and the absence of myeloma criteria are extraordinarily rare. Therefore, we describe this novel case, offer a comprehensive literature review, and discuss the underlying pathophysiology, diagnostic challenges, and optimal management strategies for extramedullary plasmacytomas, in accordance with the CARE reporting guidelines.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old woman with no personal or family history of plasma cell dyscrasias or hematologic malignancies presented in 2023 with acute-onset right elbow pain following a low-energy fall from a standing height. Radiographs demonstrated a comminuted fracture of the distal right humerus incongruent with the mechanism of injury, prompting an intraoperative biopsy at the time of plate fixation. Histopathological examination revealed a monoclonal plasma cell infiltrate; immunohistochemistry was positive for CD138 and MUM1 with kappa light chain restriction and negative for lambda, establishing the diagnosis of plasmacytoma. Subsequent staging with contrast-enhanced thoraco-abdomino-pelvic computed tomography (CT) revealed a well-circumscribed, homogeneous 40 mm para-aortic mass originating from the left adrenal gland (35 HU), which demonstrated mild fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake (SUVmax 3.4) on positron emission tomography with FDG (FDG-PET-CT) (Fig. 1). Bone marrow biopsy revealed < 5% plasma cells, and comprehensive laboratory evaluations, including complete blood count, serum calcium, renal function tests, serum and urine immunofixation, and free light chain assay, were within normal limits (Supplementary File I). The patient declined adrenal surgery and received 50 Gy of external beam radiotherapy to the humerus. She was lost to follow-up until 2025, when despite remaining asymptomatic (blood pressure 110/70 mmHg), a repeat CT scan revealed enlargement of the adrenal lesion to 70 mm (Fig. 2). Biochemical screening results for catecholamine, metanephrine, cortisol, and aldosterone excess remained negative. CT scan-guided adrenal biopsy confirmed plasmacytoma with an immunophenotype identical to that of the humeral lesion. Laparoscopic left adrenalectomy was performed with the patient in the dorsal decubitus position, using four trocars. After reflecting the left colon and parietal peritoneum, Gerota’s fascia and pancreatic tail were exposed (Fig. 3). Sharp and blunt dissection then mobilized the adrenal mass from its attachments along the lateral aortic border, inferior pancreatic tail, and superior pole of the left kidney, with careful preservation of the adjacent aorta, inferior mesenteric vein, and pancreas. The tumor was removed en bloc within an intact capsule, achieving negative margins, and extracted in an endoscopic retrieval bag to prevent spillage. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient resumed normal activities and returned to work within one week.

The patient will undergo comprehensive surveillance consisting of clinical examination and laboratory monitoring (serum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, free light chain ratio, complete blood count, electrolytes, renal and liver function, glucose, and calcium) every three months for the first two years, then every six months until year five; imaging surveillance with CT scan or FDG-PET/CT will be performed annually for the first five years.

Discussion

In contrast to the sole report by Kahara et al. in 2001, in which an adrenal plasmacytoma coexisted with regional lymph node involvement, our patient was the first documented case of truly synchronous, anatomically distant bone and adrenal extramedullary plasmacytomas in the absence of multiple myeloma [14]. The indolent enlargement of the adrenal lesion over two years without systemic progression, coupled with its occurrence in a female patient (contrary to the slight male predominance in the literature), expands our understanding of AEMP’s clinical spectrum, natural history, and demographic variability of AEMP. Although extramedullary plasmacytomas most commonly arise within the head and neck region (80–90% of cases), adrenal localization remains exceptionally rare, representing less than 5% of all plasmacytomas [5, 28–30]. A systematic review of the literature from 2001 to 2025 identified only 15 cases of AEMP (Table 1), underscoring the novelty and significance of this presentation.

Demographics and tumor characteristics

The literature reveals a remarkable geographic distribution, with cases reported in Asia (Japan, China) [13, 14, 16, 18, 20, 23, 24], Europe (Spain, Austria) [22, 25], North America (USA) [19, 27], Australia [21], Africa (Morocco, Egypt, Tunisia) [15, 26], and the Middle East (Saudi Arabia) [17], indicating a worldwide occurrence without apparent ethnic predisposition. The reported cases demonstrated a slight male predominance (approximately 1.5:1, nine males versus six females, one case did not report sex) and predominantly affected middle-aged to elderly patients, with a median age at presentation of 52 years (range, 19–81 years). The youngest patient was 19 years old, and the oldest was 81 years [13–27]. The median age at EMP diagnosis is approximately 55–60 years, with a male predominance (approximately 3:1 male-to-female ratio) in most cases [4]. While most AEMPs are unilateral (right-sided in 43.75% and left-sided in 31.25%), bilateral involvement has been documented in 18.75% of cases without concurrent bone marrow disease or evidence of multiple myeloma [13–27]. Tumor size varies widely, with a median diameter of approximately 65 mm (range, 35–135 mm), with the largest lesion (135 mm) reported in an HIV-positive patient who experienced aggressive disease and early mortality and smaller tumors (30–40 mm) observed in several asymptomatic cases [13–27]. Although exceptionally large tumors may portend worse outcomes, no clear correlation exists between lesion size and symptomatology, although exceptionally large tumors may portend worse outcomes [15].

Clinical presentation and workup

Most AEMPs are incidentally discovered as adrenal incidentalomas during imaging studies performed for unrelated indications [14, 16, 17, 22]. In our case, the adrenal lesion was initially identified on a trauma workup CT scan following a pathological fracture of the humerus caused by concurrent plasmacytoma. The patient exhibited no clinical or laboratory signs of hormonal hypersecretion or classic adrenal syndrome; blood pressure was normal, and no hormonal abnormalities were found, consistent with the non-secretory nature of AEMPs. Symptomatic presentations directly attributable to AEMPs are rare. Notably, Chennoufi et al. reported a rare case of a rapidly enlarging adrenal mass causing local compressive symptoms and systemic “B symptoms” (fever and weight loss) in a patient with advanced HIV infection [15]. However, unlike functional adrenal tumors, such as pheochromocytomas, AEMPs are typically nonfunctional and do not produce catecholamine-related symptoms.

From a diagnostic and management perspective, distinguishing solitary plasmacytomas from multiple myeloma is critical. According to the International Myeloma Working Group criteria and consensus guidelines, the diagnosis of solitary plasmacytoma (osseous or extramedullary) mandates the following: (1) a solitary mass composed of clonal plasma cells confirmed by biopsy; (2) bone marrow plasma cell infiltration < 10%, often < 5%; (3) absence of additional bone lesions on skeletal survey or advanced imaging (such as FGD-PET/CT or whole-body MRI); and (4) no evidence of myeloma-defining organ damage, specifically, no CRAB features (hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, Anemia, Bone lesions) [31].

Our patient met all the following criteria: bone marrow biopsy revealed no significant plasma cell infiltration; comprehensive imaging confirmed no other lesions beyond the known humeral and adrenal masses; and laboratory parameters, including serum calcium, renal function, and hematologic indices, were within normal limits. Thus, despite harboring two distinct plasmacytomas, the patient did not fulfill the criteria for multiple myeloma, a rare clinical scenario commonly described as multiple solitary plasmacytomas or synchronous plasmacytomas without systemic myeloma involvement. Recognizing this distinction is paramount because the prognosis and therapeutic approach for solitary plasmacytomas differ substantially from those for disseminated myeloma.

This case also highlights the necessity of exhaustive systemic evaluation whenever an extramedullary plasmacytoma is diagnosed, including bone marrow biopsy and sensitive imaging modalities such as FDG-PET/CT or whole-body MRI, to exclude occult multiple myeloma and guide appropriate management.

Given the adrenal location, all patients, including our 51-year-old with a 70 mm left adrenal plasmacytoma, underwent comprehensive biochemical testing to exclude hormone-secreting tumors and detect subclinical adrenal insufficiency [17, 18]. Plasma and urinary catecholamines and metanephrines were measured in most cases and consistently fell within normal limits, distinguishing plasmacytomas from pheochromocytomas, while cortisol and aldosterone levels were normal and no cushingoid or virilization signs were observed [13–27]. Nonetheless, because large or invasive adrenal lesions can impair adrenal reserve, formal endocrine evaluation before laparoscopic adrenalectomy, including a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test, plasma/urinary metanephrines, and aldosterone–renin ratio, is essential, with follow-up corticotropin stimulation testing at four weeks to confirm postoperative adrenal function [32]. The American Association of Endocrine Surgeons Guidelines endorse this pre- and postoperative testing, perioperative glucocorticoid supplementation tailored to surgical stress, and long-term adrenal monitoring to prevent and manage insufficiency [33].

In terms of plasma cell markers, most patients underwent serum protein electrophoresis and IgG-free light chain assays as part of the myeloma workup. In several cases, these studies showed no monoclonal gammopathy, consistent with a truly localized disease. A few exceptions exist. For instance, Kahara et al. reported that their patient had detectable monoclonal IgG-λ protein in the serum [14], even though bone marrow involvement was absent. However, M-proteins are absent or minimal in the majority of cases (reflecting the absence of systemic myeloma) [13–27]. All patients had diagnostic confirmation via histopathology and immunohistochemistry of the resected or biopsied tumor, demonstrating sheets of neoplastic plasma cells (CD138-positive, light chain restricted) consistent with plasmacytoma in each case.

Recent advances in molecular cytogenetics and next-generation sequencing have identified distinct genetic and epigenetic profiles in extramedullary plasmacytomas (EMPs) compared with multiple myeloma, elucidating their unique pathogenesis and clinical behavior. EMPs exhibit a lower incidence of t(11;14) translocations and fewer high-risk cytogenetic lesions such as del(17p) and 1q gains, which may explain their more localized clinical course [34, 35].

FISH analysis by Bink et al. of 38 EMP cases revealed IGH breaks in 37%, including t(4;14) translocations, but t (11; 14), t (14; 16), and t (8; 14) translocations, commonly seen in multiple myeloma, were notably absent, indicating alternative oncogenic pathways in EMP [36]. Loss of chromosome 13q was found in 40% of the cases, with chromosomal gains of 82%, reflecting genomic instability [37]. These findings align with those of McAvera et al., who reported high-risk abnormalities in extramedullary multiple, such as t(4;14), del(17p13), and chromosome 1 aberrations [35]. The absence of t(11;14), present in 15–20% of multiple myeloma cases and associated with cyclin D1 overexpression, is especially noteworthy in EMPs [35].

Immunophenotypic studies have demonstrated that EMPs lack cyclin D1 expression (p < 0.001) and show infrequent CD56 positivity (p < 0.001) compared to multiple myeloma [38]. These tumors also exhibited weaker Bcl-2 staining, low p21 and p53 overexpression, and lower proliferation indices than extramedullary multiple myeloma (p = 0.008). Additionally, EMP commonly downregulate CD56 and upregulate CD44, aiding diagnostic and prognostic assessments [36].

Epigenetic analyses have revealed the hypermethylation of genes such as SHP1, CDKN2A, CDH1, CD56, and CXCR4 in extramedullary sites, with SHP1 hypermethylation linked to constitutive JAK/STAT3 activation, promoting disease progression and bone marrow escape [33]. Methylation differences between bone marrow and extramedullary lesions suggest spatial epigenetic heterogeneity, potentially underlying EMP’s localized growth of EMP [33].

The distinctive molecular profile of EMPs impacts therapeutic strategies; CD56 downregulation and altered adhesion molecule expression may reduce the efficacy of certain immunotherapies used in multiple myeloma, while upregulated proliferative and metabolic pathways present novel targets [35, 39].

Although detailed cytogenetic and molecular profiling (e.g., FISH for IGH rearrangements, del(17p), t(4;14), and next-generation sequencing panels) have been shown to provide important prognostic and personalized therapeutic insights into EMP, such analyses were not performed in our series, including the present 2025 case. This limitation reflects the current lack of in-house molecular diagnostics and prohibitive cost of outsourced testing at our institution. We acknowledge that the integration of molecular risk factors (such as high-risk translocations and proliferation indices) would enhance biological characterization and refine the risk stratification. We plan to prospectively incorporate such analyses as resource permits.

Imaging characteristics

Radiological evaluation is central to the identification and characterization of adrenal masses. All patients underwent cross-sectional imaging, most often computed tomography, with selected cases using MRI and/or FGD-PET/CT for further evaluation and staging.

CT was the most frequently used modality, employed in at least 12 of the 16 cases. On CT, AEMPs usually appear as solid, heterogeneous, soft-tissue masses [20]. Unlike typical benign adrenal adenomas, ACCs lack macroscopic fat and often exhibit irregular enhancement. Fujikata et al. described a large 100 mm right adrenal tumor with heterogeneous attenuation on CT that infiltrated the surrounding structures [23]. The extensive tumor required multiorgan resection (including part of the liver and inferior vena cava). Notably, AEMPs do not exhibit rapid contrast washout characteristics of adrenal adenomas and can be confused with metastatic lesions on imaging. The presence of a solitary adrenal mass in a patient without known cancer, however, should raise a broad differential diagnosis, including the possibility of unusual tumors such as plasmacytoma.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed in six cases and provided additional soft-tissue contrast to characterize the lesion [14, 16, 18, 19, 23, 26]. On MRI, AEMPs typically show low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, reflecting their cellular soft-tissue nature [12, 14, 21, 24]. Li et al. and Rogers et al. reported heterogeneous T2 signals within tumors [16, 17]. In the bilateral case reported by Li et al., the adrenal lesions demonstrated certain MRI features (such as intermediate intensity and moderate enhancement) that helped distinguish them from other pathologies, such as adenomas or metastases [16]. MRI can be particularly useful when CT attenuation is indeterminate and further supports the suspicion of a plasma cell tumor in some instances (when combined with clinical and laboratory clues).

FDG-PET/CT has been employed in a few recent cases to assess metabolic activity and screen for additional lesions. Townend et al. used FDG-PET/CT in patients with bilateral AEMPs to ensure that no other disease foci were present [21]. In that case, the adrenal lesions were FDG-avid, which was expected because plasmacytomas are generally metabolically active. Our patient also underwent FDG-PET/CT, which showed mildly to moderately increased uptake in the left adrenal tumor (SUV_max ~ 3.4). This SUV level is somewhat lower than that in high-grade malignancies; indeed, some AEMPs have shown only mild FDG uptake overlapping with benign lesions [21, 40, 41]. In summary, although FDG-PET/CT is not diagnostic of plasmacytoma, it is valuable for staging to confirm the absence of additional lesions (crucial for verifying solitary disease) and can help monitor the treatment response or detect recurrence during follow-up.

Diagnostic challenges and differential diagnosis and risk stratification

The evaluation of adrenal masses in middle-aged patients encompasses a broad differential diagnosis, as three-quarters of AEMPs present as nonfunctioning incidentalomas that closely resemble benign adenomas. Lesions exceeding 40 mm, which are significantly larger than the typical < 40 mm adenoma (median 65 mm in our cohort), should be suspected [42, 43]. In contrast to adenomas, AEMPs often demonstrate heterogeneous enhancement on contrast-enhanced CT scans and lack the rapid washout characteristic of lipid-rich lesions [44]. Furthermore, FDG-PET/CT may reveal variable FDG uptake, distinguishing metabolically active plasmacytomas from inert adenomas [21, 40]. Given these imaging features, risk stratification is essential to guide individualized management: low-risk plasmacytomas (< 5 cm, undetectable M‐protein, no adverse cytogenetics) may be treated with radiotherapy alone (40–50 Gy) and biannual monitoring [3]; intermediate-risk tumors (5–10 cm or low-level M‐protein without high-risk cytogenetics) benefit from combined surgical resection and radiotherapy with laboratory reassessment every 3–4 months [45]; and high-risk lesions (> 10 cm, persistent M‐protein post‐local therapy, high-risk cytogenetics such as del(17p) or t(4;14), or Ki‐67 > 10%) warrant aggressive local control plus early systemic therapy in clinical trials [46]. Our patient’s adrenal plasmacytoma measured 75 × 55 × 30 mm with a Ki‐67 index < 10%, categorizing her as intermediate-risk, and supporting our decision for complete laparoscopic adrenalectomy followed by adjuvant 45 Gy radiotherapy and close systemic surveillance.

Treatment modalities

Definitive therapy for AEMPs often involves surgical resection of an adrenal mass. Indeed, 12 of the 16 reported cases (80%) underwent surgery as the primary treatment for the adrenal lesion [13, 14, 16, 18–25]. Laparoscopic adrenalectomy has been the preferred approach in recent years, reflecting the advances in minimally invasive surgery. At least 7 cases were managed via laparoscopic adrenalectomy [14, 16, 19, 20, 24, 25]. For example, Kahara et al. and Asahi et al. reported successful laparoscopic removal of AEMPs, which were among the earliest reported cases, establishing the feasibility of minimally invasive resection two decades ago [14, 16]. Gasz et al. also performed a laparoscopic resection, which involved partial resection of a large 97 mm left adrenal tumor, combined with ablation of the residual tissue [25]. Our patient underwent complete laparoscopic left adrenalectomy.

Open surgery is reserved for specific situations, particularly for very large tumors or bilateral diseases. Five patients underwent open adrenalectomy because of these considerations [13, 18, 21–23]. For instance, Li et al. required open bilateral adrenalectomies given the involvement of both glands [18]. In extreme cases, an open multivisceral resection is necessary (Fujikata et al. found that an adrenal tumor invaded the liver and inferior vena cava, necessitating a right adrenalectomy with partial hepatectomy, nephrectomy, and IVC resection [23]. Such extensive surgery is rarely required; however, it underscores the fact that AEMPs can occasionally be locally aggressive. Overall, complete surgical excision was achieved in all resected cases, providing tissue for definitive diagnosis and cytoreduction.

Given that EMPs are radiosensitive tumors, radiotherapy (RT) is often used as an adjunct or an alternative treatment. 7 of the 15 patients (46.7%) received RT to the adrenal region, typically a dose of the order of 40–50 Gy in standard fractions [14–16, 19, 23, 25]. Adjuvant RT is commonly administered after surgery to patients with residual disease or a high risk of local recurrence. For example, Asahi et al., Fujikata et al., and Rogers et al. All patients were treated with surgical resection followed by postoperative radiotherapy [16, 19, 23] to sterilize the microscopic residual plasmacytoma cells. In a few cases, RT was used as the sole locoregional treatment when surgery was not performed. Chennoufi et al. The patient did not undergo adrenalectomy because of the advanced nature of the disease; instead, the received radiation to the adrenal tumor (along with chemotherapy) in an attempt to control the bulky mass [15]. Our 2025 patient also received adjuvant RT (45 Gy) to the adrenal bed after laparoscopic resection, given the synchronous bone lesion and higher risk of recurrence.

Systemic therapy was employed in 5 of 16 cases (31.25%), particularly for those with larger, bilateral, or unresectable tumors [15–17, 22, 26]. Several chemotherapy regimens have been used, which generally mirror myeloma therapy (vincristine, adriamycin, and dexamethasone). Ahmed et al. is a noteworthy example of this. The patient had bilateral AEMPs and was treated with combination chemotherapy followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [17]. This approach led to prolonged remission; Ahmed’s case was reported to be alive and disease-free at 50 months of follow-up [17]. Chemotherapy was also administered by Blanco et al. [22] after surgery and Elbaset et al. for primary therapy [26]. The latter report described an AEMP with an inferior vena cava thrombus in which neoadjuvant chemotherapy was attempted to reduce the tumor burden [26]. Chennoufi et al. received systemic therapy as well (the patient was HIV positive and had an aggressive plasmacytoma); therefore, a combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy was administered [15]. Notably, there is no established chemotherapy protocol for solitary EMP, and decisions have been individualized based on disease extent and patient factors. Overall, the addition of chemotherapy/HSCT is relatively uncommon and generally reserved for multifocal diseases or cases with high-risk features.

Outcomes and prognosis

Despite its rarity, adrenal EMP appears to be a treatable condition with generally favorable outcomes when appropriately managed. The reported follow-up intervals vary, but the median reported follow-up among cases is approximately 18 months (range, 3–72 months) [13–27]. Most patients achieved complete remission of their plasmacytoma and survived at the last follow-up. The longest documented disease-free survival was at least 72 months (6 years) in a 26-year-old male who underwent laparoscopic adrenalectomy [20]. Only one disease-related mortality has been reported in the literature, approximately three months after diagnosis [15]. The patient’s poor prognosis was likely due to the exceptionally large tumor size and possible immunosuppression from HIV, factors that may have contributed to the aggressive disease biology [15]. No other case in this series showed overt progression to multiple myeloma at the last follow-up, and some patients have exceeded five years of survival without systemic relapse [2].

Local or systemic recurrence of AEMP after treatment is rare. To date, only one case has clearly documented disease recurrence. In a series by Ooi et al., AEMP recurred after initial therapy [27]. Unfortunately, specific details of the management and recurrence in this case are sparse (as Ooi’s report focused on the imaging aspects of plasmacytomas). In addition, at least nine other cases explicitly reported no recurrence during the follow-up period [13, 14, 17, 20, 22–25]. In conclusion, the survival rate was 90% and the recurrence rate was 10%. Our 2025 patient likewise remained in remission three months after completing therapy. These observations suggest that long-term local control is achievable when an AEMP is truly solitary and completely excised (with or without adjunctive radiotherapy). The rarity of recurrence aligns with the behavior of solitary plasmacytomas in other locations that have high local control rates with definitive therapy [47–49]. Nonetheless, vigilant follow-up is warranted in all cases, as late recurrence or progression to multiple myeloma can occur in some patients even after an initial plasmacytoma diagnosis [50]. Standard guidelines for solitary plasmacytoma recommend periodic re-imaging and laboratory evaluation (e.g., serum protein electrophoresis) to monitor any signs of relapse or progression to myeloma.

Surveillance and long-term management

Given the 10–70% risk of progression to multiple myeloma, lifelong surveillance is mandatory [24, 26, 28, 40]. The recommended protocol was based on the NCCN guidelines [51].

Study limitations

This analysis was limited by the small sample size, retrospective nature, heterogeneous follow-up periods, and potential publication bias favoring successful outcomes.

Conclusion

Adrenal extramedullary plasmacytoma is an exceptionally rare clinical entity (1.5:1 male predominance; median age, 52 years) that requires high diagnostic suspicion. Moreover, this case highlights a previously unreported pattern of synchronous distant extramedullary plasmacytomas, the potential for indolent behavior without progression to multiple myeloma, and its presentation in a female patient, thereby broadening the clinical and demographic understanding of this rare entity.

Contemporary management favors laparoscopic resection when technically feasible, combined with adjuvant radiotherapy, for optimal local control. The excellent survival rate (90%) and low risk of recurrence (10%) support the use of aggressive local therapy. However, lifelong surveillance remains essential given the inherent risk of progression to systemic multiple myeloma.

This case series establishes adrenal EMP as a distinct clinical entity with predictable demographics, treatment responses, and outcomes, thus providing a foundation for evidence-based management.
