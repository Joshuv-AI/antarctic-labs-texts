Receptive–Expressive Language Phenotypes in Infants and Toddlers With Autism Features
Authors: Torrey Cohenour, Amanda Gulsrud, Connie Kasari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildren diagnosed with autism often present with an atypical discrepancy between their receptive and expressive language levels, or an atypical receptive–expressive language phenotype. Children with an atypical receptive–expressive phenotype present with a relative receptive language advantage (expressive level < receptive level) or a relative expressive language advantage (expressive level > receptive level), whereas those with a typical phenotype have balanced receptive and expressive language levels. It remains unclear whether atypical receptive–expressive language phenotypes are evident before 24 months in children with autism features or whether they are associated with concurrent child developmental functioning or later language growth. Participants (N = 80) were drawn from a randomized comparative efficacy intervention study for 12–23‐month‐olds with autism features and elevated scores on an autism diagnostic instrument. Baseline receptive and expressive language age equivalent (AE) scores were used to describe continuous variation in receptive–expressive language phenotypes by quantifying the gap between each child's receptive and expressive language levels. These continuous metrics were then used to classify children into discrete language profile groups: expressive advantage (EA), receptive advantage (RA), and balanced. On average, children had a gap of three AE “months” between their receptive and expressive language levels. Over 75% of children presented with an atypical receptive–expressive phenotype (40% EA profile, 36% RA profile), whereas only 24% of children had a typical receptive–expressive phenotype (balanced profile). Language profiles were not concurrently associated with age, autism features, joint attention skills, motor or cognitive functioning. However, children with the EA profile at baseline showed significantly slower expressive language growth over 12 months than those with RA or balanced language profiles, suggesting that receptive–expressive language profiles may hold promise as early prognostic markers of expressive language growth in emerging autism.

Introduction

Acquisition of spoken language is a key predictor of developmental outcomes in children with autism, including social competence and quality of life in adulthood (Anderson et al. 2014; Bal et al. 2015; Gillespie‐Lynch et al. 2012). Language abilities are heterogeneous in autism but also appear highly malleable, particularly before age six (Pickles et al. 2014).

Language development in autism is characterized by both developmental delays (i.e., a tendency for skills to fall below age expectations) and deviation from the typical pace and sequence of skill acquisition. For instance, in addition to experiencing delayed acquisition of language milestones such as first words (Kover et al. 2016), children with autism are also more likely to exhibit an atypical discrepancy between their receptive and expressive language levels, or an atypical receptive–expressive language phenotype (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017; Hudry et al. 2010; Maljaars et al. 2012; Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Seol et al. 2014). Children with an atypical receptive–expressive language phenotype either have an expressive language level that is more advanced than their receptive language level (a relative expressive language advantage) or an expressive language level that is below their receptive language level (a relative receptive language advantage). Although a discrepancy between specific receptive language and expressive language skills (e.g., receptive and expressive vocabulary (Bornstein and Hendricks 2012)) is expected in typical language development given that receptive language precedes expressive language, a discrepancy between language levels is atypical and indicates that the gap between receptive and expressive language skills is much larger or much smaller than what is expected in typical language development.

Receptive–Expressive Language Discrepancies in Autism

On average, young autistic children have significantly lower receptive language scores than expressive language scores, a finding replicated across multiple instruments assessing overall language ability (e.g., Mullen Scales of Early Learning, Preschool Language Scales) and across a variety of indices of performance, including age equivalent (AE) scores (Hudry et al. 2010; Maljaars et al. 2012; Barbaro and Dissanayake 2012; Ellis Weismer et al. 2010; Luyster et al. 2008; Nevill et al. 2019; Volden et al. 2011), standard scores (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017; Kjelgaard and Tager‐Flusberg 2001; Yankowitz et al. 2022), and developmental quotient (DQ) scores (Chawarska et al. 2009). This pattern is also evident in measures of vocabulary: children with autism tend to produce more words than would be expected from the number of words they are reported to understand, signaling that the gap between their receptive and expressive vocabularies is smaller than that observed in typically developing children (Kjelgaard and Tager‐Flusberg 2001; Artis and Arunachalam 2023; Hudry et al. 2014; Kover et al. 2013; Luyster et al. 2007; McDaniel et al. 2018).

Receptive–expressive difference scores (i.e., receptive language AE minus expressive language AE) (Volden et al. 2011; Hudry et al. 2014; Swanson et al. 2017; Chawarska et al. 2012) and ratio scores (i.e., receptive language AE divided by expressive language AE) (Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Seol et al. 2014) quantify the gap between receptive and expressive language levels at the individual level (i.e., the within‐individual difference between receptive and expressive language levels). These indices have previously been used to describe variation in receptive–expressive phenotypes in autism (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017; Hudry et al. 2010; Maljaars et al. 2012) and to classify children into discrete language profile groups (e.g., balanced, expressive dominant, or receptive dominant profiles) (Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Seol et al. 2014).

Despite methodological differences across studies, there is consistent evidence indicating that atypical receptive–expressive language phenotypes (encompassing both receptive advantage [RA] and expressive advantage [EA] language phenotypes) are common among children diagnosed with autism (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017; Maljaars et al. 2012; Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Seol et al. 2014). Further, estimates suggest that a majority of autistic toddlers and young preschool‐age children present with an atypical discrepancy between their receptive and expressive language levels (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017; Seol et al. 2014). The RA language phenotype (characterized by a higher receptive than expressive language level) is common in autism as well as in other neurodevelopmental conditions. An estimated 24%–27% of autistic toddlers and preschoolers exhibit a RA, as do 60% of children with developmental language disorder (DLD), 26% with other developmental disabilities (DD), and 16% with neurotypical development (Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Seol et al. 2014). In contrast, the EA language phenotype (characterized by a higher expressive than receptive language level) is more tightly linked with autism and is relatively rare in nonautistic populations. The EA phenotype is observed in an estimated 43% of autistic toddlers and approximately 25% of autistic preschool‐age children, whereas only 11% of toddlers with DLD and fewer than 12% of preschoolers with DD or neurotypical development exhibit a relative expressive language advantage (Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Seol et al. 2014). As such, some have suggested that the EA language phenotype may be a useful marker for distinguishing language‐delayed toddlers most likely to develop autism from those who are “late talkers” without autism (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017).

Factors Associated With Receptive–Expressive Language Phenotypes in Autism

It is not known whether receptive–expressive phenotypes are associated with other developmental characteristics among infants and toddlers with signs of autism, and evidence from studies of older autistic children is limited and inconsistent. For instance, there is conflicting evidence for associations between receptive–expressive phenotypes and both nonverbal functioning and autism symptoms among autistic children. While some report that autistic children with balanced receptive and expressive language levels or a RA tend to show higher nonverbal cognitive functioning (Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Nevill et al. 2019; Volden et al. 2011) and lower levels of autism symptoms (Reinhartsen et al. 2019), others find the inverse relationship (Hudry et al. 2010) or report no associations between symptom levels and receptive–expressive language phenotypes (Kover et al. 2013).

Furthermore, our understanding of the relationship between receptive–expressive phenotypes and social communication abilities remains limited. Significant associations between receptive–expressive phenotypes and measures of social attention have been reported in children with autism, suggesting that there may be an association between receptive–expressive phenotypes and specific social cognitive abilities. These reports demonstrated that autistic children with a relative expressive language advantage tend to spend less time visually attending to a speaker's face (McDaniel et al. 2018; Chawarska et al. 2012) than autistic children with a relative RA or with balanced receptive and expressive language levels—a pattern not observed in neurotypical or DD toddlers (Chawarska et al. 2012). Though it is not known whether specific receptive–expressive phenotypes are associated with differences in other early‐emerging social cognitive skills that subserve language acquisition, such as joint attention, elucidating these relationships in very young children could help shed light on whether disruptions to preverbal communication skills contribute to differences in receptive–expressive language development.

It is not known whether receptive–expressive language phenotypes are predictive of receptive language growth and expressive language growth in autism or in children with signs of autism. Cross‐sectional evidence indicates that, on average, autistic preschool‐age children with an expressive language advantage have higher expressive language scores but lower receptive language scores than those with a relative RA (Reinhartsen et al. 2019). Given models of typical language development suggest that the integrity of early receptive language skills necessarily constrains expressive language growth (Bornstein and Hendricks 2012), elucidating the relationship between early receptive–expressive phenotypes and language growth within receptive and expressive domains could provide mechanistic insight heterogeneity in language development in autism broadly, as well as valuable clinical insight into potential language prognostic markers in autism.

The Present Study

This study examined receptive–expressive language phenotypes in 12–23‐month‐olds with autism symptoms to extend current knowledge about receptive–expressive language development in autism to very young children with signs of the condition, and to illuminate potential pathways between early receptive–expressive language phenotypes and later language growth. This study addresses three primary aims: first, to describe receptive–expressive language phenotypes in infants and toddlers with autism symptoms by quantifying individual‐level receptive–expressive language discrepancies and classifying children into discrete receptive–expressive language profile groups; second, to test whether receptive–expressive language profiles are concurrently associated with differences in child characteristics (age) and features of the emerging autism phenotype (autism features, social communication skills, cognitive and motor functioning); and third, to explore whether receptive–expressive language profiles are predictive of subsequent receptive and expressive language growth. It was hypothesized that a proportion of infants and toddlers with autism features would present with an atypical receptive–expressive phenotype (a RA or an EA), and receptive–expressive language profiles would be associated with variability in concurrent developmental functioning and autism behavioral features. It was also hypothesized that baseline receptive–expressive language profile would account for variability in the pace of receptive and expressive language growth. Understanding the associations between early receptive–expressive phenotypes and language development during a period of development typically characterized by rapid language growth could provide insight into language heterogeneity in autism and help identify markers signaling risk of delayed language acquisition or later language impairment.

Methods

Participants

Data from participants aged 12–23 months were drawn from a randomized comparative efficacy study of early interventions for infants/toddlers with autism symptoms (Gulsrud et al. 2024). Children were eligible for participation if they were (a) between 12 and 24 months of age, (b) had Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) Toddler Module (Luyster et al. 2009) scores consistent with the mild‐to‐moderate‐concern range of concern or higher, and (c) had autism‐specific clinical concerns from a healthcare provider, psychologist or other professional. Exclusionary criteria included the presence of (a) co‐occurring neurological conditions or uncontrolled seizure activity; (b) known genetic conditions (e.g., tuberous sclerosis complex); or (c) major physical or sensory impairments (e.g., cerebral palsy, blindness). Given that the primary intervention study enrolled children showing autism symptoms before the typical age of diagnosis in community settings—approximately 49 months of age in the United States (Maenner et al. 2023)—children were not required to have a documented autism diagnosis to participate. Recruitment efforts focused on community healthcare providers (e.g., pediatrician offices) and early intervention service providers. Online advertisements were also used to recruit families directly.

A total of 110 children were screened for eligibility by trained clinicians with expertise in the differential diagnosis of autism. Of those screened, 30 families were excluded because the child did not meet inclusion criteria (n = 28) or because they declined to participate after completing the eligibility screening (n = 2). The remaining participants were randomized to the active control intervention (Standard Baby classroom, n = 40) or the experimental intervention (Baby JASPER classroom, n = 40) for the primary intervention study (Gulsrud et al. 2024). Additional information about the intervention study and intervention conditions is provided in Appendix S1 of the supplemental file. The final sample for this secondary analysis includes 80 children with autism symptoms. See Table 1 for participant characteristics.

Measures

Participants completed behavioral assessments at baseline (T1, prior to the start of intervention), approximately 2 months after baseline (T2, immediate postintervention), and approximately 12 months after baseline (T3). All assessments were conducted by trained, independent evaluators blinded to intervention assignment.

Autism Symptoms

Children were screened for eligibility using the ADOS toddler module (Luyster et al. 2009). The ADOS toddler module is an autism diagnostic instrument for children between 12 and 30 months of age. The ADOS measures core autism symptoms, including social communication impairments (social affect domain) and restricted/repetitive behaviors (RRB domain). The ADOS toddler module scoring algorithm provides guidance for mapping overall total scores (calculated as the sum of SA and RRB domain scores) onto three categories reflecting the level of autism‐specific clinical concern: little‐to‐no concern, mild‐to‐moderate concern, and moderate‐to‐severe concern.

Cognitive Functioning

The Mullen Scales of Early Learning (Mullen 1995) is a standardized assessment for children up to 68 months of age. The Mullen was administered at each visit, and includes gross motor, fine motor, visual reception, receptive language, and expressive language scales. The Mullen has been used extensively in infants and toddlers with autism or other DD in research settings (Belteki et al. 2022). Scale‐level DQ scores were calculated by dividing the scale AE score by the child's chronological age in months and multiplying the result by 100. The purpose of using DQ scores, as is common in studies of young children with autism, is to avoid floor effects associated with standard scores and quantify functioning relative to chronological age expectations, thereby capturing variability in functioning even among children with profound delays (Lord et al. 2006; Munson et al. 2008).

Joint Attention

The Early Social Communication Scales (Mundy et al. 2003) (ESCS) is a structured observational measure designed to elicit early social communication behaviors, including child initiations of joint attention (IJA) and child responsiveness to an adult's joint attention bids (response to joint attention; RJA). The assessment is approximately 15 min in duration and is video‐recorded for later coding offline. IJA is quantified as the frequency with which a child spontaneously uses gaze, language/communicative vocalizations, or gestures to initiate shared attention with the assessor. RJA was assessed during a shared book‐reading task and a gaze‐following task, during which the child is typically provided a total of 14 opportunities to respond. RJA was quantified as the total number of child responses divided by the total number of opportunities for response. A trained independent assessor administered the ESCS, and independent coders blind to intervention assignment and timepoint scored the video‐recorded assessments. Coders maintained reliability of at least 80%, assessed by intraclass correlation coefficients.

Receptive–Expressive Language Discrepancy

Baseline (T1) receptive and expressive language AE scores from the Mullen Scales of Early Learning were used to calculate receptive–expressive difference scores (receptive AE − expressive AE) and ratio scores (receptive AE ÷ expressive AE). At the individual level, equivalent receptive and expressive language AE scores (corresponding with a difference score = 0 and a ratio score = 1) indicate the child's expressive language level is similar to what would be expected from their receptive language level, consistent with a typical receptive–expressive language phenotype. In contrast, receptive and expressive language AE scores of 12 and 16, respectively (difference score = −4; ratio score = 0.75), signal an atypical receptive–expressive phenotype and specifically, a relative expressive language advantage in that the child's receptive language level is lower than their expressive language level. Note that these metrics are agnostic to overall language ability and provide no information about language delay or performance relative to age expectations: children with severe language delays may still have a typical receptive–expressive language phenotype.

Receptive–Expressive Language Profiles

Using published ratio score cut‐offs (Seol et al. 2014), children were classified into three groups: those with a RA profile (RA; ratio score > 1.10), those with an EA profile (EA; ratio score < 0.90), and those with a balanced profile (ratio score between 0.90 and 1.10, inclusive). Recall that the RA profile is relatively common among autistic and nonautistic children, whereas the EA profile is considered atypical across diagnostic groups and is observed very infrequently in nonautistic populations.

Receptive and Expressive Language Growth

Mullen receptive language and expressive language AE scores from T1, T2, and T3 visits were used to map trajectories of receptive language growth and expressive language growth.

Analysis Strategy

One‐way analysis of variance was used to test for differences across language profile groups in chronological age, autism symptom levels, joint attention skills, and cognitive and motor functioning. The present sample size of N = 80 provided 80% power to detect a medium‐to‐large effect (Cohen's f = 0.35) of receptive–expressive language profile on the variables of interest. When assumptions of parametric one‐way ANOVA were violated, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test (H) with Dunn's post hoc tests was implemented.

To test whether baseline receptive–expressive language profiles were predictive of receptive and expressive language growth, hierarchical linear modeling was implemented, with separate growth models for receptive and expressive language. Time was quantified as months elapsed since baseline (T1). A linear growth model was proposed with random participant‐level intercepts and slopes (Raudenbush and Bryk 2001).

In total, 40% of children (n = 32) had ratio scores consistent with the EA language profile, 36.25% (n = 29) had scores consistent with the RA language profile, and the remaining 23.75% (n = 19) had ratio scores consistent with the balanced language profile. There were no significant associations between language profiles and sex (χ2(2) = 0.29, p = 0.881), parental educational attainment (at least one parent completed college versus no college degree‐holding parents; χ2(2, N = 77) = 0.45, p = 0.857), or annual household income ($100,000 or less versus above $100,000; χ2(2, N = 73) = 4.74, p = 0.100). Prior to fitting growth models, the predictor of interest, receptive–expressive language profile at baseline, was dichotomized by combining the RA and balanced groups (RA/balanced n = 48) and maintaining the original EA group (n = 32). This was an effort to reduce the number of model parameters estimated, given the relatively small sample size and limited number of timepoints. The decision to combine RA and balanced groups was motivated by our cross‐sectional findings and previous evidence indicating that the EA profile is more tightly linked with autism than the RA and balanced profiles, which are both common in neurotypical development and nonautism developmental conditions.

Modeling was carried out in a stepwise manner, described in detail in Appendix S3 of the supplemental file. Briefly, unconditional growth models were first fit to the receptive and expressive language AE data, with random participant‐level intercepts and slopes. Next, the predictor of interest, baseline language profile (1 = EA, 0 = RA/balanced), was added as a level‐2 predictor of initial status (i.e., language abilities at baseline) and rate of language growth. Although the purpose of the present study is not to examine intervention effects, we fit one additional model that added intervention assignment as a level‐2 control variable to evaluate whether the effects of baseline language profile on language growth parameters persisted after controlling for intervention group assignment (i.e., modeling the “controlled” effects of baseline language profile) (Singer and Willett 2003). We found that intervention assignment was not a significant predictor of receptive or expressive language growth, and that adding intervention group assignment as a control variable did not appreciably alter parameter estimates or result in marked improvements to model fit (see Appendix S3 and Tables S1 and S2 for details of the models described above). As such, and in the interest of reducing the number of model parameters estimated given our limited sample size, we report the results of receptive and expressive language models with language profile as the only level‐2 predictor in the main text. Receptive Language Growth

Baseline language profile was not predictive of linear growth in receptive language AE scores (p = 0.685) but was associated with baseline receptive language AE scores (p < 0.001). Children with the EA profile at baseline had less advanced receptive language abilities on average at baseline, but grew at a similar pace to children with RA and balanced profiles.

Expressive Language Growth

Baseline language profile significantly predicted linear growth in expressive language AE scores (p = 0.003), such that children with the EA profile at baseline showed significantly slower expressive language growth than children with RA or balanced language profiles. The growth differential between the RA/balanced group and the EA was substantial, such that the RA/balanced group was estimated to gain an additional 0.5 AE “months” per month compared with children in the EA group. Baseline language profile was not predictive of baseline expressive language AE scores (p = 0.272), indicating comparable expressive language levels across language profile groups.

Discussion

Atypical receptive–expressive language phenotypes are present and highly prevalent prior to 24 months in infants and toddlers showing signs of autism. Over 75% of children presented with an atypical language phenotype, with 40% exhibiting a relative expressive language advantage (EA profile) and 36% exhibiting a relative receptive language advantage (RA profile), consistent with findings in a cohort of older autistic toddlers (Seol et al. 2014). Fewer than 25% of infants and toddlers presented with a typical receptive–expressive phenotype (balanced profile).

This study provides the first evidence that the EA language phenotype—which is strongly associated with autism and rarely observed in nonautistic populations—is present before 24 months in children with early signs of autism. The high prevalence of the EA profile in this sample is consistent with prior evidence for a relative sparing of expressive language skills in older children with autism (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017; Hudry et al. 2010; Maljaars et al. 2012; Reinhartsen et al. 2019; Ellis Weismer et al. 2010; Luyster et al. 2008; Volden et al. 2011; McDaniel et al. 2018; Swanson et al. 2017; Chawarska et al. 2012). Future research should examine whether the presence of the EA profile in toddlerhood could help differentiate language‐delayed toddlers who will later receive an autism diagnosis from those with transient social communication delays or other developmental conditions.

Receptive–Expressive Language Profiles and the Emerging Autism Phenotype

While differences in receptive language ability were observed between EA and RA profile groups, there were no differences across language profiles in age, autism symptom levels, joint attention skills, nonverbal cognitive functioning, motor functioning, or expressive language abilities.

The absence of significant differences across language profile groups in age, autism features, and nonverbal functioning contrasts with several prior studies of older children diagnosed with autism (Hudry et al. 2010; Reinhartsen et al. 2019). This inconsistency may be an artifact of sampling differences across studies: for instance, the present study enrolled children with signs of autism rather than children with a confirmed autism diagnosis, enrolled children within a narrower age bracket (12–24 months) than previous studies (e.g., 12–60 months (Reinhartsen et al. 2019)), and included children who were younger on average. Notably, the absence of age differences across language profile groups in this 12–23‐month‐old sample suggests that the prevalence of atypical receptive–expressive language phenotypes may remain stable across toddlerhood before declining in prevalence during the preschool years, as has been described in longitudinal samples of older autistic children (Davidson and Ellis Weismer 2017).

Although baseline expressive language DQ scores did not differ across language profiles, differences in receptive language functioning were marked. Children with the RA profile had significantly higher baseline receptive language DQ scores than children with EA or balanced profiles. Indeed, nearly 25% (n = 7) of children with the RA profile had age‐appropriate receptive language AE scores (i.e., AE score ≥ chronological age, corresponding with a receptive language DQ score ≥ 100), whereas only about 10% (n = 2) of children with the balanced profile and no children with the EA profile had receptive language abilities at or above age‐level. Conversely, no children with the RA profile had expressive language scores that met or exceeded their chronological age, whereas only about 10% (n = 2) of children with the balanced profile and 6% of children with the EA profile (n = 2) had scores equal to or above their chronological age. Despite having a relative expressive language advantage, nearly all children with the EA profile had expressive language skills below their chronological age (suggesting a global pattern of language delay), whereas about a quarter of children with the RA profile had age‐appropriate receptive language skills (suggestive of isolated expressive language delays).

These findings suggest that the mechanisms that shape receptive–expressive language phenotypes in emerging autism may be distinct from those that underlie variability in receptive and expressive language functioning overall, given the lack of association between language profiles and behavioral variables with established links to language acquisition in autism (e.g., joint attention, nonverbal cognition, and motor skills). The lack of evidence for behavioral phenotypes linked to specific language profiles raises the question of whether there are neurobiological mechanisms that may shape receptive–expressive language phenotypes during the second year of life. Identifying neural markers of specific receptive–expressive language profiles and evaluating the extent to which these markers overlap with known neural correlates of receptive language and expressive language in this population (e.g., resting‐state spectral power in specific regions) (Cohenour et al. 2024) could provide insight into mechanisms responsible for the apparent dissociation between receptive and expressive language skill acquisition in autism during toddlerhood.

Receptive–Expressive Language Profiles as a Candidate Prognostic Marker

Results of growth modeling offer preliminary evidence that the EA language profile may be a candidate marker of heightened risk for slowed expressive language growth and, potentially, poorer expressive language outcomes in infants and toddlers with signs of autism.

Why might children with a relative expressive language advantage (or, put otherwise, a receptive language disadvantage) show slower expressive language growth? One possibility is that difficulties with language comprehension have cascading consequences for social and language development. For instance, language comprehension deficits may interfere with children's ability to engage in contingent social interactions, which are crucial for scaffolding language acquisition in children with autism (Adamson et al. 2019). Alternatively, disruptions in the functioning of neurocognitive mechanisms that support children's processing of linguistic input in their environment from infancy may underlie the relative receptive language difficulties, and slower expressive language growth, seen in children with an EA profile. For example, studies of infant siblings of autistic children suggest that disruptions to mechanisms that underpin efficient processing of auditory stimuli (Kolesnik et al. 2019) and adaptive social attention (Hatch et al. 2021; Jones and Klin 2013; Pierce et al. 2023) are present even before autism symptoms emerge; accordingly, the characteristics of the EA language phenotype (i.e., relative receptive impairments and slower expressive growth) may reflect very early differences in foundational domain‐general cognitive capacities that help hone receptive language skills. This hypothesis is supported by work demonstrating that levels of attention to child‐directed speech are predictive of receptive–expressive language discrepancy, such that autistic toddlers and preschoolers with a relative EA spend significantly less time attending to the face and mouth of a speaker compared to peers with balanced language levels or a relative RA (McDaniel et al. 2018; Chawarska et al. 2012). These findings suggest that key perceptual and attentional mechanisms linked with language development, including selective attention to faces (Tenenbaum et al. 2015) and the integration of audiovisual linguistic cues (Righi et al. 2018), may function differently in autistic toddlers with a relative expressive language advantage. While the present findings should be replicated in a larger sample, this preliminary evidence suggests that early weaknesses in language comprehension may constrain subsequent growth in language production.

Limitations

The present sample of 12–23‐month‐olds represents a distinct population from that which most studies of receptive–expressive language phenotypes draw, with respect to both chronological age and diagnostic status. Many children included in this sample were younger than 18 months (the age at which autism diagnosis is considered reliable) (Ozonoff et al. 2015) and had not yet received a formal autism diagnosis, in part owing to their young age. While this sampling strategy limits the generalizability of findings to other populations (e.g., children with established autism diagnoses), there are significant benefits to this approach: enrolling prediagnostic infants and toddlers with autism symptoms provides an opportunity to leverage the remarkable neuroplasticity of this period and glean insight into potentially modifiable factors linked with language outcomes. Furthermore, this symptomatic infant/toddler‐aged sample is likely more representative of the clinical population that community‐based providers (e.g., pediatricians) regularly encounter in their clinical practice.

Sample size and statistical power bear consideration. Our sample of 80 participants may have been underpowered to detect more subtle group differences in the baseline phenotypic variables of interest or in language growth. This study is a secondary analysis of data collected for a randomized comparative efficacy intervention study; thus, the number of language assessments administered, the timing of those administrations, and the duration of study enrollment were dictated by the primary intervention study protocol. Given the inherent limitations posed by the present sample size, attrition, and the relatively sparse sampling of language abilities over the 12‐month enrollment period, we have described the longitudinal findings as “preliminary” in acknowledgement that replicating this work in a larger sample is necessary. Replication in a larger sample would also allow for the evaluation of the effects of baseline language profile on language growth while controlling for other potentially important variables (e.g., nonverbal cognitive functioning, household socioeconomic status), which we were unable to include in the model due to sample size constraints.

Conclusions

In the second year of life, over 75% of infants and toddlers with autism symptoms present with an atypical receptive–expressive language phenotype. Receptive–expressive language phenotypes are not associated with differences in developmental characteristics, including age, cognitive functioning, and autism features, within this population during this period of development. However, we provide preliminary evidence that the EA language profile is predictive of slower expressive language growth in infants and toddlers with signs of autism. Measures of early receptive–expressive language phenotype may serve as clinically meaningful prognostic markers signaling increased risk of slowed language growth during the second and third years of life in children with signs of autism.
