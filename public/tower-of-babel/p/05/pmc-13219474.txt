FDMNet: frequency-domain modulation network for robust object detection in hazy aerial imagery
Authors: Xiaoxiong Zhou, Guangming Zhang, Zhihan Shi, Shanshan Huang, Xiang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To achieve object detection in UAV vision, an object detector is crucial, but haze seriously affects detector performance by physically suppressing high-frequency information, which makes it difficult to detect tiny objects. Traditional “dehazing-then-detection” paradigms are restricted to inconsistencies in tasks and restoration effects. The article provides Frequency-Domain Modulation Network (FDMNet), which is an aerial non-explicit-restoration haze-resistant object detector. Following a physical prior, FDMNet constructs an estimate-compensate architecture. A Frequency Domain Modulation (FDM) module is one that expressly estimates the loss of spectral information, and a dynamic LMF-Kernel adaptively restores the loss of mid- and high-frequency discriminative information. To be effective, we present a physical-semantic consistency loss strategy, with frequency-domain consistency loss guaranteeing physical accuracy and prompt distillation loss guaranteeing semantic consistency. We do not have enough datasets, and, therefore, we build Hazy-DOTA, Hazy-DroneVehicle, and a real-life UAV test set. Through extensive experimentation, FDMNet achieved a 7.9% improvement in mAP scores on the HazyDet dataset compared to baseline models, alongside respective gains of 9.9% and 11.5% on the Hazy-DOTA and Hazy-DroneVehicle datasets. Furthermore, it attained state-of-the-art performance relative to several advanced algorithms, balancing both accuracy and robustness.

Introduction

Among the range of applications of UAV vision systems, including the implementation of environmental monitoring, traffic management, public safety, and emergency response, aerial object detection is one of the fundamental concern tasks1–3. Contrary to general scene object detection, object detection in aerial scenes has challenging issues principally because of extremely small objects, a cluttered background, occlusion and overlap, and geometric distortions generated by changes in viewing angle4–7. Numerous approaches have been put forward in recent years to address these issues and have proven to be promising8–14. The majority of the existing approaches, however, are trained and tested on clean images, and they are not yet ready to interpret the images in unfavorable weather conditions, which are likely to affect the quality of the image and can critically impact the performance of the detector. The weather phenomenon, haze, is common practice. Haze also drastically diminishes the overall image contrast and thus makes the differentiation of objects and background more vague15. Simultaneously, haze distorts object edges and converts mid- and high-frequency details, which are essential in the detection and localization of small objects16. Figure 1 presents clean, hazy, and frequency spectra. It can be observed that hazy images present a brighter center and darker surroundings, especially in the mid- and high-frequency regions near the four corners, indicating that haze causes substantial information loss in the mid- and high-frequency bands. As a result, the feature extraction capability of common detectors is severely affected under hazy conditions, leading directly to decreased detection accuracy.

The primary challenge in conventional aerial small object detection lies in the geometric difficulties arising from spatial scale within images. As small objects occupy a minimal proportion of pixels, feature loss may occur during subsampling. Consequently, current methods focus on enhancing feature representation within the spatial domain. While these approaches perform well on clear images, this effectiveness stems from the fact that high-frequency edge and texture information remains intact compared to conditions under haze. Under haze conditions, small object detection confronts more severe physical degradation challenges. Haze not only diminishes image contrast but, more critically, atmospheric scattering strongly suppresses mid-to-high-frequency information—corresponding to edge and texture details in the image. Consequently, traditional small object detection methods often yield unsatisfactory results in foggy conditions.

Most of the studies17–19(Fig. 2(a)), conducted to date, to enhance detection performance in the fog consist of a two-step paradigm, where the input image is dehazed with the use of a dehazing algorithm, and the dehazed image is then processed by an object detector. This trick appears to be self-evident, but job tasks are not always aligned: dehazing is the maximization of low-level pixel-level visual quality, and object detection relies on positioning high-level semantic and discriminative features. More to the point, dehazing networks may introduce noise and artifacts in the restoration process, including the distortion in the texture due to over-enhancement and the disappearance of detail due to over-smoothing. Artifacts do not allow advantageous detection characteristics and can propagate into the detection network as false clues, reducing performance. Some studies20–22(Fig. 2(b)) adopt a joint optimization strategy that integrates dehazing and detection losses, but due to the fundamentally different objectives of image dehazing and object detection, potential conflicts arise between them, resembling adversarial effects. Dehazing models used in such approaches are typically neural-network-based, and the residual noise in the restored images may still negatively impact the subsequent detection model. In recent years, a few studies23,24(Fig. 2(c)) have attempted to train detection models directly on hazy images, but this strategy usually places a heavy demand on the model’s feature extraction capability. Moreover, the scarcity of suitable hazy datasets makes it difficult to adequately evaluate and demonstrate the generalization ability of such models.

In summary, existing methods either rely on the two-stage dehazing–detection paradigm, which is easily affected by artifacts and task conflicts, or depend on joint optimization or direct training on hazy images, but still suffer from severe feature loss, limited data availability, and insufficient model generalization. These methods have not fundamentally addressed the key challenge of discriminative information loss caused by the suppression of mid- and high-frequency components under haze degradation. Thus, the question of the direct modelling of the haze degradation effects through the frequency-domain viewpoint and the introduction of effective supervision to increase the detection robustness is an urgent issue.

In order to overcome the above challenges, the current paper does not follow the old paradigm of the dehazing and detection framework, instead presenting frequency-domain priors directly based on the haze degradation physical characteristics directly into the detection framework. To have a clear picture, our analysis of the haze-induced distortion of picture spectrums and dynamically adjust the detecting head attribute numbers to the best of their ability, we suggest the Frequency-Domain Modulation Network (FDMNet). FDMNet does not propagate artifacts and does not introduce inconsistency in the tasks performed by not performing dehazing preprocessing. It leads the detector to retrieve discriminative features in deteriorated areas by frequency-domain modulation. Specifically, we shall design a Frequency-Domain Modulation FDM module that will analyze the local spectral statistics through the Fourier transform and produce a guidance map that characterizes sharpness and detail loss at various locations. It is on this basis that we propose the LMF-Kernel dynamic kernel mechanism that models convolution kernels as linear combinations of pre-bored frequency bases, whereby the combination coefficients are predicted by the guidance map. This allows the convolution process to be modulated conditionally with spectral information and enhances feature extraction in those regions that exhibit the poorest mid- and high-frequency degradation. Moreover, to have the consistency of frequency-domain learning effective, we propose two auxiliary supervision methods to use in the course of learning: the Frequency Consistency Loss, which upholds congruency amid projected and ground-truth spectral statistics so as to urge solid spectral reflection, and the Hint Distillation Loss, which restrains flat feature exploring and sharp edge and detail recovery. The structure design of FDMNet gives high-frequency suppression by haze a higher priority, and its strategy of optimization combines frequency-domain and shallow feature constraints to enhance the detection of hazy scenes of UAVs.

Related works

Aerial object detection

The objects used in aerial imagery are usually of arbitrary orientation and aspect ratio. Horizontal bounding boxes (HBB) are commonly overlapping with object forms or providing backdrops to objects10. Rotation modelling has to become a research focus. In early techniques such as RRPN25, rotated anchors, and angle regression, the angle is not steady and discontinuous. RoI Transformer26 transforms horizontal boxes to rotated ones with a geometric transformation, and ReDet27 is a rotation-equivariant structure-enhancing. On these bases, the strategies of angle modelling have been improved. SCRDet + +28 improves the detection of small and dense objects with the feature denoising and IoU-Smooth L1 Loss through smoothing rotation loss. The feature encoding methods based on dual-angle line representation and direction guidance solve the angle discontinuities in DRDet29. FEADet30 employs adaptive aligned convolution and attention fusion to rectify the misalignment of features to justify the obtained results. Transformer-based techniques have been used recently. Aspect-ratio-sensitive angle categorization and rotational deformable attention address the problems of angle boundaries in ARS-DETR31. ESRTMDet32 end-to-end jointly uses super-resolution with rotating detection in low-quality scenarios to preserve orientation modelling in hazy and blurred scenarios. Also, WSODet4 is based on extending rotation modelling to weakly supervised problems, where the resulting rotated object detection is performed based solely on image-level supervision.

Several general-purpose object detection methods have been proposed for detecting small and densely clustered targets. SpecDETR33 achieves significant performance gains in hyperspectral small object detection by extracting joint depth and spectral features from hyperspectral cubes. SR-TOD34 effectively enhances the representation of weak features in small objects by introducing a self-reconstruction mechanism. DQ-DETR35 incorporates a category counting module that dynamically adjusts the number and spatial positioning of object queries, demonstrating robust performance in small object detection. Addressing the common issue of missed detections in small objects, SADet36 achieves state-of-the-art performance through enhanced extraction and fusion of semantic information. While the introduction of FPN improved object detection performance, it still lacks spatial awareness for small objects. HS-FPN37 designed a module generating high-frequency responses and spatial connections, significantly enhancing small object detection capabilities.

Aerial imagery is also difficult when it comes to detecting small objects and dense objects. Cascaded regression zeroes in on bounding boxes in R3Det38. COF-Net39 and SME-Net40 have the feature alignment and feature-splitting features, and MENet41 enhances the instance and feature discriminability. Object-centered methods like ClusDet42, DMNet43, and GLSAN44 use either clustering or density-map-based cropping to focus on the object regions, and the new YOLC8 has demonstrated good results on very small objects since it uses multi-scale regression in addition to clustering mechanisms. The minimization improvement has been researched on the optimization of small and dense objects. The classical metrics of the IoU do not optimize well in the lenticular construction of bounding boxes, and to enhance the accuracy of localization, the introduction of Gaussian-based metrics like GWD45, KLD46, and KFIoU47 has been undertaken. In the meantime, there has been the usage of attention mechanisms and context modelling. Those like non-local9 and AFA-FPN48 are used to enhance global dependency modelling, which enables target regions to be better discriminated in complex backgrounds and also makes them better at detection. Studies have been done on rotation modelling, detecting small objects, feature augmentation, and loss design, which have improved significantly. Nevertheless, all of them are confined to ideal clean-image conditions, and the research on how aerial detection can be achieved under poor weather conditions like haze or rain is somewhat sparse, with an essential practical problem not fully tackled.

Object detection in hazy weather

When the environment is hazy, the objects that are suspended in the environment scatter light, absorb light, and cause the picture to have low contrast, features to be blurred, and color distortion, which makes it hard to identify the objects. Technical solutions to enhance detectors during bad weather have been researched.

The simplest method is the two-stage paradigm (Dehaze + Detect), whereby an independent picture restoration algorithm is run on the input, and the detector is fed with the restored, clear image. LAR-Net49 employs a transformer-based dehazing unit in its pipeline for detecting. This naive approach is constrained by the objective incompatibility: dehazing optimizes visual quality at a low level, whilst detection optimizes semantic information at a high level. More seriously, there might be noise or artifacts in the process of dehazing networks such as PhDnet15, which decreases the accuracy of detection.

To alleviate task conflicts, researchers proposed a joint optimization paradigm, integrating image restoration and object detection into a single end-to-end network. Early work such as DSNet22 jointly learns restoration and detection by sharing the low-level backbone features. Subsequently, more advanced dual-branch fusion architectures were explored: one branch processes the original hazy features, while the other processes restored features, and the two are fused at later stages. For instance, BAD-Net20 introduces an Attention Fusion Module to merge both feature streams. DFENet18 further designs a Haze Prediction Module (HPM) to estimate a haze mask, and employs a Cross-Fusion Module (CFM) for dynamic weighted fusion. DeMatchNet50 adopts similar feature fusion (FFM) and feature alignment (FA) strategies for feature matching tasks. Moreover, KJRD-Net19 incorporates parallel dehazing and super-resolution branches to handle small objects in haze. Another type of joint paradigm is differentiable image processing (DIP), where classical interpretable image filters (such as gamma correction and contrast enhancement) are reformulated as differentiable modules and trained in a weakly supervised manner using only detection loss; representative works include IA-YOLO21, ERUP-YOLO51, and YOLOv5-Fog17.

More recent studies depart from the image-restoration framework and explore restoration-free paradigms aimed at improving the intrinsic robustness of detectors. Among these, knowledge distillation is an effective strategy. HRAOD23 proposes a “Clean-to-Hazy” distillation scheme in which a teacher network trained on clean images guides a student network operating on hazy images. To address domain discrepancies, it does not directly mimic feature maps; instead, it introduces gradient-guided feature imitation and degradation-weighted response distillation. Another strategy is the plug-and-play paradigm, exemplified by PPNet52. This approach freezes a pretrained clean-image detector and inserts a trainable “haze-domain plug-in subnetwork” between the feature pyramid (FPN) and the detection head (RPN). Operating in the feature space, the plug-in transforms the “hazy feature pyramid” into a “clean feature pyramid.” Additionally, some methods exploit other physical priors. For example, DeCoDet24 models haze degradation as strongly correlated with depth and introduces a Depth-Conditioned Kernel (DCK) to dynamically modulate features based on depth cues.

Overall, existing research has achieved substantial progress in joint optimization, feature fusion, knowledge distillation, and plug-and-play modules. However, whether relying on pixel/feature recovery in the spatial domain or on external depth priors, current methods still do not fundamentally address the key physical issue highlighted in the introduction—namely, the physical suppression of mid- and high-frequency components by haze. Although some studies in image dehazing have noted frequency characteristics, how to directly model and compensate for this degradation effect within the object detection framework from a frequency-domain perspective remains an unresolved challenge.

Frequency-domain methods in object detection

Traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) operate primarily in the spatial domain and are effective at capturing local patterns and texture features53. Nonetheless, the mentioned space locality leads the network to fail to recognize global contextual knowledge and valuable structural clues that exist in the frequency domain54–57. The frequency-domain pattern of object orientation, light variations, and complex backgrounds is generally different from the spatial-domain pattern53,54 in aerial imaging. In this way, feature extraction in the space domain cannot ensure effective identification in complex situations.

In order to overcome these weaknesses, investigators have started to explicitly add frequency-domain data to detection models. These means typically use devices like discrete cosine transform (DCT)48,58,59, fast Fourier transform (FFT)49,50,60, or Laplacian pyramids61 to break images or features down. In aerial object recognition, CFIL53 suggests that CNNs do not provide enough feature interaction representation and therefore introduces EFF and IFF modules to extract frequency-domain features and cross-channel interaction, respectively. It is FANet58 that asserts that frequency-domain features may be used to supplement spatial features and provides a Spectrum Augmentation Module (SAM) that may be used to extract multispectral features and an FDA fusion method. FDOL57 identifies directional frequency content with either the curvelet or the wavelet material and generates an Orientation-Enhanced Self-Attention Layer (OES-Layer) to support the selection of features belonging to aerial objects.

Frequency-domain analysis has also proven useful in problems that require the ability to discriminate between two or more similar regions in the image that can be seen, i.e., camouflaged or shiny object recognition, which is similar to hazy-image detection. FDRNet60 and DPRNet61, in the frequency domain, have significant variances between camouflaged/specular objects and the background. Contrastive learning reinstates high-confidence frequencies to eliminate texture noise and edges in FDRNet60. DPRNet61 divides low-frequency (semantic) and high-frequency (detail) parts with the help of ViT and Laplacian pyramids and uses an FIA module to combine them. FSEL55 also utilizes FFT to design an Entangled Transformer Block (ETB) for learning joint frequency–spatial representations. Recent research has begun to explore the use of frequency priors to improve robust detection under adverse conditions.

In addition, frequency-domain information plays an important role in domain generalization and data augmentation. Studies have shown that low-frequency components often encode domain-specific “style” information, whereas high-frequency components encode domain-invariant “content” information53,59,62. Based on this observation, FIT54 performs input-level image translation by swapping low-frequency components between the source and target domains. Similarly, PhysAug62 generate diverse pseudo domains or physically guided augmentations by manipulating amplitude (style) while preserving phase (content), thereby improving detector generalization on unseen domains.

In summary, whether used to enhance feature representations in aerial detection, disentangle edges of camouflaged or reflective objects, or support style transfer in domain generalization, existing research has extensively demonstrated the importance of frequency-domain information in high-level vision tasks. However, these methods mainly utilize frequency cues for general feature enhancement, domain-invariant learning, or edge extraction. They have not specifically designed frequency-domain compensation modules for haze, a unique form of degradation. Although prior work has analyzed the frequency characteristics of haze in the context of dehazing, how to integrate such prior knowledge into an object detection framework and design a detector that directly models and compensates haze degradation in the frequency domain remains an open and promising research direction.

Methods

Traditional methods for object detection under hazy conditions, such as the two-stage “dehaze + detect” paradigm, have been shown to have inherent limitations. Not only are these methods prone to the objective incompatibility between image restoration and high-level detector tasks, but more crucially, they fail to take into account the physical truth underlying the haze degradation; that is, the atmospheric scattering that causes physical inhibition of high-frequency information in images. In order to solve this physical problem deeply, we suggest FDMNet. FDMNet is an end-to-end detector, which applies an estimate-compensate architecture rather than image restoration. It evaluates spectral loss caused by haze and then replenishes missing discriminating features with the aid of a dynamic kernel. This block involves a description of the framework in detail, encompassing (A) the Frequency-Domain Modulation (FDM) module, (B) the LMF-kernel dynamic compensation mechanism, and (C) the dual auxiliary supervision strategy.

Frequency-domain modulation module (FDM)

Traditional CNN modules operate entirely in the spatial domain, which limits their ability to capture global contextual information and to model long-range physical degradation effects such as haze. To address this limitation, we introduce the Frequency-Domain Modulation module, a novel structure that functions not as an external “estimator” generating guidance signals, but rather directly performs learnable modulation on the frequency-domain representation of the features.

Specifically, the core idea of FDM is to transform the features from the spatial domain to the frequency domain, apply independent convolution operations to the real and imaginary components of the spectrum, and then transform them back to the spatial domain. This design allows the network to learn how to manipulate frequency components directly, thereby adaptively enhancing the mid- and high-frequency discriminative information suppressed by haze. LMF-kernel dynamic compensation mechanism

In hazy scenes, image degradation is spatially non-uniform. Traditional convolution kernels use static weights shared across all spatial locations, which limits their ability to adapt to spatially varying degradation—such as compensating for information loss in heavily degraded regions while preserving features in clear regions. To address this limitation, we design the LMF-Kernel module, a dynamic feature compensation mechanism whose structure is shown in Fig. 5.

Dual auxiliary supervision strategy

To ensure that the “estimate–compensate” framework of FDMNet can be effectively learned, we introduce a dual auxiliary supervision strategy. This strategy works in conjunction with the Det Loss and constrains the network from two orthogonal dimensions: physical accuracy and semantic correctness.

Frequency consistency loss (Freq Loss)

The core objective of the FDM module is to learn the physical characteristics of haze degradation. To guarantee the physical accuracy of its estimation, we must constrain its output to be consistent with the true spectral statistics of haze-free images.

As shown in Fig. 3, the output of the FDM module is fed into a Freq head and supervised by the Freq Loss. This loss measures the discrepancy between the predicted spectral representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{d}}$$\end{document} estimated from hazy features and the ground-truth spectral representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{\mathrm{g}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document}, which is precomputed from the corresponding clean image. To address the high dynamic range of spectral magnitudes, we adopt a log-transformed L1 loss (Log-L1 Loss) to compute \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{q}}$$\end{document}:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{q}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\:\parallel\:\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}(1+{F}_{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{d}})-\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}(1+{F}_{\mathrm{g}\mathrm{t}}){\parallel\:}_{1}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} is the total number of elements in the feature map.

By minimizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{q}}$$\end{document}, the FDM module is explicitly encouraged to learn the physical inverse process of haze degradation—that is, accurately reconstructing the expected clean spectral structure from a degraded spectrum. This provides strong physical prior supervision for our “estimator,” ensuring that the generated guidance signal is based on accurate physical degradation estimation.

Hint distillation loss (HD loss)

The compensation performed by the LMF-Kernel module must produce not only visually clearer features but also semantically meaningful ones for the detection task. Haze severely degrades fine edges and textures that are essential for detection. To recover these discriminative features, we adopt hint distillation. A pretrained “teacher” model (Dynamic R-CNN in our method) is used to generate hint features. FDMNet, as the “student” model, is required to mimic the intermediate feature representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{a}}$$\end{document} of the teacher using its own Feature head output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{u}}$$\end{document}. The hint distillation loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{h}\mathrm{d}}$$\end{document} is defined as the L2 distance between student and teacher features:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{h}\mathrm{d}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\:\parallel\:{F}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{u}}-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{h}\left({F}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{a}}\right){\parallel\:}_{2}^{2}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{h}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} ensures that gradients are propagated only to the student model. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{h}\mathrm{d}}$$\end{document} enforces alignment of FDMNet’s feature space with that of the strong teacher model. This enables the student to learn how to reconstruct critical semantic information—useful for classification and localization—that the teacher can extract only from clean images.

Datasets

Evaluating the robustness of object detection under hazy conditions requires diverse and representative datasets. However, large-scale real-world aerial haze datasets with precise annotations remain scarce. To comprehensively validate the effectiveness of our FDMNet, we adopt a strategy that includes “one public benchmark + two large-scale synthetic benchmarks + one real-world test set.”

HazyDet: This is the first large-scale public benchmark dataset specifically designed for hazy UAV-view object detection. It contains 383,000 real-world instances, consisting of images captured under natural haze conditions as well as clear scenes enhanced using synthetic haze effects. The standard dataset split includes 8,000 training images, 1,000 validation images, and 2,000 test images. Representative examples are shown in Fig. 6. We use HazyDet as the main benchmark to enable fair comparison with existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods.

Experimental setup

In our implementation, we adopt FCOS as the baseline detector and use a ResNet-50 backbone pretrained on the ImageNet dataset. According to the configuration, the first stage of the backbone is frozen during training. Our FDMNet head is built on top of the FPN, utilizing multi-scale feature maps from P3 to P7 for detection, corresponding to strides from 8 to 128.

We follow the standard schedule_1x training settings from MMDetection, and all models are trained for 12 epochs. All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. We enable auto_scale_lr and set the base batch size to 16. We adopt the SGD optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.01, a momentum of 0.937, and a weight decay of 0.0001. The learning rate scheduler includes a constant warm-up phase of 500 iterations, followed by reducing the learning rate by a factor of 0.1 at the 8th and 11th epochs.

Comparison with SOTA methods

To comprehensively validate the effectiveness of our FDMNet, we compare it with existing haze detection methods. These methods span four major strategies, ranging from baselines to the most recent SOTA “restoration-free” paradigm:

Baselines: This includes two FCOS baselines. The first is trained on clean images but tested on the HazyDet test set (“Clear-to-Hazy”), used to quantify the performance degradation caused by haze. The second is directly trained on the HazyDet training set (“Hazy-Training”), representing the standard “direct training” paradigm.

Two-Stage (Dehaze + Detect): This paradigm first applies an independent dehazing algorithm to process the image and then feeds the processed image into a standard FCOS detector trained on clean data. We select two representative methods: the classical DehazeNet and the frequency-based FDDN. We also include YOLOv9s-Haze, a recent SOTA method that integrates advanced GAN-based preprocessing.

Joint Optimization: This paradigm attempts to jointly learn image restoration and object detection within a single network. We select IA-YOLO as a representative method.

Restoration-Free: This is the most recent paradigm, aiming to enhance the intrinsic robustness of the detector to haze without explicitly restoring the image. We select DeCoDet as the SOTA comparison method.

Comparison of transforms

Presently, numerous methods exist for manipulating the frequency domain. We have introduced the 2D-FFT primarily to enable the decoupling of data into amplitude and phase components. Within the image, the phase encompasses object edges and geometric structures, whilst the amplitude contains information such as contrast and illumination. To validate the efficacy of our introduced 2D-FFT, we have here compared it with the Discrete Cosine Transform and the Discrete Wavelet Transform. The results are presented in Table 6.

Conclusion

To address the key issue of haze-induced suppression of mid- and high-frequency information in UAV aerial images, which leads to a significant performance decline in object detection, we propose a novel Frequency-Domain Modulation Network (FDMNet). FDMNet departs from the traditional “Dehaze + Detect” paradigm and instead constructs a physics-prior-based “Estimate-Compensate” framework. The core of this framework is the Frequency-Domain Modulation (FDM) module, which acts as an “estimator” to explicitly model haze degradation in the frequency domain and drives a dynamic LMF-Kernel as a “compensator” to adaptively compensate for missing discriminative features in the spatial domain. To guarantee the success of such a framework, we also present a new physical-semantic dual collaborative-supervision method: frequency consistency loss (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{q}}$$\end{document}) is applied to guarantee the physical accuracy of the physical side, and hint distillation loss (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{h}\mathrm{d}}$$\end{document}) is adopted to guarantee the semantic accuracy of the physical side. Vast experiments of HazyDet, Hazy-DOTA, Hazy-DroneVehicle, and real-world test sets prove that FDMNet is superior in all benchmarks to current SOTA methods.

Despite the encouraging results achieved by FDMNet, there are still certain limitations. Firstly, our dual supervision strategy, especially \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{q}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{h}\mathrm{d}}$$\end{document}, relies on paired clean/hazy data for fully supervised training. This is largely dependent on synthetic datasets, which are generated based on atmospheric scattering models and advanced monocular depth estimation algorithms. These estimated depth maps inevitably differ from real physical depths, which may introduce discrepancies between the synthetic haze and the true physical properties of haze, potentially affecting the model’s generalization ability. Exploring how to leverage unsupervised or semi-supervised learning methods to learn these physical priors from non-paired real-world data will be a valuable research direction. Secondly, the core design of FDMNet is based on the physical characteristic of haze-induced suppression of mid- and high-frequency information. Its generalization ability to other types of meteorological degradation (such as heavy rain, snow, or non-uniform haze) has not been systematically validated, as these degradations may introduce different physical artifacts. Finally, FDMNet has a good balance in terms of accuracy and efficiency; moving together, the introduction of FFT transformations and dynamic kernel mechanisms introduces computational overhead in comparison to the baseline. Future research on lightweight models and model compression for airborne platforms is worth exploring.
