Research Letter
Authors: Kevin W. Hoffman, Malay Shah, James Madsen, Tejas P. Joshi, Vicky Ren
Published: 2026

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is associated with immune dysregulation and increased risk of autoimmune disease [1]. This may manifest in a higher prevalence of atopic dermatitis (AD), which has also been independently linked to autoimmune thyroid diseases [2,3]. Recent largescale database studies have begun to clarify the epidemiology of comorbid autoimmune-mediated cutaneous disease in DS [4,5]. However, the joint contribution of DS and thyroid disease on AD risk has not been quantified due to the uncommon triple comorbid nature. We evaluated this relationship using a national electronic health record (EHR) dataset.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Cosmos EHR database, evaluating patients between 01 January 2016 and 01 January 2025. DS (ICD-10 Q90.), AD (L20.), thyroiditis (E06.), and hypothyroidism (E03.) were identified, consistent with prior literature [5]. Patients with HIV (B20., Z21.), transplant status (Z94.), common variable immunodeficiency (D83.), and combined immunodeficiency (D81.) were excluded. To reduce diagnostic bias from potential misclassification between AD and psoriasis and to minimize therapeutic confounding, we excluded patients prescribed systemic agents primarily indicated for psoriasis and other non-AD conditions (i.e., acitretin, adalimumab, anakinra, apremilast, basiliximab, benralizumab, bimekizumab, brodalumab, canakinumab, certolizumab, deucravacitinib, etanercept, everolimus, fingolimod, golimumab, guselkumab, infliximab, ixekizumab, mepolizumab, omalizumab, reslizumab, rilonacept, risankizumab, rituximab, secukinumab, sirolimus, tildrakizumab, tocilizumab, ustekinumab). Demographic characteristics (age, sex, race) were adjusted for via Mantel-Haenszel to calculate relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and χ2 testing was used for comparisons in accordance with STROBE guidelines.

Findings

AD was diagnosed in 3.77% of DS patients versus 1.72% of those without DS (Table 1, RR 1.96; 95% CI: 1.94–1.98; P<0.001). Among all patients regardless of DS diagnosis, any thyroid condition was not associated with any large change in AD prevalence (1.29%; RR 0.99; 95% CI: 0.99–0.99; P<0.001). However, splitting this cohort by thyroiditis and hypothyroid condition found that thyroiditis was associated with a mildly increased prevalence of AD compared to hypothyroidism (1.76%; RR 1.29; 95% CI: 1.28–1.29; P<.001 vs 1.28% RR 0.96; 95% CI: 0.96–0.96; P<0.001). In contrast, the co-occurrence of DS and thyroid diseases conferred a higher risk of AD. AD prevalence was 4.03% among DS patients with thyroid disease compared with 1.75% in non-DS non-thyroid condition patients (RR 2.38; 95% CI: 2.33–2.44; P<0.001). The association was stronger for DS with thyroiditis (4.86%; RR 2.77; 95% CI: 2.63–2.91; P<0.001) compared to DS with hypothyroidism (4.00%; RR 2.32; 95% CI: 2.26–2.37; P<0.001).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate a higher prevalence of AD in individuals diagnosed with DS, with thyroid diseases serving as an additional risk factor. Thyroiditis in the context of DS saw the highest risk, larger than the product of their independent adjusted RRs, suggesting a possible synergistic effect on AD development. Our results suggest that DS patients with thyroid disease could benefit from targeted dermatological surveillance. Our exploratory findings validate leveraging largescale databases to examine less common comorbid combinations to justify future prospective studies in immune-mediated dermatological conditions. Limitations include surveillance bias in DS patients, potential misdiagnosis of AD as other common cutaneous conditions, and the retrospective study design.
