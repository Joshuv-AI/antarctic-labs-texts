Efficacy of tissue-bone homeostasis manipulation on the gait and knee function for the patients with knee osteoarthritis: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zeng-qiao Zhang, Yu-wu Ding, Ying Tao, Hai-chen Xu, Ying-xi Zhong, Kun Yang, Li-ming Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) was characterized by pain and limited joint function, which seriously affected the quality of life of patients. The vast majority of KOA was closely related to degeneration of the patellofemoral joint and abnormal patellar movement trajectory. Tissue-bone homeostasis manipulation (TBHM) could correct abnormal patellar movement trajectory on the basis of loosening soft tissue. However, there was little strong evidence to verify its efficacy on the patients with KOA. The study objective was to explore the efficacy of the TBHM on gait and knee function in the patients with KOA.MethodsSixty KOA patients were randomly assigned to either the joint mobilization (n = 30) or TBHM (n = 30) group. The joint mobilization group received joint mobilization, while the TBHM group received TBHM. For two groups, the patients participated in 30 min rehabilitation sessions thrice per week for 12 weeks. The primary outcome was biomechanical gait outcomes during walking, including step length, step velocity, double support, knee range of motion (ROM), and knee adduction moment (KAM). The secondary outcomes were the Western Ontario and McMaster Index (WOMAC) and 36-Item short- form health survey (SF-36), which reflected improvements in knee function and quality of life, respectively. At baseline and 12 weeks, evaluations were conducted and compared between groups.ResultsAfter a 12-week intervention, significant group differences were observed in KAM (p = 0.018), WOMAC-Pain (p = 0.043) and WOMAC-Stiffness (p = 0.026). A noteworthy finding was the presence of a significant interaction effect between group and time specifically observed in step velocity during gait (p = 0.046), WOMAC-Function (p = 0.013) and SF-36 (p = 0.027). Further analysis revealed a significant difference in step velocity (p = 0.034), WOMAC-Function (p = 0.025) and SF-36 (p = 0.042) during post-assessment between the two groups. Moreover, a significant time effect was observed across all outcomes of the two groups (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe TBHM intervention has better improved the gait, knee function, and quality of life in the patients with KOA.Trial registrationITMCTR, ITMCTR2200005507. Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA), which is the predominant form of chronic degenerative joint pathology, is characterized by manifestations such as pain, swelling, stiffness, and compromised functionality [1]. Joint dysfunction, which leads to imbalance, abnormal gait, and falls, has a detrimental impact on both the quality of life (QOL) and the ability to engage in routine activities [2, 3]. While pharmacological and surgical interventions have indisputably demonstrated their effectiveness in alleviating pain and improving physical functioning among individuals with KOA, the concomitant expenses, potential complications, and adverse events, such as the risk of acute liver failure, constitute a substantial threat to individuals’ overall health, healthcare expenditures, and quality of life [4–6]. Non-pharmacological interventions, for instance, exercise interventions and complementary and alternative medicine, have manifested not only safety but also effectiveness in the management of KOA. Particularly, these approaches have garnered significant endorsement from the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) [7–9]. However, recent findings have revealed the extremely formidable obstacles that are inherently present when implementing exercise interventions among KOA patients who are characterized by advanced age or have compromised mobility [9]. Moreover, as of now, there still persists a deficiency of well-established and standardized treatment protocols for the management of KOA [10]. Hence, the relentless pursuit of safer and more efficacious treatment modalities stands as an imperative in clinical settings.

A comprehensive comprehension of the fundamental factors that determine the trajectory of KOA advancement acts as the cornerstone for the screening, discovery, and development of rehabilitation strategies. Perturbations in load distribution within the knee joint, particularly where approximately 70% of the load is borne by the medial compartment, have long been hypothesized to play pivotal roles in driving the progression of KOA [2, 11, 12]. Consequently, numerous rehabilitative interventions targeting KOA, which encompass techniques like weight-bearing exercises, gait training, and neuromuscular exercises, have been deliberately designed to bring about alterations in load distribution through external manipulation. Nevertheless, unfortunately, most exercise modalities have, up to this point, failed to bring about substantial changes in the pathological load distribution patterns [13, 14]. A recent investigation shed light on the complex nature of the factors that modulate load distribution within the joint, which encompassed multifaceted variables such as the reduction in muscular strength, the laxity in the soft tissues surrounding the knee, the imbalances within the musculature, and the anomalous patellar trajectories [13, 15]. Moreover, these interlinked factors display a causal relationship, where they mutually reinforce one another. This complex interplay suggests that attaining equilibrium within the knee structure, which encompasses the harmonious interaction between muscular elements, soft tissues, and the patellofemoral complex, constitutes a crucial determinant for both load redistribution and the successful rehabilitation of KOA.

Notably, the aberrant trajectory of patellar motion, closely associated with the degeneration of the patellofemoral joint, emerges as a significant instigator and exacerbator of KOA pathogenesis [16–18]. Disruption to the dynamic and static stabilizing structures surrounding the patella emerges as a crucial contributing factor to the deviant patellar motion trajectory. Under normal circumstances, the patellar motion adheres to a predetermined course. However, any deviations from the physiological trajectory result in exposing the cartilage surfaces of the patellofemoral joint to heightened stress. Prolonged and excessive concentrations of stress exerted on the cartilage surfaces can ultimately culminate in wear, thereby giving rise to the development and progression of KOA. Consequently, rectifying the patellar trajectory presents a highly compelling avenue for advancing the treatment strategies of KOA. Tissue-bone homeostasis manipulation (TBHM) emerges as a potential approach for rectifying the abnormal trajectory of patellar motion by addressing the laxity in soft tissues. TBHM primarily concentrates on restoring the equilibrium among muscles, soft tissues, and bones, thereby facilitating the rehabilitation of joint function. From a theoretical perspective, TBHM holds the potential as a therapeutic modality for facilitating KOA rehabilitation. Moreover, the preliminary observations obtained from clinical practice have indicated positive outcomes related to the implementation of TBHM in terms of improving symptoms and enhancing joint function in patients with KOA. However, the existing evidence that supports the efficacy of this approach is still restricted [19]. At the same time, this also help to further enrich our understanding of KOA treatment and promote the development of related fields.

Consequently, the purpose of this randomized controlled trial was to investigate the specific efficacy of TBHM on gait and knee function in KOA patients. By comparing it with joint mobilization, we hope to clarify the value of this maneuver in clinical practice and provide a more effective treatment option for the rehabilitation of patients with KOA.

Materials and methods

Study design

A single-center, randomized, assessor-blinded, controlled trial was conducted at the Shanghai Seventh People’s Hospital to investigate the effectiveness of the intervention. All patients provided informed consent before their enrollment into the study, ensuring adherence to ethical standards. The study received approval from the institutional review board of Shanghai Seventh People’s Hospital, under the reference number 2021-7th-HIRBSR-001.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was determined using the G*power software (v3.1.9.2). Prior to this investigation, a relevant study reported the mean difference (standard deviation) in knee adduction moment (KAM) between the Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation and control groups at baseline as 0.28 (0.14) and 0.23 (0.13), respectively. Following the intervention, these values were observed to be 0.23 (0.14) and 0.26 (0.11) in the two respective groups [13]. Based on a prior two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) F-test, the sample size for this study was determined to be 60 patients. The effect size was set at 0.51, ensuring a statistical power of 0.80 at an alpha level of 0.05. Additionally, a conservative attrition rate of 20% was accounted for in the calculation.

Randomization and allocation concealment

The eligibility of patients was assessed through an in-person screening process, ensuring adherence to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In this investigation, a randomized design was employed to impartially assign individuals into two groups, utilizing the research randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org). Following the completion of baseline assessments, each participant was provided with a sealed envelope containing a unique random allocation sequence number generated by the randomizer tool. To maintain objectivity, the statistician, outcome assessors, and data analysts remained blinded to the study’s recruitment, intervention, and evaluation processes.

Interventions

Joint mobilization group

In the long-axis traction technique, the patient’s knee was gently flexed and allowed to rest naturally on the edge of the treatment bed. The therapist carefully grasped the ankle joint with both hands, applying a distal force for approximately 15 s. This maneuver was repeated five times, with a manipulation intensity of 3 to 4 levels. The anterior to posterior gliding procedure involved securing the patient’s affected limb in a position of maximum restricted knee flexion. The therapist positioned both thumbs in front of the proximal tibia and exerted a posterior force by leaning forward, utilizing upper body strength. This gliding motion was maintained for 15 s and repeated five times, with a manipulation intensity of 3 to 4 levels. For the posterior to anterior gliding technique, the therapist positioned the thumb in front of the proximal tibia and placed the remaining four fingers in the popliteal fossa. With the patient in a supine position and their hip and knee flexed, the therapist applied an anterior force to glide the proximal tibia forward for approximately 15 s. This manipulation was repeated five times, with a manipulation intensity of 3 to 4 levels. The above-described procedures were conducted three times a week for 12 weeks.

TBHM group

As shown in Fig. 2, the patients were in the supine position, and the manipulation included the following steps:

Schematic diagram of the Tissue-bone Homeostasis Manipulation. Note: This Figure has been authorized by the therapist and the participant

Soft tissue release: The therapist applied the push and thumb kneading method to thoroughly knead the medial and lateral thigh muscles, as well as the calf muscles, promoting complete relaxation of the soft tissue surrounding the knee joint.

Painful point massage: Gentle massage techniques, utilizing the fingers or a massage ball, were employed to target and alleviate the patients’ painful points. This process lasted approximately 1–2 min, with uniform and appropriate pressure. The therapist adjusted the force intensity based on the patients’ feedback.

Infrapatellar fat pad release: Placing their thumbs at the midpoint of the patients’ patellar tendon, with the remaining fingers supporting the knee joint from behind, the therapist slightly flexed the affected knee. Simultaneously, the therapist lifted the heel of the affected side off the treatment table while extending the knee joint. The therapist’s thumbs exerted pressure on the infrapatellar fat pad.

Patellar mobilization: Taking medial gliding as an example, for the therapist, one hand’s thumb placed on the lateral side of the patient’s patella, and the four fingers on the medial side to stabilize it. The palm root of the other hand was superimposed on the top of the aforementioned thumb, and with the upper limb strength, the patella was pushed medially to the maximum displacement and kept there for 3–5 s before being put back. The same method was used for lateral gliding in the opposite direction, and each direction was repeated three times.

Tibial internal rotation: The patient’s affected knee was placed on the edge of the treatment bed and flexed to approximately 90 degrees. The therapist grasped the affected knee and performed internal rotation of the tibia to the point of maximum restriction. This position was maintained for several seconds before relaxation, and the entire process was repeated three times.

Tibial external rotation: This step was similar to tibial internal rotation, with the only variation being the direction of rotation of the tibia.

These sessions were scheduled three times per week for 12 weeks.

Outcome measures

The evaluation measures in this study were meticulously structured and classified into three distinct components: an extensive examination of the patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics, which encompassed various factors such as age, gender, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), affected side, duration since the onset of KOA, and K-L grade; the principal outcomes involved a comprehensive gait biomechanical test and subsequent analysis; the supplementary outcomes entailed a clinical assessment aimed at evaluating knee function and quality of life. Both the primary and secondary outcome evaluations were carried out prior to the initiation of the intervention and after the completion of the 12-week treatment period. To ensure the credibility and precision of the assessments and to circumvent any potential difference in results, a single evaluator who possessed extensive experience in assessment tasks conducted the secondary outcome evaluations. Additionally, to maintain impartiality, the personnel engaged in data acquisition and analysis were independent of the clinical study team.

Primary outcomes

Self-selected walking speed is a good indicator of overall gait performance and is commonly used to assess locomotor ability [21]. Since the patients were instructed to walk at a self-selected speed on a 15 m walkway. Before data collection, a total of 20 infrared-reflective markers were positioned on the following anatomical landmarks: upper edge of iliac crest; posterior superior iliac spine; anterior superior iliac spine; prominence of the greater trochanter; distal apex of the medial malleolus; distal apex of lateral malleolus; dorsomedial aspect of the first metatarsal head; dorsolateral aspect of the fifth metatarsal head; medial aspect of the head of the proximal phalanx of the hallux; calcaneus base [22–24]. Symmetrical distribution on the left and right sides. These markers were affixed in the orthostatic position with the individual in an anatomical position to align with the global coordinate system and identify the articular axes. All the markers attached on the skin remained on the participant throughout the entire session in order to minimize the variations in angles and external moments caused by different anatomical reference frame. Before the formal test, the patient were asked to perform three trials comfortably to familiarize themselves with the experimental environment and instructions. Patients were instructed to walk at their natural walking speeds with the affected foot fully striking a force plate. In addition, if the patients were found to have significant gait adjustment, followed by the visual aiming board, the experiment was omitted. A total of three valid tests were conducted for data analysis, with an interval of 60 s between each test.

For the collection of synchronous kinematic and kinetic data pertaining to patients’ gait, a cutting-edge 3D motion analysis system (Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) coupled with two force plates (Kistler Instruments AG Corp, Switzerland) was employed. The 3D motion analysis system recorded the kinematics, while the force plates accurately captured the kinetics. The sampling frequencies utilized for these data acquisitions were set at 100 Hz and 1000 Hz, respectively. To process the collected kinematic and kinetic data, state-of-the-art Visual 3D software (C-Motion, Inc., USA) was employed. The gait cycle, a critical parameter, was meticulously defined as the duration between consecutive heel contacts of the affected limb. The heel-contact moment was determined by the time of the vertical ground reaction force ≥ 20 N [11]. The study computed several kinematic indices, each serving as a crucial metric for evaluating gait characteristics. The calculated kinematic indices encompassed step length, step velocity, the percentage of double support phase, and the range of motion exhibited by the knee joint in the affected limb during the swing phase of the gait cycle.

A previous investigation substantiated that the peak knee adduction moment (KAM) serves as an informative indicator, effectively reflecting the magnitude of pressure imbalance experienced within the medial and lateral compartments of the knee joint [25, 26]. Moreover, a strong association was observed between the KAM and the functional disability experienced by individuals with KOA. Notably, higher KAM values were indicative of a greater degree of functional disability [14]. To evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention, the peak KAM of the affected limb during gait was computed using the inverse dynamics algorithm. This approach was utilized as a means to gauge the impact of the intervention on KAM values.

Secondary outcomes

Knee function

In the current study, the assessment of pain, stiffness, and physical function among patients was carried out using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC).

Quality of life

The evaluation of the patients’ quality of life, which encompassed diverse domains related to physical and emotional health in this study, was carried out by implementing the 36-Item short- form health survey (SF-36). Previous research has provided evidence attesting to the validity and reliability of the SF-36 as a powerful instrument for assessing quality of life. Therefore, its application for the evaluation of quality of life in this study is firmly established and reliable [27, 28]. The SF-36 questionnaire encompasses a set of eight multi-item subscales, each assigned a score ranging from 0 to 100. Notably, a higher score on each subscale is indicative of a more favorable quality of life outcome.

Statistical analysis

The intention-to-treat principle governed the primary and secondary outcome analyses. Baseline outcome differences among groups were examined through one-way ANOVA and the chi-square test for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The data of both groups were subjected to normality test (Shapiro–Wilk) and variance chi-square test. To assess the impact of interventions on all outcome measures, a two-way ANOVA mixed measures, incorporating the factors of group and time, was administered. In cases where the interaction effect achieved significance, a simple effect analysis was conducted. Statistical significance was evaluated at an alpha level of 0.05. IBM SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was employed to execute all statistical analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patients

In the current investigation, a total of 102 KOA patients were initially screened to assess their eligibility. Among these individuals, 42 patients failed to meet the inclusion criteria and were subsequently excluded. The remaining cohort of 60 patients underwent a pre-assessment and were subsequently randomly allocated to different groups. During the trial, three patients from the TBHM group withdrew, while four individuals from the joint mobilization group discontinued their participation. The patients withdrew from the study mainly due to personal reasons such as transferring to another hospital, loss of interest, or the occurrence of new problems in their health conditions, which made it impossible for them to continue cooperating with the study. Ultimately, the final analysis included 27 and 26 patients from TBHM and joint mobilization groups, respectively.

Discussion

Our findings disclosed that patients in the TBHM group exhibited significantly enhanced step velocity, decreased KAM, lower WOMAC scores, and higher SF-36 scores after a 12-week intervention when compared to those in the joint mobilization group. Significantly, no adverse events were reported throughout the intervention period, which prominently underlined the safety and efficacy of TBHM in improving gait, knee function, and the overall quality of life of individuals with KOA.

The normal gait predominantly depended on the optimal and efficient functioning of the knee joint [29, 30]. The stiffened-knee gait pattern manifested as the predominant feature among patients identified with KOA. This particular gait pattern had the potential to intensify the detrimental progression of KOA, as demonstrated by lower step velocities, reduced step lengths, decreased range of knee motion, prolonged double support phases, and elevated knee adduction moment [2, 11, 31]. In our current investigation, a 12-week intervention utilizing TBHM exhibited substantial potential in enhancing step velocity and reducing KAM among elderly individuals diagnosed with KOA. Significant group difference in pain and stiffness, as evidenced by the WOMAC scores, were observed. Many previous studies have also demonstrated that interventions for patients with KOA can positively affect their gait parameters [32, 33]. However, our study might be unique in some respects. Our TBHM intervention might have been more prominent in improving gait speed and decreasing KAM compared to joint mobilization. TBHM likely influenced joint biomechanics, muscle activation patterns, and proprioceptive feedback, which cumulatively contributed to these improvements. The specific ways in which TBHM achieved this could include enhancing joint range of motion, improving muscle strength and coordination, and modulating sensory input. Notably, it was worth mentioning that previous studies on manual therapy primarily concentrated on examining knee function rather than its impact on gait dynamics [34]. While the observed changes in gait parameters such as knee ROM and double support time did not reach statistically significant levels within groups, it was noteworthy that the TBHM group demonstrated a greater magnitude of improvement compared to the joint mobilization group. The conclusion of the present study provided valuable insights into the potential factors that contribute to the enhanced gait parameters that were observed in patients diagnosed with KOA who underwent targeted biomechanical interventions in the TBHM group.

For the SF-36 score, the improvement in the TBHM group could potentially be attributed to several factors. TBHM might have had a positive impact on overall physical functioning, mental health, and quality of life. It could have enhanced joint mobility and functionality, which in turn translated to better scores in various domains of the SF-36. The specific techniques and interventions within TBHM might have targeted areas that directly influenced these aspects, resulting in significant improvements. In contrast, the immobilization group might not have experienced the same level of positive effects due to the restricted movement and lack of active therapeutic input. Regarding the WOMAC score, the TBHM group likely saw improvements because of its ability to address pain, stiffness, and physical function related to the condition. The comprehensive approach of TBHM might have alleviated pain more effectively, improved joint range of motion, and enhanced the patient’s ability to perform daily activities, all of which were reflected in the WOMAC score. Immobilization, on the other hand, might have had a more limited impact on these specific aspects. However, previous studies have not found significant efficacy of manual therapy in improving pain, physical function, or QOL in patients with KOA [35].

TBHM was a form of manual therapy. Previous studies have demonstrated that manual therapy has the ability to improve blood circulation around the joints, relieve muscle tension, and enhance joint flexibility, thereby facilitating the alleviation of KOA-related symptoms [36, 37]. In the present study, the rehabilitative effects that were observed could potentially be ascribed to the distinctive concept of “tissue-bone homeostasis” within the context of KOA. Several potential mechanisms could explain these effects: (1) an imbalance between internal and external muscle groups occured due to the abnormal patellar trajectory during knee extension in KOA. Therefore, joint disorders and pain followed. By making use of the “Push patellar” manipulation, the abnormal patellar trajectory could be corrected, reducing the compression of the patella femoris and the associated pain. (2) KOA caused irreversible damage to the articular cartilage and soft tissues surrounding the knee, such as adhesions. The “Release soft-tissue” manipulation effectively increased the knee range of motion by applying traction and loosening techniques. (3) The specific mechanical stimulation that was applied to the knee joint through these manipulations had the capacity to promote the metabolism of articular cartilage cells, enhance local blood circulation, relieve muscle tension, and alleviate symptoms like joint pain, swelling, and stiffness. Importantly, TBHM stood apart from other forms of manual therapy by its focus on tissue-bone homeostasis. While the study demonstrated an improvement in evaluation outcomes and explored potential mechanisms, it lacks empirical experiments to substantiate these findings. For instance, early research suggested physiological benefits such as increased blood flow, yet more recent studies utilizing doppler ultrasound techniques have indicated that manual therapy does not impact blood pressure [38].

The present study had several limitations that need to be acknowledged. The duration of the follow-up was relatively short. A longer follow-up period would have been beneficial for assessing the long-term effects and sustainability of the observed outcomes. It was possible that the benefits seen in the short term might not be maintained over an extended period. Besides, in this study, we excluded patients who had received intra-articular injections or other interventions within one month. However, a month might be too short a period for the effects of these interventions to wear off completely, which could potentially affect the results of this study. The relatively short time interval made it challenging to accurately assess the efficacy of the current treatment, as the residual effects of the previous interventions might still be influencing the patients’ symptoms and functions. In conclusion, while our study provided valuable insights, these limitations should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results. Future studies should address these limitations and further explore the long-term effects of TBHM on a variety of patient outcomes, including not only physical function but also mental health and quality of life. They should also integrate biomechanical analyses and MRI/CT data to gain a deeper understanding of the possible mechanisms of TBHM.

Conclusion

The TBHM intervention holds promise in enhancing gait, knee function, and overall quality of life among middle-aged and elderly people with KOA.
