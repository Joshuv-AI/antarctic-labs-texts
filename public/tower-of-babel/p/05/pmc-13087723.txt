Metabolic and redox adaptations of the corneal endothelium: From metabolic plasticity to therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Dan Jiang, Dawn Jing Hui Neo, Gary Swee-Lim Peh, Pinyan Jin, Wei Chen, Jodhbir S Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe corneal endothelium, a monolayer of nonregenerating cells on the posterior cornea, maintains corneal transparency through active fluid pumping. This review examines how corneal endothelial cells (CEnCs) meet high energy demands and adapt metabolically from quiescence to stress, with particular emphasis on their intrinsic metabolic plasticity. In CEnCs, ATP is generated through both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), with their relative contributions shifting according to oxygen tension and cellular state; OXPHOS remains relatively stable, whereas glycolysis increases during repair and stress. Environmental factors, including pH and temperature, further modulate metabolic flux and pump activity. CEnCs are also continuously exposed to oxidative stress arising from mitochondrial respiration, NADPH oxidase activity, and UVA exposure. Under physiological conditions, antioxidant networks involving NRF2/DJ-1 and related enzymes limit reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. However, in ageing and endothelial disorders such as FECD and diabetes, these defences become impaired. We discuss how disrupted mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy contribute to bioenergetic failure and endothelial cell loss across disease contexts. Finally, we critically review emerging therapeutic strategies, including metabolic modulators, gene-based approaches, organelle-targeted interventions, and cell-based endothelial replacement. By integrating these mechanisms, this review highlights how therapeutic modulation of metabolism and organelle quality control, together with strategies to address translational barriers, may protect the corneal endothelium, improve graft survival, and delay the need for corneal transplantation.Keywords: Corneal endothelial cells, Metabolic plasticity, Oxidative phosphorylation, Glycolysis, Reactive oxygen species, Mitochondria

Introduction

The corneal endothelium is a monolayer of specialized hexagonal cells on the posterior corneal surface. Despite being only one cell thick, this layer sustains corneal transparency via a “pump leak” mechanism; ion transport pumps continually dehydrate the stroma, countering the natural leakage of fluid into the cornea [1]. To fuel this fluid pump, corneal endothelial cells (CEnCs) have high metabolic demands and contain many mitochondria and an extensive endoplasmic reticulum [2,3]. In vivo, human CEnCs are essentially nonproliferating and are arrested in the G1 phase of the cell cycle [4]. Consequently, as the corneal endothelial cell density decreases with age because of cell damage or cell death, the remaining cells adapt by spreading out to maintain the functional integrity of the corneal endothelium. This leads to variability in cell shape and size. However, when cell loss from injury or disease surpasses a critical threshold, this compensatory mechanism fails, disrupting corneal hydration and resulting in edema, opacity, and ultimately, corneal blindness [5]. Given the dependence of ionic pump function on adequate energy supply and cellular health, understanding the metabolic requirements of CEnCs is critical for elucidating the mechanisms underlying corneal homeostasis, ageing, and disease.

Under physiological conditions (e.g., the in vivo aqueous humor environment), ATP generation in human CEnCs includes a substantial contribution from glycolysis, which supports their high energy demands for pump and barrier function [6]. While earlier studies emphasized mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) driven by the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, more recent work highlights metabolic flexibility and a key role for glutaminolysis in CEnCs [7]. Cells cultured under low oxygen (2.5% O2) exhibit a diminished glycolytic reserve, suggesting that their baseline energy production depends more heavily on glycolysis, with a limited capacity to further increase glycolytic flux when needed [8]. Cell phenotype also influences the metabolic strategy: mature, contact-inhibited CEnCs predominantly use mitochondrial respiration, whereas CEnCs that have undergone cell-state transition (CST) and acquired a dedifferentiated, fibroblast-like phenotype shift towards glycolysis [9]. This metabolic bifurcation suggests that functionally quiescent, pump-competent cells depend primarily on efficient OXPHOS-driven ATP production, whereas migrating or proliferating cells rely more heavily on glycolytic ATP generation, which is less dependent on oxygen availability [9]. Environmental factors such as oxygen tension, pH, and temperature modulate these pathways, and metabolic dysfunction of CEnCs is now recognized as a driver of corneal endothelial diseases such as Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) and bullous keratopathy [8,10]. This review integrates the current knowledge on the metabolic adaptations of CEnCs, encompassing their glycolytic/OXPHOS flexibility, responses to oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dynamics. The review is organized into five thematic sections: (1) physiologic metabolic homeostasis within the anterior chamber microenvironment; (2) redox stress and mitochondrial quality control; (3) disease-specific metabolic alterations in aging, FECD, and diabetes; (4) emerging therapeutic strategies and translational barriers; and (5) future directions and remaining questions.

Physiologic metabolic homeostasis of the corneal endothelium

Pathways and metabolic plasticity

CEnCs are highly metabolically active and leverage both glycolysis and OXPHOS to meet their considerable ATP demands for pump and barrier functions [7,8]. Glucose catabolism via glycolysis provides rapid amounts of ATP and generates lactate as a byproduct; CEnCs possess a robust glycolytic capacity and can rapidly increase glycolysis under anaerobic or stress conditions to maintain energy supply [8]. Excess lactate is shuttled into the aqueous humor by monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1 and MCT4) to prevent intracellular acid buildup and stromal lactate accumulation [11]. Concurrently, pyruvate (produced by glycolysis) and other substrates fuel OXPHOS in mitochondria, which supports sustained ATP production for Na+/K+-ATPase pumps that keep the cornea dehydrated and transparent [7]. This dual-pathway setup affords CEnCs a degree of metabolic redundancy: baseline ATP generation is maintained via co-utilization of glycolytic and oxidative pathways, with flexible partitioning as conditions vary [8].

In addition to glucose metabolism, glutamine metabolism (glutaminolysis) plays a pivotal role in CEnC bioenergetics. Glutamine acts as an anaplerotic substrate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and it has been shown to generate a large fraction of the ATP required for corneal endothelial function [7]. Isotope tracing studies have indicated that approximately half of the carbon used in the TCA cycle in CEnCs is derived from glutamine under physiological conditions [7]. This glutamine-driven TCA flux is tightly coupled with CEnC health; for instance, impairments in the SLC4A11 transporter (an ammonia-coupled H+ channel) disrupt glutaminolysis and ATP production, contributing to endothelial dystrophies. SLC4A11 normally facilitates proton flux linked to glutamine catabolism in mitochondria, and its activity modulates the mitochondrial proton gradient and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8]. Thus, glutaminolysis not only provides energy and metabolic intermediates but also maintains the redox balance crucial for the endothelial pump. CEnCs appear to rely minimally on fatty acid β-oxidation, as fatty acid usage does not substantially increase even when glucose/glutamine metabolism is perturbed [11]. Energy pathways of CEnCs.

Metabolic homeostasis, stress, and therapeutic modulation of corneal endothelial cells.

Determinants that redistribute glycolysis–OXPHOS flux in CEnCs include oxygen tension and spare respiratory capacity, glutamine anaplerosis (SLC4A11-linked), and lactate/H+ handling (MCT1/4 with bicarbonate buffering).

Oxygen tension and decreased spare respiratory capacity: The endothelium functions under low oxygen (∼2–3% O2) conditions, and glycolysis predominates over OXPHOS in this environment [13]. Under physioxia, mitochondrial ATP output persists, but the spare respiratory capacity is reduced as more pyruvate is diverted to lactate [8]. This alteration reflects substrate reallocation rather than mitochondrial damage. CEnCs maintain ATP levels by increasing glycolysis when oxidative flux does not increase, a critical adaptation to fluctuating oxygen levels (e.g., closed-eye or surgical hypoxia) [10]. Under low O2 conditions, SLC4A11 activity and proton leakage increase, indicating increased glutamine-driven respiration to support redox balance. Thus, oxygen availability dynamically shifts the glycolysis/OXPHOS balance to sustain energy homeostasis [11,14]. The detailed mechanisms underlying this oxygen-dependent metabolic regulation are discussed in the following section.

Glutamine anaplerosis (SLC4A11-linked): Glucose fuels glycolysis, while glutamine replenishes the TCA cycle. Imbalances in diseases such as diabetes impair function and reduce aqueous glutamine levels, limiting ATP production and promoting oxidative stress. Glutamine supplementation restores pump and barrier activity by mitigating oxidative damage [15]. Proper coordination of glucose and glutamine metabolism prevents ATP loss and toxic metabolite buildup; disruption, as in SLC4A11 deficiency, leads to metabolic collapse, lactate accumulation, and corneal oedema [11]. Transporters such as SLC4A11 and MCT1/4 maintain proton and lactate flux, preserving pH and energy balance; when impaired, CEnCs lose their metabolic flexibility and become dysfunctional.

Environmental modulation of the endothelial metabolism

CEnCs exist at the interface between the cornea and aqueous humor. Within this microenvironment, metabolic behaviour can be affected. Oxygen is supplied to the endothelium primarily from the aqueous humor (which comes into contact with periocular blood vessels and the atmosphere when the eye is open). Like with extended contact lens wear, hypoxia forces a greater reliance on glycolysis. Under such hypoxic conditions, CEnCs increase glucose utilization and lactate production [8]. The accumulated lactate is then coexported with protons via monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1, MCT4), a process that acidifies the extracellular space [16]. This decrease in local pH can directly impair pump function, since Na+/K+-ATPase activity and other ion transporters are pH sensitive. The endothelium mitigates this by efficiently shuttling lactate into the aqueous humor: MCTs are highly expressed in CEnCs, and if their function is experimentally reduced, lactate builds up in the stroma, and corneal thickness increases because of oedema [1,17]. Thus, adequate lactate efflux is an important aspect of metabolic adaptation, allowing CEnCs to prevent acidosis and maintain fluid transport in hypoxia.

Environmental pH modulates the metabolism of CEnCs. CEnCs reside within an aqueous humor environment, where the pH is tightly regulated to approximately 7.3–7.5 [18]. The slightest deviations in pH can alter enzyme activity and shift the CO2/HCO3− equilibrium, affecting cellular metabolism. CEnCs possess carbonic anhydrase and bicarbonate-linked transporters that buffer pH changes and facilitate lactate transport [19]. When the extracellular pH becomes more acidic, key glycolytic enzymes such as phosphofructokinase can be inhibited, and acid-sensing mechanisms may reduce the efficiency of the overall cellular fluid pump [20]. Conversely, higher alkaline conditions with greater levels of HCO3− tend to stimulate glycolysis and lactate production via increased intracellular pH, coupling lactate/H+ export to the endothelial fluid pump [10,20]. Overall, maintaining pH homeostasis is crucial; CEnCs maintain pH homeostasis by exporting lactate/H+ and importing buffers (CO2/HCO3−) through the bicarbonate/CA network to stabilize their metabolic microenvironment [10,20].

Temperature has a profound effect on the metabolism of CEnCs. In eye banks, hypothermic storage at 4 °C is implemented to slow metabolic activity, resulting in decreased phosphate levels and the suppression of ATP-consuming pathways, which helps preserve cellular viability by lowering ATP turnover [21]. When the tissue is warmed to 37 °C for transplantation, endothelial metabolism quickly recovers; in CEnCs, glycolytic flux rapidly generates ATP to restore pump function as activity resumes [10,21]. Conversely, therapeutic cooling of the cornea transiently diminishes pump efficiency, as evidenced by temperature-induced corneal swelling and its ability to reverse the effects of rewarming [22]. Taken together, these findings suggest that temperature changes induce largely reversible metabolic modulation in CEnCs and are important features for both corneal preservation and intraoperative management.

Beyond oxygen, pH, and temperature, the specific composition of the aqueous humor (AH) critically dictates CEnC metabolic homeostasis. Unlike blood plasma, the AH harbors supraphysiological concentrations of ascorbic acid (AA), often reaching levels 20- to 50-fold higher than in the systemic circulation [23]. This AA-rich microenvironment provides an important defense against continuous UVA-induced oxidative stress [24]. Furthermore, intracellular AA, transported via sodium-dependent vitamin C transporters (SVCT2), not only scavenges free radicals in the aqueous humor but also attenuates apoptotic signaling and prevents impairment of autophagic flux, thereby preserving the cellular integrity and metabolic capacity necessary to sustain pump function under chronic light-induced stress [24].

Spare respiratory capacity and substrate flexibility

With respect to mitochondrial metabolism, CEnCs exhibit only modest spare respiratory capacity under near-physiological conditions, and this limited capacity is highly sensitive to oxygen levels and substrate availability [8]. Acute energetic stress can therefore exceed mitochondrial output, shifting the ATP supply towards glycolysis while mitochondria adjust their coupling state and substrate use. CEnCs exhibit substrate flexibility, with glutamine serving as a key anaplerotic fuel that supports oxidative phosphorylation and pump function [7]. Ammonia handling in the anterior chamber represents an additional layer of metabolic regulation. While systemic ammonia levels are tightly restricted via hepatic urea synthesis, local ammonia in the AH may fluctuate significantly. In CEnCs, ammonia is not simply a toxic byproduct but also a metabolically relevant product of active glutaminolysis [25]. Within the physiological aqueous humor environment, local ammonia contributes to SLC4A11-dependent mild uncoupling of the inner mitochondrial membrane [14]. This mechanism partially dissipates the proton-motive force, thereby limiting excessive superoxide generation at the electron transport chain while preserving oxidative phosphorylation sufficient to support Na+/K+-ATPase activity. Consequently, in the context of SLC4A11 deficiency, CEnCs lose this ammonia-sensitive mitochondrial uncoupling, leading to immediate mitochondrial membrane hyperpolarization and excess glutamine-driven ROS, which undermines bioenergetics and organelle homeostasis [26]. Targeting mitochondrial ROS (mitoROS) can be beneficial in this context; in Slc4a11−/− models, the mitochondrion-targeted antioxidant MitoQ reduces ER stress marker levels and restores ER Ca2+ release upon glutamine exposure, indicating a causal link between mitoROS levels and downstream dysfunction [27]. Overall, CEnCs rely on a dynamic balance between glycolysis and OXPHOS with limited respiratory reserve; flexible fuel use is protective, but dysregulated glutamine metabolism, especially when SLC4A11 function is lost, can promote oxidative stress and lead to pump failure [25,27].

Redox stress and mitochondrial quality control

Redox balance: oxidative stress and antioxidant defences

Like other metabolically active cells, CEnCs continuously generate ROS through the mitochondrial respiration chain and enzymatic sources such as NADPH oxidases, while UVA is an external source of ROS. Under homeostatic conditions, antioxidant systems maintain ROS at controlled levels. However, when production exceeds detoxification, oxidative stress arises—a typical feature of FECD and ageing, in which oxidative damage to DNA and proteins, along with diminished antioxidant defences, have been well documented [28,29].

Mitochondria are a dominant source of ROS in CEnCs. The proton transporter SLC4A11 provides ammonia-sensitive mitochondrial “mild uncoupling” that prevents glutamine-driven membrane hyperpolarization and excessive superoxide formation. Loss of SLC4A11 disrupts this protective brake, increasing mitochondrial ROS levels, impairing bioenergetics, and promoting apoptosis [25]. In Slc4a11-deficient models, glutamine-fuelled mitoROS trigger ER stress, perturb lysosome–autolysosome–autophagy flux, and disturb Ca2+ handling. Importantly, the mitochondrion-targeted antioxidant MitoQ, a ubiquinone derivative that accumulates in mitochondria, reduces ROS levels, relieves ER stress markers, and partially restores ER Ca2+ release in vitro; although MitoQ is not an approved therapy for FECD, it exemplifies how targeting mitochondrial redox may restore ER–mitochondrial homeostasis [27].

In addition to its role in mitochondria, NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) contributes substantially to endothelial ROS production. Normally, NOX4-derived H2O2 is buffered in part by ER-resident peroxiredoxin-4; in CEnCs and disease models, NOX4 upregulation is associated with increased ER stress and autophagy activation. Such responses are believed to be initially protective but ultimately promote cell death over time. Genetic or pharmacologic NOX4 suppression, or the reinforcement of ER antioxidant defences, has been proposed to rebalance this axis and mitigate endothelial injury [30].

NRF2 is a master regulator of the antioxidant response [31]; DJ-1 (PARK7) supports NRF2 by limiting KEAP1-mediated degradation, thereby sustaining cytoprotective gene expression [32,33]. UVA traverses the corneal epithelium and stroma, and a fraction can reach the endothelium [34]. In human CEnCs, physiologic UVA activates NRF2-regulated antioxidant defences but triggers p53/caspase-3-mediated apoptosis in response to high or prolonged exposure. Vulnerability increases when the DJ-1/NRF2 axis is compromised, suggesting that impaired antioxidant signalling is linked to UV-induced cell death and that cumulative light exposure is involved in FECD progression [35,36].

Systemic metabolic stressors further amplify ROS levels. Hyperglycaemia increases the levels of mitochondrial superoxide and advanced glycation end products and impairs pump/barrier function. In cultured human CEnC models, high glucose attenuates mitophagy, disrupts mitochondrial dynamics, and decreases Na+/K+-ATPase and ZO-1 expression. Pharmacologic upregulation of this impaired mitophagy (using the mitophagy agonist CCCP) partially rescues endothelial function, as reflected by increased Na+/K+-ATPase and ZO-1 levels, indicating that dysregulated mitochondrial quality control is a key mediator of endothelial dysfunction in the context of diabetes [37].

Collectively, these findings indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated NOX4 signalling, UVA exposure, and metabolic stress all contribute to oxidative burden in CEnCs. Strategies that restore redox balance can be achieved by increasing NRF2 signalling, moderating NOX4 activity, safeguarding mitochondrial function (e.g., MitoQ), and regulating mitophagy. These are potential therapeutic approaches for slowing endothelial decline in patients with corneal dystrophy and diabetes.

Mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy

In CEnCs, mitochondrial quality control can be best understood as a tuneable system that determines the threshold for survival under metabolic load. Fusion and fission continuously remodel the mitochondrial network, whereas mitophagy removes irreparably damaged organelles. In FECD, this balance is disrupted and shifted towards fragmentation with loss of fusion capacity. The CEnCs from FECD patients show reduced MFN2 expression, swollen and punctate mitochondria, and reduced mitochondrial mass, changes that correlate with lower respiratory complex activities and increased release of cytochrome c. This results in decreased ATP output and amplification of apoptotic signalling. These observations are consistent with mitophagy being chronically activated but imbalanced with biogenesis, which progressively erodes the mitochondrial pool required to sustain pump function [38].

Mitochondrial quality control is closely associated with redox homeostasis. The corneal endothelial transporter SLC4A11 functions as an ammonia-sensitive uncoupler within the inner mitochondrial membrane. Loss of SLC4A11 abolishes this safety mechanism during glutaminolysis, causing membrane hyperpolarization, increased mitochondrial ROS levels, lysosomal dysfunction, and impaired autophagic flux [25]. Quenching mitochondrial ROS in SLC4A11-deficient models restores ER signalling and relieves the autophagy bottleneck, emphasizing that redox regulation is essential for effective organelle turnover [25].

Systemic metabolic stressors cause dysregulation of this checkpoint. In diabetes, chronic hyperglycaemia suppresses the PINK1–Parkin axis and LC3B, resulting in enlarged, poorly recycled mitochondria and reduced Na+/K+-ATPase expression. Pharmacologic relief of this mitophagy inhibition with the uncoupler CCCP restores LC3-II and increases Na+/K+-ATPase and ZO-1 expression in CEnCs, indicating that a controlled increase in mitophagy can result in the recovery of pump-related proteins when baseline autophagic turnover is inadequate [37].

Taken together, these lines of evidence support a threshold model of mitochondrial maintenance (Fig. 1). When oxidative stress remains below damaging thresholds and respiratory capacity is preserved, controlled fission and mitophagy support mitochondrial renewal while maintaining the mitochondrial pool. When fusion is curtailed or SLC4A11-mediated uncoupling is lost, the level of ROS increases above damage thresholds, whereas the level of ATP falls below bioenergetic requirements, thereby disrupting mitochondrial quality control and promoting defective clearance and apoptosis [7,25]. In cultured CEnCs, mature, pump-competent subpopulations exhibit increased mitochondrial respiration with upregulated TCA/OXPHOS enzyme levels, whereas cell-state–transitioned (dedifferentiated/EMT-like) subpopulations rely more on glycolysis; extracellular-flux and proteomic analyses have shown that mitochondrial metabolic programming is associated with cell-state identity and functional competence [7,9].

Disease-specific metabolic alterations

Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD)

FECD is a common degenerative disease of the corneal endothelium and is the leading indication for corneal transplantation. It is characterized by the progressive loss of CEnCs and the formation of extracellular matrix deposits on Descemet's membrane known as guttae, leading to corneal oedema and vision decline. Emerging evidence implicates metabolic and oxidative stress as central drivers of FECD pathogenesis.

A hallmark of FECD is its genetic component: an expanded CTG trinucleotide repeat in the TCF4 gene, which is present in American and European patients [[39], [40], [41]]. This repeat expansion causes RNA toxicity and splicing perturbations in endothelial cells. Experimental therapies targeting expanded TCF4 mRNA are being explored to address the genetic cause of the disease [42]. Beyond the primary genetic drivers, FECD progression is accompanied by disruption of the metabolic and redox homeostasis described in Section 2. The disease microenvironment, shaped in part by guttae-associated mechanical stress and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [43,44], appears to exceed the buffering capacity of the NRF2/DJ-1 antioxidant axis. In cases associated with SLC4A11 variants, loss of ammonia-sensitive mitochondrial uncoupling may also promote inner mitochondrial membrane hyperpolarization. Together, impaired antioxidant defense and loss of mitochondrial uncoupling are associated with increased mitochondrial fragmentation. These changes are accompanied by reduced MFN2 expression, swollen punctate mitochondria, and decreased activity of multiple respiratory complexes, particularly complexes I, IV, and V [38,45]. The resulting bioenergetic impairment, characterized by reduced ATP production and increased mitochondrial ROS, may activate cytochrome c-mediated apoptotic signaling and contribute to progressive endothelial cell loss [[45], [46], [47], [48]].

Taken together, these findings indicate that FECD involves the convergence of genetic susceptibility, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and proteostatic imbalance, with substantial overlap with pathways implicated in endothelial ageing. Impaired mitochondrial ATP production, oxidative stress, and chronic ER stress likely contribute jointly to progressive endothelial cell loss. Accordingly, current therapeutic strategies are focused on restoring metabolic homeostasis and limiting oxidative damage. Gene-targeted approaches directed at TCF4 repeat expansions and cell-based therapies may provide additional options, although further validation is required before clinical application.

Bullous keratopathy and diabetic corneas

Bullous keratopathy refers to corneal oedema that typically arises from endothelial decompensation, often following surgical trauma or an event of acute endothelial loss. Under these conditions, a significant proportion of endothelial cells are lost, and the remaining cells fail to maintain corneal deturgescence. Interestingly, the metabolic phenotype of CEnCs in bullous keratopathy differs from that in chronic dystrophies such as FECD [28]. As bullous keratopathy often results from acute injury, surviving endothelial cells are usually those that were healthy and metabolically normal prior to the insult.

The diabetic corneal endothelium is another context in which metabolic factors contribute to dysfunction. Chronic hyperglycaemia in diabetes mellitus induces subtle changes in CEnCs, even in the absence of overt corneal oedema. Clinically, patients with diabetes often experience delayed corneal wound healing and some degree of endothelial cell loss over time [15,37]. At the molecular level, prolonged high glucose exposure causes mitochondrial damage and dysregulated mitophagy in CEnCs [37]. As mentioned earlier, hyperglycaemia reduces the expression of PINK1, Parkin, and LC3B, hindering the removal of damaged mitochondria [37]. The resulting accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria leads to increased ROS levels and reduced Na+/K+-ATPase pump expression, ultimately compromising pump efficiency [37]. These findings suggest that in diabetes, the corneal endothelium undergoes a state of metabolic decline rather than outright cell loss during early disease stages.

Clinically, the corneas of diabetic patients are more susceptible to oxidative stress, but interestingly, some systemic diabetic treatments may have protective effects on the cornea. A recent clinical study revealed that patients with type 2 diabetes treated with long-term metformin therapy had higher corneal nerve fibre densities and fewer corneal immune dendritic cells than nonmetformin-treated patients did [49]. While this study focused on neurotrophic and immune parameters, it suggested that the known actions of metformin, which include restoring cellular metabolism via AMPK activation and oxidative stress inhibition, may also favourably alter the corneal microenvironment. Metformin activates AMPK across various cell types, which can promote autophagy and support mitochondrial health [50]; in CEnCs, AMPK activation is known to support cell survival under metabolic stress [51]. Therefore, it is conceivable that metformin helps maintain endothelial function in diabetic patients, although direct mechanistic evidence remains under investigation.

Aging and cellular senescence

Natural aging has a significant impact on corneal endothelial metabolism and function. Endothelial cell density declines gradually at an average rate of 0.5%-0.6% per year [52], with the remaining cells showing signs of senescence. Senescent CEnCs exhibit gene changes, increased expression of cell cycle inhibitors, and sometimes a senescence-associated secretory phenotype that can influence the microenvironment [[53], [54], [55]].

One key regulator of ageing and metabolism is the family of sirtuins, which are NAD+-dependent deacetylases. In particular, SIRT1 promotes stress resistance, DNA repair, and metabolic efficiency. In the corneal endothelium, SIRT1 activity decreases with age, contributing to cellular senescence. Experimental evidence has shown that overexpression of SIRT1 in cultured human CEnCs enhances their proliferative capacity and delays senescence [56]. These findings suggest that age-related loss of SIRT1 activity contributes to endothelial senescence and, conversely, that maintaining SIRT1 activity can maintain endothelial cell health.

Another strategy to counter endothelial ageing is supplementation with NAD+ precursors such as nicotinamide or its derivatives. Nicotinamide has multiple beneficial effects on CEnCs. It serves as a precursor to NAD+, thereby acting as a substrate for sirtuins and other NAD+-dependent enzymes that support metabolism and genomic stability. In an in vivo study, nicotinamide administration prevented endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EnMT) and premature senescence of donor-derived CEnCs after transplantation [57]. Additionally, NAD+ precursors confer protection to CEnCs against acute oxidative injury; for example, in a mouse UV exposure model, nicotinamide and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) prevented UV-induced endothelial apoptosis by activating the AKT signalling pathway and reducing the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio [58]. Taken together, these data strongly support the concept that sustaining NAD+ levels and sirtuin activity can rejuvenate CEnCs and prolong their functional lifespan.

Ageing is accompanied by telomere attrition in CEnCs, as documented in human donor tissue [59]. The cornea is chronically exposed to ultraviolet light, and experimental UVA/UVB exposure induces senescence-like phenotypes in CEnCs [55]. Telomeric DNA is hypersensitive to UV-induced photolesions and relatively refractory to repair, linking cumulative UV exposure to accelerated telomere erosion [60]. Consistent with this, FECD explants have shown reduced telomere length alongside mitochondrial DNA abnormalities [61]. While telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) expression is low or undetectable in CEnCs in vivo [62], experimental hTERT overexpression extends the proliferative lifespan and preserves endothelial characteristics in culture [63]. Taken together, these findings suggest that telomere maintenance complements NAD+–sirtuin support as a strategy to delay corneal endothelium ageing.

In summary, ageing of the corneal endothelium is characterized by reduced cell density, increased oxidative damage, and cellular senescence, which are intimately tied to metabolic decline. Interventions that increase the activity of NAD+/sirtuin pathways, promote mitophagy or DNA repair, and preserve telomeres show promise in counteracting these changes. The goal is to prolong the functional viability of the endothelium, delaying or preventing the need for corneal transplantation in elderly individuals.

Emerging therapeutic strategies and translational barriers

Improved understanding of CEnC metabolism has identified several potential therapeutic strategies. Emerging metabolic therapies for corneal endothelial repair.

Pharmacologic agents that target metabolic pathways

ROCK inhibitors, such as Y-27632, ripasudil, and fasudil, are known to facilitate corneal endothelial repair and enhance the efficacy of cell therapy by modulating actin cytoskeletal dynamics. Transient ROCK inhibition has been shown to increase the bioenergetics of CEnCs, increase resistance to metabolic stress, and preserve Na+/K+-ATPase activity [78]. Mechanistically, ROCK blockade drives the translocation of hexokinase to mitochondria and rapid glycolytic upshifts. Clinically, topical ROCK inhibitors have demonstrated utility in the graft-free management of FECD and in peri-transplant “priming,” effects likely mediated through metabolic boosting and/or reprogramming [64,78].

Mitochondrion-targeted antioxidants such as MitoQ and SkQ1 appear to accumulate within mitochondria to neutralize ROS-associated corneal endothelial dysfunction. In Slc4a11 knockout mouse models, MitoQ reduces ER-related stress, restores Ca2+ signalling, and increases the survival of CEnCs [27,66]. During the ex vivo expansion of CEnCs, SkQ1 decreases free radical levels and activates NRF2-dependent genes without impairing cellular growth [30]. These preclinical data suggest potential utility for donor tissue handling and ex vivo cell preparation rather than the established in-human therapy at present [30]. A prospective study led by researchers at Massachusetts Eye and Ear is recruiting to evaluate whether topical NAC reduces reactive oxygen species levels in vivo and confers cytoprotection in FECD patients (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04440280). While not disease specific, a randomized trial in patients with hard-cataract phacoemulsification (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06781970) aimed to evaluate whether oral ascorbic acid mitigates surgery-induced oxidative stress to the corneal endothelium-an approach that, if effective, may be adaptable to FECD or post-EK windows as a metabolism-supportive adjuvant.

With respect to sirtuin activators and NAD+ supplements in the context of ageing, the increase in sirtuin activity confers significant cytoprotective benefits to CEnCs. Pharmacologic activators of SIRT1 have been proposed to promote a pro-survival, anti-senescent phenotype in the endothelium [56]. Although cornea-specific evidence remains limited, findings from other cellular systems suggest that SIRT1 activation may improve mitochondrial function and reduce apoptosis under stress conditions [79]. More directly, treatment with NAD+ precursors such as nicotinamide (NIC), NMN, or NR has been revealed to be effective. In ex vivo models, NIC prevents EnMT and apoptosis, thereby effectively increasing the proliferative capacity of CEnCs [57,58]. Taken together, these findings suggest that NAD + augmentation may help preserve metabolic function and support recovery following injury. In fact, nicotinamide formulations are currently under clinical evaluation as adjuncts to cultivated CEnCs for transplantation, with the aim of improving long-term graft survival [57].

Gene therapy and cell-based strategies

Because the corneal endothelium is largely postmitotic in vivo, gene- and cell-based approaches are of particular interest for endothelial disorders. Rather than acting only on downstream metabolic consequences, these approaches aim to address upstream cellular defects.

Gene Therapy: One gene therapy target in FECD is the pathogenic CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion within TCF4. In one study, CRISPR-dCas9 targeting the CUG trinucleotide repeat expansion in FECD patient-derived CEnCs markedly reduced the numbers of expansion-induced RNA foci and repeat transcripts without affecting TCF4 mRNA, supporting its therapeutic potential, although effective in vivo delivery remains a major challenge [67]. Similarly, introducing a functional SLC4A11 gene via a viral vector has been shown to rescue CEnCs in CHED models by restoring their intracellular pH and metabolic homeostasis [69]. With respect to SIRT1 gene therapy, augmentation has been investigated using lentiviral-driven SIRT1 overexpression in human CEnCs, leading to increased cellular proliferation and wound healing in vitro [70]. Engineered cells maintain their characteristic polygonal morphology and pump protein expression while bypassing senescence-associated barriers [70]. These approaches, however, require precise regulation to avoid uncontrolled cell division or EnMT. Rigorous safety and efficacy studies in preclinical models will be crucial before clinical translation. Translating these gene-editing and viral replacement successes to the clinic, however, is bottlenecked by delivery constraints. Achieving uniform, endothelial-specific viral tropism without inducing anterior segment inflammation, alongside mitigating potential off-target CRISPR-Cas9 edits in long-lived, post-mitotic CEnCs, represent critical safety barriers that must be rigorously addressed before in-human application. In vivo ocular gene therapy has already been used in patients with retinal disease [80], and a corneal intrastromal gene therapy trial is underway for HSV-1 keratitis (NCT06474416).

Cell Therapy: Transplantation of healthy CEnCs is an area of rapidly advancing therapeutic development, and several clinical trials are ongoing. The traditional surgery is endothelial keratoplasty, where the donor endothelium is transplanted together with Descemet's membrane as a graft. Newer approaches involve intracameral injection of cultured human CEnCs into the anterior chamber, which adhere to the patient's Descemet's membrane to reconstitute a functional monolayer. This minimally invasive cell-injection therapy, pioneered in Japan, has shown excellent results in early trials: patients with FECD recovered corneal clarity without the need for a full graft, and five-year follow-ups demonstrated sustained improvement in most cases [65]. To further improve outcomes, metabolic adjuncts are being incorporated into such cell therapy protocols. Specifically, ROCK inhibitors, known to increase the maximum ATP output from both OXPHOS and glycolysis [78], are used as adjuvants to increase the adhesion and survival of CEnCs [65]. Additionally, as mentioned, supplementation of injected CEnCs with nicotinamide prevents the onset of EnMT and cellular senescence, effectively improving graft longevity [57]. However, a major translational barrier for these cell-injection therapies is the oxidatively hostile and nutritionally depleted anterior chamber of patients with advanced FECD or bullous keratopathy. Naïve iPSC-derived or cultivated donor CEnCs may encounter substantial metabolic stress after intracameral delivery. In this setting, transient ROCK inhibition or NAD + augmentation may function as supportive measures that improve early cell survival, adhesion, and maintenance of endothelial phenotype under microenvironmental stress. Allogeneic iPSC-derived CEnCs have now progressed from the bench to clinical trials in Japan, with a first-in-human study reporting improvements in visual acuity and corneal thickness, with no serious adverse events [71]. In parallel, magnetically guided delivery of nanoparticle-loaded human CEnCs, which underpins Emmecell's EO2002 platform, has been shown in preclinical models to safely deliver cells to the posterior cornea and reduce corneal oedema, supporting its development as a nonsurgical cell-injection therapy for endothelial failure [81,82]. Building on this preclinical work, Emmecell's EO2002 magnetic human corneal endothelial cell therapy is now being evaluated as a nonsurgical, intracameral cell-injection treatment for corneal oedema in a phase 1, multicentre, open-label, dose-escalation trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04894110). Further engineering of CEnCs to enhance metabolic resilience, for example by increasing antioxidant capacity or reducing pro-apoptotic signaling, may improve graft durability [[83], [84], [85]].

Mitochondrial transfer and organelle therapy

Mitochondrial transplantation refers to the delivery of exogenous healthy mitochondria to cells with impaired mitochondrial function. This approach has been demonstrated in cardiac ischaemia [86,87] and in certain neurodegenerative models [88,89]. Beyond systemic applications, a recent review by Yeo et al. comprehensively summarized mitochondrial transplantation as an emerging therapy across corneal, optic nerve, and retinal diseases, highlighting its ability to replenish functional mitochondrial pools, alleviate oxidative stress, and rescue bioenergetic failure in ocular tissues [72]. In the eye, donor-cell mitochondria have been shown to traffic to multiple ocular cells, including CEnCs [90]. Consistent with this, cultured human FECD tissue has been shown to internalize healthy exogenous mitochondria, leading to the recovery of bioenergetics and cellular function [73]. In FECD, one proposed approach is direct injection of isolated mitochondria into the anterior chamber, with the aim of restoring mitochondrial function in stressed endothelial cells. Complementary approaches that promote mitochondrial biogenesis or enhance intercellular mitochondrial transfer may further support these effects in aged or diseased CEnCs.

Despite promising in vitro results, isolated organelle therapy faces significant translational hurdles, as the half-life and cellular uptake of naked mitochondria are severely limited by the hostile extracellular environment. Addressing these limitations will likely require improved biomaterial-based delivery strategies. Emerging biomaterial platforms, including biomimetic red blood cell membrane encapsulation [91], gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) microgels [92], and cell-penetrating peptide-modified liposomes like the MITO-Porter system [93], may help protect exogenous mitochondria from rapid degradation. These nano- and micro-scale vehicles preserve organelle viability and enhance targeted cellular uptake via membrane fusion or macropinocytosis. If effective, such delivery systems could improve intracellular mitochondrial uptake and help restore bioenergetic function in the host endothelium.

In addition to mitochondria, extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes, have emerged as promising intraocular delivery platforms. Specifically, bioactive cargo-loaded CEnC-derived small EVs were able to deliver peptides to the mouse corneal endothelium in vivo [75]. In human CEnCs, MSC-derived EVs attenuate ER stress signalling and reduce caspase-3 activation, limiting apoptosis in vitro [76]. In contrast, exosomes secreted by CEnCs appeared to have antiproliferative effects, inhibit growth, slow wound closure, and increase apoptosis in vitro and ex vivo across human, porcine, and rabbit corneas [94]. Additionally, EVs have the capacity to carry and transfer mitochondrial DNA, proteins, and even intact mitochondria, allowing for intercellular transfers of mitochondrial content. In support of this concept, aqueous humor proteomic profiling in patients with FECD revealed a selective enrichment of mitochondrial constituents, including SLC25A3, pyruvate carboxylase and DJ-1, together with reduced TOM20 expression and evidence of damaged mitochondrial ejection in the corneal endothelium [95]. In the same study, UVA-exposed human CEnCs released extracellular vesicles carrying increased amounts of mitochondrial DNA, and pharmacologic inhibition of exosome biogenesis exacerbated cell death and mitochondrial membrane potential loss in FECD endothelial cells, suggesting that exosome-mediated export of mitochondrial material acts as a protective quality-control pathway whose blockade accelerates FECD pathogenesis [95]. These observations suggest that engineered EVs may complement mitochondrial transplantation strategies aimed at improving metabolic function in diseased CEnCs [96].

Taken together, combining mitochondrial transplantation with organelle-targeted approaches, such as EV-based delivery or pharmacologic stimulation of mitochondrial biogenesis, represents a potential strategy for FECD and related endothelial disorders, although substantial translational work remains necessary.

Diet, supplements, and lifestyle interventions

Although the cornea is avascular, it remains intricately connected to systemic physiology. Systemic health and nutrition can influence corneal endothelial metabolism in the long term. One striking example is a case report of a patient with FECD who adopted an exclusively whole-plant food diet. After more than 6 weeks on this diet, the patient showed improvement in visual acuity, a notable reduction in corneal stromal oedema on OCT, and even a decrease in intraocular pressure [77]. Furthermore, an observational study on FECD linked cumulative smoking, cardiovascular comorbidities, and dietary differences (e.g., higher sodium intake and specific micronutrient imbalances) to the disease, suggesting modifiable risks [77]. In patients with diabetes, better blood glucose control is associated with reduced corneal endothelial compromise (higher HbA1c levels with lower endothelial cell density and increased corneal thickness); thus, maintaining target glucose may mitigate hyperglycaemia-related endothelial stress [97,98]. In summary, systemic metabolic health may influence long-term corneal endothelial homeostasis; therefore, dietary and lifestyle measures may be considered as adjunctive strategies, although direct evidence remains limited.

Future directions and remaining questions

Corneal endothelium metabolism is regulated by a complex interplay of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors; thus, multiomics approaches are essential for defining its mechanisms and advancing the understanding of FECD and other corneal diseases. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has already revealed heterogeneity among human CEnCs [99]. Distinct subpopulations have been identified, with cells in different states; some cells express proliferative markers while others exhibit increased expression of pump- and leakage-related genes, reflecting metabolic diversity [99]. In FECD patients, scRNA-seq further revealed disease-related endothelial heterogeneity and transcriptional gene signatures that may aid in patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making [100]. Analysis of corneal tissue from presymptomatic patients with the TCF4 repeat expansion mutation revealed early shifts in alternative splicing and extracellular matrix pathways, with mitochondrial dysfunction in the repeat cohort, providing biomarkers and a foundation for the development of integrated multiomics models that support personalized care [101].

Despite substantial progress, several questions remain, including the identity and niche of putative corneal endothelial stem or progenitor cells, the functional distinctions between central and peripheral CEnCs, and the potential of peripheral cells as a reparative reserve, as well as the practical means of clinically assessing a patient's endothelial oxidative stress and metabolic state. Integrated multiomics, spatial omics [102], and advanced imaging (e.g., RUSH3D mesoscopy) [103] may help to bridge these knowledge gaps and enable molecular phenotyping and the selection of targeted therapies.

Conclusions

The corneal endothelium is a quiescent monolayer that relies on coordinated use of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to sustain pump function and preserve corneal transparency. Evidence accumulated over the past decade indicates that disruption of this metabolic balance is closely linked to endothelial dysfunction in ageing, FECD, bullous keratopathy, and diabetes. In FECD in particular, chronic oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, and impaired organelle quality control appear to act in concert to drive progressive cell loss. These findings support the view that metabolic failure is not simply a secondary consequence of endothelial disease, but a central component of its pathogenesis.

This framework also provides a rationale for metabolism-informed therapeutic strategies. Approaches under investigation include restoration of redox balance, improvement of mitochondrial quality control, and support of cellular cofactor homeostasis, with ROCK inhibitors, mitochondrion-targeted antioxidants, and nicotinamide-based interventions among the most actively studied candidates. At the same time, advances in gene editing, cell therapy, and organelle-based approaches are expanding the range of possible interventions, although substantial translational challenges remain. Overall, a better understanding of corneal endothelial metabolism should help refine both disease stratification and treatment development, and may support future strategies aimed at preserving endothelial function and delaying the need for corneal transplantation.
