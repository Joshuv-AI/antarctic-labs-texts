Focused Power Ultrasound Mediated Inferior Perirenal Adipose Tissue Modification Therapy for Essential Hypertension: A Pilot Study
Authors: Yang Hua, Meng‐Huan Li, Ting‐Ting Wu, Lei Jing, Ming Jiang, Cui‐Ying Liu, Jia‐Ming Yang, Yu‐Xuan Lou, Yue Yuan, Yun‐Fan Tian, Min Zhang, Rong‐Sheng Wang, Jing Cai, Yan‐Hui Sheng, Wei Sun, Xiang‐Qing Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPerirenal adipose tissue (PRAT) contributes to the maintenance of elevated blood pressure through afferent nerves and serves as an important peripheral, modifiable target for hypertension management. This single‐center, prospective pilot trial assessed the feasibility and safety of a novel focused power ultrasound device for perirenal adipose tissue modification (PRATM) therapy in essential hypertension. Twenty patients (mean age 47.5 ± 11.0 years, 85% male) with office systolic blood pressure (OSBP) 140–180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (ODBP) ≥90 mmHg were enrolled. All underwent PRATM therapy and were followed for 3 months. The primary endpoint was all‐cause mortality or device‐related adverse events (AEs). Four patients experienced transient mild lumbar pain, and one had mild skin redness, all resolving spontaneously. No serious AEs or clinically significant abnormalities were observed. OSBP decreased by 14.6 mmHg at 1 month and 18.2 mmHg at 3 months; ODBP decreased by 5.8 mmHg and 2.8 mmHg, respectively. For the 24‐hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), 24‐hour ambulatory SBP decreased by 3.6 mmHg (95% CI: ‐3.6‐10.8 mmHg) and 24‐hour ambulatory DBP decreased by 2.2 mmHg (95% CI: −2.7–7.0 mmHg) at 1 month. At 3 months, 24‐hour ambulatory SBP decreased by 2.8 mmHg (95% CI: −5.4–11.0 mmHg) and 24‐hour ambulatory DBP decreased by 1.7 mmHg (95% CI: −4.0‐7.3 mmHg). PRATM shows preliminary feasibility and safety, but larger, randomized trials are needed for definitive efficacy and long‐term safety validation.Keywords: essential hypertension, feasibility, focused power ultrasound (FPU), perirenal adipose tissue modification (PRATM), safety

Introduction

Hypertension, defined by persistently elevated arterial blood pressure, is a critical public health challenge worldwide. It is a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, including ischemic heart disease, stroke, and heart failure, as well as chronic kidney disease and cognitive impairment [1]. In China, the prevalence of hypertension has risen dramatically over the past few decades, currently affecting a quarter of the adult population, equating to over 300 million individuals [2]. Despite improvements in awareness and healthcare infrastructure, the control rate of hypertension remains suboptimal, particularly in rural areas. This growing epidemic highlights the urgent need for innovative and effective interventions to alleviate the associated health and economic burdens.

The management of hypertension traditionally relies on lifestyle modifications and pharmacological therapies, such as ACE inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEI/ARBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), β‐blockers, and diuretics. However, a substantial subset of patients remains refractory to optimal medical therapy, with persistently elevated blood pressure levels [3]. This therapeutic gap is exacerbated by multifactorial barriers such as poor medication adherence, adverse side effects, and socioeconomic constraints associated with lifelong pharmacotherapy [4]. Critically, conventional strategies primarily modulate systemic hemodynamics without addressing specific underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, such as sympathetic overactivity and vascular dysfunction [5]. These inherent limitations underscore the urgent need for innovative therapeutic paradigms that address root pathogenic mechanisms while minimizing treatment‐related morbidity.

Recent research has identified visceral fat as a critical contributor to the development and progression of hypertension [6]. Perirenal adipose tissue (PRAT), which surrounds the kidneys, exerts mechanical pressure on renal structures and contributes to the activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) and sympathetic nervous system (SNS), both of which are central to blood pressure regulation [7]. The accumulation of PRAT has been strongly correlated with elevated blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk [8]. In our prior research, we reported the role of perirenal adipose afferent nerves in maintaining pathological high blood pressure, and surgical ablation of bilateral PRAT or chemical denervation led to a significantly sustained reduction in blood pressure, which demonstrated the feasibility of targeting PRAT as a therapeutic approach for blood pressure management [9].

Focused power ultrasound (FPU), a noninvasive therapeutic technology, offers a promising approach to target PRAT. By delivering precise acoustic energy, FPU can induce localized thermal or mechanical effects and has been extensively applied in clinical use [10]. Our research team has independently developed a novel FPU system, the Darwin I focused power ultrasound system, which is capable of providing personalized power output tailored to the characteristics of individual subjects and modifying PRAT. Based on preliminary animal experimental results, this innovative FPU has demonstrated both efficacy and safety in the modification of PRAT.

This pilot study aims to explore the potential application of PRAT modification therapy by using the Darwin I focused power ultrasound system as a novel noninvasive therapy for treating essential hypertension, with a focus on the safety and feasibility.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective, single‐arm, single‐center, pilot study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of perirenal adipose tissue modification (PRATM) therapy in hypertensive patients. All enrolled subjects meeting the inclusion criteria underwent PRATM treatment. Safety and efficacy assessments were performed at 24 h, 1 month, and 3 months post‐treatment. The evaluation encompassed the following parameters: subjective symptom descriptions, laboratory markers, renal and renal artery ultrasound imaging, 24‐hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), office blood pressure (OBP) measurements, and any treatment‐related adverse events (AEs).

Study Participants

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ranging from 25 to 75 years; (2) 140 mmHg ≤ office systolic blood pressure (OSBP) < 180 mmHg, or office diastolic blood pressure (ODBP) ≥90 mmHg, with no restrictions on current antihypertensive medications; (3) Ultrasound assessment confirming that the superior–inferior (SI), left–right (LR), and anterior–posterior (AP) diameters of the inferior PRAT are all ≥20 mm; (4) Voluntary participation with signed written informed consent.

Participants meeting any of the following criteria will be excluded: (1) Secondary hypertension such as renal artery stenosis, pheochromocytoma, Cushing's syndrome, or primary aldosteronism; (2) history of major abdominal surgery or procedures affecting PRAT or adjacent anatomical structures; (3) renal or hepatic dysfunction (serum creatinine [SCr], alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) >3 times the upper limit of the normal range); (4) myocardial infarction, stroke, or transient ischemic attack within the past 6 months; (5) uncontrolled hyperglycemia (fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, 2‐h postprandial blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥7%); (6) uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction; (7) urinary calculi or hematuria; (8) atrial fibrillation; (9) severe structural heart disease (including moderate‐to‐severe valve stenosis or regurgitation, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, etc.); (10) second‐degree or higher atrioventricular block; (11) coagulation disorders; (12) active infection at the treatment site or systemic infections; (13) malignant tumors; (14) and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

All participants were required to maintain the type and dosage of their antihypertensive medications throughout the 3‐month follow‐up period, unless they met the predefined criteria for blood pressure escape: (1) OSBP ≥ 180 mmHg, or (2) OSBP < 90 mmHg with associated symptoms of hypotension.

Thirty potential participants were initially screened, among whom 20 were finally enrolled based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1). Six participants were excluded due to OBP not meeting the inclusion criteria, one due to inadequate PRAT content, and three declined to participate in the clinical study. After a 3‐month follow‐up period, two participants were lost to follow‐up, and 18 participants completed the follow‐up. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: 2021‐SR‐004). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were fully informed of the study details and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Blood Pressure Measurement

OBP was measured using a validated automated oscillometric device (YE670A, Jiangsu Yuyue Medical Equipment & Supply Co., Ltd.). Measurements were performed in the seated position following a 5‐min rest period. Three consecutive readings with an interval of 1–2 min were recorded, and the average of the last two readings was used for analysis. OBP was assessed at baseline, immediately after PRATM treatment, and at each follow‐up visit.

The 24‐hour ABPM device (SunTech Oscar2, SunTech Medical Limited, Shenzhen) was programmed to measure blood pressure automatically at 20‐min intervals during the daytime (06:00–22:59) and at 60‐min intervals during the nighttime (23:00–05:59). A minimum of 30 valid measurements were required for the daytime period, and at least six valid measurements were required for the nighttime period.

PRAT Measurement

Considering the nonuniform distribution of the PRAT, the inferior PRAT represents the most abundant fat accumulation and is shaped like an inverted cone. Thus, the inferior PRAT is designated as the target zone of PRATM therapy. The thickness of the inferior PRAT was assessed under renal ultrasound examination by a qualified ultrasound physician using an EX20 ultrasound device (Lanmage, Shenzhen, China). Participants were positioned in a lateral decubitus position on the examination table with the skin over the lumbar region exposed. The ultrasound probe was aligned parallel to the longitudinal axis of the body, and the isoechoic region adjacent to the lower pole of the kidney was identified as the inferior PRAT. In this longitudinal view, the maximum SI diameter of the inferior PRAT was measured and recorded. To obtain the transverse view, the ultrasound probe was rotated 90 degrees to align perpendicularly with the skin surface and then gently moved in a caudal direction. The isoechoic structure exhibiting slight respiratory motion was confirmed as the inferior PRAT. At the level where the lower pole of the kidney was no longer visible, the maximal LR and AP diameters were immediately measured (Figure 2).

PRATM Therapy Procedure

Darwin I Focused Power Ultrasound System (Nanjing Khons Medtech Co., Ltd.) was used for PRATM therapy. The parameters of the FPU probe were as follows: transducer frequency, 1.3 MHz ± 15%; focal area ≤ 2 mm×2 mm×13 mm; longitudinal depth range of focal region, 5–13 cm; adjustable electric power range, 50–200 W. The schematic diagram of PRATM treatment is shown in Figure 3. The treatment procedure consisted of the following steps: (1) Initial localization: Participants were positioned in a lateral decubitus position. Renal ultrasound was used for initial localization of the inferior PRAT. The ultrasound probe was aligned to identify the kidney, and the inferior PRAT was visualized along the inferior renal margin. (2) Focused ultrasound localization: The robotic arm was controlled to position the FPU probe against the marked area on the participant's lumbar skin. The ultrasound probe integrated within the treatment head was activated, and the position and angle of the treatment head were fine‐tuned to ensure that both the long and short axes of the inferior PRAT were encompassed within the treatment zone beneath the probe. (3) Target area planning: Darwin I Focused Power Ultrasound System displayed both the long‐axis and short‐axis ultrasound views of the inferior PRAT on its screen. The operator used the console to delineate an appropriate treatment area based on these images. (4) Individual assessment: A temperature‐sensing ultrasound probe was placed on the participant's back region to monitor the temperature within the target area, ensuring it remained within the optimal range. The FPU system began emitting energy, and the output power gradually increased to achieve the ideal treatment temperature and optimal energy delivery. This process allowed for individualized energy selection for each subject, ensuring both efficacy and safety. (5) Initiation of treatment: Based on the size of the planned target area, Darwin I Focused Power Ultrasound System calculated the number of treatment points required (step distance: 6–8 mm; layer distance: 13 mm). Each point was treated for 60 s, and the same procedure was repeated on the contralateral side of the PRAT. All participants received PRATM therapy only once.

Safety Assessment

Subjective Symptom Record

FPU delivers concentrated energy to the inferior PRAT region adjacent to the lower pole of the kidney. As the energy traverses through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscle layers en route to the target area, symptoms indicative of potential damage along this pathway are critical safety indicators. These include skin redness, blisters, ulceration, lower back pain, lumbar soreness, and distending pain. Additionally, given the potential for energy dispersion to affect adjacent structures such as the kidneys and intestines, hematuria, abdominal pain, and hematochezia are also regarded as essential safety endpoints.

Serological Markers

The delivery of FPU energy may induce tissue cell damage or hemorrhage, which could manifest as systemic inflammatory responses and alterations in liver and kidney function, as assessed by laboratory blood tests. The following serological markers were evaluated: CK (creatine kinasealanine), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (Hb), serum creatinine (SCr), urea, and high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP).

Urinary Markers

Considering that PRAT is anatomically located adjacent to renal tissue, the potential dispersion of energy during focused ultrasound therapy may pose a risk of acute kidney injury (AKI). To assess the possibility of urinary tract bleeding, urinary red blood cell counts were tested. Furthermore, specific biomarkers indicative of AKI were evaluated to monitor renal damage, including neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin (NGAL) [11], tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases‐2 (TIMP‐2) [12], and insulin‐like growth factor‐binding protein 7 (IGFBP‐7) [12]. Urine NGAL levels > 150 ng/mL indicate an increased risk of AKI [11]. For the product [TIMP‐2·IGFBP7], levels > 300 (ng/mL)2 suggest a higher risk of AKI [13].

Renal and Renal Artery Ultrasound

Patients underwent renal and renal artery ultrasonography assessments at baseline, 24 h post‐treatment, and at 1‐month and 3‐month follow‐up. Bilateral kidney lengths, mean velocity (MV), pulsatility index (PI), and resistive index (RI) of the main renal arteries were measured and recorded. The primary focus was on the size and morphology of the kidneys, as well as detecting any abnormalities in renal blood flow or potential signs of renal hemorrhage.

Study Endpoints

Primary Endpoint

This study primarily evaluates the feasibility and safety of the novel FPU system, Darwin I, in clinical applications. Therefore, the primary endpoint is defined as all‐cause mortality and the incidence of AEs within 3 months post‐treatment. In this study, AEs are categorized as follows: (1) skin injury: thermodynamic damage to the skin at the contact site of the treatment device, characterized by redness, swelling, heat, pain, blisters, and ulceration persisting for more than 24 h. (2) Treatment‐related muscle and visceral injuries: damage to muscles and organs along the energy delivery pathways, including intestinal injury (e.g., abdominal pain, abnormal bowel movements) and renal injury (e.g., hematuria, subcapsular hemorrhage).

Secondary Endpoints

The secondary endpoints comprise changes in OBP measured in the clinic and the mean blood pressure measured by 24‐hour ABPM at the 1‐month and 3‐month follow‐up visits.

Statistical Methods

The Shapiro–Wilk test is used to assess whether continuous data follows a normal distribution. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions. Within‐group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using McNemar's test (two related samples) or Cochran's Q test (three or more related samples). For normally distributed continuous variables, within‐group comparisons were conducted using One‐way Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc Bonferroni‐corrected paired t‐tests. For non‐normally distributed continuous variables, within‐group comparisons were carried out using the Friedman rank‐sum test. Paired t‐test (for normally distributed continuous variables) or paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests (for non‐normally distributed continuous variables) was employed to compare differences in laboratory blood and urinary markers between baseline and each follow‐up post‐treatment (two related samples). AEs were reported as the number of occurrences and incidence rates. Individual missing values were imputed using the mean value imputation method. All statistical analyses were performed at a two‐sided significance level of 0.05 using SPSS version 25.0.

Results

Patient Characteristics

From October 1, 2020 to May 14, 2021, a total of 20 participants were ultimately enrolled in the trial and constituted the full analysis set for outcome analysis (Table 1). The median age was 47.5 years, and 85% of them were male. 90% of participants were receiving antihypertensive medication, with ACEI/ARB, CCB, β‐blockers, and diuretics prescribed to 60%, 30%, 35%, and 30% of the patients, respectively. At baseline, the mean OBP was 155/102 mmHg, and the mean values of 24‐hour ABPM were 138/85 mmHg.

Primary Outcomes

All participants included in the study successfully completed the FPU‐mediated PRATM therapy (Figure 4). No AEs, such as death, skin injury, or visceral organ damage, were observed during the operation or in the postoperative follow‐up period.

Renal ultrasound of one patient's inferior PRAT before and at 24 h post treatment. (A, C) short axis view; (B, D) longitudinal axis view.

During PRATM treatment, four patients experienced transient and mild lumbar pain, characterized by soreness and distending sensation. The duration of this pain was short, not exceeding 1–2 min in all cases, and it subsided immediately upon cessation of energy delivery. No additional analgesic intervention was required, and the treatment was successfully completed as scheduled in all instances. These participants were closely monitored during the immediate recovery phase and throughout the remaining procedure. Their vital signs remained stable, and no further discomfort was reported after the treatment. Follow‐up assessments were conducted at 24 h, 1 month, and 3 months after the procedure. Throughout the entire follow‐up period, there were no reports of recurrent pain, residual symptoms, or any other related complications.

One case exhibited mild redness of the local skin at the treatment site, without blistering or rupture. The condition resolved completely and spontaneously within 24 h, without the need of topical medication or further intervention. No other AEs were observed or reported throughout the entire study.

There were no significant differences in laboratory indices post‐therapy compared to pre‐therapy, except for a decreased level of LDH, indicating no aggravated muscle, liver, or kidney damage induced by FPU therapy (Table 2). Urinary NGAL, a biomarker of AKI, was elevated in only one patient post‐therapy; however, the elevation of NGAL was far below the diagnostic threshold for AKI. No cases of symptomatic hypotension or hypertensive crisis were observed during follow‐up, and no patients reported significant discomfort within 3 months post‐therapy. Discussion

In this pilot investigation, the utilization of Darwin I focused power ultrasound for PRATM therapy demonstrated both technical feasibility and an excellent short‐term safety profile in patients of essential hypertension. Notably, no device‐ or procedure‐related AEs or SAEs were observed during the 3‐month follow‐up period. Comprehensive evaluations, including serial biochemical analyses, inflammatory markers, and renal ultrasonographic assessments, verified no evidence of iatrogenic injury to muscles, kidneys, or adjacent visceral structures of PRATM therapy.

Regarding preliminary efficacy, PRATM therapy elicited clinically meaningful reductions in blood pressure parameters, particularly OSBP, with a mean reduction of 18.2 mmHg (95% CI: 10.8–25.6 mmHg) at 3 months post‐intervention. Importantly, 61.11% of participants (11/18) achieved guideline‐recommended blood pressure targets (OSBP < 140 mmHg) at the 3‐month visit, suggesting a potential therapeutic role for PRATM in hypertension management. These findings align with preclinical evidence implicating PRAT in blood pressure dysregulation via mechanical compression and neurohormonal activation (9).

Technological Advancements in FPU Systems

FPU has emerged as a transformative noninvasive therapeutic modality across multiple medical disciplines, offering precise energy delivery for tissue‐specific interventions. In oncology, high‐intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) enables extracorporeal tumor ablation through spatially confined thermal effects (peak temperature 60°C–90°C), achieving coagulative necrosis of target lesions while preserving adjacent healthy tissues—a paradigm validated in the management of prostate cancer, uterine fibroids, and liver tumors [14]. Concurrently, micro‐focused ultrasound (MFU) has demonstrated efficacy in dermatologic applications, including collagen remodeling for skin tightening, hypertrophic scar modulation, and neuromodulation‐mediated analgesia [15]. Despite such advancements, conventional FPU face several challenges and critical limitations that hinder clinical translation. First of all, the standardization deficits. The absence of consensus guidelines for key treatment parameters (e.g., frequency, spatial peak temporal average intensity, exposure duration) contributes to inter‐institutional outcome variability [16]. The second limitation lies in the personalization barriers. Current systems lack adaptive algorithms to account for patient‐specific anatomical variations (e.g., adipose tissue composition, anatomical variations, and disease severity), often resulting in suboptimal energy delivery [17].

Hence, the evolution of next‐generation FPU systems has become a critical imperative, demanding the integration of artificial intelligence‐driven adaptive algorithms and submillimeter‐level spatial precision to address the persistent challenges of tissue heterogeneity and patient‐specific anatomical variability in therapeutic strategy optimization and energy delivery execution.

To overcome the limitations of conventional FPU, next‐generation systems such as the Darwin I FPU system have been engineered with adaptive energy modulation and real‐time tissue feedback capabilities. This system integrates high‐resolution ultrasonography with machine learning algorithms to dynamically adjust acoustic parameters (e.g., frequency: 1.3 MHz ± 15%, focal depth: 5–13 cm, power: 50–200 W) based on individual PRAT morphology and acoustic impedance profiles. Such precision minimizes off‐target energy dispersion, ensuring localized PRAT modification while preserving renal parenchyma and adjacent retroperitoneal structures.

Safety Profile of PRATM Therapy

In this pilot study, comprehensive and rigorous safety assessments, encompassing patient subjective symptoms, physical signs, serial biochemical panels (SCr, ALT/AST, hs‐CRP), urinary biomarkers (NGAL, TIMP‐2, IGFBP7), and renal and renal artery ultrasound examinations, were conducted at 24 h, 1 month, and 3 months post‐treatment, confirming no evidence of procedure‐related tissue damage or organ injury along the energy delivery pathways. Furthermore, the adjacent organ structures and functions exhibited no abnormalities. No all‐cause death or device‐ or procedure‐related AEs were observed. Though, transient and mild lumbar pain occurred in 4 of 20 participants during PRATM treatment, and resolved spontaneously upon cessation of FPU energy, with subsequent follow‐ups confirming no anomalies in safety evaluation. It is likely attributed to fascial thermoacoustic interactions, the reflection of FPU beams at specific interfaces, such as the fascia layers of the quadratus lumborum, internal oblique muscle of the abdomen, or latissimus dorsi muscle. Excessive ultrasonic beam reflection at tissue interfaces may potentially induce thermomechanical tissue injuries and localized inflammatory cascades. Only one patient experienced redness of the local skin, without blister or rupture, and subsided within 24 h post‐treatment. This patient had a history of psoriasis, and it was considered as a Koebner phenomenon triggered by localized mechanical stress. Overall, no SAEs, hemodynamic instability, or late‐onset complications were observed, underscoring the procedural safety of Darwin I focused power ultrasound system for PRATM therapy.

Antihypertensive Efficacy and Clinical Implications

The preliminary efficacy of FPU for PRATM therapy in treating essential hypertension has shown promising and meaningful results. Significant reductions in OSBP were observed, with a sustained mean decrease of 14.6 mmHg (95% CI: 7.8–21.5 mmHg) and 18.2 mmHg (95% CI: 10.8–25.6 mmHg) at 1 month and 3 months post‐therapy compared to baseline, respectively, which is equivalent to the effect size of adding a second antihypertensive agent. Notably, 61.11% of patients (11/18) achieved guideline‐directed BP targets (OSBP < 140 mmHg), correlating with a projected 20%–25% reduction in cardiovascular risk based on SPRINT trial data [18]. Due to the high variability of OBP, which is susceptible to external factors such as the environment and psychological states, the application of 24‐hour ABPM can objectively reflect the patient's blood pressure variation patterns, exclude pseudo‐hypertension, reduce placebo effects, and aid in accurately assessing the efficacy of antihypertensive treatment [19]. While 24‐hour ambulatory SBP reductions (Δ24‐h SBP at 1 month: 3.6 mmHg with 95% CI: −3.6–10.8 mmHg; Δ24‐h SBP at 3 month: 2.8 mmHg with 95% CI: −5.4–11.0 mmHg) fell short of the European Consensus threshold for device therapies for hypertension [20], the concordance between office and ambulatory BP trends yet supports the biological plausibility and holds promise for PRATM‐mediated antihypertensive effects in this study.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

This exploratory investigation has several constraints:

(1) Pilot design limitations: The single‐arm, open‐label design (n = 20) precludes blinding and introduces potential placebo effects, particularly given the subjective nature of office BP measurements.

(2) Confounding factors: Unmeasured lifestyle variables (e.g., sodium intake variability, smoking status) and reliance on self‐reported medication adherence may bias efficacy estimates.

(3) Short‐term follow‐up: The 3‐month endpoint cannot exclude late‐onset complications (e.g., renal fibrosis) or assess long‐term blood pressure control durability.

Prospective large‐scale multi‐center trials employing sham‐controlled randomization, extended monitoring, and objective adherence metrics (e.g., liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry drug level assays) are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

This pilot study suggests that the novel FPU mediated PRATM therapy in essential hypertension is feasible and safe, however large, sham‐controlled study is further needed to evaluate long‐term safety and efficacy.
