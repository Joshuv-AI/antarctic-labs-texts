Integrating Nonpharmacologic Strategies for Pain with Inclusion, Respect, and Equity (INSPIRE): Cultural and linguistic tailoring for a digital health intervention
Authors: Marvyn R. Arévalo Avalos, Junlin Chen, Tess Fairchild, Kalisha Goodwin, Janice Tsoh, Adrian Aguilera, Jason M. Satterfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic pain (CP) disproportionately affects underserved populations who often experience barriers to evidence-based nonpharmacologic treatments. Digital health interventions can address these barriers by providing scalable and accessible CP management resources. However, culturally and linguistically tailored digital interventions are rare, which may limit engagement and effectiveness. The Integrating Nonpharmacologic Strategies for Pain with Inclusion, Respect, and Equity (INSPIRE) intervention combines a tailored mobile app with culturally and linguistically concordant health coaching to deliver cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based interventions (MBI), and movement-focused interventions (MFI) for CP management. This manuscript describes the process of linguistically and culturally tailoring the INSPIRE app for three target adult populations—African American/Black, Spanish-speaking Latinx, and Cantonese-speaking Chinese.Iterative tailoring was driven by multiple rounds of stakeholder focus groups followed by generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) and expert review to ensure accuracy and cultural congruence while optimizing limited resources. After initial GenAI content generation in English, the adaptation process began with surface-level adjustments in language translation and cultural representation in visual and audio elements. Deep-level adaptations incorporating culturally rooted values and beliefs about CP were made to address culturally specifically experiences with stigma and bias relevant to CP.This study highlights a replicable, efficient framework for adapting digital health interventions to improve equity and inclusion in CP management by combining AI-driven tools with human expertise to achieve optimal cultural and linguistic adaptations. Future research will evaluate the effectiveness of the INSPIRE intervention in a randomized controlled trial to assess engagement, acceptability, and improved pain outcomes across diverse populations.

Introduction

Chronic pain (CP) is a debilitating condition resulting in impairments to physical functioning, mental health, and quality of life (Schmiesing et al., 2022; Hooten et al., 2012). In 2023, nearly 1 in 4 (24.3%) adults in the United States had CP and 8.4% had high-impact CP for three or more months, limiting personal and work activities (Lucas and Sohi, 2024). The Centers for Disease Control (CDC) has recommended the use of nonopioid and nonpharmacological approaches as the preferred treatment for CP (Dowell et al., 2022). Specifically, approaches including Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Mindfulness Based Interventions (MBI), and Movement Focused Interventions (MFI) have demonstrated efficacy in improving CP management (Skelly et al., 2020; Turner et al., 2016; Williams et al., 2012; Rikard et al., 2023). Yet, recent estimates indicate that 21% of adults with CP do not seek treatment (Feldman and Nahin, 2024), only 8.4% report using nonpharmacological interventions exclusively for their CP management, and between 40 and 51% use a combination of nonopioid and nonpharmacological treatment (Rikard et al., 2023; Pritchard et al., 2022). This lack of access and underutilization of evidence-based nonpharmacologic interventions becomes even more extreme with underserved and non-English-speaking populations (Vargas et al., 2025; Crimmel et al., 2024; Bhondoekhan et al., 2023; Pritchard et al., 2024). Improving access and acceptability of effective nonpharmacological interventions are urgently needed to meet this clinical need.

Health disparities and chronic pain management

The experiences of underserved populations with CP are often negatively impacted by patient-, provider-, and system-level factors. For example, compared to their white counterparts, African American/Black and Latinx patients are less likely to receive referrals for specialty CP treatment (Vargas et al., 2025; Crimmel et al., 2024) and for nonpharmacological treatment options (Bhondoekhan et al., 2023; Pritchard et al., 2024). These disparities are exacerbated by provider implicit biases, which result in underestimation of patient pain, less comprehensive diagnostic workups, and lack of care coordination (Meints et al., 2019; Morrison et al., 2021). Furthermore, access barriers (e.g., cost, transportation), limited availability of nonpharmacological treatment options (e.g., CBT and Physical Therapy) (Driscoll et al., 2021; Becker et al., 2017, Becker et al., 2018), and lack of linguistically congruent care for patients with limited English proficiency (Diamond et al., 2019) contribute to disparities in CP management.

At the patient level, self-stigma, underreporting of pain tied to cultural beliefs such as stoicism (Tung and Li, 2014), patients' distrust of clinicians or perceived discrimination by providers, and low treatment expectations contribute to limited engagement with CP treatments in traditional healthcare systems (Anderson et al., 2009). Yet, underserved populations have been shown to rely on a variety of culturally based, nonpharmacological interventions to address CP. For example, studies have shown that African Americans/Black adults use spirituality and supportive church communities to help them cope with chronic illness (Siler et al., 2021; Park et al., 2013). Latinx individuals report seeking care from traditional healers (e.g., curanderos) who use massage-like therapies, herbalism, and spirituality as interventions, in part due to accessibility, affordability, and linguistic and cultural congruence (Park et al., 2013; Cruz et al., 2022). Chinese adults often prefer traditional Chinese medicine, including Chinese therapeutic body work (e.g. Tuina massage), herbalism and acupuncture; as well as mind-body exercises (e.g., Tai Chi and Qigong) (Dong and Li, 2018; Matos et al., 2021). Increasing access and cultural acceptability of evidence-based and culturally grounded nonpharmacological interventions for CP may result in greater uptake and improved outcomes for these populations.

Culturally adapted digital health interventions

Digital health interventions, including mobile apps and internet-delivered approaches improve CP outcomes, facilitate accurate symptom monitoring, and expand reach and access to nonpharmacological CP interventions (Weatherly et al., 2024; Moman et al., 2019; Pfeifer et al., 2020; Macea et al., 2010). Unfortunately, these tools have rarely been developed or adapted for underserved populations, despite findings that support cultural adaptation of digital interventions (Eustis et al., 2023; Ellis et al., 2022). Moreover, engagement with digital health interventions may also be limited due to issues of low digital literacy (Williams et al., 2021; Anderson-Lewis et al., 2018). Linguistically and culturally adapted interventions that are sensitive to literacy, health literacy, and digital literacy could result in both improved access and CP outcomes for populations with significant unmet needs.

Guided by a data-driven multi-step iterative process, cultural adaptations of interventions aim to balance the need for tailored or targeted content without removing or significantly altering the “active ingredients” of the existing intervention (Barrera Jr et al., 2013). Grounded in the existing literature, the process of cultural adaptation may include “surface level adaptations” that aim to improve the way in which intervention content is presented to meet the needs of the target population (e.g., translating content from English to Spanish) and/or “deep level adaptations” which involve more extensive and significant changes to the intervention content based on underlying values, beliefs, customs, and preferences of the target user's culture (Barrera Jr et al., 2013; Resnicow et al., 1999). Deep level adaptations require a more intimate and comprehensive understanding of the target user's cultural history, traditions, social norms, and communication style in order to present content in the most culturally relevant way to target a user's health behaviors and explanatory model of illness. This complex endeavor often requires active involvement of diverse stakeholders (e.g., community leaders or patients) and content experts who iteratively review, revise, and test the intervention over a long and costly process.

Recent technological advances, particularly large language model (LLM) based Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), are demonstrating effectiveness at translating, adapting and simplifying medical content with the goal of increasing patient understanding and engagement (Maddox et al., 2025; Martos et al., 2025). Using prompt engineering to provide context and guidance, GenAI tools could achieve both surface and deep level adaptations more efficiently thus saving substantial time and money. However, GenAI has also been known to provide erroneous information and may reflect broader societal biases, making the integration of expert oversight and field testing necessary.

The INSPIRE intervention

The Integrating Nonpharmacologic Strategies for Pain with Inclusion, Respect, and Equity (INSPIRE) intervention includes a mobile application that blends cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based interventions (MBI), and movement-focused interventions (MFI) that users select and compile into a tailored pain management program (Ristau et al., 2026). Content was structured into 12 weekly modules, each including lessons about CP and pain management strategies, recommended exercises and activities that can relieve pain and/or suffering, inspiring stories from patients who live with CP, and home practice activities. Each weekly module is followed by a 30-min telehealth appointment with a linguistically and culturally concordant pain management coach, which are health coaches recruited to represent the communities of patients they would be coaching and thus need to be fluent in the preferred language of the patient and have a shared cultural background. The coaches' role is to assist with skills acquisition and to address progress toward patient goals. The health coach is a critical component of the intervention, as studies have shown that the incorporation of a human health coach can improve engagement in digital health interventions (Rethorn et al., 2021; Buhrman et al., 2004). For African American, Spanish-speaking Latinx, and Cantonese-speaking Chinese patients a culturally and linguistically concordant health coach can provide support to patients not only regarding the challenges they encounter while exploring the intervention but also create a culturally familiar space to enhance trust and engagement.

The INSPIRE application also contains supplementary health education content in the “Library” and a “Quick Relief” section that allows users to address acute flare-ups. In order to best solidify new CP management skills, participants receive a “booster session” with their coach at months 5 and 11.

This manuscript describes the process of linguistically and culturally tailoring the INSPIRE intervention from a “multicultural” English-language version into three additional versions for specific cultural and linguistic groups: African American/Black adults, Spanish speaking adults (Latinx version), and Cantonese speaking adults (Chinese version). These groups were chosen as they represent groups disproportionately impacted by CP who experience significant barriers to access. Locally, Spanish and Cantonese speakers represent over 50% of the patient population within the San Francisco Bay Area study sites. The aim of this manuscript is to contribute to this emerging yet underdeveloped field of cultural adaptation by providing a detailed description of our cultural adaptation process using GenAI that may serve as a reference for future research studies seeking to culturally adapt interventions.

Step 1: stakeholders formative research

Focus groups

Two rounds of 75-minute focus groups were conducted via Zoom between January to May 2023 with the goal of eliciting suggestions and feedback on the INSPIRE mobile app design, pain coach's role, and study procedures. Eligibility criteria included age 18 years or older, having a primary care provider at UCSF Health or San Francisco Health Network (SFHN), speak and read either English, Cantonese, or Spanish, have access to a smartphone, and have non-cancer related CP for more than 3 months. Participants were recruited from primary care clinics and community-based organizations utilizing flyers and digital ads. Participants were compensated with a $50 gift card. Focus groups were facilitated by two members of the research team from each of the corresponding communities and conducted in the preferred language. Meetings were recorded and co-facilitators took detailed, time stamped field notes to facilitate review of meeting recordings when necessary. All field notes were reviewed and discussed by the larger research team in a primarily deductive analysis. Inductive findings (i.e. emergent themes from participant responses) were informally noted and used.

Round 1 “Blue Skies” Focus Groups: Three focus groups (Multicultural-English, African American/Black, and Cantonese-speaking Chinese) were conducted between January and February 2023. Participants (n = 18) were asked to share about their experiences with CP management, methods used to relieve pain and whether they were helpful, and their experience with CBT, MBI, and MFI. Participants were asked to share information about the design and features they would like to see in a CP management mobile app and to share ideas about the role of pain coaches for the INSPIRE Intervention. A Spanish-language Latinx group was not formed for round 1, but some of these more general questions (e.g., CP management experiences) were asked during round 2. Of note, two Black participants mentioned they would be more inclined to use a “white” version of the app since they assumed it would be of higher quality than the Black/African American version.

Round 2 “Wire Frames & Study Procedures” Focus Groups: Four focus groups (Multicultural-English, African American/Black, Cantonese-speaking Chinese, and Spanish-speaking Latinx group) were conducted between March and May 2023. Participants (n = 23) were presented with a sample of low-resolution mobile app wireframes (e.g., feature designs such as login process, homepage, chronic pain resource “library”, pain journals, etc.), potential intervention programmatic outlines, an overview of the 12 weekly modules and role of the pain coaches. Participants included both new members and returning members from round 1. Participants were asked to share their feedback regarding the preliminary mobile app features and design, and feedback on study protocols. All materials were translated into Spanish and Chinese and were proofread by language experts on the research team.

Advisory boards (CAB & CSPAB)

To provide consultation and guidance regarding all stages of the project, the INSPIRE team formed two independent advisory board: The Community Advisory Board (CAB) and the Clinical Science and Practice Advisory Board (CSPAB). The CAB consisted of eight members: four community experts working in advocacy focused agencies and four healthcare providers representing Black/African American, Latinx, and Chinese communities supported by the INSPIRE intervention. The CAB role was to provide patient and clinician perspectives regarding CP and CP treatment, review proposed materials and the study design, and provide support and guidance around community engagement, recruitment, retention, and dissemination. The CAB met quarterly during the duration of the study. CAB members were individually interviewed regarding potential design features and were asked to review and discuss the focus group field notes for their respective communities. The second board, the CSPAB, was a board of health care professionals - a pain medicine physician, pain psychologist (who was also bilingual and bicultural Latinx), pharmacist (who was Chinese and spoke some Cantonese), and physical therapist. They were identified as local experts commonly consulted regarding chronic pain management. This board met semi-annually to give feedback on design then reviewed the full treatment manual when we were near completion and offered feedback. The board provided an additional layer of approval that the INSPIRE intervention was a high-quality, evidence-based intervention.

Step 2: app design and English low literacy content

A "multicultural" web-based version of the INSPIRE app was first created in English using formative feedback and expert input. The intervention content, including pain self-management strategies and cultural adaptations, was developed by the investigative team. Given the substantial breadth of interventions (i.e. CBT, MBI, MFI) and the number of related pain management tools, detailed GenAI prompts were used to support language refinement, translation, adjustment of reading level, and audio narration. To promote authenticity, trust, and credibility, introductory videos featured recordings of study staff and investigators. Animated characters used in the remaining video content were created by the technology team's graphic designer using investigators as visual references. AI-generated narration was used for the English and Spanish versions, except for the voices of personal stories ‘vignettes’ embedded within the app, which were narrated by native Spanish speakers. All GenAI materials were reviewed and revised by clinical experts. Human voice recordings were used for the Cantonese version because the speech synthesis tools available within our development workflow did not consistently achieve comparable quality and naturalness. Given the tonal complexity of Cantonese (Wong and Chan, 2018) and the need for script adaptation and native-speaker review, human-recorded narration was deemed the most efficient and culturally appropriate approach during intervention development. The following is an example GenAI prompt used for a basic health education library entry:

ChatGPT prompt: You are an interdisciplinary team of pain management specialists in an outpatient medical setting. Create a 750-word educational document that defines chronic pain, identifies evidence-based nonpharmacologic interventions, and emphasizes the links between mind and body. This document should be written at the 6th grade reading level for a patient with limited health literacy. The tone should be hopeful and encouraging.

Step 3: beta-testing for app layout and functionality

After the primary app architecture, layout, and preliminary content had been developed in English, it was translated into Spanish and Cantonese and captured on detailed demonstration slides for the purpose of beta testing. Beta testers participants, N = 31, were recruited from UCSF Health or San Francisco Health Network (SFHN) primary care clinics via Epic (Electronic Health Record) data pulls, referral, or flyers (i.e. using a similar recruitment process to the RCT, see Protocol for details (Ristau et al., 2026)). The inclusion/exclusion criteria was as follows: Participants must be 18 years or older; speak English, Spanish, or Cantonese; be willing to use a smartphone app; have experienced non-malignant chronic pain for 3 months or more; must be able to provide informed consent; must have a UCSF Health or SFHN primary care provider. These participants reviewed the more detailed layout and functions and were asked specific digital literacy questions such as “If you wanted to go back one screen, what button would you push?” Areas of confusion were noted along with suggestions for simplifications especially for users with limited digital health literacy.

Given the number of materials to be adapted (over 200 written pages and over 100 videos in English), GenAI (e.g. ChatGPT, DeepL, Google Translate) was used to quickly translate language followed by expert review and correction. Improved materials were further tailored using GenAI and again reviewed and revised by experts. A sample GenAI prompt is below:

ChatGPT Prompt: Translate the [attached content file] considering the following criteria: The text is for a mobile app designed to help users learn and practice mind-body techniques to manage chronic pain. The translated text needs to be culturally appropriate for the following user population: Spanish-speaking population of immigrants predominantly from Mexico and Central America, residing in the greater San Francisco Bay Area, with a 6th-grade reading level, and using “tú”, an informal yet personable tone and style.

GenAI was also used to assist with cultural adaptations of INSPIRE content – e.g. patient stories, metaphors, analogies. As with linguistic translations, this content was reviewed and revised by experts. Linguistic and cultural adaptations emerged iteratively and concurrently. A sample GenAI prompt for cultural adaptation is below:

ChatGPT Prompt: Create a brief parable about perseverance while living with chronic pain. Use universal themes (such as patience, balance, or resilience) and imagery that would be respectful and broadly relatable to individuals of Chinese cultural backgrounds, without relying on clichés, assumptions, or cultural stereotypes. Use respectful, inclusive, person-first language that emphasizes dignity, agency, and hope.

Step 5: field testing the adapted intervention with the target populations

N = 24 participants were enrolled over a four-week period into a single arm field test of the INSPIRE intervention. The recruitment procedures and inclusion/exclusion criteria were the same as those reported in the RCT (Ristau et al., 2026) and listed in Section 2.3 (Beta testing). Participants were asked to complete 12 weekly modules in the INSPIRE app and were also asked to attend weekly telehealth visits with a pain coach. Coaches kept detailed weekly notes regarding feedback from patients that might further improve intervention tailoring. Pain management experts in medicine, pharmacy, psychology, and physical therapy were also invited to review the app and provide feedback on quality and fidelity of the interventions. This feedback was then used to further revise the intervention prior to a larger clinical trial.

Results

Formative feedback

In round 1 of the focus groups, participants shared their past experience with CP management and strategies that they tried. Most people have tried multiple non-pharmacological methods to relieve pain, including acupuncture, massage, mindfulness/meditation, yoga, stretching and heat/cold application. These methods were commonly used across three cultural groups. There were some unique methods tried by the Chinese group only, which were Taichi and Baduanjin. One participant from the English group mentioned they used hot tubs and jets at home to relieve pain, while participants from the Black/African American group mentioned about the use of chiropractic service and ear seeds. Some common concerns of these methods include high cost of the service, inconsistency, and no long-term effects of single treatment.

Most participants reflected they have minimal experience of using digital tools for CP. The English-speaking group reported they have tried digital health apps to relieve stress and pain, while the other three groups only watched meditation and exercise videos online. Most participants have some experience with mindfulness and physical therapy but reflected they have limited experience with CBT. The Latinx and Black/African American groups mentioned praying as their way to meditate. Concerns raised by the Black/African American group include effectiveness of a mobile app for managing severe pain, the accessibility of the intervention content, and the absence of human touch and connection. The Cantonese-speaking group, on the other hand, expressed concerns about the time demands of the intervention and the potential technical difficulties in using an app.

It is notable that several participants advocated for a “multicultural” version of the app. These participants were all English-speaking but racially diverse including White, Asian, Black, and more than one race individuals. While some indicated there might be bias in a tailored version and not the “white” version, others did not want to be pigeon-holed, and others felt left out (e.g. an Asian participant who did not speak Cantonese and could not identify with the Black/African American English version).

In round 2 of the focus groups, participants were presented with wireframes of some preliminary app designs. All cultural groups shared a preference for a simple, straightforward and effective pain management app. Participants from all groups provided positive feedback on the idea of the telehealth pain coach component of the intervention, emphasizing their preference for interaction with a real person in their journey of CP management. They indicated that an effective pain coach should be experienced, knowledgeable, approachable and able to communicate in the participant's preferred language. Participants appreciated images that conveyed cultural/ethnic representation.

INSPIRE mobile app adaptations

Black/African American version (English)

The cultural tailoring of the INSPIRE app was conducted by four research team members belonging to the African American community and with extensive experience working with this population in clinical and community settings (e.g., LMFT, DPT and minister in a Black church, CEO of a community organization). Team members used ChatGPT to critically analyze existing content and obtain examples of possible adaptations to improve relevance for the Black/African American community. For example, team members ensured that language referencing color, such as the positive connotations of “white clouds” and the negative connotations of “dark skies,” were changed to avoid invoking negative perceptions of Black people compared to white people. Additionally, the storytelling experience was adjusted to include aspects that were more relatable to the Black community. For instance, referencing something as simple as a Saturday morning spent cleaning while Mom played old Prince albums could spark a memory unique to their culture. Patient stories were similarly altered to include more relatable examples. Finally, some participants mentioned that they found the characters of color to be overwhelming at times and would have appreciated more variety, particularly in the gender of the person presenting the material. Multiple participants suggested the inclusion of inspirational quotes from notable Black leaders, thinkers, and artists (e.g. Maya Angelou, Oprah Winfrey, Martin Luther King Jr.).

Latinx version (Spanish)

The adaptation of the INSPIRE app from English to Spanish focused on evaluating the direct Spanish translation of the English-Multicultural material with the use of three types of AI software: Google Translate, DeepL, and ChatGPT. The Spanish-speaking and Latinx team members with expertise in clinical psychology evaluated each of the three AI translations for linguistic and cultural appropriateness and a decision was made to translate content using a combination of Google Translate and ChatGPT due to the flexibility of tailoring the prompts to create a version that would be best understood to a general audience of the Latinx population.

With the goal of improving accessibility and acceptability of the intervention content, all materials were translated using a standard of a 6th grade reading level as well as using the informal version “tú.” vs the formal version “usted” which would have created more of a disconnect between the target audience and INSPIRE team. The reasoning behind this is that utilizing “tú” is more personable and inviting within the Latinx community as this is commonly used when talking to a friend.

When needed, the translations were edited for clarity and word choice to ensure appropriate fit to the Latinx community. For example, AI's direct translation of “coach” was “entrenador” which may not have had the appropriate impact given that in Spanish, “entrenador” holds a sport or fitness related connotation. Therefore, after mutually agreeing on a more appropriate word choice, “coach” was manually updated to “apoyador” which means “supporter,” and to avoid confusing the audience on what the coach's role is in the INSPIRE study. Similarly, the translation of clinically relevant concepts were reviewed and adapted to ensure consistency across all intervention modules. For example, two such terms that needed to be manually updated included the acronym CBT to TCC (“Terapia Cognitiva Conductual”) and “Mindfulness” to “Atencion Plena”; as the translations would sometimes keep the original English-language term in the Spanish translation outputs.

Finally, to improve cultural relevance and fit of the intervention additional changes were implemented. Examples of physical movement, such as “zumba”, was included due to it being a popular form of movement within the community, as it not only provides great exercise but also provides a good opportunity for socialization with other participants. Content related to personal stories was modified to be reflective of the Latinx community. Audio and videos were narrated by members of the Spanish-speaking community in San Francisco and/or by AI cloned voices of study staff with accents from Mexico or Central American countries.

Chinese version (Cantonese-language)

As with the Latinx version, the Chinese version required both linguistic and cultural adaptations. Cantonese (most common in the San Francisco Bay Area) is a spoken language distinct from Mandarin. In most formal writing, Cantonese speakers still use Standard Written Chinese, which is based on Mandarin grammar and vocabulary, even though it is read aloud in Cantonese. Research staff and a consultant involved in this linguistic and cultural adaptation included team members from Southern China and Hong Kong who are native Cantonese speakers with expertise in linguistics and clinical psychology.

Surface cultural adaptation involved translating the English content to traditional Chinese and making sure the language used is understandable, easy to read, and meaningful to the Cantonese speaking participants from various regions of origin and educational backgrounds. Since the project introduces concepts, skills and tools which are expected to be relatively new to the study participants, there are a significant number of vocabularies and phrases that are not commonly used daily for the study participants. Therefore, translation consistency was important to improve understanding. A translation glossary was made to record the proper translations of the terminologies used and served as a reference for all translations in the app. English idioms were reviewed and translated based on group consensus. For example, in the week 1 module, participants were encouraged by this sentence “Let's roll up our sleeves and dive into the Week 1 lesson”. However, a direct translation into Chinese fails to convey the meaning of the metaphor “roll up our sleeves.” To avoid creating a feeling of culturally inappropriateness, the translation was modified to become “Let's prepare ourselves and dive into the Week 1 lesson”. This translation allowed participants to read smoothly and feel included. Other examples include changing a patient's name from “Benjamin” to “Mr. Li”, which is a more polite way to address someone in Cantonese culture, and “Li” is a common last name among Cantonese speakers.

Text content was further developed into audios and videos with simple animations to increase cultural fit and acceptability. Audios were recorded by native Cantonese speakers, and the wordings were slightly modified to sound more natural in a spoken setting because Cantonese is a spoken language. AI-cloned voices speaking Cantonese were poor quality and were not used. Most videos featured an animated depiction of a healthcare provider with Chinese facial features (Fig. 1).

Field testing and further revisions

As noted in Section 2.5 above, N = 24 participants (n = 12 English, n = 6 Spanish, n = 6 Cantonese) enrolled over a four-week period into a single arm field test of the INSPIRE intervention. Of these, n = 6 participants withdrew from the study after enrollment due to time commitment (n = 3), health issues (n = 1), or miscellaneous or unspecified reasons (n = 2). Of the n = 18 participants remaining in the study, 94.4% completed over 75% of modules and 83.3% completed over 75% of coaching visits.

Coaches recorded spontaneous and elicited feedback regarding the app, translations, and cultural adaptations. Feedback was overwhelmingly positive across language groups but there continued to be disagreement regarding nuance, appearance, and language used within the app.

After the field test, the original Multicultural English version was improved by removing redundancy, adding new content and/or concentrating the existing modules based on the feedback from the participants and additional reviews from the content developers. Additional cultural adaptation was implemented after the multicultural English version was finalized at times adding “personas” to the GenAI prompts. For example, the Chinese research team instructed ChatGPT to “translate the [attached file] from English to Traditional Chinese for a grandmother who is 65yo, American Chinese and Cantonese-speaking female, a caregiver for her grandchildren, has chronic joint pain in her knees and back affecting her ability to care for her grandchildren and engage in self-care. The pain leads to difficulty sleeping and low energy levels. Her goals are to manage pain to increase energy and motivation, balance caregiving with self-care, and improve sleep quality. She has limited prior experience with chronic pain management, and she only has basic technology skills such as using a smartphone only for communication. She is motivated by responsibility as a caregiver and her love for her grandchildren.” After setting the persona, ChatGPT was instructed to re-translate the revised content from English to Chinese optimizing phrasing and examples that might appeal to this persona.

Online video resources incorporated into the intervention were replaced with equivalent Spanish, Chinese, or Black/African-American videos available online. The adaptation teams independently searched for appropriate videos corresponding to the same topics. These videos included breathing exercises, audio-guided meditation practices, stretching exercises, yoga, Tai-Chi and others. The adaptation team then reviewed the potential videos and selected those to be used in the intervention based on their length, production quality, clarity, and public accessibility. When no suitable replacement was available, English videos that could be automatically translated to Spanish or Chinese captions or those containing minimal text were used instead.

Discussion

The linguistic and cultural adaptation of the INSPIRE intervention successfully integrated user and stakeholder feedback, expert opinion, and generative AI to more efficiently and effectively create acceptable digital health intervention content for diverse target populations. Content was created in 3 languages with special attention to general literacy, health literacy, and digital/tech literacy. Based on participant feedback, the adaptation process resulted in 4 versions of the app: Multicultural (English), Black/African American (English), Chinese (Cantonese), and Latinx (Spanish). The integration of linguistically and culturally concordant health coaches created a deeper connection with the intervention content and provided a necessary boost to module engagement.

The majority of the cultural tailoring conducted for the mobile app were surface level adaptations (Barrera Jr et al., 2013; Resnicow et al., 1999), including the translation of content into Spanish and Cantonese and review of language that might be confusing, irrelevant, or offensive to the target population. Across all three tailored versions of the intervention, audio and visual representation of content was generated with the target population in mind including changing characters' appearances, names, and voices to help improve acceptability and uptake of digital health tools (Eustis et al., 2023; Ellis et al., 2022). The 4th multicultural version represented a blend of images and visuals from the tailored app version with the core content remaining in English.

To maintain the fidelity of the INSPIRE intervention across all four versions developed, deep or structural level adaptations were used sparingly and carefully selected. For example, a request from the Spanish group to have only 6 modules could not be fulfilled although content in the 12 modules was greatly reduced and simplified. In the Black/African American version of the app, historical references and unique stressors (e.g. structural and historic anti-Black racism) were taken into account as part of the trauma and pain experienced by many Black participants. In the Chinese version of the app the professional background of characters and a nuanced cultural understanding of the causes and treatments for CP were taken into account to improve the fit of the intervention to this community. And in the Spanish language version of the app, decisions regarding formal “Usted” vs informal “Tu” in the tone and presentation of the materials were guided by a genuine understanding of the target audience's communication style and what might evoke a sense of comfort and belonging.

Use of generative AI

The use of generative AI for linguistic and cultural adaptation yielded substantial time and resource savings but has important limitations. First, the use of ChatGPT, Google Translate and DeepL for the first iteration of translations expedited the team's ability to quickly translate content while taking into account literacy levels and even sub-dialects or accents. Recent studies have published on the benefits of using software in the translation of intervention content, indicating that AI-translations perform well for Spanish but worse for Chinese (Martos et al., 2025). This is consistent with our findings as none of the direct translations were perfect, and they needed human reviewers with relevant cultural and linguistic expertise to refine and approve. The two most common errors were inconsistency in terms and tone. To address these concerns, we observed that providing clear prompt parameters, target personas, and reference materials improved the quality of translations. As GenAI rapidly improves and “learns,” the quality and subtlety of translations may similarly improve.

GenAI was also beneficial for identifying opportunities for deep level cultural tailoring. Less has been documented about the use of GenAI tools for this purpose despite the potential for these tools to help increase personalization of interventions to diverse communities (Maddox et al., 2025). However, given the documented “hallucinations” and reflection of broader societal biases, there is a critical need for bilingual and bicultural members of the target community to review the output of these tools to ensure accuracy, cultural appropriateness, and lack of bias.

Limitations

Due to the rapid pace of iteration in this intervention development project, there were limited opportunities to conduct in-depth comparisons and evaluations of the culturally tailored versions of the mobile app by the target community. Nonetheless, the formative and field testing stages of the study (see 3.1, 3.3) included target community members who provided critical feedback about the respective versions. Further, the cultural adaptation/tailoring protocol provided a foundation and guide for each team to adapt their respective version of the app while maintaining the core of the intervention. Due to time constraints, the team did not systematically collect the full number and types of adaptations or corrections performed at each step of the process nor the amount of time the adaptations ultimately took.

The INSPIRE team did not originally envision a “multicultural” version of the app that could be seen as centering white English-speakers. However, some minority group members expressed a strong interest in non-tailored content. Although intergroup variability was often clear between our target communities, there was substantial intragroup differences that may have been overlooked with INSPIRE's focus on cultural tailoring. In today's pluralistic and multicultural society, linguistic and cultural tailoring is essential to improve acceptability and engagement but should perhaps also include a “multicultural” option for those who prefer it.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the linguistic and cultural adaptations of the INSPIRE intervention demonstrate that a systematic, cross-cultural process can be used to guide changes across language and cultural groups without losing intervention fidelity. The blending of traditional methods such as focus groups with GenAI greatly saved time and resources allowing more target groups to be included. While the efficacy of the INSPIRE app has not yet been determined, the use of a mobile app will most likely improve reach while linguistically and culturally concordant coaches and app content may improve acceptability and engagement.
