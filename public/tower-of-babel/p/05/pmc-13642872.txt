Survival outcomes of conversion curative therapy following atezolizumab–bevacizumab in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ying-Hsin Chen, Wei-Feng Li, Yen-Hao Chen, Ming-Chao Tsai, Yuan-Hung Kuo, Jing-Houng Wang, Chien-Hung Chen, Sheng-Nan Lu, Chih-Chi Wang, Chao-Hung Hung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic value of curative therapies—including liver transplantation, hepatectomy, and local ablation—in patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab treatment remains unclear. This study evaluated the feasibility of curative therapy and compared survival outcomes between patients who did and did not undergo curative interventions following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, using a propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 318 consecutive patients with advanced HCC who received atezolizumab plus bevacizumab between 2020 and 2024. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were defined as the primary and secondary endpoints, respectively. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and evaluated with multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression, modeling exposure to conversion curative therapy as a time-dependent covariate.ResultsThe objective response rate to atezolizumab plus bevacizumab was 34.6%. Thirty-six patients (11%) subsequently underwent curative therapy, including liver transplantation (n = 8, 2.5%), hepatectomy (n = 11, 3.5%), and local ablation (n = 17, 5.3%). Patients receiving curative therapy demonstrated significantly improved OS (p < 0.001) and PFS (p < 0.001) compared with those who did not, with benefits persisting after PSM analysis. Multivariate Cox regression identified curative therapy as an independent predictor of OS (hazard ratio [HR] 0.398; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.179–0.885; p = 0.024).ConclusionsIn selected patients with advanced HCC who achieved disease control after atezolizumab–bevacizumab, conversion curative therapy was associated with favorable survival outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04476-y.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, Curative therapy, Overall survival, Progression-free survival

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Curative treatments, including liver transplantation, surgical resection, and local ablation, offer the best chance for long-term survival in patients with HCC, and advances in these surgical and locoregional therapies have further improved outcomes in patients with early—and intermediate-stage disease [2]. For patients with advanced HCC, systemic therapy has become the main treatment option [3–5]. Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitor-based systemic therapies have significantly improved treatment outcomes in advanced HCC [6, 7]. In particular, the combination of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab has become one of the standard first-line therapies for patients with unresectable or advanced HCC, demonstrating superior survival compared with sorafenib (6.7).

With the improved response rates observed with modern immunotherapy, some patients with unresectable or advanced HCC may achieve sufficient tumor regression to allow subsequent curative treatments, a strategy referred to as conversion curative therapy. The concept of “ABC conversion” has been proposed, particularly in patients with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE)-unsuitable intermediate-stage HCC, underscoring the potential of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab to facilitate curative interventions [8, 9]. In a multicenter study of unresectable, TACE-unsuitable intermediate-stage HCC, curative conversion following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab was achieved in approximately 30% of patients, highlighting the feasibility of systemic therapy as a bridge to definitive local treatment [8, 9].

Until now, the survival benefit of conversion curative therapies—including liver transplantation, hepatectomy, and local ablation—following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab therapy in patients with advanced HCC remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of downstaging to curative therapy after atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and to compare survival outcomes between patients who did and did not undergo curative treatment, using a propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study consecutively enrolled patients with advanced HCC who received atezolizumab plus bevacizumab therapy at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. HCC was diagnosed according to international guidelines, based on either histological confirmation or characteristic radiological findings on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [4, 5]. Patients were excluded if they lacked a first radiological evaluation after fewer than three cycles of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab or if they subsequently received non-curative-intent treatments.

Conversion curative therapy was defined as curative-intent intervention performed after atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, including liver transplantation, hepatectomy, or local ablation (radiofrequency or microwave ablation), following tumor control or downstaging that rendered curative treatment feasible. Patients who did not undergo these interventions were classified as the non-curative group. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (IRB No.: 202600437B0), with informed consent waived due to the retrospective design.

Treatment and response assessment

All patients received atezolizumab plus bevacizumab according to institutional protocols. Tumor response was assessed by contrast-enhanced CT or MRI and evaluated using the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) version 1.1 [10]. Best response was categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Objective response (OR) was defined as CR or PR. Tumor burden was calculated as the sum of maximum tumor diameter plus number of lesions, with high burden defined as ≥ 12, in accordance with a previous study [11].

Selection for conversion curative therapy

Conversion curative therapy was not guided by a fixed protocol but determined through multidisciplinary evaluation following response assessment every 12 weeks during atezolizumab plus bevacizumab treatment. Patients achieving CR, PR, or durable SD were considered for curative-intent intervention. Eligibility was assessed based on radiologic response, residual intrahepatic tumor burden, liver function reserve, performance status, technical feasibility, vascular invasion status, extrahepatic disease status, transplant candidacy, donor availability, and patient preference [4].

Hepatectomy was performed when complete macroscopic tumor removal was feasible with an adequate future liver remnant and acceptable hepatic reserve. Local ablation was applied to limited residual intrahepatic viable disease when complete ablation was technically achievable. Liver transplantation was undertaken in selected patients after tumor control or downstaging, considering liver function, tumor burden, transplant eligibility, and donor availability.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from initiation of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab to death from any cause or last follow-up. The secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from treatment initiation to radiological progression or death, whichever occurred first. Patients without events were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Propensity score matching

To reduce baseline imbalances between the curative and non-curative groups, a 1:4 PSM cohort was generated using the Greedy method with a 0.2 caliper width in NCSS 12 software (NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA, ncss.com/software/ncss.) to evaluate outcome effects. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model incorporating the following covariates: age, gender, tumor burden, best treatment response, macrovascular thrombosis (MVT), and extrahepatic metastasis. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.2 indicating acceptable balance.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on sample size. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. We conducted univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses incorporating both time-fixed and time-dependent covariates, with exposure to conversion curative therapy modeled as a time-dependent variable. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in stepwise variable selection. To handle missing data, a complete-case analysis was performed, excluding participants with missing values from the final multivariate models. All of these analyses were carried out using SAS statistical software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered to be significant. For propensity score diagnostics, balance diagnostics and overlap assessment were carried out using R software (version 4.2.2) cobalt package.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 343 patients with advanced HCC who received atezolizumab plus bevacizumab therapy were screened. Twenty-one patients without a first radiological evaluation after fewer than three treatment cycles and four patients who subsequently received non-curative-intent therapies were excluded. The remaining 318 patients were included in the final analysis. Of these, 36 patients underwent conversion curative therapy during follow-up, while 282 did not. PSM analysis at a 1:4 ratio yielded 36 patients in the curative group and 144 matched patients in the non-curative group (Fig. 1).

After PSM analysis, baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups, with all standardized mean differences < 0.2, indicating adequate covariate balance. The median number of atezolizumab–bevacizumab cycles before conversion curative therapy was 10 (range, 3–36). The median interval from treatment initiation to conversion curative therapy was 13.3 ± 9.2 months. Nine patients received bridging therapies prior to conversion, including radiotherapy (n = 5) and TACE (n = 4). Post-conversion atezolizumab–bevacizumab was administered in only three patients.

Treatment response and conversion curative therapy

The overall OR rate to atezolizumab plus bevacizumab therapy was 34.6%, comprising CR in 7.2% and PR in 27.4% of patients. Among the 36 patients who underwent conversion curative therapy, eight received liver transplantation, 11 underwent hepatectomy, and 17 received local ablation. Of these patients, four achieved CR, 16 PR, and 16 SD. Four patients achieved CR prior to curative intervention, subsequently receiving liver transplantation (n = 1), hepatectomy (n = 2), or local ablation (n = 1).

The median time from the final immunotherapy dose to liver transplantation was 5.6 months (range, 3.0–25.3). All patients were maintained on standard immunosuppressive regimens, including tacrolimus, sirolimus, and/or mycophenolate mofetil. Two patients developed allograft rejection, which was clinically manageable. One patient experienced early mortality within 1 month due to severe biliary leakage, while another died 1.5 years later from pancreatic cancer. Importantly, none of the patients developed tumor recurrence during follow-up.

Survival outcomes before and after propensity score matching

Overall survival

In the overall cohort, patients who underwent conversion curative therapy demonstrated significantly better OS compared with those who did not (p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). After PSM analysis, the survival advantage remained consistent, with significantly superior OS observed in the curative group compared with the matched non-curative group (p = 0.002; Fig. 3B). Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that conversion curative therapy was independently associated with better OS before PSM (hazard ratio (HR) 0.398, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.179–0.885; p = 0.024) (Table 2). Other independent prognostic factors included OR, tumor burden ≥ 12, albumin-bilirubin grade (ALBI) grade II/III, alpha-fetoprotein ≥ 400 ng/mL, Child–Pugh class A, and MVT (Table 2). Progression-free survival

In the overall cohort, patients who underwent conversion curative therapy had significantly longer PFS compared with those who did not (p < 0.001; Fig. 4A). After PSM analysis, the PFS benefit remained significant, with patients in the curative group continuing to demonstrate significantly prolonged PFS compared with matched patients in the non-curative group (p < 0.001; Fig. 4B). On multivariate Cox regression analysis before PSM, conversion curative therapy was independently associated with better PFS (HR 0.251, 95% CI 0.108–0.586; p = 0.001) (Table 3). After PSM, conversion curative therapy remained an independent predictor of longer PFS (HR 0.264, 95% CI 0.112–0.624; p = 0.002), while OR was also identified as an independent prognostic factor (supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In this real-world cohort of patients with advanced HCC treated with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, conversion to curative-intent therapy was feasible in approximately 11% of patients and was associated with favorable OS and PFS outcomes. Notably, the association between conversion curative therapy and favorable survival outcomes persisted after PSM analyses and multivariable adjustment, suggesting the consistency of the observed association. These findings support the potential feasibility of using effective systemic therapy as a bridge to curative-intent interventions in carefully selected patients with advanced HCC.

Our results align with prior real-world observations reporting improved survival among patients undergoing curative treatment following immunotherapy [12]. In a nationwide registry-based study using the U.S. National Cancer Database, which included more than 4000 patients with HCC treated with first-line immunotherapy between 2017 and 2020, approximately 3% subsequently underwent curative therapy and demonstrated significantly longer survival compared with those receiving systemic therapy alone [12]. In contrast, our cohort demonstrated a substantially higher conversion-to-curative rate of 11.3%. This difference may be attributable to several factors. First, treatment strategies for HCC vary across geographic regions. In many Asian centers, a more aggressive multidisciplinary approach is adopted, with surgical resection or other curative interventions considered whenever technically feasible, even in selected patients with intermediate- or advanced-stage disease [13–16]. Compared with Western treatment algorithms, several Asian clinical practice guidelines permit broader indications for surgical resection, including selected patients with multifocal disease or even portal vein invasion when adequate liver function is preserved [14–21]. Second, real-world studies from Asian centers have reported favorable tumor responses to atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, potentially increasing the proportion of patients eligible for curative therapy [22–24]. Third, our study also included selected patients with SD who subsequently underwent curative-intent interventions, which may have contributed to the higher observed conversion rate.

Importantly, curative therapy in our study was not restricted to patients achieving CR. Among the 36 patients in the curative group, only four achieved CR following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab therapy, indicating that most patients who underwent curative treatment might have residual but potentially controllable diseases. In clinical practice, candidacy for curative intervention should not be determined solely by radiologic response category. For example, some patients initially considered borderline resectable became technically resectable after systemic therapy despite achieving only SD. Moreover, in selected patients with portal vein tumor thrombosis or limited extrahepatic metastasis, local interventions targeting intrahepatic tumor burden—such as hepatectomy or local ablation—may be associated with favorable intrahepatic disease control. This concept is particularly relevant in HCC, where intrahepatic progression remains a major determinant of survival, even in the presence of extrahepatic disease [25–27].

In subgroup analyses comparing survival outcomes among patients who underwent liver transplantation, hepatectomy, or local ablation, no statistically significant differences were observed. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively small sample size within each subgroup may have limited statistical power to detect differences across treatment strategies. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent benefit across these modalities. Further investigation of modality-specific effects in larger, prospective cohorts is required. Particular attention is warranted for liver transplantation following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab therapy. Prior studies have suggested that transplantation after immune checkpoint inhibitor exposure may be feasible but could carry increased risks of postoperative complications, including graft rejection and early mortality [28–30]. In our cohort, two patients who underwent liver transplantation eventually died during follow-up: One experienced early mortality within 1 month due to post-transplant complications, while the other died 1.5 years later from pancreatic cancer. These observations underscore the importance of careful patient selection and vigilant perioperative management when considering liver transplantation after immunotherapy in advanced HCC.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces the possibility of residual confounding despite the use of PSM and multivariate analyses. In addition, PFS should be interpreted with caution, particularly in liver transplant recipients, as it does not account for competing risks such as post-surgical complications, hepatic dysfunction, or secondary malignancies. Accordingly, OS was designated as the primary endpoint, with PFS reported as a supportive exploratory outcome. Second, treatment decisions regarding curative therapy were based on multidisciplinary judgment and patient-specific clinical factors, some of which may not have been fully captured in the available dataset [31]. Third, the conversion group encompassed heterogeneous treatment modalities, including liver transplantation, hepatectomy, and local ablation, which may exert different effects on long-term outcomes [28–32]. Moreover, despite these limitations, this study provides important real-world evidence supporting the role of conversion to curative therapy following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in advanced HCC. The consistent survival benefit observed across multiple analytical approaches underscores the importance of multidisciplinary strategies that integrate systemic and curative treatments in carefully selected patients.

In conclusion, conversion curative therapy following atezolizumab plus bevacizumab treatment is feasible in selected patients with advanced HCC and may be associated with favorable survival outcomes. These findings suggest that, in the era of immunotherapy, curative strategies can be integrated into the treatment continuum for appropriately selected patients with advanced diseases. Prospective studies with standardized selection criteria and appropriate time-dependent analyses are warranted to clarify the causal impact and optimal timing of conversion curative therapy.
