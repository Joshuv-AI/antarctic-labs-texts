WIC Participant and Local Agency Staff Perspectives on Increased Cash‐Value Benefits for Fruits and Vegetables Within an American Indian Tribal Organization
Authors: Emily M. Melnick, Francesco Acciai, Nicole Vaudrin O'Reilly, Ana Bea Ronan, Mindy Jossefides, Shreya Raval, Tatum Dykstra, Punam Ohri‐Vachaspati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn 2021, participants in the United States Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) received an unprecedented increase in a cash‐value benefit for fruits and vegetables (CVB) as a part of their monthly food package. Responses to this increase among WIC staff and American Indian populations are poorly understood. The objective of this study was to assess perceptions and diet‐related behaviors of both WIC staff and participants within an Indian Tribal Organization state agency following the CVB increase. To accomplish these objectives, we conducted interviews with WIC local agency staff members (n = 15) and distributed a survey to WIC participants (n = 1,587) at the Inter Tribal Council of Arizona WIC. Thematic analysis of interviews showed that the CVB increase enabled WIC staff to provide more actionable nutrition education regarding fruits and vegetables and that staff and participants highly valued higher CVB amounts. Participant survey results showed improved fruit and vegetable consumption and high satisfaction following the increase, especially among households receiving larger amounts of CVBs. Interview and survey findings both suggested that limited access to fresh produce on rural tribal lands may limit benefits of the CVB increase. Taken together, findings indicate that both WIC staff and participants have very positive perceptions of the CVB increase. However, systemic barriers to redemption on rural tribal lands highlight the need for additional strategies to improve benefit use.

Introduction

The Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) provides critical nutrition support to participating pregnant and postpartum mothers, infants, and children under 5 years of age in the United States (US). WIC benefits include monthly healthy food packages tailored to the nutritional needs of each participant category, along with nutrition education, breastfeeding support, and referrals to health and social services (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service, n.d.). A key feature of the WIC food package for child, pregnant, and postpartum participants is the Cash Value Benefit (CVB), which allows for the purchase of fruits and vegetables.

CVBs were first introduced in 2009 to better align the WIC food package with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, with most participants receiving monthly benefit amounts ranging from $6 to $11 depending on their participant category (Oliveira and Frazao 2015). In March 2021, in response to COVID‐19 pandemic‐related food insecurity, the American Rescue Plan Act increased the monthly CVB amount to $35 for pregnant, postpartum, and child participants (American Rescue Plan of 2021, House Resolution 1319, 117th Congress 2021). This increase was extended by the Extending Government Funding and Delivering Emergency Assistance Act in September 2021, which set adjusted CVB amounts at $24 for children, $43 for pregnant and postpartum participants, and $47 for breastfeeding participants (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service 2021). Following further extensions with minor inflation adjustments to current levels between $26‐52 depending on participant category, the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Food and Nutrition Service issued a final rule making these increased CVB amounts a permanent feature of the WIC food package (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service 2024).

A growing body of literature highlights the positive impacts of the CVB increase, including higher redemption of fruits and vegetables, increased fruit and vegetable consumption, greater dietary diversity, and improved participant satisfaction with the WIC program (Anderson et al. 2023; Duffy et al. 2022, 2024; Gago et al. 2022; Halverson and Karpyn 2022; Martinez et al. 2022; Nitto et al. 2024; Tsai et al. 2022; Whaley et al. 2012). However, little is known about the experiences of American Indian WIC participants following the CVB increase. This is a critical gap because American Indians experience significant barriers to accessing healthy foods due to inequitable food systems (Berg et al. 2012; O'Connell et al. 2011) as well as disproportionately high rates of diet‐related chronic diseases (Dai et al. 2023; Hutchinson and Shin 2014). These challenges are especially severe on tribal lands, where access to fresh and affordable fruits and vegetables is consistently lower than in other parts of the U.S., reflecting systemic inequities rooted in settler colonialism and structural racism within food systems (Estradé et al. 2021; Fretts et al. 2018; Jernigan et al. 2017).

Additionally, it remains unclear how the CVB increase relates to WIC staff's delivery of nutrition education to participants. To address these gaps, we explored perceptions about the increase from multiple stakeholders within an Indian Tribal Organization WIC state agency. Specifically, we assessed (1) WIC staffs' perceptions of increased CVBs related to delivery of nutrition education and participants' ability to act on this guidance using in‐depth individual interviews, and (2) WIC participants' satisfaction with the fruit and vegetable benefits and any perceived changes in consumption following the CVB increase using a survey tool. Because prior literature suggests that living in more remote areas may present barriers to redeeming CVBs, we examined how participant satisfaction and reported changes in intake varied by urbanicity. We also examined how satisfaction and reported changes in intake varied by household composition of WIC participants, since different categories of participants receive different CVB amounts. This study offers insights into the implications of CVB changes for enhancing the effectiveness of the WIC program in supporting American Indian populations by exploring perceptions of the increase among stakeholders underrepresented in the current literature.

Methods

Study Design

This study gathered perspectives on the CVB increase from both local agency WIC employees and participants served by the Inter Tribal Council of Arizona, Inc. (ITCA) WIC. ITCA is a non‐profit organization with membership from 21 tribal nations in Arizona. ITCA operates as a WIC state agency, managing a monthly caseload of approximately 7,000 participants, and overseeing 12 local agencies in program operations. Of these agencies, 11 provide services through clinics and satellite locations on tribal lands. We conducted semi‐structured interviews with ITCA WIC local agency staff between September and October 2023 to assess their perceptions of the CVB increase. ITCA WIC conducts frequent surveys to obtain client perspectives and satisfaction on delivery of services. To gather client perspectives on changes to the CVB benefits, ITCA WIC distributed an online survey via text message to all active households who provided a valid phone number capable of receiving texts between January and February 2024. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Arizona State University Institutional Review Board, with an expedited review. All approved procedures were in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. Oral consent was obtained from interviewees after a description of the study was provided to them both orally and in writing, and before any questions were asked.

Semi‐Structured Staff Interviews

Participants

The sampling frame for in‐depth interviews included all staff members employed by local agencies under the ITCA WIC state agency who deliver nutrition education to WIC participants. Eligible staff (n = 27) were contacted via email to invite them to participate in an interview. A total of 15 staff members from 8 out of 12 local agencies agreed to participate in the interviews. Staff members who participated in interviews reflected local agencies within urban areas (n = 4) and rural areas (n = 11).

Instrumentation

The semi‐structured interview guide included questions designed to explore the impact of the CVB increase on staff's delivery of fruit and vegetable‐related nutrition education, their perception of participants' willingness and ability to make changes to their fruit and vegetable intake, and any desired changes to the nutrition education process. In total, six questions about perceptions of increased CVBs were asked. An interview guide is included in Supporting Information.

Procedures

Interviews were conducted using Zoom videoconferencing software. The lead author (EM) conducted all interviews, while another research team member took contemporaneous notes. Each interview was recorded, digitally transcribed, and subsequently reviewed and cleaned by a research team member to ensure accuracy. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min, and participants received a $50 gift card as compensation for their time.

Data Analysis

Thematic analyses of interviews were conducted using an iterative constant comparative methodology by three research team members trained in qualitative methods. The lead author (EM) developed an initial codebook reflecting the domains explored during the interviews. The research team (EM, SR, TD) independently coded all transcripts, identifying comments related to the predefined domains. Each transcript was coded by two team members to ensure accuracy. After independent coding, the team met to resolve anydiscrepancies, discuss potential new codes needed to capture points not captured by predefined domains, and finalize the codebook with names and definitions for agreed‐upon codes. After all coding was completed, the team met to identify and define final themes based on applied codes. We used Dedoose qualitative analysis software for all qualitative analyses (SocioCultural Research Consultants LLC, Manhattan Beach, CA).

WIC Participant Survey

Participants

The sampling frame for the survey distributed by ITCA WIC included all currently active ITCA WIC households. A total of 1708 participants returned the survey to ITCA, which were shared with the research team in a deidentified format. Surveys with over 50% missing values were removed (n = 121) by the research team, resulting in an analytical sample of 1587 ITCA WIC participants. This analytical sample represented about 35% of ITCA WIC households (1587 households out of the 4157 active households at the time the survey was distributed).

Instrumentation

For the current project, 12 survey items were used to evaluate participant perceptions of increased CVBs in WIC food packages and perceived impacts of the increase on their diet. These items, informed by prior research on participant perceptions of the CVB increase in other states (Duffy et al. 2022; Gago et al. 2022; Halverson and Karpyn 2022; Martinez et al. 2022), focused on various aspects related to the CVB increase, including satisfaction with household procurement of fruits and vegetables, changes in consumption, access to fresh produce in their community, and typical CVB redemption patterns. Adaptations of questions from previous research were reviewed by ITCA WIC leadership to ensure appropriateness for ITCA WIC participants. The ITCA WIC client satisfaction survey also included demographic information questions, such as gender identity, level of education, race, ethnicity, preferred language, and the respondents' local WIC agency.

Procedures

The survey was distributed by ITCA WIC study partners. Active households who provided a cell phone number capable of receiving texts were sent a link to the online survey via text message. The survey was administered through SurveyMonkey (SurveyMonkey Inc., San Mateo, CA) and was open for completion over a 6‐week period between January and February 2024. Upon completing the survey, respondents had the option to provide their email address for entry into a raffle to win one of 12 $25 gift cards. To ensure participant confidentiality, ITCA WIC staff deidentified all survey data before sharing with the study team, by removing all personal, household, and local agency identifiers. A binary variable for local agency urbanicity (urban vs. rural) was provided by ITCA WIC.

Data Analysis

Quantitative analyses, specifically descriptive statistics, were run using Stata software version 16 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Survey item response categories were collapsed based on the variable distribution to facilitate the analysis and the interpretation. For instance, questions regarding satisfaction with the dollar amount for FV purchases were condensed from five response options (Very unsatisfied, Unsatisfied, Neither satisfied nor unsatisfied, Satisfied, Very satisfied) into two: (1) “Satisfied/Very satisfied” (recoded from Satisfied and Very satisfied options) versus (2) “Others” (recoded from Very unsatisfied, Unsatisfied, and Neither satisfied nor unsatisfied options). This decision was made because 83%–91% of respondents reported high or very high satisfaction across survey questions, resulting in limited variability across the other three categories. Similarly, questions about FV consumption and ability to purchase FV in their community were condensed from five response options (Strongly disagree, Disagree, Neither agree nor disagree, Agree, Strongly agree) into two: (1) “Agree/Strongly agree” (recoded from Agree and Strongly agree options) versus (2) “Others” (recoded from Strongly disagree, Disagree, and Neither agree nor disagree options). This recoding was also based on response distribution, with 78%–84% respondents indicating agreement or strong agreement. Collapsing categories in this manner allowed for more stable estimates and clearer interpretation of results given the highly skewed distributions. For the WIC benefit usage question, five response options were collapsed into three categories. The three smallest response options (None of it [0%], A little of it [about 25%], About half of it [about 50%]) were condensed into one category: “None to some of it [Up to 50%].” The remaining two responses were retained in their original form as categories: “Most of it [about 75%]”, and“All of it [100%].”

To evaluate differences in responses across various household types, we stratified the respondents into the following dichotomous groups: (1) urbanicity of the local agency where they receive WIC services (urban vs. rural); (2) number of WIC participants receiving benefits in the household (one participant vs. multiple participants); (3) presence of a pregnant, postpartum and/or breastfeeding participant in the household (households with vs. without a woman participant). These groupings were selected based on anticipated associations with CVB satisfaction and redemption patterns. Food environments on rural tribal lands, including limited access to affordable fruits and vegetables, present barriers to redeeming CVBs for families (Kaufman et al. 2014). Additionally, the amount of CVBs households receive may influence both satisfaction and redemption patterns. Households with multiple WIC participants typically receive a higher total CVB allocation than those with only one participant, and pregnant, postpartum and/or breastfeeding participants are allocated the highest CVB amount among all participant categories. We assessed the statistical significance of differences across these groups using Pearson's chi‐square tests, with two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Semi‐Structured Staff Interview Participants

Among the 15 local agency staff who participated in semi‐structured interviews, 4 were employed within urban local agencies, and the remaining were employed within rural local agencies. The positions varied and included local agency managers and staff that provide nutrition assessment and counseling. The mean numbers of years staff had worked with ITCA WIC was 15.8 years (SD = 9.9 years).

Theme #1: CVB Increase Helps WIC Staff Deliver Actionable Fruit and Vegetable‐Related Nutrition Education

Staff reported that the CVB increase improved their ability to provide actionable fruit and vegetable‐related nutrition education to WIC participants. They felt more comfortable discussing a variety of produce options and sharing recipes, knowing that participants had more purchasing power to acquire fruits and vegetables. For example, one staff member (R1) noted “[the CVB increase] makes the fruits and vegetables education much easier and more exciting.”

In addition, staff observed more participant engagement during nutrition education sessions following the CVB increase. One staff member (R14) shared, “I think they pick it up more when we talk about it, about the fruits and vegetables.” Another (R12) noted that participants were “looking for more ways to use the fruits and vegetables” and more frequently requested recipe ideas during appointments.

Staff also reported that with the enhanced CVB, participants could incorporate more fruits and vegetables into their family's diets. As one staff member (R8) explained, “I think they feel a little bit better that they, like, that they can use it. They feel it can be used more in recipes rather than just like a snack here and there.” Another staff member (R6) stated, “I think that difference has allowed them to purchase things that they normally wouldn't purchase.”

Theme #2: WIC Participants and Staff Highly Value Increase in CVBs

The second theme identified from the interviews indicates that both WIC participants and staff strongly value the increase in CVBs. Staff voiced strong support for maintaining or even increasing the CVB amount, emphasizing the positive impact on participants. One staff member (R4) described the increase in CVBs as “one of those perks of being on the program really and I just think it's so great,” while another (R7) advocated for “keeping it at the same amount or even if they increase it again. Because it does get more of our clients to get those benefits… to get the fruits and vegetables to try them and to try new things.”

Staff also highlighted that participants greatly appreciated the increased CVB amounts. One staff member (R10) noted, “I think it's good for them because now you hear when you talk to the client, you hear, you know, they tell you that they really appreciate their fruits and vegetables.”

Additionally, staff reported that participants with infants expressed interest in exchanging their jarred infant fruit and vegetable benefits for CVBs. At the time of the interviews, ITCA WIC participants with infants aged 9 months or older could opt to exchange half of their infant's jarred fruit and vegetable benefits for $4‐8 in CVBs, depending on the woman's breastfeeding status. Staff noted that participants wanted the option to exchange more of their jarred infant fruits and vegetables for CVBs, even when their infants were younger than 9 months. For example, one staff member (R15) shared “I do have families and moms that do say that they [would] rather buy the fruits and vegetables from the WIC package and that they were excited that they can be able to introduce now more veggies to the babies.”

Theme #3: Limited Access to Fruits and Vegetables May Limit CVB Redemption

The third theme highlights factors that may limit CVB redemption of fruits and vegetables. A major barrier identified by staff was a lack of accessible quality produce, particularly in rural and remote areas where fewer stores are located. One staff member (R12) noted that sometimes in the local grocery store “their vegetables get so bad they actually give them away for free after a certain amount of time.” Staff shared that transportation challenges also often drove participants to rely on smaller stores closer to their homes but carrying more limited fresh food options to redeem their CVBs.

Within this theme, staff also reported that the availability of frozen and canned options for CVBs helped participants living on tribal lands overcome these challenges. Staff pointed to the extended shelf life of frozen and canned options as particularly beneficial for participants living in remote areas who may purchase in bulk after traveling long distances to grocery stores. For example, one staff member (R9) shared “when they started with the frozen, that was really good, because they could choose a frozen option and that had a longer shelf life. But now that they can [also] purchase canned, I'm sure that they'll adapt to it very well.”

Theme #4: WIC Staff Expressed Interest in More Materials and Strategies to Promote Fruits and Vegetables to Participants

The fourth theme highlights WIC staff's desire to expand and improve nutrition education strategies for participants. For example, staff highlighted the importance of “more culturally relevant materials and handouts with more culturally relevant foods” (R3). They also identified the need for more easily accessible and culturally relevant recipes and resources for WIC participants.

Additionally, staff advocated for broadening the channels for sharing fruit and vegetable resources beyond formal nutrition education appointments. They suggested creating and sharing short educational videos and recipes via social media, as well as hosting food demonstrations in WIC clinics to engage participants more effectively and promote fruit and vegetable consumption. For instance, one staff member (R14) proposed developing short videos of “cooking and prepping stuff from like a chef or something” to be featured within the WIC Smart platform, which offers mobile‐based nutrition education to participants.

Most respondents reported that their family consumed more fruits (79.8% agreed or strongly agreed), more vegetables (78.4%), and a greater variety of fruits and vegetables (81.1%) than they did before the pandemic. Fewer respondents attending rural WIC local agencies agreed or strongly agreed with the statement that their family consumed more vegetables than before the pandemic when compared to respondents attending urban WIC local agencies (76.7% vs. 81.2%, p = 0.032). No other statistically significant differences between subgroups were detected.

A vast majority of respondents (84.4%) agreed that it was easy to purchase fruits and vegetables in their community. However, fewer respondents from rural WIC local agencies (81.0%) agreed with this statement compared to those from urban local agencies (88.1%, p < 0.001). Additionally, households without a woman participant reported slightly lower agreement compared to households with a woman participant (83.1% vs. 87.5%, p = 0.033).

When asked to describe their typical use of their WIC food package benefits each month, 6.8% of respondents reported using none or some (up to 50% of their benefits), 33.0% reported using most (about 75% of their benefits), and 60.2% reported using all (100% of their benefits). Regarding WIC CVBs, 6.2% reported using none or some, 11.7% reported using most, and 82.1% reported using all. Subgroup analysis showed that (1) households with multiple participants tended to redeem more of their CVBs compared to households with a single participant and (2) households from urban local agencies tended to redeem more of their WIC food package benefits compared to households from rural local agencies (see Table 3).

Discussion

This study assessed the perspective of WIC local agency staff and participants on the CVB increase within an Indian Tribal Organization WIC state agency. Overall, the quantitative and qualitative findings from WIC participants and staff, respectively, indicate positive perceptions of the increase. Staff reported that the increased CVBs enabled them to deliver more actionable nutrition education, and expressed a desire for more materials and strategies to promote fruits and vegetables. Correspondingly, participants expressed high satisfaction with the quantity, quality, and variety of fruits and vegetables they could purchase and indicated that they were consuming more fruits and vegetables following the increase. These complementary findings suggest that the CVB increase not only enhanced purchasing power but also reinforced participants' ability to act on WIC nutrition guidance. Households receiving higher CVB amounts, such as those with multiple participants or a woman participant, reported the highest satisfaction levels. However, both staff interviews and participant surveys indicated that geographic contexts may present challenges to redeeming their CVB benefits for participants living in more remote areas.

Staff emphasized that the CVB increase made nutrition education more effective by allowing participants to act on guidance. As healthy eating behaviors depend on having accessible and affordable food options, staff felt more comfortable encouraging fruit and vegetable intake knowing that participants had the resources to act on this guidance. Consistent with staff observations, most participants reported that their family consumed more fruits and vegetables following the increase. These themes and survey findings align with studies in other non‐tribal populations showing improved self‐reported fruit and vegetable purchasing behaviors following CVB increases (Gago et al. 2022; Martinez et al. 2022) and support a socio‐ecological perspective, which suggests that pairing nutrition education with improved access to fruits and vegetables fosters sustainable behavior changes (Story et al. 2008).

To maximize the influence of CVBs on participants' diets and health, additional support for WIC staff may be beneficial. Staff noted participants' heightened interest in recipes featuring fruits and vegetables following the increase, including those incorporating traditional Indigenous foods, and highlighted the potential value of recipes and resources focused on traditional Indigenous foods that are culturally relevant for the community they serve.

Staff also reported that participants with infants expressed interest in exchanging jarred infant fruits and vegetables for CVBs. At the time of the interviews, infants over 9 months old participating in WIC could receive no more than $4‐8 in CVBs to purchase fresh fruits and vegetables as a substitution for jarred infant fruits and vegetables. However, changes to the federal WIC food package policy since the time of the interviews allow for a more meaningful exchange that staff noted participants were eager to see (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service 2024). As of October 2024, ITCA WIC allows households with infants over 6 months old to exchange either (a) half of their jarred infant fruits and vegetables benefits for $11 in CVBs, or (b) all of their jarred benefits for $22 in CVBs. Further, the CVBs can now be redeemed in forms other than fresh (i.e., frozen and canned), reducing barriers by offering more flexible purchasing options.

Although satisfaction and reported behavior changes were generally very positive, notable differences emerged between groups. Households receiving higher CVB amounts reported greater satisfaction, higher CVB redemption rates, and better access to fresh produce, suggesting that higher CVB amounts further improved engagement outcomes. A study conducted in Southern California similarly found that participants appreciated the CVB increase for children but noted that higher amounts would better meet their dietary needs (Martinez et al. 2022).

Additionally, both staff interviews and participant surveys showed that geographic disparities influenced redemption. Staff shared that participants in rural areas or living on tribal lands faced limited availability of produce in stores and longer travel distances to redeem benefits. Survey results also showed that participants from rural WIC local agencies were less likely to report increased vegetable consumption following the CVB increase and were more likely to experience difficulty accessing produce in their communities. These findings are consistent with previous work identifying similar barriers in rural areas (Nitto et al. 2024) and highlight the importance of Tribal food sovereignty initiatives as well as other community‐based strategies to expand access to fresh produce on tribal lands (Indian Health Service 2025; Pindus and Hafford 2019; O'Connell et al. 2011). Interventions such as WIC‐based farmers' markets and mobile markets have proven effective elsewhere (Ball et al. 2019; Leone et al. 2018). WIC policies allowing CVB redemption of frozen and canned fruits and vegetables may also help families living in areas with limited access to fresh produce. It is important to note that the allowance of frozen and canned options varies by state agency. Our findings suggest that these options could benefit participants living in areas with limited access to fresh produce. Because our study was conducted very shortly after the ITCA WIC policy change allowing CVB redemption of canned foods, further follow‐up is warranted to assess whether this flexibility has enhanced redemption rates and participant satisfaction.

Another area to examine is potential influences of the CVB increase on program retention. While WIC participation is associated with improved diet and health outcomes in early childhood (Acciai et al. 2021; Lee and Mackey‐Bilaver 2007; Weinfield et al. 2020), retaining children in the program throughout their eligibility period (ages 0‐5) has proven challenging, particularly in American Indian populations (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service 2025). Notably, the transition from the infant to the child food package is a common dropout point for WIC participants (Lora et al. 2023; Whaley et al. 2017), making it essential to investigate how increasing the monetary value of the child food package through increased CVBs may improve retention throughout the child's eligibility period. Future research should systematically assess the impact of CVB increases on WIC retention using objective metrics.

Strengths and Limitations

This study should be interpreted in light of its strengths and limitations. Key strengths include an assessment of perspectives about the increase in CVBs from both local agency staff who implement the program and from participants who receive the benefits. Interviews with staff provided rich, in‐depth insights into their perceptions of the increase, while an electronic survey distributed to WIC participants allowed us to capture perspectives from a large and geographically diverse sample. Using these methods, we were able to gather insights on increased CVBs within an American Indian Tribal Organization that serves a majority of participants residing on tribal lands. The collaborative study team, which included ITCA WIC staff, helped amplify the experiences of a population that faces a disproportionately high burden of health challenges and is often underrepresented in peer‐reviewed literature.

Representativeness of the sample may be a limitation given that participants self‐selected to participate. Although a large number of ITCA WIC households responded to the survey, respondents may not be fully representative of the population served by ITCA WIC. For instance, those who completed the survey may be more satisfied with their WIC experience and more willing to share their experiences than non‐respondents. Comparing the demographic characteristics of survey respondents to the overall ITCA participant caseload revealed some differences. Specifically, participants from urban local agencies were overrepresented in the survey sample (55% of respondents) compared to the ITCA WIC caseload for the same month (37%). Since the survey was distributed via a texted link, the lower representation of participants from rural local agencies could be partly due to unreliable cell phone coverage on tribal lands (U.S. Department of the Interior, Indian Affairs n.d.). Additionally, the proportion of households with a woman present was also lower in the survey sample (28%) compared to the overall caseload (45%). Other unobserved differences may also exist. In addition, while WIC staff interviewees were briefed that all responses would be kept confidential, there remains the potential that social desirability biases influenced insights shared by staff. Lastly, we did not collect information on ITCA WIC staff and participants before the CVB increase, preventing us from determining how the increase impacted satisfaction levels or self‐reported redemption patterns. Future analyses using longitudinal administrative data will be needed to assess changes in CVB redemption rates among American Indian WIC participants following the increase. Future studies that utilize administrative data will also help to address sampling biases and offer further validity to results. Notably, because this study focused on an Indian Tribal Organization WIC state agency serving American Indian families in Arizona, the findings reflect unique contextual factors and may not be generalizable to other settings.

Conclusion

Findings suggest that the CVB increase has positively impacted both WIC staff and participants within an American Indian Tribal Organization WIC state agency. However, barriers to redemption persist, particularly related to location, and households receiving comparatively lower monthly CVB amounts report lower satisfaction levels. These insights can inform future interventions and policies aimed at maximizing the benefits of the CVB increase for WIC‐participating American Indian families. Based on the positive perceptions of the CVB increase and reported increases in fruit and vegetable intake following this change, the WIC food package adjustment appears to be a promising policy to support healthy diets in American Indian communities. Additionally, strategies to improve access to fruits and vegetables and increase flexibility in redemption options may further enhance the benefits of the CVB increase for participants on tribal lands.
