Buttressing ligand‐binding pockets in de novo designed NTF2‐like domains
Authors: Marta Nadal, Joana Albi‐Puig, Albert Castellví, Paula M Garcia‐Franco, Sonia Vega, Adrián Velázquez‐Campoy, Enrique Marcos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNTF2‐like proteins are compact α + β fold domains with cone‐shaped architectures and internal pockets, making them attractive scaffolds for the de novo design of small‐molecule binders and enzymes. However, creating ligand‐binding pockets often compromises folding stability, posing a key challenge in de novo protein design. Here, we introduce strategies to stabilize NTF2‐like domains while preserving pocket geometry and accessibility. By expanding the hydrophobic core through computationally designed α‐helical subdomains or homodimer interfaces buttressing the β‐sheet's convex face, we enhance structural stability without blocking pocket access on the concave face. Biochemical, biophysical, and crystallographic analyses confirm that the designed buttressing elements maintain the intended fold and support diverse, well‐formed hydrophobic ligand‐binding pockets with increased preorganization. Our results demonstrate that structural stabilization and pocket optimization need not be mutually exclusive, providing a generalizable approach to create robust ligand‐binding proteins. This framework addresses a major bottleneck in protein design and should fuel the development of NTF2‐based scaffolds for applications in small‐molecule biosensing and enzyme catalysis.Keywords: beta‐sheets, ligand‐binding, molecular dynamics simulations, NTF2‐like, protein design, protein stability, X‐ray crystallography

INTRODUCTION

The nuclear transport factor 2 (NTF2)‐like structural superfamily is a sequence‐diverse group of proteins sharing a highly versatile fold (Eberhardt et al., 2013), capable of performing a broad range of enzymatic and small‐molecule binding functions in nature, such as lipid trafficking (Wong et al., 2017), steroid biosynthesis (Kim et al., 1997), detoxification from oxidized molecules (Arand, 2003; Johansson et al., 2005) or lignin depolimerization (Kuatsjah et al., 2023), among others. Characterized by a distinctive cone‐like shape with an inner pocket, NTF2‐like protein domains have an α + β fold consisting of a six‐stranded curved β‐sheet (β1–β6), with its concave face topped by a lid of three α‐helices (α1–α3) (Figure 1a). The curvature of the β‐sheet combined with the arrangement of the α‐helices can create a diverse range of pockets, with their entrances positioned at the base of the cone (Figure 1b). The simplicity, small size, and adaptability of the NTF2‐like protein architecture make it particularly attractive for engineering novel small‐molecule binding proteins and enzymes. The first study showcasing the engineering potential of these scaffolds computationally redesigned the pocket of an existing NTF2‐like domain to bind digoxigenin (Tinberg et al., 2013), a cardiac disease drug, and the progesterone hormone, which led to biosensors for these molecules (Feng et al., 2015; Griss et al., 2014). However, naturally occurring NTF2 scaffolds with known structures may lack ideal geometries for specific ligands, and sequence modifications could unpredictably disrupt the pocket structure or folding stability.

From a de novo protein design perspective (Kortemme, 2024; Marcos & Silva, 2018), the ability to custom‐design NTF2‐like structures would offer a powerful means to optimize the shape complementarity and preorganization of ligand‐binding sites of interest. Toward this goal, we previously identified how backbone irregularities influence β‐sheet curvature and leveraged these principles to computationally design the first de novo NTF2‐like domains with high stability and structural accuracy (Marcos et al., 2017). Since then, NTF2‐like scaffolds have been extensively diversified through systematic exploration of global fold parameters (Basanta et al., 2020), sampling of loop–helix–loop geometries (Pan et al., 2020), and more recently, deep learning‐based approaches such as hallucination (Yeh et al., 2023) and diffusion (Watson et al., 2023) guided by structural specifications of this fold. Collectively, these efforts have generated far greater pocket diversity than naturally occurring scaffolds. This has allowed the de novo design of a small luciferase enzyme (Yeh et al., 2023) with desirable photophysical properties and novel small‐molecule binders for a range of drugs and hormones for biosensing applications (Lee et al., 2023).

Despite recent advances, the de novo design of ligand‐binding NTF2‐like domains remains a significant challenge, with success rates for achieving active binders typically below 1% (Lee et al., 2023)—this process often demands extensive experimental screening and optimization. One of the major hurdles in ligand‐binding protein design lies in achieving the ideal protein backbone geometry of the binding site while preserving folding stability—the inherent tradeoff between protein stability and function is a long‐standing problem in protein engineering (Goldenzweig & Fleishman, 2018; Tokuriki et al., 2008). Besides having the right geometry, an ideal ligand‐binding pocket must be solvent‐accessible and supported by a sufficiently large hydrophobic core (Dou et al., 2018). In ligand‐binding design, placing the ligand deep within the protein structure enhances sidechain contacts and shape complementarity with the ligand but often reduces the hydrophobic core, creating a tradeoff. Due to the small size of the NTF2 fold, the pocket and core are closely connected—the pocket transitions to the core from the base to the apex of the cone‐like NTF2 structure—meaning that optimizing one property can compromise the other. We have previously shown that the NTF2 fold can be stabilized by designing a long C‐terminal helix that partially covers the pocket entrance (Marcos et al., 2017), but this can limit access to ligands, again creating a stability‐function tradeoff. To overcome this, we sought an approach to stabilize the NTF2 protein fold without disrupting or blocking access to the binding pocket.

Conventional protein engineering methods have been effective in stabilizing naturally occurring enzymes through sequence changes optimizing hydrophobic core packing (Korkegian et al., 2005), surface electrostatic interactions (Gribenko et al., 2009) or structural rigidity (Wijma et al., 2014); often with minimal changes, if any, in the protein backbone. Generating such sequence modifications through atomic modeling guided by phylogenetic information has been particularly successful (Goldenzweig et al., 2016; Sumida et al., 2024). However, these strategies are less helpful for de novo proteins, which often lack sequence homologs and are already computationally designed with near‐optimal hydrophobic core packing, minimal buried polar groups, stabilizing surface charge interactions, high local sequence‐structure compatibility, and short, rigid loops not directly involved in function—as a result de novo proteins are typically thermostable (Baker, 2019). While disulfide bridges (Dombkowski et al., 2014) can also enhance stability by destabilizing the unfolded state, they are unsuitable in reducing environments, may result in protein aggregation or misfolding through unintended disulfide bonding, or could interfere with catalytic sites.

By leveraging the power of computational protein design methods to build novel structures and assemblies, here we develop strategies to stabilize de novo NTF2‐like domains while preserving pocket formation and accessibility. We show that expanding the hydrophobic core through the convex face of the β‐sheet—by introducing buttressing α‐helical subdomains or homodimer interfaces—enhances folding stability while maintaining scaffold geometry (Figure 1c). Biochemical and biophysical measurements, along with x‐ray crystallography, confirm these design principles. The engineered elements effectively counteract the stability loss from pocket‐creating mutations, resulting in highly stable, sequence‐diverse pockets capable of binding hydrophobic ligands. These findings provide a framework for designing NTF2‐like ligand‐binding proteins with enhanced stability and well‐defined binding pockets.

RESULTS

Computational design of buttressing elements

We envisioned that the stability of de novo NTF2‐like domains could be enhanced by expanding the hydrophobic core through the convex face of the β‐sheet, ensuring that the pocket on the concave face remains structurally intact and solvent‐accessible. We reasoned that this core expansion could be achieved in two ways (Figure 1c). First, by maintaining the monomeric state through the addition of α‐helices at the C‐terminus, allowing them to pack against the β‐sheet. The orientation of the last strand β6 naturally positions the C‐terminus so that a short loop can connect and stabilize an α‐helix against the convex face of the β‐sheet. Second, by designing homodimeric interfaces through face‐to‐face packing of the β‐sheets, as often seen in naturally occurring NTF2‐like proteins. To test these approaches, we began with the de novo NTF2‐like domain dcs_E_2 from our previous study (Marcos et al., 2017) as a model protein, hereafter dNTF0, which was designed for maximal stability and featured a cone base exposed to solvent. The crystal structure (PDB id: 5L33) confirmed that dNTF0 is well packed throughout, from the cone base to the apex. Efficient packing near the more open cone base required several aromatic residues to fill the large space, making it an ideal starting point for designing accessible pockets. Circular dichroism (CD) experiments indicated that dNTF0 is thermostable, remaining folded at 95°C, and chemical denaturation with guanidine hydrochloride (GdnHCl) revealed an unfolding transition midpoint (C m) of 2.9 M.

For designing C‐terminal α‐helical subdomains comprising one or two α‐helices, we generated protein backbones by Rosetta (Leman et al., 2020) Monte Carlo fragment assembly using blueprints (Koga et al., 2012) appending α‐helical secondary structures to the C‐terminal β‐strand of dNTF0 and connected by short loop connections (Methods). We explored combinations of helix length (10–18 residues), βα loop length (1–5 residues) and, for two‐helix designs, αα loop lengths (2–4 residues). Short C‐terminal β‐strand extensions (of 1–2 residues) were also allowed to enable sampling of both parallel and antiparallel orientations of the βα motif, according to the βα rule (Koga et al., 2012). The last position of the generated loops was biased toward backbone structures favoring N‐terminal hydrogen bond capping with the following α‐helix (Methods). Protein backbones generated for one‐ and two‐helix subdomains correctly packing against the convex face of the β‐sheet were subjected to combinatorial sequence design calculations using Rosetta FastDesign (Bhardwaj et al., 2016). βα and αα loops were designed using consensus sequence profiles (Marcos et al., 2017) (derived from fragments with the same backbone torsions) strongly encoding for loop backbone geometry (Figure S1). Large hydrophobic amino acids were encouraged at the interface between β‐sheet and the α‐helical subdomain to maximize the amount of buried surface area gained with the appended subdomain (Figure S1). Sequences were ranked based on energy and sidechain packing metrics, as well as local sequence‐structure compatibility assessed by fragment quality analysis (Koga et al., 2012; Marcos & Silva, 2018). Folding of the top‐ranked designs was screened by Rosetta ab initio folding simulations from the extended chain (Bradley et al., 2005). The extent to which the designed sequences encode the designed structures was also evaluated through AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021; Mirdita et al., 2022) structure prediction calculations (Table S1). We selected four one‐helix and four two‐helix designs for experimental characterization, hereafter referred to as designs dNTF0‐H1‐4 and dNTF0‐HH1‐4, respectively (Figures 2a,b, S1). Overall, the selected one‐helix and two‐helix designs increased the buried surface area of the original construct dNTF0—that is, the interface area between the α‐helical subdomains and the rest of the NTF2 scaffold—in 1335 ± 108 and 1767 ± 139 Å2, respectively. As intended, most of the acquired area was hydrophobic (896 ± 119 and 1332 ± 149 Å2, respectively).

For designing homodimer interfaces, we performed deep learning hallucination (Wicky et al., 2022) using the AlphaFold2 network (Methods). During this procedure, to preserve the fold structure of dNTF0 and guide the generation of the dimer interface, we enabled mutations only on outward‐facing β‐sheet positions of dNTF0—that is, with their CαCβ vectors pointing to the convex face of the β‐sheet—and iteratively optimized the formation of homodimers through a loss function combining two AlphaFold2 confidence metrics: predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT), a per‐residue measure of the confidence in the local structure, and the predicted template modeling (pTM) score, a global measure of structure accuracy that here reflects the formation of a well‐packed dimeric structure. Hallucinated homodimeric structures with pTM >0.8, while having the intended β‐sheet interfaces and preserving the dNTF0 monomeric fold, were subsequently sequence‐optimized with ProteinMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2022) design calculations with symmetry. ProteinMPNN was applied to redesign only the hallucinated residue positions. The designed interfaces were relaxed and ranked based on protein‐binding energetics and shape complementarity with Rosetta. The top‐ranked designs were assessed for correct monomeric folding and protein–protein docking orientation with AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold‐multimer (Evans et al., 2021) in single‐sequence mode (Table S1). The three top‐ranked designs based on pLDDT and pTM confidence metrics from structure prediction were selected for experimental testing, hereafter dNTF0‐Dim1‐3 (Figure 2a,b). The three designs had an interface area of 2176 ± 52 Å2 (of which 1642 ± 27 Å2 was hydrophobic), which represented an average increase in buried hydrophobic surface area of 746 and 310 Å2 with respect to the one‐helix and two‐helix designs, respectively. A key advantage of the hallucination approach is its ability to not only optimize the orientation between the subunits but also induce subtle intramolecular rearrangements that improve interface shape complementarity—enabling orientations that would be missed by classical rigid docking methods. For instance, compared to the dNTF0 backbone, the three selected designs have a structural shift at the NTF2‐cone apex, near the N‐terminus, increasing bending of β3 (Figure S2). This shift, along with minor adjustments in helix α1 and adjacent strands, collectively enhances the binding surface—without this structural adaptation, this dimer orientation would result in steric clashes.

Experimental characterization of stabilized designs

We obtained synthetic genes encoding the selected amino acid sequences, which correspond to 4 one‐helix designs, 4 two‐helix designs, and 3 homodimeric designs. We expressed all proteins in Escherichia coli and purified them by nickel‐affinity and size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC). All designs were present in the soluble fraction and were monodisperse. The oligomeric state of all designs was confirmed by SEC coupled with multi‐angle light scattering (MALS)—that is, one‐helix and two‐helix designs behaved as monomers and homodimeric designs behaved as dimers (Figures 2c, S3–S5, and Table S2). All designs tested had far‐UV CD spectra compatible with an ɑβ protein structure and were thermostable (T m >95°C). We then conducted chemical denaturation experiments with GdnHCl and monitored protein unfolding by CD (Figures 2d, S3–S5). While the dNTF0 reference construct exhibited a C m of 2.9 M, all designed variants demonstrated increased stability to varying extents. In one‐helix designs, the C m ranged from 3.4 to 5.0 M, while two‐helix designs shifted the C m up to 5.5 M. Notably, homodimeric designs remained folded at 7 M GdnHCl. Overall, the increased size of the hydrophobic core attained by the designed buttressing elements provided a substantial increase in protein folding stability.

We successfully determined the x‐ray crystal structure of representatives from each of the three design classes—that is, one‐helix, two‐helix, and homodimeric designs (using the corresponding design models for molecular replacement). The structure of the one‐helix design dNTF0‐H4 was determined at 2.4 Å resolution (Table S3) and was found nearly identical to the design model (Figure 2e), with a Cα‐RMSD of 0.7 Å. The scaffold structure was well‐preserved, with an excellent match of the βα loop and the helix packing mode stabilized by hydrophobic sidechain interactions and salt bridges. The structure of the two‐helix design dNTF0‐HH3 at 2.0 Å resolution (Table S3) closely matched the design model (Figure 2f), showing a Cα‐RMSD of 1.1 Å, with minor deviations in the αα loop geometry and the orientation of the second C‐terminal helix, likely due to slight overpacking in the design model. The structure of the homodimer dNTF0‐Dim1, also solved at 2.0 Å resolution (Table S3), exhibited excellent agreement with the design model (Figure 2g), with a Cα‐RMSD of 0.8 Å across the dimer. The agreement at the subunit level was also remarkable (Cα‐RMSD of 0.6 Å), accurately recapitulating the intramolecular structural changes relative to dNTF0 that were incorporated around the NTF2‐cone apex during hallucination‐based design (reflected in a Cα‐RMSD of 1.2 Å between the crystal structures of dNTF0 and dNTF0‐Dim1). Overall, these results demonstrate that buttressing elements can be designed with high stability and structural accuracy.

Computational design and stability of non‐specific hydrophobic pockets

Having shown that buttressing elements on the convex face of the β‐sheet enhance stability while preserving scaffold geometry, we next evaluated the capacity of buttressed scaffolds to support cavity‐creating mutations shaping ligand‐binding pockets. We focused on carving out hydrophobic pockets, which are common in naturally occurring NTF2‐like proteins and essential for recognizing a broad range of small molecules and drugs (but more prone to collapse than solvent‐exposed polar pockets), making them a relevant test of our approach. To this end, we sought to underpack the interface between the β‐sheet and the α‐helical lid of the dNTF0 scaffold by designing mutations at inward‐facing positions, from the base to the midpoint of the NTF2‐cone (Figure 3a). We performed Rosetta sequence design calculations to incorporate combinations of mutations that reduced the size of the largest hydrophobic chains—that is, namely replacing aromatic residues (F, Y, W) and nearby residues with aliphatics (A, V, L, I, M) or uncharged polars in more solvent‐exposed positions. We then assessed pocket formation in silico using Rosetta ligand docking calculations (DeLuca et al., 2015; Fleishman et al., 2011; Lemmon & Meiler, 2012) with Prodan (Figure 3a)—a rigid, sterically demanding molecule with a naphthalene‐like core, making it a suitable probe for guiding the design of non‐specific hydrophobic pockets. We ranked designs based on ligand‐binding interaction energies and total protein energy to strike a balance between pocket formation and protein stability (Figure S6). From this, we selected three pocket designs each containing 6–7 mutations relative to the original scaffold dNTF0 (Figure 3b), herein named dNTF0‐Cav1‐3. To assess the impact of pocket‐creating mutations in the presence of buttressing elements, the three sets of pocket mutations were also introduced into the two‐helix (dNTF0‐HH3) and homodimeric (dNTF0‐Dim1) scaffolds (validated by x‐ray crystallography in the previous section), generating the dNTF0‐Cav1‐3‐HH3 and dNTF0‐Cav1‐3‐Dim1 designs, respectively (Figure 3c). Correct folding of the selected pocket‐containing designs was verified with AlphaFold2 (Table S1) and Rosetta ab initio structure prediction (Figures S7 and S8).

All nine pocket‐containing proteins were expressed and purified as described above, with the three dimers showing higher soluble expression yields than the two‐helix‐stabilized scaffolds. SEC‐MALS confirmed that each design adopted its intended oligomeric state (Figures S7–S9 and Table S2), and CD indicated that the proteins remained folded at 95°C (Figures S7–S9). GdnHCl titrations monitored by CD revealed that introducing the pocket mutations reduces protein stability, but this effect was compensated when the pockets were buttressed by two‐helix subdomains or homodimer interfaces (Figures 4a–c and S7–S9). For instance, while dNTF0‐Cav2 showed reduced stability compared to dNTF0 (C m decreased from 2.9 to 1.6 M GdnHCl) (Figure 4b, top), the two‐helix (Figure S8) and homodimeric (Figure 4c, top) buttressed variants remained fully folded at 4 and 7 M GdnHCl, respectively. Similarly, dNTF0‐Cav3 had a slightly lower C m (2.7 M) than dNTF0, whereas its stabilized variants remained folded at 5 M (two‐helix; Figure S8) and 7 M (homodimer; Figure 4c, bottom, and Figure S9) GdnHCl.

Crystal structures of pocket‐containing designs

To further investigate the structural features of the Cav2 pocket, we attempted structure determination for the three formats (i.e., reference monomer, with two‐helix extension, and homodimeric), and successfully solved the crystal structure of the homodimer dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 at 1.5 Å resolution (using the dNTF0‐Dim1 crystal structure for molecular replacement) (Figure 4d; Table S4). The asymmetric unit contains one protein molecule, which forms the dimer with a neighboring molecule related by crystallographic symmetry. The backbone structure of the dimer was nearly identical to the parent dNTF0‐Dim1 (without pocket), with a Cα‐RMSD of 0.6 Å across the dimer and 0.5 Å at the subunit level. The designed pocket was partially occupied by a small electron density in the pocket that could not be unambiguously assigned to a specific ligand (Figure 4d, bottom inset; Figure S10). This density, surrounded by hydrophobic residues, is positioned to form a hydrogen bond with glutamine Q86, a polar mutation introduced into the pocket, which in turn interacts with glutamate E84, stabilizing the glutamine rotamer. Overall, the structural fidelity of this pocket‐containing design to the parent dimeric scaffold (without pocket) highlights the structural robustness of the scaffold to pocket‐creating mutations.

Next, we successfully determined the x‐ray structure of dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1 at 1.9 Å resolution (using the dNTF0‐Dim1 crystal structure for molecular replacement), revealing two copies of the dimer in the asymmetric unit (Table S4). Each dimer closely resembled the reference dimer dNTF0‐Dim1, with Cα‐RMSDs of 1.1–1.2 Å across the dimer and 0.8–1.4 Å at the subunit level (Figure 4e). Closer inspection of the subunits revealed conformational differences: while one subunit in each dimer (chains A and C) closely matched the design model (Cα‐RMSDs of 0.8–0.9 Å), the other subunit (chains B and D) displayed an increased Cα‐Cα packing distance between the N‐terminal helix α1 (A7) and the adjacent helix α2 (V26), shifting from 5.7–5.8 Å to 10.7–10.9 Å, resulting in a more open, loosely packed conformation (Figure 4e, middle; Figure S11a, middle). These conformational differences suggest greater flexibility of helix α1 compared to dNTF0‐Dim1 (without pocket) and dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 (different pocket), both of which have their subunits in the same closed conformation as shown above. Interestingly, we observed a large, elongated density inside the pocket of all four subunits (Figure 4e, right). This density corresponds to an unknown ligand likely originating from the E. coli expression host, as the shape of the density does not resemble that of any chemical used in either purification or crystallization—its persistence throughout protein purification indicates strong binding affinity. Given the hydrophobic character of the pocket and the elongated shape of the electron density, the ligand is likely an endogenous lipid with a long aliphatic chain. This finding is consistent with previous reports of NTF2‐like domains crystallized in complex with unidentified hydrophobic ligands originating from the expression host, including phospholipids (Johansson et al., 2005; Pan et al., 2020; Wong et al., 2017).

We performed all‐atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (three replicas of 1 μs each) starting from the crystal structure of dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1 (without ligand), and monitored the openness of helix α1 through the Cα–Cα distance between residues A7 (helix α1) and V26 (helix α2), a convenient metric for evaluating packing between the two adjacent helices (Figure S11). Subunit A showed a modest increase in this distance (6.9 vs. 5.7 Å in the crystal), while subunit B shifted from an open conformation (10.9 Å) toward a more closed state (7.9 Å). In both subunits, distances remained larger than in subunit A of the crystal or in dNTF0‐Dim1 (5.6 Å). For comparison, MD simulations of dNTF0‐Dim1 and dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 (with helix α1 initially closed at 5.6 and 5.9 Å, respectively) showed that α1 retained its closed conformation throughout the simulations, and the overall structure remained rigid (Figure S11a–c). These results indicate that helix α1 is more flexible in dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1, but to a limited extent: open conformations appear disfavored in the MD—likely due to weaker hydrophobic packing—and the crystal structure with ligand may have captured one of the possible conformations. Despite the flexibility of helix α1 and the presence of ligands, the crystal structure of the remaining scaffold (excluding helix α1) is nearly identical to the parent dNTF0‐Dim1, which supports the structural robustness of the dimeric architecture to sequence modifications and ligand binding, particularly from the base to the middle of the NTF2‐cone.

Computational design of naproxen‐binding pockets

Having established that the dimeric dNTF0‐Dim1 scaffold is highly robust for accommodating pocket‐creating mutations in terms of expression, stability, and structure, we next sought to design a ligand‐specific binding pocket in this scaffold. This presents a more stringent test of the scaffold's ability to tolerate functional mutations, as it typically requires more extensive sequence modifications than our previous pocket designs. We focused on binding Naproxen (Nap), a widely used anti‐inflammatory drug that, when overused, can cause severe side effects such as stomach ulcers and heart disease (Doomra & Goyal, 2020). Its high stability and water solubility also make it an emerging environmental pollutant (Mishra et al., 2023; Wilkinson et al., 2022). Developing Nap‐binding receptors is thus essential for developing biosensors enabling cost‐effective and continuous monitoring of this small molecule (Li et al., 2021; Qian et al., 2020). We reasoned that the shape of our scaffold is well‐suited to bind Nap, as its structure closely resembles Prodan—both share a naphthalene core with small substituents at positions 2 and 6—which was used as a probe to guide the design of non‐specific pockets in the previous section. The main chemical difference between Prodan and Nap is at position 6, where Nap has a carboxylate group instead of Prodan's propionyl group, making it water‐soluble as a sodium salt. Given the chemical similarities between both molecules, we expected our non‐specific pocket‐containing designs might display some level of Nap binding and measured this interaction using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). Both dNTF0‐Cav1‐Dim1 and dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 bound Nap with a K d of 11 μM (Figure S13), while dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1 showed no detectable binding. This lack of binding for dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1 is consistent with the presence of a tightly bound ligand occupying the pocket, as seen in the crystal structure. As expected, the negative control dNTF0‐Dim1, which lacks a binding pocket, also showed no binding. Supporting this interpretation, dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1 exhibited weak binding (K d ~ 200 μM) after treatment with GdnHCl followed by dialysis, suggesting partial displacement of the bound ligand.

To design novel Nap‐binding pockets with improved shape‐ and chemical complementarity, we sought to explore larger sequence modifications in the context of the ligand (Figure 5a). We began by incorporating valine mutations on a starting set of candidate pocket positions on dNTF0‐Dim1, from the base to the midpoint of the scaffold NTF2‐cone. We then generated initial ligand configurations around this poly‐valine pocket using Patchdock (Schneidman‐Duhovny et al., 2005) and performed iterative cycles of LigandMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2025) sequence design on the ligand's first shell, Rosetta‐based scoring (Leman et al., 2020), and DiffDock (Corso et al., 2022) redocking to refine ligand placement (see Methods). This process yielded Nap‐binding complexes with progressively improved ligand‐binding energies and docking metrics (Figure S13). The final designs featured hydrophobic interactions with the ligand's aromatic rings and electrostatic complementarity with the surface‐exposed carboxylate group of Nap through positively charged sidechains. These designs incorporated 14–18 mutations relative to the starting dNTF0‐Dim1 scaffold—that is, 2‐3‐fold more mutations than the non‐specific pockets—and 12–19 mutations relative to all non‐specific pockets tested above (Cav1‐3); thereby exploring a larger sequence space. We verified that the designed Nap binders folded correctly based on AlphaFold2 structure prediction (Table S1), subsequently confirmed that Nap bound in the intended pockets by performing structure prediction in the presence of ligand using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) (Figure S14), and selected three of them for experimental validation, hereafter named dNTF0‐Nap1‐3‐Dim1 (Figure 5b,c). All three designs were successfully expressed and purified as described previously, and SEC traces confirmed they are monodisperse. The three designs were thermostable by CD (Figures 5d, and S15), dimeric by SEC‐MALS (Figures 5e and S15), and bound Nap with K d's in the 5.9–8.6 μM range by ITC (Figures 5f and S12). Thermodynamic parameters estimated by ITC (Figure S12e) show that the dominant contribution to binding is entropic, accompanied by a small and favorable enthalpic contribution, suggesting that binding is mainly driven by protein‐ligand hydrophobic interactions. No cooperative effects stemming from the binding of naproxen molecules to the two subunits conforming the homodimeric proteins were observed (i.e., the two binding sites in the homodimeric proteins behaved as independent and identical binding sites).

High structural robustness of homodimers with pockets

Our observations suggest that the homodimeric scaffolds are exceptionally stable, highly tolerant to sequence changes, and amenable for functionalization. To further investigate how the dimer interface affects structural rigidity and improves binding site formation, we performed MD simulations (three replicas of 1 μs each) to compare the flexibility of pocket‐containing dimers exhibiting Nap binding to their monomeric counterparts. In particular, we compared dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 (non‐specific) and dNTF0‐Nap2‐Dim1 (Nap‐binder), one from each pocket class, with monomers dNTF0‐Cav2 and dNTF0‐Nap2, respectively (i.e., the same pocket on the reference dNTF0 scaffold). During the simulations, both dimers remained similarly rigid as the parent dNTF0‐Dim1 scaffold, which lacks a pocket (Figure S16). In contrast, the corresponding monomeric counterparts turned out to be more flexible, as shown by their higher RMSFs, particularly in three regions at the NTF2‐cone base that shape the pocket entrance (Figures 5g and S16): the β‐hairpin formed between strands β1 and β2, helix α3, and the β‐hairpin formed between strands β4 and β5. To assess how well the pocket entrance was maintained in the course of the simulations, we monitored the distances between three equidistant, inward‐pointing residues from these regions—that is, 35 on strand β1, 52 on helix α3, and 84 on strand β5 (Figure 5h, left). While the dimers maintain the geometry of the pocket entrance very close to the starting structure, the monomers sampled a much broader range of geometries (Figure 5h, right; Figures S16 and S17), with several replicas collapsing the pocket due to helix α3 approaching the β‐sheet (Figure S17). The dimer interface likely rigidifies the β‐sheet, and in turn restricts the flexibility of helix α3, as it is connected to the β‐sheet from both ends, ultimately preserving pocket accessibility; suggesting that the homodimeric interface helps maintain the structural integrity of the binding pocket, enhancing its preorganization and favoring ligand‐binding. To experimentally test whether dimerization favors Nap binding, we expressed the two monomers (dNTF0‐Cav2 and dNTF0‐Nap2) and measured their binding affinities using ITC (Figure S12). As anticipated, both monomers showed markedly reduced binding compared to their dimeric counterparts, which bind Nap in the low μM range (as described above). Monomeric dNTF0‐Cav2 bound Nap with a K d of 220 μM—approximately 20‐fold weaker than dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 ‐ while dNTF0‐Nap2 showed no detectable binding.

DISCUSSION

NTF2‐like domains are becoming increasingly valuable in biotechnology, particularly for custom‐designing small‐molecule binding sites in biosensing applications or enzymes catalyzing chemical reactions of interest. However, the design of pockets in such small protein folds can severely compromise stability and structural integrity due to the close proximity of the hydrophobic core to pocket‐forming regions. Most studies have aimed to increase protein stability by changing the sidechains while largely overlooking backbone remodeling or oligomeric design. Here, we introduce a strategy to enhance the stability of de novo designed NTF2‐like ligand‐binding proteins by computationally designing novel buttressing elements on the convex face of the NTF2's curved β‐sheet. With this approach, C‐terminal extensions of α‐helical subdomains substantially increase the protein stability of dNTF0, raising the C m from 2.9 up to 5.5 M GdnHCl, and homodimer interfaces maintain folding even at 7 M GdnHCl. A key advantage of this approach is that it enhances stability without altering the inward‐facing residues forming the pocket. Ultimately, this stability enhancement efficiently addresses the function‐stability tradeoff typically encountered in the design of ligand‐binding sites.

Although de novo designed proteins typically include features that enhance thermostability—for example, local interactions favoring secondary structures and loop conformations, high content of surface salt bridges and large hydrophobic cores—they can still misfold or adopt molten globule‐like structures with poorly defined tertiary structures. For de novo proteins, we have previously found that high resistance to GdnHCl denaturation is a stronger indicator of rigid, well‐folded proteins than thermal stability (Marcos et al., 2017). GdnHCl screens surface charge interactions, allowing for a better assessment of the strength of hydrophobic interactions that define the tertiary structure (Monera et al., 1994; Vecchio et al., 2002). This revealed the substantial structural stabilization contributed by α‐helical subdomains and, to an even greater extent, by homodimer interfaces. Achieving such structural hyperstability can serve two purposes. First, it increases tolerance to destabilizing functional mutations while preserving structural integrity. Therefore, our approach alleviates stability constraints when designing ligand‐binding sites, minimizing design failures due to misfolding and, thus, making the design process more efficient. Second, ligand‐binding proteins with enhanced structural stability are particularly attractive for working in harsh environmental conditions required for biosensing applications or industrial settings.

We found that ligand‐binding pockets could be designed with high sequence diversity on the dNTF0 scaffold. Initially, we designed non‐specific hydrophobic pockets containing 6–7 mutations, with geometries compatible with Prodan—a bulky hydrophobic probe. This lack of specificity allowed them to accommodate other ligands, as evidenced by x‐ray structures of dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 and dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1, which showed electron densities of varying size and shape within their pockets. Notably, some of these pockets also bound the small‐molecule drug Naproxen, which is chemically similar to Prodan. Subsequent design efforts to increase Naproxen binding using the same dNTF0 scaffold generated stable pockets incorporating 14–18 mutations, which resulted in modest increases in binding affinity—this may be another example of the well‐known enthalpy‐entropy compensation phenomenon in protein binding (i.e., enthalpy and entropy are more sensitive than the Gibbs energy to perturbations in the system, such as mutations in the binding partners. In general, this results in large and opposite changes in these contributions, whereas smaller changes in the Gibbs energy are observed). The dNTF0 backbone may be limiting and further gains in binding affinity may require backbone remodeling to enlarge the binding pocket and enhance ligand burial. Nevertheless, while backbone remodeling could yield higher‐affinity binders, we chose to base our designs and analysis on the original dNTF0 scaffold to allow systematic evaluation and comparison across design strategies. This allowed us to identify principles that can guide future designs where de novo NTF2 scaffolds with geometries tailored for specific ligands are stabilized by buttressing elements enhancing folding stability and maintaining pocket structure to a larger extent. Importantly, the tested pockets (non‐specific or Nap‐binding), which were well tolerated when the scaffold was buttressed, highlighted regions permissive to cavity‐creating mutations that could guide future NTF2 designs: (1) the interface between the C‐termini of helix α1 and the β‐sheet; (2) solvent‐exposed, inward‐facing β‐sheet positions (in strands β1, β5, β6); and (3) the segment extending from the middle of helix α3 to the beginning of strand β3. Most cavity‐creating mutations preserved the original amino acid character—that is, large hydrophobics replaced by smaller ones or polars replaced by charged sidechains enhancing interactions with Nap–though some amino acid changes, such as W86Q in dNTF0‐Cav2, were also well tolerated.

Both buttressing elements—α‐helical subdomains and homodimer interfaces—not only restore but surpass the stability loss due to pocket‐creating mutations. However, the significantly higher expression of dimeric constructs makes this approach particularly effective. Beyond improving stability, our findings suggest that designing homodimer interfaces is a powerful strategy for enhancing structural robustness against pocket‐creating mutations. This is supported by the nearly identical crystal structures of dNTF0‐Dim1 (without pocket) and dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1, with the pocket partially occupied by a small electron density. MD simulations for designs containing a non‐specific or Nap‐binding pocket showed that the homodimer interface enhances the structural rigidity of the pocket with respect to the monomeric counterparts. This restricts sampling of alternative conformations that are less productive for binding. Thus, the homodimer interface can be effective at reducing the need for structural arrangements to bind ligands, enhancing binding site preorganization. Overall, we have observed that the dimerization buttressing strategy can stabilize pocket volumes approaching ~300 Å3 (Kim et al., 1997) (Figure S18), which were unstable in the monomeric state as indicated by Nap‐binding experiments and MD simulations. The high stability of the dimeric pockets suggests the potential to add destabilizing mutations to enlarge pocket volumes, raising the important question of where the practical limits lie—a matter to be addressed in future experimental work. However, given the small size of the dNTF0 scaffold, such mutations would require targeting the apex of the NTF2‐cone—where the hydrophobic core is concentrated and secondary structure elements are tightly packed—which would likely result in significant stability loss while yielding only modest increases in pocket volume. Consequently, we anticipate that the buttressing strategy could be effectively applied to larger NTF2 scaffolds, enabling the formation of larger pockets through alternative backbone geometries with longer β‐sheets and α‐helices, as observed in naturally occurring NTF2‐like domains.

The effectiveness of the dimerization buttressing strategy is reflected in its recurrent use by nature, particularly among naturally occurring enzymes (e.g., ketosteroid isomerase3) and ligand‐binding proteins with the NTF2 fold. Notably, the Nuclear Transport Factor 2 protein (Bullock et al., 1996)—after which this fold is named—is itself a homodimer that binds RanGDP to facilitate protein transport into the nucleus. Our results are also in line with previous studies showing the key role that oligomerization (East et al., 2022) and overall rigidity (Hindson et al., 2021) can play to enhance the catalytic activity of enzymes. Overall, the simplicity and effectiveness of our hallucination‐based design approach for β‐sheet homodimer interfaces in NTF2‐like domains should make it broadly applicable. It can be used to stabilize pre‐existing NTF2‐like domains or optimize the design of novel NTF2 binders by integrating dimer interfaces from the beginning. Furthermore, buttressing via dimer interfaces opens exciting possibilities beyond enhancing stability and binding site formation to be explored in the near future, such as enabling the addition of a distal domain that could be harnessed for allosteric regulation or for tuning enzyme activity in response to temperature (Van Der Kamp et al., 2018).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Computational design of α‐helical buttressing subdomains

The Protein Data Bank (PDB) entry 5L33 (Marcos et al., 2017) served as the starting model for designing C‐terminal α‐helical subdomains with RosettaScripts (Fleishman et al., 2011). We built protein backbones by fragment assembly using the Blueprint Builder mover (Koga et al., 2012) in combination with the fldsgn_cen centroid scoring function. We used custom python scripts to generate the input blueprint files.

For one‐helix designs, we sampled combinations of loop length (1–5 residues), loop ABEGO (Lin et al., 2015) types (X, XB, XXXB, XXXB, XXXB), and helix length (10–18 residues). Backbone geometries of loop residues are conveniently described using ABEGO torsion bins, which correspond to distinct regions of the Ramachandran plot: “A” for right‐handed α‐helix, “B” for extended β‐strand, “E” for extended regions with positive ϕ angles, “G” for left‐handed α‐helix, and “O” for non‐planar peptide bonds. The “X” torsion bin is used to avoid biasing the ABEGO in that particular position during fragment picking. We encouraged the “B” torsion bin for the last residue of each loop to favor N‐terminal hydrogen‐bonded capping with the following helix. Based on the βα rule (Koga et al., 2012) relating the relative orientation between the α‐helix and the sidechain directionality of the last β‐strand residue, we connected the loop either to the C‐terminus or its preceding residue to sample the two possible sidechain directionalities. For each of the 90 combinations, we generated 150 backbone models, and those without kinked α‐helices were subjected to sequence design calculations with the FastDesign mover (Bhardwaj et al., 2016) using the Ref2015 energy function (Alford et al., 2017). We enabled mutations on the newly built elements and on residue positions at the interface with these elements, which were identified with the InterfaceByVector residue selector (using cb_dist_cut = “10.0,” nearby_atom_cut = “5.5,” and vector_angle_cut = “45.0”). Interface loop positions were not allowed to mutate to minimize their impact on protein folding. To minimize the number of unnecessary mutations at interface positions, we gave a bonus to the original sequence using the FavorSequenceProfile mover. The resulting designs were filtered based on Rosetta total score, secondary structure shape complementarity (between the α‐helical subdomain and the rest of the structure), and the buried surface area of the α‐helical subdomain. The top 1000 designs were selected for fragment quality analysis to assess the local compatibility between the designed backbones and sequences. The top‐50 designs were selected for Rosetta ab initio folding simulations (Bradley et al., 2005), generating about 6000 trajectories per design. Based on this analysis, we identified the seven most promising combinations of loop ABEGO type and helix length, and for these, we intensified backbone and sequence sampling in a second design round to achieve more optimal designs following the same procedure. From this second round, four designs with funnel‐shaped energy landscapes obtained from ab initio folding simulations were selected for experimental validation. We verified that these sequences had high‐confidence AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021) structure predictions (using the ColabFold implementation (Mirdita et al., 2022)) closely matching the design models.

For two‐helix designs, we sampled combinations of helix lengths (10–16 residues) and common ABEGO types for the βα (B, XB, XXB, XXXB) and αα (GB, GBB, XXXB) loop connections. For the resulting 588 combinations, we generated and filtered the designs as described above and selected the most promising combinations of ABEGO and helix lengths for a second round of intensified sampling. Four designs with consistent structure prediction were selected for experimental validation.

Deep network hallucination of buttressing homodimer interfaces

To design homodimers of dNTF0, we adapted a design strategy described previously (Roel‐Touris et al., 2023; Wicky et al., 2022) to hallucinate homodimeric interfaces using the AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021) network—with the index gap approximation for oligomer prediction. Residue positions on the convex face of the β‐sheet—previously targeted during the design of α‐helical subdomains—were selected for hallucination (loop positions were excluded to ensure proper folding), while the rest of the positions were kept fixed. To enhance interface contacts, we also explored C‐terminal extensions of 0–2 residues, as this area is proximal to the intended interfaces. For each design combination, we ran 20 Monte Carlo hallucination trajectories (500 steps each), guided by a loss function incorporating structure confidence metrics (pLDDT and pTM) from AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021). All hallucinated models with pTM >0.8 were subjected to ProteinMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2022) sequence calculations on the hallucinated positions. We ran 10 ProteinMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2022) calculations per backbone, and the resulting sequences were clustered with MMseqs2 (Steinegger & Söding, 2017) using a 90% sequence identity threshold. The designed sequences were threaded on the corresponding backbones and the resulting structures were energy‐minimized using Rosetta FastRelax (Leman et al., 2020). Final designs were filtered based on interface energy (ddG), contact molecular surface, interface shape complementarity, and Rosetta total energy score. The top 500 designs were then evaluated with AlphaFold2‐multimer (Evans et al., 2021) predictions using the ColabFold implementation (Mirdita et al., 2022). From these, three designs showing excellent agreement between the design model and the predicted structure were selected for experimental validation.

Design of non‐specific hydrophobic pockets

To design binding pockets at the base of the dNTF0 cone, we manually selected inward‐facing residues spanning the core, boundary, and surface layers—primarily aliphatic or aromatic side chains. Based on this selection, we carried out sequence design with the FastDesign mover (Bhardwaj et al., 2016) by allowing mutations on various subsets of these residues. Allowed mutations ranged from conservative to more radical substitutions (e.g., aromatic to small aliphatics), primarily to reduce the size of side chains and open up pocket space. Eleven independent design runs were performed, sampling different combinations of mutable residues and allowed amino acids. The designs were pre‐filtered using the CavityVolume filter and Rosetta total score. Top‐ranked candidates were further evaluated via Rosetta ligand docking (DeLuca et al., 2015; Lemmon & Meiler, 2012), implemented in RosettaScripts (Fleishman et al., 2011), using Prodan—a bulky hydrophobic probe. The designs were ranked based on the quality of the docking prediction, mainly the fractional change in the ligand's exposed surface (dSASA), interface ligand‐binding energy (ddG), and the Rosetta total score. Designs showing deep ligand penetration and low total energy were considered to have well‐formed, stable pockets. These were further filtered by fragment quality analysis and ab initio folding simulations (Bradley et al., 2005) to verify correct folding after the introduction of pocket‐creating mutations. Three designs were selected for experimental validation, and the same pocket mutations were directly transferred onto the stabilized scaffolds dNTF0‐HH3 and dNTF0‐Dim1. Pocket volumes for final designs were identified using the KVFinder (Guerra et al., 2021) implementation in ChimeraX (Meng et al., 2023).

Design of naproxen‐binding pockets

Initial cavities were generated by randomly mutating subsets of 7 or 12 residues within 4.5 Å of the protein core to poly‐valine. Naproxen was then docked into these cavities using PatchDock (Schneidman‐Duhovny et al., 2005), a rigid‐body docking method that efficiently samples diverse protein‐ligand configurations based on shape complementarity. Docked poses were filtered using PatchDock interface metrics—including docking score, maximal surface penetration, atomic contact energy (ACE)—as well as the minimum distance between cavity residues and the ligand's center of mass to ensure proper ligand positioning.

The filtered complexes were used as starting points for iterative optimization of the binding pocket. Residues in contact with the ligand were subjected to sequence design using LigandMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2025) at sampling temperatures of 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 to promote sequence diversity. Native proline and glycine residues were preserved, and cysteine mutations were disallowed to maintain structural integrity. Designed sequences were threaded onto the corresponding backbones and refined using Rosetta's FastRelax protocol, applying Cα constraints to preserve backbone geometry while optimizing sidechain packing around the ligand. Refined models were redocked using DiffDock (Corso et al., 2022). Among the top five complexes (ranked by DiffDock confidence), those with no buried unsatisfied polar atoms in the binding site were evaluated using Rosetta scoring metrics, including ligand‐binding energy (ddG) and fractional ligand buried surface area (dSASA). The top‐scoring sequence‐complex pair was used as the input for the next round of design. This cycle of design, docking, and evaluation was repeated over 15 iterations, guided by a Combined score integrating ddG, dSASA, and DiffDock's confidence score. Briefly, the combined score at iteration i was calculated as the average percent change in ddG, dSASA, and DiffDock confidence relative to iteration i–1. For complexes with DiffDock confidence scores below zero, the combined score was computed using only ddG and dSASA. If LigandMPNN‐generated designs failed the buried unsatisfied polar filter, a negative bias of −10 was applied to polar amino acids at positions that consistently favored polar residues. Folding of the optimized designs was finally assessed with AlphaFold2.

MD simulations

MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2021.4 (Abraham et al., 2015) with the Amber ff99SB‐ILDN (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2010) force field. The protein systems were solvated in a dodecahedral periodic box using the TIP3P water model and a buffer distance of 11 Å to the box edges. Na+ and Cl− ions were added to neutralize the total charge of the system at a concentration of 0.137 mM. Parameters for proteins and NaCl were generated with the ff99SB‐ILDN force field. Before the production runs, the initial structure was minimized with the steepest descent algorithm for 50,000 steps, and was gradually heated from 100 to 300 K over 500 ps. The system was then equilibrated for 500 ps under constant volume (NVT ensemble) at 300 K, followed by a 1 ns equilibration in the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar. Long‐range electrostatic interactions were modeled using the particle mesh‐Ewald (PME) method, and short‐range interactions were modeled with Lennard‐Jones and Coulomb electrostatic interactions using a 10 Å cut‐off. Bond constraints were applied using the LINCS algorithm, enabling a time step of 2 fs. Each of the simulations consisted of three independent MD runs of 1 μs. Trajectory frames were saved every 50 ps. Simulations were analyzed with GROMACS tools and custom python scripts using the mdtraj module (McGibbon et al., 2015). To ensure proper equilibration from the initial conformation, the first 50 ns of each production run were excluded from the analysis.

Recombinant expression and purification of the designed proteins

Synthetic genes encoding the designed amino acid sequences were obtained from GenScript and cloned into the pET‐28a‐TEV expression vector within NdeI and XhoI restriction sites. The constructs encoded an N‐terminal hexa‐histidine tag followed by a Tobacco‐Etch‐Virus peptidase (TEV) cleavage site. These plasmids were used to transform Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells (Sigma). Starter 20 mL cultures from single colonies were grown at 37°C in LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. These cultures were used to inoculate 800 mL of LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin and cells were grown at 37°C under shaking until an optical density (OD600) of 0.6–0.9 was reached. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM of IPTG and cultures were incubated overnight at 19°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation for 30 min at 4500g and resuspended in 10 mL of buffer containing 30 mM Tris HCl pH 8, 250 mM sodium chloride, 30 mM imidazole, and EDTA‐free cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Life Sciences). Cells were lysed using a sonicator and soluble protein was clarified by centrifugation for 30 min at 25,000g. Recombinant proteins were captured on nickel‐sepharose HisTrap HP columns (Cytiva), which had been previously equilibrated with the same buffer and mounted on a Bio‐Rad chromatography system. Recombinant protein purifications were performed with a gradient of 30–500 mM imidazole in the same buffer. Proteins were further purified by SEC using a Superdex 75 10/300 GL (Cytiva) column. The expression of purified proteins was assessed by SDS‐polyacrylamide gel, and protein concentrations were determined from the absorbance at 280 nm measured on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific) with extinction coefficients predicted from the amino acid sequences using the ProtParam tool.

SEC‐MALS

SEC coupled to multiple‐angle light scattering (SEC‐MALS) was performed in a Dawn Helios II apparatus (Wyatt Technologies) coupled to a SEC Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva). The column was equilibrated with 30 mM Tris·HCl, 250 mM sodium chloride, pH 8 at 25°C and operated at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Samples were simultaneously monitored with a UV detector (SPD‐20, Shimadzu), a differential refractometer (OPTI‐rEx, Wyatt Corp.), and a static multiangle laser light scattering (MALLS) detector (DAWN‐HELEOS, Wyatt Corp.). Data processing and analysis proceeded with Astra 7 software (Wyatt Technologies).

Protein crystallization and structure determination

Crystallization screenings were performed at the joint IRB/IBMB Automated Crystallography Platform. Screening solutions were prepared and dispensed into the reservoir wells of 96 × 2‐well MRC crystallization plates (Innovadyne Technologies) by a Freedom EVO robot (Tecan) using the sitting‐drop vapor diffusion method.

dNTF0‐H4 crystals appeared at 20°C in drops consisting of 100 nL protein solution (at 15 mg/mL in 30 mM Tris pH 8, 250 mM NaCl) and 100 nL reservoir solution (27% v/v PEG 3350 and 0.1 Bis‐Tris at pH 5.4). dNTF0‐HH3 crystals were obtained at 20°C in drops consisting of 100 nL protein solution (at 15 mg/mL in 30 mM Tris pH 8, 250 mM NaCl) and 100 nL reservoir solution (27% w/v PEG 400, 0.1 M cadmium chloride and 0.1 sodium acetate pH 4.6). dNTF0‐Dim1 and dNTF0‐Cav3‐Dim1 crystals were obtained at 20°C in drops consisting of 100 nL protein solution (at 15 mg/mL in 30 mM Tris pH 8, 250 mM NaCl) and 100 nL reservoir solution (23% w/v PEG 335 and 0.05 M zinc acetate). dNTF0‐Cav2‐Dim1 crystals were obtained at 20°C in drops consisting of 100 nL protein solution (at 15 mg/mL in 30 mM Tris pH 8, 250 mM NaCl) and 100 nL reservoir solution (2.5 M ammonium sulfate, 0.01 M magnesium acetate and 0.05 M pH 5.6). Protein crystals were cryoprotected with a multicomponent cryoprotectant (12.5% v/v diethylene glycol, 12.5% v/v MPD, 37.5% v/v 1,2‐propanediol and 12.5% v/v dimethyl sulfoxide), harvested with LithoLoops (Molecular dimensions), and flash‐vitrified in liquid nitrogen.

X‐ray diffraction datasets were collected at the XALOC beamline of the ALBA synchrotron (Cerdanyola, Catalonia, Spain) or at the IO3 beamline at the Diamond Synchrotron (Oxfordshire, United Kingdom). The diffraction images were processed with autoPROC (Vonrhein et al., 2011) and scaled using Aimless (Evans & Murshudov, 2013). The structures were solved by molecular replacement with MolRep (Vagin & Teplyakov, 2010) and using the original design as a model. The structures were refined using Refmac5 (Murshudov et al., 2011) and phenix.refine (Afonine et al., 2012) and manual building with Coot (Casañal et al., 2020). Final models were evaluated at the wwwPDB Validation Service (https://wwpdbvalidation.wwpdb.org/validservice).
