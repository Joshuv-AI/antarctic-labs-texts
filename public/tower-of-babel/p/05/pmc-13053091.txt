The growing burden of spine surgical wait times: a retrospective cohort study of longitudinal trends and impact on perioperative outcomes
Authors: Jessica CW Wang, John Street, Tamir Ailon, Michael Bond, Raphaële Charest-Morin, Nicolas Dea, Marcel Dvorak, Charles Fisher, Brian K Kwon, Scott Paquette, Charlotte Dandurand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Surgical delays are common in public health care systems such as Canada’s. Trends in wait times for elective spine surgery and their impact on outcomes remain uncharacterized.Methods:We performed a single-centre analysis of elective spine surgery data between 2009 and 2020. We assessed wait times between referral and initial consultation (T1), consultation and surgical booking (interval wait time; Ti), and booking and surgery (T2) in terms of trends and impact on perioperative outcomes (adverse events and hospital length of stay [LOS]).Results:In total, 2041 patients were included. Over the study period, total wait time (T1+Ti+T2) increased 5.6% annually (p < 0.001). Specifically, T1 decreased 4.8% (p < 0.001), Ti increased 14.9% (p < 0.001), and T2 increased 8.5% (p < 0.001) from year to year. The cumulative increase in total wait time was 72.4%, and the cumulative increase in Ti was 301.1% over the 10-year period. Longer total wait time and Ti were associated with increased rate of adverse events (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively) and odds of adverse events (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). Delays in all wait time intervals were associated with longer LOS (p < 0.001), with T2 having the largest effect of a 10.8% increase in LOS per 100 days of T2 (p < 0.001).Conclusion:Total wait times (T1+Ti+T2) for elective spine surgery significantly increased between 2009 and 2020. Ti showed the largest increase over the study period and was associated with a significant increase in adverse events. Future studies should investigate the impact of various efforts implemented and whether this translates to improved surgical outcomes and resource management.

Canada is one of many countries with a single-payer public health care system in which surgical wait times have become a growing concern to the public and policy-makers. Symptomatic degenerative spinal disease is common; in North America, the incidence is 4501 individuals per 100 000, with a prevalence of 16.1 million individuals affected annually.1 An aging population implies increasing prevalence of spinal disease and with it an ever-growing demand for spinal surgery.2

Increased wait times have been associated with worse patient-reported outcomes after lumbar spine surgery. In 2007, Braybrooke demonstrated in a small cohort that longer surgical wait times negatively affected patient-reported physical function and pain postoperatively.3 Additionally, a prospective study in 2016 showed that patients who received surgery within 12 months reported greater improvement on the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) at 1-year follow-up than those who waited more than 12 months.4 Multiple studies in 2024 further demonstrated that patients who received surgery within 6 months were more likely to achieve minimal clinically important differences in ODI scores at 1-year follow-up.5,6 There is a paucity of literature on the association between wait-time intervals and objective rates of adverse events and length of stay (LOS).

Long surgical wait times contribute to the overall delay in receiving timely care. Previous health care delivery assessments have not investigated long-term changes in total wait time and its constituent wait-time intervals for spine surgery. There is also little information on their effects on objectively measured patient outcomes. To address surgical wait-time challenges and their effects on patients, we sought to provide more granular data on total wait time and wait-time intervals to assess how they have evolved over time, with the aim of informing targeted areas of improvement for resource allocation in an already overloaded health care system.

In this study, the primary goal was to characterize wait-time interval trends over the last 10 years. Our secondary goal was to assess the association of each wait-time interval with perioperative outcomes such as the rates of adverse events and LOS.

Methods

Types of surgical wait-time intervals

Surgical wait times have been defined in a number of ways, but the most common definitions used reflect the different stages of the patient’s journey through the health care system (Figure 1).

Paradigm of the wait-time intervals that describe different stages in access to surgical care.

We describe 3 main intervals. Wait time 1 (T1) is defined as the duration between a patient’s initial referral by a health care provider to a surgeon and the date of initial consultation.7–10 Wait time 2 (T2) is defined as the period between booking of the surgical procedure and the date of the actual procedure.7,9,10 Finally, the interval wait time (Ti) is the time between initial surgical consultation and the date of booking for the procedure. In this time period, there are usually further assessments, diagnostic tests, and imaging studies that are required by the surgeon to determine patient appropriateness, confirm diagnosis, and assess the extent of disease or facilitate surgical planning.

Patient population

We performed a retrospective analysis of cohort data collected for adult patients (≥ 18 yr) who underwent elective spinal surgery for degenerative disease at a quaternary high-volume centre in the province of British Columbia, Canada, between Jan. 1, 2009, and Dec. 31, 2020. The institution included in this study serves a population of 5.1 million individuals across a wide demographic area. A total of 11 568 clinic consultations occurred during the study period, although 8928 patients were excluded from the analysis owing to nondegenerative spinal pathology, hospital admission on an emergent basis, or both. Of the remaining 2640 patients who were admitted electively for degenerative spine surgery, 599 patients were further excluded from the study analysis owing to incomplete data regarding wait-time intervals. For inclusion within the study, all 3 wait-time intervals (T1, Ti, and T2) had to have been available at the time of analysis. A total of 2041 patients met inclusion. As the COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately affected hospital and clinic operations, we omitted data from 2021 and onward from the analysis.

Primary and secondary end points

We collected wait-time category data (T1, Ti, T2) for the entire cohort. The primary outcome of the study was wait time in days for T1, Ti, and T2, as well as total wait time, which was a summation of these intervals (T1, Ti, and T2). Our secondary outcome included LOS for post-operative admission in days, excluding rehabilitation or long-term care stays, and perioperative adverse events (yes or no). Intraoperative and postoperative adverse events were reported using the validated Spinal Adverse Events Severity System (SAVES) version 2 (Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.000625/tab-related-content).7 Of note, in 2017, the treating institution developed a Rapid Access Clinic for all referrals that were led by advanced practice physiotherapists (APPs) who received specific training from the spinal surgeons. Patients with nonemergent spinal conditions who were referred to the Rapid Access Clinic had the opportunity to meet with APPs for an in-person clinical assessment. Advanced practice physiotherapists assess and triage which patients may benefit from surgical consultation, those who may require ongoing monitoring but do not yet need surgical intervention, and those who present with nonlocalizing spine symptoms that do not require further follow-up. The implementation of the Rapid Access Clinic in 2017 improved the quality of referrals to spinal surgeons and ultimately decreased T1 times.11,12

Covariates

Demographic and clinical data were available through an established prospective cohort and included patient age, sex, and LOS. Patients were discharged as per local protocol when they met discharge criteria and had been cleared by a multidisciplinary team.

Statistics

We used descriptive statistics for demographics. We summarized categorical variables with frequency and percentage, and continuous variables using n, means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs). We calculated average wait times using medians and IQRs for all wait-time intervals. We identified adverse events through SAVES.

We modelled longitudinal trends in wait-time intervals during the study period by linear regression. Results were unadjusted. We analyzed longitudinal trends in surgical volume by calculating the percent change in cases performed for each year compared with those in the year before. We calculated this for each year between 2010 and 2019, inclusive, to generate the mean percentage change in annual surgical volume for the study period.

We used multivariable regression models to evaluate the association between wait times and secondary outcomes. We used Poisson regression to model the association of wait times and number of adverse events (wait times were divided and presented by increments of 100 days). This allowed for an interpretable scale where multiplicative effect on the expected count was per each additional 100 days in wait time. Effects were expressed as expected count ratios and adjusted for age and sex. We performed additional sensitivity analysis considering adverse events as binary (i.e., having any adverse event or not), via logistic regression with wait times divided and presented by increments of 100 days. Effects were expressed as odds ratios. Finally, we treated LOS as a continuous variable and modelled it by linear regression using log-transformed LOS.

Effects were expressed as additive regression coefficients in the log-outcome scale. We ascertained linear model fit via normal quantile–quantile (Q-Q) plots of the standardized residuals. We assessed logistic model fit via the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.3 We performed analyses using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board.

Results

Patient demographics

In total, 2041 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Figure 2). The median age was 58 (IQR 46 to 69) years. The median T1 was 60 (IQR 20 to 403) days, the median Ti was 99 (IQR 22 to 412) days, the median T2 was 57 (IQR 22 to 132) days, and the median total wait time was 407 (IQR 158 to 1239) days. Of these patients, and given the retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data in this study, a subset of 1424 patients had documented adverse events. In this subset, the data showed that 323 of 1424 (22.7%) patients experienced at least 1 perioperative adverse event. The remainder of our analysis regarding longitudinal changes in wait times is based on the overall cohort, which includes our primary cohort of 2041 patients. The median LOS was 3 (IQR 1 to 6) days. Longitudinal changes in wait times

Between 2009 and 2020, total wait time increased by 5.6% annually (p < 0.001). The relative increase between 2009 and 2019 was 72.4% (Figure 3), meaning that a patient referred by their family physician in 2009 with an anticipated 12-month total wait time for surgery would have waited 20 months in 2019. The T1 wait time decreased by 4.8% annually (p < 0.001; Figure 4), likely owing to the institution of the Rapid Access Spine Clinic at the study site in 2017. Compared with 2009, the relative change was a 38.9% decrease in waiting time to see a specialist in 2019. The Ti wait time increased 14.9% annually (p < 0.001), resulting in a 301.1% increase in 2019 compared with 2009. This means that a patient who would have waited 6 months in 2009 for interim investigations before surgical booking would have waited 24 months in 2019. The T2 wait time increased 8.5% annually (p < 0.001), corresponding to a 126.1% longer T2 wait in 2019 than in 2009.

The clinic facilitated the decrease in T1 wait time by triaging and diverting nonurgent patients from surgical consultation wait-lists so they could meet with a spine physiotherapist or clinician who specializes in conservative management.

Annual surgical volume

Between 2009 and 2020, the number of surgeries for degenerative spinal disease performed at this institution has consistently increased. An average increase of 11.5% per year was observed between 2009 and 2019. In total, 98 surgeries were performed in 2009, whereas 268 surgeries were performed in 2019 (Figure 5). As can be seen, the number of cases dramatically decreased in 2020 owing to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Wait-time influence on perioperative outcomes

Total wait time (T1+Ti+T2)

An increase in total wait time was associated with an increase in adverse events and in LOS. For every additional 100 days in total wait, the number of adverse events increased 1.3% (p < 0.001; Table 2) and the odds of having an adverse event increased 1.6% (p < 0.001; Figure 6). For every additional 100 days in total wait time, the LOS increased 1.3% (p < 0.001; Table 3).

T1 wait time

With respect to T1, we observed a significant association between longer T1 and more adverse events, at a rate of 0.9% increase per 100 days (p = 0.019; Table 2). We saw no association with the odds of an adverse event (p = 0.074, Figure 6). An increase in T1 wait time was associated with an increase in LOS. For every additional 100 days in T1 wait time, the LOS increased 1.2% (p < 0.001; Table 3).

Ti wait time

An increase in Ti was associated with an increase in adverse events and in LOS. For every additional 100 days in Ti wait time, the number of adverse events increased 2.5% (p < 0.001; Table 2) and the chance or odds of having an adverse event increased 3.3% (p < 0.001; Figure 6). For every 100 additional days in Ti wait time, the LOS increased 1.8% (p < 0.001; Table 3).

T2 wait time

Interestingly, we observed no association between T2 wait times and the number of adverse events (p = 0.139; Table 2), but there was a significant association with increased odds of having an adverse event. For every 100 additional days in T2 wait time, the odds of an adverse event increased by 12.2% (p = 0.019; Figure 6). An increase in T2 wait time was associated with an increase in LOS. For every additional 100 days in T2 wait time, the LOS increased by 10.8% (p < 0.001; Table 3).

Discussion

This study characterizes the trends in wait times for degenerative spinal disease at a major spinal centre in Canada. Further analysis showed the association of specific wait-time intervals and early postoperative outcomes. Whereas surgeries for other degenerative diseases like knee and hip arthritis have been designated as priority procedures by the Canadian Institute for Health Information, degenerative spinal surgery has not yet received the same attention. With the shift in population demographics and an increasing population of older adults, it is critical that we assess how a publicly funded, single-payer health care system will manage this increasing burden. The results of this study demonstrate that the total wait time for degenerative spine surgery increased by 5.6% annually between 2009 and 2020. Furthermore, the wait-time interval with the largest increase was Ti, at a rate of 14.9% increase per year. In other words, there was an astounding 301.1% increase in Ti between 2009 and 2019. This shows that the increasing demand for spinal assessment and treatment is not being addressed adequately by current health care infrastructure, despite the findings of increased surgical volumes completed per year (Figure 4).

The largest increase observed in Ti wait time may be a result of the increasing delays and systemic barriers in obtaining necessary investigations, such as diagnostic and therapeutic injections, and computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for decision-making and surgical planning. The 2013 Canadian Association of Radiologists and 2020 British Columbia Guidelines for MRI both suggest a maximum of 60 days’ wait for an MRI for patients with chronic, persistent, painful, and severe spine symptoms for which surgery is considered.13,14 However, data from the Canadian Institute for Health Information show that only 50.0% of patients in BC receive their diagnostic MRI scans for all body parts within 43 days from the date of requisition, with most patients receiving their scans within 208 days, far exceeding the recommended time frame.7 Future resource planning may consider improving accessibility and availability of outpatient MRI to optimize imaging centre workflows and help reduce Ti wait times. Further, the use of imaging-appropriateness checklists for spinal pathology has been shown to decrease MRI demand and avoid unnecessary imaging, ultimately shortening wait-lists for time-intensive scans.15,16 Increased Ti wait times may also be related to the usage of spinal injections for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. One 2013 study that analyzed wait times for 91 patients with radiculopathy reported a mean wait of 54.5 days for a transforaminal epidural injection in individuals who eventually underwent surgery.17 Timely access to a trial of epidural injection can pose additional barriers and contribute to delays in surgical treatment.

Initiatives to reduce T1 wait times are commonly promoted by health authorities and policy-makers. In our study, T1 decreased by 4.8% annually (p < 0.001). The decrease is most notable in the years since 2017 and can be explained by the introduction of a Rapid Access Spine Clinic at the institution. Patients with degenerative spine pathology were first screened by APPs before consultation with the surgeon. This reduced T1 through nonurgent referral assessment by the physiotherapy team and diverting patients with non-spinal-related symptoms away from the surgical consultation wait-list. Similar programs have been established internationally with similar success for a number of musculosketal-related disorders in reducing wait times, improving patient satisfaction, and decreasing health care costs.11 For example, a 2023 provincial audit of the Saskatchewan Spine Pathway clinic18,19 revealed that fewer than 20.0% of patients seen at the multidisciplinary clinic went on to require surgical consultation.20 These numbers, combined with our experience, show that multidisciplinary clinics improve health care efficiency, and patients with operative spine disease experience shorter wait times to surgical consultation (i.e., T1).

Our study extends previous literature reporting that longer wait times have clinical implications. Previous studies have demonstrated that increased wait times are associated with diminished functional improvement after surgery.3,5,6 Our results show that longer wait times are associated with an increase in early perioperative adverse events. For every additional 100 days in total wait time, the odds of experiencing an adverse event increased by 1.6% (p < 0.001). Additionally, for every additional 100 days of Ti, odds of an adverse event increased 3.3% (p < 0.001). This may be related to prolonged immobility, deconditioning, and disease progression. More advanced disease at the time of operation increases surgical complexity and is associated with poorer outcomes. It may also be a consequence of prolonged pain medication use and higher dosages of opioid pain medication requirements, resulting in more complicated recovery protocols.21 To reduce peri-operative morbidity, strategies aimed at reducing wait times for spinal surgery are essential, and should involve publicly funded government initiatives such as APPs or central intake systems.

Increases across all wait-time intervals were associated with longer LOS. However, an increase in T2 wait time demonstrated the largest effect by increasing LOS by 10.8% for every additional 100 days waited. This may be explained by the fact that patients booked for surgery commonly experience severe symptoms affecting their mobility and functional status. Patients waiting longer for surgery are exposed to a longer morbidity period caused by the untreated disease and are likely experiencing worsening symptoms and poorer mobility as they wait. These patients likely require a longer postoperative hospital course for pain and mobility management before safe discharge. This is consistent with a previous Canadian study for joint osteoarthritis that showed that wait times longer than 180 days were associated with increased LOS for elective total knee arthroplasty.22 Therefore, in the aim of improving health care resource utilization, efforts should continue to focus on reducing wait times, with specific emphasis on reducing T2 (i.e., those patients who have a condition severe enough to warrant surgery). Some solutions may include hiring more spine specialists or having more dedicated operating capacity for elective spine surgery.

Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insight, our findings have to be interpreted in the context of the study design. A strength of this analysis is the large volume of data, which encompasses a 10-year period at a large academic and high-volume facility. However, this was a single-centre study, which may affect the generalizability of our results. Our institution is also centralized to receive patients with spinal cord injuries, spinal deformity, and spinal oncology. These factors may intermittently limit access to the operating room and bed availability, causing cancellations and further delays for patients undergoing elective degenerative spinal surgery.

The heterogeneity of degenerative spinal disease was not captured and is another limitation of this study. Ranging from single-level discectomy to multilevel decompression and instrumentation, degenerative spinal pathologies have a wide range of expected operative durations. Patients with degenerative diagnoses requiring shorter operations can be accommodated more easily with regard to logistical planning of elective slates. The downstream effect is likely a shorter wait time for those with single-level or simple degenerative pathologies, although case complexity and operative duration were not accounted for in our study.

Finally, our study was retrospective in design; therefore, information on long-term postoperative clinical outcomes was not available at the time of analysis. However, this has been examined in a separate study that analyzed 1-year postoperative outcomes for various wait-time benchmarks and found that longer wait times negatively affect long-term outcomes.5

Conclusion

Cumulative wait times (T1+Ti+T2) for elective spine surgery significantly increased between 2009 and 2020. The Ti wait showed the largest increase over the study period and was associated with a significant increase in adverse events and prolonged LOS. This study illustrates the overload of the health care system and identifies Ti and T2 as immediate targets for reducing perioperative morbidity and resource optimization. Future studies should investigate the impact of a number of well-established strategies, such as APP utilization and central referral systems, to improve wait times, postoperative outcomes, and resource allocation for degenerative spinal conditions.
