Harmonized Time-Series Supply and Use Tables for sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Emmanuel Mensah, Gaaitzen de Vries
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Africa Supply and Use Tables (ASUT) dataset provides harmonized annual tables for eleven sub-Saharan African countries from 1990 to 2019. Official supply and use tables are benchmarked to national accounts and trade statistics and harmonized across 20 sectors of the total economy. Consistent time series information is provided on production, intermediate use disaggregated into domestic and imported components, trade flows, and final demand. The dataset supports applications in value-added trade measurement, jobs related to exports, and studies accounting for inter-industry linkages. It is designed to facilitate comparative research on structural transformation, trade integration, and economic development in African economies.

Background & Summary

The export of goods and services to meet foreign demand has long been viewed as a central driver of growth and poverty reduction. In several Asian economies, including South Korea and China, export-led strategies not only accelerated economic expansion but also acted as a catalyst for structural transformation, spurring industrialization. Recent work by Goldberg and Reed1 emphasizes the centrality of demand in the development process, arguing that modern production technologies require scale to be viable. For producers in developing countries, this implies the need to access high-income foreign markets or cultivate an expanding domestic middle class capable of sustaining demand-led growth.

Assessing the socio-economic implications of globalization requires addressing production, consumption and trade patterns. These analyses are typically conducted using national or multiregional input-output databases. Currently, a wide variety of input-output databases exist, each of which with different features and built using different methodological approaches and data sources (some of the most widely used ones are described in Tukker and Dietzenbacher2 and Inomata and Owen3). However, comparative analysis for sub-Saharan African countries is often missing or underrepresented in such analyses. The 2023 release of the OECD Inter-Country Input-Output Tables (ICIOTs) includes just five sub-Saharan African countries: Cameroon, Ivory Coast, Nigeria, Senegal, and South Africa. The Global Trade Analysis Project (GTAP) project provides input-output tables for many SSA countries, but these need not adhere to official national account statistics and are only available for certain benchmark years, which precludes analyses of long-term trends4. CEADs provides a global multi-regional input–output (MRIO) framework, and is designed to offer broad country coverage within a consistent global system (see Huo et al.5 and Li et al.6). Another dataset, Eora, covers all countries in the world, but heavily relies on imputation methods to fill up the many blanks in data for countries with less well-developed statistical systems7. In particular, to construct input–output tables for African countries, EORA applies a generic template that combines industry–product structures observed in Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Japan, and then benchmarks this template to each country’s national accounts (see FAQs on the EORA website)7. This approach implicitly assumes that African production technologies, sector specialization, and sourcing patterns can be approximated using the cost structures of large advanced economies. That is a strong assumption: patterns of comparative advantage, sectoral specialization, and input–output linkages in those advanced economies differ markedly from those in African economies.

To this end, the Structural Transformation and Economic Growth (STEG) research initiative funded by the Foreign Commonwealth and Development Office, supported a project to build the Africa Supply and Use Tables (ASUT) dataset (see Valentinyi8 for further arguments for the need for such a dataset to advance our understanding of structural change and economic growth in African countries). The ASUT database provides annual time series of supply and use tables for eleven African economies, namely Cameroon, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Mauritius, Nigeria, Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa, Tanzania, and Zambia, from 1990 to 2019. These countries have been chosen by considering both the requirement of data availability of sufficient quality and the desire to cover a major part of economic activity in Africa. The ASUT adheres to national accounts data and has been constructed following the methods introduced by Temurshoev and Timmer9 and Dietzenbacher et al.10. The ASUT Database can be combined with satellite data, such as the employment data in the Economic Transformation Database9, to analyze the socio-economic implications of globalization.

The ASUT database, while developed to study the role of exports in income and employment creation, offers much broader applications. Its harmonized annual time series of supply, use, and input–output tables for eleven Sub-Saharan African economies enables empirical research on productivity, structural change, and inter-industry linkages, including the role of informal sectors often absent from international datasets11,12. For example, the database now makes it possible to include African countries into analyses of investment networks and their role in development12. Second, it enables the construction of PPPs for both gross output and intermediate inputs for African countries for the first time, and is essential for meaningful cross-country productivity comparisons13. Third, it allows researchers to study structural change in Africa in an open-economy setting, moving beyond the standard closed-economy treatment in the earlier literature14. Beyond research, the database can inform policy debates on industrial strategy, trade diversification, and regional integration under the African Continental Free Trade Area. Its coverage also facilitates comparative studies of development trajectories and resilience to external shocks. In addition, the ASUT can spur ongoing initiatives such as the construction of the African Continental Input-Output Table15 and provide a regional complement to the OECD’s ICIOTs. ASUT can also serve as building blocks for future MRIO compilation efforts involving African economies, including initiatives such as EORA and CEADs. Because the ASUT data are grounded in national statistics, they enhance the credibility of empirical results and strengthen the integration of African economies into input–output research frameworks.

Methods

Approach

A distinctive feature of the ASUT database is that it has been grounded in national statistics. Supply and use tables were linked with time series data on production and expenditure from the national accounts. Subsequently, domestically produced and imported intermediate and final use were distinguished. The conceptual approach closely followed established procedures9,10.

Figure 1 presents the standard structure of a Supply and a Use Table. The Supply Table records the total supply of goods and services (product 1…n), imports by product, and sectoral gross output. The Use Table details how this supply was allocated, distinguishing between intermediate consumption by sectors, final demand components, and exports. Sector value added was added at the bottom of the Use Table, ensuring that total supply equals total use.

Illustration Supply and Use Table. The grey and gold-shaded cells are the external time series data used in combination with the official supply and use tables to estimate time series SUTs using the SUT-RAS algorithm.

The gold-shaded cells represent harmonized annual time-series data from national accounts. The grey-shaded cells indicate the trade shares from BACI and SUTs, which were applied to the total trade values from national accounts. The figure shows that Supply and Use Tables are organized in a product-by-sector dimension. This structure enabled the linkage of SUTs with national accounts data (classified by sector) and international trade data (classified by product).

To construct annual SUTs, time series from the national accounts, including (gross) output and value added by sectors, total imports, total exports, and final use by category, were used as constraints in the application of the SUT-RAS method9. This approach, a bi-proportional updating technique commonly referred to as the RAS method, requires initial estimates provided by the SUT data described below. These estimates were iteratively adjusted, with the gold-shaded and grey-shaded values (national accounts data and international trade data, respectively) treated as fixed. The SUT-RAS procedure yielded harmonized annual estimates for the Supply and Use Tables.

Content and sources

Table 1 presents the content of the ASUT database. The database provides supply and use tables for eleven African economies annually for the period 1990–2019. The countries included are: Cameroon, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Mauritius, Nigeria, Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa, Tanzania, and Zambia. These countries account for about 70 percent of sub-Sahara Africa’s GDP. It includes countries from East Africa (Ethiopia, Kenya, Mauritius, Rwanda, and Tanzania), West Africa (Ghana, Nigeria, and Senegal), and Central and Southern Africa (Cameroon, South Africa and Zambia). The supply and use tables are valued at basic prices expressed in millions of national currencies. The database distinguishes the 20 main sectors of the total economy, classified in accordance with the International Standard Industrial Classification (ISIC), revision 4. These sectors include agriculture, mining, construction, utilities, nine manufacturing industries, finance, business services, personal services, trade and transport services and public services.

SUTs provide a core framework for national accounts, offering a detailed representation of production processes, the income generated from these activities, and the allocation of goods and services. They form the basis for estimating GDP through the production, income, and expenditure approaches. While these approaches are theoretically consistent and should yield identical results, discrepancies commonly arise in practice. SUTs are therefore employed to reconcile these differences and produce the official GDP estimate.

Compiling SUTs requires extensive economic surveys and detailed information on institutional and sectoral expenditure, income, and economic transactions. Collecting this information is challenging for NSIs with limited statistical capacity and resources. Nevertheless, most African countries compile and disseminate SUTs. A report by UNECA16 on the implementation of the 2008 system of national accounts showed that out of the 54 African countries, 37 countries compile supply and use tables. Out of the 37 countries, 22 compile SUTs annually. The 22 countries that compile SUTs annually are members of the Observatoire economique et statistique d’Afrique Subsaharienne (AFRISTAT), which includes two countries of the ASUT database, Cameroon and Senegal. The NSIs that are members of AFRISTAT use the ERETES module, which is a software package for national accountants developed by INSEE and Eurostat. The ERETES software provides a full integration of concepts, definitions and methods for compiling national accounts. AFRISTAT member states have a common nomenclature for economic activities (NAEMA) and for products (NOPEMA). This serves as a crucial tool for member states to harmonize their statistical work, particularly when collecting data and compiling national accounts. These classification systems are rooted in the United Nations International Nomenclatures, such as the International Standard Industrial Classification of All Economic Activities (ISIC Rev 4) and the Central Product Classification (CPC version 2). These international standards enabled meaningful comparisons with non-French speaking African countries in our dataset. In essence, the concepts, classifications, and methodologies employed in constructing SUTs and national accounts remain consistent across countries. Benchmark SUTs are estimated every five years, making use of economic census data. Importantly, recent economic surveys cover both formal and informal economic activities, further discussed below. The benchmark SUTs are updated annually using ‘non-survey’ methods. The remaining 15 out of 37 countries compile SUTs every 5 years or over longer time intervals. South Africa has compiled benchmark SUTs at five-year intervals since 1995, corresponding to the years 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2015. For non-benchmark years, beginning in 1993, SUTs have been updated using non-survey methods. Mauritius compiles SUTs every five years, drawing on the Census of Economic Activities. By contrast, most other countries in the ASUT database compile SUTs at ten-year intervals. For example, Nigeria released its 2010 SUT and is currently compiling the 2020 edition. Both benchmark and updated SUTs, as summarized in Table 2, were treated as key sources in the construction of the ASUT Database.

Over time, SUTs evolve as they are compiled using updated survey frames and new methodologies. For example, the National Bureau of Statistics of Nigeria estimated the 2010 SUT in accordance with the 2008 SNA and in ISIC rev. 4. It revised its survey frame to capture economic activities previously unmeasured, including new sectors such as entertainment, research, and patents and copyrights, in addition to a broader coverage of services, particularly informal services. It used a sample frame of 851,628 establishments17,18. Similarly, the Kenyan National Bureau of Statistics compiled a new SUT in 2016. It used new surveys to improve coverage of economic activities in the informal sector19. For Ghana, the 2005 SAM was based on industry information from the 2003 Industrial Survey which covered the formal industrial sector. Conversely, the 2013 SUT was constructed using the integrated business enterprise survey, which was the first non-household economic census covering all sectors, including informal economic activities20. A similar approach was followed by Rwanda21, Mauritius22, Tanzania23, and Zambia24,25 that all aimed for a comprehensive coverage of informal sector activities. For South Africa, the 2010 benchmark SUT implemented the 2008 SNA and refined the method for calculating financial intermediation services indirectly measured. In turn, the 2015 benchmark SUT refined the methods for calculating trade and transportation margins on products, for calculating net taxes on products, and for calculating trade services26.

The main sources used to construct the SUTs were economic censuses, living standard surveys, informal sector surveys, administrative data from the national tax and revenue office, and administrative data and financial statements from ministries and government agencies. The sources aimed to cover both formal and informal economic activities. We briefly outline recent approaches to SUT compilation in Zambia, Ghana, and Kenya. The 2010 SUT of Zambia was constructed using an economic census and a non-farm informal sector survey. The economic census did not cover productive activities conducted by unincorporated enterprises in the household sector that are unregistered and/or are less than a specified size in terms of employment, and that have some production for market or for own final use. The non-farm informal sector survey was conducted to cover these activities. These informal activities accounted for 33.6 percent of GDP in Zambia in 201024,25. Ghana constructed the 2013 benchmark SUT using the Integrated Business Enterprise Survey (IBES). Phase I of the IBES produced an exhaustive business register for the total economy. Phase II of the IBES sampled all firms with more than 50 employees and randomly selected representative firms which employ less than 50 workers. This survey design did not completely capture informal manufacturing activities. The IBES II data was therefore complemented by data from the non-farm enterprise module of the Ghana Living Standard Survey (GLSS) round six. According to the data from IBES and GLSS, the informal economy was estimated to account for 29.2% of GDP in Ghana in 201320. For Kenya’s 2016 Supply and Use Table, informal economic activities were measured using data from the 2016 Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprise Survey and the 2015/2016 Kenya Integrated Household Budget Survey, and the Integrated Survey of Services 2017, while formal sector activities were measured using information from the 2016 Census of Establishments and the 2016 and 2017 Census of Industrial Production. Estimation of informal manufacturing was notably improved using data from both the household budget survey and the Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises survey. Ghana, Kenya, and Zambia estimated informal activities using non-farm surveys or a combination of non-farm enterprise surveys and household surveys. For the 2011 SUT of Rwanda, the estimation of informal sector output was based on data from the 2011 Household Living Condition Survey (Enquête Intégrée des Conditions de Vie 3, see NSIR21). Whilst enterprise surveys aimed to capture data related to the economic activities of enterprises, household surveys focus on the economic circumstances and living standards of households. These differences in sources and approaches likely resulted in disparities in the accuracy of estimates of informal sector activities.

National statistical offices differ in their approach to calculating gross output and intermediate inputs. Ghana derived manufacturing gross output and intermediate input use directly from the IBES II survey for the 2013 SUT. Data was subsequently extrapolated to non-benchmark years using output and intermediate input data derived from annual financial statements of manufacturing firms obtained from the Ghana Revenue Authority20. Kenya’s National Bureau of Statistics derived manufacturing output and intermediate input use from censuses and surveys for benchmark years. For homogenous products, the statistical office used volume indexes to extrapolate gross output. For heterogeneous products, benchmark estimates were extrapolated to other years using values derived from sales turnover reported in the Monthly Survey of Industrial Production19. In contrast, Tanzania estimated manufacturing output for the 2015 benchmark year using the volume index estimated from the 2013 Census of Industrial Production and the 2015 Annual Survey of Industrial Production. Gross output at current prices was calculated by reflating gross output at constant prices using the consumer price index for corresponding industries. Next, a fixed input-output ratio was applied to gross output at constant prices to derive intermediate input in constant prices. Intermediate input in constant prices was reflated using a weighted consumer price index for industries to obtain intermediate input in current prices23.

The compilation of SUT required international trade data, which was typically sourced from customs offices. However, official customs data does not capture the pervasive smuggling and overwhelming role of informal cross-border trade in African countries. In their recent estimation of SUTs, African statistical institutes conducted cross-border informal trade surveys to adjust official customs data. For example, the 2016 SUT for Kenya incorporated improved estimates of trade using a cross-border informal trade survey19.

In a nutshell, most national statistical institutes in Africa compile supply and use tables. Progress is made to improve the accuracy of these tables by making use of new sources and methods. While this progress is welcomed, it affects the intertemporal consistency of time series SUTs. Next, we discuss the harmonization of the SUTs.

Harmonization and standardization

The aim was to estimate consistent time series supply and use tables. The supply and use tables should distinguish the main sectors of the total economy in the columns and the main products in the rows. Values should be at basic prices (expressed in millions of national currencies). This follows standard practice in the input–output literature, because basic prices correspond more closely to the production-cost concept underlying the technical coefficients used in such analysis. Basic prices exclude product taxes net of subsidies as well as trade and transport margins, which otherwise drive a wedge between what producers receive and what purchasers pay. Including these elements directly in intermediate transactions would blur the interpretation of input coefficients as production relationships and would also reduce comparability across countries and over time because differences in tax systems and distribution margins would mechanically affect measured input shares.

SUTs are typically available for a limited set of years and once released by the statistical institute, revisions are rare. National Accounts time series on the other hand are frequently revised. These revisions can be substantial, especially at the level of sectors. Most African countries revised their GDP in the 2010s in accordance with the 2008 SNA, leading to adjustments of GDP and sector data27,28. This can result in discrepancies between information from the latest version of the National Accounts and the published SUT for that year. In our approach, any revision of the National Accounts led to adaptation of the SUTs to ensure they matched. To estimate the time series SUTs, we followed the approach proposed by Dietzenbacher et al.10. It requires time series for (gross) output and value added by sector, imports and exports by product and final use by end-use category. These data acted as constraints when generating time series SUTs, using the so-called SUT–RAS method9.

Although the statistical concepts, classifications and definitions used for the construction of national SUTs are standardized, differences in products-by-industry disaggregation and price valuations were common. For example, whilst the SUTs of countries like Cameroon, Mauritius, Tanzania, and South Africa were detailed in terms of products and industries distinguished, the SUTs from countries like Ghana, Kenya and Rwanda were typically condensed with little or no details for manufacturing industries. To harmonize SUTs and ensure consistency over time, national tables were converted into a standardized format comprising 20 sectors and 20 products, using concordance tables. Table 1 lists the 20 sectors included.

A key challenge in constructing a common sectoral classification across countries was the variation in the level of disaggregation provided by NSIs. These constraints guided the choice of 20 sectors, striking a balance between retaining as much detail as possible and minimizing the extent of imputation required. In instances where a country’s Supply and Use Tables provided a more detailed breakdown, we aggregated up to the 20 products and sectors. Conversely, for countries with more condensed tables, we disaggregated the data using supplementary information from national accounts or UNIDO’s industrial statistics.

Supply was typically valued at basic prices, whereas intermediate use was valued at purchasers’ prices (except for Ethiopia, where gross output and value added were at producer prices). To obtain input-output tables (discussed below), it was essential for both the supply and use tables to adhere to a consistent pricing concept. By incorporating estimates of margins and net taxes, the data at purchasers’ prices in the use table were converted into basic prices. This involved the deduction of taxes, as well as trade and transportation margins. It required two valuation matrices, one containing product-specific trade and transportation margins and the other containing net taxes on products. The valuation matrices were not available from African NSIs, except for Tanzania, and needed to be estimated. We closely followed Dietzenbacher et al.10. First, the margins and net tax rates by product were extracted from the supply tables and preserved to the greatest extent feasible. Second, these rates were proportionally applied to the rows of the use table.

Finally, we observed country-specific idiosyncrasies. We observed statistical discrepancy between total use and total supply for some countries. These discrepancies could be substantial, especially for the 1990s. For example, in the 1993 SUT of South Africa, the discrepancy was up to 11% of total use for agricultural products. This was labelled as a “residual” in the use table. Similar discrepancies by industry were observed for Nigeria. We distributed the residuals proportionally across final demand categories for each product, a procedure proposed by Dietzenbacher et al.10, such that the estimation of the other elements in the SUTs would not be greatly affected. The supplementary file outlines the detailed step-by-step procedures used for each country to transform raw SUTs from the NSIs into 20-product by 20-industry SUTs. These procedures produced the benchmark SUTs contained in the input files used to estimate the SUT time series. In the Data Records section, we provide further information on the input files and the programs to reproduce the SUT time series.

We harmonized national account data used in the benchmarking of the SUTs. The SUTs were linked to consistent national account data on value added, output, and expenditure as well as product level trade data for 1990–2019. The national account data on value added was taken from the Economic Transformation Database (ETD)29. The ETD, combined with the 2-digit manufacturing database29, provides consistent time series of value added for the 20 sectors. The ETD was created through a thorough investigation of the availability and reliability of statistical sources on a country-specific basis. Recent estimates of sectoral value added which covers both formal and informal activities were used and linked to historical series. This linking procedure adjusts the levels for years in which informal activities are undercounted to reflect current estimates while maintaining historical growth rates. Moreover, international consistency was achieved by adhering to the System of National Accounts for value added, which is consistent with the classification used in SUTs.

Gross output by sector were often not provided in the national accounts. To construct a consistent time series for sectoral gross output, we acquired data from NSIs for recent years, which was generally consistent with the output data from official SUTs. The NSIs also report sectoral output data in the UNSD National Accounts Main Aggregates Database (UN NAMA), which may include UNSD Secretariat estimates where official country data are not available or require harmonization. To ensure consistency between gross output, value added, and intermediate inputs, we used output data from the SUTs or NSIs as benchmark values. We interpolated or extrapolated from benchmark years using the historical series reported in the UN NAMA. For manufacturing industries, we extrapolated from benchmark years using output data from UNIDO INDSTAT (2022 version). This approach adjusted output levels in non-benchmark years to reflect recent estimates covering both formal and informal activities while maintaining the historical growth rate. This adjustment ensured consistency with the SUTs and value added data.

We obtained data for final use categories from the Penn World Tables, version 10. These final use categories are household consumption inclusive of consumption by nonprofit organizations serving households, government consumption, gross fixed capital formation, exports, imports, and changes in inventories. These final demand categories add up to the GDP as calculated from the expenditure side. These expenditure categories were harmonized in a way that is consistent across countries and over time (see Feenstra et al.30).

Finally, we harmonized trade data accounting for two fundamental issues i) cross-border informal trade and ii) inappropriate product classification. Official customs data do not account for smuggling and cross-border informal trade. However, as discussed above, most NSIs estimated cross-border informal trade using supplementary survey data when constructing the SUTs. Therefore, we used trade data from SUTs as benchmark and extrapolated from benchmark years using trade data from the BACI database31. A second issue we dealt with was the likely misclassification over time of products that are closely related. For example, for Ghana, trade in mining and basic metal products often showed erratic patterns suggesting products were misclassified in the reporting. The share of mining in total goods exports increased from 3% in 2010 to 21% in 2011, whereas the export share of basic metal manufactures fell from 48% to 31% between 2010 and 2011. Our investigation revealed the mining of gold and other precious metals, which should be classified under mining, and the production and refining of precious metals such as gold, which should be classified under the manufacture of basic precious and other non-ferrous metals, were often mixed up in the reported UN commodities trade database (and BACI by extension). To harmonize and address such erratic patterns, we used the trade data from official SUTs as benchmark level estimates. We analyzed trade data from the BACI database to identify erratic patterns in product pairs. By summing trade data from the two products that mirrored each other plausibly due to misclassification, we used the growth trend derived from this aggregated value to extrapolate from benchmark years for these product pairs. This methodology yielded trade shares that were consistent with benchmark SUTs and also improved consistency over time. Finally, in most countries, purchases on the domestic territory by non-residents are recorded as a negative item under household consumption and as a corresponding positive item under exports. Following Dietzenbacher10, we did not retain the offsetting entries separately in the benchmark table; instead, we netted out the negative household consumption item and recorded the corresponding amount under exports. The same treatment was applied symmetrically to direct purchases abroad by residents on the import side.

The harmonization of national accounts data on a country-by-country basis is outlined in the supplementary file. The raw data files in the replication package, together with the procedures outlined in the supplementary file, allow one to reproduce the consistent national accounts data in the input files used in the estimation program.

Splitting intermediate use into domestic and imported components

The estimated time series SUTs do not distinguish between domestically produced and imported use. Panel b of Fig. 1 shows the use of each product (both locally produced and imported) by each sector and final use categories (consumption by households and government, investments, inventories, and gross exports). The distinction between domestically produced and imported intermediate use is essential for key research applications. For example, Los et al.32 show that domestic value-added in gross exports and the measure of vertical specialization introduced by Hummels et al.33 can be computed from national tables that distinguish domestic and imported intermediate inputs.

We distinguished between domestically produced and imported goods in both intermediate and final use, following the approach of Dietzenbacher et al.10 and Pahl34. For each product, the share of imports allocated to intermediate consumption, final consumption, and gross fixed capital formation (the end-use categories) was determined using the Broad Economic Categories classification. Within each end-use category, allocation was based on a proportionality assumption. Accordingly, when distributing intermediate use across sectors, consistent ratios between imported use and total use were applied. Unlike the standard proportionality assumption, this approach allowed country import shares to vary across end-use categories, while remaining constant within each category. After the split, for each sector in the use table, gross output is equal to the sum of imported intermediate use, domestic intermediate use, and sector value added.

Technical Validation

We validate the ASUT dataset using two approaches. The first approach evaluates how closely the SUT-RAS-based SUT estimates match actual supply and use tables. The second compares empirical outcomes derived from the ASUT dataset with those obtained from an alternative database.

The availability of supply and use tables from NSIs varies across countries, and for most countries they are not produced annually. In years where we did not have supply and use tables, the SUT-RAS approach estimated supply and use tables using information from the official tables and national accounts data. To evaluate how close these estimated SUTs are to the actual production structure of African economies, we calculate three indicators that show how the estimated SUTs may deviate from their true values (see Temushoev and Timmer9).

We apply the three indicators to Cameroon. Cameroon is one of the few countries in our sample with annual supply and use tables from the early 1990s through 2020. This allows us to drop selected years, re-estimate the missing-year SUTs using SUT-RAS, and then compare these estimates to the actual tables. In doing so, we assess how well SUTRAS recovers the actual production structure.

We evaluate how well the estimated 2019 Cameroon SUTs reproduce the actual 2019 SUTs reported by the Cameroon NSI, using the three matrix deviation indicators above: WAPE, SWAD, and PSI. We do this under three scenarios that differ in how far the nearest table is from 2019: one year away (2018), three years away (2016), and five years away (2014). Intuitively, a lower score on any of these indicators means the estimated table is closer to the actual 2019 table. We report results both for (i) the supply matrix (panel A), and (ii) all matrices combined, namely supply, use, and final demand (panel B).

Starting with the make matrix in panel A of Table 3, the table shows WAPE values of about 3.3% when the actual table is one year away, 2.7% when the table is three years away, and 6.6% when the table is five years away. SWAD and PSI follow the same pattern: they are low (around 0.01 and 0.03) for the one-year and three-year gaps, but roughly triple and double (to about 0.03 and 0.06), respectively, once the actual table is five years apart. The key point is that projecting the 2019 SUT from a 2014 benchmark does not produce a systematic deviation from the actual supply structure, but the estimates begin to drift in several economically relevant cells. But with a benchmark only one or three years apart, the estimated 2019 supply table of Cameroon is quite close to the actual table. The fact that the three-year case performs slightly better than the one-year case suggests that projection quality is not purely a mechanical function of “closer is always better,” but also depends on how “good” the supply table is in the benchmark year we are using. In other words, if 2018 was itself somewhat atypical (e.g. shocks to particular sectors), then anchoring the update on 2016 can fit 2019 slightly better than anchoring on 2018.

When we add trade data, first imports (make matrix + xM) and then both imports and exports (make matrix + xM + xX), the fit improves. For example, with a one-year gap, WAPE falls from 3.28% to 2.95%, and PSI stays around 0.03. With a five-year gap, including imports and exports cuts WAPE from 6.61% to about 5.96%. This is economically intuitive: as indicated in Fig. 1, trade flows are among the few components we can usually observe annually with some reliability, so feeding them in as constraints prevents the algorithm from “guessing” implausible reallocations of demand across domestic vs. foreign sources. Our results are consistent with what Temurshoev and Timmer9 find for Spain: providing the SUT-RAS procedure actual import and export data as additional constraints tends to pull the estimates closer to the actual structure, and improves goodness-of-fit indicators like WAPE, SWAD, and PSI in most cases. The Cameroonian results behave in the same qualitative way: trade data helps discipline the estimates, especially when the benchmark is further apart.

The combined matrices (supply + use + final demand) in panel B show WAPE values of about 9.0% for the one-year gap, 9.1% for the three-year gap, and 15.9% for the five-year gap. SWAD rises from roughly 0.04 to 0.06 as we move from a one/three-year gap to a five-year gap, and PSI rises from about 0.09 to 0.15. These are larger than the indicators for the supply table in panel A. They indicate that once we estimate the full SUTs, not just who produces what, but also which industries use which inputs and how final demand is allocated, the deviation increases.

Again, adding imports (COMBINED + xM) and then imports plus exports (COMBINED + xM + xX) improves the estimate. For the five-year gap, WAPE for the combined matrices falls from 15.86% to 14.29% once we include both imports and exports. PSI similarly falls from 0.15 to 0.13.

The estimated SUTs fit the actual tables less closely than in a comparable exercise for Spain9. At five-year gaps, the indicators are roughly twice as large. Cameroon’s single-digit WAPE and low PSI values at one- and three-year gaps are encouraging, but by five years the indicators nearly double. This highlights limitations of the data38, where more accurate estimates are conditional on recent benchmark tables and external information, especially on trade flows.

In the second validation of the ASUT database, we compare empirical findings to an alternative dataset that includes few sub-Saharan African countries. In particular, the 2023 release of the OECD Inter-Country Input-Output Tables (ICIOTs) includes five sub-Saharan African nations: Cameroon, Ivory Coast, Nigeria, Senegal, and South Africa. These tables are compiled for the time span between 1995 and 2020, enabling a comparative analysis with the ASUT dataset, which pertains to the period from 1995 to 2019.
