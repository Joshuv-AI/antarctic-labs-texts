Assessing through focus groups the demand, facilitators, and barriers of a cognitive stimulation intervention for healthy older adults
Authors: Vitória F Velloso, Valentina Herrera, Iris Bomilcar, Julia de Almeida, Daniel C Mograbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith increasing life expectancy and declining birth rates, a global demographic shift toward an aging population is evident. While health organizations recommend cognitive stimulation and education to reduce cognitive decline, there is a lack of standardized protocols for implementation in health services. Focus groups involving older adults, their families, health professionals and researchers on aging (n = 32) were conducted. The data were recorded and transcribed and then analyzed via Framework Analysis. Five main themes (‘Demand’, ‘Facilitators’, ‘Barriers’, ‘Targets for stimulation’, ‘Expected outcomes’) and 23 subthemes emerged regarding the implementation of a cognitive stimulation protocol for healthy older adults. Stakeholders acknowledged the demand and emphasized the importance of social interaction and attractive presentation. They discussed multifaceted barriers, and numerous stimulation targets indicated the need for general cognitive stimulation. The primary indicators of intervention success included enhanced mood, social engagement, functionality, and cognitive health preservation. Future interventions should consider the impact of stigma on naming and advertising, pay attention to cost-effectiveness and prioritize an intervention structure and group activities to enhance social support networks and cognitive health. Background

As life expectancy increases and birth rates decrease, the world is undergoing a significant demographic transition characterized by an increasingly older population1. Projections from the United Nations2 suggest that the global population aged 65 years or older will Surpass 1.6 billion people and live 20 additional years by 2050. In response to these changes, the Plan of Action of the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing emphasizes the fundamental rights of having the highest attainable standards of physical and mental health, access to education, an adequate standard of living, particularly in the community, and involvement in public, political, and cultural life3. One recommended action is to support national authorities in using evidence-based, multisectoral action to address gaps in the response to noncommunicable diseases3.

Alzheimer’s disease and other forms of dementia are noncommunicable diseases and the second most prevalent cause of disability in the 80–84 age group and the leading cause of disability for people aged 85 and over4. The World Alzheimer Report 2023 advises that individuals continue to learn5although it does not cite any structured protocol that can be strongly recommended for the prevention of cognitive decline in older adults. For people with dementia, Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST) protocol is recommended6,7. Generally, cognitive stimulation (CS) involves engagement in a range of activities and discussions, typically in a group, aimed at the general enhancement of cognitive and social functioning8,9.

Cognitive stimulation or training, as recommended by WHO’s guidelines for reducing risk of cognitive decline and dementia, can help maintain brain health and prevent cognitive decline by improving cognitive reserve10which refers to the brain’s ability to adapt or compensate for neuropathology or damage11. Not every intervention design seems to provide the same cognitive benefits12. Although the quality of evidence may be limited at the moment10low costs and lack of side effects can be advantageous for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with better evidenced protocols. In fact, establishing widely accessible cognitive programs is a challenge due to the scarcity of high-quality evidence and heterogeneity in reported findings13.

One alternative is establishing multi-domain interventions including cognitive training (CT). An effective example in reducing cognitive decline among older adults is the FINGER protocol14,15which is currently being studied in 25 countries across high-, middle-, and low-income economies, including Brazil16. It targets individuals at an increased risk of developing dementia and has been associated with sustained cognitive benefits, evidenced by either improvements or attenuated decline in cognitive functioning, observed two years following its start. Similarly, the ECOG project is a cognitive stimulation (CS) initiative that has demonstrated promising results for cognitive outcomes in the Portuguese population17,18which shares cultural and linguistic similarities with the Brazilian population. Importantly, it also targets individuals with cognitive frailty or impairment. While these findings demonstrate the potential to maintain cognitive function through preventive strategies in at-risk populations, interventions designed for cognitively healthy older adults have yielded comparatively less robust results19.

In this sense, the literature on cognitively healthy older adults suggests that CS methods yield highly heterogeneous results, with combined small effect sizes19. Moreover, only a few systematic reviews tried to do those comparisons19. It has been found, for example, that virtual reality can enhance the outcomes of CS protocols20and that CS using leisure activities can improve self-esteem21which also suggests that comparing different CS protocols applied to different populations is not accurate. It is crucial to establish which protocol is most effective.

Furthermore, cognitive interventions, which largely originate from high-income nations22reflect a broader trend of LMICs being underrepresented in health research23. Highlighting this issue, the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean4 noted a pronounced fast aging trend in the region. Consequently, there remains a pressing need to develop interventions that are not only effective but also culturally designed for the specific needs of developing nations and feasible for large-scale implementation in LMICs’ health systems. Some evidence indicates that CS can be more effective for individuals with lower educational levels, which can be advantageous for LMICs24.

Brazil is home to over 32 million people aged 60 and above25and the rapid decline in fertility rates is expected to lead to older individuals outnumbering children and adolescents by 2030 4. The National Policy for the Comprehensive Care of People with Alzheimer’s Disease and Other Dementia26 and the Statute of the Elderly27 establish that preventative strategies are important, but there is no cognitive protocol currently applied on the Unified Health System (SUS). Cognitive training programs are available for those who can afford them, and some companies and independent professionals offer different programs based on published evidence. However, there are no options for a tested protocol.

Although there is some evidence suggesting that commercial CT can enhance cognitive performance on tests, the presence of clinically significant outcomes is still lacking sufficient evidence28. Currently, the cognitive assessment and training market is expected to reach USD 11.4 billion by this year29leading to a proliferation of cognitive training apps. Despite the appeal of equating brain training with other forms of personal ‘fitness’30>, the rapid expansion of this market has outpaced the evidence supporting these interventions.

When new alternatives are being developed to improve health and healthcare, one of the key steps is to involve stakeholders, including those who will deliver, use, and benefit from the intervention31. Engaging stakeholders allows for adjustments to be made to better align with their needs and preferences, consequently enhancing the feasibility, quality, and relevance of the study32. The use of focus groups enables researchers to gain a comprehensive understanding of stakeholders’ perspectives and to identify potential issues related to the demand and acceptability of interventions33. By examining stakeholders’ perceptions, comprehension, and appraisal of the questions posed, different models can be designed and empirically verified33.

Considering this, the current study aims to explore the demand for, facilitators of, and barriers to implementing a CS protocol for healthy older adults within the context of a Latin American LMIC, specifically Brazil. To achieve this goal, focus groups were conducted with stakeholders via semi structured interviews. The objectives were to ascertain (1) the existence of demand for such an intervention, (2) factors that could enhance its efficacy and feasibility, (3) potential barriers to implementation, (4) target areas for stimulation and (5) the population’s expectations regarding the most important outcomes for program success. It is anticipated that these data will inform the design of effective and reliable interventions for this population.

Methods

The focus groups methodology was selected to enhance comparability, because the well-established Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST)6 for persons with mild to moderate dementia frequently relies on it as a helpful resource for qualitative results and cultural adaptations34–45. Semi structured interviews were conducted to assess the demands, facilitators and barriers for the implementation of a cognitive stimulation protocol for healthy older adults in the Brazilian context. The main characteristics of the intervention to be developed are also discussed. Two complementary formats of focus groups were used: face-to-face and remote.

Recruitment

An open invitation, which involved social media outreach and pamphlets, was extended for the local community to participate in focus groups. In addition, we contacted a public day center in the city of Rio de Janeiro to interview its health professionals and healthy older adults (indicated by the local staff) using the service. The desired sample size was between 15 and 40 participants, a regular size for qualitative studies. Healthy older adults (> 60 years) who reside in the community, their family members and health professionals who work in the field of aging were eligible. The main exclusion criterion was the presence of cognitive impairment. Despite not conducting a formal test, psychologists from the team assessed cognitive health during the interviews.

Procedure

Due to the difficulty of coordinating volunteer schedules, the size of each focus group was determined based on the participants’ schedule, ranging from 2 to 10 people. Focus groups were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Informed consent was obtained from the participants. Each group was attended by at least two members of the research team, one of whom took on the role of facilitator and led the group discussion (VV or IB), whereas the others observed the group and took notes to supplement the audio data collected (VV or VH or JA). The interview schedule was established based on a set of predefined focus points, which the research team believed were crucial for the development of an intervention (Table 1). A questionnaire was created that was based on these focus points and included essential questions, with occasional additions made using a semi-structured method. A brief description of the research project and the principles of cognitive stimulation were given. The participants discussed the feasibility and demand for the planned intervention, including opinions about the modality, frequency of sessions, duration and structure for each session, duration of intervention, group size and composition, settings, possible activities, targets for stimulation and expected outcomes. The English version of the basic questionnaire can be found in Supplementary File 1. Each group lasted approximately 90 min in total.

Analyses

The analysis of the transcripts followed a systematic approach known as Framework Analysis46a common method for analyzing focus group data on healthcare interventions for older adults39,45,47–52which enhances comparability between studies. This method was used because it offers both a systematic and flexible approach that supports thematic analysis and ensures transparency through a clear supervision53. It also allows for deep researcher immersion, enhancing understanding of participants’ experiences, and can be applied with or without software53. The process involves five steps: (1) familiarization with the transcript data (2) identification of the thematic framework, (3) coding the themes, (4) charting examples and (5) interpretation of the framework46. Initially, two researchers (VV and VH) read all the transcripts to discern broad themes. These themes were gathered and refined to create a comprehensive coding key that covers both broad themes and subthemes. The transcripts were subsequently categorized and charted accordingly. Finally, a mapping and interpretation process was then performed to determine factors that will guide the design of a cognitive stimulation intervention for healthy older adults in Brazil. Saturation of information was reached when all the data have been accommodated in categories by two independent graders.

Results

Nine focus groups were conducted with healthy community-living older adults, their relatives, and multidisciplinary health professionals, including psychologists, nutritionists, physical educators, physiotherapists, professional caregivers and researchers. The greatest difficulties in organizing the groups were reaching the target audience and reconciling schedules. Most of the participants preferred to participate remotely, including the target audience. Only two groups occurred in person, with professionals and users of a day center performing cognitive stimulation work. No volunteers needed to be excluded based on our criteria. Theme 1: demand

Is there a demand for this type of program? Illustrative quotes can be seen in Table 3.

Is there a demand?

Most responses were positive. However, some participants expressed that not all older adults would be interested in participating, depending on the specific program.

Reasons

When asked about the reasons for this demand, the respondents cited the interest in maintaining cognitive health and the lack of evidence about current cognitive preventive strategies.

Theme 2: facilitators

Factors that can help with program implementation, performance, and maintenance. Illustrative quotes can be seen in Table 4.

Naming

When terms related to good health and high functionality, such as active aging and stimulation, were used, naming was mentioned as a facilitator.

Publicizing and instruction

Some respondents suggested that dissemination strategies should focus on preventive results, whereas others suggested focusing on the positive experience of meeting people and participating.

Structure of intervention

With respect to the structure of the intervention, participation in groups was quoted as a significant facilitator. In accordance with the preference of each possible participant, the modality (online or in person) was also cited as a key factor in the success of the program.

Activities

Moments of social interaction, such as walks and sharing food, drew the most interest from participants.

Accessibility

Places that are prepared with accessible distances, paths, and movements should facilitate face-to-face participation. Online access would be easier for those with good technological skills by avoiding barriers on the route.

Partnership

Partnerships with existing groups of older adults, public managers, and private institutions were mentioned as potential catalysts for implementation.

Finances

Facilitators for the financial aspects of the program would be the use of low-cost materials, the potential reduction in public health costs and the prospect of entering a niche market in the case of privately paid interventions.

Interest

According to the respondents, the prior interest of certain individuals in maintaining their functionality and the use of strategies to increase participants’ interest during sessions can be of great value in attracting and keeping the target audience.

Familiarity

Familiarity was deemed an important facilitator. Prior familiarity with other types of activity groups, professionals involved, or other cognitive activities can facilitate joining. Over time, the target audience’s familiarity with the developed program could lead to an increase in adherence.

Outcomes

The participants suggested that the benefits they would receive (or anticipate receiving) from the program would have an impact on their motivation to stay.

Theme 3: barriers

Factors that can hinder the implementation, performance and maintenance of the program. Illustrative quotes can be seen in Table 5.

Naming

When terms related to health treatments and clinical conditions, such as therapy and intervention, were used, naming was mentioned as a barrier. Moreover, the term ‘healthy older adults’ may inhibit the participation of cognitively healthy people who have some physical health issues.

Publicizing and instruction

The respondents recommended that dissemination strategies steer clear of the natural and pathological damage associated with aging, as well as direct references to terms such as ‘elderly’. In addition, there was disagreement about the utilization of digital media to reach the intended audience.

Structure of intervention

The long duration and high frequency of the intervention were perceived as barriers due to potential conflicts with other activities of the participants. For older adults who lack autonomy using digital technologies, the remote mode was perceived as an obstacle.

Activities

Activities that are not acceptable include those that are infantilized, based on very recent references, have only one possible answer, are excessively difficult, can cause embarrassment or require skills that the person lacks. Predicting the negative effects of an activity is not always possible, and the team needs to be prepared to handle these situations.

Accessibility

The most significant hindrances to face-to-face participation are large displacements, inadequate transportation, urban insecurity, and disregard for older citizens’ rights. Large and heterogeneous groups can make it difficult to plan activities that are accessible to all.

Partnership

The lack of specialized private agents who are interested in working with this target audience and the complexity of public agencies make establishing partnerships for implementation difficult.

Finances

The greatest financial challenge is finding a balance between the program’s need to generate resources (and profit, in a private model) and the financial reality of older people, living off their pensions.

Recognition and/or prioritization of demand

Even when demand exists, it is often not recognized or prioritized by the target audience or the agents who can meet it.

Interest

The participants noted that the target audience’s lack of interest, either because of a lack of demand recognition or stigma surrounding psychosocial programs, can act as a significant obstacle to its implementation.

Screening for healthy older adults

Finding and screening healthy older adults for the program is a barrier, as they often decline during the program or retain some cognitive abilities but not others. Furthermore, a significant number of older individuals are already experiencing the long-term consequences of unhealthy lifestyles.

Theme 4: targets for stimulation

What would be the most important targets to stimulate and/or measure this type of program? The respondents reported 14 targets, including global cognition, attention, memory, reasoning, executive functions, speed of processing, language, orientation, subjective cognitive performance, ADLs and IADLs, social skills, behavior, mood and self-awareness. Examples of quotes were not illustrated because of the often-vague form in which nonprofessional respondents mentioned each function. To improve clarity, we present only the primary function associated with the skill that respondents aim to improve. Furthermore, several functions were merely only nominally cited by professionals, lacking sufficient elaboration for demonstration.

Theme 5: expected outcomes

The benefits and outcomes that respondents want to see at the end of the program. Illustrative quotes can be seen in Table 6.

Participation

It was noted that people’s constant and enjoyable participation benefits itself.

Improved mood

Mood improvement was highlighted as a major outcome, both for itself and its importance in protecting cognition.

Socialization

The expansion of opportunities for socialization, the development of new relationships and partnerships, and the strengthening of support networks were highlighted as beneficial outcomes for both the older adults and the social circles around them.

Cognitive health

Properly demonstrating the maintenance of cognitive health was indicated as important for the intended social impact.

Quality of life

The respondents stated that they would like to achieve a better quality of life as a result.

Functionality maintenance

Maintaining functionality and autonomy for daily activities is a desirable outcome.

Learning new things

Expectations to learn new things and listen to different points of view.

Discussion

The present study aimed to assess the demand and feasibility of a preventive cognitive stimulation program in an LMIC by exploring the potential barriers, facilitators, expectations and characteristics required for its implementation and effectiveness. Different model possibilities for the program led to numerous questions about facilitators, barriers, and stimulation targets, as well as varied expectations about possible results. Indeed, five themes (i.e., Demand, Facilitators, Barriers, Targets for Stimulation and Expected Outcomes) and twenty-three subthemes were identified consistently across the interviews.

There was an agreement among health professionals, researchers, older adults and their families that there is demand for this type of cognitive stimulation program. This is a significant contribution to the literature, as, to the best of our knowledge, no other studies have previously explored this demand from the personal perspective of the target audience, especially in developing regions. Although there is evidence that cognitive interventions can improve cognition and mood22,54respondents cited a current lack of access to reliable interventions. However, it should be noted that the older population has a variety of profiles and interests based on individual differences such as age, culture, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, work, literacy, health and functional status, family structure, sexual orientation and gender identity55. The fact that there is demand from respondents does not mean that everyone is interested.

Both facilitators and barriers were identified for naming and publicizing and instruction, depending on their features. A main barrier is that mentioning cognitive decline or disease can lead to public disinterest, even in the context of prevention. Consistent with the literature on the search for mental health services in general, stigma, high costs, and negative perceptions of mental health services appear to be central obstacles for older adults56. Similarly, Fernandes et al.57 reported that stigma is a barrier to engaging in fall prevention strategies, as older adults often associate falls with the necessity of institutional care, a concern that can be extended to interventions aimed at preventing cognitive decline. Although some argue that fear appeals effectively influence attitudes, intentions, and behaviors58promoting efficacy59 or hope60 may be more effective than emphasizing threats.

Regarding the structure of intervention, activities, and accessibility, which have been noted as both facilitators and barriers, it seems that any design option of the program will hinder the access of part of the population, whether face-to-face or online, with activities that are either very easy or very difficult. For example, although in-person participation was more common for the program in general, most people preferred to participate online in the interviews because of schedule difficulties. Fernandes et al57. reported similar outcomes, in which participants expressed a desire for personalized interventions that met their specific needs. A program with guidelines that can be adapted to different groups within the older population could act as a facilitator. Nonetheless, although stakeholders note that remote interventions could mitigate transportation issues, it is important to highlight that individuals with lower socioeconomic status and older age, who experience greater mobility difficulties, are less likely to engage in telehealth interventions61. Online healthcare may also exacerbate health disparities for those lacking digital literacy or internet access62.

Generating interest in the program appeared both as a challenge and as a facilitator. Strategies that prioritize personal development and positive emotions while also being compatible with participants’ daily lives appear to be the most desirable. Familiarity was found to act as a facilitator, demonstrating that including professionals, activities, or places that are familiar to participants can be an effective method of stimulating their interest. While some may believe that excessive familiarity can be harmful, as novelty can increase motivation and increase learning and memory63recent research suggests that the beneficial effects may be more pronounced in children and young adults64. Furthermore, it appears that older adults have a stronger tendency to remember stimuli that they like more than younger adults do65.

Additionally, these recommendations could be extended to group interventions based on leisure activities, which also have the potential to protect cognitive health66–68. For a cognitive stimulation program to demonstrate advantages over these strategies, it must show superior benefits in terms of health outcomes, financial efficiency, or applicability in public policies. Recognized positive outcomes, influential partnerships, and high cost-effectiveness were also identified as potential facilitators. However, the financial burden associated with retirement remains a major barrier, for which low costs appear to be an effective solution. The cost‒benefit aspect is crucial in facilitating partnerships with professionals and public and private institutions; neglecting this factor could prevent program implementation. Biedenweg et al.69 emphasized that low costs are a significant motivator for older adults to engage in health interventions. Nevertheless, health system choices are often influenced by conflicts of interest among various stakeholders when partnerships are established70. Therefore, there is no certainty that favorable cost‒benefit and positive outcomes will suffice to overcome competition in this field.

Recognition and/or prioritization of demand and screening for healthy older adults appeared to be barriers, both of which are linked to the health worker/participant relationship. A gap has been found between the services provided and the needs of older adults, which is evident in their lack of participation71. The perception of functional decline as an inevitable part of the aging process and the notion of services being neither accessible nor acceptable in the context of complex health and psychosocial needs were both reasons for this. The significance of trust, proximity, and presence was highlighted by health care professionals, who work to improve the acceptability and accessibility of health promotion services71which is in accordance with the findings of the present study.

While global cognition, memory and attention are frequently mentioned, we did not find a consensus on the best targets for stimulation. This is consistent with the typical definition of cognitive stimulation, which is aimed at the general enhancement of cognitive and social functioning8,9. The interviewees had a higher degree of certainty about their expected results, demonstrating greater certainty about the end than the means. The expected outcomes were linked and interrelated, including participation, enhanced mood, socialization, cognitive health, functional maintenance, quality of life, and learning new things. The factors that influence effectiveness cannot be properly evaluated without performing the first step, which is to guarantee the expressive participation of the population.

Notably, socialization appeared both as a desirable activity and as an expected result of stimulation. Cognitive health, functionality maintenance and enhanced mood appeared both as targets of stimulation and as expected results. In interpreting these facts, it seems that there is a desire for group activities to strengthen social networks, maintain cognitive health, enhance mood, and preserve autonomy. Silva et al.72 analyzed the evidence for this in a systematic review, highlighting the positive outcomes of cognitive interventions on quality of life and reducing depressive symptoms in older and mature adults without dementia or depression. In addition, Sommerlad et al.73 collected observational evidence suggesting that a lower level of engagement in social activities is linked to a greater relative risk of dementia. Initial evidence has shown that group social interventions can improve cognition, but there is no evidence regarding dementia prevention, and interventions that have had an effect on cognition may not continue to delay or prevent dementia73.

Focus groups have their own set of limitations, including the possibility of bias and manipulation by leading/dominating participants and difficulties in distinguishing between individual and group views, generalizing the results from the small focus group to the larger population74. The interviewers managed these limitations encouraging the less active volunteers to speak more. Furthermore, the small number of older adults involved limits the ability to generalize results or to create a profile of who would be more interested or better served by this demand, which would have improved the necessary information to select the ideal intervention characteristics. Although we found a great demand for this type of intervention, it is possible that only people who already believed in this demand agreed to participate in the interviews. In the future, larger experiments can be conducted to obtain a more representative sample. This may involve refining recruitment strategies – for example, by engaging with pre-existing networks of older adults – to reduce sampling biases.

The specific needs of other LMIC countries should be considered by local researchers when following or not our suggestions, as all participants were Brazilian. For example, the Brazilian population is anecdotally known for being especially receptive and welcoming, which may influence their preference for group programs. In addition, due to most of the sample being from a big metropolis, barriers and limitations could not accurately represent the reality of people living in other regions. Brazil encompasses an immense diversity of cultural, musical, artistic, and religious references that vary widely in each region. To test for national distribution in Brazil, any protocol to be studied must either be tested in multicenter or have its adaptation assessed when integrated into the health system of other regions, particularly the most distant and socioeconomically divergent regions.

Although the findings of the current study may inform initiatives in other LMICs, preferences may vary according to cultural differences. For instance, the use of the term ‘elderly’ and other terms associated with a negative – or even overly positive - image of aging were identified as a potential obstacle for participants to participate in the program, considering the stigma surrounding aging in Brazil. For example, 81% of Brazilian older adults believe that there is prejudice against them, and 45% believe that this prejudice is extensive75. Conversely, in other LMICs, Such as India, where only 10% of older adults perceive their age as the primary cause of discrimination76, aging may be viewed more positively, which could differently influence the reception of related programs.

Conclusions

The need for a preventive cognitive stimulation program in developing regions was highlighted by the Brazilian stakeholders, who expressed the lack of evidence-based options for those looking to maintain cognitive health. The primary aim of this article was to serve as a guide for research groups in LMICs who want to design programs that promote cognitive health for older adults. Based on this Framework Analysis, we recommend that new interventions focus on developing attractive promotional materials and intervention designs targeted at addressing different necessities of time and resources, to engage the population of interest and potential partnerships. For the Brazilian population, care should be taken when mentioning terms related to aging or treatments in the title of the intervention or its promotional materials, because it may hinder participation among older adults due to the associated stigma. Group activities for global cognitive stimulation, whether conducted online or in person, seem to be crucial for enhancing social support networks, boosting mood, and maintaining cognitive health and functionality. Considering the observed demand, the information gathered in this study should be used to develop and test interventions in Brazil and other LMICs, tailored to address the needs identified by local stakeholders. For instance, such an intervention could make use of small-group dissemination strategies, publicize it as a program rather than a therapy, promote active aging, test different versions for structural aspects that focus groups failed to answer definitively (such as duration, online versus in-person modality, and frequency), and invest in comprehensive group-based cognitive stimulation. A general recommendation, based on current findings, is to avoid long durations (> 1 h) and/or high frequency of the sessions, provide activities with adequate difficulty and cultural references personalized for the interests and capabilities of each group, and to foster participation and socialization for all members. To increase the appropriateness of the activities, one alternative is to develop a structured intervention supported by an accessible manual, particularly in settings with heterogeneous educational levels, such as LMICs. Moreover, in these contexts, the feasibility of delivering the program through professionals from diverse backgrounds is critical, given the limited financial and human resources in public health settings. Pilot studies and RCTs are necessary to explore this further and create evidenced protocols designed for the specific needs of LMIC.
