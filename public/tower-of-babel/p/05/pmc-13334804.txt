Alteration in adenosine monophosphate deaminase activity and ferric reducing capacity in diabetic renal failure patients
Authors: Lubna Sameer Hammad, Jwan abdulmohsin Zainulabdeen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Renal failure is the final stage of chronic kidney disease (a common complication of diabetic mellitus) usually occurs due to delayed treatment or poor disease control with Glomerular filtration Rate falling below 15 mL/min leading to severe deterioration of renal function. Meanwhile Adenosine Monophosphate Deaminase (AMPD) participate in purine metabolism; affecting energy balance.The aim of this study was to investigate the diagnostic significance of AMPD activity with some parameters including ferric reducing capacity (FRC) as an indicator of oxidative stress.The participants of the work divided into: healthy Subject (H), renal failure patients (RF) and renal failure with diabetes mellitus (RF + DM). The results showed a significant increase in AMPD activity in diabetic RF group than RF group without (25.57 ± 3.06 and 16.30 ± 3.44) as well as when compared with healthy subjects (5.89 ± 1.59), levels of FRC were increased also especially in the RF + DM indicating a compensatory increase in antioxidant capacity in response to oxidative stress that caused from RF and DM. Cluster analysis reveals systematic signature in RF where most parameters are closely correlated identifying sensitive indicators of renal health while FRC demonstrate an independent outlier from traditional filtration metrics. ROC curve showed that both markers had excellent diagnostic accuracy with (AUC = 0.9) indicating that they are strong and early biomarkers for RF particularly in diabetic patients. The findings also suggest that inhibition of AMPD activity may help improve metabolism, reduce inflammation, and oxidative stress leading to providing vital protection against RF.Keywords: Renal failure, Diabetes mellitus, Adenosine monophosphate deaminase, Ferric reducing capacity

Introduction

Renal Failure (RF) is a long-term life-threatening condition, exhibit when both of kidneys are no longer working on their own and develop quickly by getting worse over time which later becomes fatal. Mortality rates have remained high in spite of the introduction of dialysis and the advances in supportive care. Its symptoms vary between people due to a combination of genetic, environmental, lifestyle and clinical factors1, 2. In the other hand, Diabetes Mellites (DM) is a major contributor to the global rise in chronic kidney disease and is the primary Complication to RF individuals driven by complex interplay of both inflammatory and immune-mediated mechanisms. This transition from metabolic dysfunction to renal failure is a result of chronically elevated blood glucose levels, relationship between RF and DM is a vicious cycle. When blood sugar levels remain chronically high, it causes scarring in the kidney's filtering units (nephrons), leading to renal failure. However, renal failure leads to a buildup of toxins (uremia) that increases insulin resistance and impairs how the body processes glucose. In short, diabetes starts the problem by destroying kidney tissue over time, leading to glomerular hypertension and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products, resulting in thickening of the glomerular basement membrane. This progression termed diabetic nephropathy, manifests as a silent deterioration, beginning with protein leakage into the urine.3

Adenosine monophosphate deaminase (AMPD; EC: 3.5.4.6) converted Adenosine monophosphate (AMP) to Inosine Monophosphate (IMP); helping the regulation of adenine nucleotide levels and control intracellular energy metabolism. Changes in AMPD activity can lead to metabolic disturbances resulting in excessive accumulation of AMP affecting energy balance.4 Besides, the altered AMPD activity can increase OS, which plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of RF. Globally, studies have examined AMPD activity in the red blood cells of RF patients, comprehensive human data remain limited.5 As for Ferric reducing capacity (FRC) is a valuable measure of antioxidant activity. It is a method used to measure total antioxidant capacity of various substances including biological materials. This method is used to evaluate the ability of antioxidants to reduce Fe3+-(phenanthroline) to Fe2+-(phenanthroline). In the context of RF, studies indicate that FRC values provide insights into OS and antioxidant balance in affected individuals.6 However, AMPD inhibition is a therapeutic tool for CKD and DM. In cases of glomerular injury, AMPD2 inhibition restores energy metabolism in the mitochondria of foot cells and reduces albuminuria. In diabetes, Metformin may partially inhibit AMPD activity, leading to reduced conversion of AMP to IMP and subsequent activation of AMPK to reduce inflammation and OS which protects renal from damage against diabetic induced nephropathy contributing to improvement of diabetic complications Since AMPK considered as energy sensor related to AMPD for renal protections.7, 8 also, the progression of RF influenced by a pathological interaction between the rentals and muscles. Systemic insulin resistance, resulting from elevated urea, impairs the muscles' ability to absorb essential inorganic phosphate. This intramuscular phosphate deficiency is a critical metabolic event, as phosphate normally acts as a natural inhibitor of the AMPD1 enzyme. Once this is removed, AMPD1 overactivation leads to a rapid depletion of cellular energy (AMP) and excessive production of pro-inflammatory uric acid. This cascade of reactions exacerbates RF by increasing systemic inflammation. Restoring phosphate homeostasis and inhibiting the AMPD which therefore may serve as vital therapeutic targets for maintaining muscle and kidney health.9

Since OS arises from an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the antioxidant defenses of the body. In the context of renal health, excessive ROS production can damage renal cells, impair blood flow, and contribute to inflammation.10, 11, 12 This variability in AMPD activity driven by genetic and environmental factors suggests that the exact relationship between AMPD activity, OS and renal function is not yet fully understood. We hypothesized that advanced RF due to DM triggers an accelerated cascade of purine catabolism, driven by increased cellular energy consumption and intracellular phosphate deficiency. Consequently, the hyperactivation of AMPD acts as a major metabolic driver, leading to a significant accumulation of purine breakdown products such as UA, which is directly manifested in the altered and elevated FRC levels seen in RF with DM patients.

Therefore, the objective of study is to assess the potential use of AMPD activity and FRC level as a reliable diagnostic biomarker in different groups of RF.

Material and methods

Patients selection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire form at Al-Noor Hemodialysis Center, Al-Karama Teaching Hospital, Baghdad, Iraq, between October 2025 and January 2026 to conduct a hospital-based case- study. A total of 180 contributors aged between 25 and 45 years, 113 of them were patient at stage 5 proved to have RF according to KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline, which emphasize the integration of clinical history, examination, and laboratory findings. Hence, staging of RF was based on GFR calculation regarding the 2021 CKD-EPI Equation. The participant in this case-control study were strictly classified into three distinct experimental group1: Healthy control group (H, n = 67),2: RF without DM group (RF, n = 50), and3 RF with DM group (RF + DM, n = 63). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated [BMI=Weight (kg) / Height (m2)]. Both Patients group were received only supplements consisting of Calcium, phosphate, and iron. Participants with a history of cancer, other chronic conditions like liver diseases, systemic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, leukemia, Cushing's disease, chronic pancreatitis, as well as those patients who have history of smoking, or alcohol drinking were excluded. Peripheral blood was collected and the serum was separated by centrifugation (3000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C), aliquoted into 1 mL cryovials, and stored at −20 °C until use.

Biochemical assay

The FBG, Total serum protein, Albumin, Creatinine and UA level were measured using Linear Chemicals S.L (Barcelona City, Spain) with Spectrophotometer (PD-307, Japan). Globulin were calculated by: Globulin (g/dL) = Albumin (g/dL)-Total protein (g/dL). The glomerular filtration rate can be estimated using the 2021 CKD-EPI KDIGO equations: GFR = 142 × min (s. Crea/κ, 1) ᵅ × max (s. Crea /κ, 1) −1·200 × 0.9938ᴬᵍᵉ [× 1.012 if female]. AMPD activity was determined by Giusti and Galanti method,13 the specific activity was expressed as follows: AMPD sp. activity (U/g) = AMPD activity (U/L) / Total Protein conc. (g/L). The concentration of FRC was determined using the Benzie and Strain method.14

The study protocol complies with the moral standards approved by the College of Science Ethics Committee at the University of Baghdad (Ref. CSEC/1025/0107). Written consents were obtained from each participant in the current study.

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, the distribution normality of all measured biochemical markers was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since all variables showed a normal distribution, the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and parametric analyses were subsequently performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test. The relationship between two quantitative variables was assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Also, the Cluster analysis group applies similar individuals or variables to reveal hidden patterns, identify subtypes, simplify complex data, and guide research interpretations to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of selected parameters, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed; all statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 24, Chicago, USA).

Discussion

The key findings show no significant differences in age, weight, height, BMI among studied groups (P > 0.05) after applying ANOVA-one-way to know which groups different from each other specifically, implies that they were similar in these respects, as it ensure that metabolic markers are not skewed by age-related physiological decline or obesity related inflammatory states. The GFR serves as the definitive marker for renal function by exhibiting highest level in the Healthy group compared to RF groups places both patients group in stage 5 of CKD or end-stage renal disease, indicating that while diabetes may complicate further failure in renal, The similar degree of filtration loss in this group allowed for a reliable comparison of filtration capacity between the two affected groups. Mechanisms drive the large differences in their metabolic indicators lies in the systemic metabolic disturbance caused by diabetes.15 All this leading that AMPD activity was significantly higher in the diabetic group (p = 0.0001) by converting the AMP molecule to IMP to produce uric acid16 which is a predominant contributor to the FRC value, suggesting that diabetes acts as a metabolic accelerator, where the energy demands of elevated blood glucose levels force an increased rate of purine deaminization to manage the excess AMP produced by the rapid metabolic pathways in diabetes. In the healthy control group, the FRC level rise.17 In the RF group, this increase in FRC concentration is not statistically significant that purine metabolism in isolated RF is relatively balanced.18 However, in the RF with DM group, where AMPD enzyme activity reaches its highest level (25.57 U/L), FRC concentration rises sharply to 3536.7 mmol/L (p = 0.0001). This indicates a synergistic flood of purine metabolism products that the failing renal are unable to eliminate.17, 19 While comparing FBG levels in healthy group and RF patients shows normal blood glucose levels, while the RF group with DM exhibits a severe hyperglycemia which acts as a metabolic stimulator, driving AMPD enzyme activity to its peak of 25.57 units/L to manage excess adenosine monophosphate (AMP) resulting from rapid metabolic pathways in diabetes.20 This accelerated purine breakdown leads to a massive accumulation of reduced byproducts, such as uric acid ultimately causing a significant increase in FRC to 3536.7 mmol/L. Thus, the data suggest that while RF initiates biochemical stress, it is the large increase in FBG to 211 mg/dL that overwhelms cellular energy balance and leads to a significant increase in both AMPD and FRC activity, characterizing the phenotype of diabetic uremic syndrome as a state of severe metabolic exhaustion and uremic waste accumulation.21 However, a study revealed causes of diabetic neuropathy are numerous and include metabolic disorders resulting from chronically elevated FBG, OS, and neuropathy. Despite significant progress in understanding its underlying mechanisms, effective disease-modifying therapies are still lacking, and current clinical treatment focuses primarily on blood sugar control and symptom relief.22

In the other hand, the results indicate a significant (P < 0.01) reduction in total protein and albumin level compared to healthy ones, in RF, protein level drops due to urinary loss (protein loss) and often state of chronic inflammation that suppresses albumin synthesis. However, in diabetic nephropathy, the non-enzymatic glycosylation of glomerular basement membrane increases its permeability to proteins, furthermore, diabetes induces a state of malnutrition complex syndrome (MICS).23 Also promotes the formation of advanced glycation end product (AGEs), which further damages the renal architecture and accelerate protein wasting suggesting diabetes adding secondary layer of protein loss that RF alone does not account for at this GFR levels.24 also, a related study reveal that Proteinuria indicating a progressive impairment of glomerular filtration barrier function. The progression from microproteinuria to macroproteinuria represents a dynamic, continuous chain of glomerular damage, not a fixed clinical threshold. Microproteinuria indicates early, reversible changes in glomerular permeability due to hemodynamic stress, adipokine, chronic low-grade inflammation, and oxidative injury. Continued exposure to these pathological stimuli leads to basement membrane remodeling, and mesenchymal enlargement, ultimately resulting in protein leakage. This advanced stage of indicates irreversible structural damage and is strongly associated with accelerated renal function decline. It is important to note that proteinuria is not only a biomarker of kidney damage but also an active mediator of inflammation and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, which accelerates disease progression. Monitoring the progression of the disease provides important information about disease activity, prognosis, and response to treatment.25 The elevation in UA in both patients groups is due to a combined impairment of renal filtration and active metabolic retention. Thereby The Metabolic disturbances such DM exacerbate this structural defect by chemically since the DM forces the already damaged kidneys to reabsorb and retain UA instead of clearing it from the bloodstream and the primary accumulation observed in the RF cohort may be a compensatory antioxidant mechanism. Also UA trigger the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) from monocytes, leading to increased insulin resistance and increased urate reabsorption in the kidneys.26

Thus, this study conducted a pearson correlation to reveal the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables in which shows, positive correlation between AMPD activity and weight is since there is no renal filtration, AMPD in the blood becomes highly dependent on the total amount of the enzyme leaking into the body. Also, positive correlation shows between AMPD activity and its specific activity in both RF groups due to the increase in AMPD activity. While total protein decreases, the specific activity must rise exponentially, indicating that as the disease progresses27 the patient's protein profile becomes increasingly dominated by catabolic enzymes such as AMPD, suggesting a shift from to catabolic metabolism. The higher creatinine levels in the RF group with DM (7.01 mg/dL) compared to the RF group alone (5.56 mg/dL) indicate that diabetes exacerbates renal damage, The resulting toxic environment in the blood promotes the elevation of AMPD activity.9, 28

While elevated FRC indicates robust antioxidant defense, in this particular patient cohort, it is indicative of metabolic dysfunction rather than health. The elevated FRC is due to the accumulation of uremic waste (purines digested via the AMPD pathway) rather than an adequate supply of functional proteins like albumin.29 The mechanism of hypoalbuminemia in these patients involves two main aspects, A decreased GFR rate indicates severe glomerular damage, allowing albumin to leak into the urine, and Environments of high OS indicated by severe metabolic shifts repress albumin synthesis in the liver.

Thus, the RF + DM group is in a critical condition, possessing the highest FRC (due to purine waste accumulation) but the lowest albumin levels, the primary functional antioxidant. This represents a state of a pseudo-antioxidant protection30 that fails to protect the body from systemic damage caused by diabetes and RF.23 In the RF + DM phenotype it is characterized by a dual effect on metabolic stability. The primary effect is a loss of renal filtration capacity leading to an initial increase in AMPD activity and an initial decrease in total protein.31 The secondary effect is diabetes, which significantly accelerates the purine catabolism pathway (elevated AMPD) and exacerbates protein loss (decreased albumin).32, 33 The resulting increase in FRC should be interpreted not as an improvement in antioxidant status, but rather as a biomarker of excessive purine turnover and urea accumulation.

In the other hand, the traditional cluster analysis is the Average linkage (between groups) method used to group data or variables into cluster, where parameters within the same cluster are very similar but different in behavior from parameters in other cluster to discover hidden patterns and relationships in data without preconceived assumptions using a dendrogram, no prior assumption of the clustering is made. The similarities via studied variables was discovered using this analysis. Depending on the coefficients, the variables in all studied groups were distributed clusters with mild shifting in some groups so that in both graphs. In RF group, approximately 90% of the variables (age, weight, BMI, glomerular filtration rate, albumin) cluster together at a remeasured distance close to 0, This suggests that RF produces systemic signature whether or not a patient has diabetes, their physical measurements (BMI/weight) and biochemical indicators (GFR/total protein) are closely correlated. Also, AMPD activity and AMPD spac are part of the immediate cluster in both groups. AMPD activity joins the cluster shortly after the physical characteristics. In RF with DM group, these variables move up the list, joining the base cluster almost immediately, this indicates that AMPD measurements are highly sensitive indicators of Renal health and are not significantly affected by the metabolic disturbances associated with diabetes. They remain a reliable part of the RF profile. In both diagrams FRC, was the complete outlier, only joining the other variables at the furthest point =25. This suggests that a patient's ability to resist OS is independent of their glomerular filtration rate or nutritional status. A patient may have a relatively stable GFR but suffer from a severe deficiency in antioxidant defenses, or vice versa.

In ROC analysis, which is a statistical analysis that uses a plot of the association between sensitivity and 1-specificity to determine a diagnostic test's optima specificity and sensitivity. In which showed that when comparing AMPD in healthy ones to RF patient shows AUC of 0.932 and a sensitivity of 90%, which is excellent at detecting the disease. The specificity of 69% suggests the possibility of some false positives (healthy ones showing low activity), but it remains a very powerful screening too, as for healthy individual with RF diabetes patient Performance improves here, AUC 0.975, sensitivity/specificity 95%). and between RF and RF With DM exhibit sensitivity with 92%, specificity 84% with AUC 0.959 at cut-off value 21.257. This indicates that the combined metabolic stress of diabetes and RF produces a more distinctive AMPD,9 making it an Excellent diagnostic indicator for this subgroup.

As for FRC, Control group and RF group, AUC is 0.549, and This is a poor result. Indicates that FRC is practically ineffective in diagnosing simple renal failure. Furthermore, the low specificity (32%) means it cannot accurately distinguish between a healthy individual and a renal patient. While Comparison between the control group and RF with diabetes with AUC 0.946, and between RF and RF With DM reveal sensitivity 87%, specificity 84% with AUC 0.895 at cut-off value 2532.286. The accuracy increases significantly. With a sensitivity of up to 94%, FRC measurement becomes a highly reliable method for diagnosing this co-morbid condition.34

Conclusion

This study concludes that AMPD activity and FRC are potent biomarkers for RF, since AMPD provides excellent diagnostic accuracy, especially in diabetic cases while elevated FRC signifies metabolic waste accumulation effectively identifies the sever metabolic exhaustion of diabetic uremia. Also, AMPD may represent a potential therapeutic role for disease treatment. In which metformin and phosphate may be used as inhibitor for AMPD by activating AMPK to provide future therapeutic value, restoring metabolic balance, reduces inflammation and provides vital protection against RF.

Limitations and future prospects

Although this study demonstrates a strong metabolic association, one of its major limitations is its single-center, hospital-based design with a relatively limited age range to exclude comorbidities in the elderly. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and a broader age range are needed. Furthermore, evaluating the long-term therapeutic efficacy of small-molecule AMPD inhibitors in vivo transition models will be crucial to validating these indications as dynamic clinical therapeutic targets.
