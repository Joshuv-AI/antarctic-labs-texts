Anti-infective activity of simvastatin against Candida and selected periodontal pathogens – An in vitro study
Authors: Sayantan Mukherjee, Pratibha Gopalkrishna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Statins, in addition to their hypolipidemic action, also show antibacterial and antifungal activity. The present research examines the antimicrobial activity of simvastatin (SMV) against periodontal microorganisms: Prevotella intermedia (Pi), Tannerella forsythia (Tf), Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn), and Candida tropicalis (Ct).Materials and Methods:SMV was evaluated for minimum inhibitory concentration by microdilution and for antimicrobial susceptibility by agar disc diffusion. The antibiofilm effect of SMV was further compared with chlorhexidine (0.2%) (CHX) at 6 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h.Results:SMV inhibited all three periodontal bacteria at 0.8 μg/ml and inhibited Ct at a minimum concentration of 50 μg/mL. The mean diameter of the zone of inhibition by SMV for Pi, Fn, Tf, and Ct was 13 ± 0.81 mm, 13.33 ± 0.47 mm, 15.33 ± 0.47 mm, and 14 ± 0.81 mm, respectively. SMV showed a peak reduction in colony-forming unit (CFU) counts from baseline (223.42 ± 10.50 CFU/mm) to 6 h (157.25 ± 8.081 CFU/mm), which was subsequently sustained. Compared with SMV, CHX demonstrated a slower but more consistent reduction in CFU counts for up to 72 h.Conclusion:The tested microorganisms were sensitive to SMV. In addition, SMV was as effective as the common antiplaque agent CHX at preventing these organisms and their proliferation in a biofilm environment during the testing periods. The study provides preliminary evidence of the antimicrobial effects of SMV, which warrants further exploration for its dual therapeutic benefits in periodontal applications.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal disease has been linked to hyperlipidemia and cardiovascular disease through shared risk factors such as smoking and diabetes, elevated plasma lipid levels, the presence of inflammation, and the dissemination of Gram-negative bacterial infection.[1] Platelet activation by oral bacteria can also lead to thrombogenesis.[2] Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2011–2012) showed a positive association between elevated serum total cholesterol levels and periodontitis.[3]

Treating hyperlipidemia has been shown to benefit periodontal health. Statins are a class of drugs prescribed for lowering cholesterol. Simvastatin (SMV) competitively inhibits the enzyme 3-hydroxy-3-methyl coenzyme A reductase in patients with hyperlipidemia. In addition to its lipid-lowering action, its anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, antithrombotic, and bone-formation effects are well established.[4]

Pathogenic microflora such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Tannerella forsythia, Prevotella intermedia, Candida albicans, and Candida tropicalis are commonly linked to periodontitis.[5] Prevotella intermedia is a black-pigmented anaerobic rod that boosts the production of Matrix metalloproteinase-1 and 8, which break down the collagen matrix.[6] Tannerella forsythia, a Gram-negative bacterium within the red microbial complex, secretes multiple virulence factors and is associated with pathogenic plaque formation.[7] Fusobacterium nucleatum bridges early and later bacterial colonizers, facilitating the development of dental plaque.[8] Moreover, invasive microbes such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Prevotella intermedia, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans often coexist with Candida species in the subgingival environment.[9] Candida tropicalis has also been isolated from the subgingival biofilm of patients suffering from periodontitis.[1011]

Many topical and systemic anti-infective agents have been evaluated as adjuvants to scaling and root planing, although nonsurgical mechanical debridement remains the first-line treatment for periodontitis. However, systemic antimicrobial therapy for chronic infections like periodontitis is still debatable.[12] Chlorhexidine (CHX) is a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent that affects Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, various fungal infections, and viruses[13] and is beneficial in treating gingivitis.[14] The evidence is largely from in vitro studies on single-species cultures. The impact of these anti-infective agents on a multispecies biofilm would be more representative of the oral environment.

Statins have also demonstrated antibacterial properties by improving survival in patients with high bacterial loads.[15] SMV has been shown to reduce gingival inflammation and promote bone healing as an adjunct to periodontal treatment.[16] In addition, the antibacterial potential of SMV would support its periodontal applications in various forms. SMV’s antimicrobial effects against P. intermedia, F. nucleatum, and T. forsythia in a complex biofilm environment remain unexplored. The current study investigated the efficacy of SMV against specific periodontal bacterial and fungal species. A secondary objective was to compare its antibiofilm efficacy with CHX.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Institutional Ethics Committee approval was obtained before commencing this in vitro study (IEC2: 81/2022). Thirty-six tooth specimens, selected for extraction but caries-free and devoid of structural abnormalities and periodontal pathology, were obtained from the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery department. Intact enamel surfaces of these teeth served as substrates for biofilm growth. After extraction, the teeth were disinfected with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Subsequently, they were rinsed and sectioned with a carbide bur to form 4 mm2 × 4 mm2 crown fragments.

Pure SMV powder (1 g, Pharmaceutical Secondary Standard; Certified Reference Material, having CAS number-79902-63-9; Material number/SKU-PHR1438-1G, Sigma Aldridge) was used in the serial tube dilution technique for evaluating the drug’s minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and antibacterial activity, according to the criteria of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.[17] Nine SMV dilutions of brain–heart infusion (BHI) broth were made using 20 μL of SMV in 380 μL of BHI. Each of the nine tubes received a 200-μL addition of BHI broth for each dilution. Two hundred μL of BHI broth were transferred to the first tube for a 10:1 dilution. Two hundred μL of the 10:1-diluted tube were transferred to the second tube to prepare the 10:2 dilution. Serial dilutions were continued up to a 10:9 dilution.

Furthermore, 5 μL were obtained from the preserved stock cultures of the required organisms and added to 2 ml of BHI broth. Subsequently, 200 μL of the culture suspension was added to each serially diluted tube. Turbidity of the tubes was measured after a 24-h incubation period, indicating growth of the tested organisms. MIC of the drug for that specific test organism was defined as the lowest drug concentration in the tube that did not exhibit any turbidity.

Subsequently, a few cells were recovered and subcultured from frozen stock cultures of Prevotella intermedia (American Type Culture Collection number 25611), Tannerella forsythia (American Type Culture Collection number 21028), and Fusobacterium nucleatum (American Type Culture Collection number 25586). Species of Candida tropicalis (clinical isolate) were obtained from human culture isolates from the culture collection division of the Maratha Mandal Medical Microbiology Centre.

The selected bacterial species were inoculated onto blood agar and incubated for 48–72 h in separate anaerobic glass jars. SMV’s MIC against the selected test bacterial strains was determined by adjusting the turbidity to 0.5 McFarland standards. The fungal species was transferred onto Sabouraud’s dextrose broth immediately after clinical isolation.

The Agar disc-diffusion approach was used to assess antimicrobial susceptibility.[18] After warming the BHI broth, a sterile cotton swab was inserted into the broth and used to streak the bacterial colonies on the agar plate three times to achieve even distribution. Before creating wells, the infected plate was incubated for 3–15 min.

Ten milligrams of SMV, diluted in 1 ml of dimethyl sulfoxide, formed the stock solution. A 5-mm diameter hollow tube was heated and used to create five wells on the agar plate by pressing and removing instantly. Using a micropipette, 75 μL, 50 μL, 25 μL, 10 μL, and 5 μL were added to every well. This step was carried out only for SMV. After 15 min, the CO2 jar was used to incubate the plates at 37°C for 18–24 h to promote the growth of facultative anaerobes (F. nucleatum). The plates containing T. forsythia and P. intermedia were kept in an anaerobic jar maintained at 37°C in the incubator (Labotech, India). Sabouraud agar medium was utilized for the antifungal disc diffusion method and incubated in a CO2 jar at 37°C. The petri dishes were read only if the lawn of growth was clear or nearly confluent. A caliper was used to determine the inhibitory zone diameter to the nearest whole millimeter.

In the next objective, the antimicrobial effectiveness of SMV on laboratory-grown dental plaque was compared to that of CHX. The lowest MIC against which all the tested microorganisms were susceptible was selected. We prepared 10 ml of mouthwash containing the MIC (50 μg/mL) of the SMV drug in a 9.6% alcohol solution, along with various additives. The additives used were vanillin (flavoring agent), saccharin (sweetener), PEG40 (surfactant), cosurfactant (glycerol), and distilled water (DW) (to make up the volume).

The antibiofilm effect was assessed using a mixed biofilm model.[19] Biofilms were allowed to grow on the enamel surfaces of the extracted teeth. The sample size was determined to be 36 (12 per group) healthy human teeth. Three groups of teeth (12 teeth per group) were segregated to receive different antimicrobial agents. Laboratory-grown biofilm (on the surface of an extracted tooth) treated with CHX was considered the positive group, biofilm treated with SMV was the test group, and the biofilm on a square enamel surface treated with DW was the negative group.

Each tooth fragment was placed in a well of a sterile polystyrene cell culture plate, incubated with saliva for 4 h, and then gently shaken at room temperature to form a salivary pellicle. The saliva (from the saliva pool source of the Maratha Mandal Medical Microbiology Centre) was then aspirated from each well and was replaced with a mixture of saliva (800 μL) + mFUM (800 μL) containing 0.3% (w/v) glucose.

Following the aspiration of saliva from each well, 200 μL of the total microorganisms was transferred to the wells. Thirty-six culture plates were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37°C for 3 weeks. The culture medium was replenished by removing the depleted medium from the wells and replacing it with new medium every 6 h. The plates were subsequently incubated in an anaerobic chamber.

At 3 weeks, phosphate-buffered saline was used to rinse all wells after saliva was discarded. The biofilms on the tooth fragments were inoculated onto blood agar to assess microbial growth. The tooth fragments with biofilm on the enamel surfaces were transferred to fresh wells containing either SMV in 10% aqueous ethanol, CHX, or DW (negative control). Following treatment with either of the two agents (SMV/CHX), the teeth were incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 6, 24, 48, and 72 h.

The harvested cells were then examined. The modified cells were serially diluted (10:2–10:5) in saline, vortexed, and aliquoted onto Columbia Blood Agar plates containing sodium tellurite and erythromycin. CBA plates were incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 72 h. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted immediately after administration of either agent (SMV or CHX) at baseline, and at 6, 24, 48, and 72 h. The total microorganism count in CFU/mm was measured and was compared within and between the agents at the same intervals.

Statistical analysis

The outcome measures were MIC, zone diameter (mm), and antibiofilm activity (CFU/mm).

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.0.1 (Franz Faul Universität, Kiel, Germany) for an effect size of 0.24 and a significance threshold of 0.05. A sample size of 36 (12 per group) was calculated, providing 90% power to detect significant differences in biofilm activity. The Mann–Whitney test was used to compare groups. The antibacterial efficacy of the groups required repeated-measures analysis of variance across time points, with post hoc Bonferroni comparisons used for pairwise comparisons at successive time points.

RESULTS

The present study evaluated the antimicrobial effects of SMV against Prevotella intermedia, Tannerella forsythia, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Candida tropicalis.

The microdilution method employed for MIC determination utilizes the BHI medium. We observed turbidity after 24 h of incubation. SMV was tested at concentrations of 0.2 μg/mL, 0.4 μg/mL, 0.8 μg/mL, 1.6 μg/mL, 3.12 μg/mL, 6.25 μg/mL, 12.5 μg/mL, 25 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL, and 100 μg/mL. Bacterial and fungal growth in broth served as controls. After 24 h, T. forsythia, P. intermedia, F. nucleatum, and C. tropicalis were resistant at a 0.2 μg/mL concentration of SMV. At a 0.4 μg/mL concentration of SMV, P. intermedia and C. tropicalis were resistant, whereas other organisms were susceptible. However, all four organisms were susceptible to SMV at concentrations from 0.8 μg/mL to 100 μg/mL. The MIC for SMV was 0.4 μg/mL against Fn and Tf, 0.8 μg/mL against Pi, and 50 μg/mL against Ct. The MIC for CHX against Fn, Pi was 0.4 μg/mL, 1.6 μg/mL for Tf, and 6.25 μg/mL against Ct [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Altered microbial ecology in the dental biofilm and diseased periodontal tissues impairs local periodontal tissue healing and induces a concomitant chronic low-grade inflammatory burden on overall systemic health. Studies have linked periodontitis with cardiovascular disease via Gram-negative bacterial infection. P. intermedia, associated with chronic periodontitis,[20] was consistently found in both coronary atherosclerotic plaque and subgingival plaque. Andriankaja et al. evaluated the risk of myocardial infarction (MI) in 1060 men and women using indirect immunofluorescence microscopy and isolated six periodontal microbes. They observed that T. forsythia and P. intermedia were associated with increased odds of MI (odds ratios of 1.62 and 1.40, respectively).[21]

The present study assessed the antimicrobial effects of SMV, a cholesterol-lowering agent, on the biofilm composition, particularly on bacteria such as P. intermedia, T. forsythia, F. nucleatum, and C. tropicalis, an oral fungus.

P. intermedia cells can also create biofilm that increases bacterial cell resistance to drugs.[22] F. nucleatum was detected in atherosclerotic plaques in blood vessels and correlated with the severity of periodontitis. F. nucleatum interacts with other species to influence atherosclerosis in the multibacterial infection paradigm.[23] T. forsythia is prevalent in aggressive forms of periodontitis.[24] In the dental biofilm, T. forsythia binds with F. nucleatum through an adhesin protein.[25] Hence, the mixed species in the biofilm appear to interact.

Experimental animal model studies have demonstrated that Candida culture can yield a high molecular weight fraction, Candida albicans water-soluble fraction (CAWS), that produces cardiomegaly and arteritis, followed by an increase in systemic cytokines.[26] C. tropicalis has also been implicated in infective endocarditis.[27] Moreover, C. tropicalis is resistant to conventional antifungal agents such as fluconazole, necessitating a search for alternative agents.[28] Candida is a well-known pathogen that contributes to polymicrobial dysbiosis in humans by producing multispecies biofilms.[29] Compared with other Candida species, C. tropicalis and C. albicans exhibit greater genetic similarity.[30] Adhesins from C. tropicalis are also involved in biofilm production.[31]

The MIC indicates the lowest drug concentration at which visible microorganism growth is inhibited. The MIC of SMV was 0.8 μg/mL for the three bacterial species in this study. Emani et al. found that SMV was effective against P. gingivalis at MIC 2 μg/mL and against A. actinomycetemcomitans at 1 μg/ml.[32] Whitaker and Alshammari noted that the MIC of SMV against oral streptococci ranged from 7.8 to 15.6 μg/ml.[33]

Similarly, at a concentration of 50 μg/mL, SMV inhibited C. tropicalis. Brilhante et al. reported an MIC of 29.45 μg/mL for SMV against Candida albicans, whereas for C. tropicalis, it was 70.12 μg/mL.[34] The MIC of antifungal agents such as fluconazole and voriconazole against C. tropicalis in the bloodstream ranged from 0.12 to 0.128 μg/mL and from 0.01 to 4 μg/mL, respectively.[3536]

Although conventional antifungal drugs act through multiple mechanisms, SMV specifically inhibits the ergosterol pathway.[37] Nonetheless, SMV can synergize with azoles, thereby reducing the dose and systemic toxicity of coadministered antifungal agents.[38] Parihar et al. proposed that SMV decreased membrane cholesterol via the mevalonate–isoprenoid branch of the sterol pathway, facilitating bacterial clearance.[39] Furthermore, SMV’s hydrophobic nature aided its antibacterial activity through causing “soap-like” membrane disruption, ultimately resulting in bacterial death.[40]

Using the Agar disc diffusion method, CHX exhibited a significantly larger zone of inhibition against P. intermedia compared to SMV. Similarly, for C. tropicalis, chlorhexidine produced a significantly wider zone of inhibition than SMV, due to its fungicidal action. SMV is regarded solely as a fungistatic agent.[37]

We compared the antibiofilm activity of SMV with that of CHX against a laboratory-grown biofilm. F. nucleatum can coaggregate with P. intermedia and T. forsythia in dental biofilm formation using autoinducer-2.[41] Moreover, Candida species have been shown to bind to oral F. nucleatum.[42] Hence, these periodontal pathogens were chosen in this study to create a multispecies biofilm.

SMV significantly inhibited biofilm growth from baseline to 48 h, with a relatively earlier reduction in CFU count at 6 h compared with CHX. Few studies have demonstrated antibiofilm activity of statins, including decreased cell proliferation, disruption of the bacterial cell membrane, and reduced formation of bacterial extracellular polysaccharides.[4043] Therefore, SMV could reduce bacteria within a complex biofilm network, which is otherwise resistant to the action of antimicrobial agents, suggesting its therapeutic potential.[44] CHX exhibited consistent antibiofilm activity for up to 72 h.

Kamińska et al. observed that developing biofilms are more susceptible to statins than established biofilms. SMV eliminated P. gingivalis from the biofilm after 48 h of exposure at 45 μg/mL. It did not seem to affect the commensal bacteria.[45] In another study, the MIC of SMV for P. gingivalis ranged from 3.12 to 6.25 μg/mL. Treatment with 312.5 μg/mL SMV and 625 μg/mL SMV reduced total bacterial counts (82% and 87%, respectively) and metabolic activity (79%) in a multispecies biofilm. SMV also showed additive activity with other antibiotics, such as amoxicillin and metronidazole. However, F. nucleatum was not susceptible in their study.[46] Chatzigiannidou et al. observed that CHX exposure to a 14-species biofilm resulted in an initial decrease in viable cells but did not completely inactivate the biofilm. By 72 h, the regrowth was even faster. Nevertheless, the pathobiont composition of the biofilm was relatively less post exposure to CHX.[47] The study suggested that oral biofilms could not be solely managed by local antiseptic agents.

Liu et al. demonstrated that SMV reduced C. albicans biofilm development after 16 h of incubation at 1, 2.5, and 5 μM.[48] SMV at higher doses (10, 20, and 25 μM) reduced biofilm growth by >90%. In the present study, SMV at 50 μg/mL reduced mixed colonies in the biofilm. Its antifungal effect in mixed biofilm models is suggested to be through interference with the ergosterol pathway, geranylgeranylation, farnesylation, or exopolysaccharide production.[48]

The present study is limited by its in vitro design, which may not accurately reflect the complex microbial environment and the diverse interactions observed in the oral cavity. Moreover, the CFU assay, as a measure of biofilm reduction, does not reflect changes in individual microbial species, nor does it assess metabolic activity within the biofilm.

The MIC of a drug assesses a pathogen’s susceptibility in vitro. It guides in vivo use by helping determine effective medication doses or select suitable antibiotics for specific infections. However, clinical interpretation must be conducted carefully, considering factors such as the site of infection, host immunity, the drug’s pharmacokinetics, and protein-binding capacity, all of which can influence its efficacy against infection. Therefore, it is vital to combine the MIC with other pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters to achieve predictable therapeutic outcomes.[49] Future research can explore these issues further and compare different antibiotics. Anti-infective agents that do not disturb commensal flora can also be investigated.

CONCLUSION

To conclude, although CHX remains the benchmark, SMV has demonstrated sufficient antimicrobial activity against selected oral microflora. All microorganisms were sensitive to SMV at 50 μg/mL. SMV reduced biofilm growth over the tested durations.

Oral pathogens from sites with periodontitis induce low-grade chronic inflammation and pose a significant risk for systemic conditions like hyperlipidaemia/cardiovascular disease. The composition of the dental biofilm can also influence periodontal treatment outcomes. Pathogens such as P. intermedia exhibit resistance to certain commonly used antibiotics. Recognizing alternative, nonantibiotic anti-infective medications, such as SMV, which are safe and accepted treatment modalities for systemic conditions, provides an opportunity to treat coexisting infections, such as periodontitis, concurrently. The adverse effects associated with CHX are well known. SMV may offer a lower tissue toxicity option, given its protective role for gingival fibroblasts.[50] Furthermore, the dual advantages of SMV’s antimicrobial and bone-forming properties place it within the scope of periodontal therapeutic approaches.
