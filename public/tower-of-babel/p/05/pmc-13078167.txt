Spatial proteomics for the analysis of host-pathogen interactions in mice lungs infected with Aspergillus fumigatus
Authors: Saskia Wolter, Thomas Krüger, Daniela Pelzel, Ilse D Jacobsen, Axel A Brakhage, Ferdinand von Eggeling, Olaf Kniemeyer, Carmen Buchrieser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding host-pathogen interactions at the molecular level requires methods capable of linking spatial context with proteomic information. Here, we present an integrated workflow combining matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) and laser microdissection (LMD)-based liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to investigate Aspergillus fumigatus infection in murine lung tissue. Consecutive formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were used for spatially resolved MALDI-MSI and subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis of laser-microdissected fungal-infected and non-infected regions. MALDI-MSI revealed reproducible m/z features specifically associated with infected areas. Corresponding tissue microregions were microdissected and analyzed by proteomics to identify candidate proteins underlying these spatial signals. Comparative proteomics of fungal-infected with non-infected alveolar lung regions via LC-MS/MS identified host proteins involved in leukocyte recruitment, inflammatory signaling, and reactive oxygen species formation, including a 424-fold increase in formyl peptide receptor 2 (Fpr2) during fungal invasion of the lungs. Fungal regions were also enriched in proteins encoded by the gliotoxin biosynthetic gene cluster. Spatial and proteomic data were linked by matching theoretical peptide-adduct masses to MALDI-MSI features, using a semi-quantitative scoring system to prioritize protein assignments. Fungal regions showed contributions from both host and pathogen proteins. This workflow establishes a conceptual basis for spatial proteomics of host-pathogen-interactions in fungal infections and enables association of characteristic m/z signals with plausible protein candidates.Keywords: Aspergillus fumigatus, MALDI-imaging, lung infection, proteome, laser microdissection, neutrophils

Introduction

Aspergillus fumigatus is a ubiquitously spread filamentous fungus and a decomposer of organic matter in the soil. It propagates via asexually produced spores, called conidia, which can be easily inhaled by humans. These conidia are usually cleared by the immune system, but in immunocompromised patients the fungus can cause lung infections that can disseminate to distant organs. This invasive form of mycosis is known as invasive aspergillosis (IA) and occurs frequently in patients undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplantation, with severe influenza or inherited immunodeficiencies (Latge and Chamilos 2019, van de Veerdonk et al. 2025). More recent estimates indicate that over 2.1 million people develop IA annually with death rates varying between 40%–80% (Denning 2024). Although more than 250 Aspergillus species have been described, the majority of IA are caused by A. fumigatus and related fungi that belong to this species complex (Lass-Flörl and Cuenca-Estrella 2017). It is not yet fully understood, why A. fumigatus is such a successful opportunistic pathogen in humans. Several virulence attributes have been discovered for A. fumigatus and its pathogenicity is based on a complex of traits (reviewed in Earle, 2023). Besides its thermotolerance (Bhabhra and Askew 2005), secondary metabolites like gliotoxin (Sugui et al. 2007, Gunther et al. 2024), the conidial pigment DHN-melanin (Langfelder et al. 1998, Schmidt et al. 2020), cell wall components (Gravelat et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2015), iron acquisition via siderophores (Schrettl et al. 2004) and extracellular proteins (Aimanianda et al. 2009, Voltersen et al. 2018, Jia et al. 2023) have been identified and characterized as virulence factors among others.

To identify further genes which contribute to virulence or which allow adaptation of the fungus to the mammalian host niche, several studies used transcriptome approaches to characterize the gene expression under in vivo conditions. In a pioneering study by McDonagh et al. (2008) the transcriptome of A. fumigatus germlings from mouse BAL fluids were investigated and revealed the differential expression of genes involved in adaptation to iron limitation, alkaline challenge, nutrient starvation, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Further in vivo studies in mice showed an adaptation of A. fumigatus to hypoxia, zinc and nitrogen limitation during in vivo growth. Another interesting finding was that in vivo infection stimulates unique transcriptional regulatory pathways, which are different to in vitro data (reviewed in Bignell 2024).

In contrast to transcriptome data, little is known about the composition of the proteome of A. fumigatus and the associated host response. This information would be important because proteins are the actual functional molecules. The challenge of in vivo proteomics is the small amount of proteins of the infectious agent in comparison to the host proteome (Krüger et al. 2015). Nevertheless, advances in mass spectrometry now make it possible to characterize host-pathogen interactions in vivo as shown, e.g. for a murine pneumonia model with the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus (Michalik et al. 2017) or an ex-vivo pig lung model for A. fumigatus (Curtis et al. 2025). However, these proteomic data lack a spatial resolution of the infection process. A spatially resolved molecular analysis is valuable, though, for describing the interactions between host and pathogen within a specific organ, as it enables the direct association of localized pathogen presence with site-specific host responses, which are spatially heterogeneous and obscured in bulk molecular analyses. Spatially resolved proteomics can be performed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI). Its simultaneous, label-free detection of numerous biomolecules in intact tissue sections is an important advancement over current histopathological techniques. Until now, this technique is predominantly used in cancer research to locate known peptides to a specific region (Arolt et al. 2023), sub-typing of cancer entities (Pertzborn et al. 2022) or to find new tumor markers in a top down analysis (Arentz et al. 2017).

In the analysis of lungs from mice infected with A. fumigatus, MALDI-MSI has been recently employed to enable a spatial identification of an antibacterial endopeptidase secreted by A. fumigatus during lung infections (Machata et al. 2025). The challenge with spatial resolved proteomic is the heterogeneity and the complexity of tissue sections. Prior to genomic and proteomic analyses, it is therefore indispensable to identify the exact part of the tissue or even the exact cell. Laser-based microdissection is a technique able to produce pure and cell-defined cell material for further analysis with proteomic and genomic techniques.

The combination of laser-based tissue microdissection offers the possibility to excise MALDI-MSI defined regions and transfer them to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis (von Eggeling and Hoffmann 2020). In case of host-pathogen interactions between mammalian cells and pathogenic fungi like A. fumigatus this combination opens up the possibility to elucidate this biological cross-talk. Here, we report the application of a MALDI-MSI and LMD-based LC-MS/MS workflow to formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) lung tissue derived from a murine model of A. fumigatus infection, enabling spatially resolved proteomic characterization of pathogen-associated and host-derived regions.

Methods and materials

Overall experimental workflow

The overall workflow is illustrated in (Fig. 1). From each paraffin-embedded lung sample, two consecutive sections were prepared: one mounted on a conductive indium tin oxide (ITO) slide for MALDI-MSI and one mounted on a polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) membrane slide for laser microdissection (LCM) and subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis. MALDI-MSI provided spatially resolved molecular profiles, which were co-registered with histological annotations after Grocott–Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) staining to delineate fungal and alveolar regions. In parallel, the corresponding regions were isolated from the PEN-mounted sections by LMD and analyzed by LC-MS/MS, yielding protein identification and quantification data. Finally, datasets from both approaches were integrated by comparing theoretical peptide-adduct masses from the proteomic analysis with m/z features detected in the MALDI-MSI measurements.

Tissue samples

The use and preparation of tissue samples have been described previously (Jöhnk et al. 2016). In brief, five murine lungs were obtained from corticosteroid-treated mice intranasally infected with A. fumigatus strain CEA17 ΔakuBKU80 (da Silva Ferreira 2006). After fixation in 4% (v/v) formalin (Histofix), the samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 µm thickness using a microtome (Leica Biosystems, RM 2265). For each biological replicate, two consecutive sections were prepared: The first was mounted on a conductive ITO slide (Bruker Daltonics, 8 237 001) pre-coated with poly-L-lysine [0.1% (v/v), Sigma P8920] and IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma, I8896) for MALDI-MSI measurements, while the second was mounted on a PEN membrane slide (Zeiss, 415190–9042-000) for subsequent LMD and LC-MS/MS analysis. All sections were deparaffinized as described below.

MALDI imaging

Sample preparation

Sample preparation for MALDI imaging was conducted as described previously (Hoffmann et al. 2019). Briefly, FFPE tissue sections were subjected to deparaffinization, rehydration, pH conditioning, and antigen retrieval using a SunTissuePrep system (SunChrom). Deparaffinization was performed by washing slides three times for 180 s with 100% xylene (Merck, 108 297), followed by 90 s drying with compressed air. Rehydration was carried out sequentially with 100% (v/v), 95% (v/v), and 70% (v/v) ethanol (VWR Chemicals, 153386F; each 180 s), followed by 180 s drying with compressed air and a final wash with Milli-Q® water (Merck, Milli-Q® Advantage A10). Antigen retrieval was performed in 10 mM citric acid (pH 6; Sigma, C0706), followed by pH conditioning with two washes (180 s each) in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (Sigma, A6141). Sections were then washed with Milli-Q® water (180 s) and dried with compressed air (270 s).

Enzymatic digestion

Trypsin (0.1 µg/µl) was prepared in a solution containing 20% (v/v) acetic acid (50 mM), 75% (v/v) ammonium bicarbonate buffer (50 mM), and 5% (v/v) acetonitrile. The enzyme solution was deposited onto the tissue using a SunCollect sprayer (SunChrom GmbH, Germany). Spraying was performed with the following parameters: line distance 2.00 mm, Z position 29.0 mm, Z offset 0.0 mm, and a flow rate of 10 µl/min per layer. A total of eight layers were applied consecutively without delay between layers. Spraying speeds were set to Low 7 for the x-axis and Medium 1 for the y-axis. Subsequently, enzymatic digestion was carried out in the SunDigest chamber (SunChrom GmbH) under “BASIC” mode at 50°C in a humidified atmosphere (95% relative humidity) for 2 h.

Matrix deposition

Matrix application was executed as described previously for FFPE tissues (Hoffmann et al. 2019). The matrix consisted of 10 mg/ml α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) in 60% (v/v) acetonitrile and 0.2% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid. Deposition was performed with the SunCollect system in three layers: 10 µl/min for the first layer, and 35 µl/min for the second and third layers. Spraying parameters were set to: x-speed = low 3, y-speed = medium 1, vial distance = 2 mm, z-position = 29 mm.

MALDI-MSI acquisition

Imaging experiments were performed as described previously (Machata et al. 2025) using an UltrafleXtreme mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). Spectra were acquired in positive ion reflector mode with a pixel size of 50 µm, medium laser beam size, 200 shots per pixel and a mass range of m/z 500–4000. External calibration was executed with a peptide calibration standard (Bruker, 8 222 570).

GMS staining and digital scanning

Following MALDI-MSI analysis, residual matrix was removed by washing the sections twice for 5 min each in 80% (v/v) ethanol. For histopathological annotation, sections were stained using a methenamine silver plating kit according to the Gomori method (Sigma, 1 008 200 001). Stained slides were digitized with a NanoZoomer-SQ scanner (Hamamatsu) at 40× magnification. The resulting whole-slide images were imported into the open-source bioimaging software QuPath (version 0.5.0) for subsequent tissue annotation (Bankhead et al. 2017).

MALDI-MSI data acquisition and processing

Data preprocessing and region annotation

The SCiLS Lab software Version 2026a (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) was used for MALDI-MSI preprocessing and imaging analysis (Trede et al. 2012). After loading, all data were binned and total ion count (TIC)-normalized. In order to find m/z-values co-localized with the fungal areas, fungal regions were manually annotated in QuPath and subsequently exported to SCiLS Lab using the QuPath-SCiLS annotation plugin. In addition, alveolar tissue regions and matrix background areas were annotated to serve as control regions.

Identification of fungal-associated features

Annotated fungal regions were imported into SCiLS Lab and analyzed using the “Find Values Co-localized with Region” tool, which calculates the Pearson correlation between ion distributions and annotated regions (Trede et al. 2012). Correlation thresholds were adjusted such that ∼130 m/z features were initially retained. All resulting ion images were visually inspected, and peaks displaying excessive noise, intensity gradients, or other artefacts were removed. This yielded a curated list of ∼100 m/z values highly correlated with fungal regions.

Identification of alveolar tissue-associated features

Annotated alveolar and background regions were subjected to Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis in SCiLS Lab. Thresholds were set to retain ∼130–150 candidate features, which were then also curated through manual inspection. After removal of artefactual signals, a final control list of ∼100 m/z values was obtained, representing features characteristic of alveolar tissue rather than background.

Export and downstream processing

Both curated peak lists (fungal and alveolar tissue) were exported as CSV (comma-separated values) files from SCiLS Lab and subsequently processed in a custom Python pipeline for downstream analyses. Peak lists were harmonized across samples by clustering m/z values within a defined tolerance, followed by generation of presence/absence matrices and normalized intensity profiles. This enabled cross-sample comparison of spectral features and identification of consistently detected peaks within biological groups.

Laser capture microdissection

Sample preparation

For microdissection, tissue sections mounted on PEN membrane slides (Zeiss, 415190–9042-000) were processed according to an adapted protocol (Makhmut et al. 2023). Slides were first incubated at 60°C for 1 h to melt residual paraffin. Sections were then washed twice in 100% xylene (10 min each), twice in 100% ethanol, once in 80% (v/v) ethanol, and once in 70% (v/v) ethanol, followed by two washes in Milli-Q® water (2 min each). After dehydration, slides were stored in a vacuum chamber (VWR, BELAF424004101) for at least 15 min or until microdissection.

Microdissection procedure

Laser capture microdissection was carried out on a PALM MicroBeam system (Zeiss) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. For each biological replicate, discrete tissue areas of ∼1 mm2 were isolated and collected for subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS

Tryptic digestion

Laser microdissection samples captured in adhesive caps of 500 µl centrifugation tubes were incubated with 20 µl of 1% (w/v) AALSII acid labile surfactant (Progenta) upside down for 30 min at room temperature, 30 min at 60°C, and also three freeze-thaw cycles were applied. Subsequently, the samples were released from the caps and centrifuged down to the bottom of the tube at 6000 × g. Lysis of the dissected tissue was further promoted by ultrasonic treatment for 10 min at 100% amplitude with cycles of 6 s treatment and 1 s break. Samples were diluted with 80 µl of 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) and proteins were digested at 37°C for 20 h with 4 µg Trypsin/LysC mixture (Promega). Finally, AALSII has been cleaved by addition of 4 µl TFA followed by an incubation at 37°C for 30 min. Tryptic peptides were desalted by using Affinisep AttractSPE µSpin C18 columns and in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, dried in vacuum concentrator, and resolubilized in 30 µl of 0.05% (v/v) TFA in H2O/ACN 98/2 (v/v) filtered through Ultrafree-MC 0.2 µm hydrophilic PTFE membrane spin filters (Merck Millipore). The filtrate was transferred to HPLC vials and injected into the LC-MS/MS instrument.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Each sample was measured in triplicate (three analytical replicates). LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Ultimate 3000 nano RSLC system connected to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (both Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with FAIMS. Peptide trapping for 5 min on an Acclaim Pep Map 100 column (2 cm × 75 µm, 3 µm) at 5 µl/min was followed by separation on an µPACneo 110 column. Mobile phase gradient elution of eluent A [0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water] mixed with eluent B [0.1% (v/v) formic acid in 90/10 acetonitrile/water] was performed using the following gradient: 0 min at 4% B and 750 nl/min, 10 min at 9% B and 750 nl/min, 12 min at 9.5% B and 300 nl/min, 55 min at 25% B and 300 nl/min, 70 min at 50% B and 300 nl/min, 75 min at 96% B and 300 nl/min, 78–80 min at 96% B and 750 nl/min, 80.1–90 min at 4% B and 750 nl/min. Positively charged ions were generated at spray voltage of 2.2 kV using a stainless steel emitter attached to the Nanospray Flex Ion Source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The quadrupole/orbitrap instrument was operated in Full MS/data-dependent MS2 mode. Precursor ions were monitored at m/z 300–1100 at a resolution of 120 000 FWHM (full width at half maximum) using a maximum injection time (ITmax) of 50 ms and 300% normalized AGC (automatic gain control) target. Precursor ions with a charge state of z = 2–5 were filtered at an isolation width of m/z 4.0 amu for further fragmentation at 28% HCD collision energy. MS2 ions were scanned at 15 000 FWHM (ITmax = 40 ms, AGC = 200%). Each sample was measured in triplicate with a different compensation voltage (−42 V, −57 V, −72 V).

Protein database search

Tandem mass spectra were searched against the UniProt databases (2025/09/11) of Mus musculus and A. fumigatus (Pan database restricted to AFUA/Af293 and AFUB/CEA10 annotation) using Proteome Discoverer (PD) 3.1 (Thermo) and the database search algorithms (threshold search engine scores in parenthesis) Mascot 3.1 (>30) with MS2 Peak Intensity Prediction (MS2PIP), Comet (>3), MS Amanda 3.0 (>300), Chimerys (>2), Sequest HT (>3) with and without INFERYS Rescoring and MSPepSearch (>600, spectral library from NIST, mouse_hcd_selected) with Precursor Detector node. Two missed cleavages were allowed for the tryptic digestion. The precursor mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and the fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da. Modifications were defined as dynamic Met oxidation, phosphorylation of Ser, Thr, and Tyr, protein N-term acetylation with and without Met-loss as well as static Cys carbamidomethylation. A strict false discovery rate (FDR) < 1% (peptide and protein level) was required for positive protein hits. The Percolator node of PD3.1 and a reverse decoy database was used for q-value validation of spectral matches. Only rank 1 proteins and peptides of the top scored proteins were counted. Label-free protein quantification was based on the Minora algorithm of PD3.1 using the precursor abundance based on intensity and a signal-to-noise ratio >5. Normalization was performed by using the total protein amount method. Imputation of missing quan values was applied by using abundance values of 75% of the lowest abundance identified per sample. Differential protein abundance was defined as a fold change of >2, P-value < 0.05 and at least identified in two of three replicates (donors) of the sample group with the highest abundance. Statistics and data visualization were performed with R 4.5.1 and RStudio 2025.05.1. In addition, the pathway enrichment detection functionality of the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was used to identify and visualize enriched host pathways based on GO (gene ontology) term classification (Szklarczyk et al. 2025).

Mass matching

Protein selection

For downstream analyses, protein identification lists including quantitative values were exported from the LC-MS/MS analysis in tabular format. From each dataset, the 100 most abundant proteins were selected based on their quantitative values. Proteins were removed from the list of identified proteins of the fungal-associated region if they were also included in the list of common proteins from the alveolar region in order to examine only common proteins that are actually related to the infection and inflammatory response.

In-silico digestion

The corresponding UniProt accession numbers were extracted from the identification tables and used to retrieve the full protein sequences in batch mode via the UniProt database. Sequences were downloaded in FASTA format. The protein sequences retrieved were subjected to in-silico tryptic digestion using the pyteomics module in a custom Python script. All possible tryptic peptides were generated according to standard cleavage rules, and their monoisotopic masses were calculated.

Adduct modelling

To account for ionization and matrix-related effects in MALDI-MSI, adduct masses were added to each peptide. The following adducts were considered: H⁺ (+1.0073 Da), Na⁺ (+22.9892 Da), NH₄⁺ (+18.0338 Da), K⁺ (+38.9632 Da), and CHCA (+189.0426 Da), the latter corresponding to the CHCA matrix used in the MALDI-MSI experiment.

Peak matching

Theoretical peptide–adduct masses were matched against experimental m/z values within a tolerance window of m/z ±0.218, corresponding to half of the MALDI spectral bin width. A match was considered valid if a calculated peptide mass (including its adduct) fell within this tolerance of a detected MALDI feature. For each match, the observed m/z value, theoretical mass, mass deviation (Δm/z), peptide sequence, adduct type, and protein accession were recorded in a structured results table for downstream analyses. For each MALDI-derived m/z feature, all possible peptide matches were aggregated, including annotation of the corresponding protein accession, peptide sequence, and adduct.

LC-MS/MS-based proteome analysis of microdissected lung tissue

Histological staining of each mouse lung allowed selection of regions without clear histological signs of infection and regions that clearly showed invasive growth of fungal hyphae. Based on the five infected mouse lungs analyzed by MALDI-MSI, four were further analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The lung samples prepared by laser microdissection were analyzed with the primary goal of utilizing the higher detection sensitivity of the nanoLC-MS/MS instrument, in particular in the MS/MS mode, compared to MALDI-MSI. Specific m/z values determined by MALDI-MSI that distinguish a fungal infection site from histologically unremarkable lung tissue were to be identified using the more sensitive proteomics technique. We aimed to identify the fungal proteins predominant in infected lung tissue as well as potential infection markers of invasive aspergillosis on the host proteome side. In total, 470 fungal and 1753 murine proteins were identified from the dissected areas of 1.0 × 106–1.3 × 106 µm2 (Table S1, Samples 1–4) with a thickness of 5 µm. The most abundant proteins of fungal and murine origin are shown in Fig. 4A and B and all identified proteins are listed in Table S2.

Bar chart of Top 20 most abundant fungal (A) and murine (B) proteins. Abundances were observed over all samples by both relative spectral counts and relative peak intensity in relation to the number of amino acid residues and the sequence coverage of each protein. For each abundance metric, the given percentage of each protein was calculated based on the sum of the abundance values of all proteins.

Not surprisingly, the predominantly identified fungal proteins belong to the most abundant fungal proteins involved in protein biosynthesis, energy generation and nucleosome organization, such as histones, ribosomal proteins, ATP synthase, elongation factors, etc. The 20 most abundant proteins (Fig. 4A) also included the glycolytic enzyme enolase (AFUA_6G06770), which has been described as a moonlighting protein and virulence factor. It recruits several complement regulators and plasminogen in humans, and allows the fungal pathogen to control complement at the level of C3 to favour fungal survival and to damage endothelial cell layers and tissue components (Dasari et al. 2019). Peroxiredoxin Asp f3 is one of the most abundant proteins in A. fumigatus, which represents a fungal allergen, virulence factor, and reactive oxygen scavenger. Asp f3 helps to overcome iron limitation during infection by compensating for the loss of iron-dependent antioxidant enzymes (Brantl et al. 2021). Another top 20 protein, which contributes to fungal virulence, was GliN (AFUA_6G09720), an N-methyltransferase involved in gliotoxin biosynthesis. Besides GliN, several other proteins involved in biosynthesis of this mycotoxin were identified in the infected lung region, including the thioredoxin reductase GliT, the cytochrome P450 monooxygenase GliF, the nonribosomal peptide synthetase GliP, the glutathione S-transferase GliG, the O-methyltransferase GliM, the aminotransferase GliI, and the dipeptidase GliJ. This indicates that gliotoxin biosynthesis is induced at high level during fungal invasion of alveolar lung tissue.

GO term enrichment analysis underscored this observation, as the GO terms “gliotoxin biosynthetic process” and “mycotoxin biosynthetic process” in particular were significantly enriched in our analyses (Figure S1). Further enriched GO terms were in a broader sense connected to energy metabolism (tricarboxylic acid cycle, ATP metabolic process, mitochondrion, etc.) and protein biosynthesis (ribosomal subunits, translation, RNA binding, etc.). Interestingly, the GO term “symbiont-mediated suppression of host immune response” showed one of the highest enrichment levels of all terms. Aspergillus fumigatus suppresses the host’s immune response at the molecular level, thereby operating under the radar and promoting its spread (Heinekamp et al. 2015). However, in particular the aforementioned enzymes involved in gliotoxin biosynthesis contribute significantly to the enrichment of this GO term and gliotoxin is known as an immunosuppressive agent and virulence determinant of A. fumigatus (Scharf et al. 2016)

For the murine proteome, we detected typical abundant proteins in alveolar lung tissue such as histones, cytoskeletal keratins and actin as well as various haemoglobin subunits from erythrocytes, which enable the gas exchange in the lung alveoli (Fig. 4B). However, even among the most abundant proteins, proteins of the immune defence were found, such as S100-A9, which belongs to calprotectin, an antimicrobial metal ion (Fe2+, Zn2+, Mn2+) sequestration protein complex (S100-A8/A9). Both S100-A8 (8.3-fold) and S100-A9 (6.2-fold) were also significantly increased in the region of the A. fumigatus infected mouse lungs. Calprotectin is one of the most abundant cytosolic proteins in neutrophils, discussed as biomarker for diagnosing infections (Sejersen et al. 2025) and is known to limit A. fumigatus hyphal growth (McCormick et al. 2010). The antimicrobial protein lactoferrin, can can bind Fe3+, has similar growth-inhibiting effect on A. fumigatus and increased by 6.2-fold in infected lung regions (Zarember et al. 2007). Further abundant immune defence proteins identified in the analysis of microdissected lung tissue were the neutrophilic granule protein (NGP), a cathepsin B inhibitor, and the chitinase-3-like protein 1 (Chi3l1). Chi3l1 plays a critical role in the antipathogen response by promoting microbial clearance while simultaneously conveying tolerance via controlling cell death, inflammation, and remodelling (Dela Cruz et al. 2012, He et al. 2013).

We identified 235 differential abundant mouse proteins, when comparing A. fumigatus infected lung areas with lung areas without hyphal infestation. Of these, 187 proteins were increased in abundance and 48 were decreased (Fig. S3).

As expected for the corticosteroid-model of invasive aspergillosis in mice (Arastehfar et al. 2021), there was clear evidence of immune cell recruitment to the site of infection. The formyl peptide receptor 2 (Fpr2) was the protein with the highest elevated abundance (424-fold increase) under fungal lung invasion (Fig. 5). Fpr2 is a high affinity G protein-coupled receptor for N-formyl-methionyl peptides and other ligands such as the pro-resolving lipid mediators lipoxin A4, which are powerful neutrophil chemotactic factors that mediate migration of neutrophils and other leucocytes to the site of infection (Colby et al. 2016). The second most elevated host protein (323-fold increase) under hyphal intrusion of the lung was the lung-specific mast cell-expressed membrane protein 1 (Mcemp1), which is involved in inflammatory responses and was reported to be highly expressed in sepsis mice (Chen et al. 2019).

Top plot of the 20 most increased and the 20 most decreased murine proteins in abundance based on the Log2FC. All proteins have to match a P-value < 0.05 and were identified in at least three of four replicates of the sample group with the highest abundance.

The aldehyde dehydrogenase family 3 member B1 (Aldh3b1) showed a 185-fold increase in the fungal-infected lung. Aldh3b1 re-oxidizes fatty aldehydes to fatty acids in the plasma membrane and thus protects cells from lipid peroxidation and other damaging effects of ROS generated during inflammatory immune responses (Marchitti et al. 2007).

Furthermore, the mechanosensitive cation channel TMEM63A was highly increased (27.9-fold) in the fungal region (Fig. S2). TMEM63A conducts cation currents in alveolar type 1 and 2 cells triggering lamellar body exocytosis and surfactant secretion into airspace during lung inflation. Lack of TMEM63A results in lung collapse and respiratory failure in mice due to a deficit of surfactant secretion (Chen et al. 2024). Mechanosensitive ion channels like TMEM63 play also a role in the inflammatory response of immune cells and mediate lysosomal mechanosensitivity and function (Li et al. 2024)

Many other proteins which increased upon A. fumigatus infection are associated with neutrophil degranulation, leucocyte migration and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion as revealed by GO term enrichment analysis (Fig. 6) and included surface proteins such as annexin A1 (10.0-fold), CD44 (12.0-fold) and CD177 (22.9-fold), which represents a specific marker of neutrophil activation. Lytic and antimicrobial granule proteins like cathepsin G (19.2-fold), neutrophil elastase (6.6-fold), neutrophilic granule protein (7.1-fold), and the cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide (8.7-fold) increased in abundance as well. The scaffold protein flotillin-1 (7.1-fold) forms microdomains in the plasma membrane, which are essential components of the phagolysosomal membranes of immune cells such as macrophages (Schmidt et al. 2020). In contrast, proteins associated with cell adhesion, such as tight junction and adhesion proteins decreased in abundance in A. fumigatus infected regions of the lung (Fig. 6), e.g. the tight junction proteins Tjp1 (-19.1-fold), Tjp2 (-16.2-fold) as well as several laminin proteins (Lamb1, Lama5, Lamc1, Lama3).

In sum, GO term enrichment of significantly increased mouse proteins in the fungal region of the lung compared to the intact alveolar lung region indicates leukocyte recruitment to the site of infection, neutrophil activation, inflammatory response, elevated reactive oxygen species production, and blood coagulation. (Fig. 6A). In contrast, the decreased mouse proteins point to reduced cell adhesion, endothelial barrier development, and extracellular matrix organization. This suggests that the cellular integrity and the structural organization of the lung is significantly affected by fungal hyphae invading the alveolar tissue (Fig. 6B).

Mass-matching of MALDI features to candidate proteins

In order to link region-specific MALDI signals to their underlying molecular components, characteristic m/z features observed in the tissue were matched to candidate peptides identified by LC-MS/MS. A protein-scoring approach allowed the ranking of potential protein sources for these m/z features, providing insight into the contributions of host and fungal proteins within distinct tissue regions (Fig. 7).

Scores are derived from matching-detected MALDI m/z features to theoretical peptide masses, accounting for peptide length, delta mass, and adduct type. Labels indicate protein name and organism (MM = Mus musculus, AF = A. fumigatus).

In order to gain more insight, the following analysis focused on the 20 proteins with the highest matching scores. In the areas of alveolar lung tissue, protein matches were predominantly found for several keratin isoforms, spectrin alpha and beta chains, vimentin, moesin, and cytoskeletal protein 4. In addition to these structural proteins, metabolic enzymes such as pyruvate kinase PKM and immune-related proteins such as myeloperoxidase were also detected. Secretory proteins such as mucin-2 and serum albumin were also among the top matches. In the fungal regions, the most abundant proteins exhibited a mixed host-pathogen profile. On the host side, proteins such as the structural proteins plectin, desmoplakin, spectrins, filamin-A, and myosin-9 were matched to the MALDI signals. Host proteins with immunological relevance, including complement C3 and class I histocompatibility antigen, were also observed. At the same time, several fungal proteins were among the most prominent hits, including acetyl-CoA carboxylase, RNA polymerase subunit β, ATP synthase subunit α, and catalase B (Cat1).

Across all five biological replicates of the fungal regions, nine prominent MALDI m/z-features were consistently detected as previously described (m/z 821.9, 858.4, 1027.6, 1065.7, 1135.4, 1144.5, 1145.7, 1166.7, and 1173.7). To investigate potential protein identities underlying these conserved features, an exemplary mass-matching analysis was performed for the fungal region in sample 1 (Fig. 8). For the peak at m/z 821.9, high-scoring matches were obtained for the proteins spectrin alpha chain and spectrin beta chain. The peak at m/z 858.4 yielded multiple possible assignments, including fungal proteins such as ATP synthase subunit alpha and a WD repeat protein, as well as murine proteins such as class I histocompatibility antigen, spectrin alpha and beta chains, and synaptotagmin-like protein 4. The signal at m/z 1027.6 was associated with adhesion G-protein coupled receptor V1 and plectin, while the peak at m/z 1065.7 matched serine/threonine-protein kinase TAO3 and adhesion G-protein coupled receptor V1. The m/z 1135.4 feature was linked to spectrin beta chain. For m/z 1144.5, matches were found to complement C3 and adhesion G-protein coupled receptor V1, and the adjacent peak at m/z 1145.7 was associated with the same receptor as well as filamin-A. The peak at m/z 1166.7 was linked to plectin and spectrin alpha chain. Finally, the m/z 1173.7 peak displayed strong matches to both catalase B (Cat1) from A. fumigatus and the murine adhesion G-protein coupled receptor V1.

Discussion

The aim of our study was to investigate the molecular basis of region-specific signals detected by MALDI-MSI in lung tissue infected with A. fumigatus. By combining spatially resolved MALDI-MSI with downstream protein identification using LMD and LC-MS/MS, we attempted to link characteristic m/z features with potential protein identities. This integrated workflow represents a targeted application of established spatial proteomics strategies to fungal infection models in mammalian tissue. While similar combinations of imaging mass spectrometry, microdissection, and LC-MS/MS are common in oncology and biomarker discovery (Lukowski et al. 2025), their use in infectious disease research has been limited to date (Zhang et al. 2025). Here, we show that the same principle can be effectively transferred to host-pathogen systems, allowing fungal structures and adjacent host regions to be spatially delineated and analyzed at the proteome level. This provides a framework for investigating molecular host-pathogen interactions directly in infected tissue.

The definition of region-specific spectral features in MALDI-MSI was based on statistical correlation and ROC analyses, followed by manual curation to remove artefactual signals. While this procedure ensured high-quality peak lists, it inevitably involved a certain degree of subjectivity, since threshold choice and visual inspection can influence the final outcome. Nevertheless, the identification of nine reproducible peaks consistently detected across all five fungal regions highlights the robustness of this approach and provides evidence for region-specific molecular signatures (see Fig. 2). These reproducible fungus-associated m/z-features, together with broader sample-wide protein-matching, provided the foundation for subsequent LC-MS/MS-based mass-matching in order to assign plausible candidate protein identities to MSI features and to disentangle host- and pathogen-derived molecular contributions.

In addition to determining the proteins for identification of the MALDI-MSI signals, functional proteomics analyses were also carried out in order to gain more detailed insights into the host-pathogen interaction. Despite the small amount of proteins which can be extracted from sectioned paraffin-embedded murine lung tissues, a sufficient number of proteins (470 fungal and 1753 mouse proteins) could be identified. In general, our data reflected the recruitment of neutrophils/leukocytes to the A. fumigatus-infected sites of the lung (Bruns et al. 2010) illustrated by the strongly enriched GO terms leukocyte migration, leukocyte cell-cell adhesion and neutrophil activation. This is consistent with reports that in corticosteroid-treated mice the local recruitment of neutrophils to infected sites of the lung is not impaired (Amich et al. 2020). Proteins involved in all major antimicrobial activities of neutrophils were detected (McKenna et al. 2021), namely NET formation (e.g. calprotectin), phagolysosomal processing (e.g. flotillins, NADPH oxidase) degranulation and extracellular vesicle formation (azurophilic and specific granule proteins, e.g. Elane, Ctsg, Prtn3, Lyz2, CD177, Mmp9).

Plasminogen, which acts as a complement inhibitor and which binds to A. fumigatus conidia (Behnsen et al. 2008) showed more than five-fold increased levels in infected lung regions and may exhibit immunomodulatory activity. In this context, the increased levels of the surface protein C5aR1 (CD88), a C5a receptor, is worth mentioning. C5a/C5aR1 signalling increases locally in the lung of critically ill patients with COVID-19, in particular in neutrophils through neutrophil extracellular trap formation (Silva et al. 2023), which may also be the case in invasive aspergillosis.

The sharp increase in the receptor Fpr2 at the site of infection was striking (424-fold increase), which could point to a role of Fpr2 in modulation lung inflammation during A. fumigatus infection. However, it is still not clear whether Fpr2 dampens or boosts the immune response. In mice infected with Streptococcus suis, Fpr2 was shown to exhibit an anti-inflammatory activity by regulating neutrophil migration via the ligand annexin A1 (Ni et al. 2021), while Fpr2 promoted basal pulmonary inflammation upon LPS-induced acute lung injury, but protected against prolonged injury-induced inflammation in a mouse study by Virk et al. (2025). The protein with the second highest increase in abundance in fungal infected regions, Mcemp1, is discussed as septic marker (Malic et al. 2025) and an anti-inflammatory regulator of neutrophil accumulation and survival (Dobosz et al. 2021). In sum, the highly increased levels of Fpr2 and Mcemp1 suggests a highly active resolution of inflammation in A. fumigatus infected mice lungs.

On the fungal protein side, the ROS-detoxifying peroxiredoxins Asp f3 and Prx1 showed a relatively high abundance. However, it is questioned whether ROS have a direct role in killing A. fumigatus during infection (Lessing et al. 2007). Asp f3 also plays an important role during iron deficiency (Brantl et al. 2021). The detection of the siderophore biosynthesis enzyme SidA (Schrettl et al. 2004) corroborates the fact that A. fumigatus is confronted with iron-depleting conditions during mouse lung infection. This iron deficiency is probably caused by the iron-sequestering proteins calprotectin and lactoferrin, presumably mainly released by neutrophils (Zarember et al. 2007, Peet et al. 2024). This illustrates how such antimicrobial proteins have an impact on the host-pathogen dynamics. Another interesting finding was that 8 out of 12 proteins involved in the biosynthesis of the mycotoxin gliotoxin, a discussed virulence factor, were detected in infected lung sections (Scharf et al. 2016), and suggests high expression levels of the corresponding gliotoxin biosynthetic gene cluster during infection. Indeed, in mouse models of IA high levels of gliotoxin were detectable (Sugui et al. 2017). It was recently reported that zinc depletion triggers gliotoxin biosynthesis in A. fumigatus via ZafA, which is a zinc-responsive transcriptional activator (Seo et al. 2019). It seems reasonable to assume that the Zn2+-chelating calprotectin detected in infected lung regions contribute to the activation of the gliotoxin biosynthetic gene cluster. Due to their high abundance, gliotoxin biosynthesis enzymes may also be suitable for use as diagnostic marker for the detection of A. fumigatus in patient samples.

Our analyses provide valuable data and represent the first lung proteome profile of a murine in vivo infection model of invasive aspergillosis. Curtis et al. (2025) used an ex vivo pig lung model to characterize changes of the proteome during A. fumigatus-host interaction. In accordance with our data, the authors found increased abundance of A. fumigatus proteins involved in carbon metabolism and gliotoxin production (GliT). Also, in line with our data, the porcine proteome revealed higher levels of the inflammatory marker calprotectin, while several tight junction and cell adhesion proteins decreased in abundance probably due to the disruption of the lung tissue as a result of the infection. In both proteome data, the level of surfactant protein A dropped, which was explained by the activity of neutrophil-derived proteases. In contrast to the proteomic data from pig lungs, we did not observe a decrease, but rather an increase of proteins associated with neutrophil degranulation. In addition, no evidence was found for the increase of fibrotic markers. This could be due to the fact, that in an ex vivo infection model neutrophils have probably a shorter lifespan and no further neutrophils can be recruited from the blood. However, differences between species cannot be ruled out either and the ex vivo model did not include immunosuppressants. There are also in vivo transcriptome data of invasive aspergillosis available. In a study of the transcriptional response to an A. fumigatus infection in mice, the authors described a higher expression of proteins associated with arginine biosynthesis and metabolism as a precursor for nitric oxide (NO) generation (Kale et al. 2017). We did not observe a generally alteration of the arginine metabolism, but found a drastically reduced abundance of the enzyme Ddah1 (−27-fold), which regulates NO production by degrading the NO-synthase (NOS) inhibitor ADMA. Interesting in this context is that an inhibition of Ddah1 during septic shock was shown to improve survival (Wang et al. 2014). Thus, one might speculate that lower levels of Ddah1 may also be beneficial for the survival of an A. fumigatus infection.

Based on the proteins identified by LC-MS/MS, potential protein identities were then to be assigned to the region-specific m/z features obtained by MALDI-MSI. Due to the limited mass accuracy and spectral resolution of MALDI-MSI, as well as the fact that each m/z feature may represent a composite signal of several co-localized peptides, no definite one-to-one assignments could be determined. Instead, matches were considered putative, and a scoring system was employed to prioritize the most likely protein candidates. Higher scores were obtained for matches with longer peptide sequences, smaller mass deviations, and commonly observed adduct types (e.g. [M+H]⁺). Since scores were summed across all peptide m/z matches assigned to a protein, proteins supported by a larger number of consistent matches also accumulated higher overall scores, thus providing a relative weighting of possible identifications. In addition, the evaluation was focused on the most abundant proteins detected by LC-MS/MS, as proteins of low abundance are less likely to generate detectable signals in MALDI-MSI.

In the alveolar regions, protein matches were dominated by murine structural proteins, including keratins, spectrin isoforms, and vimentin, which together reflect the cytoskeletal and adhesion-related baseline profile of healthy lung parenchyma. This was confirmed by the LC-MS/MS analyses, since these and other cytoskeletal proteins were among the most abundant proteins. Interestingly, myeloperoxidase was also found to be a likely assignment among the alveolar-associated proteins and showed relative high abundance levels in the LC-MS/MS data set. This enzyme is an effector of neutrophil oxidative burst activity and its presence is necessary for killing A. fumigatus hyphae (Gazendam et al. 2016). Its localization outside of visible infection sites suggests that inflammatory activity extended beyond the focal lesions to non-affected tissue areas. This points to a more widespread inflammatory response within the infected lungs.

The fungal regions, by contrast, showed a mixed host-pathogen profile. Structural host proteins such as plectin, desmoplakin, spectrins, filamin-A, and myosin-9 remained prominent, but several proteins with immune-related functions were also considered as possible matches. Among these, complement C3 was consistently matched, which is in line with its reported binding and activation on A. fumigatus conidia and hyphae (Kozel et al. 1989, Dumestre-Perard et al. 2008, Wong et al. 2020). In addition, class I histocompatibility antigen was observed, suggesting presentation of fungal-derived antigens to cytotoxic T cells, as previously highlighted in dendritic cell-fungus interactions (Ramirez-Ortiz and Means 2012, Heinekamp et al. 2015). Another recurring host assignment was TAO3 kinase, a multifunctional signalling protein with roles in stress and immune signalling (Fang et al. 2020). Fungal proteins were also detected with high scores, including acetyl-CoA carboxylase, RNA polymerase, and ATP synthase subunit α, all of which are central to fungal primary metabolism and energy production. Catalase B (Cat1), an antioxidant enzyme and A. fumigatus antigen, was also found to be a probable association, which might contribute to protecting fungal hyphae from the respiratory burst of neutrophils (Calera et al. 1997, Paris et al. 2003).

Finally, the subset of nine MALDI peaks consistently observed across all biological replicates was examined more closely in an exemplary matching experiment using one fungal sample. This analysis revealed that individual features could often be attributed to multiple candidate proteins, spanning both fungal and host origin. For example, the conserved m/z feature at m/z 858.4 could correspond either to murine MHC class I complexes or to the fungal ATP synthase. Such overlaps underline both the challenges and the exploratory nature of this mass-matching, while the scoring-based prioritization provides a framework for identifying the most plausible candidates for future targeted validation.

In terms of mass accuracy and peak annotation, the present workflow faces the general limitations of MALDI-TOF-based imaging, namely moderate mass resolution and the resulting one-to-many mapping between measured features and theoretical peptide masses. While ultra-high-resolution MALDI-FTICR or Orbitrap-based systems (Schober et al. 2011, Dilillo et al. 2017) can substantially reduce mass ambiguity, such instrumentation remains less accessible and often involves trade-offs in acquisition speed and throughput. To mitigate these constraints, several computational approaches have been proposed for matching MALDI and LC-MS/MS datasets, including MALDImID (Oliveira and Longuespee 2023) ImShot (Aftab et al. 2022) and HIT-MAP (Guo et al. 2021). These tools generally implement matching strategies at the peptide level, which rank candidate identifications based on mass deviations, probabilistic models and, in some cases, calculated scores. In contrast, the present study applies a related, yet distinct approach by aggregating evidence at the protein level, integrating peptide length, mass deviation, and adduct probability. This decision was made, since studies have shown that MALDI and LC-MS/MS exhibit distinct and complementary selectivity toward different subgroups of peptides, depending on physicochemical properties such as amino acid composition, hydrophobicity, and the presence of modifications (Stapels and Barofsky 2004, Nadler et al. 2017). As a result, peptides that are robustly detected with ESI ionization in LC-MS/MS are often not observable under MALDI-MSI conditions, especially in complex tissue environments, and vice versa. Rather than seeking definitive identification, the aim was to create a structured framework for prioritizing plausible candidates within the limits of MALDI-TOF resolution.

While the present study demonstrates the potential of MALDI-MSI combined with LMD-LC-MS/MS for linking spatially defined m/z features to putative protein identities, further validation is required to strengthen these findings. Immunohistochemical approaches (Challa et al. 2015, Thomas et al. 2025) or antibody-based mass-tagging (Stauber et al. 2010, Yagnik et al. 2021) could be employed to spatially confirm the presence of selected host or fungal proteins within tissue sections. Such methods would, however, depend on the availability of highly specific antibodies, especially for fungal targets, which currently remain a limiting factor.

The integrated workflow itself provides a robust and flexible strategy for linking spatially resolved ion signals with proteomic identities. Compared to alternative protein identification methods summarized by Ryan et al. (2019) such as hydrogel extraction, bulk homogenization, or liquid extraction surface analysis, laser microdissection provides precise control over the geometry and composition of the isolated region while preserving histological integrity. In the present dataset, we successfully identified nine MALDI m/z features in the fungal region which are pathogen-associated and which allow to visualize A. fumigatus-infected areas of the lung. The corresponding proteomic analyses further revealed host proteins with increased abundance in infected lung tissue and several highly abundant fungal proteins. These proteins might serve as signatures for invasive A. fumigatus infection and could provide further insights into the host-pathogen interaction in prospective studies.
