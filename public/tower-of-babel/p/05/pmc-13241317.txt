Immuno-molecular features and therapeutic implications of brain metastases in clear cell renal cell carcinoma patients
Authors: I. Roodhooft, L. Kinget, G. Mammone, M. Baldewijns, PR Debruyne, E. Roussel, M. Albersen, O. Demeulenaere, AD Garg, PM Clement, J. Van Loon, S. De Vleeschouwer, H. Van Den Bulck, H. Vandermeulen, L. De Wever, A. Laerte, B. Beuselinck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain metastases (BrM) in metastatic clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (m-ccRCC) are resistant to most systemic therapies, but respond better to cabozantinib. We documented intracranial response on common systemic therapies for m-ccRCC, described molecular mechanisms underlying efficacy or resistance and performed whole transcriptome sequencing on primary ccRCC-tumors and metastatic lesions in distinct host organs, including the brain. 30 patients with BrM were available for the evaluation of intracranial response on 39 systemic therapy lines. No partial responses (PR) were observed upon ipilimumab/nivolumab (0%), and pazopanib (0%). PRs were rare upon nivolumab (14%), sunitinib (25%), axitinib (28%) and axitinib/pembrolizumab (33%), but higher upon cabozantinib/nivolumab (50%) and cabozantinib in monotherapy (64%). Median time-to-intracranial-progression was the longest (not reached) with cabozantinib and cabozantinib/nivolumab and the shortest with nivolumab and ipilimumab/nivolumab (2.5 months). mRNA-expression analysis showed higher cMET- and AXL-expression in BrM compared to the primary tumor and other metastatic sites, lower angiogenesis and immune scores, less CD8 + T-cells and more M2-like (anti-inflammatory) macrophages. Based on this translational and clinical data, cabozantinib is the optimal systemic therapy for m-ccRCC patients with BrM. As the presence of BrM impacts the choice of first-line treatment, we advise to screen for BrM at baseline, even in asymptomatic patients.

Introduction

At start of systemic therapy, BrM are found in 7-8% of patients with m-ccRCC [1, 2]. BrM lead to high morbidity and are usually more resistant to systemic therapies compared to other metastatic sites, due to the presence of the blood-brain-barrier (BBB), which reduces intracerebral penetration of therapeutic agents.

Data regarding the intracranial efficacy of systemic therapy in m-ccRCC remains limited, as most pivotal trials excluded patients with brain metastases. Moreover, many patients receive whole brain or stereotactic radiation therapy (RT) on the BrM before the start of systemic therapy, making the evaluation of intracranial responses on systemic therapy even more challenging. In case of shrinkage or stabilization, it is unclear if this effect was obtained by RT or by systemic therapy. Pseudoprogression induced by RT may also complicate interpretation. Intracranial response rate (RR) have been only reported in smaller retrospective patient series with cabozantinib [3] or prospective phase II studies with nivolumab [4] and sunitinib [5]. Data on efficacy on BrM of the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (VEGFR-TKIs) sorafenib, pazopanib, axitinib, lenvatinib, tivozanib and immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICP) combinations such as ipilimumab/nivolumab, axitinib/pembrolizumab, cabozantinib/nivolumab and lenvatinib/pembrolizumab are lacking.

Few data are available on molecular or broad immunological features of RCC BrM that could predict efficacy of systemic therapies. cMET, a specific additional target of the VEGFR-TKI cabozantinib, seems to be overexpressed in m-ccRCC BrM [6], which could explain the high intracranial activity of cabozantinib [3].

We aimed to compare intracranial response to different systemic therapies and then unravel potential molecular features that could underpin treatment response.

Materials and methods

We checked the UZLeuven database of m-ccRCC patients treated since 2005 with targeted therapies, containing data of 632 patients in March 2025. Most patients were treated at UZLeuven, but the database also includes data of patients treated at az groeninge (Kortrijk, Belgium) and az Imelda (Bonheiden, Belgium). This study was conducted according to the declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the hospital ethics committees (UZLeuven S63833 and S53479). All patients who start with therapy in the metastatic setting sign an informed consent that allows data collection and analysis. However, the ethic committee also permit the retrospective use of data of patients who died.

In order to study the specific effect of systemic therapies, without confounding factors, requirement for inclusion were: (A) presence of BrM at start of systemic therapy and (B) no RT on BrM within one month before the start of systemic therapy. (C) The patient had to reach at least one follow-up CT-scan of the brain after start of systemic therapy. (D) Finally, if after previous RT, performed more than one month before start of systemic therapy, the BrM were still decreasing at baseline CT-scan, the patient was excluded.

Intracranial response assessment was performed by RECIST, measuring the largest diameter of the BrM. We estimated median time-to-intracranial progression (TTIP), as calculated between the start of the systemic therapy and progression of the BrM, with Kaplan-Meier estimates and compared median TTIP between therapeutic subgroups with log-rank test. We also assessed intracranial response by RANO-criteria, combining anatomic evolution by RECIST with clinical evolution (deterioration or improvement) and evolution of corticosteroid use (decrease or increase). In patients receiving immunotherapy, the second CT-scan was also reviewed upon progression to rule out pseudoprogression.

We checked mRNA-expression of potential predictive biomarkers for distinct systemic therapies on the UZLeuven ccRCC molecular database. In March 2025, this database contained whole mRNA-expression of 716 samples: 9 BrM, 345 non-BrM, and 350 primary kidney tumors. The majority of these samples were resected before the start of systemic therapies. RNA extraction, sequencing and bio-informatical processing was performed as previously described in Kinget et al. [7]. In brief, archived formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary tumor samples and samples of resected metastases were retrieved and upon review of H&E slides, tissue blocks with the highest tumoral content were selected. Blank slides (5μm thickness) were produced and micro dissected to only include tumoral tissue. RNA was extracted using the Maxwell RSC RNA FFPE kit (Promega, Madison, WT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, cDNA libraries were prepared using the Forward QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using 5 μl of RNA and 16 PCR cycles. cDNA concentrations and fragment length were measured with the QubitTM dsDNA HS assay (Thermofisher, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and Bioanalyzer HS DNA electrophoresis (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Illumina cBOT was used for clonal cluster generation and RNA sequencing was performed using the HISeq 4000 kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, adaptors and optical duplicates were trimmed from the raw sequencing reads. Alignment was performed using HiSat2 and quantified using featureCounts and HTSeqCount. DESeq2 was used for further processing and normalization using VarianceStabilizingTransformation. Batch effects were corrected for using ComBat.

We studied intratumoral expression of several genes that could be involved in response to VEGFR-TKIs and ICPs: specific targets of cabozantinib (cMET, AXL), genes involved in angiogenesis (VEGFR1 (FLT1), VEGFR2 (KDR), VEGFR3 (FLT4) and IMmotion150-angiogenesis-score and JAVELIN101-angiogenesis-score) and genes and pathways involved in the immune microenvironment (CTLA4, PD1 (PDCD1), PDL1 (CD274) and IMmotion150-T-effector score and JAVELIN101-immuno-score). We also checked FGFR1, FGFR2 and FGFR3, involved in angiogenesis and specific targets of lenvatinib. We also performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (levering Hallmark gene sets) and CIBERSORTX (Leland Stanford Junior University, Stanford, CA) immune deconvolution. We compared the expression of these molecules and pathways in normal kidney tissue, primary kidney tumor, BrM and metastasis in other host organs in a paired (within one patient) and non-paired (in the global series) approach with a non-parametric approach (Kruskal-Wallis test). Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (version 10, San Diego, CA) and R Statistical Software (v.4.2.1, R Core Team, 2022). As few samples were available for a paired analysis, reliable statistical comparison of gene expression was not possible.

Results

We start with a case report, because it illustrates well the current challenges and possibilities in m-ccRCC patients with BrM.

Case report: impact of cabozantinib on brain metastases and oedema

A 61-year old male patient (patient 15 in the tables) underwent a nephrectomy for a pT1bN0M0 Fuhrman Grade 1 ccRCC. Twenty months later, brain, liver, lung, adrenal, peritoneal and paravertebral metastases were diagnosed. Cabozantinib (40 mg/day) and nivolumab (240 mg Q2W) were started since cabozantinib is very active intracranially and has antiphlogistic activity. We aimed to avoid the use of corticosteroids for control of the perilesional oedema, because of the specific adverse events of corticosteroids and the potential negative impact on nivolumab efficacy. We also aimed to avoid RT to avoid radionecrosis with oedema necessitating even more and longer exposure to corticosteroids. The BrM were asymptomatic.

At baseline, the two BrM had a diameter of 10 and 5 millimeters (mm) surrounded by oedema (Fig. 1A). After two months of cabozantinib/nivolumab, on CT-scanner the largest BrM measured 9 mm and the smallest BrM was barely detectable (Fig. 1B). After 5 months, the largest BrM had shrunk to 5 mm and the smaller BrM was no longer visible, with significantly diminished oedema (Fig. 1C). The peritoneal implants showed a decrease in volume, while extracranial disease in other locations remained unchanged. After 8 months, the diameter of the largest BrM was 4 mm. Progression at the level of fifth dorsal vertebra led to embolization and tumor debulking with hemilaminectomy and pediculectomy. Cabozantinib was interrupted peri-operatively. Due to postoperative complications adjuvant RT on the fifth dorsal vertebra was postponed and cabozantinib discontinued longer. Two months later, the patient presented with speech disorders, dysarthria and word-finding problems. On CT scan, the largest BrM had increased to 13 mm and multiple new BrM had emerged (Fig. 1D). Cabozantinib was restarted at 40 mg/day. After 1 month of cabozantinib, the largest lesion had decreased to 8 mm (Fig. 1E). After 4 months of cabozantinib, this BrM measured 5 mm and the other BrMs as well as the oedema had resolved. Two months later, fulminant thoraco-abdominal disease progression led to a switch to axitinib. Four months later, disease progression was observed with concomitant acute renal insufficiency. Together with the patient, we opted for a best supportive care. Soon after, the patient died.

This case demonstrates a clear, dose-dependent benefit of cabozantinib, both on the size of the BrM as on oedema, without use of corticosteroids or RT. The impact of nivolumab on the BrM seems of less importance, given the fact that the size of the BrM evolved depending on the dose of cabozantinib, while nivolumab administration was continued at the same dose since the start till the final progression on cabozantinib/nivolumab.

Included patients

According to the inclusion criteria, 30 patients were included in this study (patients characteristics in Table 1). In 6 patients, the intracranial efficacy of 2 sequential therapies could be studied and in one patient, the intracranial efficacy of 4 sequential therapies could be studied. Hence, a total of 39 observations were possible: 7 upon nivolumab, 1 upon ipilimumab/nivolumab, 4 upon sunitinib, 2 upon pazopanib, 3 upon axitinib/pembrolizumab, 7 upon axitinib, 4 upon cabozantinib/nivolumab and 11 upon cabozantinib. Systemic therapies were used in the approved setting: ipilimumab/nivolumab, axitinib/pembrolizumab, cabozantinib/nivolumab, sunitinib and pazopanib in first-line and axitinib, nivolumab and cabozantinib in monotherapy in second- or further line. Starting dose of the immunotherapy was the standard approved dose. Starting VEGFR-TKI dose was the standard approved dose in 23 cases and a lower dose in 8 cases: two patients started with axitinib at 2 * 3 mg/day (together with pembrolizumab), 5 patients with cabozantinib in monotherapy at 40 mg/day and one patient with cabozantinib in monotherapy at 20 mg/day. Hence, in cabozantinib-treated patients, the starting dose was more often inferior to standard of care compared to patients treated with other VEGFR-TKIs.

We excluded 31 cases from the study (Flowchart in Supplemental Figure. 1). Although these patients also had BrM at start of systemic therapy, they were not included for the following reasons. In 26 patients, radiation therapy was performed within one month before start of systemic therapy. In 4 patients, BrM were still decreasing after previous radiation therapy performed more than one month before the start of systemic therapy. Finally, one patient died before the second CT scan of the brain could take place in order to check intracranial response. These data show that there has been an evolution over the last years. As older therapies (sunitinib, axitinib, nivolumab and pazopanib), were considered to be less efficient on BrM, patients underwent more often radiation therapy before the start of systemic therapy. Nowadays, with the higher intracranial efficacy of cabozantinib, patients do not undergo upfront radiation therapy anymore.

Intracranial responses following systemic therapy

Intracranial response rate (RR) is reported for each systemic treatment in Table 3. No partial responses (PR) were seen in patients treated with ipilimumab/nivolumab (0%) and pazopanib (0%). PR were rare with nivolumab (14%), sunitinib (25%), axitinib (28%) or axitinib/pembrolizumab (33%), but higher upon cabozantinib/nivolumab (50%) or cabozantinib as monotherapy (64%). Two patients, treated with cabozantinib or cabozantinib/nivolumab, reached a complete intracranial response. The waterfall plot (Fig. 2A) depicts best response in each case. We performed a Kaplan-Meier estimate of TTIP of the BrM depending on type of systemic therapy (Fig. 2C). Poorest intracranial disease control duration was observed with ICPs (2.5 months), followed by sunitinib and pazopanib (8 months) and axitinib with or without pembrolizumab (not reached). Median intracranial disease control was the longest (not reached) in patients treated with cabozantinib with or without nivolumab (p = 0.008).

In preparation of a multivariate analysis, we checked the impact of possible confounders on univariate analysis (Supplemental Table 1). Given the reduced number of events and given the reduced number of factors associated with TTIP on univariate analysis, we performed in parallel two bivariate analysis. Patients were divided in two groups: cases treated with a regimen including cabozantinib (n = 15) and cases treated with other therapies (n = 24). Supplemental Figure. 2 shows that in both bivariate analysis (including corticosteroid use or previous radiation therapy), cabozantinib remained as independently associated with TTIP. Note that previous radiation therapy is correlated with poorer TTIP. This is due to the fact that with the availability of cabozantinib, we are less prone to irradiate BrM. BrM were more often irradiated in the era where less efficient systemic therapies were used. Note also that intracranial response on cabozantinib was not driven by more favorable patient characteristics such as reduced number of BrM, lower IMDC risk or neurologic symptoms.

In four patients (patients 15, 20, 21 and 28), we noticed a cabozantinib dose-dependent response: upon cabozantinib interruption or dose reduction, progression was noticed, while upon dose re-escalation or rechallenge, intracranial disease control was again observed. Patient 15 is reported extensively above. Patient 21 was treated with cabozantinib 40 mg/day plus nivolumab. He required a cabozantinib dose reduction to 20 mg/day due to toxic hepatitis. The clinical consequence of this reduction was progressive BrM. Good disease control was regained after the dose was re-escalated to 30 mg/day. Patient 20, treated with cabozantinib 40 mg/day, had a dose reduction to 20 mg/day after two months of therapy due to skin blisters. This led to the emergence of new BrM. Disease regression of the BrM was subsequently achieved after re-escalating cabozantinib to 40 mg/day. Patient 28, treated with cabozantinib in monotherapy, discontinued cabozantinib for two months due to toxic hepatitis, resulting in progressive BrM. While control was initially regained upon restarting at 20 mg/day, progression recurred eight months later. After a dose increase to 30 mg/day, the BrM were again under control.

We checked whether the poor response of BrM on ICPs could be attributed to pseudoprogression. Of the 5 patients whose BrM displayed progressive disease (PD) as best response, four received whole brain radiotherapy after the first CT scan showed PD. Hence, pseudoprogression could not be assessed. The other patient underwent a subsequent CT scan evaluation, that confirmed PD, and hence, excluded pseudoprogression.

Finally, we also studied intracranial response with RANO-criteria, taking in consideration, on top of RECIST, corticosteroid tapering and clinical improvement. Results are shown in Table 3. Briefly, the number of PR were identical between RECIST and RANO, because in all RECIST PR patients, there was no increase of corticosteroid use nor clinical degradation. All patients with RECIST PD as best response had also RANO PD, by definition. Only 4 out of 17 patients with RECIST SD switched to RANO PD because of an increase of corticosteroid use or clinical degradation.

Analyses of the molecular targets of systemic therapies

A total of 716 samples were included: 9 BrM (among them 2 pre-treated, one with ipilimumab/nivolumab and one with pazopanib followed by nivolumab), 345 non-BrM, 12 normal kidney samples and 350 primary kidney tumors. For the paired samples analysis, between BrM and primary kidney tumor, 6 patients were available (all not-pretreated samples).

Among all metastatic sites, AXL-expression was the highest in BrM. In a paired approach, AXL-expression was higher in BrM compared to primary tumor. Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to discover the underlying molecular reason for cabozantinib efficacy on BrM in m-ccRCC, in particular cMET and AXL-expression. Secondary aim was the analysis of other molecular pathways that are potential targets of available systemic therapies. Finally, we aimed to document intracranial responses on distinct systemic therapies.

In our series of 30 mRCC patients with BrM, allowing the evaluation of intracranial responses on 39 systemic therapy lines, we observed limited intracranial efficacy of ICPs and first generation VEGFR-TKIs such as sunitinib and pazopanib. Axitinib showed more intracranial activity but cabozantinib superseded all other systemic therapies. The high intracranial activity of cabozantinib can be explained in part by a high expression of its specific targets cMET and AXL in BrM compared to other metastatic sites, the primary tumor and normal kidney tissue. VEGFR2-expression and the IMmotion-angiogenesis score were significantly lower in BrM compared to other metastatic sites and the primary tumor, which could explain in part why VEGFR-TKIs, that do not inhibit cMET or AXL, have a reduced efficacy on BrM. Finally, several characteristics of the immune microenvironment could be correlated to reduced efficacy of ICPs on BrM: a lower infiltration of CD8 + T-cells, less T-regulatory cells, activated NK-cells and resting dendritic cells, and more monocytes and M2-like macrophages. The JAVELIN-immune and IMmotion-T-effector scores were lower in BrM compared to other metastatic sites. Such immunological features typically distinguish pervasive immuno-suppression in human malignancies, including in glioblastoma (GBM), together with profound hostility against T-cells thereby compromising ICP efficacy [7, 10, 11].

Six studies have been published looking at intracranial responses of systemic therapies in metastatic RCC (mRCC), excluding a potential effect of local therapies. Other studies combined local and systemic therapies. A phase II trial assessed the intracranial efficacy of sunitinib in 17 mRCC patients (16 clear-cell histology, 1 unknown histology) not treated with local therapies for BrM in the last six months. Among 16 evaluable patients, 5 patients (31%) displayed stable intracranial disease, but no responses were seen [5]. In a retrospective series of 7 mRCC patients with BrM (one with sarcomatoid dedifferentiation and six with clear-cell histology) treated with sunitinib, and who did not receive local therapy for their BrM, two patients (29%) achieved a PR, three (43%) SD, and two (28%) early PD [12]. The GETUG-AFU 26 NIVOREN phase II trial looked at intracranial responses on nivolumab in 73 m-ccRCC patients with BrM not requiring steroids. 39 (54%) patients had no prior local therapy (surgery or RT) for BrM. 5 patients were unevaluable due to rapid clinical progression and death before first evaluation. Response assessment on BrM in the remaining 34 patients was 12% of CR (all responders had CR), 38% of SD and 50% of early PD as best response [4]. In the Italian nivolumab expanded-access program, 389 patients (356 with clear-cell histology) were included. The inclusion criteria allowed systemic treatment with corticosteroids equivalent to up to 10 mg/day prednisone. 32 patients with BrM were included. RR on nivolumab was 19% (5 PR, 1 CR). It is unknown whether these patients received locoregional therapy on the BrM after the initiation of nivolumab [13]. In a multicentre, international, retrospective cohort study of patients with mRCC and BrM treated with cabozantinib, 33 patients (31 with clear-cell histology) did not receive concomitant RT. Intracranial RR was 55% [3]. One retrospective study included 12 mRCC patients with BrM who received cabozantinib in second (25%) or further treatment line (75%). Four patients received cabozantinib without brain-directed therapy: 2 of them obtained PR (50%) and 2 SD (50%) [14]. The CABRAMET phase II trial is the first prospective study to evaluate the effectiveness of cabozantinib on BrM. First results were presented at ASCO-GU 2025. 26 patients were included with at least one non-locally pretreated BrM. 16 patients (61.5%) reached a PR [15]. There is no data on axitinib, except a case report, showing a CR of BrM in second line after progression on first-line ipilimumab/nivolumab, showing that axitinib has activity on BrM [16].

cMET is a known HIF-1 inducible target, activated by the paracrine growth factor hepatocyte growth factor (HGF; also called Scatter Factor, SF). cMET is located on chromosome 7 and induces tumorigenesis. It is also involved in the immune microenvironment, modulating through the recruitment of neutrophils, driving an inflammatory immunosuppressive environment and inducing PDL1 expression [17, 18]. Evaluation of tyrosine kinase receptors in BrM of ccRCC reveals that cMET-overexpression is associated with a worse prognosis [6] and its activation correlates with the development of BrM, as shown in lung cancer [19]. cMET-expression was evaluated in 50 ccRCC BrM. cMET was expressed in 26 of 50 (52%) BrM, and in 2 out of seven (29%) primary tumors with corresponding BrM. The authors did not find any correlation between cMET-expression and polysomy 7, which points towards an alternative mechanism driving cMET-overexpression [6]. In 42 primary RCC tumors and 87 BrM, PD-L1-expression in tumor cells and immune cells and cMET-expression were assessed by immunohistochemistry. Positivity rate in the primary tumors was 12%, 50% and 0%, respectively. Positivity rate in BrM was 23%, 47% and 35%, respectively. Thus, cMET-expression was higher in BrM compared to the primary tumor [20]. In primary brain tumors, cMET is also highly expressed. A meta-analysis pooled the data of 5 studies assessing cMET-expression in GBM through immunohistochemistry. cMET was overexpressed in 33,9%-60,5% of the patients and associated with lower PFS and OS [21]. In another study, an elevated cMET-expression occurred in 35% of primary and 75% of recurrent GBM [22].

AXL is part of a subfamily of receptor tyrosine kinases called tumor associated macrophages (TAM) receptors. TAM receptors have a pro-carcinogenic role. Their overexpression is associated with tumor cell growth, metastasis and invasion. AXL is associated with drug resistance and poor prognosis in many cancer types, including ccRCC. Recently, in breast, pancreatic and lung cancer, AXL was linked with immune suppression and resistance to antitumor immunity. In 316 m-ccRCC patients treated with nivolumab after failure of VEGFR-TKIs, high AXL-expression in tumor cells was associated with lower RR and a trend to shorter PFS. Patients with tumors displaying concomitant PD-L1-expression and high AXL-expression had the worst OS [23]. AXL is expressed in 74% of GBM patients, across different expression patterns, with worse OS observed in the subgroup where AXL was expressed both in the tumor vasculature and hypercellular regions [24]. In GBM, AXL-overexpression is associated with therapy resistance to temozolomide and RT. However, when temozolomide and RT are combined with the AXL-inhibitor TKI-R428, the therapeutic effects significantly increase [25]. In lung carcinoma, AXL and ABL2 are involved in the development of BrM and elevated AXL-expression is associated with poor survival [26].

Treating BrM with ICPs is challenging. First, the large size of monoclonal antibodies limits their ability to cross the BBB [27], although the BBB might be disrupted in BrM. However, activated tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are able to traffic into the central nervous system in several ways: through the choroid plexus and into the cerebral spinal fluid, through superficial leptomeningeal vessels and into the subarachnoid space, or through postcapillary venules and into the perivascular space [28]. Secondly, studies that compared the tumor microenvironment (TME) of BrM with other sites demonstrated an intracranial immunosuppressive TME which can impact tumor progression and therapeutic efficacy. For instance, CD8 + T-cells in BrM have an exhausted-like hypofunctional phenotype [9, 28], which might be distinct from the severe dysfunction often observed in GBM-infiltrating CD8 + T-cells. [10]. In line with the literature, which indicates that immune-inflamed tumors are characterized by abundant CD8 + T-cells and improved responses to immunotherapy, we found a higher density of CD8 + T-cells in non-BrM [9]. Additionally, studies also show that this can also explain why some BrM are less sensitive to immunotherapy [29]. Our analysis indicates that BrM are characterized by a prominent accumulation of M2-like macrophages, which promote immunosuppression and negatively impact patient outcomes. We observed a lower expression of NK-cells in BrM. NK-cells can play a role in controlling metastatic disease, and their function can be impaired by the TME of the brain. Steindl et al. investigated 20 matched intracranial and extracranial specimens from 10 m-ccRCC patients with BrM. They found no difference in the PD-L1-expression consistent with our study. Unlike our study, there was no difference in CD8 + T-cells between intracranial and extracranial locations [30]. RT may promote inflammation and stimulate the immune system, which could contribute to improved responses to ICPs [31, 32].

Beside their expression of intestinal cells, kidney tubule cells and hepatocytes, efflux pumps such as multidrug resistance ATP binding cassette (ABC) transporters [33] are also expressed at the BBB. These efflux pumps can play a role in absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion of VEGFR-TKIs, but also control their intracranial penetration. While sunitinib is a substrate of ABCB1 (also called P-glycoprotein) and ABCG2 [34–36], cabozantinib is not known to be a substrate of ABCB1, which can explain higher BBB penetration. As an ABCB1 substrate, sunitinib will have lower intracerebral penetration and lower efficacy on BrM. Analysis of whole-brain lysates of non-tumor-bearing mice showed 20% of peak plasma levels, suggesting the ability of cabozantinib to cross the BBB [37]. Additional research on efflux pump and cabozantinib interactions is warranted.

VEGF is a highly potent agent for inflammation and vasodilatation. Hence, the antiphlogistic effect of VEGF-targeting agents can reduce brain oedema surrounding BrM. VEGFR-TKIs such as cabozantinib, pazopanib, sunitinib and axitinib were shown to reduce by 90.8% brain oedema volume surrounding brain lesions [38]. Hence, VEGFR-TKIs could lead to reduced need for corticosteroids. Efficient systemic therapy for BrM can also avoid adverse events of RT: radionecrosis after stereotactic RT and cognitive disorders after whole brain RT.

A limitation of our study are the small samples numbers for paired analysis, leading to results without statistical significance. A second limitation is the fact that we did not treat until now patients with BrM with lenvatinib (with or without pembrolizumab), as these compounds were only approved recently. Moreover, our results might be specific for RCC, because in malignant melanoma, ICPs perform well on BrM.

Conclusion

Among all available systemic therapies, cabozantinib seems to be the most efficient to control BrM in m-ccRCC. In part, this efficacy can be due to high cMET- and AXL-expression in ccRCC BrM. Hence, as the presence of BrM impacts the choice of first-line treatment, with a preference for cabozantinib/nivolumab, we propose to recommend screening for BrM at baseline, even in asymptomatic patients.
