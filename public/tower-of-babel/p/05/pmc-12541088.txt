Spatiotemporal modeling and mapping of traffic crashes using R programming and GIS: a case study of Bole sub city, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Meskelu Nedaga Haile, Kanenus Fufa Dararo, Yenealem Aderaw Agumasie, Firdissa Sadeta Tiye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoad traffic crashes are a significant public health concern globally, particularly in developing countries like Ethiopia, where the fatality rate is alarmingly high—reaching 4,984.3 deaths per 100,000 vehicles annually. This issue is especially severe in Addis Ababa, highlighting the urgent need for effective road safety interventions. This study aims to examine the spatial, temporal, and explanatory aspects of traffic crashes in order to provide evidence-based insights for policy development and intervention strategies. Using three years of crash data comprising 17,285 incidents, a comprehensive methodological approach was employed. Spatial analysis using Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) was conducted to identify crash hotspots, temporal analysis was used to assess trends over time, and explanatory analysis explored relationships between traffic characteristics, crash severity, and driver demographics. The findings reveal that traffic crashes are highly concentrated around squares and intersections, which are identified as major hotspots. The highest crash rates occur during the summer months (August to September), on weekends, and during peak traffic hours—morning (6 AM–12 PM) and afternoon (12 PM–6 PM). Over the three-year period, crash incidents peaked in 2020 and declined in 2022. Demographic analysis indicates that younger, less experienced, and male drivers are most frequently involved in crashes. Vehicle-to-vehicle collisions account for 88.7% of incidents, with 78.7% resulting in property damage only. Based on these findings, the study recommends improving data collection systems for continuous monitoring, upgrading road infrastructure in high-risk areas, and implementing educational campaigns to promote safer driving behaviors, particularly among vulnerable groups. The insights from this study are valuable for policymakers, local authorities, and the community in reducing traffic crashes and enhancing road safety in Bole Sub-City.Keywords: Bole Sub-City, GIS, Modeling, R Programming, Road Traffic Crashes, SpatiotemporalSubject terms: Biophysics, Engineering, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Data integration, Data mining

Introduction

Road traffic crashes, commonly referred to as collisions, have profound consequences in terms of loss of life, injuries, and significant economic harm. They represent a significant worldwide public health issue, affecting both developed and developing nations. According to the World Health Organization’s Global Status Report on Road Safety (WHO1), approximately 1.19 million people die each year as a result of road traffic crashes, with over 50 million people sustaining non-fatal injuries and many resulting in permanent disabilities. According to the report the majority, 90%, of global road fatalities happen in low and middle-income nations, despite these countries accounting for only about 54% of the world’s vehicles.

While traffic crashes are a global concern, their impacts are disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for 90% of global road fatalities despite possessing only about 54% of the world’s vehicles (WHO2). This disparity is especially acute in sub-Saharan Africa, where rapid urbanization and increasing motorization have far outpaced the development of safe and efficient transport infrastructure. The resulting imbalance creates hazardous environments for both motorists and pedestrians, compounding the public safety challenge.

Ethiopia is one of the East African countries most severely affected by traffic crashes. The country records an exceptionally high fatality rate—estimated at 4,984.3 deaths per 100,000 vehicles annually—far exceeding rates observed in other developing nations. In 2021 alone, 15,034 traffic crashes were recorded nationally, resulting in 4,161 deaths and over 10,000 injuries, not to mention significant property losses. Notably, driver behavior—such as failure to yield, tailgating, improper lane usage, and illegal turning—has been identified as the primary contributing factor in most crashes.

Addis Ababa, the capital city, bears a disproportionate share of the national burden, with over 60% of crashes occurring within its boundaries. The city’s rapid urban growth, dense traffic, and expanding road networks have not been matched by adequate safety interventions or data-driven traffic planning. Between 2000 and 2005, nearly 44,000 traffic crashes occurred in Addis Ababa, with a significant portion happening in just a few hotspot districts.

Among these, Bole Sub City has emerged as one of the most critical crash-prone zones. Characterized by high vehicular and pedestrian flow due to commercial centers, international gateways (e.g., Bole International Airport), shopping malls, and major arterial roads, Bole experiences a persistent and troubling frequency of crashes. The area not only serves as a major conduit for intra-city traffic but also features a dense mix of vulnerable road users. According to the Bole Sub City Traffic Police Department, the sub-city frequently records crashes that result in fatalities, injuries, and significant property damage—especially in commercial corridors with high pedestrian activity.

Despite the growing severity of the issue, there remains a notable lack of localized, data-driven studies that explore the spatial and temporal patterns of traffic crashes in Ethiopian cities. Most existing research is limited to national statistics or general trends, offering little actionable insight at the sub-city level where interventions are most needed.

This study addresses this gap by conducting a comprehensive case study of traffic crash patterns in Bole Sub City. Leveraging detailed crash records and geospatial analysis, the research seeks to identify high-risk zones, understand contributing factors, and propose context-specific solutions. By focusing on a highly dynamic and crash-prone area within Addis Ababa, the study not only adds granularity to the national discourse but also provides a model for localized traffic safety interventions. The findings aim to inform urban planners, policymakers, and traffic safety agencies, thereby contributing to evidence-based strategies for reducing crashes and improving road safety in rapidly urbanizing African cities.

Its topography is characterized by a blend of moderately sloping hilly terrain, flat expanses around major roads and transport hubs, and diverse drainage patterns, with low-lying areas susceptible to flooding. Bole Sub-City was selected as the study area because of its significant traffic volume, which stems from its dual role as a commercial center and a major transportation hub. The area experiences heavy vehicle and pedestrian traffic, largely due to the presence of the Central Business District, which serves as a critical transit point and a key route for vehicles entering and exiting the region.

Sources of data

The study utilizes secondary data, which typically includes information on historical traffic crashes, including date, day, and time of accident, Location of accident, age of defendant, education level, and degree of severity from the Bole sub city police department. These data are used for mapping crashes and understanding their geographical distribution. On the other hand, non-spatial data encompasses various attributes that describe the crashes and the individuals involved in the crashes. This includes the date and time of crashes, which help in identifying temporal trends such as peak accident day, week, and month variations. The types and severity of crashes provide insights into the nature and impact of these incidents, which is critical for developing targeted intervention strategies. Demographic details such as the age, gender, education level, and driving experience of the defendants are used for understanding the human factors contributing to traffic crashes. Methods of data analysis

The method of data analysis involves several key steps: first, data is collected from the Bole Sub City Police Department, including non-spatial data on traffic accidents and spatial data on road networks and administrative boundaries. Next, data preprocessing is conducted, which includes cleaning to remove duplicates, standardizing formats, geocoding for geographic coordinates, and integrating the datasets. The analysis phase consists of explanatory analysis to explore relationships between traffic accidents and various factors, temporal analysis to identify long-term and short-term trends, and spatial analysis to pinpoint hotspots and distribution patterns of accidents (Fig. 2). Finally, model development is performed to create visualizations such as maps, graphs, and charts to effectively communicate the findings.

Methodological flow chart of the study.

Data preprocessing

The data preprocessing phase consisted of several critical steps to prepare both spatial and non-spatial data for integrated analysis. Initially, data cleaning was performed to correct typographical errors, standardize units, and address inconsistencies in the crash attribute dataset. Duplicate crash entries were then identified and removed to ensure the uniqueness of each record and maintain data integrity. The next step involved transforming various file types and structures into a format compatible with geographic information system (GIS) tools and statistical software. Geocoding was subsequently carried out, assigning geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) to each crash location based on available descriptive information. Finally, data integration was completed by merging the geocoded crash data with spatial layers, including road networks and administrative boundaries obtained from the Space Science and Geospatial Institute (SSGI). This resulted in a unified, analysis-ready dataset.

Explanatory analysis

The explanatory analysis phase focused on exploring the relationships between traffic crash characteristics and demographic, behavioral, and contextual variables. Specifically, the study examined how crash types and severity levels were associated with factors such as the age and gender of involved individuals, time of day, and type of road user (driver, passenger, or pedestrian). This analysis aimed to identify which demographic groups are most vulnerable to crashes and under what circumstances they tend to occur. The resulting insights provided evidence on how different population segments are disproportionately affected by traffic crashes, informing potential safety interventions tailored to specific risk profiles within Bole Sub City.

Temporal analysis

Temporal analysis was conducted to investigate patterns and trends in traffic crash occurrences over time. The analysis was divided into two scales: long-term and short-term. Long-term trends were examined on a yearly basis to detect any increase or decrease in crash frequency over the study period. Short-term trends included monthly, daily, and hourly patterns, which helped uncover high-risk periods such as peak traffic hours or specific days of the week. This level of granularity allowed for the identification of temporal hotspots, such as rush-hour congestion periods or weekend spikes, that can inform time-based traffic enforcement and awareness strategies.

Spatial analysis

Spatial analysis was used to understand the geographic distribution and clustering of traffic crashes within Bole Sub City. Using GIS-based techniques such as kernel density estimation (KDE) and hotspot analysis, the spatial component of the study identified areas with high concentrations of crashes—commonly referred to as crash hotspots. This analysis also revealed spatial disparities in crash severity and frequency, allowing for the prioritization of specific road segments, intersections, or pedestrian zones for intervention. The integration of spatial analysis provided actionable insights for urban traffic planners and safety authorities seeking to reduce crash risks through location-specific infrastructure improvements or policy measures.

Visualization

The final step involved developing models and creating visual outputs to communicate findings effectively. These outputs included thematic maps showing crash locations and hotspots, bar charts and line graphs to illustrate crash trends over time, and pie charts to summarize categorical variables such as crash type or involved road users. Visualization played a central role in translating complex datasets into digestible formats for decision-makers, stakeholders, and the general public. The visualized results not only enhanced the interpretability of the findings but also supported evidence-based planning, policy formulation, and community-level awareness initiatives.

Results and discussions

Occurrence of traffic crashes in Bole sub-city (2020 to 2022)

As shown in Fig. 3, the number of traffic crashes in Bole Sub-city of Addis Ababa declined consistently over the three consecutive years (2020–2022). The highest number of crashes—6450—was recorded in 2020, followed by 5707 in 2021, and 5128 in 2022. This downward trend is likely attributed to improved traffic management, stricter enforcement of traffic regulations, and increased public awareness campaigns. A linear regression analysis of the trend shows a very strong fit, with a coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.995), indicating that 99.5% of the variation in crash numbers can be explained by the year. Moreover, the p-value of 0.0455 indicates that the decline is statistically significant at the 5% level, suggesting that the observed trend is unlikely due to random chance. These findings align with those reported by the Johns Hopkins International Injury Unit (2022), which emphasized the effectiveness of evidence-based interventions in reducing urban traffic-related incidents. Likewise, research conducted in Kampala, Uganda, by Tumwesigye et al.3 found that intensified public education programs and the deployment of more traffic police officers contributed to a noticeable reduction in road traffic crashes. These findings suggest that institutional commitment to traffic law enforcement and public education can yield positive outcomes in reducing road traffic accidents in rapidly urbanizing African contexts.

The relationships between trafic crashes and level of education

The heatmap(Fig. 4) illustrates the relationship between education level and crash severity. Notably,it shows that individuals with higher education exhibit the highest proportion of fatal crashes (0.57) and minor injuries (0.52), followed by a substantial share in major injuries (0.39). This finding may appear counterintuitive but aligns with previous research suggesting that more educated drivers often exhibit overconfidence, engage in high-speed driving, or experience higher road exposure due to frequent travel. For instance, a study by Assefa et al.4 in Addis Ababa found that drivers with higher education levels were more likely to violate speed regulations, which increased the likelihood of fatal outcomes. Similarly, international research from Iran by Jahanbakhsh et al. observed that drivers with secondary and tertiary education were disproportionately involved in serious crashes due to risky behavior in urban environments.

Drivers with secondary education are associated with the highest proportion of property damage crashes (0.54) and major injuries (0.48). This pattern may indicate a moderate level of driving skill and road engagement that does not necessarily translate into safer behavior. Although these individuals may have sufficient knowledge to navigate roads, they might lack advanced defensive driving skills, resulting in moderate to severe crashes. Conversely, those with primary education show the lowest proportions across all crash severity levels, suggesting limited road exposure or possibly more cautious driving behavior. The illiterate group, interestingly, shows zero involvement across all crash categories, which may reflect underreporting, lack of formal licensing, or exclusion from formal transport systems—an issue highlighted by Bishaw and Teferi5 in their study on road safety in Ethiopia.

Globally, the World Health Organization2 has emphasized that education alone does not guarantee safer driving outcomes. While education can enhance awareness of traffic rules, it does not necessarily translate into safer behavior on the road. Research from South Africa by Mabunda et al.6 supports this view, showing that educated drivers are not immune to distractions, overconfidence, or risky driving patterns. The results in the heatmap thus indicate a non-linear relationship between education and crash severity, challenging assumptions that higher education equates to lower crash risk.

Between 2020 and 2022, traffic accident trends in Bole Sub-City of Addis Ababa revealed both notable increases and decreases across different accident types, reflecting shifting dynamics in road safety and urban traffic behavior (Table 3). As shown on the table, the total number of crashes decreased significantly from 6450 in 2020 to 5128 in 2022, indicating some improvement in traffic management or behavioral change. This overall decline aligns with findings by Deme and Minale (2021), who reported that enhanced enforcement and public campaigns in Addis Ababa led to a measurable reduction in traffic crashes. Similarly, Yilma et al. (2020) found that interventions like increased police patrols and installation of speed calming measures contributed to a decline in accident rates in certain districts of Addis Ababa. Among accident types, vehicle-to-vehicle crashes dominated throughout the three years, constituting around 86% of total crashes. This high percentage supports the findings by Abegaz et al., who noted that inter-vehicle collisions are the most common type of traffic crashes in urban Ethiopia due to congestion, poor lane discipline, and aggressive driving behavior. Despite a decrease in absolute numbers, the consistent dominance of this accident type highlights a continued challenge in managing urban traffic flow and driver behavior.

In contrast, vehicle-to-pedestrian crashes declined both in frequency and share—from 319 (4.9%) in 2020 to 177 (3.5%) in 2022. This is encouraging and may reflect the effect of increased pedestrian infrastructure, improved traffic signal systems, and awareness campaigns. This trend is supported by Gizaw and Yifredew (2023), who noted that pedestrian fatalities in Addis Ababa declined following the construction of more sidewalks and pedestrian bridges. However, this contradicts findings from some other African cities, such as Nairobi, where pedestrian accident rates remain high despite similar interventions, due to enforcement gaps and infrastructure maintenance issues (Kimathi et al., 2021).

Interestingly, vehicle-to-inanimate object crashes increased both in absolute numbers and percentage—from 428 (6.6%) in 2020 to 480 (9.4%) in 2022. This trend may be attributed to distracted driving, poor visibility, and inadequate road markings. Studies by Asmare and Andualem (2022) highlight that urban crashes involving stationary objects in Ethiopia are increasingly associated with the growing use of mobile phones while driving, poor signage, and deteriorating road infrastructure. This finding is consistent with international studies, such as those by Peden et al. (2013), which link urban crashes with inanimate objects to vehicle speed, road design, and driver inattention.

Vehicle-to-passenger crashes showed a dramatic decline from 33(0.5% )cases in 2020 to only 3(0.1%) in 2022, likely due to stricter regulations on public transport safety and possibly improved vehicle standards. This reduction corresponds with the findings of Mengistu et al. (2022), who observed that the introduction of transport modernization programs in Addis Ababa (such as the BRT system and standardized taxis) contributed to safer conditions for passengers.

Finally, vehicle overturns and vehicle-to-rail crashes remained rare and relatively stable, together making up less than 1% of total crashes. These low numbers are not uncommon in urban centers like Addis Ababa, where road networks are more regulated and heavy or high-speed vehicle movement is less frequent. These findings align with those of WHO (2023), which states that overturn crashes are more prevalent on highways or poorly maintained rural roads.

Traffic crashes by their severity level

Between 2020 and 2022, the analysis of crash severity types reveals a generally declining trend in total reported crashes in the study area (Table 4). Specifically, total crashes decreased from 6,450 in 2020 to 5,707 in 2021, and further to 5,128 in 2022. This steady decline may suggest improvements in traffic law enforcement, road safety awareness. However, this trend warrants further investigation to determine whether the decline is due to actual improvements in road safety or underreporting.

An important trend observed is the significant increase in minor injury crashes, which rose from just 63 in 2020 to 131 in 2022. This increase, alongside the simultaneous decline in serious injuries—from 313 in 2020 to 156 in 2022—may indicate that while crashes are still occurring, their severity has reduced. Possible contributing factors include improved emergency response systems, increased public awareness about road safety, and the growing use of protective measures such as seatbelts and helmets. It may also reflect a reduction in vehicle speeds due to better traffic calming mechanisms or urban congestion.

Property damage-only crashes have also shown a downward trend, decreasing from 6,015 in 2020 to 4,786 in 2022. This decline could be attributed to safer road designs, improved vehicle maintenance, or increased caution among drivers. Meanwhile, fatal crashes, although fluctuating slightly, show no clear trend—declining from 59 in 2020 to 49 in 2021, then rising again to 55 in 2022. This inconsistency points to a need for more targeted and sustained interventions to reduce road fatalities, especially in high-risk areas.

These findings align with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and other countries. For instance, Abegaz et al. highlighted that traffic fatalities in Ethiopia have long been a public health concern due to speeding, overloading, and weak enforcement. The decline in serious injuries observed in this study suggests that some of the recent road safety interventions in Ethiopia—such as improved driver training and urban traffic enforcement—are beginning to yield positive results. Tulu et al. (2017), in their study of road safety in Addis Ababa, emphasized the role of pedestrian behavior and poor road infrastructure in causing serious injuries. The significant reduction in serious injury crashes observed from 2020 to 2022 may be attributed to improved pedestrian infrastructure and better traffic control systems in urban areas.

Internationally, the World Health Organization’s Global Status Reports on Road Safety (2018, 2023) highlight the effectiveness of consistent enforcement and emergency response systems in reducing crash severity. Countries that implemented similar measures—such as Rwanda and Vietnam—have also recorded declines in serious and fatal injuries. Jørgensen and Abane7, studying road safety in Sub-Saharan Africa, found that infrastructure upgrades, such as speed bumps and roundabouts, significantly reduced crash severity. The trends observed in Ethiopia mirror these findings, suggesting that continued investment in infrastructure and behavioral change campaigns are crucial (Table 5).

Temporal Patterns of Traffic Accident Occurrence in Bole Sub-City

Analyzing the temporal patterns of traffic accident occurrence in Bole Sub-City is essential for improving road safety, optimizing resource allocation, and informing policy decisions. Temporal analysis helps identify high-risk periods—specific hours of the day, days of the week, or months of the year—when crashes are more frequent. This enables traffic enforcement agencies to deploy patrols and emergency services more strategically (Yilma et al., 2020).Moreover, studies have shown that understanding time-based trends contributes to reduced accident severity and fatalities through timely interventions and effective law enforcement.

Distribution of crashes occurred during the daytime hours

Figure 5 illustrates the temporal distribution of traffic crashes in Bole Sub-City, Addis Ababa. It reveals that the highest number of crashes occurred during the daytime hours, specifically between 06:00–12:00 (4,800 crashes) and 12:00–18:00 (4,700 crashes). This trend is likely influenced by high traffic volume during commuting and business hours when people are traveling to work, school, or conducting other activities. In contrast, accident frequency drops significantly in the evening (18:00–24:00, with 2,100 crashes) and is at its lowest in the early morning (01:00–06:00, with only 700 crashes), likely due to reduced traffic flow during these times.

This temporal pattern aligns with findings from both Ethiopian and international studies. For instance, a study conducted by Abera et al. (2018) in Addis Ababa similarly reported that most crashes occur during peak traffic hours, especially in the morning and early afternoon, correlating with intense vehicular and pedestrian activity. Internationally, Quddus (2008) reported comparable results in a study conducted in the UK, where traffic crashes peaked during rush hours and declined during nighttime. Likewise, Mohammed et al. (2020), analyzing traffic crashes in Mekelle City, Ethiopia, found that traffic volumes and pedestrian movements during daytime were significant factors contributing to accident rates.

However, studies such as Getachew (2016) have noted some divergence, suggesting that while daytime crashes are more frequent, crashes occurring during nighttime hours may be more severe due to factors like high-speed driving, low visibility, and impaired driving. This contrast highlights that while daytime may have higher frequency, nighttime can pose greater severity in individual incidents.

Overall, the current finding underscores the importance of time-specific traffic management strategies in urban areas like Bole Sub-City. These may include stricter law enforcement during high-risk daytime periods, traffic calming measures, and awareness campaigns targeting both drivers and pedestrians.

Studies show that the types of crashes tends to vary with time, with late-night and early-morning crashes often being more severe due to factors like fatigue, alcohol, and lower visibility (Zhou et al., 2014; NHTSA, 2020).

In contrast, other accident types—such as vehicle-to-rail, vehicle-to-pedestrian, vehicle-to-passenger, vehicle-to-inanimate objects, and vehicle overturns—occurred far less frequently, suggesting a lower probability of these incidents or potentially underreported cases.

The distribution of traffic crashes Occurrence across the days of the week

Analyzing the distribution of traffic accident occurrences across the days of the week is important because it helps identify temporal patterns and high-risk periods when crashes are more likely to happen. Understanding these patterns enables traffic safety authorities and policymakers to allocate resources more effectively, such as increasing traffic enforcement or awareness campaigns on days with higher accident rates10.

As shown on Fig. 7, there was variation in the distribution of crashes across the days of the week. The findings reveal that Fridays were experienced the highest number of crashes, followed by Mondays, Saturdays, Thursdays, Tuesdays, and Wednesdays. Notably, Sundays show the lowest number of crashes, which could be attributed to reduce traffic volume as fewer vehicles are typically on the road. This pattern suggests that weekdays and the start of the weekend carry greater risks for traffic incidents, potentially due to increased commuter and leisure travel. These findings are consistent with previous studies such as those by Smith et al. and Lee and Park, who also reported elevated accident rates on Fridays and Mondays, correlating with peak traffic congestion periods.

Overall, the data underscores the importance of targeted traffic safety interventions on high-risk days, supporting the development of preventative measures such as enhanced law enforcement, public awareness campaigns, and road safety audits tailored to specific weekday risks.

Similarly, in the United States, reports from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) indicate that weekdays—especially during peak commuting hours—are associated with a greater number of accidents. In India, Ranjan et al. (2021) also reported that traffic accidents tend to increase during weekdays as a result of school and office-related mobility. Moreover, research from South Africa highlighted a similar pattern, though it also noted that while fewer crashes occur on weekends, they tend to be more severe due to risky behaviors such as drunk driving (Peden et al., 2004).

Overall, the results of this analysis are consistent with international trends, reinforcing the importance of implementing weekday-specific traffic safety measures. These could include targeted traffic law enforcement during peak hours, improved traffic management systems, and public education campaigns focused on commuter safety.

Moreover, global studies such as Zhou et al. This pattern reinforces the need for targeted traffic management and enforcement strategies during weekdays to mitigate the risk of crashes, particularly vehicle-to-vehicle collisions.

For instance, studies by Elvik and Vaa11 and Shankar, Mannering, and Barfield report increased crashes during winter months due to poor road conditions and reduced visibility, which may explain the higher accident numbers in January and December. In general, the findings this research demonstrate notable distinctions from earlier research on traffic accident analysis. For example, Temesgen Haile’s (2019) investigation into road traffic accident hotspots in Hosanna Town primarily utilized GIS methodologies to pinpoint high-risk areas but did not incorporate advanced temporal modeling or demographic considerations into the analysis. Conclusions and recommendations

Conclusion

This study investigated the spatial and non-spatial characteristics of traffic crashes in Bole Sub-City, analyzing three years of data comprising 17,285 reported incidents. The analysis integrated temporal and spatial dimensions to examine patterns in accident occurrence, severity, driver demographics, and high-risk locations. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) was used to visualize accident hotspots, which tended to cluster around major intersections and traffic squares.

The temporal analysis suggested that traffic crashes were relatively higher during the summer months—particularly in August and September—and appeared more frequent on weekends, especially from Friday to Saturday. Most incidents were concentrated during high-traffic periods in the morning and afternoon. The data showed a spike in crashes in 2020, followed by a decline by 2022, pointing to fluctuations in road safety over time.

Demographic patterns indicated that younger, male, and less-experienced drivers were more often involved in reported traffic crashes. Drivers with secondary-level education also appeared more frequently among those recorded in collisions. Vehicle-to-vehicle crashes represented the largest share of incidents (about 89%), with property damage being the most common reported outcome.

Spatial analysis suggested that certain urban features—such as intersections, bus terminals, and commercial hubs—were associated with higher concentrations of crashes. These results provide useful insights for urban planners and traffic authorities, emphasizing the importance of considering both driver-related and infrastructural factors in future road safety interventions.
