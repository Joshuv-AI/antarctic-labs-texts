Mulberry leaf powder alleviates fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome in laying hens via the gut–liver axis and hepatic metabolic regulation
Authors: Ling Xuan, Hongli Liu, Heng Zhang, Qingtao Gao, Yan Shang, Xuelan Liu, Peipei Yan, Tianhong Shi, Chunyan Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of mulberry leaf powder (MLP) on alleviating fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) in laying hens. A total of 180 healthy 33-week-old Hy-Line Gray laying hens were randomly assigned to 3 groups, with 6 replicates per group and 10 chickens per replicate, and fed a basal diet (CON), high-energy–low-protein (HELP) diet (FLHS), and HELP + 5% MLP diet (MLP), respectively. The experiment lasted 56 days. Results showed that the HELP diet successfully induced FLHS, characterized by an increased liver index, hepatic steatosis, and oxidative stress (P < 0.05). MLP significantly alleviated FLHS-induced hepatic steatosis, and enhanced antioxidant capacity (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, total antioxidant capacity, and glutathione) in serum and liver (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic analysis identified 102 differentially expressed genes, whose expression was increased by FLHS but reversed by MLP, including SREBF1, SLC27A4, PLIN2, ACOT13, APOA4, GLRX, and MSRB1, which mainly participate in lipid droplet dynamics and redox homeostasis. qRT-PCR confirmed that upregulated expression of lipogenic genes (such as FASN, ACACA, SCD, and ELOVL6) and the downregulated expression of oxidative stress–responsive genes (SOD1 and GPX1) in the FLHS group were normalized by MLP (P < 0.05), accompanied by increased expression of PPARα, CPT1A, and CD36 (P<0.05). 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that MLP reduced the abundance of Desulfovibrio, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Intestinimonas, and Shuttleworthia, and enriched Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium in the cecum of FLHS laying hens (P < 0.05). Furthermore, MLP increased acetate, propionate, and butyrate levels, and decreased isobutyrate and isovalerate levels (P < 0.05). Correlation analysis showed that cecal acetate, propionate, butyrate, Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium were positively correlated with hepatic antioxidant enzyme activities (P < 0.05), and negatively correlated with hepatic lipogenic gene expression, hepatic lipid deposition and oxidative stress markers (P < 0.05). These findings indicated that MLP alleviated hepatic lipid metabolism disorders and oxidative stress in FLHS laying hens, which might be achieved by modulating gut microbiota structure and short-chain fatty acid production.

Introduction

Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) is one of the major metabolic diseases in high-yielding laying hens, which impairs liver function and reduces egg production performance (Zhuang et al., 2019). This disease is characterized by excessive lipid deposition in the liver, oxidative stress, and increased susceptibility to liver rupture and hemorrhage. It is usually induced by nutritional imbalances, such as high-energy and low-protein (HELP) diets (Rozenboim et al., 2016; Shini et al., 2019). Although FLHS is prevalent in modern intensive poultry production systems, effective nutritional strategies for its prevention or alleviation remain limited.

Previous studies showed that metabolic liver diseases are not only regulated by hepatic metabolic processes, but also closely associated with the gut microbiota. Gut microbiota dysbiosis can disrupt host lipid metabolism and promote fatty liver development through altered production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which participate in gut–liver axis signaling. SCFAs can enter the circulation via the portal vein, and thereby regulate lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in the liver (Joon et al., 2024). Conversely, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can influence SCFA production, impair intestinal barrier function, and increase microbial translocation to the liver, thereby exacerbating hepatic metabolic disorders (Hsu and Schnabl, 2023). In poultry, alterations in gut microbiota composition have been confirmed to be associated with host lipid metabolism disorders and fatty liver development (Zhang et al., 2026), suggesting that FLHS is closely linked to dysregulation of the gut microbiota–SCFA–liver axis. Therefore, nutritional interventions targeting the gut microbiota–SCFA–liver axis may represent a promising strategy for the prevention and alleviation of FLHS in laying hens.

Mulberry leaves contain a variety of bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides, flavonoids, 1-deoxynojirimycin, and alkaloids (He et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018), and these bioactive compounds have been reported to exhibit multiple biological activities, such as regulating lipid metabolism, improving glucose homeostasis, and exerting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Wang et al., 2010, 2022). Previous studies showed that supplementation with 3% fermented MLP in diet improved intestinal structure and feedstuff nutrient digestibility, and enhanced growth performance in broilers (Ding et al., 2021). Supplementation with 6% MLP in canine diets reduced the serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1 (APO-A1) levels, increased serum immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels, and altered gut microbiota composition (Yu et al., 2023). Supplementation with 9% MLP in the diets of finishing pigs enhanced serum immune function, improved meat quality, upregulated the mRNA expression level of Occludin and Mucin-2 in intestine, and promoted beneficial microbiota enrichment in the hindgut (Liu et al., 2021). Adding 40 mg/kg of 1-deoxynojirimycin in broiler diets significantly enhanced jejunum antioxidant activity via increasing the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), and improved intestinal barrier function (Zhao et al., 2024). Supplementation with 60 mg/kg mulberry leaf flavonoids in the diets of aged breeder hens effectively regulated hepatic lipid metabolism and promoted fatty acid β-oxidation, ultimately improving reproductive performance (Huang et al., 2022). These studies indicated that MLP and its bioactive components play important roles in regulating lipid metabolism and maintaining intestinal homeostasis. However, the effects of MLP and its bioactive compounds on alleviating FLHS in laying hens, and whether the gut microbiota–SCFA–liver axis is involved in these regulatory effects remain unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of MLP on alleviating FLHS and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the gut microbiota–SCFA–liver axis. These findings will provide a theoretical basis for the practical application of MLP in laying hens.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China (Approval No. SAAS-2024-G23).

Chemical analysis

All diets were analyzed according to AOAC (2006) methods to determine crude protein (CP; method 984.13), calcium (Ca; method 968.08), and total phosphorus (method 946.06).

Experimental materials

Mulberry leaves were collected from a local farm, washed thoroughly with clean water, air-dried at room temperature for 24 h, and subsequently oven-dried at 55 °C to constant weight. The dried leaves were ground using a laboratory grinder and passed through a 40-mesh sieve to obtain uniform MLP. The prepared MLP was sealed in airtight bags, stored at 4 °C, and used within 4 wk to preserve its quality. Experimental design and animal management

A total of 180 healthy 33-week-old Hy-Line Gray laying hens with similar body weight (1.69 ± 0.14 kg) were randomly assigned to three groups, with six replicates per group and ten hens per replicate. The control group (CON) was fed a basal diet. The FLHS group was fed the HELP diet. The MLP group was fed the HELP diet supplemented with 5% MLP, which was previously reported to effectively improve laying performance and egg quality in laying hens (Yi et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026). The MLP was homogeneously mixed into the HELP diet using a horizontal mixer. The ingredient composition and nutrient levels of the diets are shown in Table 2. The experimental diets were formulated according to the nutrient requirements for laying hens recommended by the National Research Council (NRC, 1994). The experimental period lasted 8 weeks. All hens had free access to feed and water. Productive performance

Daily egg number and total egg weight were recorded for each replicate. Laying rate (%) was calculated as the number of eggs produced divided by the number of hens housed, multiplied by 100. Feed intake was recorded weekly to calculate average daily feed intake (ADFI). mean egg weight was calculated as total egg weight divided by the number of eggs. Egg mass (g/hen/d) was calculated as the product of mean egg weight (g) and laying rate (%). The feed-to-egg ratio (F/E) was calculated as feed intake divided by egg mass.

Sample collection

On day 56 of the experiment, one hen was randomly selected from each replicate (n = 18) after 12 h of fasting with free access to water. Blood was collected from the wing vein, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min. The serum was collected and stored at −80 °C. The hens were weighed and then euthanized by jugular exsanguination. Fresh liver and abdominal fat were weighed to calculate the liver index and abdominal fat percentage.Liverindex(%)=(liverweight/bodyweight)×100Abdominalfatpercentage(%)=(abdominalfatweight/bodyweight)×100

The whole liver of each hen was photographed. Liver samples were stored at −80 °C for further analysis, and a portion of liver tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for histopathological examination. Cecal contents were aseptically collected from each bird and stored at −80 °C.

Serum biochemical and antioxidant assays

Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), malondialdehyde (MDA), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Histological examination

Liver tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 24 h, processed routinely, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) staining. For hepatic lipid accumulation, fresh liver samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek, Japan), cryosectioned at 8–10 μm, and stained with Oil Red O (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Hepatic biochemical and antioxidant assays

Hepatic TG, TC, MDA, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), T-SOD, glutathione (GSH), and GSH-Px levels were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance values were recorded using a microplate reader (Nanjing Detie Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) at the specified wavelength.

Transcriptomic analysis

Transcriptome sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Total RNA was extracted from liver samples using TRIzol reagent and quantified using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Poly(A) mRNA was enriched, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed to synthesize cDNA for strand-specific library construction. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. Transcriptome data were processed using the OmicStudio cloud platform. Gene expression levels were quantified as Fragments Per Kilobase Million (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria of |log2 fold change| > 1.2 and P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction assay

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using a Simply P Total RNA Extraction Kit (Bioer Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized using an Evo M-MLV RT Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver.2 (Accurate Biotechnology, Hunan, China). qRT-PCR was performed using a SYBR Green Premix Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, Hunan, China) on a LifeReady 1000XVI Real-Time PCR System (Suizhen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). The specific primers used are listed in Table S1. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the internal control gene (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The consistency between qRT-PCR data and transcriptomic FPKM values was evaluated by linear regression after min–max normalization (scaling coefficient = 0.1).

16S rRNA sequencing analysis

16S rRNA sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Total DNA was extracted from cecal contents using a Fecal Genome DNA Extraction Kit (BioTeke, Beijing, China). The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 341F/805R and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed using QIIME2 to generate amplicon sequence variants, which were taxonomically annotated against the SILVA database (Release 138). Alpha-diversity and beta-diversity were analyzed, and differences in microbial abundances among groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P < 0.05). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed with LDA > 3.0 and P < 0.05.

SCFA analysis

Briefly, 50 mg of cecal contents was mixed with 1000 μL of 80% methanol pre-chilled at −80 °C for protein precipitation. After centrifugation, 20 μL of supernatant was derivatized with EDC and 3-NPH. The derivative was diluted to 500 μL with initial mobile phase, vortexed, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and analyzed for SCFAs using a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Jasper Triple Quad™ 4500MD).

Statistical analyses

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical differences between CON and FLHS groups and between FLHS and MLP groups were evaluated using unpaired Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the Mean (SEM), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Laying performance, serum metabolism, and antioxidant status

As shown in Table 3, the laying rate was significantly decreased in the FLHS group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05), whereas MLP supplementation significantly restored it to a level comparable with that of the CON group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in ADFI, feed-to-egg ratio, egg weight, or egg mass among the CON, FLHS, and MLP groups (P > 0.05). As shown in Fig. 1A, compared with the CON group, serum AST, ALT, TG, TC, and LDL-C levels were significantly increased in the FLHS group (P < 0.05), whereas serum HDL-C levels were not significantly altered (P > 0.05). Compared with the FLHS group, MLP supplementation significantly decreased serum AST, ALT, TG, TC, and LDL-C levels (P < 0.05), while significantly increasing serum HDL-C levels (P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 1B, serum MDA levels tended to increase, whereas serum CAT, T-SOD, and GSH-Px activities tended to decrease in the FLHS group compared with the CON group; however, these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Hepatic lipid metabolism and antioxidant status

As shown in Fig. 2A, compared with the CON group, the FLHS group exhibited significantly increased abdominal fat ratio and liver index (P < 0.05). MLP supplementation significantly reduced both parameters, indicating alleviation of hepatic lipid accumulation. Hepatic TG and TC contents were also significantly increased in the FLHS group and were markedly reduced following MLP supplementation (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). Gross examination showed that the livers of FLHS laying hens were pale, fragile, and highly susceptible to hemorrhage (Fig. 2C). H&E staining revealed severe hepatocellular vacuolar degeneration and disorganized hepatic architecture, while Oil Red O staining showed extensive lipid droplet accumulation in the liver. In contrast, MLP supplementation markedly alleviated these pathological alterations, with hepatic morphology largely restored to that of the CON group, accompanied by reduced hepatocellular vacuolization and hepatic lipid deposition. Consistent with these histological observations, the relative lipid droplet area was significantly increased in the FLHS group compared with the CON group, whereas MLP supplementation markedly reduced hepatic lipid accumulation (P < 0.05; Fig. 2D). As shown in Fig. 2E, hepatic MDA content was significantly increased, whereas GSH content was significantly decreased in the FLHS group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). GSH-Px activity showed a decreasing trend (P > 0.05). In contrast, MLP supplementation significantly decreased hepatic MDA levels and increased T-SOD activity, T-AOC, GSH content, and GSH-Px activity (P < 0.05), indicating that MLP effectively attenuated FLHS-induced oxidative stress. Hepatic transcriptome

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of MLP in alleviating liver injury in FLHS laying hens, hepatic transcriptomic profiling was performed using RNA sequencing. A total of 1,346 DEGs were identified between the CON and FLHS groups (with 665 upregulated and 681 downregulated) and 1,285 DEGs were identified between the FLHS and MLP groups (with 764 upregulated and 521 downregulated) (Fig. 3A). Cluster analysis of the DEGs revealed that MLP restored the expression of certain genes disrupted in FLHS (Fig. 3B). Overlap analysis identified 234 genes whose expression patterns were reversed by MLP, including sterol regulatory element binding transcription factors 1 (SREBF1), perilipin 2 (PLIN2), Solute Carrier Family 27 Member 4 (SLC27A4), Acyl-CoA thioesterase 13 (ACOT13), glutaredoxin (GLRX), apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), and methionine sulfoxide reductase B1 (MSRB1)—associated with lipid metabolism and antioxidant functions (Fig. 3C). Verification of transcriptomic gene expression

To validate the transcriptomic findings, several key genes were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. As shown in Fig. 3F, compared with the CON group, the relative mRNA expression of fatty acid synthesis genes, such as de novo fatty acid synthesis (FASN), Acetyl CoA carboxylases 1 (ACACA), desaturation (SCD), and elongation-of-very-long-chain-fatty acids 6 (ELOVL6), in the FLHS group was significantly upregulated (P < 0.05), while the relative mRNA expression of antioxidant genes GPX1 and SOD1 showed no significant difference (P > 0.05). However, MLP supplementation significantly decreased the relative mRNA expression of the fatty acid synthesis-related genes FASN, ACACA, SCD, and ELOVL6 compared with the FLHS group (P < 0.05). Although the relative mRNA expression of GPX1 and SOD1 did not differ significantly between the CON and FLHS groups (P > 0.05), both genes were significantly upregulated following MLP supplementation compared with the FLHS group (P < 0.05). Compared with the CON group, the relative mRNA expression of PPARα, a key transcriptional regulator of fatty acid β-oxidation, was significantly downregulated in the FLHS group (P < 0.05), whereas the relative mRNA expression of carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1A (CPT1A) and cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) showed no significant differences (P > 0.05). Compared with the FLHS group, MLP supplementation significantly increased the relative mRNA expression of PPARα, CPT1A, and CD36 (P < 0.05).

Cecal microbiota

As shown in Fig. 4A, α-diversity analysis revealed no significant differences in the Chao1 index or Simpson index among the CON, FLHS, and MLP groups (P > 0.05). The Shannon index was not significantly different between the CON and FLHS groups (P > 0.05), but was significantly lower in the MLP group than in the FLHS group (P < 0.05). PCoA based on binary Jaccard distances and unweighted UniFrac distances revealed distinct clustering patterns among the CON, FLHS, and MLP groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the dominant phyla across all groups (Fig. 4C). Compared with the FLHS group, MLP supplementation significantly increased the relative abundance of Acidobacteriota and decreased the relative abundances of Verrucomicrobiota, Desulfobacterota, and Spirochaetota (P < 0.05; Fig. 4E). There were no significant differences in the relative abundances of these phyla between the FLHS and CON groups (P > 0.05). At the genus level, stacked bar plots showed obvious alterations in the cecal microbiota composition among the three groups (Fig. 4D). Compared with the CON group, the FLHS group exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance of Subdoligranulum and a significantly lower relative abundance of Oscillibacter (P < 0.05), neither of which was significantly altered by MLP supplementation (P > 0.05). Discussion

FLHS is a prevalent metabolic disorder in commercial laying hens, characterized by excessive hepatic lipid accumulation, oxidative stress imbalance, and hepatocellular hemorrhage (San et al., 2023). Increasing evidence suggests that the pathogenesis of FLHS extends beyond hepatic metabolic dysfunction and is closely associated with gut microbial dysbiosis and altered microbial metabolites (Qin et al., 2025). MLP contains a variety of bioactive constituents, including flavonoids, polysaccharides, alkaloids, and polyphenols, and has been reported to improve lipid metabolism, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal health in both poultry and mammals (Liao et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021). These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of MLP may involve coordinated regulation of hepatic metabolism together with modulation of gut microbial function. Accordingly, by integrating hepatic transcriptomic, gut microbiota, and SCFA analyses, the present study sought to determine whether coordinated remodeling of hepatic metabolism and gut microbial function contributes to the protective effects of MLP against FLHS from a gut–liver axis perspective.

Hepatic lipid accumulation and oxidative stress are key pathological features of FLHS progression (Cheng et al., 2024). Excessive lipid accumulation disrupts hepatocellular structure and membrane integrity, leading to elevated serum AST and ALT activities (Ipsen et al., 2018). Moreover, lipid metabolic disorder and oxidative stress reinforce each other during FLHS development. Excessive lipid deposition promotes lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, whereas ROS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction further impairs fatty acid oxidation, thereby aggravating hepatic lipid accumulation and establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic dysfunction (Chen et al., 2020; Zámbó et al., 2013). Previous studies have shown that dietary MLP supplementation reduces hepatic triglyceride accumulation and improves lipid metabolism in laying hens (Chen et al., 2023). Consistent with these findings, MLP supplementation markedly alleviated hepatic steatosis, decreased serum markers of liver injury, and restored hepatic antioxidant capacity in the present study. These findings suggest that MLP effectively disrupted the pathological interplay between lipid metabolic disorder and oxidative stress, thereby restoring hepatic metabolic homeostasis.

Transcriptomic analysis further supported these phenotypic improvements by demonstrating that MLP improved hepatic metabolic function through coordinated reprogramming of hepatic glucose, lipid, and energy metabolism. Compared with the CON group, the FLHS group exhibited enrichment of pathways related to carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation, indicating enhanced central carbon flux and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Such metabolic reprogramming may increase substrate availability for hepatic de novo lipogenesis while simultaneously promoting mitochondrial ROS generation, thereby contributing to lipid accumulation and oxidative stress (Li et al., 2022). In contrast, MLP supplementation markedly attenuated these alterations, as evidenced by the downregulation of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, tricarboxylic acid cycle, and fatty acid metabolism pathways, together with restoration of the PPAR signaling pathway. These coordinated metabolic changes suggest that MLP suppresses excessive carbon flux toward lipid biosynthesis while improving hepatic lipid utilization, ultimately re-establishing hepatic metabolic balance. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that the major bioactive constituents of mulberry leaves, including flavonoids, polysaccharides, and alkaloids, regulate glucose and lipid metabolism and contribute to the maintenance of hepatic metabolic homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2022). Consistent with these reports, the present transcriptomic data suggest that the reduction of glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, and tricarboxylic acid cycle activity might decrease the availability of acetyl-CoA and NADPH, which thereby limiting substrate supply for SREBF1-mediated lipogenesis. This was supported by the downregulated expression of key lipogenic genes, including FASN, ACACA, SCD, and ELOVL6. Collectively, these findings suggested that MLP contributes to hepatic lipid homeostasis primarily through reprogramming of glucose and lipid metabolic pathways, thereby alleviating hepatic steatosis in FLHS. In addition to coordinated metabolic reprogramming, transcriptomic analysis further indicated that the development of FLHS was accompanied by disruption of hepatic redox homeostasis. The enhanced oxidative phosphorylation may increase mitochondrial electron transport activity and ROS production, thereby aggravating oxidative stress under metabolic stress conditions (Murphy, 2009). Consistently, the decrease of mRNA expression of antioxidant-related genes, including GPX1, glutaredoxin (GLRX), and methionine sulfoxide reductase B1 (MSRB1), as well as the increased hepatic MDA levels, further confirmed the impairment of hepatic redox homeostasis. MLP supplementation markedly alleviated these abnormalities, indicating that improved hepatic metabolic function was accompanied by enhanced hepatic redox balance. Collectively, these findings suggested that the coordinated regulation of glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, and redox homeostasis constitute a central mechanism by which MLP restores hepatic metabolic homeostasis and alleviates FLHS.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to metabolic disorders through modulation of microbial metabolites and host metabolic signaling (Hsu and Schnabl, 2023). In the present study, FLHS was characterized by reduced abundances of Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium, together with an increased abundance of Desulfovibrio, indicating a marked disruption of gut microbial homeostasis under the HELP diet. Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium are important SCFA-producing bacteria associated with intestinal barrier integrity and carbohydrate fermentation (Cai et al., 2025; Martín et al., 2023), whereas Desulfovibrio is associated with hydrogen sulfide production and intestinal epithelial damage (Huang et al., 2024). Collectively, these compositional changes indicate a functional shift in the gut microbiota, characterized by reduced saccharolytic fermentation capacity and enhanced proteolytic fermentation activity. And this shift may impair the production of beneficial microbial metabolites, particularly SCFAs, thereby linking gut microbial dysbiosis to systemic metabolic disturbance in FLHS.

SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are key microbial metabolites that serve as important mediators in the gut–liver axis. SCFAs have been reported to regulate host metabolic homeostasis through G protein-coupled receptors (GPR41 and GPR43) and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition, thereby influencing lipid metabolism and redox balance (Silva et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2023). In the present study, MLP supplementation increased the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria and elevated SCFA levels, suggesting restoration of microbial saccharolytic fermentation capacity. Importantly, these changes were accompanied by hepatic transcriptomic alterations, including the downregulation of lipogenic genes (such as SREBF1, ACACA, and FASN), and the upregulation of fatty acid oxidation-related genes (such as PPARα and CPT1A), as well as the modulation of the PPAR signaling pathway. And these coordinated regulation indicated that SCFA-related signaling is closely linked to hepatic metabolic reprogramming. Mechanistically, SCFAs transported via the portal vein may act as signaling molecules to regulate hepatic lipid metabolism, contributing to the suppression of lipogenesis, enhancement of fatty acid oxidation, and improvement of redox homeostasis through AMPK-related and lipid metabolism regulatory pathways, thereby promoting hepatic metabolic recovery (den Besten et al., 2013; Koh et al., 2016). Taken together, these findings supported SCFAs as the key mediators which could link gut microbial fermentation remodeling with hepatic metabolic regulation in FLHS. Collectively, this gut-derived metabolic signaling axis complements the direct hepatic regulatory effects of MLP, suggesting that coordinated modulation of intestinal fermentation and host hepatic metabolism underlies its protective effects against FLHS.

Conclusions

In summary, dietary MLP supplementation may alleviate FLHS and improve laying performance by regulating gut microbiota and SCFA production via the gut-liver axis to restore hepatic lipid metabolism and antioxidant capacity. Nevertheless, the independent regulatory effect of bioactive ingredients of mulberry leaf (such as flavonoids and polysaccharides) remains unclear, which needs to be further investigated.
