Fulminant regrowth of primary pulmonary artery angiosarcoma following pulmonary endarterectomy with tumor resection, identified within nine days of surgery
Authors: Sang-Ho Cho, Jongbae Son, Dae Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary pulmonary artery angiosarcoma (PAAS) is an exceptionally rare and aggressive malignancy often misdiagnosed as pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE). PAAS typically exhibits aggressive biological behavior, with rapid local invasion, early recurrence, and distant metastasis, and the prognosis of PAAS remains extremely poor. We report the case of PAAS initially misdiagnosed with PTE, demonstrating its rapid postoperative recurrence within days after tumor resection and aggressive progression with pulmonary and systemic metastases. This case underscores the importance of avoiding misdiagnosis as PTE, which may delay definitive treatment and facilitate disease progression. It also highlights how rapidly residual tumor cells can proliferate and serves as a reminder to carefully consider the potential role of systemic therapy in PAAS management.

Background

Primary pulmonary artery angiosarcoma (PAAS) is a rare malignant vascular tumor. It often presents with nonspecific symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, or cough, and radiologic findings frequently overlap with pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE). Because of this resemblance, misdiagnosis is well-documented, which may delay definitive treatment and worsen prognosis.[12] PAAS typically exhibits aggressive biological behavior, with rapid local invasion, early recurrence, and distant metastasis. Median survival without treatment is only a few months, and even with surgical resection, outcomes remain poor.[34567] Multimodal therapy may improve disease control, yet evidence-based guidelines are lacking.[457] We report a case of PAAS initially misdiagnosed as PTE, demonstrating its rapid postoperative recurrence within days after tumor resection and aggressive progression with pulmonary and systemic metastases.

Case Report

A 73-year-old male presented to an outside hospital 2 months earlier with right chest wall pain and shortness of breath. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a filling defect in the right main pulmonary artery (PA), interpreted as PTE. Anticoagulation therapy was initiated, and he was discharged on a non-Vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant. Over the following 2 months, his dyspnea progressively worsened, ultimately prompting referral to our institution.

At our hospital, CT showed a large filling defect nearly occluding the right main PA, partial obstruction in the right upper lobar artery, and complete obstruction of the proximal segments of the right middle and lower lobar arteries [Figure 1a and b]. In addition, small pulmonary nodules were noted in the right upper and lower lobes [Figure 1c and d]. Positron emission tomography–CT (PET-CT) revealed a hypermetabolic mass in the right main PA (SUVmax 4.9), raising the suspicion for a malignant vascular tumor [Figure 1e].

FDG: fludeoxyglucose

Given the possibility of primary PA malignancy with concomitant pulmonary metastases, radical resection including pneumonectomy was theoretically reviewed. However, due to his advanced age, poor medical condition, and the possibility of pleural or extra-pulmonary metastasis, pulmonary endarterectomy (PE) followed by adjuvant chemotherapy was selected as the treatment strategy. PE with tumor resection was performed successfully via sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass [Figure 1f], and histopathological analysis confirmed PA angiosarcoma measuring 7 cm × 3 cm × 2.8 cm, with strong immunohistochemical positivity for endothelial markers CD31 and CD34. Tumor cells were also identified in the intimal specimen of the right upper lobar artery, whereas the middle and lower lobar arterial specimens were free of tumor, indicating that microscopic residual disease (R1 resection) could not be excluded.

On the postoperative day 9, follow-up CT revealed a recurrent mass, measuring 3.5 cm × 2.8 cm × 2.8 cm, at the original site [Figure 2a and b]. PET-CT demonstrated a hypermetabolic mass in the right main PA (SUVmax 5.8) [Figure 2c]. Given the rapid recurrence, a second surgical resection was performed. Intraoperatively, the recurrent mass was found adherent to the posterior PA wall, adjacent to the left atrium, with a narrow stalk-like attachment measuring approximately 1.5 cm. The tumor was resected en bloc with the bulging PA wall (similar in size to the tumor) [Figure 2d], and the defect was reconstructed using a bovine pericardium measuring 3 cm × 3.5 cm. Pathology confirmed that the resected PA walls were free of tumor cells.

Rapid recurrence of primary pulmonary artery angiosarcoma (PAAS) after initial tumor resection, (a and b) Axial and coronal computed tomography (CT) images obtained on postoperative day 9 demonstrate a recurrent intraluminal mass at the original site in the right main pulmonary artery, (c) Positron emission tomography–CT on postoperative day 12 reveals intense FDG uptake in the same location, consistent with recurrent tumor, (d) Gross specimen from the second surgery shows a well-circumscribed, tan-white, soft mass measuring approximately 4 cm, consistent with recurrent PAAS, photographed from the luminal side. The narrow stalk-like attachment of the lesion is located on the opposite side.

On retrospective review of intraoperative findings from the first surgery, a small (7–8 mm) lesion with subtly altered attenuation and no definitive stalk or elevation was recognized but not considered clinically significant at the time. This lesion was presumed to represent overlooked residual tumor tissue that underwent rapid proliferation within days. The tumor cells detected in the right upper lobar arterial intima at the first surgery likely represented microscopic involvement adjacent to the mass, rather than true residual disease within the lobar artery itself.

The patient declined adjuvant chemotherapy and was followed up in the outpatient clinic. At 17 weeks postoperatively, CT revealed no local recurrence at the surgical site, but demonstrated progressive pulmonary metastases in the right lung and malignant pleural effusion, and a destructive mass at the T8 vertebra consistent with bone metastasis [Figure 3]. The patient died 21 weeks after the initial surgery.

Red arrow indicates the right main pulmonary artery at the site of the previous mass, with no evidence of local recurrence, (c) Coronal CT image showing the right main pulmonary artery (red arrow) without recurrent tumor, with pulmonary metastases and malignant pleural effusion, (d) Axial CT image demonstrating a destructive mass involving the T8 vertebra (black arrow), consistent with bone metastasis.

Discussion

PAAS is an extremely rare and aggressive malignant neoplasm arising from the PA, with a reported incidence of 0.001%–0.03%.[37] It typically exhibits an endoluminal growth pattern, leading to progressive vessel obstruction with occasional distal tumor embolization.[4] Because its clinical and radiologic features frequently overlap with those of more common pulmonary vascular diseases, such as acute PTE, PAAS is often misdiagnosed.[34]

The prognosis of PAAS remains extremely poor, with historical reports citing median survivals of 1.5 months without surgery and approximately 10 months with resection.[4] Given this poor outlook, early and accurate diagnosis is essential. CT remains the first-line diagnostic modality; however, when there is no identifiable embolic source from the major venous system, and no thrombogenic tendency, the likelihood of PTE is reduced. In such cases, the possibility of a malignant PA tumor should be considered, and further evaluation with PET-CT or other advanced imaging modalities may be warranted to aid in differentiation. Timely identification of PPAS is crucial to avoid the delays in appropriate management and improve the patient outcomes.[23]

There are no standardized treatment guidelines; however, surgical resection is generally considered the primary therapeutic approach. When feasible, radical resection, with or without pneumonectomy, is regarded as the most effective therapeutic approach, and is associated with the most favorable long-term survival outcomes.[35] However, surgical strategy, including the extent of resection and choice of procedure, needs to be individualized based on the tumor presentation, the presence of pulmonary hypertension, and the patient’s comorbidity. PE may be performed as a palliative measure to restore pulmonary perfusion, relieve pulmonary hypertension, and provide symptomatic relief. While PE is rarely curative due to microscopic residual disease, it may be associated with a modest survival benefit.[468] Adjuvant chemotherapy – with or without radiotherapy – has demonstrated additional survival benefit over surgery alone. A multimodal approach should be considered, in patients at high risk of recurrence, unresectable disease, or recurrence after initial surgery. Integration of systemic therapy may enhance disease control and prolong survival.[457]

Conclusion

To summarize the present case, PAAS was diagnosed approximately 2 months after symptom onset, following initial misdiagnosis as PTE. The tumor rapidly recurred within 9 days following PE with tumor resection. The patient declined adjuvant chemotherapy and subsequently developed progressive pulmonary metastases, malignant pleural effusion, and bone metastasis, leading to death 21 weeks after surgery. This case underscores the importance of avoiding misdiagnosis as PTE, which may delay definitive treatment and facilitate disease progression. It also demonstrates how rapidly residual tumor cells can proliferate, highlighting the need for meticulous intraoperative inspection of all areas suspicious for tumor attachment. When intraoperative findings reveal even subtle vascular wall irregularities, aggressive resection with PA wall reconstruction should be considered to minimize early recurrence. Finally, although the effect of postoperative systemic therapy in this patient cannot be determined, this case underscores the importance of considering its potential role in disease control and survival in PAAS.
