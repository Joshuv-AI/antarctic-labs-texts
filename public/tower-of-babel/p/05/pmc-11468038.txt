Combinatorial Effect of Biomaterials and Extracellular Vesicle Therapy for Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction: A Systematic Review of Preclinical Studies
Authors: Kamal Malhotra, Sophie Van Remortel, Valentina Ly, Darryl R Davis
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure, a pervasive global health burden, necessitates innovative therapeutic strategies. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as promising contenders for cardiac repair, owing to their profound influence on fibrosis and inflammation. Merging EVs with biomaterials holds the potential for a synergistic leap in therapeutic efficacy. In this review, the impact of combining EVs with biomaterials in preclinical heart failure models is scrutinized. Fifteen studies, predominantly employing mesenchymal stromal cell‐derived EVs along with hyaluronic acid or peptides in coronary ligation models, meet these stringent criteria. The amalgamation of EVs and biomaterials consistently enhances cardiac ejection fraction (1.39; 95% CI: 0.68, 2.11; p = 0.0001) and fractional shortening (1.46, 95% CI: 0.70, 2.22; p = 0.0002) compared to EV monotherapy. Secondary outcomes similarly showcased improvement in the combined treatment group. Although the number of studies analyzed is modest, no indications of publication bias surface. In summary, combination therapy with EVs and biomaterials enhances therapeutic benefit in preclinical heart failure models. The consistent improvement observed across diverse EV sources, biomaterials, and animal models underscores the exciting potential of this synergistic approach.Keywords: biomaterials, exosomes, extracellular vesicles, heart disease, meta‐analysis

Introduction

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) remains a global challenge with a staggering mortality rate of ≈75% at 5 years.[ 1 ] Conventional therapeutic approaches, while implemented, have not significantly improved outcomes in HFrEF. To address this unmet need, scientists have turned to the potential benefits of extracellular vesicles (EVs). EVs, small membrane‐bound particles discharged by precursor cells, undertake a pivotal role in facilitating intercellular communication and have demonstrated encouraging therapeutic potentials for both ischemic[ 2 ] and non‐ischemic[ 3 ] heart failure. At a mechanistic level, EVs exhibit a multifaceted profile. One facet of their action involves attenuating fibrosis after cardiac injury—a process characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix components that contribute to tissue scarring and impaired cardiac function.[ 4 ] EVs appear to exert a regulatory influence on this fibrotic response by modulating the activity of fibroblasts and their interactions with other cell types involved in tissue repair.[ 5 ] Moreover, EVs also demonstrate the ability to reduce inflammation within injured cardiac tissue. Inflammatory responses play a pivotal role in the aftermath of heart damage, and excessive or prolonged inflammation can lead to adverse remodeling and further deterioration of cardiac function.[ 6 ] EVs seem to orchestrate an anti‐inflammatory effect, which may involve modulating immune cell behavior, secreting anti‐inflammatory factors, or suppressing pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways.[ 5 , 7 ] Another noteworthy attribute of EVs is their capacity to reactivate dormant myocytes—cardiac muscle cells that have entered a quiescent state. Typically representing a reservoir of cells with the potential for revitalization, these dormant myocytes could contribute to cardiac repair and regeneration.[ 8 ] EVs appear to possess the capability to stimulate these dormant cells, possibly by transferring molecular signals that prompt cell‐cycle re‐entry, proliferation, and engagement in tissue healing processes.[ 9 ]

Nonetheless, the delivery of suspended EVs into the dynamic, perfused heart poses challenges in terms of rapid clearance, limited distribution, and suboptimal targeting, thereby restricting their therapeutic impact. In response, the integration of biomaterials with EVs has emerged as an intriguing avenue for heart failure treatment. This partnership capitalizes on the synergistic potential of biomaterials and EVs to enhance delivery, stability, targeting, tissue regeneration, and the modulation of the local microenvironment.[ 10 ]

However, to date, there has been a lack of systematic evaluation regarding the combined effect of biomaterials and EVs in the context of HFrEF. Specifically, it remains unclear whether the combination of biomaterials with EVs can provide additive benefits when compared to treatment with EVs alone. Such insights are crucial for understanding the potential of this combinatorial approach and identifying strategies to optimize its therapeutic efficacy.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is to comprehensively evaluate the impact of combining biomaterials with EVs on cardiac function in preclinical models of HFrEF, in comparison to treatment with EVs alone. By synthesizing the available evidence from animal studies, we seek to determine whether the combination of biomaterials and EVs leads to superior improvements in cardiac function compared to EVs alone. Additionally, we will assess the underlying mechanisms and potential sources of heterogeneity among the studies. The findings from this review will provide critical insights into the additive effects of biomaterials in combination with EVs, elucidate their therapeutic potential in HFrEF, and guide future research directions for the development of effective treatment strategies.

Results

As outlined in Figure 1 , our initial screening identified 3977 records which included 797, 1372, and 1808 results from Medline Ovid, Embase classic + Embase Ovid, and Scopus, respectively. Of these, 1439 duplicates were removed which left 2538 articles for title‐abstract screening. This analysis identified 2506 articles that did not meet our inclusion criteria for full text screening (i.e., 32 articles retrieved for detailed screening). Fifteen of these articles then met the inclusion criteria for the final analysis as 17 articles were excluded because of no functional primary outcome (i.e., no measure of ejection fraction or fractional shortening), full text not available, irrelevant outcome or were not written in English.[ 18 ]

Eleven of the 15 studies (73%) used MSCs as a producer cell line to generate EVs (Figure 2B). These EVs were isolated using a variety of methods with ultracentrifugation being the most common (40%). When the storage conditions were reported, all groups stored EVs prior to use at −80 °C. As shown in Table 2 , a wide variety of methods were used to characterize the EVs used in these preclinical studies with most groups reporting biophysical properties using nanoparticle tracking analysis (60%) while most of the studies showed that the EVs were round/spheroid in shape (n = 9, 60%) and ranged in size from 50–220 nm in diameter (Table S4, Supporting Information). Although most studies relied upon using rats as their experimental model, four studies used pigs with three studies using pigs, rats, and mice (Figure 2C).

As shown in Table 2, 93% of the studies examined the effect of biomaterials and/or EVs in animal models of cardiac ischemic with only one study exploring effects in animal models of non‐ischemic heart failure.[ 18b ] The sex of the animal model was reported by nine studies whereas only one study used both male and female animals. The dose of EVs was most often reported as protein weight and varied between 20–150 µg. When EV particle number was reported (n = 6), the dose of EVs applied ranged between 2.0 × 107 and 1 × 1011. All studies used a single dose of EVs injected into the myocardium (n = 10) or applied as patch on the epicardial surface (n = 5) at the time of surgery (Figure 2C). When the study was designed to track EV retention after delivery (n = 9), the interval between EV administration and detection ranged between 2 and 21 days (14 ± 7 days; Table S3, Supporting Information).

As indicated in Table 3 , not all investigations examined every primary and secondary outcome. Solely those studies that provided data on the primary outcomes (i.e., ejection fraction or fractional shortening) were incorporated into the meta‐analysis. In cases where data was lacking, efforts were made to communicate with the authors to seek clarification or acquire any absent information. When the size of the animals was presented as a range rather than a precise value, the lower figure was used to compute the outcomes. Studies were omitted from the analysis if they did not furnish details about error types, error magnitudes, or sample sizes.

As shown in Figure 3 , animals treated with biomaterials + EVs demonstrated a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 3.44 [95% CI:2.29, 4.60; p < 0.00001] in cardiac ejection fraction when compared to animals that received a no treatment control (i.e., administration of vehicle or no intervention). When compared to animals that received EVs alone, combining biomaterials with EVs provided a consistent further improvement in cardiac ejection fraction (SMD = 1.39; 95% CI: 0.68, 2.11; p = 0.0001). Similar results were seen when fractional shortening was reported (Figure 4 ) as treatment with biomaterials and EVs enhanced cardiac function when compared to a no treatment control (SMD = 3.81, 95% CI: 2.76, 4.86; p < 0.00001) or EVs alone (SMD = 1.46, 95% CI: 0.70, 2.22; p = 0.0002).

Analysis of secondary outcomes displayed a similar effect where combined treatment with biomaterials and EVs resulting in reduced left ventricular end diastolic diameter (SMD = −1.57, 95% CI: −2.46, −0.67; p = 0.0006; Figure 5A upper panel) or left ventricular end systolic diameter (SMD = −2.24, 95% CI: −3.73, −0.74; p = 0.003; Figure 5B upper panel) when compared to no treatment controls. Interestingly, combination therapy with biomaterials and EVs did not have a consistent effect on left ventricular end diastolic diameter (SMD = −0.35, 95% CI: −1.07, 0.37; p = 0.34; Figure 5A lower panel) or left ventricular end systolic diameter (SMD = −0.68, 95% CI: −1.36, −0.00; p = 0.05; Figure 5B lower panel) when compared to treatment with EVs alone. As shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information, combination therapy with biomaterials and EVs displayed similar effects on left ventricular end diastolic and end systolic volumes when compared to no treatment controls or EVs alone; although these measures were not significantly different, this observation likely reflects the few studies reporting these variables (n = 1–5) rather than a true biologic effect.

An analysis for the risk of bias was performed using the SYstematic Review Centre for Laboratory animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) tool (Table 4 ). The risk of bias was unclear for many variables including selection (i.e., allocation concealment), performance (i.e., random housing, blinding of participants and personnel), detection (i.e., random outcome assessment, blinding of outcome assessment), and attrition (i.e., incomplete outcome data) bias. According to the collaborative approach to meta‐analysis and review of animal data from experimental studies (CAMARADES) criteria (Table 5 ), the average study quality was 5.9/10 which was attributable to the few studies that outlined maintenance of physiologic temperatures during procedures (20%), sample size calculations (33%), dose response relationships for biomaterials (8%) or EVs (7%), and blinded analysis of the primary outcome (53%).

Several studies have explored the fundamental mechanisms underlying their results through in vitro testing. As illustrated in Table 6 , in vitro testing suggests that combining biomaterials with EVs could enhance vessel formation, reduce apoptosis, and promote the proliferation of relevant cells, such as cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells. Histological analyses confirmed that this combination increases vessel density and cell proliferation, decreases apoptosis, reduces immune cell infiltration, stimulates cardiomyocyte precursor differentiation (i.e., WT‐1 cells), and encourages the salutary polarization of macrophages toward anti‐inflammatory phenotypes. The authors proposed that the mechanism through which biomaterials amplify the effects of EVs primarily revolves around enhancing cardiomyocyte cell proliferation and migration, fostering angiogenesis, and reducing inflammation in the infarct region (Table 7 ). This amplification in therapeutic efficacy correlates with increased EV retention (Table S5 and Figure S2, Supporting Information), leading many to hypothesize that extending the delivery duration of EVs to the injured myocardium is central to the observed effects.

Discussion

In this systematic review, we present a comprehensive analysis of the published studies investigating the therapeutic effect of combining extracellular vesicles (EVs) with biomaterials in preclinical animal models of heart failure. Our findings highlight the distinct advantage of the combination therapy over EV treatment alone in improving cardiac function. These results provide compelling evidence for the synergistic effect achieved through the combination of EVs and biomaterials, supporting the potential of this strategy as a superior therapeutic approach for heart failure. The significance of these findings warrants further exploration and consideration in future studies and clinical applications.

Although our search initially identified 2506 articles for review, only 32 met our screening criteria. This discrepancy reflected our initial filter that eliminated a substantial number of articles that includes review articles, case studies, abstracts, duplicates, in vitro or ex‐vivo studies, or were not published in English. However, the final number of articles included in the study largely reflects the nascent nature of studies involving extracellular vesicles and biomaterials. A recent systematic review focusing on the preclinical landscape of extracellular vesicle therapy for myocardial infarction identified only 37 papers, and a recent systematic review outlining the application of extracellular vesicle therapy for non‐ischemic heart failure found 17 articles.[ 19 ] Given the overall timeline for a basic science study, which can typically range from 1.5 to 3+ years from inception to publication, it is not surprising that the number of studies that were finally enrolled in the systematic review was relatively modest.

In our analysis, we observed a notable heterogeneity in the producer cell lines and biomaterials utilized across the included studies. A substantial portion of the studies (73%) used MSCs as the producer cells for EVs while 60% of the biomaterials used were almost evenly split between hyaluronic acid and peptides. While the combination of biomaterials and EVs consistently demonstrated a beneficial effect on cardiac function, it is plausible that certain biomaterials or cell sources may offer unique properties or interactions that enhance the therapeutic potential of the combined approach but detecting the advantages of a specific EV producer cell line or biomaterials in a systematic review can be challenging due to the heterogeneity in study designs, limited sample sizes, potential publication bias, lack of standardized isolation protocols, and the complexity of EV cargo.[ 20 ] Standardized methodologies in well‐designed studies with larger sample sizes will be critical to identify the best approaches to move this promising strategy forward.[ 21 ]

From a mechanistic standpoint, the combination of biomaterials with EVs arises through a variety of mechanisms. First, encapsulation of EVs within biomaterials enables controlled release and targeted delivery to specific tissues or cells.[ 22 ] This controlled release enhances therapeutic efficacy. Second, biomaterials provide stability and protection to EVs, safeguarding them from degradation and preserving their bioactivity during storage and delivery.[ 23 ] Third, biomaterials can serve as scaffolds in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine applications. When combined with EVs, they facilitate cellular communication and promote tissue regeneration processes. Additionally, EVs derived from specific cell types have the ability to modulate immune responses.[ 22a ] When combined with biomaterials, they promote immune tolerance, reduce inflammation, and enhance tissue healing. The combination of biomaterials with EVs can also lead to synergistic effects. Biomaterials create a supportive microenvironment that optimizes the therapeutic effects of EVs, including enhanced cellular uptake, stability, retention at the target site, and delivery of therapeutic cargo. Furthermore, biomaterials enable the customization of EVs for specific therapeutic purposes by modifying their surfaces and incorporating targeting ligands, thereby improving therapy efficiency and specificity while minimizing off‐target effects.[ 23 ]

Moving forward, a logical progression involves bridging the gap between preclinical research and clinical application. While the promising therapeutic effects of EVs have been observed in animal models, transitioning to clinical trials introduces a new set of challenges. Critical aspects such as safety, dosing, delivery methods, and establishing efficacy in humans warrant rigorous investigation.[ 24 ] Clinical trials of EVs alone are just emerging, largely comprising phase 1 trials designed primarily to demonstrate safety. These trials are crucial in establishing the initial safety profile of EV‐based therapies in humans. Moreover, it is important to note that, as of now, there has yet to be a cardiac trial of EVs performed. Instead, the current trials have predominantly focused on treating acute respiratory distress syndrome associated with conditions like COVID‐19.[ 25 ]

Integrating biomaterials into EV clinical trials and production presents another layer of complexity. However, this endeavor will be challenging, primarily due to the limited number of approved injectable biomaterials for cardiac indications.[ 26 ] Currently, only one biomaterial, Alginate, has received approval for cardiac injection. Alginate, delivered percutaneously to patients with advanced heart failure, was approved by the EMA in 2014 and is currently being investigated in early stage clinical trials (NCT03082508).[ 27 ] This scarcity of approved biomaterials poses a significant hurdle to the integration of biomaterials with EV therapies. Developing and gaining regulatory approval for biomaterials suitable for cardiac delivery will be a critical prerequisite for advancing this combined approach. Given our paper demonstrates a consistent benefit seen by combining EVs with biomaterials, future work will be needed to integrate clinical grade EVs with clinically compatible biomaterials.

Beyond clinical trials, consideration of scaling and production for human use is also a priority. The production of EV‐based therapies on a larger scale requires addressing challenges related to manufacturing, quality control, and scalability.[ 28 ] Ensuring the reproducibility and quality of EVs in larger quantities is essential to meet the demands of potential clinical applications. Exploring methodologies for cost‐effective, large‐scale EV production while maintaining their therapeutic efficacy will be vital.[ 24b ] Moreover, regulatory approval and compliance with established guidelines are key factors in advancing EV‐based therapies toward clinical implementation.[ 29 ] Collaborations between researchers, clinicians, regulatory bodies, and industry partners will be instrumental in navigating the complex landscape of translating experimental findings into tangible clinical interventions.

Based on the findings in our review, combining biomaterial with EVs appears safe and effective at treating HFrEF. However, this result must be taken with caution as small animal models are not suitable to detect subtle differences in product safety, and no trial has performed long‐term or toxicological studies needed to detect off‐target effects. It is notable that four studies used large animal models to evaluate product effectiveness and the feasibility of clinical staging, which may serve as a useful indication for future clinical studies. But rigorously designed Phase 1 studies will be needed to detect any signal for safety concerns before moving forward with clinical efficacy studies.

A limitation of the studies included in our systematic review pertains to the animal models utilized. Our analysis revealed that only one out of the 15 studies employed a model of non‐ischemic heart failure,[ 18b ] while the remaining studies focused on coronary ligation models. Reliance on coronary ligation models, which predominantly mimic ischemic heart disease, introduces a potential bias in the interpretation of the findings in the context of non‐ischemic heart failure. As such, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the outcomes of our review to the non‐ischemic heart failure population. Future studies specifically employing non‐ischemic heart failure models are clearly warranted as these investigations would provide more direct insights into the efficacy and translational relevance of this combined approach. The studies included in our systematic review also universally focused on HFrEF, rather than heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, a condition also associated with a 75% mortality rate at 5 years.[ 1 ] Future work is clearly warranted to address this research gap as recent work has shown that paracrine cell therapy attenuates endothelial dysfunction in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.[ 30 ]

Ultimately, it is worth highlighting that all the investigations included in our systematic review embraced a preventative stance, wherein the therapeutic interventions were administered during the surgical procedure rather than after disease onset. This limitation raises questions about the translational potential and effectiveness of these therapies in more clinically realistic scenarios, particularly when considering their application as a secondary or salvage treatment option after conventional therapies have failed.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our systematic review and meta‐analysis provides compelling evidence that the combination of EVs and biomaterials confers enhanced cardioprotection in preclinical models of heart failure. The observed therapeutic effect is likely mediated through the reduction of myocardial fibrosis and inflammation, highlighting the potential mechanisms underlying the beneficial outcomes. These findings support the notion that EV therapies hold promise as a potential treatment strategy for HFrEF. Moving forward, further investigations are needed to determine the most appropriate dosing regimen, identify the ideal recipient population, and optimize the EV product to facilitate the successful translation of EV therapies into clinical practice. The translation of these preclinical findings into effective clinical interventions has the potential to significantly improve the management and outcomes of patients suffering from HFrEF. Continued research and collaborative efforts are essential to unlock the full therapeutic potential of EV‐based treatments in addressing the unmet needs of individuals with HFrEF.

Experimental Section

Search Strategy

The search approach was formulated by a certified librarian specializing in health sciences (VL) and was separately assessed by another librarian.[ 11 ] The databases MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), and Scopus were explored from their inception up to November 25, 2022 (Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information). The study plan was pre‐registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, Registration # CRD42022378273). No language or publication date restrictions were imposed. The primary search terms encompassed extracellular vesicles, heart disease, biomaterials employed for cardiac conditions, and animal studies, with the last category being determined by a standardized filter designed for laboratory animal research.[ 12 ] The findings from the search were imported into Covidence (located in Melbourne, Australia), where duplication identification algorithms were employed to remove duplicates.

Study Selection

All identified articles underwent independent evaluation by two investigators (K.M. and S.V.R.) during both the title‐abstract and full‐text assessment phases, adhering to predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Before formally initiating the screening procedure, a calibration test employing ten randomly selected articles was conducted to establish the reliability of agreement between the raters. In cases where a unanimous decision regarding inclusion could not be reached, a third investigator (DRD) was consulted. Papers were included if they presented outcomes regarding the effectiveness of EV therapeutic interventions facilitated by biomaterials and encompassed physiologic evaluation that included measurements of ejection fraction and/or fractional shortening. Additionally, the papers had to involve animal models simulating either ischemic or non‐ischemic cardiomyopathies (e.g., dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, restrictive cardiomyopathy, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and drug‐induced cardiomyopathy). Articles were not considered if they involved human subjects (such as clinical trials), exclusively evaluated effectiveness outcomes via laboratory‐based studies (in vitro or ex vivo), solely explored therapeutic attributes of biomaterials without involving EV administration, did not delve into the therapeutic properties of EVs (such as biodistribution studies), failed to provide information on animal dimensions, or neglected to report primary outcomes (i.e., ejection fraction or fractional shortening). Additionally, case reports, review papers, materials from the grey literature, unpublished manuscripts, or articles published in languages other than English were excluded.

Data Extraction and Analysis

A predefined data abstraction table, conforming to standardized parameters, was established in advance to systematically extract pertinent information from complete text articles. Two reviewers (K.M. and S.V.R.) independently carried out data extraction and subsequently cross‐compared their findings for validation purposes. The extracted data encompassed various aspects, including characteristics of the study (such as sample size, randomization, study design, blinding of outcome analysis, and endpoints), details of the intervention (like the nature and frequency of the intervention, method and dosage of administration), features of the animal model (comprising species, models of both ischemic and non‐ischemic cardiomyopathy, and baseline traits), primary outcome measures (involving ejection fraction and/or fractional shortening), and secondary outcome measures (ranging from end systolic volume, end diastolic volume, end systolic diameter, end diastolic diameter, myocardial fibrosis, to in vitro cumulative release).

For outcomes presented solely in graphical form, the software WebPlotDigitizer (Version 4.5; https://www.automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer/) was utilized to extract the data. To assess the risk of bias in the studies, the SYRCLE risk of bias tool was applied.[ 13 ] Each component of the SYRCLE tool (selection, performance, detection, attrition, reporting, and other bias) was evaluated by two independent reviewers (K.M. and S.V.R.), assigning a categorization of low, high, or unclear risk of bias to each study included.

Furthermore, the quality evaluation followed the CAMARADES checklist.[ 14 ] Each study underwent an individual quality assessment, leading to an overall quality designation of poor, average, or good, based on the checklist criteria. In instances of disparities, discussions were conducted to arrive at a consensus and resolve disagreements.

Data were presented in the form of mean plus standard deviation, unless explicitly stated otherwise. The predetermined significance level was set at P < 0.05. If necessary, standard deviations were calculated from standard error data, following the established methodology recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration. In order to address the variability in treatment effects observed across the studies under review, random effects meta‐analyses were executed using the random effects inverse variance approach for continuous variables (such as ejection fraction and/or fractional shortening). This was done to ascertain the comprehensive effect size for each outcome,[ 15 ] which was subsequently reported as standardized mean differences. A Z‐test was conducted to establish the P value related to the overall effect of the comparisons. The degree of variation in effect sizes was gauged using the Cochrane I2 statistic, where specific thresholds were employed: 0–40% (indicating low heterogeneity), 30–60% (representing moderate heterogeneity), 50–90% (indicating substantial heterogeneity), and 75–100% (reflecting considerable heterogeneity).[ 16 ]

To assess the potential presence of publication bias, funnel plots were generated to examine the symmetry of the plotted data points.[ 17 ] The meta‐analysis was executed using the statistical software review manager from the Cochrane Collaboration (RevMan, version 5.4.1).
