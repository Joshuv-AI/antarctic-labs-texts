Discrimination of first-onset patients with bipolar disorder or major depressive disorder using screened biochemical parameters
Authors: Yuncheng Zhu, Sheng Li, Ju Gao, Fang Wang, Ni Zhou, Chuangxin Wu, Xiaojia Huang, Ningning Li, Xiaohui Wu, Hongmei Liu, Lu Yang, Xiaoyun Guo, Zuowei Wang, Yiru Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTheoretical hypotheses suggest differing oxidative stress levels in mood disorders. We evaluated eight oxidative stress-related biochemical markers for further verification in first-onset patients with bipolar disorder (BD) or major depressive disorder (MDD).MethodsWe extracted 224 first-onset patients (BD = 93 and MDD = 131) from the overall sample database of 4647 candidates. And we analyzed eight parameters: direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), uric acid (UA), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), prealbumin, and progesterone.The above parameters enter the hierarchical multiple linear regression model for analysis.ResultsWhen compared to MDD, the BD group showed decreased prealbumin and elevated UA, LDH, and FT3 in males while decreased prealbumin and DBIL and elevated IDBIL, LDH, FT3 and progesterone in females (p’s < 0.05). The model effectively differentiated between BD and MDD, achieving AUCs of 0.894 for men and 0.897 for women.ConclusionThis result elucidates the validity of these biomarkers for identifying first-episode patients with BD or MDD. The discrimination was satisfactory, with good diagnostic accuracy for both gender subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-025-00605-6.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Major depressive disorder, Biological markers, First onset

Introduction

Psychiatric diagnoses have historically relied on on empirical symptom patterns, yet the consistency of bipolar disorder (BD) diagnosis continues to confront substantial challenges of inaccurate treatment and adverse patient outcomes [1]. Especially, the diagnosis and clinical differentiation of BD and unipolar depression remains a difficult subject. Longitudinal follow-up reveals frequent misdiagnosis, precipitating cascading negative consequences. Even though BD and MDD both fall within the affective spectrum [2], these disorders are likely to involve distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. The lifetime prevalence of BD was reported much lower than that of major depressive disorder (MDD) (0.5% vs. 3.9%) by a latest Chinese epidemiological study [3]. MDD is more common in women over 90 different nations with an odds ratio of 1.95 [4], while BD does not have such a trend [5].

However, a major diagnostic challenge lies in accurately identifying first-onset mood disorders, since initial depressive episodes present nearly identically in both BD and MDD (unipolar depression) [6]. Longitudinal MDD studies indicate rapidly escalating misdiagnosis rates. A decade-long study revealed that 13.8% of MDD were reclassified as BD upon overlapping symptomology and later onset of mania [7]. Although BD diagnosis stabilizes when major depressive episodes (MDEs) precede manic/hypomanic manifestations, BD type II proves especially challenging to distinguish from recurrent MDEs [8].

Consequently, research efforts prioritize biomarker discovery to facilitate rapid, precise differentiation. It was highlighted that assessing uric acid levels could be beneficial in daily psychiatric clinical practice [9]. Evidence suggests roles for immune inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter abnormalities in differentiating BD and MDD [10]. It is implied that potential biomarkers (such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [11], uric acid [12] and bilirubin [13]) represent pathophysiologic processes that differ between BD and MDD can both inform each diagnosis and provide biological targets for the development of new and personalised treatments [14]. Based on the background of autoimmunity and oxidative stress in mood disorders [15], we began to explore whether oxidative burst activity has the potential to recognize BD. We hypothesized that gender-specific dysregulation of oxidative stress pathways—reflected in peripheral biomarkers like bilirubin and uric acid—could robustly discriminate first-episode BD from MDD [16]. Leveraging the Shanghai Mental Health Center (SMHC) Clinical Research Center Special Project for Big Data Analysis Project, we used these peripheral biochemical parameters and found that these high-risk factors may have corresponding changes in mood disorders through the mechanism of oxidative stress [17, 18], enabling objective detection and predictive modeling. so that we can objectively detect and establish models for prediction.

We incorporated all seven factors previously identified through sex-stratified multiple linear stepwise regression modeling in high-quality preliminary studies [19–21].

The Oxidative Stress Injury Biomarker (BIOS) Model to distinguish between BD and MDD from our former research achieved a certain level of discriminative accuracy (75.1%), however its parameters were compromised by dynamic changes associated with patients’ long-term medication use and disease progression [21]. In one series of studies, the range of progesterone level during menstruation to identify female subgroups (n = 1299) was used: follicular phase (0.181-2.84nmol/l), pre- or post-ovulatory phase (2.85-5.81nmol/l), and luteal phase (5.82-75.9nmol/l). We observed the largest area under the curve (AUC) of the predictive model is up to 0.848 in the luteal phase subgroup of women compared with 0.772 among men and 0.793 among women [22]. Therefore, progesterone in our database was integrated as the eighth factor into the model for discussion to testify if it could improve the new model’s accuracy. We aimed to identify objective biomarkers for discriminating patients with first-onset affective spectrum disorders, thereby minimizing the confounding effects of long-term medication use and disease progression to achieve an optimized model.

Patients and methods

Study design

This study is a single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional study. We extracted mood disorder candidate data from 4,647 hospitalized patients (2009.01-2018.12) in the SMHC database. The collected blood samples corresponded to routine laboratory tests covered by residents’ medical insurance.

Setting

Patients meeting the diagnostic criteria of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) were enrolled. Diagnoses were confirmed by chief physicians. This study was registered for a clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier:.NCT03949218; Registration Date:2019-05-13) at the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform.

Participants

The first episode was defined as the initial 3-month period following symptom onset to ensure sample homogeneity. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Availability of biochemical data in the Hospital Information System (HIS); (2) Diagnosis of BD, MDD, or their subtypes—initial (hypo)manic episodes were classified as BD type I or II per Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria for BD [23]; 3)Acute-phase hospitalization. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Substance-related and addictive disorders; (2) Pregnancy, postpartum lactation, or indeterminate menstrual history; (3) Comorbidity with other mental disorders; (4) Comorbidity with severe physical illness; (5) Rehospitalization. The Maximum Word Frequency Segment Algorithm was used to select samples in Chinese texts [24].

Variables

All participants abstained from smoking and alcohol for ≥ 16 h prior to assessment, consistent with SMHC’s smoke-free policy. Fasting venous blood samples were collected between 7:00–8:00 a.m. using standardized protocols. Biochemical analyses were performed using the Roche Cobas e601 automatic electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) system. Long-term medication orders were typically deferred until ward rounds (after 8:00 a.m.); temporary prescriptions for sedation/sleep aid were administered as needed. Moreover, physical therapies were not utilized. Eight candidate high-risk factors were analyzed: direct bilirubin (DBIL, indirect bilirubin (IDBIL), uric acid (UA), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), prealbumin, progesterone (See Supplementary Material 1).

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS22.0, with statistical significance set at P value < 0.05. All data were evaluated by normality test and test of homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation; non-parametric data (Mann-Whitney U test) are reported as medians. Cohen’s d effect sizes were interpreted as 0.2(small), 0.5(medium), and 0.8(large). Gender-stratified binary logistic regression and hierarchical multiple linear regression models incorporating all eight parameters were constructed. Missing data were excluded listwise (Fig. 1). Parameter weights generated Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate accuracy of algorithm. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test calibrated the ability of the prediction models [25]. Internal consistency of biochemical parameters was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Events per variable (EPV = 224/8 = 28) of the effective sample size (n = 224) to the predictor variables (n = 8) exceeded the recommended threshold (≥ 10) [26, 27]. Machine learning techniques were not employed due to sample size constraints.

Results

Comparison of demographic characteristics between BD and MDD groups

As a result, 3112 first-onset patients were identified through the HIS data extraction process, with medical histories missing in 30 cases, and finally 3082 cases were included in the sample database. We excluded 2517 patients with recurrent episodes (1054 males, 1463 females), hormone data missing (137 cases), 37 cases younger than 18 years old (10 males, 27 females), and 167 cases older than 45 years old (67 males, 100 females). Seven MDD patients were diagnosed as BD during their first hospitalized period (misdiagnoses). Six MDD patients were converted to BD from 2009 to 2018 (BD-converters). We identified 86 BD (38.4%) patients, 131 MDD (58.5%) patients, and 7 BD-converters (5.3%). Consequently, the median observation period was five years, with a conversion time from first hospitalization to BD diagnosis of 74.0 ± 39.4 months. Finally, 224 patients were included in the study. Discussion

Key results

Initial comparisons revealed that both mean age and age of onset in BD patients were approximately three years younger than those with MDD, though these differences lacked statistical significance—potentially attributable to limited sample size or our exclusive inclusion of first-onset cases with total disease duration under three months. This acute-phase criterion aligns with the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) Guidelines, which define acute episodes within an 8–16 week window (approximately 12 weeks) [28]. Earlier BD onset (frequently presenting with depressive episodes) versus MDD is well-documented [29].

After controlling for these confounding factors, we evaluated eight previously selected predictors. Male BD patients exhibited alongside lower prealbumin versus MDD MDD controls. Meanwhile, female BD patients demonstrated elevated higher IDBIL, LDH, FT3, progesterone levels with reduced DBIL and prealbumin. Notably, LDH, FT3, and prealbumin differentiated BD from MDD in both genders, whereas UA was male-specific and IDBIL, DBIL and progesterone were female-specific (Table 2).

All parameters except TSH showed significant intergroup differences in at least one sex-stratified subgroup. Among first-onset patients, TSH, DBIL, IDBIL, and progesterone in males and UA/TSH in females showed no significant differences. Small first-episode sample sizes likely limited statistical power for all biomarkers. Consequently, fine-grained patient stratification was implemented to enhance effect size calibration through sample homogenization [30].

Comparing to MDD, the BD group exhibited elevated IDBIL and decreased DBIL in females. This suggested that bilirubin metabolism undergoes more pronounced alterations in women than in men between the disorders. In the general clinical bioanalysis, neurotoxic bilirubin was considered merely the terminal heme catabolism product with no physiological utility and adverse health implications [31]. Its clinical significance was largely confined to diagnosing jaundice when elevated [32]. However, subsequent discovery of its potent antioxidant properties has revealed a broader physiological role [33]. Bilirubin is a potent endogenous antioxidant with strong antioxidant activity and protective effect against oxidative stress damage [34], and inhibiting low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol oxidation 10–20 times more effectively than Trolox (a vitamin E analog) [35].

Our findings suggest greater oxidative stress burden in BD. Antioxidant processes likely deplete DBIL, reducing its levels in BD patients, while homeostasis adaptations and cell auto-regulation induce a compensatory IDBIL increase, which may explain the divergent bilirubin levels between BD and MDD groups to maintain the relatively stable total bilirubin [36]. Notably, DBIL and IDBIL emerged as significant predictive parameters in the female regression model.

Physiologically, females exhibit lower baseline uric acid (UA) levels and milder purine metabolism than males. Consequently, while UA differed significantly between diagnostic groups in males, no such difference was observed in females, aligning with its model weighting contribution.

Elevated gout risk in BD patients supports our results of heightened oxidative stress in BD versus MDD [37]. Oxygen free radicals are one-electron reduction products of oxygen, which are the main form of free oxygen in organisms. They can be produced through enzymatic reactions. Serum markers of inflammation including xanthine oxidase and LDH, which are two important enzymes, were significantly increased indicating the induction of oxidative stress [38]. Xanthine oxidase is rate-limiting enzyme of purine metabolism. When xanthine oxidase activates, it catalyzes the conversion of xanthine into UA and produces oxygen free radicals at the same time [39]. Then, UA itself can scavenge oxygen free radicals and other active free radicals, and protect organs against peroxidation. Therefore, male BD patients have an elevated UA level that indirectly supports the activation of xanthine oxidase by which purine metabolism is co-occurring with oxidative stress injury.

LDH, a sensitive indicator of hypoxia, inflammation and oxidative stress [40], increases during BD immune-inflammatory processes due to tissue hypermetabolism-induced hypoxia and lactate accumulation. We acknowledged LDH further catalyzes oxygen radicals generation, causing mitochondrial dysfunction, which is a classic oxidative burst pathway [41]. Higher LDH in BD versus MDD corroborates greater oxidative stress severity in BD and significantly predicted female group classification.

TSH cannot be used as a predictive value for BD/MDD differentiation, and the level of significant difference is also not reached in the regression model. It may be related to a long-term hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis negative feedback. Thyroxine regulation operates slower humoral mechanisms compared to neuromodulation [42].

Differences emerged in FT3 and prealbumin (also known as transthyretin) across gender subgroups. Prealbumin is a thyroid hormone-binding protein and an acute phase response protein. It is synthesized by the choroidal plexus and secreted into the cerebrospinal fluid after synthesis. The concentration of thyroid hormone and its transport to target organs are the two main reasons for the homeostasis of thyroid function. We find low prealbumin levels in both BD [43] and MDD [44]. In cerebrospinal fluid, prealbumin levels in BD are also downregulated [45]. Decreased prealbumin level in cerebrospinal fluid is significantly related to low serotonin level of MDD (β = 0.34) and suicidal behavior (β=−0.58) [46], which can explain the relationship between dysfunction of thyroid transport and the severity of MDD. In the acute onset of mood disorders, expression changes and postoxidation translation modifications may be related to the decrease of prealbumin [47].

Lower prealbumin in BD versus MDD suggests more severe transport dysfunction of thyroid hormone, potentially indicating impaired central nervous system delivery. While MDD associates with subclinical hypothyroidism [48], BD links to hyperthyroidism, particularly during lithium therapy [49]. Compared with MDD patients, the prevalence ratios (4.9) for BD with hyperthyroidism is significantly increased [50]. However, reduced secretion of thyroid hormone in MDD patients occurs more frequent in MDD [51]. The phenomenon is contrary to the prealbumin level, indicating that thyroid synthesis and transport may have different mechanisms: BD may have higher level of FT3 concentrations but lower thyroxine transport capacity. Especially the transport disorder of thyroid hormone targeting the central organs may be a potential pathophysiological differentiator. Prealbumin significantly predicted female group classification.

Progesterone showed non-significant intergroup differences in males. In females, the difference may reflect limited sample size. We chose progesterone because the sex differences in MDD incidence [18]. Unlike males, female progesterone cyclically increases until mense [22]. Its withdrawal may underlie premenstrual syndrome risk. Allopregnanolone exerts a beneficial effect in cases of neuropathic pain and it is also a potential candidate for the treatment of mood disorders [52].

Therefore, we assumed that progesterone is a better predictor. The concentration of progesterone is significantly different between female diagnostic groups. Then, we observed the internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) and accuracy (AUC) of the models including progesterone are both improved versus progesterone-excluded models, supporting its diagnostic utility. The concentration of progesterone positively correlated with the diagnosis of BD and also significantly predicted male group classification.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Most fundamentally, the cross-sectional design cannot disentangle whether observed parameters reflect transient state markers or enduring trait features. Furthermore, while internal validation was robust, our specialized first-episode focus and resultant sample size limitations prevented external validation—an important direction for future research. Finally, although the study accounted for chronic medication effects, the possible influence of acute-phase (first 3-month) pharmacotherapy remains a potential confounder.

Interpretation and generalisability

After gender classification, two ROC curves were constructed by multiple linear regression showing good diagnostic accuracy, with the AUC of 0.706 (male) and 0.752 (female) in teenagers [12]. Based on that study, we used the multivariate logistic hierarchical regression model to absorb the eight parameters, and obtained accuracies are improved: the AUC of 0.894 (male) and 0.897 (female). Cronbach’s α suggests that the model has good internal stability. The statistical parameters of calibration indicated that the difference between the predicted values and the actual observations is not significant, and the prediction models also have good calibration ability. Thus, we observed two good accuracy models using the differential diagnosis model by the eight predictors in the list of routine examinations. From the perspective of such accuracy and stability, the model can try the next step for external verification and popularization. Discrimination and calibration could be found with fair-to-good diagnostic accuracy and external calibration capability in our previous prediction models from big data analysis [22].
