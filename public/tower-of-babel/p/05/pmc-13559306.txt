Association between preoperative blood glucose levels and postoperative pneumonia in neurosurgical patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Yan-Hong Lu, Ji-Peng Liu, Zhao-Yao Wang, Chao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood glucose levels are associated with the occurrence of infections. However, the relationship between preoperative blood glucose (POBG) levels in neurosurgery patients and the development of postoperative pneumonia (POP) remains uncertain. This study aimed to evaluate the association between POBG levels and the occurrence of POP in patients undergoing neurosurgery.MethodsA retrospective study enrolled 1,234 patients who had undergone neurosurgery at The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2021 and June 2024 to investigate the association of POBG levels and POP. The endpoint was defined as pneumonia occurring within 30 days after surgery. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was applied to identify the association between POBG and POP. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of POBG levels for the development of POP.ResultsAmong the 1,234 participants, 122 cases of POP were identified. POBG was positively correlated with the occurrence of POP after controlling for potential confounders [odds ratio (OR) =1.204, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.114–1.302, P<0.001]. After full adjustment for covariates, the POBG showed a nonlinear relationship with POP, with a threshold of approximately 6.4 mmol/L. The area under the curve for POBG was 80.8% (95% CI: 77.4–84.2%) with an optimal cut-off value of 6.37 mmol/L, resulting in a sensitivity of 65.6% and a speciﬁcity of 85.9%.ConclusionsThere exists a saturated nonlinear relationship between POBG levels and the occurrence of POP in post-neurosurgery patients. POBG levels correlate with POP risk in neurosurgical patients and offer auxiliary reference information when clinicians formulate preventive plans.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This retrospective analysis of 1,234 neurosurgical patients investigated the correlation between preoperative blood glucose (POBG) and postoperative pneumonia (POP).

• After rigorous adjustment for confounders, elevated POBG remained significantly correlated with increased POP risk (odds ratio =1.204, 95% confidence interval: 1.114–1.302, P<0.001).

• A saturated nonlinear relationship was observed at a 6.4 mmol/L inflection point. Below it, POP risk rose rapidly with increased POBG but saturated above the threshold. Receiver operating characteristic analysis confirmed favorable discriminatory capacity for POP at an optimal cut-off of 6.37 mmol/L (area under the curve =80.8%, sensitivity = 65.6%, specificity =85.9%), supporting POBG as a reliable POP risk biomarker.

What is known and what is new?

• Hyperglycemia impairs immune defense and pulmonary pathogen clearance, exacerbating postoperative infectious complications. Dysregulated perioperative blood glucose correlates closely with POP across multiple surgical cohorts. Nevertheless, the specific association between POBG and POP remains unclear in general neurosurgical populations.

• This study identifies a nonlinear saturated dose-response relationship between POBG and POP, determining a 6.4 mmol/L risk threshold. Routine POBG testing is low-cost and accessible, enabling POP risk screening for neurosurgical perioperative assessment.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This study provides an exploratory POBG threshold for risk stratification, helping stratify POP high-risk individuals and guide proactive prevention.

• The threshold can be applied to individualized perioperative stratified glycemic monitoring and targeted pneumonia prevention, with routine preoperative glucose testing integrated into POP risk screening.

Introduction

Postoperative pneumonia (POP) is a frequent, severe, and potentially life-threatening complication (1). The occurrence of POP not only elevates patient mortality rates but also extends hospital stays and increases hospitalization costs, thereby incurring substantial losses to society in terms of human, material, and financial resources. A 5-year Canadian cohort study involving 4,033 patients revealed that the 378 individuals with POP exhibited markedly reduced 5-year survival rates compared to those without POP (2). A systematic review of 49 studies indicated that non-ventilator healthcare-associated pneumonia significantly increased in-hospital mortality and prolonged hospital stay by an additional 10 to 48 days (3). In the United States, each mechanically ventilated patient complicated with pneumonia bears an average additional medical cost of $9,966 (4). However, there is limited knowledge about pneumonia following neurosurgery. Growing pieces of evidence indicate the prognostic value of managing preoperative hyperglycemia (5-7).

Blood glucose levels are an independent marker of infection in patients (8). Recent research has emphasized the role of hyperglycemia in increasing the risk of pneumonia through multiple pathways. Firstly, hyperglycemia has been found to suppress normal immune system function, impacting both cellular and humoral immunity (9). Additionally, it can also compromise the function of alveolar macrophages, essential for clearing pathogens from the lungs (10). Furthermore, hyperglycemia can lead to tissue damage and activation of immune cells, exacerbating the inflammatory response, and thereby contributing to the development of postoperative complications (11). However, research on the impact of preoperative glucose levels, as recommended by the American Endocrine Society clinical practice guidelines, on postoperative outcomes remains limited (12). The relationship between preoperative blood glucose (POBG) levels and the risk of POP in neurosurgical patients remains to be elucidated. Consequently, it is essential to gain a deeper understanding of the role of POBG in POP during neurosurgery to better inform targeted preventive measures.

Therefore, our study aimed to assess whether POBG levels are associated with the increased rate of POP in patients after neurosurgery. The findings of this study can generate valuable scientific evidence to improve risk stratification for patients with POP and support the adoption of more aggressive preventive measures after surgery. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0951/rc).

Methods

Study design and subjects

This retrospective cohort study analyzed patients who underwent neurosurgery at The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2021 and June 2024. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) diagnosed with preoperative pneumonia (n=38), preoperative intracranial infection (n=4), and preoperative soft tissue infection (n=2); (II) no available data with preoperative glucose (n=122), and diagnosis with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) (n=6) (Figure 1). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. 2024-241041). Given the retrospective design of this study, the requirement for written informed consent was formally waived by the ethics committee. Because of the retrospective observational study design, we did not predefine the sample size but included all eligible patients from the dataset.

Definition of POP

POP is characterized as a pulmonary infection affecting one or both lungs after surgery. The diagnosis of POP is confirmed by the presence of new or progressive chest radiographic infiltrates, not attributable to atelectasis or vasogenic edema, coupled with one or more of the following (13): (I) new or worsening symptoms such as cough, sputum production, fever, or hypothermia without an alternative etiology, accompanied by positive laboratory results (e.g. increased levels of leukocyte count, serum procalcitonin, and C-reactive protein); (II) auscultation revealing signs of lung consolidation and/or rales; (III) isolation of a pathogen from blood, sputum, transbronchial lavage fluid, or transbronchial brush specimens; (IV) histopathological confirmation of pneumonia.

Information on clinical parameters

Clinical parameters, including demographics, preoperative laboratory values, surgical details, and postoperative outcomes, were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical record system. Demographic data included gender and age. Preoperative baseline characteristics contained aspiration risk, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status, preoperative respiratory failure, normal consciousness, and preoperative medication history. The preoperative medication history was further divided into insulin use, steroid exposure, and antibiotic prophylaxis. Complications assessed included hypertension, diabetes, and cancer, while surgical information covered emergency operation, cranial implant modification, ventriculoscopic surgery, ventricular drainage, tracheotomy, and procedure type, with operative duration recorded as an intraoperative variable. Postoperative indicators involved postoperative respiratory support requirements and intubation duration. Preoperative laboratory indicators, including blood glucose, total protein, albumin, globulin, creatinine, and hemoglobin, were measured using a blood analyzer (AU6700, Beckman Coulter, Inc). All laboratory data were measured within 24 hours before the operation. For patients with multiple preoperative laboratory measurements, the values recorded nearest to the surgery date were utilized in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Differences in continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data comparisons were conducted using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the appropriateness of each. To correct for the potential inflation of type I error due to multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted according to the Tukey method. The association between POBG and POP was evaluated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. When the proportion of missing values for continuous variables was less than 2%, the missing values were substituted with the mean or median values. Logistic regression was applied to assess the association between POBG and the occurrence of POP. Confounding variables were selected based on expert consultation or if they resulted in a change in the effect estimate of more than 10%. Covariates were incrementally adjusted across four sequential models: Model 1 included age and gender; Model 2 added diabetes, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and smoking status to Model 1; Model 3 further incorporated neurological status, ASA physical status, preoperative respiratory failure, and emergency operation to Model 2; and Model 4 (fully adjusted) additionally accounted for antibiotic prophylaxis, insulin use, and steroid exposure. Multicollinearity assessment confirmed that variance inflation factors for all variables included in the model were below 5, confirming that there was no multi-collinearity in the regression model. We determined the optimal cutoff value via receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and adopted 1,000-time bootstrapping resampling to internally verify the stability of this exploratory threshold.

All of the analyses were performed with the statistical software packages R. version 4.2.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and the Free Statistics software (version 2.0: Beijing FreeClinical Medica Technology Co.Ltd., Beijing. China). FreeStatistics is a software package that offers intuitive interfaces for conducting common analyses and visualizing data (14,15). It leverages R as its statistical engine and employs a Python-written graphical user interface. The software facilitates most analyses through a few simple clicks, emphasizing reproducible research and interactive computation. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance, adhering to the norm in hypothesis testing. This threshold discerns chance findings from genuine data correlations.

Results

Study participants and baseline characteristics

A total of 1,234 patients were enrolled in the present study (Figure 1). Patients were categorized into four groups based on quartile of POBG levels: <4.93 mmol/L, 4.93 to 5.63 mmol/L, 5.63 to 7.52 mmol/L, and ≥7.52 mmol/L (Table 1). The average age of the study patients was 54.4 (16.1) years, and 518 (42.0%) were female. Emergency operation, aspiration risk, ventriculoscopic surgery, ventricular drainage, tracheotomy, operative duration, postoperative respiratory support requirements, intubation duration, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, creatinine, globulin, total protein, albumin, steroid exposure, female proportion, cancer, and normal consciousness varied significantly across POBG quartiles. Patients in higher POBG groups exhibited greater prevalence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, elevated creatinine, and increased globulin, alongside reduced proportions of females, cancer cases, and individuals with normal consciousness (all P<0.05). COPD, smoking, preoperative respiratory failure, cranial implant modification, procedure type, insulin use, antibiotic prophylaxis, and hemoglobin did not differ significantly between groups.

Associations between POBG and POP

The univariate logistic regression analysis revealed significant differences in sex, cancer, albumin, globulin, creatinine, emergency operation, ASA physical status (Grade IV and V), aspiration risk, preoperative respiratory failure, normal consciousness, cranial implant modification, ventriculoscopic surgery, ventricular drainage, tracheotomy, procedure type (infratentorial/posterior fossa), postoperative respiratory support requirements, intubation duration, and ICU admission (all P<0.05, Table S1). After full adjustment for age, gender, diabetes, cancer, COPD, smoking, neurologic status, ASA physical status, preoperative respiratory failure, emergency operation, antibiotic prophylaxis, insulin use, and steroid exposure in Model 4, POBG remained significantly and positively associated with an elevated risk of POP when compared with the reference group, with the adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) being 1.204 (1.114–1.302) (P<0.001). The positive association of POBG with POP across all four models is presented in Table 2. Figure 2 illustrates the results from the subgroup analyses. We observed a stable relationship between POBG and POP within each stratified subgroup, and the limited sample volume of emergency operation patients restricts the reliability of the detected interaction effect, necessitating further large-sample verification.

The nonlinearity relationship between POBG and POP

Through the smooth curve fitting, we observed that the relationship between POBG and POP was nonlinear (Figure 3). The two-piecewise regression model showed that the inflection point was 6.4 mmol/L after adjusting for covariates (age, gender, diabetes, cancer, COPD, smoking status, neurological status, ASA physical status, preoperative respiratory failure, emergency operation, antibiotic prophylaxis, insulin use, and steroid exposure). Below the threshold, the incidence of POP rose more rapidly with a multivariable-adjusted OR of 4.395 (95% CI: 1.742–11.087, P=0.002; Figure 3 and Table 3). In contrast, surpassing this threshold revealed a consistent correlation between elevated POBG levels and the incidence of POP in patients after neurosurgery (OR =1.119; 95% CI: 0.998–1.256, P=0.055; Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we identified an independent correlation between POBG and POP in patients undergoing neurosurgery. Notably, we observed a positive association between POBG and POP, which remained consistent and robust even after adjusting for underlying covariates and confounding factors. In summary, we found that POBG is an independent risk factor for POP in patients undergoing neurosurgery.

Previous studies on surgical or no-surgical fields were similar to our study (11,16-18). For example, You et al. conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of 3,124 acute ischemic stroke patients and found that high blood glucose level was independently associated with pneumonia (19). Zonneveld et al. found that hyperglycemia predicts poststroke infections in a large acute ischemic stroke cohort of 1,669 patients (20). Jones et al., in their retrospective observational cohort study analyzing 23,094 surgeries, determined that hyperglycemia is a significant independent risk factor for the development of POP in patients who underwent gastrointestinal surgery (21). Using multivariate and subgroup analysis, this study found a significant association between POBG in neurosurgery patients and the risk of POP. Intriguingly, despite a higher incidence of POP observed in the subgroup analysis among patients admitted to the ICU in this study (22.4% vs. 3.9%), the influence of POBG levels on pneumonia occurrence was relatively similar. This could be attributed to the more proactive blood glucose monitoring and intervention strategies employed for patients in the ICU. The significant interaction regarding emergency operation, while noteworthy, may be confounded by the small sample size. A larger prospective study is warranted to confirm the validity of this finding.

Furthermore, we identified a nonlinear and nonisometric trend in the effect size variation across various POBG groups, suggesting a dose-response relationship between POBG and POP. Notably, a significant difference in the correlation between POBG and POP was observed when stratified at a threshold of 6.4 mmol/L. Specifically, when POBG levels were below 6.4 mmol/L, the incidence of POP in post-neurosurgery patients increased with rising POBG levels. However, when POBG exceeded 6.4 mmol/L, the association became statistically nonsignificant. This indicates a nonlinear relationship with a saturation effect between POBG and POP in post-neurosurgery patients.

Therefore, our findings indicate that POBG levels are closely correlated with the risk of POP. The AUC of 80.8% for POBG when distinguishing patients with and without POP was calculated. Early identification of individuals susceptible to POP carries great clinical significance, and perioperative POBG testing can deliver auxiliary reference evidence for clinical management strategies. Further prospective investigations are warranted to confirm whether POBG could act as an easily obtainable perioperative risk marker. Given its low cost and ease of accessibility, POBG may serve as a more practical alternative to complex prediction models in clinical practice, offering a convenient method for the rapid assessment of POP. To address the limitations of this study, several improvements and explorations are needed. Firstly, this study cannot establish causal relationships merely based on correlational analyses. Secondly, the subjective nature of clinical diagnosis and the potential for overestimation or underestimation of the actual incidence of POP due to this subjectivity and variability warrant the use of more objective measures for diagnosing POP in future studies. Thirdly, despite adjustment for multiple confounders, residual confounding stemming from uncollected or missing data (e.g., glycated hemoglobin) and unmeasured peri-operative stress-induced preoperative hyperglycemia may bias effect estimates, so external independent cohorts are required to replicate our overall results. Fourthly, the 6.4 mmol/L inflection point yielded by exploratory piecewise regression only demonstrates the correlation between POBG and POP rather than a clinically applicable glycemic target. Excessively lowering glucose to this value may induce hypoglycemia-mediated secondary brain injury. Prospective cohort studies are warranted to validate this threshold and formulate guideline-compliant stratified glycemic targets for brain-injured patients. Finally, the reported incidence of pneumonia in ICUs has been documented to exceed 30% (22,23). It is important to note that our study included a relatively high number of patients who were transferred to ICUs, which may have contributed to the observed higher rate of pneumonia in our research. By addressing these limitations in future research, we can improve the robustness and reliability of our understanding of the relationship between POBG level and the risk of POP.

Conclusions

There exists a nonlinear relationship between POBG levels and the occurrence of POP in patients after neurosurgery. POBG levels are closely correlated with POP risk among neurosurgical patients, which provides supportive evidence for individualized preventive strategies of this complication. Nonetheless, additional studies are imperative to substantiate and augment the validity of these results.
