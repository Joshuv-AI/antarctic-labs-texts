LDL-C Remains Elevated in Patients With Very High Cardiovascular Risk in India and Elsewhere
Authors: Daniel A Added, Raul D Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: atherosclerosis, cholesterol, ezetimibe, statins

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a leading worldwide cause of morbidity and mortality. Over the past decades, lipid management has been increasingly shaped by randomized trials and meta-analyses showing that more intensive further lowering of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduces ASCVD risk without safety concerns compared with more conservative therapies.1,2 Studies with combined therapies with ezetimibe and more robustly with monoclonal PCSK9 inhibitors show additional benefits in comparison with statin therapy alone, even when the latter are used in their highest doses.2

In this evolving landscape, the challenge in preventive cardiology is no longer to justify LDL-C lowering but to determine whether scientific advances, broader therapeutic options, and increasingly ambitious treatment goals are being translated into more intensive lipid-lowering strategies and more reliable goal attainment in routine practice. In this context, the nationwide Indian report by Puri et al3—the Lipid Association of India in Coronary Artery Disease (LAI-LCARE) study—published in this issue of JACC: Asia is a timely real-world assessment of whether contemporary, evidence-based preventive care is truly delivered in clinical practice. The findings are sobering and not unique to India. Among 9,658 patients with documented coronary artery disease receiving stable lipid-lowering therapy, the median treated LDL-C level was 66.1 mg/dL; however, only 27.0% achieved an LDL-C level <50 mg/dL, the recommended goal in the Indian guidelines for patients considered at very high risk of ASCVD events.

These observations are directionally consistent with contemporary real-world reports from other regions of the world, including healthcare systems in high-income countries, in which LDL-C goal attainment in secondary prevention has likewise remained suboptimal despite widespread statin use and the availability of effective adjunctive therapies.4, 5, 6 For instance, in Europe, in DA VINCI, only 18% of patients achieved an LDL-C level <55 mg/dL4; in Dyslipidemia International Study II, an LDL-C level <70 mg/dL (an easier-to-reach goal) was attained in only 29.4% of patients with stable coronary disease at baseline and in 37% after an acute coronary syndrome.5 In SANTORINI, another European registry, only 20% of participants had a baseline LDL-C level <55 mg/dL.6 In the multinational non-European, non-US International Cholesterol Management Practice Study, only 28.5% of very-high-risk patients reached an LDL-C level <70 mg/dL,7 whereas in Survey of Risk Factors in Coronary Heart Disease II, another global registry, only 20.8% achieved an LDL-C level <55 mg/dL and 40.8% achieved an LDL-C level <70 mg/dL.8

LAI-LCARE3 therefore not only describes an isolated regional shortcoming but also reinforces a globally recognizable pattern of incomplete treatment, insufficient intensification, and therapeutic inertia. Particularly in India, among patients with an LDL-C level >50 mg/dL, only 37% of physicians uptitrated lipid-lowering therapy. Moreover, combination therapy, even with ezetimibe, a generic, inexpensive medication, was used by only 12% of participants. Of interest, men had a 50% higher chance of attaining LDL-C goals than women, a fact showing a worrisome sex gap in therapy.

LAI-LCARE enrolled a particularly informative study group, patients with objective evidence of clinical coronary atherosclerosis already under the care of cardiologists and receiving stable therapy. This was therefore a highly selected, specialist-treated, secondary-prevention cohort in whom the rationale for intensive LDL-C lowering should be among the clearest and the expected absolute benefit among the greatest. Yet even in this setting, target attainment remained poor.

The authors appropriately acknowledge that their data likely reflect the upper bound of contemporary clinical practice rather than routine care across all healthcare settings in a continental country like India. Participating physicians were specialists, and most centers were urban or semiurban; the study likely overestimated what would be observed in nonspecialist practice, in rural settings, or in lower-resource study groups. If LDL-C control remains inadequate in this context, the implementation gap may be even wider among patients treated outside cardiology clinics.

At the same time, the contemporary implications of LAI-LCARE extend beyond the study group directly studied. The registry was intentionally restricted to patients with objective coronary disease, and only a small proportion had cerebrovascular disease or peripheral artery disease.3 This strengthens the clinical clarity of the cohort, but it also highlights that the study does not capture the full spectrum of patients for whom intensive LDL-C lowering now matters. This is where the recent US 2026 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association dyslipidemia guideline is particularly important.9 By restoring explicit LDL-C and non–HDL-C goals and extending goal-directed lipid management across both primary- and secondary-prevention settings, the guideline substantially enlarges the study group for whom the achieved LDL-C levels may now be considered above target, beyond very-high-risk patients with ASCVD, including adults with severe hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, HIV, and subclinical coronary atherosclerosis. Accordingly, contemporary guideline-intensive LDL-C goals now apply to a substantially broader study group than that captured in LAI-LCARE, increasing the clinical relevance of treatment gaps observed even within this selected secondary-prevention cohort.

The practical implications of LAI-LCARE are straightforward: We need to accept less passively “reasonable” LDL-C values. The mean of 66 mg/dL achieved in the study was not bad, but it was not enough according to the current evidence.9 More systematic follow-up after statin initiation, more consistent upfront use of ezetimibe and other nonstatin agents when goals are not achieved, and greater recognition that this intensive prevention now extends beyond patients with manifest ASCVD. LAI-LCARE should therefore not be read simply as a report from India. It should be read as a warning to the rest of the world. The authors should be congratulated for their efforts.
