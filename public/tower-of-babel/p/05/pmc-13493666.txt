Longitudinal Performance of the Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool (PEST) in Canada and Latin America: Results from the Prospective, Observational, 5-Year PURE Registry
Authors: Jennifer Beecker, Lorne Albrecht, Ignacio Dei-Cas, Melinda Gooderham, Charles Lynde, Kim A Papp, Paul Keown, Antonio Vieira, Mahmoud S Farag, Marie Maguin, Tanya Zappitelli, Philip S Helliwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool (PEST) is a 5-item questionnaire designed to identify patients with psoriasis who have co-existing psoriatic arthritis (PsA). This analysis investigated the longitudinal performance of the PEST questionnaire in the prospective, observational PURE registry.MethodsThe PURE registry included 2132 patients with psoriasis who completed the PEST at baseline, 3 and 6 months then every 6 months for 5 years. Baseline diagnostic performance was assessed using existing PsA diagnoses as the reference standard. PEST positivity changes over time were assessed, and regression models determined factors linked to baseline PEST status and conversion from negative to positive. Cronbach’s alpha assessed internal consistency, while exploratory factor analysis evaluated changes in individual PEST questions over time.ResultsAt baseline, 906 patients (42.5%) were PEST positive and 373 (17.5%) had PsA, for a diagnostic sensitivity of 89% and a negative predictive value of 97%. Overall, the proportion of patients who became PEST positive increased from 42.5% at baseline to 63.9% at 5 years, rising from 89.0% to 95.8% and 32.6% to 57.2% respectively in those who were PEST positive or negative at baseline. Regression modeling confirmed that increased age (p < 0.0001), weight > 90 kg (p < 0.0001), female sex (p = 0.0107) and prior biologic exposure (p < 0.0001) were related to PEST status at baseline and of which the first three were also related to change from negative to positive during follow-up. Cronbach’s alpha showed high internal consistency of the PEST questionnaire (0.693). Exploratory factor analysis found that responses to questions 1, 2, 4, and 5 increased over time, while question 3 decreased. Eigenvalues revealed consistent dominance of question 1 and two-factor structure in the questionnaire.ConclusionThe PEST questionnaire is an informative, easy-to-use tool which may be useful to monitor and identify patients with psoriasis who are at risk of PsA over time.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT02786186; date of registration May 30, 2016.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01826-7.Keywords: PEST questionnaire, Plaque psoriasis, Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool, Psoriatic arthritis, Secukinumab, PURE registry

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?The Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool (PEST) is a simple and practical instrument designed to help healthcare professionals identify patients at increased risk for PsA who require further assessment.This analysis is the first of its kind which explores the clinical use, change over time, and psychometric characteristics of the PEST questionnaire when used longitudinally in a large, real-world psoriasis cohort.What was learned from the study?Many patients with psoriasis who were initially PEST negative at baseline became PEST positive over 5 years of observation, highlighting that a significant number of patients with psoriasis who are at risk of PsA may not be receiving appropriate monitoring.This analysis adds to the expanding research on the utility of the PEST questionnaire to identify patients with psoriasis at risk of PsA, reinforcing its use as a standardized instrument in clinical practice.

Introduction

Psoriasis is an inflammatory, systemic disorder affecting the skin as well as peripheral and axial joints [1]. It has a wide range of clinical manifestations and sequelae among which psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a major complication occurring in up to 30% of patients [1–3]. PsA is heterogenous in presentation with no verified specific imaging or laboratory diagnostic tests, making early and precise diagnosis challenging [4]. This has important clinical implications as PsA can be progressive and cause irreversible joint damage [5], and early diagnosis and treatment may lead to better clinical outcomes [6]. A timely clinical diagnosis of PsA requires knowledgeable assessment of joints and skin, and possibly imaging [7], so that the role of dermatologists is pivotal to ensure timely referral to rheumatologists for expert diagnosis and optimal outcomes [7].

The Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool (PEST) is a simple and practical instrument designed to help healthcare professionals identify patients at increased risk for PsA who require further assessment [8, 9]. The PEST is a self-administered questionnaire consisting of five simple questions related to markers of PsA, with a minimum score of zero and a maximum total score of five [8]. This questionnaire has demonstrated good sensitivity and specificity and has been reported to be comparable or superior to other common PsA identification tools [8–11]. The National Psoriasis Foundation and the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence have both recommended that the PEST questionnaire be used annually or semi-annually to identify PsA. Despite this, there is little evidence of its current use in the longitudinal setting as a screening tool to assess disease progression and treatment response. Further, the harmonization of outcome measures for PsA has been highlighted as an important area of research [12].

The PURE registry, an ongoing longitudinal study designed to enroll 2500 adult patients with moderate to severe psoriasis from community and hospital specialists across Canada and Latin America, provides a unique opportunity to evaluate the performance of the PEST questionnaire under conditions of carefully monitored clinical practice [13]. Here we describe the baseline and longitudinal performance of the PEST questionnaire in this registry, confirming its cross-sectional discriminant capacity at baseline, its quantitative change over time in patients with and without clinical PsA at study entry, and report the psychometric performance of this assay for longitudinal use.

Study Design

The full details of the rationale, objectives, and design of the PURE registry (NCT02786186) have been reported previously [13]. The PURE registry is a prospective, observational, cohort study conducted in over 60 sites across Canada and Latin America, designed to enroll patients who, prior to and independent of study enrolment, commenced treatment with secukinumab or another indicated and approved therapy for moderate to severe psoriasis [13]. Two study cohorts were defined comprising patients treated with secukinumab, and patients treated with other indicated therapies [13]. Patients were followed for 5 years from the baseline assessment with a recommended schedule of visits at 3 months each for the first 2 visits and every 6 months thereafter for a total of 12 (baseline + 11 follow-up) visits [13].

At the time of this interim analysis, patient enrolment had been completed, and follow-up was proceeding.

Patients

Eligibility criteria included adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) who had a confirmed diagnosis of moderate to severe, chronic, plaque-type psoriasis diagnosed by a specialist, who were initiating psoriasis treatment as per regional clinical policy with treatments prescribed according to regional regulatory and reimbursement policies [13]. Patients were excluded if they were currently participating in another clinical trial or had done so within 30 days prior to enrolment [13].

Endpoints and Assessments

All required licenses and/or permissions for use of the DLQI, HADS, and PEST in the PURE registry (NCT02786186) were obtained at study initiation, including authorization from Professor Philip Helliwell (University of Leeds) for the use of PEST.

The principal outcome measure in this analysis was the PEST score that was captured as part of the PURE registry. Participants were requested to complete the PEST questionnaire at baseline and every 6 months thereafter until month 60 using an online electronic patient-reported outcome system or a paper version depending on the clinic. To avoid selection bias in this non-interventional trial, the results of the PEST questionnaire were not reported to the treating physicians and no follow-up was mandated if a positive PEST score was recorded.

Statistical Analysis

This analysis was conducted in a modified intent-to-treat (mITT) data set from the PURE registry which comprised all enrolled participants with an assigned treatment cohort at baseline who had at least one post-baseline physician visit, electronic submission of patient-reported outcomes, or evaluation of adverse events (AEs). Each question in the PEST questionnaire was considered a binary response (no = 0; yes = 1) and scores were summated to give an overall score and subsequent categorization (PEST positive: ≥ 3; PEST negative: < 3). PEST scores were assessed at each timepoint and the time to change from negative to positive values recorded. At baseline, the presence of PsA was defined by a confirmed physician diagnosis or by a patient indicating such a diagnosis to the treating physician. Concordance between PEST scores and the diagnosis of PsA at baseline was assessed to determine sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values of the PEST score. Patients were stratified at baseline to PsA-positive and PsA-negative groups which were analyzed longitudinally using cumulative incidence models to evaluate the time to becoming PEST positive. Continuous variables were analyzed using the mean, median, standard deviation (SD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) and categorical variables using frequency distributions. Logistic regression, cumulative incidence and Cox models were developed to assess the influence of baseline characteristics on the probability of being PEST positive at baseline and of becoming PEST positive during the period of study observation. Internal consistency of the responses from individual PEST questions was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and exploratory factor analysis was employed to determine the underlying relationships between the measured variables. Statistical analyses were performed using Version 9.4 of SAS®.

All patients provided informed consent to participate in the study. The study design was reviewed and approved by the ethics committees or institutional review boards, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principle of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964, and its later amendments.

Results

Patient Population

A total of 2254 patients who were included in the PURE registry fulfilled the mITT criteria, of whom 2132 had evaluable PEST data at baseline and were included in this analysis. Baseline patient demographic and disease characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Overall, patients had a mean (standard deviation [SD]) age of 49.1 (14.1) years, with over half of all patients being male (56.4% [1202/2132]), and the majority being White (78.7% [1677/2132]). At baseline, the mean (SD) time from psoriasis diagnosis to enrolment in the study was 16.6 (13.8) years and 29.3% (624/2132) of patients had prior exposure to biologics.

Baseline PEST Status

Baseline patient disposition by PEST status and PsA diagnosis is shown in Fig. 1. Of the 2132 patients enrolled, 906 (42.5%) were PEST positive and 1226 (57.5%) were PEST negative. As shown in Table 1, the mean (SD) overall PEST score at baseline was 2.2 (1.6); the 906 patients who were PEST positive had a mean (SD) score of 3.9 (0.8) while the 1226 who were PEST negative had a mean (SD) score of 1.0 (0.8). Patients who were PEST positive were older (mean age 51.5 versus 47.2 years) (p < 0.0001), more commonly female (47.2% versus 40.9%) (p = 0.004), had a higher body mass (50.6% versus 41.5% were > 90 kg) (p < 0.0001), had a higher frequency of prior exposure to biologics (35.9% versus 24.4%) (p < 0.0001), and were receiving current systemic biologic therapy (81.6% versus 70.7%) (p < 0.0001) versus patients who were PEST negative.

At baseline, 17.5% (373/2132) of patients were medically diagnosed with PsA. As shown in Table 2, 89.0% of these were PEST positive, while of the 82.5% (1759/2132) without a PsA diagnosis, 67.4% (1185/1759) were PEST negative (p < 0.0001). The baseline sensitivity of the PEST questionnaire for diagnosing PsA at baseline was 89% with a negative predictive value of 97%.

Longitudinal Change in PEST Response

Patient retention was high during the 5-year period of observation and less than 10% of subjects were withdrawn or lost to follow-up at the point of this interim analysis. Since the study was conducted under conditions of normal clinical practice, and partly during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic, not all patients had reached or were present for each of the 12 protocol visits because of clinical status, physician instructions, COVID-19 infection and other factors. Despite this, at least 80% of eligible patients who attended each respective visit provided a PEST response (Fig. 2).

Completion rates for the PEST as a proportion of patients in the study at each follow-up visit. Bar graphs indicating the proportion of patients who completed the PEST questionnaire in the PURE registry at each visit. M month, PEST Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool

During the 60 months of follow-up, 322 additional patients became PEST positive. The cumulative incidences of these changes in PEST score are shown in Fig. 3. Overall, the proportion of patients who were PEST positive increased from 42.5% at baseline to a cumulative incidence of 63.9% by month 60 (Fig. 3a). Among the 373 patients who were medically diagnosed with PsA at baseline, the cumulative incidence of becoming PEST positive rose from 89.0% at baseline to 95.8% by the end of follow-up, while the corresponding proportion among the 1759 patients not medically diagnosed with PsA at baseline increased from 32.6% to 57.2% (Fig. 3b).

Increased age per decade (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.18–1.36, p < 0.0001), female sex (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.06–1.57, p = 0.0107), weight > 90 kg (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.26–1.85, p < 0.0001), prior treatment with biologics (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.38–2.08, p < 0.0001) and increased time from diagnosis of psoriasis to enrolment (per each additional year OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.002–1.02, p = 0.0106, p < 0.0129) were significant predictors of being PEST positive at baseline. Quality of life, as determined by DLQI, was also highly associated with PEST positivity (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04–1.07, p < 0.0001).

Among patients who were PEST negative at baseline (N = 1226), Cox modeling showed that increased age per decade (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.14–1.35, p < 0.0001), female sex (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10–1.80, p = 0.0068), weight > 90 kg (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.09–1.76, p = 0.0070) and DLQI score (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, p = 0.0034) were associated with changing from negative to positive during follow-up (Fig. 5b). Neither treatment with biologic therapy before enrolment (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.85–1.48, p = 0.4143) nor at baseline (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.65–1.10, p = 0.2103) was independently associated with change from PEST negative to PEST positive over, time perhaps reflecting diametrically opposed indicators of risk. Of the 1544 patients receiving biologic therapy at baseline, 629 (40.7%) remained PEST negative and 705 (45.7%) PEST positive while 210 (13.6%) changed from negative to positive during follow-up (Table 3). When grouped by mechanism of action, 11.5% of patients treated with an IL-17 antagonist became PEST positive over time, compared with 18.2% of those treated with IL-23 and 18.3% treated with TNF antagonists (Table 3).

Questionnaire Performance

The PEST questionnaire response rate at baseline was high; the minimum completion rate for individual questions was 99.6%, and 99.3% [2118/2132] of patients completed all five questions. The responses to each question based on baseline PEST status are shown in Fig. 6. In patients who were PEST negative, question 1 (swollen joints) and question 3 (pits in nails) were reported as “yes” in approximately 30% of cases, while question 2 (arthritis), question 4 (heel pain) and question 5 (swollen digit) were reported as “yes” in 8.7‒18.8% of cases. In contrast, among patients who were PEST positive, question 1 (swollen joints) was reported as “yes” in 95.1% of cases, and question 2 (arthritis), question 3 (pits in nails), question 4 (heel pain) and question 5 (swollen digit) were reported as “yes” by 70.7–79.8% of cases.

Cronbach’s alpha showed high internal consistency of the PEST questionnaire (0.693). Exploratory factor analysis showed a relatively high loading of 0.58 for question 1, with intermediate loading for question 2 (0.41), question 3 (0.48) and question 4 (0.42), and a more modest loading for question 5 (0.35) (Table 4). During follow-up, completion rates for individual questions among the responders were 100% for question 1, 99.8% for question 2, 94.4% for question 3, 91.4% for question 4 and 91.4% for question 5. Internal consistency, as shown by Cronbach’s alpha, was nearly consistent across the duration of the study being 0.69 at baseline, 0.68 at month 12, 0.66 at month 24, 0.66 at month 36, 0.65 at month 48 and 0.66 at month 60.

The mean value for question 1, for example, rose from 0.58 at baseline to 0.70, 0.75, 0.79, 0.82 and 0.85 at months 12–60, respectively, while that for question 3 declined from 0.48 at baseline to 0.33 at month 60 (Table 4). Discussion

This analysis evaluated the longitudinal performance characteristics of the PEST questionnaire as part of the PURE registry. The PURE registry represents a unique opportunity to assess the longitudinal performance of the PEST questionnaire using a large cohort of patients with psoriasis. This is the first real-world evidence study of its kind to evaluate the longitudinal responses of the PEST questionnaire and provides evidence on the utility of the PEST questionnaire in identifying patients at risk of having PsA.

In routine clinical practice, the PEST is intended to screen patients with psoriasis without a prior diagnosis of PsA; a positive screen helps identify individuals who may warrant further assessment and/or referral to a rheumatologist. Accordingly, a patient with an already confirmed diagnosis of PsA would not typically be evaluated using PEST for diagnostic purposes. In the PURE Registry, however, PEST was captured as one of the patient-reported outcomes included in the prespecified secondary outcome measures. Therefore, PEST was administered to all enrolled participants as part of the standardized registry data collection schedule at baseline and every 6 months thereafter, irrespective of baseline PsA status.

In this analysis, the PEST questionnaire demonstrated good specificity for correctly identifying patients with existing, medically diagnosed, PsA. At baseline, 42.5% of patients (906/2132) were PEST positive and 17.5% (373/2132) of patients had been diagnosed with PsA. This proportion aligns with the reported prevalence of PsA in patients with psoriasis, indicating that the sample population may reflect the wider psoriasis cohort [14]. When concordance between those with PsA and a positive PEST score was assessed, a sensitivity of 89% was identified as 332/373 of patients who had PsA also screened PEST positive on the PEST questionnaire. This finding is concordant with prior reports on the utility of the PEST questionnaire for identifying patients who have a high risk of PsA and excluding those at low risk, given its very high sensitivity and negative predictive value [8, 9, 15, 16].

The PEST questionnaire response rate was high at baseline and remained above 80% throughout the period of follow-up supporting its simplicity and practicality for longitudinal use in real-world clinical settings. Cronbach’s alpha showed high internal consistency, and the high loading factor for question 1 in exploratory factor analysis, combined with its relative contribution to an overall positive PEST score at both baseline and during observation suggested it to be the most informative question in the questionnaire. In contrast, question 3 (pits in nails) appeared to contribute least to a positive PEST score. Of interest, question 3 is the only feature of the PEST that requires patients to make a self-examination. These novel longitudinal data may provide an opportunity for review and revision of the PEST instrument to increase positive predictive value. Given these observations, future iterations of the PEST questionnaire might benefit, for instance, from a closer evaluation of items that rely on patient self-assessment, such as question 3, to enhance the accuracy of identifying individuals at risk.

This analysis adds to the expanding research on the utility of the PEST questionnaire to identify patients with psoriasis at risk of PsA, reinforcing its use as a standardized instrument in clinical practice. The proportion of patients with a positive PEST score rose from 42.5% at baseline and peaked at 63.9% at month 60. This finding is important as it highlights that over time, a substantial number of patients with psoriasis become PEST positive, though we do not yet know whether they were referred or evaluated for PsA. Many factors may be contributing to this as a result of the many subtle presentations and manifestations of PsA such as low symptom intensity [17], and the assumptions of the PEST questionnaire that disregard the distribution of PsA intensity and duration. Previous studies report that a high proportion of patients with psoriasis with PsA may be undiagnosed when treated in a dermatology setting alone, highlighting the importance of regular screening and referral to rheumatology for a prompt and accurate diagnosis of PsA [3].

On the basis of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, increased age, female sex, higher weight category, time from diagnosis, DLQI total score, and prior biologic exposure were identified as significant covariates in patients who were PEST positive at baseline, and increased age, female sex, higher weight and DLQI score were significant in those who became PEST positive over time. Most of these align with previous reports relating these characteristics to PsA [18, 19], including the CorEvitas study [20], and a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis. Initial analysis of the influence of biologic class of treatment indicated that patients receiving anti-IL-17 therapy at baseline had a slightly lower rate of conversion from PEST negative to PEST positive, followed by those on IL-23 and TNF antagonists. This clinically interesting observation may reflect a treatment effect; notably, real-world evidence from a large retrospective electronic health records cohort (> 100 million patient lives) reported a lower cumulative incidence of PsA/inflammatory arthritis over 5 years with IL-17i versus IL-12/23i and TNFi, and numerically lower versus IL-23i [21], although interpretation is limited by the observational study design. The effect of continuing or initiating biologic treatments during the follow-up period was not assessed in this analysis. As patients in the study may have discontinued or started biologics, switched between therapies, or experienced adjustments in dose and dosing frequency, a more detailed dynamic analysis is planned for future research. Alternatively, these results may indicate a selection effect or a questionnaire-related artifact, since variations in patient characteristics, symptom perception, or altered responses due to repeated questionnaire use could impact PEST positivity independently of actual PsA risk, or result from multifactorial influences.

These data highlight the importance of appropriately assessing and treating patients with psoriasis to minimize the risk of disease sequelae [22–25]. Appropriately using the PEST questionnaire longitudinally in patients with psoriasis may help identify patients who are at risk of PsA. This longitudinal report of the dynamic performance of the PEST questionnaire is a first of its kind and provides valuable insight into the potential benefits of integrating the PEST questionnaire into routine clinical practice to improve the diagnosis of PsA. Between baseline and month 60, a total of 472 patients became PEST positive, supporting the utility of the PEST questionnaire in detecting patients at risk of PsA. The PEST may be a tool that can be utilized as an early predictor of those at risk of PsA, but it merits further investigation. This tool provides a unique opportunity for early evaluation and diagnosis of patients with psoriasis to prevent the progression of a very serious and debilitating disease.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations including potential selection bias, information bias and confounding which are inherent to observational design. The registry-based nature of the study limits causal interpretation of the findings. To minimize selection bias, the study included data from adult patients at multiple participating hospitals across Latin America and Canada, though the potential for time-varying differences in patient referral, case mix, unit services and care patterns remain. While information bias may occur from many sources, stringent efforts have also been made to reduce this by continuous review of reported data, identification of missingness, implausible values and outliers, and repeated query of the participating sites to resolve any data issues observed. Since this study spanned the period of the COVID-19 pandemic, altered patient follow-up and other related factors may have influenced the clinical case mix and distribution of patients within the study although all practical efforts were made to minimize related biases.

Additionally, the progressive increase in PEST positivity observed over time could reflect true accumulation of inflammatory musculoskeletal disease over time; however, it may also be influenced by age-related musculoskeletal complaints that are not attributable to PsA, changes in symptom interpretation by patients as they become more familiar with the questionnaire, or other forms of questionnaire drift/response shift across repeated administrations. While these factors could inflate PEST positivity and overestimate PsA risk when interpreted in isolation, the observed trend may nonetheless identify a patient subgroup with a higher burden of musculoskeletal symptoms who could be well served by timely evaluation by a rheumatologist. Importantly, PEST results were not communicated to clinicians, preventing assessment of their real impact on clinical decision-making or referral patterns. The potential implications on clinical decision-making or referral patterns to rheumatology are important considerations, but were not assessed within the PURE registry so should be evaluated and elaborated separately from this longitudinal analysis. Finally, treatment effects—including biologic therapies such as secukinumab—were not fully accounted for dynamically, which may have influenced musculoskeletal symptom reporting and, consequently, PEST scores over time. The absence of corroborated external verification of PsA status, both at baseline and follow-up, represents a limitation introducing potential misclassification bias, as PsA status relied on physician report or patient disclosure. The clinical significance of PEST conversion in the absence of a confirmed PsA diagnosis remains unclear and warrants further investigation in studies incorporating rheumatologic assessment.

Conclusions

This analysis is the first of its kind which assessed the longitudinal performance of the PEST questionnaire in a population study of patients with psoriasis. Over time, many patients with psoriasis who were initially PEST negative at baseline became PEST positive over 5 years of observation, highlighting that a significant number of patients with psoriasis who are at risk of developing PsA may not be receiving appropriate monitoring. The PEST questionnaire may be an informative, easy-to-use tool to identify patients with psoriasis at risk of PsA over time. Ongoing efforts are currently under investigation to further explore and validate the use of the PEST questionnaire in routine dermatology.
