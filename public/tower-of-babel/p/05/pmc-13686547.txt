Weight Loss Intentionality and Strategies in Urinary Incontinence
Authors: Eric S Chai, Jonathan T Ryan, Vismitha Naik, Nicolas Tapia‐Stoll, Katherine A Amin, Ricardo Palmerola, Adam Williams, Raveen Syan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionUrinary incontinence (UI)−including stress (SUI), urge (UUI), and mixed (MUI) subtypes−is associated with obesity, and weight loss is commonly recommended for intervention. However, the comparative effects of intentionality and weight loss strategies on UI severity remain poorly defined.Material and MethodsWe analyzed 16,331 adults from the 2001–2020 NHANES cohorts who self‐reported 1‐year weight loss and completed the UI questionnaire. Our use of a large, diverse sample of U.S. men and women and self‐reported intentionality and strategies (dietary changes, exercise, intermittent fasting, and diet pills) was unique. UI severity was defined by number of leakage episodes. Ordinal logistic regression, adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities, evaluated associations between weight loss intentionality and UI severity.ResultsAmong 16,331 adults, 9581 (58.7%) reported intentional and 6750 (41.3%) unintentional weight loss. After adjustment, intentional weight loss was associated with lower odds of more frequent SUI (0.79 OR [95%CI 0.68–0.92]), UUI (0.85 OR [95%CI 0.73–0.99]), and MUI (0.79 OR [95%CI 0.64–0.98]) compared to unintentional weight loss. Commonly used strategies included dietary modification (n = 8818), exercise (n = 5714), intermittent fasting (n = 1584), and diet pills (n = 238). Exercise was associated with the lowest prevalence of frequent SUI, UUI, and MUI symptoms in both males and females. Stratified analysis demonstrated that exercise remained associated with lower UUI symptom frequency even among individuals achieving ≤ 5% weight loss.ConclusionsIntentional weight loss was associated with lower likelihood of frequent UI symptoms compared to unintentional weight loss. Exercise was the intervention associated with the lowest severity of SUI, UUI, and MUI in both males and females. Our findings are consistent with current recommendations for active behavioral modification for UI weight loss. Additional prospective comparative analysis of exercise and other weight loss strategies is warranted.Keywords: exercise, NHANES, obesity, overactive bladder, urinary incontinence, weight loss

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) consists of three major subsets: urge urinary incontinence (UUI), stress urinary incontinence (SUI), and mixed urinary incontinence (MUI). UI costs healthcare systems tens of billions per year and up to thousands per patient [1, 2]. Additionally, UI has been linked to increased rates of depression, anxiety, and stress [3, 4]. Given that 24–45% of elderly women and 11–34% of elderly men report some form of UI, it accounts for significant burden both financially and socially worldwide [3, 5].

Obesity has a strong association with UI in both males and females [6, 7, 8]. The likelihood of developing overactive bladder (OAB) and SUI have both been observed to increase directly in proportion to body weight [7, 8]. Higher body mass index (BMI), has also been linked to higher severity and frequency of incontinence [7, 8]. Previous studies have demonstrated significant improvements in quality of life and the reductions in frequency of urinary incontinence symptoms following weight loss [8, 9, 10]. Weight loss has thus long been proposed as an initial lifestyle intervention for patients with UI. However, many studies do not reflect typical real‐world weight loss behaviors. Additionally, differences between intentional and unintentional weight loss in UI is unclear. Intentional weight loss represents those pursuing active intervention in metabolic or functional health. Unintentional weight loss lacks active changes, often reflecting underlying disorders or non‐conscious behaviors. It is unclear if weight loss intentionality relates to differences in UI. Differences may clarify possible benefits of goal‐directed active intervention in clinical practice.

The effects of intentional weight‐loss strategies are well studied in isolation from each other [8, 9, 10]. Prior studies evaluating physical activity and UI have largely focused on women or specific exercise modalities, limiting generalizability to real‐world exercise behaviors for both sexes. Increases in overall physical activity, particularly walking, have been linked to a lower incidence of UUI and MUI but not SUI in postmenopausal women, even when accounting for changes in weight [11, 12]. Additionally, yoga and pure barre workouts have been linked to improvements in self‐perception of UI symptoms, with yoga being particularly effective in SUI [13, 14, 15]. These studies did not focus on individuals pursuing activity for the goal of weight loss with recreational and work‐related activity accounting for most. Although increases in work or recreational physical activity may contribute to unintentional weight loss, there was limited focus on weight loss in these studies. Ultimately, many who pursue physical activity lack the consistency and intensity of those who are directly studied. It is important to understand the level of benefit real world, often sub‐optimal, exercise regimens offer.

Diet is another major modality of weight loss, but prior studies with UI have been limited. Higher protein intake has been associated with increased UUI risk [11]. Lower‐fat diets, on the other hand, have been linked to decreased rates of self‐reported UI symptoms [16]. It is unclear how multi‐factorial, goal‐directed dietary changes, intermittent fasting, or diet pill use associated with intentional weight loss interact with UI. Notably, direct comparison of common weight loss strategies and UI in a diverse U.S. adult cohort has been limited. Current patient counseleing on UI weight loss lacks clear regimens to maximize symptom relief. It is important to study this gap for future counseling.

We aimed to evaluate associations between intentional and unintentional weight loss and UI in a large, diverse sample of United States adults. This included comparative analysis of intentional weight loss strategies (exercise, dietary changes, intermittent fasting, and diet pills) practiced in real‐world manners. This study offers a unique analysis on weight loss intentionality, which is important in assessing how being goal directed in weight loss interacts with UI symptoms. Furthermore, this study uniquely compares different active strategies in their real world implementation to infer if there may be any differences in UI symptomatology. Unlike prior studies, both males and females will be analyzed.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a large‐scale population‐based survey conducted in the United States by the National Center for Health Statistics. It is unique for being a national cross‐sectional database running since 1999 that is frequently referenced for epidemiology research. We examined 16,331 self‐identified males and females (age 19 and older) in the 2001–2020 NHANES cohorts with 1‐year weight loss and response to the UI questionnaire. Those who were pregnant at the time of response were excluded. Additionally, those with a 1 year weight loss greater than 100 pounds were removed due to likely reporting error or confounding factors.

Definitions

UUI was defined as a positive response to “during the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine with an urge or pressure to urinate and you couldn't get to the toilet fast enough?”, with severity quantified by a follow‐up question inquiring “how frequently does this occur? (a few times daily, a few times a week, a few times a month, or less than once a month)”. SUI was defined as a positive response to “during the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine with an activity like coughing, lifting, or exercise”, with severity quantified in an identical follow‐up question to UUI. Lastly, MUI was defined as positive response to both the UUI and SUI questions, with the highest symptom frequency of the two serving as the MUI frequency. The use of these questions in classifying UI type has been established in previous studies [16].

At the time of survey, self‐reported current weight and weight from 1 year prior is obtained from respondents. We quantified weight loss using the difference between these two values. Analysis was performed in terms of percent weight loss to normalize values across the wide range of respondent heights. Our body mass index (BMI) calculation utilized the weight from 1 year prior to represent pre‐intervention values. In those who lost weight, intentionality was determined by response to either of two questions: “Was the change between your current weight and your weight a year ago intentional?” or “During the past 12 months, have you tried to lose weight?”. Unintentional weight loss was those who reported weight loss and responded negatively to those two questions.

Weight loss strategies (exercise, dietary changes, intermittent fasting, and diet pills) were determined by questions inquiring about strategies used by respondents in an attempt to lose weight over the past year. Only those with successful reported weight loss and who classified as overweight or obese via BMI were included. Multiple strategies could be reported by respondents. Those who practiced multiple strategies were included in each respective strategy cohort they reported. Strategies were then analyzed as an aggregate and stratified by percent weight loss or sex. Dietary change was defined as a positive response to reduced intake of fat, carbohydrates, total food, sugar/sweets, or fast food; consumption of diet foods/products; switch to lower‐calorie foods; or increased ingestion of fruits, vegetables, or salads expressly for weight loss (NHANES codes: WHD080A, WHD080B, WHD080C, WHD080F, WHD080N, WHD080O, WHD080Q, WHD080R, WHD080S, or WHD080T on NHANES). Intermittent fasting was defined as a positive response to skipping meals to lose weight (WHD080E). Use of both nonprescription (WHD100J) and prescription diet pills (WHD080I/WHD100I), including glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs), was included in our diet pill subset. Lastly, the exercise group was composed of individuals who reported exercising (WHD080D) with the intention of losing weight.

Outcomes

Our objective was to quantify the symptom frequency of UUI, SUI, and MUI in the context of weight loss. We examined whether intentional versus unintentional weight loss was differentially associated with urinary incontinence. Secondary analysis of intentional weight loss strategies aimed to determine if any differences in effectiveness existed for males and females.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Categorical variables were represented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using a Shapiro‐Wilks test. Differences between groups of categorical variables were compared using Chi‐Square tests.

To assess the association between different predictors and UUI, SUI, and MUI severity, univariable and multivariable ordinal logistic regression were performed. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, gender, race/ethnicity, prior BMI, and comorbidities (coronary artery disease [CAD], diabetes, and cancer) to account for the differences between these groups. Results were reported as Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS v9.4. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Of the 16,331 individuals with 1‐year weight loss, 7816 (47.9%) and 8515 (52.1%) represent males and females, respectively. More females (n = 5192) than males (n = 4389) underwent intentional weight loss. 9581 (58.7%) individuals indicated weight loss was done intentionally and 6750 (41.3%) did so unintentionally. Subsequent questioning determined 8818 individuals implemented dietary changes, 5714 exercised, 1584 practiced intermittent fasting, and 238 used diet pills with successful weight loss.

Of all examined individuals, 3584 (21.9%) were classified with SUI and 3772 (23.1%) with UUI; both exhibited a greater distribution of respondents reporting a fewer number of leakage episodes (few times a month or less) than higher numbers of occurrences (few times a week or daily). However, in 1732 (10.6%) individuals who reported MUI, there was a less pronounced difference in distribution across the frequency spectrum.

Increased age was observed to be associated with higher likelihood of frequent SUI (1.02 OR [1.01, 1.02]), UUI (1.03 OR [95% CI 1.02–1.04]), and MUI (1.03 OR [95% CI 1.02–1.03]) symptoms. Female sex was also observed to be associated with increased odds of severe symptoms in UUI (1.74 [95% CI 1.5–2.05]) and MUI (1.51 OR [95% CI 1.14–2.00]). Race had a limited association with UI symptomatology. Non‐Hispanic Blacks had lower likelihood of severe SUI symptoms (0.76 OR [95% CI 0.62–0.92]) when compared to Non‐Hispanic Whites (Supplemental Tables).

Associations With Weight Loss

Weight Loss and Incontinence

BMI–prior to 1‐year weight loss−was closely associated with UI symptom severity. Even post‐weight loss, originally overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2 BMI) individuals were associated with higher SUI (1.38 OR [95% CI 1.15–1.67]) and MUI (1.42 OR [95% CI 1.08–1.87]) symptom frequency. Obese BMI (30+ kg/m2) individuals were linked with more frequent SUI (1.50 OR [95% CI 1.24‐1.81]), UUI (1.34 OR [95% CI 1.10–1.63]), and MUI (1.75 OR [95% CI 1.32–2.31]) symptoms (Supplemental Tables).

Compared to unintentional weight loss, intentional weight loss was associated with a lower proportion of daily and weekly UI symptoms (Table 1). However, a higher proportion of those with intentional weight loss had monthly or yearly SUI, UUI, and MUI episodes. When accounting for age, gender, race/ethnicity, prior BMI, and comorbidities, intentional weight loss was associated with less frequent SUI (0.79 OR [0.68, 0.92]), UUI (0.85 OR [95% CI 0.73–0.99]), and MUI (0.79 OR [95% CI 0.64–0.98]) symptoms compared to unintentional weight loss. Given the observed efficacy of active interventions in symptom severity, comparison of specific strategies is warranted.

Weight Loss Strategy Effectiveness

Strategies and Weight Change

Among males, median percent weight loss was 6.6 (IQR 4.1–10.3) for intentional dietary changes, 6.3 (IQR 4.0–10.0) for exercise, 7.0 (IQR 4.3–10.9) for intermittent fasting, and 10.0 (IQR 6.4–15.5) for diet pill use. Among females, median percent weight loss was 7.1 (IQR 4.4–11.2) for intentional dietary changes, 7.1 (IQR 4.3–11.3) for exercise, 8.0 (IQR 4.9–12.2) for intermittent fasting, and 10.1 (IQR 6.5–14.6) for diet pill use. Because many respondents reported use of multiple strategies, statistical comparison of weight loss between strategies was not performed (Table 2).

Discussion

Summary of Findings

UI prevalence has been well studied with Non‐hispanic whites, older individuals, and higher BMI being associated with the highest prevalence [17, 18]. Study of symptom frequency has been limited. Consistent with previous findings, older age, female sex, and higher BMI were associated with increased occurence of UI symptoms [18]. The lower incidence of frequent SUI symptoms in Non‐hispanic Blacks has also been observed [19].

Weight Loss and Incontinence Severity

Improvements in UI episode frequency with a behavioral weight loss program have previously been demonstrated [10]. However, participant behavior was highly controlled, pelvic floor therapy was encouraged, and no comparable weight loss was observed in the control group. It thus did not reflect real world weight loss regimens and did not clarify if active intervention offered any difference in UI symptoms from passive weight loss.

Passive weight loss is not goal‐directed and can be the result of nonpathologic changes in behavior (increased activity at work, changes in eating patterns, etc.) or illness.

In our study, we observed a shift toward lower UUI, SUI, and MUI symptom frequency associated with intentional weight loss. Thus, it appears real‐world, goal‐directed intervention is associated with lower UI symptom burden compared to passive changes. This invites the question of if specific strategies are associated with UI symptom differences or if being goal directed alone accounts for most differences.

Weight Loss Interventions and Incontinence Severity

Exercise was associated with the lowest rates of SUI, UUI, and MUI across all symptom frequencies when compared to other methods of weight loss. Whereas other studies have primarily focused on female UI and weight loss, our study demonstrated lower UI symptom frequency in both males and females who achieved weight loss with exercise. Additionally, even exercisers with 5% or less weight loss had substantially lower rates of frequent UUI symptoms. Given differences were observed at very low levels of weight loss, exercise may be linked to particularly strong mechanisms tied to UUI.

Exercise with weight loss greater than 5% was associated with relatively lower rates of daily SUI and MUI symptoms. This suggests a less pronounced association between exercise and SUI or MUI. Nonetheless, exercise remained significantly associated with less SUI and MUI symptom frequency for those with greater than 5% weight loss.

The link between exercise and UI symptoms may be related to strengthening of the pelvic floor and metabolic changes. Pathophysiologic links between metabolic syndrome and OAB have been previously established, and exercise is strongly associated with metabolic benefits [20, 21, 22].

It is important to caveat that associations between exercise and reduced SUI severity could be secondary to deliberate lifestyle changes from activity‐induced urinary leakage. We cannot rule out the possibility that fewer individuals with more severe SUI would pursue exercise as a method of weight loss. However, most exercise would exacerbate SUI symptoms. It is notable that rates of high SUI symptom frequency (e.g. few times weekly and daily) with exercise is lower than other interventions despite this. Thus, there could also be further benefits to SUI in everyday life missed due to additional reporting of exercise‐induced leakages.

Notably, diet pill use was associated with more frequent UI symptoms relative to other strategies despite its level of weight loss. We believe this is likely due to some over‐the‐counter diet pills containing caffeine or diuretics, which may exacerbate UI symptoms.

Limitations

It is important to note that because NHANES is a cross‐sectional survey, conclusions about causality cannot be made. Additionally, NHANES relies on self‐reported information from voluntary participants, introducing possible bias. UI symptom frequency and 1‐year weight changes may not be precise.

UI severity was defined solely by self‐reported leakage frequency and not a validated questionnaire (ICIQ, UDI‐6, PFDI‐20), bladder diaries, or objective measures. However, symptom frequency is clinically significant in ascertaining UI severity.

Many respondents practiced multiple weight loss strategies. Thus, results should be interpreted as associations with strategy participation rather than mutually exclusive comparisons.

In analyzing intentionality, not all possible comorbidities could be captured. Additionally, frailty and illness could not be fully accounted for. Menopause also could not be captured and represents a factor for future study.

Definition of dietary changes broadly included many distinct changes in meal composition. The heterogeneity of the inclusion criteria may obscure the effects of specific modifications. Similar limitations apply to specific workouts performed by exercisers.

Information on the exact diet pills used was ambiguous in survey data. A portion (n = 64) were confirmed to be GLP‐1 RAs in separate questionnaire sections; however, the majority were not able to be identified.

Conclusion

Intentional weight loss, with its goal‐directed interventions, was associated with less frequent UI symptoms compared to unintentional weight loss. Our findings are consistent with guideline recommendations for active behavioral inteventions for UI weight loss. Given the emphasis on behavioral modifications for UI, it is important to understand the comparative efficacy of different interventions to better guide patient counseling. In our study, dietary changes, exercise, intermittent fasting, and diet pill use were examined. Exercise was associated with the lowest relative prevalence of severe UUI, SUI, and MUI symptoms, even at modest weight loss (≤ 5%) for UUI. Notably, the association between exercise and lower UI symptoms was demonstrated in both males and females. It appears that the specific method in which weight loss is achieved may be linked to differences in UI symptoms for both sexes. Clinically, it may thus be relevant to identify which goal‐directed strategies are used for weight loss in patients with UI. Prospective comparative study on exercise and other weight loss strategies should be performed in males and females to help establish optimal weight loss regimens for UI patients.
