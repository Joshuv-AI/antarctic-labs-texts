Non-pharmacological nursing interventions in patients with myocardial infarction: a systematic review
Authors: Yunjing Du, Qi Wang, Yueqin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple interventions have been implemented with varying degrees of effectiveness. A comprehensive analysis is necessary to evaluate and characterize the efficacy of each intervention. This study aimed to examine the impact of non-pharmacological nursing interventions on patients with myocardial infarction.MethodsA literature search was conducted across five databases: Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and MEDLINE. Each database was last searched on May 13, 2024. The PICO (population, intervention, comparator, and outcome) approach was used to create a comprehensive and well-structured research question. The quality of the studies included was assessed using the critical appraisal tools from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI).ResultsThis review included 52 studies. A total of eighteen distinct non-pharmacological interventions were assessed to evaluate their efficacy in improving outcomes for patients with myocardial infarction. The findings of this review highlight that various types of nursing interventions can, to some extent, impact patient outcomes. Regarding intervention types, telephone follow-up/remote management were used in 8 studies; psychological interventions in 8 studies; nursing care pathways in 8 studies; rehabilitation nursing care in 4 studies; home-care nursing in 4 studies; exercise nursing care and evidence-based nursing in 3 studies each; nurse-led individualized self-management program, educational interventions, and targeted nursing care in 2 studies each; and structured team nursing model, whole-course nursing care, acupressure, spiritual nursing care, systematic nursing care, high-quality nursing, ice bag application, and music intervention were each reported in 1 study. Regarding outcome types, death/complications/readmission were investigated in 16 studies; quality of life (QOL) in 12 studies; cardiac function in 14 studies; anxiety/depression/stress in 6 studies; adherence in 2 studies; and pain in 2 studies. The studies were predominantly limited in scale and demonstrated substantial variability in the types of interventions implemented as well as in the outcomes assessed. Furthermore, most of the included studies were rated as moderate to low quality.ConclusionsThe findings of this review highlight that non-pharmacological nursing interventions can, to some extent, impact patient outcomes. However, due to the methodological constraints, the results should be interpreted with prudence. Further investigation employing larger sample sizes and improved methodological rigor is necessary to more conclusively ascertain the effectiveness of these nursing interventions.Keywords: Non-pharmacological interventions, myocardial infarction (MI), quality of life (QOL), cardiac rehabilitation (CR), cardiac function

Highlight box

Key findings

• The findings of this review highlight that various types of nursing interventions can, to some extent, impact patient outcomes.

What is known and what is new?

• Non-pharmacological nursing interventions are widely used in patients with myocardial infarction (MI) to support recovery and improve patient outcomes.

• A variety of nursing interventions have been implemented for patients with MI; however, their effectiveness varies across intervention types and outcomes, and the overall evidence remains heterogeneous.

• This systematic review included 52 studies evaluating 18 types of non-pharmacological nursing interventions and summarized their effects across a range of clinical and patient-reported outcomes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Non-pharmacological nursing interventions may improve a range of outcomes among patients with MI, including cardiac function, quality of life, and other clinical and psychological outcomes. However, the available evidence is limited by substantial variability in interventions and outcomes, relatively small sample sizes, and predominantly moderate-to-low methodological quality. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with larger sample sizes, standardized interventions, and improved methodological rigor are needed to more conclusively determine the effectiveness of these nursing interventions and guide evidence-based clinical practice.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are a group of diseases that include heart attack or stroke, congestive heart failure, arrhythmia (heart rhythm disorders), and heart valve diseases. In general, 30% of all deaths worldwide are due to cardiovascular diseases (1-3). In the United States, 1 out of every 42 people (2.35%) has cardiovascular disease (4). Multiple factors can be involved in the development of cardiovascular diseases (5-7).

Myocardial infarction (MI) is an injury to a part of the heart muscle due to blockage in the coronary arteries and the loss of blood flow in that area. This ultimately results in impaired oxygenation to the affected area and the death of cells in that part of the heart. MI is the most prevalent cardiovascular disease and is the second most common disease in developed and developing countries (8,9). Changes in lifestyle and increased stress have been identified as the most important causes of this disease. In addition to social, economic, and medical consequences, MI is also associated with adverse psychological outcomes including anxiety, stress, and depression (9,10).

In recent years, interest in the evaluation and improvement of the quality of life (QOL) of patients with chronic diseases has grown substantially, and strengthening these patients’ ability to perform daily activities—and thus their QOL—has become a clinical objective (11,12). QOL is defined as a feeling of well-being that comes from satisfaction or dissatisfaction with different aspects of life that are important to a person (13). It includes the health, work, economic, social, psychological, and familial domains. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), QOL is a multidimensional concept that represents individuals’ understanding of life, principles, aims, standards, and interests. There is an association between disease and QOL, as physical disorders and symptoms directly impact all dimensions of the latter (14-16). Therefore, QOL is one of the most significant concepts in the management of patients with acute diseases (including MI), which are detrimental to the physical, mental, and social functioning of patients and their overall QOL (9,17).

Interventions are implemented to reduce morbidity and mortality, improve QOL, decrease social and medical costs, enhance economic productivity, facilitate the patient’s treatment process (17,18). Interventions involve treatments, actions, and educational sessions aimed at caring for patients and improving their well-being (9,19). Various interventions, including educational interventions, comprehensive interventions, and evidence-based nursing (EBN) are used to differing levels of effectiveness (9,18).

Numerous studies have examined a wide variety of interventions for patients with MI, but the outcomes regarding their efficacy on patients’ physical and psychological well-being vary.

Several reviews have previously examined nursing-related interventions in cardiovascular populations. Prvu Bettger et al. (20) reported that transitional care interventions following hospitalization were associated with improvements in care continuity and some patient outcomes; however, the evidence was inconsistent and included both stroke and MI populations. Allen et al. (21) found that nursing interventions for secondary prevention could improve risk-factor management and health behaviors among patients with coronary artery disease and heart failure, although the interventions and outcome measures varied considerably across studies. Thisawalangu et al. (22) concluded that nursing interventions may enhance recovery after MI, but the review was descriptive in nature and was limited to only ten studies. Gok Metin et al. (23) demonstrated that theory-based nursing interventions were associated with improvements in self-care behaviors and selected clinical outcomes among patients with coronary heart disease; however, the evidence was derived from only eight randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Similarly, Lawler et al. (24) found that exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation (CR) after MI reduced cardiovascular risk and improved functional outcomes, but the review focused exclusively on exercise interventions.

Although these reviews provided important insights, they primarily evaluated specific intervention categories, such as transitional care, theory-based interventions, secondary prevention programs, or exercise-based rehabilitation. Furthermore, several reviews included broader cardiovascular populations rather than focusing specifically on patients with MI, and most synthesized evidence published more than a decade ago. Consequently, the overall effectiveness of the full spectrum of non-pharmacological nursing interventions for MI remains unclear. The present systematic review addresses these gaps by providing an updated and comprehensive synthesis of diverse non-pharmacological nursing interventions and their effects on multiple patient outcomes in individuals with MI.

Therefore, a comprehensive analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of non-pharmacological intervention. Specifically, a systematic review was performed to summarize the research findings and to determine the impact of non-pharmacological on patients with MI. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://cdt.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/cdt-2025-493/rc).

Methods

This study was registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under the registration number CRD42024620842. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Figure 1.

Eligibility criteria

All study designs, including trials, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and case-control studies involving interventions for patients MI, were deemed eligible for inclusion. If the full text was unavailable for any of the included studies, these were excluded from the review.

Search strategy

Five databases were searched, including Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and MEDLINE, as of May 13, 2024. The keyword search strategy was as follows: “intervention*” AND “myocardial infarct*” OR “cardiovascular stroke” OR “heart attack” OR “myocardial ischemia” OR “cardiogenic shock” OR “congestive heart failure” OR “heart arrest” OR “MI”. The comprehensive search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix 1.

Selection process

Two researchers (Y.D. and Q.W.) independently conducted a screening of the titles and abstracts of all articles, and those identified as irrelevant by both reviewers were excluded. Based on predefined eligibility criteria, full-text versions of potentially relevant articles were obtained and assessed for inclusion by the same two investigators. Complete concordance was observed between the investigators regarding study selection, rendering the involvement of a third reviewer (Y.S.), initially designated for resolving uncertainties or disagreements.

Data collection process

Initially, unrelated studies—those not specifically conducted on patients with MI—and study protocols were excluded, and two reviewers (Y.D. and Q.W.) independently examined all full-text articles, extracting data according to predefined criteria. Relevant information was subsequently retrieved from the selected studies and systematically organized by the same investigators. The extracted information included the first author, publication year, country, study type, sample size, gender, age, disease type, intervention type, intervention duration, outcome measures, service providers, and findings.

PICO (population, intervention, comparator, and outcome) statement

The PICO approach was designed to create a comprehensive and well-organized research question. All articles that did not meet the PICO requirements were excluded (Table 1). In this review, interventions were defined as any actions undertaken by nurses or nursing staff aimed at helping patients achieve the desired health outcomes.

Quality assessment

The quality of the investigated studies (all study deigns) was assessed with the critical appraisal tools developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). We used the RCTs checklist for RCTs containing 13 questions, the quasi-experimental studies checklist for non-RCTs comprising 9 questions, the cohort studies checklist for cohort studies including 11 questions, the case-control studies checklist for case-control studies containing 10 questions, and the cross-sectional studies checklist for cross-sectional studies with 8 questions (27). The critical appraisal instruments developed by JBI facilitate the evaluation of the credibility, applicability, and findings of published studies. We did not have access to the original data and therefore relied on the definitions used by the authors of the studies. Studies that used all clinical information, including imaging, to define MI or a comprehensive International Classification of Diseases (ICD) definition (ICD-10 codes 121–123.8) were considered at low risk of selection bias. A diagnosis of MI required two of the following criteria to be satisfied: the presence of ischemic symptoms, new alterations in the ST segment or the occurrence of a left bundle branch block on the electrocardiogram (ECG), and the identification of pathological Q waves on the ECG. Studies that used a less comprehensive definition were considered have a high risk of selection bias. Regarding exposure assessment, it was challenging to evaluate the accuracy of exposure measurement because of variable reporting in the original studies. However, the frequency of measurements (whether daily or intermittent monitoring) was determined with a high level of accuracy, and studies that employed daily measurements were considered at low risk of bias. Concerning adjustment for confounders, studies were deemed at moderate risk of bias if they included age in the adjustment parameters. Studies were considered to be at low risk of bias if they included sex in the adjustment parameters. Studies were categorized as high risk of bias if no adjustment factors were mentioned. In these checklists, the total number of affirmative responses was aggregated, and based on this sum, the articles were classified into three categories: low quality (scores of 1 to 2 out of 9), moderate quality (scores ranging from 3 to 6 out of 9), and high quality (scores between 7 and 9).

Data synthesis

Characteristics of the included studies were systematically described, including aspects such as study populations, geographic locations, interventions, and methodological quality. Taking this into consideration, two researchers (Y.D. and Q.W.) conducted data analysis, calculating the agreement ratio between the outcomes via the Cohen kappa (κ) coefficient, which is a statistical measure employed to assess interrater reliability. It is widely regarded as a more robust metric than is straightforward calculation of percent agreement, as κ accounts for the likelihood of agreement arising by chance (28,29). This coefficient was found to be 0.89 in our review, which was acceptable.

Results

Search results and study selection

In the search stage, 3,127 studies were found, of which 418 studies were duplicate articles and excluded. Two investigators (Y.D. and Q.W.) subsequently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining 2,649 studies. Of these studies, 2,566 papers did not meet the criteria for inclusion and exclusion, 15 studies were excluded because they were not conducted only on patients with MI, and 16 studies were protocols. Ultimately, 52 studies were included in the current investigation for further analysis.

Study characteristics

The comprehensive information of the extracted papers is presented in Table S1. Of the selected 52 studies, 28 were conducted in China, 7 in Canada, 5 in Iran, 4 in the United States, 2 in Turkey, and 1 in each of Spain, Pakistan, the United Kingdom, Singapore, the Netherlands, South Korea, and Germany (Figure S1).

Forty-nine studies were conducted on both males and females, with one paper not reporting the gender of the participants, and only two studies specifically focusing on males. Regarding intervention types, telephone follow-up/remote management were used in 8 studies; psychological interventions in 8 studies; nursing care pathways in 7 studies; rehabilitation nursing care in 5 studies; home-care nursing in 4 studies; exercise nursing care and EBN in 3 studies each; nurse-led individualized self-management program, educational interventions, and targeted nursing care in 2 studies each; and structured team nursing model, whole-course nursing care, acupressure, spiritual nursing care, systematic nursing care, high-quality nursing, ice bag application, and music intervention were each reported in 1 study.

Regarding outcome types, death/complications/readmission were investigated in 16 studies; QOL in 12 studies; cardiac function in 14 studies; anxiety/depression/stress in 6 studies; adherence in 2 studies; and pain in 2 studies (Table S1). Death/complications/readmission was assessed using medical history and hospital records. QOL was evaluated using coronary revascularization outcome questionnaire (CROQ), 36-item short form health survey (SF-36), questionnaire of quality of life in patients with cardiovascular diseases (CQQC), generic quality of life inventory-74 (GQOLI-74), EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L), and Seattle coronary heart disease patients’ quality of life evaluation scale [Seattle angina questionnaire (SAQ)]. Cardiac function was evaluated using echocardiography, EPIQ7 color Doppler ultrasonography, SAQ, coronary artery disease self-management scale (CSMS), index of microcirculation resistance (IMR), and myocardial infarction dimensional assessment scale (MIDAS). Anxiety/depression/stress was assessed using generalized anxiety disorder (GAD-7) questionnaire, patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9), Hamilton anxiety scale (HAMA), Hamilton depression scale (HAMD), self-rating anxiety scale (SAS), self-rating depression scale (SDS), Beck’s depression inventory, and the Spielberger state anxiety inventory. Adherence was evaluated using Morisky-Green and Hayness-Sacket scales and treatment adherence questionnaire. Pain was assessed using numerical rating scale (NRS) and visual analogue scale (VAS). In the included studies, various quality-of-life domains were evaluated, including symptoms, physical function, cognitive function, psychosocial function, adverse reaction control, role physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. Fatality rates, unplanned readmissions, and hospitalization rates were also assessed. Various complications were evaluated, including arrhythmia, thrombocytopenia, angina pectoris, venous thrombosis, vagus reflex, hypotension, nausea and vomiting, local bleeding, urinary retention, heart arrhythmia, infection, cardiogenic shock, heart failure, recurrent coronary stenosis, and myocardial reinfarction. Additionally, cardiac function indices were assessed, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD).

Regarding study design, there were 23 trials, 22 Case-control studies, 6 cohort studies, and 1 cross-sectional study. The duration of interventions ranged from 6 hours to 2 years. The results of all studies, except for four (which focused on multifactorial CR, early hospital discharge approach, stressor perception and response targeted nursing, and nurse-based case management interventions), showed that different types of interventions could have a positive impact on patients’ improvement and recovery.

Telephone follow-up/remote management (n=8 studies)

Eight studies evaluated telephone follow-up, telecardiology, remote management, or digital health-based nursing interventions among patients with MI (16,30-36). Overall, these interventions demonstrated beneficial effects on several clinical, behavioral, and psychosocial outcomes, although the findings were not entirely consistent.

Several studies reported improvements in clinical outcomes. Telecardiology and remote early intervention models were associated with shorter treatment delays, including reduced first medical contact-to-balloon time and shorter time to primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) (30,36). Remote family rehabilitation and WeChat-based continuous nursing interventions were associated with lower rates of heart failure, unstable angina, unplanned readmissions, and post-discharge complications (16,31).

Positive effects on CR and self-management were observed in multiple studies. Patients receiving remote follow-up interventions demonstrated improved adherence to prescribed exercise programs, medications, and lifestyle modifications, as well as better performance on the 6-minute walk test and greater participation in CR programs (16,33,35). Educational and behavioral support interventions also enhanced self-efficacy, coping ability, and adaptive health behaviors, particularly with respect to nutrition and physical activity (35).

QOL and psychological outcomes were among the most consistently improved measures. Several studies reported significant improvements in health-related QOL, patient satisfaction, and psychological well-being, including reductions in anxiety and depression symptoms following remote nursing interventions (16,31,35).

However, not all outcomes demonstrated significant benefits. Some studies found no significant differences between intervention and control groups in mortality, rehospitalization rates, medication adherence, left ventricular remodeling, cardiac function measures, smoking cessation, or QOL (32,34). Similarly, certain cardiac indicators, including LVEF and selected biomarkers of infarct size, were not consistently improved across studies (30,32).

Overall, the evidence suggests that telephone follow-up and remote management interventions may enhance treatment timeliness, self-management behaviors, rehabilitation participation, psychological well-being, and selected clinical outcomes in patients with MI (16,30,31,33,35,36). Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of intervention components and outcome measures, combined with inconsistent findings across studies (32,34), limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding their overall effectiveness.

Psychological interventions (n=8 studies)

Eight studies evaluated psychological interventions for patients with MI, including comprehensive psychological support, cognitive intervention combined with psychological flexibility training, stressor perception and response-focused nursing, acceptance and commitment therapy, family visit nursing, psychosocial intervention programs, stress reduction interventions, and life stress monitoring programs (9,37-43). Overall, psychological interventions were associated with improvements in psychological well-being, self-management abilities, QOL, and, in some cases, clinical outcomes.

The most consistent benefits were observed in psychological outcomes. Several studies reported significant reductions in anxiety, depression, psychological distress, negative emotions, and stress levels following psychological interventions (9,37,41-43). Improvements in sleep quality were also reported, including reductions in sleep latency and overall Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) scores (38).

Psychological interventions also demonstrated positive effects on patients’ adaptive capacities and self-management. Studies found significant improvements in psychological resilience, coping strategies, self-care ability, self-management skills, and adaptive health behaviors among patients receiving these interventions (37,39). These findings suggest that psychological nursing approaches may facilitate patients’ adjustment to illness and promote active engagement in recovery.

Several studies reported improvements in QOL and patient-centered outcomes. Comprehensive psychological interventions, family visit nursing, and psychosocial support programs were associated with enhanced QOL, greater patient satisfaction, and improved overall well-being following MI (9,37,40,41).

In addition to psychological benefits, some studies reported favorable clinical outcomes. Stress reduction and life stress monitoring interventions were associated with lower mortality rates, while psychosocial interventions may contribute to improved long-term prognosis among patients recovering from MI (41-43). Family visit nursing was also found to improve sedation levels and reduce both the incidence and duration of delirium in patients undergoing PCI after acute myocardial infarction (AMI) (40).

Overall, the evidence suggests that psychological interventions can positively influence emotional well-being, stress management, self-care behaviors, QOL, and selected clinical outcomes in patients with MI (9,37-43). Although the findings were generally favorable, the diversity of intervention approaches and outcome measures limits direct comparisons across studies and warrants cautious interpretation of the overall effectiveness of these interventions.

Nursing care pathway (n=8 studies)

Seven studies evaluated nursing care pathways and process-based nursing interventions for patients with MI, including standardized clinical pathways, clinical nursing pathways, critical care pathways, optimized emergency nursing protocols, CR pathways, integrated emergency care processes, and multifaceted intervention bundles (44-51). Overall, these interventions were associated with improvements in care efficiency, treatment timeliness, clinical outcomes, and patient satisfaction, although some findings regarding healthcare utilization were mixed.

Several studies demonstrated that nursing care pathways improved the efficiency of healthcare delivery. Significant reductions in hospital length of stay were reported following the implementation of standardized clinical pathways and critical care pathways (44,46). Optimized emergency nursing protocols and integrated emergency care processes were also associated with shorter rescue times, reduced ECG and PCI initiation times, and more rapid delivery of acute care services (47,49). Similarly, clinical nursing pathways contributed to shorter door-to-balloon times and reduced hospitalization duration among patients with AMI (45,51).

Positive effects on clinical outcomes were observed across several studies. Nursing pathway interventions were associated with lower rates of postoperative complications, recurrent MI, adverse reactions, and disease recurrence (45-47,49). Furthermore, integrated emergency care processes were reported to improve rescue success rates while reducing mortality and complication rates among patients with AMI (49).

Patient-centered outcomes also improved following pathway-based interventions. Studies reported higher levels of nursing satisfaction and greater participation in CR programs among patients managed through structured nursing pathways (45,48). In addition, optimized emergency nursing protocols were associated with reduced negative emotional responses during treatment (47).

Evidence regarding healthcare resource utilization was less consistent. While several studies reported reductions in hospitalization duration and costs (44-46), one study found that a multifaceted intervention bundle increased the use of serial cardiac troponin testing and was associated with a longer emergency department length of stay (50).

Overall, the evidence suggests that nursing care pathways can improve the organization and delivery of care for patients with MI, resulting in more timely treatment, enhanced clinical outcomes, and greater patient satisfaction (44-49). However, the impact on healthcare resource utilization remains variable across studies, highlighting the need for further evaluation of pathway-based interventions in diverse clinical settings (50).

Rehabilitation nursing care (n=4 studies)

Four studies evaluated rehabilitation nursing interventions for patients with MI, including early rehabilitation nursing, early CR nursing, multifactorial CR, and a seven-step rehabilitation training program (52-55). Overall, rehabilitation nursing interventions were associated with improvements in cardiac function, functional recovery, psychological well-being, and QOL, although one study reported no significant benefits.

Improvements in cardiac function were reported in several studies. Rehabilitation nursing interventions were associated with increased cardiac output, improved LVEF, and enhanced overall cardiac function compared with conventional care (52,53,55). In addition, early rehabilitation nursing was associated with reductions in inflammatory markers, including lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), suggesting potential benefits in mitigating post-infarction inflammatory responses (52).

Several studies demonstrated positive effects on functional recovery and self-management. Patients receiving rehabilitation nursing showed better performance in activities of daily living, higher Barthel Index scores, and greater self-care capacity than those receiving routine care (53,55). Rehabilitation interventions were also associated with shorter hospitalization durations and enhanced recovery following PCI for AMI (55).

QOL and psychological outcomes generally improved following rehabilitation nursing care. Significant improvements were observed in health-related QOL, while reductions in anxiety, depression, and pain scores were reported among patients receiving rehabilitation interventions (52,53,55). Furthermore, higher levels of nursing satisfaction were observed in patients undergoing early CR nursing (53).

Favorable clinical outcomes were also reported in some studies. Rehabilitation nursing interventions were associated with lower complication rates and reduced incidences of arrhythmia and angina pectoris during follow-up (53,55). However, one study evaluating multifactorial CR found no significant differences between the intervention and usual care groups regarding mortality, clinical outcomes, health-related QOL, or psychological well-being (54).

Overall, the available evidence suggests that rehabilitation nursing interventions may improve cardiac function, functional recovery, QOL, and selected clinical outcomes in patients recovering from MI (52,53,55). Nevertheless, the presence of inconsistent findings across studies indicates that further high-quality research is required to establish the long-term effectiveness of rehabilitation nursing programs (54).

Home-care nursing (n=4 studies)

Four studies evaluated home-care nursing interventions for patients recovering from MI, including home visits based on the Orem self-care model, home-based psychosocial nursing programs, and interventions designed to enhance self-efficacy among older adults (56-59). Overall, home-care nursing interventions demonstrated positive effects on self-care behaviors, psychological well-being, and recovery processes, although evidence regarding clinical outcomes was limited.

Several studies reported improvements in self-care and health-related behaviors. Home visits grounded in the Orem self-care model were associated with enhanced self-care agency and improved adherence to healthy low-fat dietary behaviors among patients recovering from MI (56). Interventions aimed at strengthening self-efficacy also appeared to facilitate recovery among older patients, suggesting potential benefits for post-discharge adaptation and disease management (59).

Psychological outcomes were among the most consistently improved measures. Home-based psychosocial nursing interventions were found to alleviate psychological distress and reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression in patients recovering from MI (57,58). These findings suggest that home-care nursing may provide valuable emotional support during the recovery period and promote psychological adjustment following a cardiac event.

However, the evidence for clinical outcomes was less conclusive. Although psychosocial home-care interventions improved emotional well-being, one RCT found no significant effect on overall survival despite modest reductions in anxiety and depression symptoms (58). Similarly, improvements in self-care agency did not consistently translate into enhanced self-efficacy across all behavioral domains, such as healthy eating behaviors (56).

Overall, the available evidence suggests that home-care nursing interventions may enhance self-care behaviors, psychological well-being, and post-discharge recovery among patients with MI (56-59). Nevertheless, the limited number of studies and the lack of consistent evidence regarding clinical outcomes highlight the need for further research to determine the long-term effectiveness of home-care nursing programs.

Exercise nursing care (n=3 studies)

Three studies evaluated exercise-based nursing interventions for patients with MI, including structured rehabilitation exercises, theory-guided rehabilitation exercise nursing, and walking aerobic exercise programs (60-62). Overall, exercise nursing interventions were associated with improvements in functional outcomes, QOL, and selected clinical indicators.

Improvements in QOL were consistently reported across studies. Structured rehabilitation exercise programs and walking aerobic exercise interventions were associated with significant enhancements in overall health-related QOL, including improvements in symptom severity, physical functioning, cognitive functioning, and psychosocial functioning compared with routine care (60,62).

Functional and physiological benefits were also observed. Rehabilitation exercise nursing based on a cross-theoretical model was associated with favorable changes in myocardial tissue characteristics, including increased fractional anisotropy and reduced mean diffusivity in infarcted regions, suggesting potential improvements in myocardial structural integrity following intervention (61).

In addition, exercise-based interventions demonstrated potential benefits in reducing adverse clinical outcomes. A walking aerobic exercise program was associated with a lower incidence of cardiovascular adverse events compared with standard care, indicating possible protective effects on post-infarction recovery (62).

Overall, the evidence suggests that exercise nursing interventions may improve QOL, support functional recovery, and reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients recovering from MI (60-62). However, the limited number of studies and heterogeneity in exercise protocols and outcome measures indicate that further well-designed research is needed to confirm these findings.

EBN (n=3 studies)

Three studies evaluated EBN interventions in patients with MI, including EBN applied to patients with AMI complicated by heart failure, EBN combined with exercise rehabilitation after PCI, and comprehensive EBN care programs (63-65). Overall, EBN interventions were associated with improvements in cardiac function, psychological outcomes, self-management behaviors, metabolic indicators, and patient satisfaction.

Improvements in cardiac function and biochemical indicators were consistently reported. EBN interventions were associated with increased LVEF and reduced LVEDD, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels compared with routine care (63,64). These findings suggest potential benefits of EBN in supporting myocardial recovery and improving cardiac performance following MI.

Positive effects on functional capacity and physical recovery were also observed. Patients receiving EBN combined with exercise rehabilitation demonstrated significantly longer six-minute walk distances at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively compared with control groups (64), indicating sustained improvements in exercise tolerance and functional status.

Psychological and self-management outcomes improved following EBN interventions. Studies reported significantly lower anxiety and depression scores after nursing care, as well as higher scores in self-management ability, particularly in disease management and lifestyle behaviors (63,64). In addition, improvements in QOL and patient satisfaction were consistently observed across studies (63-65).

EBN interventions also demonstrated favorable effects on metabolic and clinical outcomes. Reductions in blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein levels were reported following combined EBN and rehabilitation programs (64). Furthermore, lower complication rates and shorter hospital stays were observed in patients receiving EBN compared with those receiving conventional care (64,65).

Overall, the evidence suggests that EBN interventions may enhance cardiac function, psychological well-being, functional capacity, metabolic control, and overall recovery in patients with MI (63-65). However, the limited number of studies and variability in intervention components highlight the need for further high-quality research to confirm these findings.

Educational interventions (n=2 studies)

Two studies evaluated educational interventions for patients with MI, including tailored adherence education programs for elderly patients and structured multi-session educational interventions (66,67). Overall, educational interventions were associated with improvements in treatment adherence, although evidence on broader health outcomes was limited.

Both studies consistently reported improvements in medication and treatment adherence. A tailored adherence education program significantly increased long-term therapeutic adherence at 12 months among elderly patients with MI compared with usual care (66). Similarly, structured educational sessions (four to six sessions per patient) resulted in significantly higher adherence to treatment protocols in the intervention group compared with the control group (67).

Overall, the evidence suggests that educational interventions may effectively enhance treatment adherence in patients with MI (66,67). However, due to the small number of studies and limited reporting of broader clinical and psychosocial outcomes, further research is needed to determine their impact on long-term patient health outcomes.

Nurse-led individualized self-management programs (n=2 studies)

Two studies examined nurse-led individualized self-management programs for patients with MI, including patients undergoing PCI and elderly patients receiving case management interventions (68,69). Overall, these interventions showed mixed effects, with improvements in self-management behaviors and risk factor control but inconsistent effects on long-term health outcomes.

One study demonstrated that nurse-led individualized self-management programs significantly improved health behaviors and health-related QOL. Participants showed better control of cardiovascular risk factors, including higher rates of smoking cessation, reductions in low-density lipoprotein levels and body mass index, and increased engagement in physical activity compared with control groups (68).

In contrast, a nurse-led case management intervention in elderly post-MI patients did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) compared with usual care (69), suggesting limited impact on long-term health utility outcomes in this population.

Overall, the evidence indicates that nurse-led individualized self-management programs may improve health behaviors and cardiovascular risk factor control; however, their impact on long-term quality-adjusted outcomes remains uncertain (68,69).

Targeted nursing care (n=2 studies)

Two studies evaluated targeted nursing care interventions for patients with MI, including targeted nursing strategies for emergency AMI patients and invasive blood pressure-guided nursing during PCI (70,71). Overall, targeted nursing care was associated with improvements in microcirculatory function, cardiac structure and function, and a reduction in cardiovascular complications.

Both studies reported favorable effects on cardiac function and hemodynamic outcomes. Targeted nursing interventions were associated with reductions in left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic dimensions and increases in LVEF compared with standard care, indicating improved cardiac remodeling and functional recovery (70,71). In addition, one study reported enhanced coronary reperfusion and reduced myocardial ischemia duration following invasive blood pressure–guided nursing during PCI (71).

Targeted nursing care also demonstrated benefits in microcirculatory and clinical outcomes. One study found a significantly lower incidence of microcirculation disturbances in the intervention group, suggesting improved peripheral and myocardial perfusion (70). Furthermore, both studies reported a reduced incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events and other cardiovascular complications in patients receiving targeted nursing interventions compared with control groups (70,71).

Overall, the evidence suggests that targeted nursing care may improve cardiac function, perfusion dynamics, procedural efficiency, and clinical outcomes in patients with MI (70,71). However, the limited number of studies and variability in intervention components warrant cautious interpretation of these findings.

Structured team nursing model (n=1 study)

One study evaluated a structured team nursing model in patients with MI, focusing on its impact on acute care efficiency, cardiac function, functional outcomes, and clinical safety (72). Overall, the intervention demonstrated broad improvements in treatment efficiency, cardiac performance, patient recovery, and care quality indicators.

The structured team nursing model was associated with significantly improved treatment efficiency. Patients in the intervention group experienced shorter door-to-balloon time, reduced emergency department length of stay, shorter balloon dilation time, and reduced overall hospitalization duration compared with those receiving routine care (72), indicating more efficient coordination of AMI management.

Improvements in cardiac function were also reported. Post-intervention assessments showed reduced LVEDD and LVESD, alongside increased LVEF in the intervention group compared with the control group (72), suggesting enhanced cardiac recovery.

Functional and patient-reported outcomes were similarly improved. The structured team nursing model was associated with better performance in physical function and activities of daily living, improved angina stability, reduced frequency of angina attacks, and enhanced disease awareness and treatment satisfaction as measured by the 6-minute walk test (6-MWT), Modified Barthel Index (MBI), and SAQ (72).

In addition, the incidence of complications was significantly lower in the intervention group, indicating improved clinical safety during and after treatment (72). Nursing staff also reported higher satisfaction with teamwork, procedural organization, clinical practice, and emergency response efficiency under the structured team model (72).

Overall, the available evidence suggests that a structured team nursing model may improve treatment efficiency, cardiac function, functional outcomes, and clinical safety in patients with MI (72). However, as these findings are based on a single study, further research is needed to confirm their generalizability.

Acupressure (n=1 study)

One study evaluated the effect of acupressure on the prevention of constipation in patients with AMI undergoing PCI (73). Overall, the intervention demonstrated a beneficial effect on bowel function compared with both sham and routine care.

On day 3 of the intervention, the proportion of patients with normal defecation was significantly higher in the acupressure group (93.3%) compared with the sham intervention group (46.7%) and the control group (50.0%) (73). The differences among the three groups were statistically significant (P<0.001), indicating a clear positive effect of acupressure on postoperative gastrointestinal function.

Overall, the evidence suggests that acupressure may effectively reduce constipation in patients with AMI undergoing PCI (73). However, given that this finding is based on a single study, further research is required to confirm its effectiveness and generalizability.

Whole-course nursing care (n=1 study)

One study evaluated whole-course nursing care for patients with AMI undergoing PCI (74). Overall, the intervention was associated with improvements in cardiac function, psychological status, QOL, postoperative complications, and patient satisfaction compared with routine care.

Both intervention and control groups showed significant improvements in cardiac function at discharge; however, the magnitude of improvement was greater in the whole-course nursing care group. Specifically, patients receiving the intervention demonstrated higher LVEF and cardiac output, as well as greater reductions in left ventricular end-systolic and end-diastolic diameters compared with controls (74), indicating enhanced cardiac recovery.

Psychological outcomes also improved following intervention. Both groups exhibited significant reductions in anxiety and depression scores (HAMA and HAMD), with more pronounced improvements observed in the whole-course nursing care group (74), suggesting stronger effects on emotional well-being during recovery.

In addition, QOL improved significantly across all domains of the GQOLI-74 scale in both groups, with significantly greater gains in the intervention group (74). The incidence of postoperative complications was also lower among patients receiving whole-course nursing care, accompanied by higher nursing satisfaction compared with routine care (74).

Overall, the evidence suggests that whole-course nursing care may enhance cardiac function, psychological recovery, QOL, and clinical outcomes in patients with MI undergoing PCI (74). However, given that these findings are based on a single study, further high-quality research is needed to confirm their effectiveness and generalizability.

Ice bag application (n=1 study)

One RCT evaluated the effect of ice bag application to the femoral region on pain and vital signs in patients with AMI undergoing PCI (75). Overall, the intervention demonstrated beneficial effects on pain relief and selected physiological parameters compared with standard care.

The ice bag application was associated with significantly lower pain scores during and after the intervention compared with the control group (P<0.001) (75), indicating a clear analgesic effect during femoral catheter removal. In addition, patients in the intervention group showed significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure, body temperature, and respiratory rate during and after the procedure compared with controls (P<0.05), suggesting improved physiological stability (75).

However, no significant differences were observed between groups in mean heart rate across all measured time points (P>0.05) (75), indicating that the intervention did not affect this parameter.

Overall, the evidence suggests that ice bag application to the femoral region may reduce procedural pain and stabilize certain vital signs in patients with MI undergoing PCI (75). Nevertheless, given that these findings are based on a single study, further research is required to confirm its efficacy and safety.

Spiritual nursing care (n=1 study)

One study evaluated spiritual nursing care grounded in the sound heart model in patients with AMI (76). Overall, the intervention demonstrated beneficial effects on spiritual well-being compared with routine care.

The spiritual care program was associated with significant improvements in religious health, existential health, and overall spiritual well-being scores following the intervention (76), indicating enhanced spiritual adjustment and coping capacity during the recovery process after MI.

Overall, the evidence suggests that spiritual nursing care may improve spiritual well-being in patients with AMI (76). However, given that these findings are based on a single study, further research is needed to determine its broader effectiveness and clinical relevance.

Music intervention (n=1 study)

One non-randomized controlled study evaluated the effect of Turkish classical music on physiological parameters, pain, and analgesic consumption in patients with MI (77). Overall, the intervention demonstrated beneficial effects on hemodynamic stability and physiological outcomes compared with standard care.

Music intervention was associated with significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and pulse rate compared with the control group (P<0.001) (77), indicating improved cardiovascular stability during recovery. In addition, oxygen saturation levels were significantly higher in the intervention group, suggesting enhanced respiratory efficiency and overall physiological status (77).

Overall, the evidence suggests that music intervention may improve physiological stability in patients with MI (77). However, as these findings are based on a single non-randomized study, further rigorous research is required to confirm its effectiveness and clinical applicability.

Systematic nursing care (n=1 study)

One RCT evaluated the effect of systematic nursing interventions on CR enrollment among patients following MI (78). Overall, the intervention demonstrated a strong positive effect on participation in CR programs compared with standard or indirect nursing approaches.

The study found that patients receiving direct, face-to-face nursing interventions were significantly more likely to enroll in CR than those receiving indirect interventions (χ2=32.84, P<0.001) (78). In addition, patients who participated in an initial structured entrance interview showed the highest likelihood of CR enrollment (χ2=86.80, P<0.001) (78), indicating that early and proactive nursing engagement plays a key role in improving rehabilitation uptake.

Overall, the evidence suggests that systematic nursing care can significantly enhance CR enrollment among patients with MI (78). However, given that these findings are derived from a single study, further research is required to confirm their generalizability and long-term impact on patient outcomes.

High-quality nursing (n=1 study)

One study evaluated the effect of high-quality nursing care in patients with AMI (79). Overall, the intervention demonstrated beneficial effects on physiological and biological markers compared with routine nursing care.

High-quality nursing was associated with improvements in immune response and endothelial function following the intervention (79), suggesting potential benefits in modulating inflammatory and vascular processes during MI recovery.

Overall, the evidence suggests that high-quality nursing care may positively influence immune and endothelial function in patients with AMI (79). However, these findings are based on a single study, and further research is needed to confirm their clinical relevance and broader applicability.

Discussion

A total of eighteen distinct non-pharmacological interventions were assessed to evaluate their efficacy in improving outcomes for patients with MI. The results from all studies-except for four, which focused on multifactorial CR, early hospital discharge approach, stressor-perception and response-targeted nursing, or nurse-based case management interventions—showed that various types of non-pharmacological interventions could impact on patients’ improvement and recovery to some extent.

A key advantage of non-pharmacological interventions is their comprehensive approach to health management. These strategies not only target the symptomatic expressions but also address the fundamental causes of medical conditions, thereby fostering long-term health maintenance. Lifestyle modifications, such as adherence to a balanced diet and regular physical activity, have been demonstrated to significantly enhance patient outcomes (24,55). Additionally, non-pharmacological methods typically exhibit a lower frequency of adverse effects and reduced risk profiles in comparison to pharmacological treatments. These interventions also facilitate patient empowerment by encouraging active participation in healthcare, thereby improving self-care skills and psychological resilience. Notably, psychological nursing care has shown considerable effectiveness in managing mental health disorders through its focus on cognitive processes and coping strategies (9).

Prvu Bettger et al. conducted a systematic review to examine transitional care following hospitalization for acute stroke or MI. Their analysis encompassed 62 articles representing 44 studies focused on transitional care interventions for acute stroke (27 studies) and MI (17 studies). Four categories of interventions were identified: hospital-initiated support (n=14), patient and family education (n=7), community-based support (n=20), and chronic disease management (n=3). The majority of studies (68%) were rated as having fair methodological quality. Moderate-strength evidence indicated that hospital-initiated support was associated with a reduction in length of hospital stay for stroke patients, while low-strength evidence suggested a decrease in mortality among MI patients receiving such support. However, evidence regarding the benefits of other intervention types and potential adverse effects of transitional care services was deemed insufficient (20). Allen et al. performed a systematic review to evaluate RCTs assessing nursing interventions aimed at secondary prevention in patients with coronary artery disease and heart failure. From an initial pool of 2,039 citations retrieved from electronic databases, 55 articles met inclusion criteria. The predominant intervention approach involved education combined with behavioral counseling and support, accounting for 65% of the interventions, often delivered through multiple modalities. Over half of the included trials (57%) reported statistically significant improvements in at least one outcome measure, including blood pressure, lipid profiles, physical activity, dietary habits, smoking cessation, weight loss, healthcare utilization, mortality, QOL, and psychosocial parameters. Nonetheless, no consistent associations were identified between specific intervention characteristics and their effectiveness. The average methodological quality score of the studies was 2.8 on a scale from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating superior quality (21). Thisawalangu et al. conducted a descriptive literature review to assess nursing interventions for patients recovering from MI. Their findings highlighted four principal nursing interventions: psychological support, monitoring and assessment, medication management, and patient education. These interventions were found to positively influence both physiological and psychological outcomes. Furthermore, patients who received individualized nursing care exhibited reduced anxiety and depression, enhanced medication adherence, improved self-care knowledge, and greater satisfaction with care. The study emphasized the importance of patient-centered and holistic nursing care in facilitating long-term health and recovery post-MI. It was recommended that further research be undertaken to investigate the long-term effects of these interventions and to broaden their application across diverse clinical settings (22). Lawler et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs to evaluate the effectiveness of exercise-based CR following MI. The analysis included 34 RCTs encompassing a total of 6,111 participants. The findings indicated that patients assigned to exercise-based CR exhibited a significantly reduced risk of reinfarction [odds ratio (OR) 0.53, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.38–0.76], cardiac mortality (OR 0.64, 95% CI: 0.46–0.88), and all-cause mortality (OR 0.74, 95% CI: 0.58–0.95). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that these beneficial effects were consistent across different study periods, durations of CR, and follow-up intervals beyond the active intervention phase. Furthermore, exercise-based CR was associated with improvements in cardiovascular risk factors, including reductions in smoking prevalence, blood pressure, body weight, and favorable modifications in lipid profiles (24).

Geographical variations in nursing practice significantly impact the efficacy of nursing interventions. An intervention demonstrating robust outcomes in one locale may yield diminished, enhanced, or qualitatively different effects in another due to disparities in healthcare systems, resource availability, cultural norms, patient demographics, and professional roles.

Several critical factors contribute to these differences:

❖ Healthcare system variability: nurses’ roles and responsibilities differ markedly across countries and regions. In certain contexts, nurses exercise considerable autonomy in patient assessment, prescribing, and care planning, whereas in others, nursing practice is predominantly physician-directed. For instance, a nurse-led chronic disease management program may achieve favorable results in settings where nurses can independently modify treatment regimens but may be less effective where nurses have restricted decision-making authority.

In conclusion, geographical variations can serve as effect modifiers, altering the magnitude or direction of an intervention’s impact. Consequently, it is imperative to thoroughly comprehend the local healthcare infrastructure, cultural factors, and organizational contexts when extrapolating evidence from one environment to another.

The review revealed multiple common methodological shortcomings that restricted the ability to derive conclusive findings. These shortcomings included: (I) limited sample sizes; (II) challenges in study comparability due to the scarcity of research evaluating identical interventions, further complicated by variability in outcome measures; (III) inconsistencies in the timing and methods of assessments; (IV) an almost universal lack of blinding among data collectors, participants, or both, and most of the included studies were rated as moderate to low quality. Furthermore, this systematic review included the restriction of the database search to English-language studies, and expanding the language inclusion criterion may yield a more representative sample. Moreover, we sought to examine which interventions have been applied in patients with MI to help policymakers select appropriate interventions in clinical practice. Therefore, the search scope was broad, and there were no restrictions related to interventions or outcomes, which generated highly variable findings. Furthermore, interventions were frequently poorly explained and lacked details, which prevented their systematic evaluation. Moreover, it was not always clear who delivered the intervention. A significant proportion of research is characterized by methodological deficiencies and substandard reporting practices. Commonly observed limitations include the absence of straightforward case descriptions, insufficient or inappropriate sampling methodologies, inadequate consideration of confounding variables, lack of detailed accounts of assessment procedures, and failure to provide comprehensive information on sample characteristics. Additionally, there was considerable heterogeneity among studies concerning the nature of interventions and the timing of their application in relation to treatments. In light of these methodological limitations, the findings of the review should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

A total of eighteen distinct non-pharmacological interventions were assessed to evaluate their efficacy in improving outcomes for patients with MI. The findings of this review highlight that various types of nursing interventions can, to some extent, impact patient outcomes. However, due to the methodological constraints, the results should be interpreted with prudence. Further investigation employing larger sample sizes and improved methodological rigor is necessary to more conclusively ascertain the effectiveness of these nursing interventions.
