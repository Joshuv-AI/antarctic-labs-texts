A Longitudinal Case Study of Renal Cyst Progression and Regression in Trisomy 13
Authors: Fumiko Yamabe, Takahiro Shindo, Kentaro Haneda, Yoshiyuki Namai, Yutaka Harita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTrisomy 13 is a chromosomal disorder frequently associated with congenital anomalies, including polycystic kidney disease (PKD). Although the link between trisomy 13 and PKD is recognized, the timing and progression of renal cyst development remain unclear. We report a male neonate with trisomy 13 in whom we performed serial renal ultrasounds, enabling real‐time monitoring of PKD progression. Prenatal and immediate postnatal imaging revealed no renal abnormalities. However, progressive microcystic changes became evident by day 86, peaked around day 169, and subsequently regressed by day 434. Despite transient kidney enlargement, renal function remained stable at chronic kidney disease stage 2. This case demonstrates that regular and serial postnatal renal ultrasound is necessary to detect and manage PKD progression in trisomy 13. We recommend structured renal follow‐up to enable early intervention and improve clinical care in these patients.

Introduction

Trisomy 13, first reported by Patau et al. in 1960, is a chromosomal abnormality with an incidence of 1.9 cases per 10,000 individuals [1]. Historically, trisomy 13 has been associated with poor survival rates, with Meyer et al. reporting a 1‐year survival rate of 11.5% based on data from 1999 to 2007 [2]. However, recent advancements in neonatal intensive care, including both cardiac and non‐cardiac surgeries for children with chromosomal abnormalities, have led to improved prognoses [1, 2]. Kato et al. reported that more patients with trisomy 13 who died between 2009 and 2021 had a surgical history (28.0%) than those who died between 1996 and 2008 (16.0%). They also noted a one‐year survival rate of 29.4% based on the data from 2017 to 2021 [3].

Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), including hydronephrosis, kidney duplication, retroiliac ureter, vesicoureteral reflux, and polycystic kidney disease (PKD), are common complications of trisomy 13, highlighting the broad spectrum of renal anomalies associated with this condition [4, 5]. Among these, PKD has been reported with a relatively high incidence, ranging from 5.7% to 34% [6, 7, 8]. Although it has long been known that trisomy 13 is associated with PKD, there are few reports on when it develops, despite some evidence suggesting prenatal onset [9]. To date, the progression and underlying mechanisms of renal cysts in trisomy 13 remain poorly understood.

Here, we report a neonate with trisomy 13 in whom we performed frequent renal ultrasounds, enabling us to document the detailed progression of polycystic kidney formation. This observation contributes new insights into PKD onset and evolution in this population.

Case History/Examination

A Japanese male neonate with trisomy 13 was born at 35 weeks and 4 days of gestation via emergency cesarean section due to fetal dysfunction. No prenatal diagnostic testing was performed. Fetal ultrasound showed no renal cysts or enlargement. The patient presented with typical trisomy 13 features, including scalp and skull defects, auricular hypoplasia, a bulbous nose, micropenis, and bilateral overlapping fingers. We confirmed a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) by echocardiography. Karyotype analysis using Giemsa banding of peripheral blood revealed full trisomy 13 (47,XY,+13).

The patient's clinical course was complicated by jaundice, recurrent urinary tract infections, and PDA (Figure 1, Table 1). Jaundice peaked at a direct bilirubin level of 14.06 mg/dL on day 56 and resolved spontaneously, while abdominal ultrasound showed no abnormalities of the liver or bile ducts except for transient bile sludge accumulation. The patient developed two episodes of upper urinary tract infections caused by extended‐spectrum beta‐lactamase (ESBL)‐producing Escherichia coli on days 54 and 94, both of which were successfully treated with appropriate antimicrobial therapy. Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) on day 195 confirmed bilateral grade 2 vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), and subsequent urinary tract infections were effectively managed with sulfamethoxazole‐trimethoprim prophylaxis. Management of the PDA required diuretic therapy and continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). On day 131, he developed oliguria due to decreased renal perfusion during an increased dose of diuretics and was diagnosed with acute kidney injury (AKI) as the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) declined to 31.63 mL/min/1.73 m2. Renal function improved to baseline with diuretic reduction and aggressive fluid resuscitation, but this led to severe respiratory failure due to exacerbation of heart failure. Surgical ligation of the PDA on day 153 resolved respiratory distress and allowed discontinuation of diuretics and CPAP.

We performed frequent renal ultrasounds to monitor cardiac function and renal perfusion (Figure 2). They revealed a progressive development of PKD. The initial ultrasound on day 1 showed bilaterally normal kidney volumes, with mild increased echogenicity of the renal parenchyma and obscured corticomedullary differentiation. No cysts were detected at this stage. By day 86, both kidneys were mildly enlarged and hyperechoic without corticomedullary differentiation. They exhibited a characteristic “salt‐and‐pepper” appearance in which a diffuse microcystic structure was observed. On day 94, cysts increased in size but were smaller than 10 mm. Magnetic resonance urography on day 127 (Figure 3) confirmed the development of PKD, with multiple cysts scattered throughout the cortex and subcortical areas of both kidneys. The cysts continued to enlarge until day 259, reaching a maximum diameter of 10 mm, with no significant laterality observed. By day 317, the cysts tended to regress, and by day 434, they had shrunk to 4 mm in diameter and become less apparent on ultrasound examination. The cyst regression was observed symmetrically in both kidneys, suggesting a systemic rather than a localized renal anomaly as the underlying cause.

Kidney size, weight gain, and renal function fluctuated throughout the patient's course (Figure 4). The eGFR was calculated using the equation for Japanese children reported by Uemura et al. [10]. An episode of AKI occurred on day 131 during PDA treatment, temporarily affecting renal function. By day 169, the kidneys reached maximum sizes of 8.23 cm (left) and 8.37 cm (right), followed by a spontaneous reduction in size. Despite the subsequent decrease in kidney size, there was no significant decline in renal function. Renal imaging consistently showed abnormalities and reduced kidney function throughout the follow‐up period, thereby meeting the diagnostic criteria for chronic kidney disease (CKD). As the eGFR remained within the range of 60–90 mL/min/1.73 m2, we classified the condition as stage 2 CKD per KDOQI guidelines.

The patient was discharged at day 334 but died suddenly at day 435 due to status epilepticus.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Our case exhibited no clinical features suggestive of ARPKD (autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease), such as significant hepatobiliary involvement, and there was no family history of ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease). Genetic testing showed no abnormalities in the PKHD1 gene (MIM* 606702; polycystic kidney and hepatic disease l) responsible for ARPKD. We could not fully exclude other genetic causes of cystic kidney disease, such as nephronophthisis, Joubert syndrome, Bardet–Biedl syndrome, or Meckel–Gruber syndrome. Importantly, beyond manifestations typically linked to trisomy 13, the patient showed no extrarenal features typical of ciliopathies, such as retinal degeneration, polydactyly, or occipital encephalocele. Nevertheless, the absence of confirmatory genetic testing renders this a key limitation of our report.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our case highlights the importance of frequent and prolonged postnatal renal imaging in infants with trisomy 13. By documenting the timeline of cyst appearance, progression, and regression, this report underscores the need for ongoing surveillance—beyond the neonatal period—to promptly identify and manage PKD progression. We recommend routine, serial renal ultrasound examinations to optimize the follow‐up and care of patients with trisomy 13.

A key limitation of this report is the lack of comprehensive genetic testing associated with PKD, such as whole‐exome sequencing. While our findings strongly suggest a link between trisomy 13 and the observed PKD, we cannot fully exclude other rare genetic causes. Further research is needed to clarify the underlying genetic mechanisms.

Discussion

The reported incidence of PKD in trisomy 13 varies widely across different studies, suggesting that the prevalence may be underestimated. Autopsy studies show an incidence of 34% [6], while reports on surviving cases range from 5.7% [7] to 17% [8]. This discrepancy suggests that fine renal cysts, undetectable by ultrasonography [11], may be present during fetal and neonatal periods. Our case suggests that even in the absence of detectable renal cysts on fetal and neonatal ultrasonography, cysts may develop and progressively enlarge over time, ultimately leading to PKD.

The ultrasound findings in our case closely resembled those of ARPKD, particularly the “salt‐and‐pepper” appearance and kidney size changes [12, 13]. ARPKD kidneys consistently appear hyperechoic with absent corticomedullary differentiation in most cases, while also exhibiting evolutionary changes in ultrasound patterns over time. This dual characteristic reflects both the initial histologic changes (tubular dilation creating hyperechogenicity) and the disease's progressive nature, where imaging findings may shift from homogeneous hyperechogenicity to cystic transformations as microcystic structures develop [14]. To date, ciliopathy genes have not been well characterized on chromosome 13. While gene expression imbalances from aneuploidy may secondarily disrupt ciliary function or developmental pathways, the precise mechanisms remain unclear. Grlić et al. discussed the association between ciliopathies and chromosomal abnormalities, including trisomy 13, and note that clinical features of trisomy 13 may resemble ciliopathy‐related conditions due to potential disruptions of cilia‐related genes or pathways [15].

Blickman reported that ARPKD patients' kidneys initially undergo transient enlargement before stabilizing or decreasing in size, hypothesizing that increased fibrosis leads to reduced renal parenchymal volume [16]. Our case followed a similar pattern, with peak kidney size at day 169, followed by gradual regression of both kidney size and cysts.

There is currently a paucity of literature specifically addressing the mechanisms or patterns of cystic regression in trisomy 13. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the observed cyst appearance and shrinkage is a unique feature of this case or a phenomenon directly linked to the genetic characteristics of trisomy 13. Likewise, there is very little data in the existing literature on long‐term renal function and the prevalence of specific CKD stages in patients with trisomy 13. This makes it difficult to determine how common this case's level of renal function is. These observations highlight an important area for future research and underscore the need for more reported cases to elucidate the underlying genetic factors contributing to renal cyst dynamics in trisomy 13.

With advances in the management of trisomy 13 improving prognoses, cases of PKD may be identified more frequently in patients after the neonatal period. While renal ultrasound screening is performed on trisomy 13 neonates upon NICU admission, there are no established guidelines for subsequent follow‐up frequency [1]. Given the potential for PKD development even in the absence of neonatal renal abnormalities, increasing opportunities for renal ultrasound, such as performing examinations at each routine outpatient visit, could contribute to further case identification.

It is important to note that cysts may regress over time, potentially leading to underdiagnosis on ultrasound. However, even in the absence of visible cysts, underlying tissue changes may already be present, potentially contributing to renal function decline. Therefore, regular monitoring is crucial, as PKD may progress later even when not apparent on imaging.

Trisomy 13 can be associated with PKD, with an incidence potentially higher than previously recognized. As fine cysts may be difficult to visualize on ultrasonography in early stages, regular renal ultrasonography should be considered not only during the neonatal period but also for follow‐up monitoring of PKD development and progression.
