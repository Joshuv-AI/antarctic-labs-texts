Layer-by-layer deposition of polyelectrolyte assemblies on crop seeds as a platform for active ingredient loading and controlled release
Authors: Kateryna Rudich, Sakurako Tani, Afia Aleem, Yongki Choi, Andriy Voronov, Mohiuddin Quadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zea mays (corn) seeds were coated via aqueous layer-by-layer assembly of polyelectrolytes. Electrostatic force microscopy confirmed controlled multilayer buildup. No adverse effects on seed health or germination were observed. As one of the first studies of layer-by-layer coatings directly on seeds, this approach offers a sustainable alternative of seed coatings.

Seed coatings are one of the agricultural practices which employ polymeric coatings applied on seed surfaces for the protection and promotion of seed health. In addition to provide physical protection, these coatings are often loaded with active ingredients (e.g. antifungal agents, pesticides, growth promoters) to aid and abet the germination event.1–5 Materials for effective seed coatings must be safe, biodegradable, adhere to seed surfaces, and be permeable to water and oxygen to support germination and controlled molecular transport.6–8 Commercial seed coating formulations commonly rely on a limited set of synthetic film-forming polymers (e.g., poly(vinyl alcohol), acrylic and styrene–acrylic copolymers, and siloxane-based polymers).9,10 The objective of using these coatings is to enable fungal protection and controlled release of active ingredients (such as growth promoters), and reduce dust-off. Industrially, these coatings are typically applied by rotary drum film coating, powder coating, or fluidized-bed coating processes.11–18 Modern agriculture faces increasing pressure to reduce reliance on synthetic polymers and agrochemicals due to their environmental and health risks.19–24 The Layer-by-Layer (LbL) technique is a robust approach to assemble multilayered biomaterial coatings from charged polyelectrolytes, allowing precise control over composition and functionality through electrostatic, hydrogen-bonding, or covalent interactions.25,26 Repeated alternating adsorption of oppositely charged layers generates stable nanostructured polymeric films and also allows tunable cargo-loading and release profiles.27–29 As a layering process, LbL allows the use of water as a solvent, and as such, various hydrophilic biopolymers can be used for the coatings. Biopolymer-based coatings present an eco-friendly alternative with customizable architecture and aqueous-based processability.3,10,30–33 Most importantly, the LbL seed-coating approach may provide a basis for future studies aimed at incorporating delicate biological macromolecules, such as proteins, enzymes, and microorganisms.

Among natural polysaccharides, chitosan (CHI) and sodium alginate (ALG) become particularly attractive biopolymers for agricultural applications and they are widely investigated in the area of formulating controlled-release systems.34–36 Their use improves soil hydro-physical properties and biodegradability over synthetic counterparts.37 CHI, a cationic polysaccharide derived from chitin, forms polyelectrolyte complexes under acidic aqueous conditions.38–42 ALG, an anionic polysaccharide from brown algae, forms polyelectrolyte complexes with cationic CHI under aqueous conditions.5,43 As such, these two polyelectrolytes constitute a well-established pair for forming LbL coatings on seeds under mild aqueous conditions. The coating system is inherently biodegradable by certain soil microorganisms, providing an auxiliary carbon source for seeds.44,45 This study presents a proof-of-concept biopolymer multilayer seed coating assembled via the LbL technique. Zea mays (corn) was selected as a model species due to its susceptibility to environmental stresses and extensive use of seed treatments – reportedly applied to over 90% of corn seed planted in the U.S.46 In this study, seeds were coated with alternating layers of positively-charged CHI and negatively charged ALG. Rose bengal dye (RB) was used as a representative molecular payload. The overall scheme of the seed coating process is shown in Fig. 1.

To evaluate how to control cargo content and release through layers, two coating deposition strategies were adopted and compared. In model 1 (Fig. 2A), RB was introduced as a separate deposition step between CHI and ALG. In model 2 (Fig. 2B), RB was mixed into both polyelectrolyte solutions. Successful deposition of the LbL layers is governed by a balance of electrostatic, entropic, and solvent interactions between the polyelectrolytes and the substrate. The LbL assembly also depends strongly on substrate properties, such as charge density, hydrophilicity, roughness, and impurities, all of which affect film uniformity and stability.25 The outer pericarp of corn seeds consists primarily of hemicelluloses (notably arabinoxylans with glucuronic acid substitutions), cellulose microfibrils, and low lignin content. The presence of uronic acid residues in arabinoxylans and minor pectin imparts a net negative surface charge to the seed coat, favoring initial adsorption of cationic polymers such as chitosan.47–51 Thorough rinsing between deposition steps was employed to remove weakly bound species and prevent inter-polyelectrolyte complexation or precipitation.29,52

SEM analysis (Fig. 3) was used to assess surface morphology. Uncoated seeds (Fig. 3A) exhibited the surface features containing distinct ridges and pores. Seeds coated with RB-free CHI/ALG coating (no RB loaded, Fig. 3B) showed minimal morphological changes; a subtle increase in surface opacity and smoothness likely indicative of the presence of a thin CHI/ALG film, which remained difficult to resolve due to its low density and conformal nature. The observation is consistent with prior LbL studies on biological substrates, where ultrathin polyelectrolyte films often produce limited contrast in SEM.25,27,35,53 While direct thickness determination on corn seeds is experimentally challenging, the LbL-deposited coating in the present work is expected to be nanoscale.

Bottom row (scale bar: 5 µm): higher-magnification images of the corresponding regions highlighting surface morphology, film buildup, and aggregate formation.

To confirm surface charge reversal during sequential deposition of oppositely charged biopolymers, electrostatic force microscopy (EFM) was employed (Fig. 4).

Electrostatic force microscopy (EFM) phase (top row) and corresponding topography (bottom row) images of corn seed surfaces during sequential CHI/ALG LbL deposition: (A) uncoated (0 layers, native negative surface), (B) CHI1 (0.5 layers, cationic), (C) CHI/ALG (1 bilayer, anionic), (D) CHI/ALG/CHI (1.5 layers, cationic), and (E) CHI/ALG/CHI/ALG (2 layers, anionic). (F) Average phase shift as a function of the number of deposited biopolymer layers.

Specifically, the EFM images revealed alternating changes in surface electrostatic response after each oppositely charged layer, consistent with the expected charge reversal behavior characteristic of successful LbL buildup. The uncoated corn seed surface exhibited a predominantly dark contrast in the phase-shift image, indicative of a net negative surface potential consistent with the presence of native anionic groups (e.g., carboxylates and hydroxyls) on the seed coat. Following deposition of the first CHI layer, the EFM contrast inverted to a lighter (positive) phase contrast, confirming electrostatic overcompensation and formation of a positively charged surface. Subsequent adsorption of ALG restored a darker (negative) phase signal, and this alternating contrast persisted through successive CHI/ALG layers. The corresponding topographic maps showed no significant morphological differences across layers, indicating that the EFM contrast originated from variations in electrostatic potential rather than surface roughness (Fig. 4A–E). To quantify the observed charge-reversal behavior, the average EFM phase shift was extracted from the phase images and plotted as a function of layer number (Fig. 4F). This zig-zag trend quantitatively confirms sequential surface charge inversion during CHI/ALG LbL assembly. These results collectively confirm that LbL assembly on the seed surface proceeds through sequential charge inversion, validating the controlled buildup of polyelectrolyte multilayers.

Because corn seeds are anatomically heterogeneous, SEM and EFM measurements were focused primarily on the smoother pericarp region, which provided a sufficiently flat and accessible surface for reliable imaging and comparison of sequential deposition steps. Regions such as the dent and tip cap/hilum are more porous and topographically irregular, and therefore may exhibit local differences in coating thickness, charge density, and film morphology.

The encapsulation efficiency of the model molecule (RB) was quantified using water-assisted sonication followed by UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. S1A and B). RB loading was normalized to seed mass (mg g−1) and plotted as a function of the number of deposited layers (Fig. S2A). In model 1 of LbL deposition, where RB was added in a separate RB-only deposition step between CHI and ALG, loading increased steadily up to five layers before declining, suggesting a depletion of the loaded amount of RB beyond this point. This behavior is consistent with prior LbL assembly reports showing cumulative losses during repeated deposition/separation steps (e.g., up to ≈5% material loss per bilayer), which can practically limit multilayer buildup to ∼8–10 layers depending on the substrate.54,55 In model 2, where RB was blended with both polyelectrolyte solutions, the loading profile exhibited higher variability and more pronounced aggregate formation, consistent with RB–polymer association occurring already in solution.

RB was selected as an anionic model payload for this proof-of-concept study because dye-only controls showed negligible adsorption on uncoated seeds, allowing polymer-mediated loading to be distinguished from nonspecific adsorption of dye to the native seed surface. Although payload chemistry is expected to be a critical variable in LbL-based delivery systems, and cargo loading in CHI/ALG coatings should depend on molecular charge, solubility, hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity, and affinity for both the polyelectrolyte multilayer and the seed surface.

Controlled release of active ingredients (such as fungicides and growth promoters) to affect their biological targets is a critical function of seed coating. Release profiles of RB were compared between the two loading architecture models (SI for more details). Model 1 (RB as an independent layer) demonstrated a more sustained release profile, whereas model 2 (RB blended with polymers) exhibited a faster initial release with higher variability. This difference likely reflects the distinct spatial distribution of RB within the multilayer structure. In model 2, the faster early-stage release is consistent with a larger contribution from polymer swelling, while model 1 appears to provide better confinement of RB within the coating. Kinetics modeling supported these trends, with model 1 best described by the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, with an exponent suggesting predominantly diffusion-controlled release. Because the threshold values for ideal Fickian behavior in the Korsmeyer–Peppas model are geometry-dependent and were derived for ideal slabs, cylinders, and spheres, the value obtained here for the irregular corn seed geometry is interpreted as consistent with Fickian-dominated, rather than strictly ideal Fickian, transport.56 On the other hand, model 2 fits to first-order and Hixson–Crowell models, consistent with a release process influenced by changes in matrix polymers structure.57 Within the scope of this study, these results indicate that the loading architecture in CHI/ALG LbL coatings provides a handle to tune the rate and extent of small-molecule release behavior from sustained to faster, erosion-assisted profiles.

It should be noted that the release experiments were performed in DI water as a simplified model medium. Under soil conditions, divalent cations such as Ca2+ and Mg2+ may ionically crosslink alginate-rich layers, potentially reducing swelling and slowing cargo release relative to DI water.

To evaluate the ability of the coating system to mimic the release of encapsulated compounds into emerging seedlings for their systemic protection against various pathogens, an in vivo RB transport experiment was conducted using RB-loaded coated seeds. After 7 days of germination, RB presence in seedling tissues was confirmed by stereomicroscopy, with visible transport into the root and shoot systems (Fig. 5). This result supports the potential of CHI/ALG LbL seed coating to enable sustained release and localized delivery of cargo during early seedling development systemically.

In vivo transport of RB in corn seedlings on 7 days of germination test using RB-loaded LbL-coated seeds. Bright-field microscopy images show RB-associated pink coloration in the shoot (left) and root (right) tissues (scale bar: 1 mm). RB used as a molecular surrogate, represents various agrochemicals that can be potentially included within the seed coatings for providing protection and promoting seedling growth, and is available to the plant tissues.

To assess the potential phytotoxic effects of the coating, germination assays were performed using seeds coated with blank CHI/ALG seed coating (10 layers, no RB loaded). Coating did not negatively affect seed germination or seedling vigor, with coated seeds showing germination rates (93.4%) comparable to uncoated controls (96.5%). Additionally, the dry and wet weight of biomass was measured post-germination. The experiment revealed no significant differences in root or shoot development (Fig. 6A).

(A) Germination percentage and shoot/root biomass (wet and dry) were measured on day 7 of the germination test for uncoated control and LbL-coated seeds. (B) Time to first radicle emergence of uncoated control and CHI/ALG-coated corn seeds. Radicle emergence was monitored over 168 h under standard germination conditions. Values represent the percentage of seeds showing visible radicle protrusion for uncoated control seeds and seeds coated with 5 or 10 CHI/ALG layers.

Since acidic medium is required to dissolve chitosan, and seeds were exposed to the pH 4 chitosan solution during the layer-by-layer deposition process, time to first radicle emergence was monitored to determine whether this step affects early germination kinetics (SI for more details). No delay in time to first radicle emergence was observed for CHI/ALG-coated seeds compared with uncoated controls under the tested conditions (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that the mildly acidic chitosan solution used for layer deposition did not measurably alter early germination timing. This observation is consistent with literature reports that germinating maize seeds can tolerate mildly acidic environments (pH ∼ 4–5) during early imbibition without adverse effects on germination behavior.58,59

Coating mechanical durability was evaluated using a shaking-stress test (SI for more details). Seeds coated with five RB-containing layers were subjected to orbital agitation for 1 h, and detached RB-containing material was collected by rinsing and quantified by UV-vis spectroscopy. The remaining coating-associated RB was then extracted from the same seeds by ultrasound-assisted digestion. Based on measured RB concentrations, about 25.0% of the total recoverable RB was detected in the agitation/rinse fraction, while 75.0% remained associated with the seeds after agitation. These results provide a semi-quantitative indication that the CHI/ALG multilayer coating is substantially retained under mechanical stress.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that LbL assembly directly deposited on seeds enables the formation of stable, conformal multilayer coating on seed surface that supports tunable cargo loading and release without impairing germination. We showed that the model payload can be encapsulated with the coating and subsequently transported into emerging root and shoot tissues, indicating that the encapsulated payload is effectively delivered to, and retained within, developing seedling tissues at early growth stages. Comparison of two RB loading strategies further reveals that coating architecture governs release behavior, indicating LbL assembly as a sustainable platform for seed-localized delivery with potential for microbial inoculants and diverse crops. Lastly, despite crop-specific differences, the demonstrated approach provides a broadly adaptable framework that can be extended to other species through targeted optimization of loading strategies.
