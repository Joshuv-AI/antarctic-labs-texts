Sub-Clinical Peripheral Neuropathy in Indian Adolescents with Type 1 Diabetes: Estimation of Prevalence and Modifiable Risk Factors
Authors: Shruti A. Mondkar, Keyur Desai, Chirantap Oza, Sandeep Borse, Madhura Karguppikar, Sushil Yewale, Nimisha Dange, Raj Ganacharya, Vaman Khadilkar, Anuradha Khadilkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Clinical screening for diabetic neuropathy diagnoses only the tip of the iceberg and may miss sub-clinical neuropathies whose progression can be prevented/reversed if detected early. Hence, this study was conducted to compare nerve conduction (NCS) parameters of adolescents with type 1 Diabetes (T1D) with healthy controls and to assess prevalence and risk factors for sub-clinical peripheral neuropathy in Indian adolescents with T1D.Methods:Case-control study. Cases (n = 120): 10–19 years, T1D duration >2 years. Exclusion: Comorbidities/illnesses/medication affecting nerve function. Controls (n = 40): Healthy, age, sex-matched.Results:Mean age: 15.1 ± 3 years; diabetes duration: 7 ± 3.5 years, HbA1c: 9.6 ± 2.1%. None had symptoms/signs of peripheral neuropathy. NCS comparison demonstrated significantly prolonged latency, reduced action potential duration, and nerve conduction velocity (P < 0.01) in motor nerves (median, ulnar, peroneal, tibial) in patients with T1D. Sensory action potential duration in median and ulnar nerves, and sural conduction velocity were significantly lower in T1D (P < 0.05). 26.7% had at least one abnormal NCS parameter. Sub-clinical neuropathy prevalence- demyelinating motor: upper limbs (UL)-5.8%, lower limbs (LL)-11.7%; demyelinating sensory: UL-5.0%, LL-5.0%; axonal motor: UL-4.2%, LL-2.5%; axonal sensory: UL-2.5%, LL-5.0%. Six (5.0%) had stage-1a distal symmetrical polyneuropathy. HbA1c (OR = 1.5), age (OR = 1.4), female gender (OR = 0.2), iron intake (OR = 0.8), and hypertension (OR = 19.7) were significantly associated with sub-clinical neuropathy.Conclusion:The prevalence of sub-clinical peripheral neuropathy in Indian adolescents with T1D was considerable, with the highest prevalence observed in the lower limbs. Despite no symptoms, NCS parameters were adversely affected in T1D, highlighting the need for early NCS-based screening. Poor glycaemic control, reduced iron intake, and hypertension were modifiable risk factors associated with sub-clinical neuropathy, apart from non-modifiable factors like male gender, age.

INTRODUCTION

Poor glycaemic control in type 1 Diabetes (T1D) causes chronic complications like neuropathy.[1] Although diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) presents overtly in adulthood, sub-clinical neuropathy has been reported in children despite shorter disease duration.[23] DPN involves sensory, motor, and autonomic nerves, causing sensory loss, autonomic blunting, and sarcopenia.[4] Globally, DPN prevalence ranges from 10-50%, with 25% prevalence (clinical/sub-clinical) in children/adolescents with T1D at diagnosis.[56] While chronic hyperglycaemia is a well-identified risk factor, emerging evidence suggests additional modifiable contributors (obesity, dyslipidaemia).[7]

The International Society for Paediatric and Adolescent Diabetes (ISPAD) recommends annual neuropathy screening after age 11 with 2–5 years diabetes duration by clinically assessing sensation, vibration, tendon reflexes in the feet, and orthostatic heart rate variability.[1] However, subjective criteria are unreliable in children, and even if relied upon, significant nerve damage likely precedes symptom development.

Given the unreliability of subjective methods, objective methods are the need of the hour. Nerve conduction study (NCS) is a non-invasive, objective method for assessing axonal and myelination-related abnormalities. In India, with the highest global burden of paediatric T1D, detecting sub-clinical DPN could prevent polyneuropathy progression and associated morbidity. Hence, to study the burden of sub-clinical DPN, to assess the reliability of ISPAD screening criteria and identify potential reversible risk factors, this study was planned to:

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and subjects

This cross-sectional, case-control study was conducted between September 2022 and February 2023. Adolescents with T1D aged 10-19 years attending the pediatric T1D clinic at our tertiary center (Western Maharashtra), with diabetes duration >2 years, were approached. Those with comorbidities, namely celiac disease, vitamin B12 deficiency, uncontrolled hypothyroidism, chronic disorders (e.g. Wolfram syndrome), or on medications (e.g., isoniazid, phenytoin) affecting nerve function were excluded. Of 141 participants screened, 9 were excluded, 12 declined participation, 120 participants/their parents (60 girls) gave consent/assent and were enrolled as cases. Forty, disease-free, age- and sex-matched controls (56 screened, 10 excluded, 6 refused participation) were recruited from among the siblings of cases (cases: controls = 3:1). Using G*Power 3.1.9.4, using A-priori analysis for t-test between two independent groups and allocation ratio of 3:1, total sample size of 160 was found to be adequate for effect size = 0.5, α error = 0.05, power = 0.8.

Data

Clinical data: Data on age, diabetes duration, daily insulin dose, and neurological symptoms/signs were collected using questionnaires. Neuropathic symptoms (burning, tingling, numbness, fatigue, pain, cramps, unsteady gait) were scored using the Diabetic Neuropathy Symptom score.[89] Scores >3 were considered abnormal.[9] Activity was assessed using the activity questionnaire adapted for Indian adolescents.[10] Food intake was assessed by a trained nutritionist using the 24-h dietary recall method over three days. Nutrient intakes were calculated using C-diet software.[11]

Anthropometry: Height (Seca portable stadiometer, up to 0.1 cm accuracy) and weight (Seca 876 Flat scale, up to 100 g accuracy) were measured, BMI was computed (weight (kg)/height (m2)), and converted to Z-scores.[12]

Neurological examination: The Neuropathy Disability Score was used; scores >3 were considered abnormal.[89] Cutaneous touch sensation was tested using a 10g monofilament over the plantar aspect of the great toe and 1st, 3rd, and 5th metatarsal bases bilaterally. It was considered abnormal if the monofilament was not sensed. Pain was assessed using a disposable pin over the dorsum of the hallux with pressure just enough to deform the skin. Vibration sense was tested using a tuning fork (128 Hz) over the dorsum of the great toes and medial malleoli. Flexion, extension movements at the wrists, elbows, ankles, and knees were assessed for muscle power.[8]

Biochemical: These were performed only for the cases. 6–8 mL of blood was drawn after 8–10 hours of fasting; total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured using the enzymatic method, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was calculated. Dyslipidemia was defined as one or more abnormal lipid parameters. Glycaemic control, i.e., glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), was evaluated using high-performance liquid chromatography (BIO-RAD, Germany). Glycemic control was defined as good/intermediate (HbA1c <9.5%) or poor (HbA1c >9.5%).[13] Hemoglobin was estimated by spectrophotometry using a Horiba-Yumizen H500 hematology analyzer, urinary albumin by immunoturbidimetry, and creatinine by Jaffe’s method.

Insulin sensitivity: Calculated as: exp[4.64725 – 0.02032 (waist; cm) – 0.09779 (HbA1c;%) – 0.00235 (Triglyceride; mg/dl)].[14]

Body composition: Fat and lean mass were assessed using a Bioelectrical Impedance Analyzer (Tanita BC420MA) in standing position after 3 hours of fasting, and voiding before measurements; Z-scores were computed using Indian reference data.[15]

Nerve conduction studies: NCS were conducted on all participants using conventional surface electrodes using the Nihon Kohden 4-channel NCS electromyography measuring system (NeuropackS3 MEB-9600K, Nihon Kohden, Tokyo) by a single technician blinded to clinical information [Figure 1]. Skin temperature was measured before testing, and whenever necessary, limbs were warmed with warm towels to ensure a temperature of 34°C. Motor conduction studies were performed bilaterally at the median, ulnar, common peroneal, and posterior tibial nerves; sensory parameters were assessed at the median, ulnar, and sural nerves. As NCS machines do not routinely have provisions for pediatric normative values, published pediatric reference data were used for analysis.[416] Amplitudes, velocities, distal latencies, and action potential duration were assessed and considered abnormal if values were beyond ± 3SD of pediatric reference values.[416] Sub-clinical demyelinating neuropathy was defined as conduction velocity below 3SD (1st centile) or distal latency prolonged beyond +3SD (99th centile) for age and sex.[46] Sub-clinical axonal neuropathy was defined as compound muscle action potential (CMAP) or sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude below 3SD for age and sex.[46] Diabetic distal symmetric polyneuropathy was defined as ≥1 abnormal NCV attribute (≥ 3 SD) in at least two nerves (one of them being the sural nerve).[68] It was staged as Stage-0: No neuropathy (absent signs, symptoms, normal NCS), stage-1a: abnormal NCS without signs and symptoms, stage-1b: abnormal NCS and signs but no symptoms, stage-2: abnormal NCS, presence of signs and mild symptoms, stage-3: disabling neuropathy with severe symptoms.[68]

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS, Bangalore, India). All variables were tested for normality. Mean (standard deviation), median (interquartile range), and proportions (percentages) were used for descriptive statistics. An independent sample t-test was used to test differences between cases and controls for normally distributed variables, and non-parametric tests for non-normal variables. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to study correlations between variables. Multivariable binary logistic regression and Odds ratio (OR) were used to assess associations between predictor variables and sub-clinical neuropathy. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, Jehangir Clinical Development Center Pvt Ltd, dated 18.08.2022. Written informed consent/assent was obtained from participants/their parents for study participation and use of de-identified data for research and educational purposes. The study was conducted by the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki 1964.

RESULTS

Mean age of adolescents with T1D was 15.1 ± 3 years, diabetes duration was 7 ± 3.5 years, HbA1c was 9.6 ± 2.1% (range: 6.4–17.7%). 51 (46.3%) patients had dyslipidemia, and 17 (15.6%) had albuminuria. Mean hemoglobin was 13.6 ± 1.7 g/dL, serum creatinine was 0.7 ± 0.6 mg/dL, total cholesterol was 153 ± 30 mg/dL, HDL-C was 46.7 ± 13.7 mg/dL, LDL-C was 90.5 mg/dL, and triglyceride was 81.8 ± 68.9 mg/dL. None of the patients had examination findings/history suggestive of motor/sensory neuropathy.

Both groups had similar anthropometry and activity duration [Table 1]. On comparing NCS parameters, latency was significantly prolonged (P < 0.01); action potential duration (P < 0.01 for tibial and P < 0.001 for other nerves) and nerve conduction velocity (P < 0.001) were significantly lesser in motor nerves of upper (median, ulnar) and lower limbs (peroneal, tibial) in patients with T1D [Table 2a]. SNAP duration was significantly lower in median (Right: P = 0.009, Left: P = 0.016) and ulnar nerves (Right: P = 0.048) at the wrist among patients with T1D [Table 2b]. In the lower limbs, sural nerve conduction velocity was significantly lower among cases (Right: P = 0.047, Left: P = 0.006) [Table 2b].

Among patients with T1D, 32 (26.7%) had at least one form of sub-clinical DPN [prevalence depicted in Figure 2a]. Sub-clinical demyelinating motor neuropathy prevalence in the upper limbs (UL) was 5.8% and 11.7% in the lower limbs (LL). 5.0% had sub-clinical demyelinating sensory neuropathy in the UL and 5.0% had LL involvement. Sub-clinical UL motor axonal neuropathy was observed in 4.2%; 2.5% had similar LL involvement. Sub-clinical axonal sensory neuropathy was observed in the UL in 2.5% and the LL in 5.0%. Six patients (5.0%) had stage-1a distal symmetrical polyneuropathy.

The earliest evidence of sub-clinical neuropathy was noted at 11.2 years in a girl with diabetes for 6.6 years. At the time of testing, she weighed 23.2 kg (−2.1 SDS), was 121.5 cm (−3.0 SDS) in height, with a normal BMI of 15.7 kg/m2 (−0.6 SDS). She was in Tanner stage 2 of puberty. Her HbA1c was 9.5%. She was normotensive but dyslipidemic (low HDL-C = 36mg/dL, elevated LDL-C = 108.6 mg/dL). She had evidence of sub-clinical demyelinating sensory and motor neuropathy in both lower limbs (tibial, peroneal, sural nerves).

The highest proportion of abnormal (<-3SD of normative data) motor NCV was observed in the tibial and peroneal nerves-4.2% [Figure 2b]. Sensory NCV was most affected in the sural nerve-5.0% [Figure 2c]. CMAP and SNAP amplitudes were most affected in the ulnar (3.3%) and sural nerves (6.7%), respectively. Both motor and sensory nerve distal latencies were most affected in the LL (tibial nerve-8.3%; sural nerve-4.2%).

HbA1c levels correlated negatively with median (r = −0.2; P = 039), ulnar (r = −0.3; P = 0.001), tibial motor NCVs (r = −0.2; P = 0.049), median sensory NCV (r = −0.3; P = 0.014) and median (r = −0.3; P = 0.017), ulnar (r = −0.2; P = 0.042), sural SNAP amplitudes (r = −0.3; P = 0.026).

Multivariable binomial logistic regression performed to assess the association between predictor variables and sub-clinical neuropathy development revealed that the odds of developing sub-clinical neuropathy increased by 1.4 (95%CI 1.10, 1.78) for a unit increase in age, reduced by 0.7 (95%CI 0.6, 0.9) for every unit increase in dietary iron intake, increased by 1.5 (95%CI 1.0, 2.2) for every unit increase in HbA1c. The presence of hypertension was associated with a 19.7 (95%CI 1.9, 195.5) times higher odds of sub-clinical neuropathy. Female gender was associated with a marginally lower risk, i.e., exp (B) =0.2 (95%CI 0.04, 0.9) of sub-clinical neuropathy compared to males [Table 3].

DISCUSSION

This study observed significantly worse NCS parameters in all motor and sensory nerves in adolescents with T1D versus healthy controls, despite no history/clinical findings of neuropathy. None of the patients demonstrated neuropathy using the ISPAD screening criteria. Considerable prevalence (26.7%) of sub-clinical DPN was observed. 5.0% had stage-1a distal symmetrical polyneuropathy. The highest prevalence of sub-clinical motor demyelinating neuropathy was observed in the LL (peroneal and tibial nerves). The sural nerve was the most common sensory nerve affected by demyelination and axonal damage. Advancing age, male gender, low dietary iron intake, increased HbA1c, and hypertension were associated with sub-clinical neuropathy.

Distal symmetrical sensorimotor polyneuropathy is the commonest type of DPN caused by an interplay of chronic hyperglycaemia, microangiopathy, oxidative and autoimmune nerve damage, with diabetes duration and poor glycaemic control as its strongest determinants.[26] We observed a 26.7% prevalence of sub-clinical DPN in Indian adolescents. DPN prevalence varies globally, depending upon diagnostic criteria used, clinical/sub-clinical classification, and age group studied.[617] Recent literature has increasingly demonstrated sub-clinical neuropathy/abnormal NCS parameters in adolescents with T1D.[35] Another Indian study reported a 56% prevalence of sub-clinical DPN in children (8–18 years) with T1D > 2 years.[2] Singh et al.[18] reported an 18.2% prevalence in Indian children with T1D >2 years. In Austria, Walter-Höliner et al.[5] observed a 13.2% prevalence by neurological examination versus 31.6% sub-clinical neuropathy prevalence on NCS at baseline, which increased to 34.2% and 63.2%, respectively, over 5 years.

While clinical DPN is mostly noted in the LL, sub-clinical nerve involvement patterns vary considerably in children.[6] Similar to our study, Singh et al.[18] reported the commonest involvement of the peroneal and tibial motor nerves in children with T1D. We observed no significant differences in CMAP amplitudes between cases and controls. Similarly, Heyden et al.[19] noted no differences in CMAP amplitude between cases with T1D (duration 2.5-5 years, with good glycemic control) versus controls, but reported reduced axonal excitability (higher stimulus intensity for maximal response elicitation) in patients with T1D. In contrast, adolescents with T1D >10 years and poor glycaemic control had significantly lower CMAP amplitudes in their study, implying CMAP amplitude alterations occur relatively late in the course of DPN.[19] Our study noted the highest prevalence of sub-clinical motor demyelinating and sensory axonal neuropathy in the LL, consistent with global data.[18] Sub-clinical demyelinating sensory neuropathy was equally prevalent in UL and LL, while sub-clinical axonal motor neuropathy was higher in the UL, which is an atypical finding.

We observed a significant association between poor glycemic control and sub-clinical DPN. Although the majority of studies have reported glycemic control as an implicated factor, results remain divided. While assessing sub-clinical neuropathy, the Diabetes Control and Complications (DCCT) trial reported significantly better NCS parameters in the intensive treatment group compared to conventional treatment following 5 years of treatment.[20] In contrast, few studies have demonstrated neuropathy despite the absence of poor glycaemic control.[21] Recent evidence highlights the relationship between the degree of glycaemic excursions and neuropathy. Kwai et al.[21] observed that glycaemic variability, and not acute glucose levels, correlate with excitability markers of altered sensory and motor axonal function. Thus, not only good control but also lesser glycaemic variability seems to be protective against DPN.

A significant finding observed in our study was the association between hypertension and sub-clinical neuropathy. This has been reported in very few studies assessing clinical (not sub-clinical) neuropathy in adults and youth with T1D/T2D.[2223] The SEARCH for diabetes in youth study reported a significant association between increased diastolic blood pressure and clinically-diagnosed DPN in youth with T1D.[23] To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to report this association with sub-clinical peripheral neuropathy in T1D. Preclinical/clinical studies have demonstrated renin–angiotensin system overactivation in diabetes and its role in neuropathy development, suggesting the preventive role of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors in T2D.[24] Aggressive hypertension management may help prevent/reduce DPN risk in T1D, too, though further studies are required to consolidate this finding.

Another novel finding was the inverse association between iron intake and sub-clinical neuropathy. Animal studies have demonstrated the physiological role of iron in myelin synthesis.[25] Transferrin upregulation and increased iron uptake at nerve damage sites have been reported.[25] Thus, iron is crucial for both myelination and nerve repair. Reduced dietary intake may hinder the repair of diabetes-mediated nerve damage, thereby perpetuating DPN development. Studies in T2D have demonstrated the role of anaemia in worsening DPN, owing to erythropoietin deficiency, endoneural hypoxia, and oxidative stress.[26] Iron supplementation may thus reduce DPN risk in T1D, however, further trials are required to confirm this hypothesis.

Unlike many studies, ours did not reveal diabetes duration as a predictor of sub-clinical DPN. Studies in adults with T1D have demonstrated NCV slowing at diagnosis, improving following insulin administration.[27] Comi et al.[27] observed abnormal peroneal motor NCV in 11.7%, sural sensory NCV in 13.6%, and ≥ 1 abnormalities in 21.6% of children with T1D at diagnosis, reducing to 7.1%, 10.6%, 14.8% after a year. Although DPN risk increases following longstanding diabetes, sub-clinical DPN may not be duration-dependent due to the possible reversibility of abnormal NCS findings.

NCS involves functional assessment, enabling abnormality detection before structural nerve damage occurs, thereby opening doors to the possibility of reversing functional abnormalities if detected early.[2] Though the application of ISPAD screening guidelines based on clinical assessment may serve as a tertiary prevention strategy (prevention of complications after diagnosing neuropathy), it diagnoses only the tip of the iceberg. Against a background of potential reversibility, a sizeable number of sub-clinical neuropathies whose progression can be prevented/reversed if detected early will be missed. Thus, objective assessment with NCS may be more suitable, particularly in children, where subjective findings are both unreliable and occur late in the course. The age criteria for neuropathy screening in T1D, as recommended by ISPAD, are appropriate, and our study confirms the same, as evidenced by the absence of sub-clinical neuropathy in children below 11 years of age. The earliest incidence of sub-clinical neuropathy was noted at 11.2 years of age.

An ideal screening test is sensitive, easy, detects high disease proportion in its pre-clinical state, and appropriate preventive measures are available following abnormality detection.[28] NCS fulfils all these criteria. However, opinions on using it for DPN screening remain divided due to the belief that DPN can only be prevented by ensuring glycemic control.[2930] Though previously believed that only normoglycemia can prevent/delay DPN, recent evidence suggests that this is an oversimplification, and emerging risk factors include dyslipidemia, obesity, albuminuria, and other microvascular complications.[7] The current study’s findings suggest that hypertension control and increased iron intake could play a preventive role, in addition to glycaemic control. Moreover, studies have demonstrated reversibility of sub-clinical peripheral neuropathy diagnosed early using NCS.[2730] Hence, it seems prudent to consider NCS as a screening tool for DPN in pediatric T1D. However, in the present scenario, NCS-based screening may not be feasible for all patients owing to availability and accessibility issues, particularly in developing countries. Considering the economic burden of NCS-based screening in resource-limited settings, the development of a risk-stratification model based on the presence/absence of risk factors is a possible way forward and provides scope for further research. At present, pediatric diabetes societies do not recommend NCS-based screening for DPN, and larger multicentric studies are required to substantiate our study findings to suggest NCS as a screening tool. As patients with sub-clinical neuropathy do not have symptoms, the use of common medications for alleviating pain is not required.[6] There is no clear consensus on guidelines for pharmacotherapy in pediatric diabetic neuropathy. Emphasis is primarily on meticulous glycaemic control, reducing glycaemic variability, and treatment of other microvascular complications to prevent progression to symptomatic neuropathy, as various studies have demonstrated the reversibility of sub-clinical neuropathy with these interventions.[6]

The limitations of our study are the unavailability of electromyography and CGM data. Further, glycaemic control of our cases was not optimum, hence, results may not apply to well-controlled subjects. The strengths of our study include a good sample size, availability of matched controls from similar socioeconomic backgrounds, and assessment of several parameters that may impact nerve conduction. Besides the universally reported causative role of poor glycaemic control in DPN, the associations of sub-clinical DPN development with hypertension and dietary iron intake are not only novel findings but may also be considered additional treatment strategies for preventing/halting DPN progression.

CONCLUSION

We report considerable sub-clinical peripheral neuropathy prevalence in Indian adolescents with T1D, with the highest prevalence in the lower limbs. Poor glycaemic control, hypertension, advancing age, reduced iron intake, and male gender were significantly associated with sub-clinical DPN. NCS parameters are adversely affected in T1D, despite the absence of signs and symptoms, highlighting the need for early NCS-based screening for reversal/prevention of progression of abnormal NCS parameters.
