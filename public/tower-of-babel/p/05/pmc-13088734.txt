Serological evaluation of brucellosis in nomadic small ruminants in Southeastern Türkiye
Authors: Songül Ötkün, Sevil Erdenliğ Gürbilek, Ayfer Güllü Yücetepe, Osman Yaşar Tel, Oktay Keskin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrucellosis is a globally widespread disease due to the presence of multiple bacterial species and a broad host range. In regions where livestock production is conducted under a ‘nomadic lifestyle’, detection and consequently control of the disease become challenging. The aim of this study was to obtain epidemiological data for surveillance of brucellosis in aborting nomadic sheep and goats using the Rose Bengal plate test (RBPT) and indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (i-ELISA). For this purpose, 343 serum samples collected from nomadic animals in the Southeastern Anatolia region of Türkiye were included in the study.MethodA total of 343 serum samples (n = 155 goats, n = 188 sheep) obtained from aborting nomadic small ruminants in the Southeastern Anatolia region of Türkiye were included. The samples were evaluated for brucellosis using classical and modified RBPT, as well as i-ELISA employing synthetic trisaccharide antigens.ResultsOf the serum samples, 20.99% tested positive by RBPT, while 24.78% tested positive by i-ELISA. According to the Kappa coefficient, the two test results were concordant; however, McNemar’s test indicated a statistically significant difference between the tests. To increase diagnostic specificity, 67 samples (19.53%) that tested positive by both RBPT and i-ELISA were considered ‘confirmed positive’.ConclusionIn regions where small ruminant production is a major livelihood due to socioeconomic and geographical conditions, brucellosis-associated abortion cases constitute a serious problem. While RBPT plays an important role as a practical diagnostic tool in resource-limited settings, the widespread use of diagnostic methods employing highly sensitive and specific antigens is considered essential for obtaining more reliable and accurate results in endemic areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05372-4.

Introduction

Brucellosis is a bacterial infection with the potential to occur worldwide. As a zoonotic disease, its epidemiology is closely linked to the socio-economic development of regions. Due to its wide host range and the presence of multiple identified etiological agents, brucellosis is distributed across a broad geographic area [1, 2]. Infections caused by Brucella species lead to significant health and economic losses in both humans and livestock [3, 4]. The main clinical signs of the disease in ruminants include abortion during mid or late gestation, stillbirth or the birth of weak offspring, and retention of fetal membranes symptoms that are characteristic but not pathognomonic [1, 5]. Among the Brucella genus, B. melitensis, B. abortus and B. suis are the most important species for humans and livestock. Due to mixed farming systems and shared grazing areas, small ruminants may be infected with various Brucella species; however, B. melitensis is the species most commonly associated with sheep and goat brucellosis and is also the leading agent in human brucellosis cases [1, 5].

Although bacteriological culture is the gold standard for diagnosing brucellosis, the requirement for high-biosafety, well-equipped laboratories due to the high biosafety risk of the pathogen makes cultural methods economically and practically unsuitable [1]. Therefore, the most commonly preferred diagnostic tools are indirect (serological) tests based on humoral immune responses [6]. Among these, the complement fixation test (CFT), rose bengal plate test (RBPT), and indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (i-ELISA) are widely used [1, 7, 8].

These tests typically aim to detect antibodies produced against the cell wall lipopolysaccharides (LPS), an immunodominant antigen [9, 10]. Cross-reactions may occur with agents from other bacterial genera that possess epitopes similar to the smooth lipopolysaccharide (s-LPS) structure found in the Brucella cell wall. Such false-positive reactions can create serious issues for disease control and eradication programs [1, 11, 12]. However, recent studies highlight that compared to s-LPS, synthetic antigens offer advantages such as reducing false positives due to cross-reactivity and posing lower biosafety risks during production [13–15].

It has been reported that the O-polysaccharide terminal region of Brucella is a strong epitope and that short synthetic antigens are superior to natural s-LPS antigens in terms of test specificity [14]. Brucella infection induces an early IgM antibody response in animals, followed by IgG1, and later IgG2 and IgA responses. Since IgM-based serological tests detect antibodies at very early stages, they are not preferred test choice especially in chronic stages of infection. IgG2 and IgA antibodies appear inconsistently and at low levels; therefore, diagnostic tests targeting the IgG1 antibody response are considered more useful [16]. RBPT, a well-known serological test using rose-bengal-stained B. abortus S99 or B. abortus S1119.3 strains, relies on IgG1-mediated agglutination because its low pH (3.65) partially inhibits IgM agglutination [16]. Due to being rapid, simple, low-cost, and sensitive, RBPT is frequently preferred in determining the seroprevalence of brucellosis [17–20]. The i-ELISA test, which targets Ig isotypes produced against Brucella in serum, stands out as a more sensitive and specific serological test for screening sheep and goat brucellosis compared to RBPT [1, 7].

Due to its geographical position, Türkiye and its surrounding regions have historically served as a major crossroads for migration routes and livestock activities [21]. Therefore, similar to many Middle Eastern countries, brucellosis remains endemic and difficult to control. In socioeconomically disadvantaged regions in particular, nomadic livestock practices and limited veterinary health services (e.g., lack of animal identification and registration) hinder effective disease control [22–25]. In this study, the serological presence of brucellosis was investigated in blood serum samples collected from aborting sheep and goats belonging to livestock operations practicing a nomadic lifestyle in the Southeastern Anatolia Region of Türkiye.

Materials and methods

Study area and population of study

The study was conducted using blood serum samples obtained from aborting sheep and goats submitted to Harran University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine for diagnostic purposes between the years 2022 and 2024 from the Southeastern Anatolia Region of Türkiye. A total of 343 serum samples (n = 155 goats, n = 188 sheep) were tested. Of these samples, 93 originated from Batman, 141 from Siirt, 32 from Şanlıurfa, and 77 from Şırnak, belonging to nomadic animals registered with the respective provincial agriculture directorates. Due to the animals’ nomadic lifestyle and their movement across provinces throughout the year, provinces were presented descriptively, and data were evaluated at a regional level (Fig. 1). Reference sera

As positive sera, samples obtained from five different cows that had aborted and from which B. abortus had been isolated were used. Ten sera from brucellosis-free certified farms were used as negative control sera.

Rose Bengal Plate Test (RBPT)

All serum samples were analyzed using RBPT according to the guidelines of the World Organisation for Animal Health. For the analysis, 30 µl of serum was mixed with an equal volume of antigen, and agglutination was evaluated. Results were classified as no agglutination (−), barely perceptible agglutination (+), fine agglutination with slight clearing (++), and coarse clumping with distinct clearing (+++). Serum samples found positive by i-ELISA using trisaccharide antigens but negative by RBPT were re-tested for agglutination using 75 µl serum and 25 µl RBPT antigen [1, 20].

i-ELISA using synthetic trisaccharide antigens

In the present study, an i-ELISA using synthetic trisaccharide antigens obtained from the Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA Scientific, Addlestone, UK) [14] was performed. For the i-ELISA using synthetic oligosaccharides conjugated to bovine serum albumin (BSA), the antigen (2.5 µg/ml) was diluted in carbonate coating buffer and added at 100 µl per well to flat-bottom polystyrene Maxisorp plates, followed by overnight incubation at + 4 °C. After incubation, plates were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.5% Tween 20 (PBST) and dried. Plates were then blocked for 2 h with BLOTTO solution (5% skim milk powder containing 0.5% Tween 20 in PBS). After washing, 1:100 dilutions of test and reference sera prepared in PBST were added at 100 µl per well; test sera were run in duplicate. Five positive and ten negative control sera were included, and two wells served as blanks. Plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature on a shaker set to 160 rpm. Following incubation, plates were washed four times with PBS using an automated washer. Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated recombinant Protein A/G (Pierce 32490) was added at 100 µl per well as the secondary antibody, incubated for 30 min, and washed and dried as previously described. Subsequently, 100 µl of substrate solution containing 0.015% H₂O₂ and 0.5 mg/ml ABTS (2,2′-azinobis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline sulfonic acid)) diluted in pH 4.0 buffer was added to each well. After ~ 15 min of incubation, the reaction was stopped by adding 100 µl of 1 mM sodium azide, and optical absorbance values were read at 405 nm in an ELISA reader [14]. The cut-off value was determined as the mean of negative control sera plus 3 standard deviations (SD). All positive control sera were expected to show an optical density of at least 1.00.

Statistical analysis

Pearson’s Chi-Square test was used to evaluate differences in seropositivity between provinces. Agreement between test results was assessed using the Kappa coefficient, and differences between tests were analyzed using McNemar’s test (SPSS v22.0).

Results

A total of 343 blood serum samples collected from aborted sheep and goats in Southeastern Anatolia Region were tested for brucellosis using i-ELISA with synthetic trisaccharide antigens and RBPT according to WOAH guidelines.

Of the serum samples, 85/343 tested positive by i-ELISA. In the RBPT analysis using equal volumes of serum and antigen (1:1), agglutination of varying degrees was observed in 58 serum samples. Serum samples that were positive by i-ELISA but negative by RBPT (n = 32) were reanalyzed for agglutination using 75 µl serum and 25 µl RBPT antigen (mRBPT); agglutination was detected in 14 (43.75%) of these samples, yielding a total of 72 RBPT-positive sera.

Consequently, 16.77% and 13.55% of goat sera were positive by i-ELISA and RBPT, respectively, while 31.38% and 27.13% of sheep sera tested positive by the same methods (Table 1).

This study represents one of the first serological investigations of brucellosis in nomadic sheep and goat herds with a history of abortion in the Southeastern Anatolia Region. Therefore, to increase diagnostic specificity, samples positive in both RBPT and i-ELISA were considered “serologically confirmed positive.” A total of 67 (19.53%) serum samples tested positive by both tests and were classified as confirmed positives. Considering this, 19/155 (12.26%) of goat sera and 48/188 (25.53%) of sheep sera were identified as positive (Table 1).

No statistically significant difference was found between the proportions of sera testing positive by both i-ELISA and RBPT across provinces (χ²=6.954, df = 3, p = 0.073). The highest positivity rate was detected in Şırnak (29.9%), while the lowest was in Siirt (15.6%) (Table 2).

Discussion

Brucellosis, a widespread zoonosis, is estimated to affect 2.1 million people annually worldwide and causes severe economic losses in low-income regions where ruminant farming is practiced for livelihood purposes [4, 26]. Although culture is the gold standard for diagnosis, the major causative agents of brucellosis (B. melitensis, B. abortus, B. suis) are zoonotic, have very low infectious doses, and can be transmitted via ingestion, inhalation, skin, and mucous membranes. Therefore, culture-based diagnosis requires high-biosafety laboratory conditions and experienced personnel, making it costly and hazardous [1]. For this reason, serological tests are widely used in the diagnosis of brucellosis due to their safety and cost-effectiveness.

RBPT plays an important role as a screening test for detecting brucellosis in resource-limited regions [1, 27, 28]. In recent years, the use of synthetic antigens instead of natural antigens in i-ELISA has increased the specificity of the analysis by eliminating cross-reactions caused by bacteria possessing O-polysaccharide (OPS) structures similar to those of Brucella [13, 14].

In the present study, classical RBPT and i-ELISA using synthetic trisaccharide antigens were performed. Since the animals included in the study were nomadic, their movement between provinces throughout the year limited the epidemiological representativeness at the provincial level. Therefore, the evaluation was conducted regionally, focusing on the comparison of diagnostic tests applied within the Southeastern Anatolia Region.

Dubie et al. analyzed serum samples from 300 clinically healthy animals (36 goats, 231 sheep) using RBPT and i-ELISA to investigate the seroepidemiology of brucellosis and reported a seroprevalence of 6.7%. They also showed that 20 of 34 RBPT-positive sera (6.7%) were confirmed positive by i-ELISA [29]. Ramdani et al. investigating the epidemiological situation of brucellosis in small ruminants in southeastern Algeria using RBPT, CFT, and i-ELISA, considered samples positive only when all three serological tests yielded positive reactions and found a positivity rate of 3.98% in 612 samples (332 goats, 280 sheep) [19].

In the present study, positivity was detected in 19.53% of the 343 sera (155 goats, 188 sheep) when considering samples positive by both i-ELISA and RBPT. Previous studies have reported that a history of abortion increases brucellosis positivity fivefold [19]. The high brucellosis rate found in the current study may be associated not only with the abortion history of sampled animals but also with the inability of nomadic livestock systems to access adequate veterinary services, including vaccination. This supports the comparability of the current findings with those of Ramdani et al.

i-ELISA is generally used as a confirmatory or complementary test for RBPT in brucellosis diagnosis [29]. Awais et al. detected 9.69–10.2% positivity by RBPT and 7.14% positivity by a commercial i-ELISA kit in 392 small ruminants in Pakistan; they confirmed the presence of Brucella in i-ELISA-positive sera by PCR [30]. Jamil et al. found 0.99% positivity in 1,821 sheep and goat serum samples collected near the Pakistan-Afghanistan border using RBPT and i-ELISA, reporting concordant results between the two tests but no detection of Brucella DNA by PCR [17].

In the present study, among 343 sheep and goat sera analyzed, 20.99% were positive by RBPT and 24.78% by i-ELISA. Although the Kappa coefficient showed good agreement between the tests, the McNemar test revealed a statistically significant difference between positivity rates. This suggests that i-ELISA using synthetic antigens exhibits higher sensitivity than RBPT, particularly in endemic regions. Moreover, when the findings of Jamil et al. [17] and Awais et al. [30] are considered alongside the present results, the variability in reported RBPT and i-ELISA positivity rates may be attributed to differences in antigens used. The controlled production process of synthetic antigens is expected to enhance interlaboratory standardization and provide more reliable and comparable results in field studies.

Small ruminants are preferred for livestock production in pastoral regions due to their low maintenance requirements and adaptability to harsh climatic conditions. Mixed sheep and goat farming is common in Türkiye’s Southeastern Anatolia region [28, 29, 31]. However, up-to-date data on the prevalence of brucellosis in small ruminants in this region are limited [32–35].

As highlighted by Moriyón et al. livestock production carried out under nomadic conditions complicates the control of animal movement and is one of the major challenges in combating brucellosis [28]. The high seropositivity rate (19.53%) found in nomadic small ruminants with abortion history in the present study underscores the need for region-specific brucellosis control programs in the Southeastern Anatolia Region.

Serological tests are important diagnostic tools in regions where brucellosis is endemic. However, accessible and practical tests such as tube agglutination and RBPT may yield false positives due to cross-reacting antibodies produced against Gram-negative bacteria with antigenic similarities to Brucella [7]. Legesse et al. (2023) reported that RBPT produced 16/139 (11.5%) false positives in sheep and goat sera when i-ELISA was used as the reference test [7]. In the present study, when i-ELISA using synthetic antigens was considered the reference, RBPT yielded 5/72 (6.9%) false positives. Additionally, 18 i-ELISA–positive samples were negative by RBPT. False positives may result from cross-reactions [12], whereas false negatives may be associated with antigen structure or blocking antibodies [36].

RBPT sensitivity in small ruminants is known to be influenced by the fact that the protocol was originally developed for confirmatory testing in cattle [1, 27]. In the present study, although 32 samples were i-ELISA positive and RBPT negative when tested using a 1:1 serum/antigen ratio, 14 of these samples became positive when re-evaluated using a 3:1 serum/antigen ratio. This finding highlights the need to evaluate negative RBPT results with modified RBPT, which supports the recommendation in the literature [1] that applying the modified protocol may increase the sensitivity of the RBPT.

Conclusion

The lack of effective control of animal movement and trade due to insufficient animal identification and traceability is among the major factors hindering the control of brucellosis. Therefore, brucellosis control in animals raised under a nomadic lifestyle is challenging [25, 28]. The present study demonstrates that the combined use of RBPT and synthetic antigen–based i-ELISA may enhance diagnostic accuracy and reliability under field conditions. These findings are informative for monitoring brucellosis, improving control strategies in endemic regions, and ensuring interlaboratory test compatibility.
