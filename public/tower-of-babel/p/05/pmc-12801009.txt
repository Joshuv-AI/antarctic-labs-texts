Single-Bundle Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction Using Autologous Hamstrings With Mesh and Suture Tape Augmentation With Transtibial Tubercle Fixation
Authors: Meng Wang, Yiran Wang, Hongyu Li, Hangzhou Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is the largest and strongest ligament of the knee, primarily functioning to restrict posterior tibial translation and serving as the main posterior stabilizer. However, posterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (PCLR) frequently is complicated by residual laxity and relatively high failure rates. Mesh has been widely used in clinical practice, and suture tape augmentation in PCLR has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes. In addition, the combined transtibial tuberosity fixation significantly enhances fixation strength. In this Technical Note, we describe a PCLR technique incorporating mesh augmentation, suture augmentation, and the combined transtibial tuberosity fixation. This technique may reduce graft degeneration resulting from the “killer turn” and improve posterior knee stability.

The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is the primary posterior stabilizer of the knee and plays a crucial role in maintaining knee stability. PCL injuries often are accompanied by other ligament, meniscus, and cartilage injuries, increasing symptomatic osteoarthritis risk 6.2-fold over 12 years.1 As a result, posterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (PCLR) has become the standard treatment for complete PCL ruptures, especially with other injuries. However, residual knee laxity remains common after PCLR, with subjective failure rates reported at 49.5% for isolated PCLR and 46.5% for combined procedures at 2 years postoperatively.2 In order to reduce early failure, avoid the adverse outcomes associated with revision surgery, and improve the clinical outcomes of patients after PCLR, the suture tape augmentation technique has been proposed. The authors of a cadaveric study reported that the SA-PCLR group exhibited significantly less tibial displacement under posterior tension loading compared with the standard PCLR group.3 Recent study have shown that SA-PCLR offers considerable advantages in long-term follow-up, with significant reductions in posterior tibial translation.4 However, the acute angle between the graft and the tibial tunnel at the tunnel's opening often leads to graft degeneration or even failure due to wear.5,6 Polypropylene mesh is widely used in clinical practice for hernia repair.7 Recent studies have used the mesh for the superior capsular reconstruction in patients with irreparable massive rotator cuff tears.8 Li et al.9 used mesh to reconstruct the medial and lateral collateral ligaments and the posterolateral capsule of the knee joint to restore knee stability.

In this Technical Note, we use a mesh to envelop the graft to protect the graft from wear at the "killer turn" and reduce postoperative laxity and failure. High-strength sutures are used to absorb excessive loads and protect the graft from elongation and failure during ligamentization. For tibial fixation, the combined transtibial tuberosity fixation is applied, which can increase the ultimate failure load.

Patient Positioning and Preparation

The patient is placed in the supine position. Knee stability is evaluated under general anesthesia and compared with the contralateral healthy side.

Graft Harvest and Preparation

With the knee flexed at 90°, a 2- to 3-cm oblique incision is made between the tibial tuberosity and the posteromedial edge of the tibia. The oblique incision helps reduce the potential for damage to the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve.10 The semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are bluntly dissected and subsequently harvested using a tendon stripper. Two No. 2-0 OrthoCord sutures (DePuy Mitek, Raynham, MA) are looped through a suspension device (20-mm ENDOBUTTON; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) as suture tape augmentation, and then the semitendinosus tendon and gracilis tendon are looped. Both ends of the tendons are whip-stitched with No. 2 ULTRABRAID suture (Smith & Nephew) to combine the suture and the four-strand autograft into a unit. Finally, the surface of the graft was wrapped with mesh (polypropylene mesh; Covidien, Mansfield, MA) to strengthen the PCL graft (Fig 2). Notably, the diameter of the graft increases by about 1 mm after suturing the mesh.

Graft preparation. (A) The graft with the suture tape augmentation. (B-C) The graft diameter increases by about 1 mm after suturing the mesh (arrows indicate graft diameter). (D) Overall graft length shown.

Anterolateral and Anteromedial Portal Creation

During arthroscopic exploration, inspect other intra-articular structures, evaluate the meniscus and the ACL, and debride any proliferative synovium within the joint.

Femoral Tunnel Creation

With the knee flexed at 90°, an arthroscope is inserted through the anterolateral approach. The remnant of the posterior cruciate ligament is debrided and coagulated, with attention to preserving as much of the PCL remnant as possible (Fig 3). The femoral tunnel is drilled based on the original PCL femoral footprint (Fig 4). A femoral guideset is introduced via the AM portal at an angle of 60° using a FlipCutter drill guide system (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The femoral guide is directed to the medial femoral condyle at the 1:30-o’clock position for the right knee or the 10:30-o’clock position for the left knee. A K-wire is drilled until it penetrates the medial femoral skin, followed by the creation of a tunnel using a drill along the guidewire. And the femoral bone tunnel is then enlarged according to the graft measured diameter. Finally, a guide pin is used to insert a guide wire into the bone tunnel, which is then pulled out through the anterolateral portal.

Tibial Tunnel Creation

The PCL tibial tunnel guide system is introduced via the anteromedial portal, navigating through the medial compartment to target the PCL tibial insertion site (1.0-1.5 cm below the tibial plateau). The angle of the guide is adjusted to 55° to 60°. Intraoperative radiographic examination is used to confirm the proper placement of the guide system (Fig 5). Subsequently, a hollow reamer is used to create the tibial tunnel along the guide pin according to the measured diameter of the graft. Finally, a guide wire is threaded into the tibial tunnel following the same procedure as for the femoral tunnel, and the end is pulled out through the anterolateral portal (Fig 6).

The patient is placed supine with the knee flexed to 90°. (A-B) Femoral and tibial tunnel guidewires are shown (arrows).(left side anterolateral portal)

Graft Fixation

The graft is introduced into the joint via the anterolateral approach, initially passed into the femoral tunnel using the guide wire, and fixed with an Endobutton (Smith & Nephew). Then the graft is passed through the tibial tunnel using the prepositioned guidewire (Fig 7). Before tibial fixation, the graft is cyclically tensioned for 20 cycles through full knee range of motion (ROM) to minimize creeping. Applying an anterior drawer force at 70° knee flexion, tibial fixation was applied using an interference screw (Smith & Nephew). A K-wire is used to transversely drill through the tibial tuberosity 1 cm below its apex, creating a narrow bone tunnel. The internal brace sutures are then threaded through this tunnel to the lateral side and independently secured at the tuberosity. Subsequently, the ULTRABRAID sutures (Smith & Nephew) are passed through and fixed using a FOOTPRINT Ultra PK suture anchor (4.5 mm; Smith & Nephew) (Fig 8). Finally, the graft was examined with arthroscopy and the posterior drawer test was performed (Figs 9 and 10).

Rehabilitation

Quadriceps training is started on postoperative day 1. ROM exercises start on day 2, with 0 to 30° allowed for 2 weeks. At weeks 2 to 4, a functional PCL brace is used in the unlocked position, allowing a passive ROM of 0 to 60°, and at 4 to 6 weeks, the range of motion is 0 to 90°. Thereafter, full activity is reached. Partial weight-bearing is allowed 4 weeks postoperatively; full weight-bearing is allowed 8 weeks postoperatively. Slow walking, cycling, and swimming are allowed 3 to 4 months postoperatively. Return to high-intensity sports is recommended after physician evaluation at 12 months postoperatively.

Discussion

The "killer turn" effect at the tibial tunnel is one of the primary causes of failure after posterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Jia et al. have proposed that the graft turning angle in the sagittal plane and the graft turning angle in the coronal plane together constitute the 3-dimensional "killer turn."5,11 In a postoperative assessment using magnetic resonance imaging, Park et al.12 observed that the mean signal intensity ratio of the graft's midportion was greater compared with the femoral and tibial portions, and the thickness of the midportion was also greater. It suggests that these findings may be indicative of more advanced ligamentization and vascularization during the healing process. Polypropylene mesh has been widely applied in clinical practice, such as for hernia repair, superior capsular reconstruction of the shoulder joint, repair of quadriceps tendon tears, and posterolateral capsular reconstruction of the knee joint.8,9,13,14 The mesh serves as an effective protective barrier for the graft, significantly reducing the wear impact associated with the "killer turn." In addition, mesh possesses excellent biocompatibility, and its surface microporous structure facilitates the ingrowth of new tissue. This provides mechanical support for the successful ligamentization of the PCL graft, thereby significantly enhancing the postoperative success rate.15 In their study, Mariscalco et al.16 indicate a significant correlation between increased graft diameter and improvements in patient-reported outcome scores and a reduced risk of revision surgery. Intraoperatively, we observed an approximate 1-mm increase in the diameter of the mesh-wrapped graft, which is beneficial to patients with smaller grafts by reducing failure risk. Biomechanical studies have shown that suture augmentation can significantly increase the final failure load of grafts and reduce the graft slippage.17 Some scholars suggest that the suture augmentation functions through a checkrein mechanism within this construct, rather than a load-sharing mechanism.3 Clinical studies have demonstrated that SA-PCLR can significantly improve International Knee Documentation Committee scores and reduce posterior tibial translation.4 In addition, the combined transtibial tuberosity fixation can significantly increase the failure load.18 Also, during surgery, we endeavor to preserve the PCL remnant, which acts as a "cushioning," mitigating graft wear at the turn and contributing to postoperative knee stability, graft ligamentization, and proprioceptive recovery.19,20 Disadvantages of the preservation include difficulty with arthroscopic visualization.

In this technique, we first apply mesh in PCL reconstruction to protect the graft from "killer turn" wear, reducing laxity and failure. Suture augmentation helps to absorb excessive loads, protecting the graft during its vulnerable phase. The combined transtibial tuberosity fixation provides more reliable fixation strength. The main purposes of this technique are to mitigate the wear effect of the "killer turn" on the graft and to enhance the strength of fixation, thereby decreasing the rate of graft failure.
