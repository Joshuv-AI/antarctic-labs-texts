A modified solution for evaluating the sliding direction and stability of 3D slopes
Authors: Feng Zhang, Pingting Dong, Jiaping Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we present an improved and efficient analytical solution for evaluating the stability of 3D slopes based on the minimum potential energy method. The equilibrium equation of the landslide is employed to determine the shear stress on the failure surface, and a new formulation for shear potential energy is introduced. The vector-combined direction of the force acting on the landslide is considered equivalent to the sliding direction (SD). Two classic examples are reanalyzed to validate the accuracy and performance of the proposed method. The analysis demonstrates that the obtained results align well with reference solutions and tend to be conservative in engineering applications. Parameter investigations are conducted to explore the influences of SD, shear strength parameters, slope angle, and soil unit weight on the safety factor (SF) of 3D slopes. The findings indicate that a larger SD corresponds to a less stable slope. Finally, the effects of optimization parameters, landslide volume, and surcharge on the location of the failure surface, SF, and SD are also examined. The developed analytical approach offers a novel and straightforward solution with sufficient accuracy and eliminates the need for iteration in determining the SF, making it a valuable complementary method for assessing the stability of 3D slopes.Keywords: Sliding direction, Safety factor, Failure surface, Minimum potential energy principle, Shear potential energy

Introduction

The stability of tunnels, underground structures, slopes, and other engineering projects has become a key research focus in geotechnical engineering [1], [2], [3], [4]. In recent years, the expansion of human activities and the increase in construction projects have led to frequent slope instabilities, drawing significant attention from scholars [5], [6]. The development of accurate and high-performance methods for evaluating the stability of slopes are one of the most important research topics, and are of great importance in the geotechnical engineering field [7], [8]. With the efforts of many scientists, several methods have been proposed for stability assessment of slopes and have shown good performance in practice, such as limit equilibrium method (LEM) [9], limit analysis method [10], [11] and advanced numerical analysises [2], [12], [13], [14], [15]. The above methods assess the stability of 3D slopes by identifying the location of the critical failure surface and calculating the corresponding SF.

Generally, SF is an essential index for assessing the stability of 3D slopes [16], [17]. To the best of the authors' knowledge, there are three types of methods commonly applied for defining the SF. One of the categories uses the ratio of the resistance to the mobilized shear stress as SF [18]. The other type defines SF based on the concept of strength reserve [19], [20]. To summarize, the soil strength parameters are reduced to meet the ultimate equilibrium state, and the corresponding reduction coefficient is regarded as SF [21]. Although the above-mentioned ways of defining the SF are widely accepted, the vector characteristics of the force are ignored in calculation of SF [22], [23]. To overcome the limitations of the above methods in the definition of SF, some scholars have proposed the vector sum method from the basic concept of force [24], [25], [26]. This method treats the self-weight of landslide and external loading as the slip force, and the cohesion, friction of soil and the normal force on the failure surface as the resistance. SF is defined as the ratio of the anti-slip force to the projection of the sliding force in SD [21], [27]. There is other method used for safety factor assessment which is based on the gravity increase, and the details can be found in the existing literature [28], [29].

Zhang et al. [30] believes that the physical significance of the above mentioned definition of SF is clearer because the directionality of force is considered. Referring to some published literature [20], [31], it is necessary to obtain the stress state on the failure surface for evaluating the slope stability using the vector sum method. In general, the vector sum method cannot give an analytical solution for the stresses on failure surface, and the finite element method is usually applied to solve it [30]. As far as author knows, the engineers on the site have varying levels of expertise. Although the application of the finite element method in engineering has been very mature, it is not easy to master for engineers. Therefore, a convenient method needs to be developed to directly solve for the stresses on the failure surface. Fortunately, the minimum potential energy method proposed by Mauldon and Ureta [32] can determine the stresses on the failure surface without iteration. Recent studies have indicated that this method has sufficient accuracy in the stability assessment of 3D slopes [33], [34]. Nevertheless, authors think that there are two aspects that deserve further study. One of them is that the shear potential energy model needs to be further optimized because the results obtained from the existing computational models overestimate the slope stability compared with the reference solution. Although the minimum potential energy method performs well in practical engineering [35], the obtained results are conservative for engineering. As noted in some studies [17], [20], [36], [37], while conservative stability assessments can reduce costs in slope construction, reinforcement, and other areas, they inadvertently increase the risk of slope instability, which is detrimental to slope safety. For another, the calculation of sliding direction needs to be refined. In the previous studies [33], [35], SD of landslide is determined by the direction of synthesis of resistance. However, this model ignores the effect of external force acting on the slip body on SD. Therefore, further research on these two aspects is crucial for advancing the theory of minimum potential energy slope stability analysis.

In this paper, a novel analytical solution for evaluating SD and stability of 3D slopes is developed using the minimum potential energy method. Firstly, a new model related to the shear potential energy is proposed. Subsequently, the moving direction of landslide is determined by the resultant force of the forces acting on the landslide. Then, SD is defined as the angle between the moving direction and the positive direction of x axis. Finally, SF is determined by the ratio of the projection of resistance to the sliding force on SD. Compared with the existing methods, the novelties and contributions of the present work are illustrated as follows: (1) Based on the existing researches, the shear force on the failure surface is solved by the equilibrium equation of landslide in the horizontal direction, and then a new shear potential energy model is established; and (2) The landslide is taken as the study object, and the direction of the vector sum of external load, normal force and the ultimate shear force on the failure surface is selected as the moving direction of 3D slopes. Finally, both SD and SF are calculated.

The structure of the manuscript is listed as follows. The implementation process of the present work is described in Section 2. Subsequently, two examples are used for testing the performance and effectiveness of the developed method in Section 3. Finally, some significant conclusions are summarized in Section 4.

Some scholars have proved that the above simplicity performs well in practical applications and have achieved better results [44], [49], [50]. Thus, the shape of the critical slip surface is considered as a rotating ellipsoid with reference to the existing results [35], [49]. 3D critical failure surface of example 2.

The minimum SF is 0.940 using the proposed method, and the corresponding SD is equal to 58.216∘, which is in line with SF=1.026 gained by Yamagami and Jiang [59]. The relative error is only 2.6%, and which indicates that the present method can be applied for determining the critical failure surface of 3D slopes. Moreover, Kalatehjari et al. [60] gives the solution as SF=0.95 using the limit equilibrium method with the particle swarm algorithm. By comparison, the result from the present work is also reasonable. Simultaneously, the authors conclude that the discrepancy between the SF calculated by the present approach and the rest of methods is induced by many factors, such as optimization algorithm, assumptions used in the analysis method and failure mechanism. Yamagami and Jiang [59] uses the dynamic programming algorithm and Kalatehjari et al. [60] adopts the particle swarm algorithm, while the GA is selected in this paper. As described by Li et al. [61], SFs are generally different because the strategies of the algorithms are different to seek the optimal solution. Wan et al. [62] concluded in a recent study that the different results are obtained even for the identical failure surface because of different assumptions of the analytical model. The listed reference solutions are resulted from the limit equilibrium method, and it assumes that the landslide is non-deformable. On the contrary, we assume that the interface between landslide mass and slip bed is elastic in this study. Finally, a reason not to be ignored is that the failure mechanism of the 3D slope will affect the shape and location of the critical failure surface, and then the different SFs will be got. It is undeniable that the SF obtained by the method in this paper is less than the reference solution under the requirement of satisfying the accuracy, which is beneficial for engineering.

Fig. 11(a)-(c) display the critical failure surface of the 3D slope when q is chosen to be 20 kPa, 30 kPa and 40 kPa, respectively, and its projection in the xoz plane is illustrated in Fig. 11(d). From Fig. 11(a)-(c), we can conclude that the transverse width increases as q increases. Meanwhile, x2 gradually approaches to the slope shoulder with the enlargement of q, and it can be clearly observed in Fig. 11(d). Also, x1 is always near the slope toe regardless of the value of q. Although x1 gradually increases as q decreases, the effect of q on x1 is much larger than x2. Furthermore, the depth of failure surface (i.e., the vertical distance from slope surface to failure surface) gradually increases as q increases, which means that the area of failure surface will be enlarged. The implication is that the increased area of failure surface will allow the soil to provide more shear resistance against slope failure when q is larger. It cannot be overlooked that the failure extent of CSS gradually expands as q increases. The author attributes this to the following reasons: with the increase in q, the mobilized shear stress on the sliding surface also increases. For the slope to remain stable, the potential slip surface must provide greater shear resistance. Clearly, the shear resistance of the soil not only increases with higher shear strength indices (i.e., c and ϕ) but also with an expanded failure surface area. Thus, as q increases, the failure extent of the potential sliding surface must expand to enhance shear resistance for stabilizing the slope.

Conclusions

To address the issue of overestimating slope stability in the existing minimum potential energy method and further improve the theory, this paper proposes improvements related to shear potential energy and SD of the sliding mass. Specifically, the shear force on the failure surface is determined using the static equilibrium equation in the horizontal direction of the sliding body, and the moving direction v of landslide mass is considered as the resultant vector of all forces acting on the landslide. Then, SD is determined according to the angle between v and the positive direction of x-axis. To test the performance of the present study, three benchmark cases are employed to validate the effectiveness and accuracy of the proposed method. Some significant conclusions are listed below:

•The developed analytical method without iteration is in line with the results from the limit equilibrium method. Meanwhile, the proposed solution is safer for engineering compared to other approaches, while still satisfying accuracy requirements.

The developed analytical method without iteration is in line with the results from the limit equilibrium method. Meanwhile, the proposed solution is safer for engineering compared to other approaches, while still satisfying accuracy requirements.

•There is an inherent relationship between SF and SD, that is, the larger SF, the smaller SD. Also, c and ϕ perform a positive role in slope stability, while q, γ and θ promote slope instability. More interestingly, SF and SD remain almost constant at b/a ≥ 5.

There is an inherent relationship between SF and SD, that is, the larger SF, the smaller SD. Also, c and ϕ perform a positive role in slope stability, while q, γ and θ promote slope instability. More interestingly, SF and SD remain almost constant at b/a ≥ 5.

•The combination of the present method and GA for determining the critical failure surface of 3D slopes has a good convergence. Also, parametric studies show that the optimization parameters will have an important influence on the slope stability as well as SD, while the minimum SF is given as the global optimal solution in this study.

The combination of the present method and GA for determining the critical failure surface of 3D slopes has a good convergence. Also, parametric studies show that the optimization parameters will have an important influence on the slope stability as well as SD, while the minimum SF is given as the global optimal solution in this study.

•The spatial characteristic of sliding mass and the position of CSS determined by the method presented in this paper closely align with field measurements, demonstrating the method's applicability to the stability assessment of actual slope engineering projects. Furthermore, the enhancements made to the minimum potential energy method are validated as reasonable.

The spatial characteristic of sliding mass and the position of CSS determined by the method presented in this paper closely align with field measurements, demonstrating the method's applicability to the stability assessment of actual slope engineering projects. Furthermore, the enhancements made to the minimum potential energy method are validated as reasonable.

The conclusion drawn in this paper—that ‘the greater the SD in the sliding direction, the higher the likelihood of slope instability’—is of universal significance. However, it raises an important question: Is there a quantitative mathematical relationship between SD in the sliding direction and the probability of slope instability? Developing such a relationship is a problem that warrants thorough investigation and research. Addressing this issue would significantly enhance the application of analytical methods in geotechnical engineering and contribute meaningfully to the development of the minimum potential energy theory in geotechnical stability analysis.
