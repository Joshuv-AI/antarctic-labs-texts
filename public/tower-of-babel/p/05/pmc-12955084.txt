Hyaluronic acid engineered melanin-MOF nanoreactor synergistically remodeling redox and immune homeostasis for targeted acute lung injury therapy
Authors: Yun Qu, Jingjing Zhang, Haixia Ou, Yuting Yan, Ruiping Zhang, Jinghua Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReactive oxygen species (ROS)-triggered oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and overactivation of immune cells are critical factors exacerbating acute lung injury (ALI). Conventional clinical therapies are often limited by non-specificity, single-target limitations, and significant side effects to precisely modulate the complex pathological microenvironment. Here, we designed a hyaluronic acid-engineered nanoreactor (HA@MZ) through in situ encapsulation of melanin (MA) within ZIF-67, allowing multi-target synergistic therapy of ALI. The HA coating facilitates mucus penetration and targeted accumulation in injured lungs, exhibiting good biocompatibility. Under acidic inflammatory conditions, HA@MZ responsively releases MA, which efficiently scavenges ROS. This process activates Nrf2/HO-1 and GPX4 pathways while inhibiting NF-κB driven inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization, leading to reduced lipid peroxidation and recovery of redox and immune balance. Both in vitro and in vivo results confirm the lung microenvironment-balancing capacity of HA@MZ, offering a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for ALI and other inflammatory diseases.Keywords: Acute lung injury, Targeted therapy, ROS scavenging, Melanin

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a severe respiratory disorder in critical care medicine, clinically characterized by progressive hypoxemia and diffuse inflammation [1,2]. If not treated promptly, it can further develop into acute respiratory distress syndrome with a mortality rate of up to 40%. Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) plays a central role in the pathogenesis and progression of ALI [3]. On one hand, excess ROS can disrupt the tight junctions between endothelial cells and the alveolar-capillary barrier, directly inducing oxidative damage [4]. More importantly, ROS activates the NF-κB pathway, upregulates the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, and leads to massive infiltration of alveolar macrophages (AMs) into the lungs [5]. As the dominant immune cells controlling the initiation and recovery phases of ALI, AMs undergo excessive M1 polarization upon Toll-like receptor stimulation, releasing large quantities of inflammatory cytokines and recruiting neutrophils into the alveoli [6,7]. This results in the continuous excessive production of ROS and oxidative stress, exacerbating the inflammatory cascade. The overactivation of M1-type AMs and ROS further downregulates the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [8]. This leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides (e.g., 4-HNE), which further exacerbates lung injury. Ultimately, oxidative stress, macrophage polarization, and lipid peroxidation form a mutually reinforcing vicious cycle that collectively drives ALI progression and disrupts both redox balance and immune homeostasis in the pulmonary microenvironment. Therefore, scavenging ROS to intervene in oxidative stress and AMs polarization reprogramming is crucial to restore pulmonary immune homeostasi for ALI therapy.

Currently, clinical management of ALI primarily relies on mechanical ventilation, anti-inflammatory, or antioxidant agents to alleviate symptoms [9,10]. However, the therapeutic efficacy for ALI is significantly compromised by limitations such as ventilator-induced lung injury, non-specificity, singular treatment targets, low cellular uptake, rapid metabolism, and substantial side effects [[11], [12], [13]]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop targeted drug strategies against disrupting ROS-mediated cascade reactions for multi-targeted treatment of ALI.

As an endogenous natural substance in the human body, melanin (MA) exhibits excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and modifiability, thus being widely employed in the drug delivery platforms [14,15]. Notably, its abundant reductive functional groups and π-electron characteristics enable MA to efficiently counteract various ROS and nitrogen species, demonstrating outstanding broad-spectrum ROS scavenging capacity [16,17]. Extensive studies have confirmed the efficacy of MA in treating multiple inflammatory diseases (such as ALI, colitis, and rheumatoid arthritis) through biological effects including antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and regulation of macrophage polarization [[18], [19], [20]]. However, the non-targeting and small size of MA prevents it from effectively penetrating the pulmonary mucus barrier. Consequently, their therapeutic potential is constrained by a short pulmonary residence time and inadequate permeability [21].

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), serving as drug reservoirs, leverage their structurally tunable, porous architecture, and high drug-loading capacity to enhance the utilization of MA in target organs [22,23]. ZIF-67 (a classic member of the MOF family) can achieve preferential passive targeting to injured sites through leaky blood vessels following intratracheal administration [24]. Simultaneously, ZIF-67 responds to the acidic microenvironment of ALI inflammation, enabling precise controlled release of drug, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [25]. More importantly, chemical modification can further confer ZIF-67 with precise targeting capability towards macrophages, which is particularly advantageous for macrophage-mediated inflammatory diseases [26,27]. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a linear glycosaminoglycan composed of repeating disaccharide units of N-acetylglucosamine and glucuronic acid, is naturally present in the extracellular matrix [28]. It exhibits high specificity for the cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44) receptor, which is overexpressed on M1-type macrophages [29]. Based on this, we propose that nanoreactor targeted to macrophages (CD44 target) can reach the lung injury site, where they locally scavenge ROS while synergistically inhibiting oxidative stress and M1 macrophage polarization. This approach may provide a comprehensive protective mechanism for prognosis of ALI.

Herein, this study developed a HA-engineered MA-MOF nanoreactor (HA@MZ) for the treatment of inflammatory cascade in ALI. HA@MZ was constructed by chemically modifying HA onto the ZIF-67 framework loaded MA (MA-ZIF). ZIF-67 and HA serve as inert delivery platforms, while MA is the core therapeutic component. HA@MZ effectively breaks the ROS-mediated vicious cycle by efficiently scavenging excess ROS, significantly alleviating oxidative stress, inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and the cytokine storm, thereby attenuating lipid peroxidation. Ultimately, it restores pulmonary redox and immune homeostasis (Fig. 1). This nanoreactor possesses the following advantages: (1) As a nanocarrier, ZIF-67 enables efficient and stable loading of MA. Following intratracheal administration, it enhances pulmonary accumulation, retention time, and bioavailability of MA through passive targeting. (2) The HA shell provides a charge shielding effect and specific recognition of CD44, thereby improving mucus penetration and enabling hierarchical targeted delivery from the organ level to macrophages. (3) Under acidic inflammatory conditions, HA@MZ enables site-specific release of MA, which activates the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway while suppressing the NF-κB pathway. This dual regulation synergistically inhibits oxidative stress and M1 macrophage polarization, thereby significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, HA@MZ provides a promising strategy to overcome the limitations of clinical ALI treatment, such as non-specificity and single targeting.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, AR) and 2-methylimidazole (C4H6N2, AR) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Hyaluronic acid (HA, AR, molecular weight: 10000) was bought from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and melanin raw material were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. DPPH was sourced from TCI Chemicals. CCK-8, hydroxyl radical (•OH), and superoxide anion (•O2−) assay kits were supplied by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. Assay kits for SOD, GSH, CAT, MDA, FITC, JC-1, and ROS were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science&Technology Co., Ltd. The lipid peroxidation probe BODIPY 581/591C11 was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology. The ferrous ion (Fe2+) assay kit was sourced from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. Mouse enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, along with anti-CD11b, anti-Ly6G, anti-F4/80, anti-CD86, and anti-CD206, were provided by BioLegend. WB antibodies P65, p-p65, Nrf2, HO-1, GPX4, and β-actin were obtained from Immunoway Biotechnology Company. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis and characterization of HA@MZ

To synthesize MA-ZIF, two separate solutions were first prepared. Solution A contained 0.3 g of Co(NO3)2·6H2O in 40 mL of methanol. Solution B contained 0.3 g of 2-methylimidazole in 40 mL of methanol. Solution B was then added dropwise to solution An under magnetic stirring. After 5 min of mixing, 10 mg of MA was introduced. The reaction was continued for 120 min at room temperature. The resulting particles were collected by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with absolute ethanol, and lyophilized. Subsequently, an aqueous solution of MA-ZIF (15 mg mL−1) was mixed with HA solution (2 mg mL−1) and the pH was maintained at 7. The reaction was carried out at 25 °C for 30 min. The final product (HA@MZ) was collected by centrifugation and lyophilized.

The morphologies of the nanoreactors were observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-100CXII, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and fourier transform infrared spectrometry (FTIR) measurements were carried out on Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250xi (UK) system and BRUKER TENSOR 27 spectrometer, respectively. The pore structure was obtained on ASAP 2020 Plus apparatus. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was conducted on a Japanese Physiology Rigaku-D/max-2005 X-ray Diffractometer. Thermogravimetric analysis was performed using NETZSCHTG209F3, Germany. Zeta potential and particle size were determined on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90. Drug release was explored by UV spectrophotometry (UV; JH 756 MC, China).

Cell culture

MLE-12 (RRID: CVCL_3751) and Raw 264.7 (RRID: CVCL_0493) cells were obtained from Shanghai Academy of Biosciences. MLE-12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Raw 264.7 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. All the cell lines used in study are free from contamination.

Mucus penetration and cell uptake

Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After treatment with LPS for 2 h, the cells were incubated with FITC-labeled MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ (concentration: 50 μg mL−1) for 6, 8, and 10 h, respectively. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with DAPI for nuclei visualization. Cellular internalization was observed using confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan). To further quantify cellular uptake, cells were scraped and collected by centrifugation. Fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry (FCM).

Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h. A simulated mucus layer (mucin/sodium chloride solution, mass ratio 3:9) was added to the wells, followed by incubation with FITC-labeled HA@MZ for 4, 6, 8, and 10 h, respectively. After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were acquired using CLSM.

In vitro ROS scavenging of HA@MZ

Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and stimulated with LPS for 2 h to establish the cellular model. The cells were then treated with MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA probe for 30 min, followed by DAPI staining. Intracellular ROS levels were evaluated using fluorescence microscopy and FCM.

Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity

Raw 264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ for 24 h, followed by three PBS washes and cell collection. Cellular antioxidant capacity was assessed by measuring the levels of MDA, SOD, and CAT with corresponding biochemical assay kits. Similarly, the levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-10 (IL-10)and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in the collected cell culture supernatant were determined with commercial mouse ELISA kits.

Macrophage polarization regulation of HA@MZ

Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and stimulated with LPS for 2 h to establish the cellular model. The cells were then treated separately with MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were collected by centrifugation, and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C with the following fluorescently-labeled antibodies: APC-conjugated anti-F4/80, PE-conjugated anti-CD86, and BV421-conjugated anti-CD206. Following antibody staining, the cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by FCM.

Model and treatment of ALI

All animal procedures were performed under protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. 2022-025, Taiyuan, China) and complied with ethical regulations. (1) We standardized the mouse strain (male BALB/c mice) and age (6-8 weeks). Throughout the experiment, a sample size of 8 mice per group was used. This sample size ensured sufficient statistical power to detect differences between groups and reduced the impact of individual variability. Tracheal instillation was performed by the same skilled operator using a standardized procedure. At the same time, the LPS dose was standardized according to body weight and the mice were randomly grouped to minimize individual differences. (2) The anesthetized mice were securely fixed at a fixed angle with their limbs taped together to prevent movement and straighten the airway. (3) The mouse tongue was clamped with forceps and pulled outward as much as possible to expose the glottis. A cold light was placed on the mouse's neck to observe the tracheal opening before inserting the injection needle. (4) The marked indwelling needle was inserted along the glottis, ensuring a consistent needle insertion depth. An acute lung injury model was induced by instilling LPS (5 mg kg−1). After completion, the mice were left on the animal intubation stand for 2 min to ensure fluid flow into the lungs. Two hours later, mice were treated with 50 μL of MA, MA-ZIF, or HA@MZ (2 mg kg−1 based on MA) via intratracheal infusion using the same method. In the control group, mice underwent the same tracheal instillation procedure, but were instilled with an equal volume of sterile PBS instead of LPS. All animals were sacrificed 24 h later.

In vivo biodistribution

Following the establishment of the model as described above, mice were treated with MA, MA-ZIF, or HA@MZ via tracheal administration. At 24 h post-treatment, in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using NIR-II fluorescence imaging system. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging.

Lung wet/dry (W/D) weight ratio

Following euthanasia, lung tissues were harvested, washed in saline, and blotted dry for wet weight measurement. They were then dried at 65 °C for 48 h to obtain the constant dry weight. The lung W/D ratio was subsequently calculated.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis

BALF was collected from euthanized mice and centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was harvested for pro-inflammatory cytokine quantification by ELISA to determine the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10 and TNF-α. The cell pellet was resuspended for total cell counting. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with FITC-conjugated anti CD11b and APC-conjugated anti Ly6G antibodies for 15 min, and the proportion of neutrophils in BALF was analyzed by FCM.

Lung tissue homogenate analysis

Lung tissue samples (100 mg) were homogenized in 1 mL of extraction buffer using an ice bath. The homogenates were centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were collected. The levels of MDA, SOD, CAT, GSH, and Fe2+ were measured according to the instructions provided with the respective commercial assay kits.

Histological analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, pathological changes in the lungs and other major organs were evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Intracellular ROS levels in lung sections were detected by dihydroethidium (DHE) staining. Lipid peroxidation was assessed via immunohistochemical staining of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE). Furthermore, immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed to evaluate macrophage polarization and oxidative stress regulation by examining the expression of CD86, CD163, Ly6G, Nrf2, HO-1, and ZO-1.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). We assessed the normality of the data. For data sets that satisfied the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons among groups. When the normality assumption was not met, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied instead. The significance levels were defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and***P < 0.001; "ns" denotes not significant. The fluorescence intensity in all experiments was quantified using Image J software.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of HA@MZ

A one-step, in situ self-assembly approach was employed to encapsulate MA within ZIF-67, forming MA-ZIF with guaranteed loading efficiency and homogeneity. In this study, the amount of HA in the reaction system was 2 mg. We investigated the morphology and antioxidant properties of HA@MZ at MA of 5 mg, 10 mg, and 15 mg. As shown in Fig. S1a, when the MA ratio increases to 7.5:1, the crystal structure of HA@MZ was destroyed, and polyhedra could not be formed. Fig. S1b shows that the antioxidant properties of HA@MZ increase with increasing MA loading. However, when the MA ratio increases to 7.5:1, the antioxidant properties decrease due to structural destruction. Therefore, a MA:HA ratio of 5:1 was ultimately selected as the optimal ratio. The encapsulation rate of MA-ZIF is 93%. As shown in Table S1, the particle size and encapsulation efficiency of HA@MZ remained relatively stable across different production batches. Furthermore, storing HA@MZ at 25 °C in a desiccator for 3 months maintained good activity. This composite was further functionalized with HA into a targeted nanoreactor (Fig. 2a), designed for a cascaded targeting mechanism and precise MA release at the pulmonary injury site.

The morphologies and particle sizes of ZIF-67, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ were systematically investigated by TEM. As shown in Fig. 2b, ZIF-67 exhibits a regular and uniform polyhedral structure with an average diameter of approximately 2.2 μm. After loading with MA (Fig. S2), the framework of MA-ZIF was altered, resulting in a reduced particle size of about 801 nm and a rougher surface. The changes in the ZIF-67 framework structure caused by MA mainly stem from coordination competition and hydrogen bonding between MA and the ZIF-67 precursor. HA@MZ maintains the polyhedral morphology, with a distinct HA polymer layer observable on its surface and a particle size increased to 902 nm. These morphological changes confirm the successful HA engineering. EDS mapping confirms the homogeneous distribution of C, O, Co, and N elements on HA@MZ (Fig. 2c and S3). DLS analysis further reveals that the hydrodynamic diameter of HA@MZ increases by approximately 232.9 nm compared to MA-ZIF. This size increment was attributed to the HA coating, which was consistent with the TEM results (Fig. S4).

The XRD pattern of ZIF-67 exhibits characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 10.5°, 12.8°, 14.8°, and 18.1°, which correspond to the (011), (002), (112), and (222) crystal planes, respectively, indicating its high crystallinity (Fig. 2e). The peak positions of both MA-ZIF and HA@MZ remain highly consistent with those of ZIF-67. It suggests that MA loading and HA modification had no effect on the crystal structure of ZIF-67. XPS survey spectra confirm that HA@MZ is composed of C, N, O, and Co atoms (Fig. 2f). The high-resolution Co 2p spectrum shows characteristic peaks at binding energies of 781.4 eV (Co(III) 2p3/2), 797.2 eV (Co(III) 2p1/2), 785.7 eV (Co(II) 2p3/2), and 802.9 eV (Co(II) 2p1/2) (Fig. 2g) [30]. In the FTIR spectrum, MA-ZIF not only maintains the typical Co–N bond (691 cm−1) and the C=C stretching of imidazole ring (1423 cm−1) but also displays a new absorption band at 1623 cm−1, attributed to the C=N stretching vibration of MA (Fig. 2h). For HA@MZ, the appearance of new characteristic peaks at 1032 cm−1 (C–O) and 2929 cm−1 (–CH2) further confirms the successful coating of HA polymer [31].

The thermal stability and composition of ZIF-67, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ are investigated by TG (Fig. 2i). ZIF-67 and MA-ZIF show final weight losses of 36.9% and 44.7%, respectively. The increased weight loss in MA-ZIF is attributed to the successful encapsulation of a substantial amount MA within the ZIF-67 framework. Compared to MA-ZIF, HA@MZ exhibits a further increased weight loss of 54.8%. This decreased thermal stability originates from the decomposition of HA polymer, and the observed difference further confirms the presence of HA coating. As shown in Fig. 2j and k, ZIF-67, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ all exhibit type I N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms, indicating the presence of abundant microporous structures (Table S2) [32]. ZIF-67 possesses a high specific surface area of 1662 m2 g−1, highlighting its significant advantage as an efficient carrier for MA. After MA loading, the specific surface area of MA-ZIF decreases to 778 m2 g−1, suggesting that MA incorporation occupies part of the micropores and providing additional evidence for the successful in situ loading of MA within ZIF-67. During HA engineering, the HA polymers on HA@MZ partially shielded its microporous structure, leading to a slight decrease in the specific surface area (SBET = 550 m2 g−1). This observation further confirmed the presence of HA polymer. All these results collectively demonstrate the successful loading of MA into HA@MZ and its functionalization with HA, forming an effective nanoreactor.

Owing to the modification with negatively charged MA and HA, HA@MZ exhibits a ZETA potential of −33.4 mV, which facilitates its penetration through the pulmonary mucus layer (Fig. 2l) [33]. During the progression of ALI, substantial ROS generation leads to a locally acidic microenvironment. In response to this, the pH-responsive drug release behavior and antioxidant capacity of HA@MZ were further investigated. Due to the non-covalent coordination between Co2+ and 2-methylimidazole, HA@MZ demonstrates both pH sensitivity and biodegradability under acidic conditions. As shown in Fig. 2d and m, the cumulative release of MA from HA@MZ in an acidic environment (pH = 6.5) reaches 74%, significantly higher than that under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), which is conducive to enhancing the accumulation and therapeutic efficacy of MA at inflammatory sites. It is worth emphasizing that, benefiting from the abundant reducing groups in both MA and HA, HA@MZ exhibits concentration-dependent scavenging activity against DPPH•, •O2−, and •OH. At a concentration of 100 μg mL−1, the scavenging rates reach 89.6%, 78.5%, and 85.6%, respectively (Fig. 2n). These results demonstrate the excellent potential of HA@MZ in maintaining pulmonary redox homeostasis.

Mucin permeation and targeted uptake abilities of HA@MZ

Tracheal drug administration represents an effective strategy for enhancing pulmonary drug delivery efficiency. Therefore, evaluating the ability of the nanoreactor to effectively penetrate the lung mucus barrier is crucial for therapeutic efficacy. The mucosal penetration depth of HA@MZ was measured at different time points in simulated mucus. Results demonstrated that at 4 h, the penetration depth reached 25.5 μm, which was 4.6-fold greater than that at 1 h (Fig. 3a and b). This confirms that the negative surface charge of HA@MZ facilitates efficient mucus penetration through “charge repulsion” and “reduced adsorption” mechanisms [34]. Prior to functional assessment at the cellular level, the cytotoxicity of HA@MZ was evaluated via MTS assay. Cell viability of both Raw 264.7 and MLE-12 cells remained above 80% even at HA@MZ concentrations up to 100 μg mL−1, indicating favorable cytocompatibility (Fig. 3f). As shown in Fig. S5, the hemolysis rate of HA@MZ was below 5% within the tested concentration range, laying a foundation for in vitro and in vivo applications. Subsequently, we further examined the mucus-penetrating ability of HA@MZ by observing its uptake by Raw 264.7 cells through artificially simulated mucus (Fig. 3c). FITC-labeled HA@MZ exhibits time-dependent accumulation and high fluorescence intensity in Raw 264.7 cells, indicating its effective mucus-penetrating ability and highlighting its potential as a targeted nanomedicine for lung diseases (Fig. 3d and S6). Given the critical role of macrophages in the progression of ALI, we investigated the uptake of HA@MZ by inflammatory macrophages. CLSM images revealed that MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ were all internalized by Raw 264.7 cells to varying degrees over time (Fig. 3g). However, at identical time points, MA-ZIF exhibits stronger green fluorescence intensity than MA (Fig. S7), indicating that ZIF-67 nanocarrier enhances the passive endocytosis of MA. Notably, HA@MZ demonstrates the highest fluorescence intensity, which was 4.5-fold and 2.5-fold greater than that of MA and MA-ZIF, respectively (Fig. 3h), suggesting that the HA coating strategy confers high affinity between MA-ZIF and Raw 264.7 cells. This observation was further confirmed by flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 3i). Subsequently, the internalization of HA@MZ in macrophages was compared with that in MLE-12 cells. The results show that the fluorescence intensity in Raw 264.7 cells was 3.5 times that in MLE-12 cells, indicating significantly higher uptake. This further confirms the targeting properties of HA@MZ in Raw 264.7 cells (Fig. S8). The significantly enhanced cellular uptake is attributed to the specific targeting of CD44 receptors, which are overexpressed on M1-type macrophages, by HA ligands on MA-ZIF surface [35]. To further confirm the overexpression and targeting effects of CD44, LPS-stimulated Raw 264.7 cells were co-incubated with FITC-labeled HA@MZ and different concentrations of free HA for 4 h. Cell uptake efficiency is shown in Fig. S9. Flow cytometry results show that uptake efficiency decreases with increasing HA concentration, indicating that free HA competitively reduces the uptake of HA@MZ by Raw 264.7 cells. At a HA concentration of 50 μg/mL, cellular uptake decreases to 57.9%. Increasing the HA concentration to 100 μg/mL results in a significant decrease in uptake efficiency to 13.4%, indicating that most sites were occupied by free HA. These results suggest that CD44 is specifically expressed on the surface of LPS-stimulated Raw 264.7 cells, and that HA@MZ is specifically taken up by Raw 264.7 cells via CD44 receptor mediation.

Effective ROS scavenging of HA@MZ in vitro

Oxidative stress triggered by excessive ROS is a critical factor influencing the progression of ALI. Efficient ROS scavenging has the potential to mitigate the severe damage of ALI by breaking the vicious cycle. We established an inflammatory model using LPS-stimulated Raw 264.7 cells to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of HA@MZ (Fig. 3e). As shown in Fig. 3j, a markedly higher fluorescence intensity was observed in Raw 264.7 cells after ROS stimulation compared with the control group, confirming the successful establishment of the model [36]. Treatment with MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ reduced intracellular ROS levels to varying degrees. Among them, HA@MZ exhibits the strongest ROS scavenging ability, which can be attributed to its efficient targeting of Raw 264.7 cells and ROS elimination activity of both HA shell and encapsulated MA. Similar results were obtained by quantitative fluorescence analysis (Fig. S10) and flow cytometry (Fig. 3k). SOD and CAT work cooperatively to catalyze the conversion of highly toxic superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, thereby maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis [37,38]. To further evaluate the antioxidant effect of HA@MZ, the levels of SOD and CAT in treated Raw 264.7 cells were measured (Fig. 3l and m). The results show that SOD and CAT activities were significantly decreased in the LPS group. However, HA@MZ treatment resulted in a more pronounced recovery of both enzyme levels compared to the other treatment groups. Meanwhile, HA@MZ also exhibits an indirect protective effect on MLEC cells (Fig. S11). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HA@MZ possesses an excellent ability to suppress oxidative stress and protect cells from ROS damage.

Inhibition of macrophage M1 polarization and lipid peroxidation by HA@MZ

Excessive ROS generation and the resulting oxidative stress response promote the polarization of Raw 264.7 cells toward the M1 phenotype, leading to an inflammatory cytokine storm that damages lung tissue [39]. In contrast, M2 Raw 264.7 cells can promote inflammation resolution and tissue repair [40]. Therefore, reducing ROS levels to regulate macrophage polarization plays a crucial role in alleviating the progression of ALI. To evaluate the phenotypic regulation ability and anti-inflammatory effect of HA@MZ on Raw 264.7 cells, the aforementioned cellular inflammatory model and treatment methods were adopted (Fig. 4a and b). The expression levels of CD86 and CD206 in Raw 264.7 cells were detected by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 4c, the level of M1 Raw 264.7 cells significantly increases (42.1%) after LPS stimulation. Notably, CD86 level in the group treated with HA@MZ (14.9%) was lower than that in other treatment groups, indicating that HA@MZ could effectively inhibit M1 polarization of Raw 264.7 cells. In addition, ELISA results show that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) are the highest in LPS group (Fig. 4d‒f). After treatment with MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ, the levels of these cytokines were reduced to varying degrees, with HA@MZ exhibiting the most significant therapeutic effect. These results demonstrate that HA@MZ shows the ability to reprogram macrophages and effectively reduce the pro-inflammatory mediators, which was attributed to its excellent macrophage targeting capability and MA responsive release.

To verify the regulatory mechanism of HA@MZ on lipid peroxidation induced by LPS-triggered inflammatory responses, relevant indicators were further detected. The formation of lipid reactive oxygen species is a key event in the final stage of ALI [41]. After LPS stimulation, Raw 264.7 cells exhibit disrupted iron homeostasis with intracellular Fe2+ accumulation (Fig. 4h). Following HA@MZ treatment, the intracellular Fe2+ content decreases (Fig. S12), which can be attributed to effective ROS scavenging and suppressed M1 polarization. Lipid peroxidation levels were assessed using C11 BODIPY 581/591 probe. Results show significantly increased oxidized lipids (green fluorescence) in LPS group, while HA@MZ treatment restores the level to near-normal conditions (Fig. 4i). The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity further confirms this trend (Fig. S13). Simultaneously, the changes in MDA (end product of lipid peroxidation) levels across different groups are consistent with the lipid peroxidation results (Fig. 4g). As shown in Fig. 4j, HA@MZ treatment could restore mitochondrial damage in Raw 264.7 cells caused by lipid peroxidation. This was evidenced by a 3.5-fold increase in the JC-1 red/green ratio in the HA@MZ group compared with LPS group (Fig. S14). Furthermore, HA@MZ also demonstrates a protective effect on MLE-12 cells (Fig. S15‒S18). This protective effect is largely derived from low-level non-specific internalization and extracellular interactions that indirectly protect MLE-12 cells.

In vivo biodistribution and oxidative stress regulation of HA@MZ

Capitalizing on the large surface area and low metabolic activity of lung tissue, HA@MZ was administered via tracheal instillation for direct pulmonary delivery, thereby avoiding first-pass metabolism (Fig. 5a) [42]. Persistent macrophage inflammation is a key unfavorable prognostic feature of ALI. Based on the in vitro macrophage targeting ability of HA@MZ, its biodistribution and retention in LPS-treated mice were further investigated. As shown in Fig. 5b and Fig. S19, the fluorescence signal of HA@MZ in lung tissues was significantly stronger than that of MA-ZIF and MA at the same time points, indicating that it exhibits excellent lung targeting ability and superior retention effect. This advantage stems from the combined effect of passive targeting (due to the nanoreactor size) and active targeting (mediated by HA binding to macrophages), facilitating precise accumulation at inflammatory alveolar sites. Ex vivo fluorescence images further confirm that HA@MZ had preferential localization in inflamed lung tissues compared with MA-ZIF and MA. The fluorescence signals in other organs were negligible, demonstrating better bioavailability. To further investigate the biodistribution of HA@MZ in the lungs, we labeled HA@MZ with FITC and assessed its distribution in the lungs using frozen sections. The results (Fig. S20) show strong and uniform fluorescence in the deeper regions of the lung tissue sections, indicating that HA@MZ can be delivered across lung mucus to the deeper parts of the lung.

Oxidative stress plays a critical role in the progression of ALI. ROS are commonly used to evaluate oxidative damage in lung tissues. We evaluated ROS generation in lung sections using DHE staining. As shown in Fig. 5c and d, LPS group exhibits the most intense red fluorescence, indicating severe oxidative damage and substantial ROS production. All treatment groups show varying degrees of ROS suppression, with HA@MZ displaying the most potent therapeutic effect. This demonstrated that HA@MZ possessed excellent ROS scavenging capacity and simultaneously enhanced the antioxidant capacity of lung tissues. The above results confirmed that HA@MZ could alleviate ALI by scavenging ROS and restoring the redox balance of lung tissues.

Nrf2, a key transcription factor regulating antioxidant stress, protects against ALI by modulating a cascade of antioxidant genes [43]. Therefore, we investigated whether HA@MZ exerts its protective effect by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway through ROS scavenging. First, the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 in lung tissues was qualitatively and quantitatively evaluated via immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5e–h). The fluorescence intensities of Nrf2 and HO-1 in the normal group are relatively weak, as the signaling pathway remained inactive. After treatment, HA@MZ significantly upregulated the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 compared with other groups, which was attributed to its superior ROS scavenging capacity. These results indicate that HA@MZ alleviates ALI by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway through restoring redox balance. To further verify this hypothesis, we assessed Nrf2 and HO-1 expression using western blot analysis. After LPS stimulation, Nrf2 expression decreases, possibly due to accelerated degradation and feedback downregulation caused by excessive ROS (Fig. 5i). In contrast, HA@MZ treatment significantly up-regulated both Nrf2 and HO-1 expression levels (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5i and j), outperforming both MA-ZIF and MA, indicating that HA@MZ effectively mitigates oxidative stress injury by restoring endogenous antioxidant pathways.

Under oxidative stress conditions, excessive ROS promotes the dissociation of Keap1 from Nrf2. Nrf2 then translocates into the nucleus and binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE), initiating the expression of a series of antioxidant genes (HO-1, SOD, and CAT). As shown in Fig. 5k and l, HA@MZ significantly increases the activities of SOD and CAT enzymes, restoring them to near-normal levels. In summary, HA@MZ effectively enhances antioxidant capacity and alleviates oxidative stress-induced damage.

Restoration of inflammatory microenvironment and immune homeostasis

Previous studies have confirmed that the inflammatory microenvironment formed by macrophage infiltration, polarization phenotype, and release of related pro-inflammatory factors is a crucial factor affecting tissue damage and repair in ALI [44]. Therefore, to evaluate the ability of HA@MZ to regulate inflammation and immune homeostasis, the status of macrophage polarization and related inflammatory indicators were detected. Lung tissue sections were subjected to immunofluorescence co-staining of F4/80 with CD86 (M1 marker) and CD163 (M2 marker). As shown in Fig. 6a–d, LPS challenge induces a pronounced accumulation of F4/80+CD86+ pro-inflammatory macrophages in lung tissue, whereas HA@MZ treatment markedly reduces CD86 expression and significantly increases the proportion of F4/80+CD163+ macrophages. Notably, CD163 represents a reparative M2c-like macrophage phenotype that plays a central role in inflammation resolution and ROS detoxification in acute lung injury. In addition, the expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-10 in BALF were detected by ELISA (Fig. 6e–g, Fig. S2). Consistently, compared with LPS group, HA@MZ treatment significantly reduces the levels of these pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, compared to other groups, the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in the HA@MZ group was found to be higher. These phenomena indicate that HA@MZ can alleviate pulmonary inflammation by effectively scavenging ROS to inhibit M1 polarization of macrophages.

As shown in Fig. 6h–j, under oxidative stress induced by excessive ROS stimulation, Fe2+ content was significantly elevated in lung tissues. Sustained Fe2+ accumulation further amplifies lipid peroxidation, leading to decreased GSH levels and eventual inactivation of GPX4, thereby triggering severe lung injury [45,46]. Consequently, the levels of lipid peroxidation products MDA and 4-HNE are markedly increased (Fig. 6k). As expected, HA@MZ treatment restore both markers to near-normal levels. Subsequently, we investigated the therapeutic mechanism of HA@MZ (Fig. 6l–o) [47]. Western blot analysis demonstrates that HA@MZ effectively suppresses lipid peroxidation by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway while inhibiting NF-κB signaling. HA@MZ restores intracellular redox balance by scavenging ROS. The reduction in oxidative stress promotes the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant defense pathway. Simultaneously, the reduction in ROS inhibits ROS-dependent NF-κB activation, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling. Subsequently, the improved oxidative and inflammatory signaling creates a favorable environment for the restoration of GPX4 activity and the inhibition of lipid peroxidation.

In vivo therapeutic effect

The therapeutic effect of HA@MZ was tested by survival curves. As shown in Fig. 7a, the 7-day survival rate in LPS group was only 30%. MA and MA-ZIF treatments increases the survival rate to 50% and 70%, respectively. Most importantly, HA@MZ treatment shows the most significant protective effect, restoring the survival rate to the same level as the normal group. BALB/c mice were treated 2 h after ALI induction, and lung tissues along with BALF were collected to further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of HA@MZ (Fig. 7b). As shown in Fig. 7c, lungs in LPS group exhibites severe swelling and vascular leakage, while HA@MZ treatment markedly alleviated these alterations. H&E staining revealed significant pathological differences (Fig. 7d). The ALI group displays substantial inflammatory cell infiltration, interstitial edema, thickened alveolar walls, and extensive tissue damage [48]. In contrast, HA@MZ group shows restored pulmonary architecture with clear and intact alveolar structures, attributable to the targeted delivery and potent ROS-scavenging capability of HA@MZ. The lung wet/dry weight ratio reached 5.93 in ALI group but was restored to near-normal levels following HA@MZ treatment, indicating significantly mitigated pulmonary edema (Fig. 7e).

Furthermore, total protein content and cell count in BALF were evaluated (Fig. 7f and g). The inflammatory response in ALI increases pulmonary vascular permeability, leading to protein exudation and immune cell infiltration. Results demonstrate that HA@MZ significantly reduces protein leakage and cellular infiltration in LPS-challenged mice, corroborating the histological observations. Neutrophil infiltration serves as a key indicator of inflammatory microenvironment restoration [49]. As shown in Fig. 7h–k, HA@MZ treatment substantially attenuates neutrophil recruitment compared to LPS group, reducing the proportion from 78.5% to 11.6%. Owing to the favorable therapeutic effect of HA@MZ, it not only effectively restores the inflammatory microenvironment and immune homeostasis but also upregulates the degradation of ZO-1 protein induced by injury (Fig. 7l and m). This maintaines the integrity of epithelial and endothelial cells and plays an important role in restoring the air-blood barrier [50].

In vivo biocompatibility of HA@MZ

The biosafety of nanoreactor is a fundamental prerequisite for effective ALI treatment [51]. Therefore, pathological analysis was conducted by comparing H&E staining of major organs between different treatment groups and control group to evaluate the biosafety of HA@MZ (endotracheal infusion). The results show that no significant pathological changes are observed in heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys after treatment with all intermediate and final products of the nanomedicine compared with control group (Fig. 8a) [52]. This confirms the excellent in vivo biocompatibility of HA@MZ. Blood biochemical indices also demonstrate that albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), urea (UREA), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and creatinine (CREA) are within the normal range in both control group and each treatment group [53] (Fig. 8b). Meanwhile, MA, MA-ZIF, and HA@MZ also exhibit good biocompatibility when administered via intraperitoneal injection (Fig. S22). These findings indicate that HA@MZ holds promising potential for clinical application.

Conclusions

In this study, to address the challenges of clinical ALI therapy such as non-specificity, single therapeutic targets, low cellular uptake, and significant side effects, we developed a HA engineered nanoreactor. HA@MZ effectively crosses the alveolar-capillary barrier, achieves active and passive targeting to inflammatory sites, and responds to the inflammatory microenvironment, enabling efficient pulmonary delivery of MA. Benefiting from the synergistic effect of MA and HA, the nanoreactor exhibits potent ROS scavenging capacity. Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirm the excellent lung targeting and retention of HA@MZ. Compared with pure MA, HA@MZ demonstrates superior antioxidant activity, significantly suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines and macrophage pro-inflammatory phenotypes, and reduces lipid peroxidation in lung tissue, thereby alleviating pulmonary injury in ALI mice. The favorable therapeutic outcomes are attributed to the ability of HA@MZ to effectively remodel pulmonary redox and immune homeostasis by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 and GPX4 pathways while downregulating the NF-κB pathway. These findings suggest that HA@MZ could serve as a promising nanomedicine-based therapy for ALI, providing new insights into treating ROS-driven complex inflammatory disorders.
