Peptide-mediated immunomodulation of tumor microenvironment in triple-negative breast cancer: A comprehensive review
Authors: Adnan Murad Bhayo, Paola Marcato, Marya Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer, characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 expression. The absence of molecular targets in TNBC limits treatment options and contributes to increased rates of recurrence, metastasis, and resistance to conventional therapies. TNBC, however, is rich in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes; hence, elevated programmed death-ligand 1 expression makes these tumors amenable to immunotherapy. A variety of antibodies and immunomodulatory drugs are being explored for TNBC treatment, this review specifically focuses on design of immunomodulatory peptides for TNBC treatment. This review comprehensively discusses the peptide-based approaches for immunomodulating tumor microenvironment (TME) of TNBC and to enhance antitumor immune response. The peptide-mediated modulation of innate immune cells including tumor-associated macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells, as well as T cells of the adaptive immune system is explored in detail. The applications of peptides as immune checkpoint inhibitors and highlights of emerging strategies that employ peptides to induce immunogenic cell death to stimulate antitumor immunity are discussed. Immunogenic cell death inducing peptides promote the release of immunogenic signals such as damage-associated molecular patterns from dying cancer cells, which further activate dendritic cells, T cells, and neutrophils, thereby reshaping the TME to support robust antitumor immunity. These strategies underscore the transformative potential of peptide therapeutics to harness the immune system and reshape TME, offering a promising avenue for more effective and durable TNBC treatment.Significance StatementWith growing interest in peptides as tumor microenvironment modulator, this review provides an in-depth analysis of interactions and crosstalk between immune and tumor cells and explores therapeutic potential of peptides in modulating immune cell signaling pathways, with ultimate impact as anticancer agents for triple-negative breast cancer.Key words: Triple-negative breast cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Immunomodulatory peptide, Immunotherapy, Breast cancer treatment

Introduction

According to “Cancer Statistics-2025” published by the American Cancer Society in 2025, 2,041,910 new cancer cases and 618,120 cancer deaths are projected to occur in United States.1 Among various subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents a particularly aggressive form, comprising 15%–20% of all breast cancer cases. TNBC is defined by the lack of estrogen receptors (ERs), progesterone receptors, and human epidermal growth factor receptors (HER2), as determined by immunohistochemistry.2 TNBC is characterized by high invasiveness, rapid disease progression, and has limited treatment options because of the absence of established hormonal or HER2-targeted therapies. Clinically, TNBC is associated with a high risk of distant metastases, particularly to the brain, liver, lungs, and bones, which often contributes to its poor prognosis. The 5-year overall survival rate of TNBC was reported 81.28%, and that of metastatic TNBC was 10.81% in 2022, respectively.3

Current treatment paradigms for TNBC continue to rely on surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy.4 In recent years, however, immunotherapy has reshaped the therapeutic landscape of TNBC treatment. The phase III KEYNOTE-522 trial demonstrated that the addition of the anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) antibody pembrolizumab to standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved pathological complete response, and subsequently enhanced event-free survival and overall survival in patients with high risk early-stage TNBC.5 For patients who undergo resection but present with residual invasive disease after neoadjuvant therapy, adjuvant strategies are used to eradicate micrometastatic lesions and reduce recurrence risk. The CREATE-X trial showed that adjuvant capecitabine significantly improved 5-year disease-free survival and overall survival among TNBC subgroups with residual disease compared with observation (hazard ratio for recurrence ≈ 0.58 for disease-free survival).6 Similarly, a phase III OlympiA trial demonstrated improved survival for patients carrying germline pathogenic variants in breast cancer 1 (BRCA1) and germline pathogenic variants in breast cancer 2 (BRCA2) genes that are associated with early breast cancer. The patient demonstrated significantly reduced invasive disease-free survival events (hazard ratio ≈ 0.58) and improved overall survival (hazard ratio ≈ 0.68) compared with placebo group when treated with 1 year of adjuvant olaparib, after standard (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy.7

Nonetheless, a substantial subset of TNBC remains resistant to immune checkpoint blockade. Mechanistic studies have shown that resistance arises from an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) characterized by low infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes, dominance of M2-polarized macrophages, defective antigen presentation, and activation of intrinsic oncogenic pathways such as canonical Wingless and Integrated/β-catenin that exclude effector immune cells.8,9 These immune-evasive mechanisms collectively limit long-term efficacy of current immunotherapies.

Given the lack of effective treatments for TNBC, the development of novel therapeutic strategies is being actively explored. Peptide-based immunotherapeutics are among the active research areas for cancer treatment because of their low molecular weight, biocompatibility, biodegradability, efficient tumor penetration, low production costs, and ease of synthesis and modification—making them particularly attractive candidates for immunotherapeutic strategies against TNBC.10,11 Despite growing interest in peptide-based approaches, only a handful of reviews specifically address peptide-mediated targeting and drug delivery in TNBC.12 Given the aggressive nature of TNBC and the central role of TME in driving its progression and resistance to traditional therapeutics, this review provides an in-depth analysis of the functions and interactions of innate and adaptive immune cells, including tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells and T cells within the TME and further explores the therapeutic potential of immunomodulatory peptides and peptide functionalized nanoparticles in immune cell signaling pathways. The peptide-based strategies that induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), are also discussed, highlighting the role of peptides in modulating TME of TNBC.

Tumor microenvironment of TNBCs

TME of TNBC contains multiple immune cell types, including neutrophils, NK cells, DCs, regulatory T cells (Tregs), TAMs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and lymphocytes (B and T cells). In addition to immune cells, the TME also contain stromal components such as fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and malignant tumor cells (Fig. 1). Compared with other breast cancer subtypes, TME of TNBC are generally considered “immunologically hot,” showing higher infiltration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, greater expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and increased presence of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, relative to luminal A/B and HER2-positive breast cancers. This increased immune infiltration reflects a stronger baseline immunogenicity, partly driven by higher genomic instability and greater neoantigen load in TNBC. However, despite this apparent immune activity, TNBC tumors often remain functionally immunosuppressed because of the dominance of protumor TAMs, expansion of MDSCs and Tregs, and upregulation of inhibitory pathways, as discussed below.13, 14, 15, 16

Tumor cells in TNBC actively engage with surrounding cells by secreting cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, and promote immune cell recruitment, and immunosuppressive environment that aids tumor growth, while evading effective immune surveillance.17 Within the innate immune system, several key cell types including TAMs, DCs, NK cells, and neutrophils are altered in the TME. TAMs polarize toward anti-inflammatory or an M2-like phenotype, suppress antitumor immunity and support tumor progression through the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines. Neutrophils are reprogrammed into tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and promote tumor progression and metastasis. Similarly, DCs essential for initiating innate immune response are frequently impaired in TNBC, leading to reduced activation of T cells. Moreover, NK cells, which play a key role in tumor surveillance, become dysfunctional in the TME, preventing their ability to eliminate tumor cells. These dysregulations in the innate immune system contribute to the overall immunosuppressive nature of the TME, facilitating tumor evasion.18 Emerging evidence underscores the critical role of innate immunity in cancer therapy, including the reprogramming of M2-polarized TAMs into proinflammatory phenotypes, antigen presentation by DCs, immune surveillance by NK cells, and the phenotypic switching of neutrophils toward an antitumor state.19, 20, 21

Because innate and adaptive immune responses are intrinsically linked, tumor dampening signals from innate immune cells and direct interactions with the tumor cells ultimately affect the adaptive immune system. The tumor cells hinder the activation and functions of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which are crucial for eliminating tumor cells. B cells, important for antigen recognition and antibody production, are often dysfunctional in the TME. Concurrently, Tregs and MDSCs accumulate, further dampening the immune response. Additionally, immune checkpoint pathways, such as PD-1/PD-L1 axis, are upregulated in the TME, inhibiting the activation and functions of T cells, and allowing the tumor to evade immune detection.15 Modulating the TME to counteract its immunosuppressive nature is crucial for improving therapeutic outcomes. The peptide-based strategies can modulate TME of TNBC, by interacting with the innate and adaptive immune cells, regulating immune checkpoint pathways, and increasing ICD of cancer cells by secreting danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Immunomodulation of innate immune cells by peptides

Immunomodulation of TAMs

TAMs exhibit significant functional heterogeneity and can be broadly categorized into 2 phenotypic subtypes based on surface marker expression and cytokine profiles: classically activated macrophages (M1-like TAMs) and alternatively activated macrophages (M2-like TAMs).60 M1-like TAMs are associated with an overall proinflammatory, antitumor phenotype and secrete cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-12 (IL-12), and IL-6, enhancing cytotoxic immune response and supporting tumor suppression. In contrast, M2-like TAMs display protumoral phenotype, producing cytokines such as IL-10 and IL-1, along with matrix metalloproteinases upregulation, particularly Matrix Metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9, contributing to extracellular matrix remodeling, tumor invasion, and metastasis.61 In healthy tissues, macrophages maintain a dynamic balance between the proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory phenotypes. In TNBC, TAMs predominantly exhibit an M2-like phenotype, contributing to tumor progression and immune evasion.62 TAM-specific immunomodulatory peptides function by either targeting key pathways that sustain the immunosuppressive phenotype, such as the CD47–signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) axis and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 signaling, or act by selective binding and delivery of immunotherapeutic drugs to M2-like macrophages. These peptides enhance macrophage-mediated antitumor response by restoring phagocytosis, inducing M2-to-M1-like repolarization, or by selectively depleting M2-like TAMs.63,64

For example, M2-like TAM targeting chimeric synthetic peptide, ChiP-RS [KYEQDPWGVKWWYK], loaded with Resiquimod (R848), a synthetic agonist of Toll-like receptor 7 and 8 (TLR7/8) and Src homology-2 domain containing protein tyrosine phosphatase (SHP2) inhibitor to promote macrophage phagocytosis, restored macrophage proinflammatory phenotype.22 ChiP-RS treatment of RAW264.7 macrophages increased the expression of M1-markers (CD80) and inducible nitric oxide synthase, whereas decreased expression of M2-markers (arginase-1, CD206), was observed by flow cytometry and Western blot analysis, indicating robust macrophage repolarization. The coculturing ChiP-RS-treated macrophages with murine TNBC cells did not directly kill tumor cells, but markedly suppressed SHP2 expression, restoring macrophage phagocytic functions. The in vivo treatment of 4T1 lung metastatic model with ChiP-RS enhanced antitumor immune activation, evidenced by increased infiltration of CD3+ CD8+ T lymphocytes and a reduction in immunosuppressive Treg cells.22

Similarly, a combination of peptide design and computational analysis techniques were used to develop CD206 targeted peptides that can deliver immunomodulatory drugs in M2-like macrophages.23 The synthetic peptide termed as MACTIDE [G(CTKSIPPIC)SPGAK-OH] developed by inserting CSPGAK sequence in sunflower trypsin inhibitor-1 followed by acyclization of the peptide was conjugated with verteporfin (MACTIDE-V), a Yes-associated protein (YAP)/transcriptional c-activator with PDZ-binding mortif (TAZ) pathway inhibitor, capable of modulating M2-like macrophages toward M1-phenotype. The peptide drug conjugate mediated selective binding with CD206+ TAM, compared with mannose based targeted peptides, indicating higher binding affinity of newly designed peptides. The docking simulations and structural representations of MACTIDE binding to CD206 demonstrated 3.8 Å displacement of an α-helix in cluster node 2 of second C-type lectin domain (CTLD2), creating a binding pocket that accommodated MACTIDE, suggesting peptide-induced receptor flexibility (Fig. 2A). Comparison of the peptide-bound configuration with the crystal structure of CD206 (PDB 5XTS) further revealed pronounced conformational rearrangements in the cysteine rich domain, indicating that MACTIDE binding triggers structural adaptations beyond the immediate binding site (Fig. 2B). Hydrogen bonding analysis showed multiple interactions between MACTIDE and CD206, stabilized the complex and contributed to the high binding affinity essential for effective receptor targeting (Fig. 2C) MACTIDE-V treatment induced repolarization of TAMs toward an M1-such as phenotype, enhanced phagocytic activity, and promoted exclusion of YAP from the nucleus, resulting in improved antitumor immune responses. MACTIDE-V significantly reduced tumor growth and lung metastasis in the orthotopic 4T1 BALB/c model and enhanced phagocytosis and antigen presentation in macrophages, promoting increased CD8+ T cell and NK cell infiltration and a more immunogenic TME.23

Compared with the free peptide, peptide functionalized nanoparticles are demonstrated for improved targeting and drug delivery efficacies. The selective binding and stimulation of TLRs by self-assembled peptide nanoparticles was recently demonstrated to modulate M2-like TAMs toward M1-phenotype.65, 66, 67, 68, 69 A synthetic, macrocyclic, cationic amphipathic peptide, derived from chicken angiogenin-4 (termed as mCA4) [CONH2-CRFKFRIVIC-NH2] demonstrated selective activation of TLR-4, modulating downstream signaling pathways including c-Jun amino-terminal kinase, extracellular signal-regulated kinase, and mitogen-activated protein kinase, and upregulating the production of proinflammatory proteins such as nitric oxide synthase, aconite decarboxylase 1, guanylate binging proteins, and interferon stimulated proteins (IFIT) in macrophages and in coculture models with TNBC. Interestingly, mCA4, a nontoxic peptide, when combined with chemotherapeutics exponentially enhanced anticancer efficacies of the drugs in macrophage/TNBC coculture model, by activating phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 pathway and modulating the M2-phenotype of macrophages toward M1-state.69

Similarly, M2-targeted peptide functionalized chitosan-curcumin nanoparticles prepared by conjugating M2pep [YEQDPWGVKWWY] to chitosan via carbodiimide chemistry, followed by curcumin loading were developed to specifically target M2 macrophages, while delivering curcumin to enhance antitumor immunity.25 The nanoparticles treated TAMs demonstrated reduced expression of cell surface makers (CD163, M2 marker) cytokines (IL-10) and key genes (cyclooxygenase-2) involved in TAM polarization through TNF-α and IL-17 signaling pathways. The coculture of nanoparticles treated TAMs with MDA-MB-231 cells inhibited proliferation, migration, and invasion of TNBC. The combination of peptide nanoparticles with anti-PD-L1 therapy further enhanced tumor inhibition and downregulated tumor proliferation markers including Kiel-67 (Ki67) and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression in orthotopic 4T1 model.24

In addition to TAM immunomodulation, selective exhaustion of M2-like TAM using proapoptotic peptide eliminated tumor promoting macrophages from TNBC. PEG-melittin-dKLA8–26 designed by combining a synthetic version of truncated melittin fragment (residues 8–26) [VLTTGLPALISWIKRKRQQ], with proapoptotic d(KLAKLAK)2 motif was PEGylated to improve physiological stability. The truncated melittin exhibited the strongest binding and uptake in CD206+ M2-like macrophages compared with M0- and M1-phenotype, whereas proapoptotic peptide interacted and compromised mitochondrial membrane integrity, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis. The peptide complexes tested in 4T1 tumor bearing BALB/c mice significantly inhibited of tumor growth and lung metastasis compared with non-PEGylated analog, and reduced the expression of M2-like macrophage markers (transforming growth factor-β, arginase-1, vascular endothelial growth factor, and MMP9), confirming effective targeting and depletion of M2-like TAMs.25

The combination of immunomodulatory peptides with other therapeutics such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 molecules and chemotherapeutics, and photothermal therapy are demonstrated to improve the anticancer in TNBCs. A synthetic, amphipathic peptide, lucicebtide (ST101) [VAEAREELERLEARLGQARGELKKWKMRRNQFWLKLQR] that targets transcription factor CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein beta (C/EBPβ), a central regulator of the immunosuppressive M2-like macrophage phenotype and disrupt C/EBPβ dimerization, induced proteasomal degradation, suppressing M2-associated gene expression and reprogramming macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype. Lucicebtide-treated macrophages cocultured with CD8+ T cells restored T cell cytotoxic activity, as was evidenced by elevated interferon gamma (IFN-γ) production. Lucicebtide monotherapy significantly inhibited tumor growth, and its combination with anti-PD-1 further enhanced tumor suppression in 4T1 tumor model.26

Similarly, combining peptides that bind with the receptors involved in the crosstalk between TNBCs and TAMs with chemotherapeutics and photothermal therapy yielded a highly effective system to target and eradicate TNBC in a single punch. CD47 overexpressed on TNBCs binds to SIRPα on TAMs, transmitting inhibitory signals that suppresses macrophage phagocytosis and facilitate immune evasion. Blocking the CD47–SIRPα axis restores phagocytic activity and enhances antitumor immune response.70 A synthetic amphipathic peptide RS17 [RRYKQDGGWSHWSPWSS] with positively charged residues serving as molecular hooks to the negatively charged regions, whereas hydrophobic residues inserting into hydrophobic pockets of CD47 demonstrated selective CD47 targeting, blocking “don’t eat me” signal and promoting macrophage-mediated tumor clearance. The antitumor efficacies of the peptide were further enhanced by embellishing RS17 on the surface of nanoparticles loaded with chemotherapeutics (shikonin), photosensitizer (IR820) and immunomodulator polymetformin, yielding multifunctional nanoparticles namely, SPI@hEL-RS17 (Fig. 3). The engineered nanohybrid system was significantly uptaken in tumor cells upon intravenous injection and thermographic images of the tumor sites indicated a sharp increase in temperature upon laser irradiation, causing effective tumor ablation and validating the superior photothermal therapy of SPI@hEL-RS17.27

Collectively, these studies indicate that peptide-based TAM modulation in TNBC has progressed beyond simple macrophage depletion strategies and toward precision reprogramming approaches that functionally modulate immune activity within TME. Peptide-based strategies target M2-like TAMs via surface receptors such as CD206 or by modulating immunosuppressive signaling pathways, including CD47–SIRPα, SHP2, YAP/TAZ, and C/EBPβ. The peptide constructs not only restore macrophage phagocytic activity, but also promote secondary immune activation, as evidenced by enhanced infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells. These findings indicate that functional modulation of TAMs strengthens antitumor immunity and enhances the overall immune-mediated control of TNBC.

Despite this progress, critical knowledge gaps remain in peptide-mediated TAM modulation for TNBC treatment. Macrophage polarization exists along a spectrum rather than as discrete M1/M2 phenotypes, however, many studies use oversimplified markers, leaving the stability and persistence of peptide-induced TAM repolarization in chronically immunosuppressive microenvironments unclear. Systemic considerations, including peptide pharmacokinetics, cationic or amphipathic membrane interactions, immunogenicity, and long-term remodeling of the immune compartment, have not been rigorously evaluated. Activation of additional inhibitory receptors and parallel immunosuppressive pathways in TAMs can reduce the efficacy of peptide-mediated modulation and the resulting antitumor immune response.

Looking forward, the field is poised to transition toward refined and personalized peptide immunomodulators. Future directions may include stimuli-responsive peptide systems activated by TNBC-specific biochemical cues (eg, redox gradients and hypoxia), artificial intelligence-guided sequence optimization for enhanced selectivity, and dual-targeting constructs capable of synchronizing TAM reprogramming with T cell checkpoint blockade. Integration of spatial transcriptomics and single-cell sequencing technologies may further enable rational peptide design tailored to patient-specific immune landscapes. Moreover, combining TAM-repolarizing peptides with emerging modalities such as mRNA therapeutics or adoptive cell therapies could unlock synergistic immune remodeling effects. Ultimately, successful clinical translation will depend on moving beyond proof-of-concept tumor suppression toward demonstrating robust immune memory and relapse prevention in TNBC.

Immunomodulation of neutrophils

Neutrophils are the most abundant type of circulating leukocytes, the neutrophils that infiltrate tumors are termed TANs. Influenced by the cytokines in the TME, TANs can polarize into 2 distinct phenotypes: a protumorigenic N2-type or a proinflammatory, antitumorigenic N1-type.71 N1-type TANs exert cytotoxic effects by releasing fatty acid synthase (CD95), intercellular adhesion molecule 1, C-C motif chemokine ligand 3, and type I interferons, and by activating NK cells via IL-18. In contrast, N2-type TANs support tumor progression by expressing C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4; CD184), arginase-1, CCL5, vascular endothelial growth factor, IL-8, and MMP-9, and by promoting proliferation through neutrophil elastase (NE) and a proliferation-inducing ligand. Before tumor progression, neutrophils are typically in the N1-phenotype, and shift to the N2-phenotype as tumor progresses. Although neutrophil depletion may suppress tumor growth and metastasis, the strategy comes with systemic risks compromising immune defense. Therefore, immunomodulatory strategies aimed at reprogramming N2-like TANs toward N1-like phenotype or blocking TAN recruitment into TME offer promising therapeutic approaches for TNBC treatment.72 Compared with macrophages, where M2-like phenotype was largely used for the design of peptide-mediated delivery of immunotherapeutics, metabolic profile of N2-like TANs and cloaking cargo in neutrophil membranes were used as tools for targeted delivery of peptide and chemotherapeutics. Furthermore, peptides that can block the receptors involved in chemotaxis were designed and evaluated.

In TME of TNBC, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)—web-like DNA and protein structures—contribute to tumor progression and metastasis by promoting immunosuppression and preventing effective cytotoxic immune cell infiltration. Driven by iron-dependent oxidative stress, NETs create an environment that suppresses antitumor immunity, making them a critical therapeutic target.73 To address this, a peptide-based transformable iron nanochelator (TIN) comprised of 4 functional motifs: a hydrophilic polyethylene glycol (PEG) chain for improved solubility and circulation; an AAPV peptide sequence cleavable by NE for tumor-specific activation; a β-sheet-forming FFVLK peptide to drive nanofiber assembly; and a tripyridyl-based iron (II) chelator (TPY) to deplete intracellular labile iron was developed (Fig. 4). The peptide components of the nanoformulation played a central role in both activation and structural transformation; AAPV motif ensured selective cleavage by NE within the TME, exposing the hydrophobic FFVLK motif, rich in β-sheet-prone residues, promoting nanofiber formation through strong hydrophobic interactions. This charge and hydrophobicity-controlled transformation enhanced iron chelation and disrupted neutrophil iron metabolism, thereby suppressing the formation of extracellular traps. The detailed in vitro and in vivo analysis of TIN in TNBC model demonstrated excellent inhibition of NET formation at structural, biochemical, and molecular levels, suggesting its strong potential to modulate neutrophil-driven immunosuppression within TME and substantially suppressed tumor growth compared with PBS control. The combination of TIN with anti-PD-L1, further increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and substantially decreased CD45+Ly6G+citH3+ neutrophils, indicating the potential of dual therapy, by suppressing protumoral neutrophil activity and activating cytotoxic T cell responses.28

As discussed earlier, TME of TNBC is characterized by poor immune cell infiltration, elevated oxidative stress, and dense stromal barriers, all of which hinder effective drug penetration and limit immune-mediated tumor clearance.74 A biomimetic nanodevice (FKPN) that leveraged neutrophil-inspired targeting and peptide-mediated nuclear delivery to induce selective tumor cell apoptosis without relying on antigen recognition was designed. The nanodevice comprised of porcine pancreatic elastase, a porphyrin linked to a nuclear localization signal (NLS), and an iron-based metal-organic framework, was synthesized by the covalent conjugation of NLS [KRRRRG] on an iron-porphyrin metal-organic framework core, followed by porcine pancreatic elastase adsorption onto the surface. The entire construct was coated with neutrophil membrane, mimicking natural immune cell such as phenotype to enhance tumor targeting and immune evasion (Fig. 5). In vivo treatment of TNBC with the nanodevice combined with laser irradiation resulted in a significant suppression of primary tumor growth and triggered a pronounced abscopal effect, leading to a 90% reduction in the size of distant tumors compared with control groups. The abscopal response was tumor-specific and was associated with increased infiltration of CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in the distant tumors, which correlated with enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and necrosis.29

In addition to neutrophil targeting and repolarization, inhibition of neutrophil recruitment in TME has been successful to improve anticancer efficacies of therapeutics. CXCR4, a chemokine receptor for stromal-derived factor 1α, mediates neutrophil recruitment to tumors, polarizing the incoming neutrophils into protumorigenic phenotype and promoting tumor progression. Targeting CXCR4 signaling could reduce N2-type TAN accumulation and boost antitumor immunity.75 For example, CXCR4-targeted antitumor peptidomimetic conjugates (CTCE-KLAK), comprised of an antagonist peptide (CTCE-9908) [KGVSLSYR] to specifically block CXCR4 function and KLAK proapoptotic peptide [D(KLAKLAK)2] to disrupt mitochondrial membrane releasing cytochrome-c, caused apoptotic cancer cell death were developed. CTCE-KLAK conjugates demonstrated tumor-selective cytotoxicity, rapidly killing MDA-MB-231 cells, without affecting normal cells. In an orthotopic mouse model using 4T1 cells, CTCE-KLAK significantly reduced tumor volume and demonstrated fewer metastatic nodules compared with both untreated controls and the mixture of single peptides, showing improved therapeutic effect through conjugation.30

Similarly, CXCR4/ C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) axis was blocked using novel peptide antagonist E5 [GGRSFFLLRRIQGCRFRNTVDD] to suppress TAN neutrophils recruitment at the tumor site and was combined with chemotherapeutics to yield synergistic anticancer efficacies. E5, an amphipathic peptide with a net positive charge allowed specific interactions with the receptor, stabilizing its binding and effectively disrupting CXCL12-mediated signaling. E5 treated 4T1 cells showed concentration-dependent cytotoxicity and apoptosis, and inhibited CXCL12-induced migration of 4T1 cells and of human umbilical vein endothelial cells, inhibiting angiogenesis. E5 treatment decreased the adhesion of 4T1 cells to stromal cells in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating its effectiveness in blocking CXCL12-mediated chemotaxis and disrupting CXCR4/CXCL12 axis, respectively. In an orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model, E5 in combination with anticancer drug (paclitaxel or cyclophosphamide) significantly inhibited tumor growth. E5 treatment decreased CD31 (an endothelial cell marker) and CXCR4 expression, along with reduced phosphorylation of protein kinase B and extracellular signal-regulated kinase signaling proteins, suggesting that E5 inhibits tumor angiogenesis and progression by blocking the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis.31

Surface functionalization of nanoparticles with CXCR4 targeting peptides further enhanced anticancer efficacies of therapeutics. A CXCR4-binding sequence (a cationic amphipathic peptide comprised of D-amino acids, termed as DV1 [LGASWHRPDKCCLGYQKRPLPA]) derived from the N-terminus of viral macrophage inflammatory protein II, was functionalized on the liposomes surface by copper-free “click” chemistry for immunotherapy in TNBC model. Liposome-conjugated DV1 (L-DV1) formulations engineered with varying surface densities of the peptide (9000–74,000 mol/μm2) demonstrated surface density dependent affinity to anti-CXCR4 monoclonal antibody and were established as superior binding agents compared with both its l-amino acid counterpart and the clinically approved CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100. L-DV1-24k, with peptide density of 24,000 mol/μm2, specifically showed strong inhibition of MDA-MB-231 cell migration. The enhanced activity of peptide conjugated liposomes was correlated with marked suppression of migration-associated proteins, including p115-RhoGEF and p85-PI3K, indicating effective blockade of CXCR4-driven signaling pathways. The L-DV1 treatment of metastatic model of MDA-MB-231-Luc cells markedly reduced metastasis in the lungs, brains, livers, spleens, and kidneys, with 5 out of 6 mice showing tumor free survival.32

IL-8 is a known ligand for CXCR1/2 receptors that promotes recruitment of protumor neutrophils and stimulate cancer cell migration and invasion.33 A combination of synthetic peptide RF16 [RCQCIKTYSKPFHPK] that competitively bind with CXCR1/2 with chemotherapeutics demonstrated reduced reduced tumor growth and improved survival in MDA-MB-231 xenografts mouse model. RF16 effectively inhibited IL-8-mediated activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase and phosphoinositide 3-kinase pathways and significantly enhanced the migration and invasion of MDA-MB-231 cells in vitro. RF16 inhibition also suppressed epithelial-mesenchymal transition, a key process in metastasis enabling cancer cells to lose epithelial traits, degrading the extracellular matrix, and invading surrounding tissues.33

Collectively, peptide-based strategies in TNBC selectively target TANs to suppress their immunosuppressive and protumorigenic activities. These approaches focus on modulating specific functional pathways that contribute to tumor progression. Neutrophil-targeted peptide systems predominantly act by suppressing NET formation, inhibiting chemokine-mediated recruitment via the CXCR4/CXCL12 and CXCR1/2 axes, and harnessing neutrophil membrane properties for tumor-directed delivery, thereby disrupting tumor–neutrophil crosstalk and limiting prometastatic niche formation. A notable conceptual advancement emerging from these studies is the recognition of neutrophil metabolic and structural vulnerabilities, particularly iron-dependent NETs and chemokine-driven trafficking, as potential therapeutic targets. Transformable peptide nanostructures such as iron-chelating assemblies illustrate how biochemical cues within the TNBC microenvironment (eg, NE activity) can be harnessed to trigger spatially controlled immunomodulation.

Among the knowledge gaps, such as the M1/M2 macrophage framework, TANs immunomodulation studies likely oversimplify neutrophil heterogeneity. Emerging single-cell analyses suggest substantial transcriptional diversity among intratumoral neutrophils, raising questions about whether current peptide strategies adequately address this complexity. Furthermore, the extent to, which NET suppression or CXCR4 blockade translates across distinct TNBC molecular subtypes remains unclear, as most data is available on 4T1 tumor models. The long-term systemic consequences of chemokine axis inhibition require careful evaluation, as CXCR4 and CXCR1/2 signaling are critical for hematopoietic homeostasis and host defense. Additionally, although NET inhibition reduces metastasis in preclinical settings, its impact on durable immune memory and recurrence prevention has not been thoroughly investigated.

Future directions in neutrophil-based peptide immunotherapy are likely to move toward more selective and temporally controlled strategies. Stimuli-responsive peptides activated by neutrophil-specific enzymes, hypoxia, or oxidative gradients may improve spatial precision while minimizing systemic effects. Integration of multiomic immune profiling could enable identification of patient subsets with NET-high or CXCR4-driven TNBC phenotypes who may benefit most from neutrophil-targeted interventions. Moreover, dual-modality systems that simultaneously inhibit neutrophil recruitment and enhance antigen presentation or T cell priming may provide synergistic immune remodeling. Ultimately, translating neutrophil targeting peptides into clinical practice will require not only suppression of metastasis but also restoration of sustained antitumor immunity without compromising innate immune competence.

Immunomodulation of DCs by peptide

DCs, a diverse population of professional antigen-presenting cells, represent up to ∼1% of the total hematopoietic cells present in the blood and lymphoid tissues.76 Immature DCs capture antigens derived from tumor cells (endogenous sources) and from extracellular components such as dying cells and secrete tumor antigens (exogenous sources). Upon antigen uptake, DCs undergo maturation and migrate to nearby lymph nodes, where the processed tumor antigens are presented to naïve T cells. This interaction is essential for activating naïve T cells and directing their differentiation into various effector subsets, including T helper cells (TH1, TH2, and TH17) cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and Tregs with immunosuppressive functions.77 Peptides specifically target DCs delivering immunotherapeutics and restoring cytotoxic functions of Tregs. Among various peptide-based nanoformulations, strategies for targeting CD40, a receptor constitutively expressed on DCs, are well studied. CD40 is a costimulatory molecule expressed on DCs, and its activation is primarily triggered by the binding with its natural ligand, CD40L, leading to the clustering of CD40 receptors. This clustering induces downstream signaling pathways that promote DC maturation, enhancing antigen presentation capability and ability to activate T cells.78

For example, a polyvalent peptide-based CD40 nano-agonist (PVA-CD40) was constructed by conjugating 2 peptides: an anchoring arm peptide [ATYSEFPGNLKPC] to target CD40 on DCs through polar and charged residues, and an assembly-driving peptide [KLVFFAE] capable of multivalent assembly via a hydrophobic β-sheet-forming core by click chemistry. PVA-CD40 treatment activated CD40 receptor oligomerization, as was evaluated by fluorescence resonance energy transfer analysis and increased the expression of costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 and proinflammatory cytokines IL-12p70 in bone marrow-derived DCs compared with the individual peptides (Fig. 6). PVA-CD40 treated orthotopic 4T1 tumor model demonstrated efficient localization of conjugates (within 30 minutes) and retention in draining lymph nodes compared with CD40 monoclonal antibody, indicating prolonged DC stimulation. Combined with doxorubicin (DOX), PVA-CD40 significantly improved tumor inhibition and extended median survival from 29 to 39 days in tumor bearing mice.34

Others used multivalency of nanoparticles to design targeted nanoassemblies for DC immunomodulation. A tumor-associated peptide antigen, survivin (epitopes 66–74) [GWEPDDNPI], was chemically linked with deoxycholic acid forming a hydrophobic core, with hydrophilic peptide on the surface, capable of binding with major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules of DCs, ultimately activating cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (as part of the adaptive immune response). The immunomodulatory capabilities of the complexes were enhanced by loading R848, a TLR7/8 agonist, and SD-208, a transforming growth factor-β receptor inhibitor—into the hydrophobic core to enhance DC maturation and counteract tumor-driven immunosuppression. A lipid membrane coating of the assembly further improved structural and biological compatibility, and lymph node targeting. The peptide played an essential role in directing antigen-specific immunity, whereas the assembled architecture ensured efficient delivery, immune activation, and strong antitumor effects in preclinical models. Orthotopic model of 4T1, injected with the nanoparticles demonstrated significant reduction in primary tumor size and markedly decreased lung metastatic nodules compared with controls. Immune analysis showed increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells and a reduced ratio of Tregs in tumors after treatment, indicating enhanced antitumor immunity.35

Similarly, multicomponent peptide nanoparticle system integrating antigen specificity, enzyme-responsive release, and DC-targeting capability was developed and cloaked with erythrocyte membranes to enhance antigenic delivery of peptides to DCs. The peptidic core comprised of tumor-associated peptides MAGE-A1 [TIWMLLMLQ] and NY-ESO-1 [SQYGEPRKL], possessing hydrophilic and hydrophobic properties respectively, was chemically linked with cathepsin B-cleavable dipeptide Val-Cit. Cathepsin B and other lysosomal proteases are typically overexpressed in DCs and are involved in antigen processing hence, incorporation of Val-Cit linker ensured specific cleavage and release of the peptide epitopes in the target cells. The peptide core was further functionalized with amphiphilic DSPE-PEG-Man, to enhance colloidal stability and to mediate targeted uptake via mannose specific receptors on immature DCs. The nanoparticles with modular chemical architecture demonstrated enhanced MHC-I antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell activation, offering a broadly applicable and potent peptide-based immunotherapeutic strategy. The peptide-based nanoparticles demonstrated significant upregulation of maturation markers CD86 and CD11c on DCs and induced higher IL-12 secretion compared with untreated controls. Upon coculture with CD8+ T lymphocytes, DCs treated with the nanoparticles triggered the release of IFN-γ and TNF-α, confirming effective T cell activation driven by mannose-targeted delivery of dual-antigen peptides and showed strong tumor inhibition in the MDA-MB-231 model.36

In addition to using CD40 and MHC-I receptors for DC maturation, human neutrophil peptide-1 (HNP1) [ACCDCRGVGRCRVDWENWWCC], an α-defensin amphipathic peptide that attracts DCs via chemotactic activity, promoting DC maturation, and enhancing T cell-mediated antitumor immunity was developed. A recombinant secretable plasmid encoding HNP1 expression in DCs significantly increased tumor infiltration of CD11c+ DCs coexpressing CD40 and CD86 receptors, indicating enhanced maturation. HNP1 treatment also increased mature DCs in tumor-draining lymph nodes, supporting its role in recruiting and activating immune cells to drive T cell-mediated antitumor responses. Secretable plasmid encoding HNP1 expression in 4T1 xenografts significantly reduced tumor growth and showed increased infiltration of CD8+ CTLs and elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-12, indicating that HNP1 induced DCs activation and stimulated robust antitumor immune response.37

The peptide-based DCs immunomodulation strategies in TNBC demonstrate a transition from simple antigen delivery toward rationally engineered systems designed to actively stimulate antitumor immunity. These approaches position DCs as central regulators of immune activation by strengthening the link between antigen recognition and T cell priming. The shared mechanistic features among the studies include enhancement of CD40-mediated costimulatory signaling to promote DC activation, development of antigen-specific peptide assemblies that improve MHC class I presentation and CTL response, and incorporation of chemotactic or receptor targeted elements to increase DC recruitment and maturation within the TME. These strategies reflect deliberate optimization of peptide platforms to enhance DC function and sustain effective immune responses in TNBC.

Despite these promising developments, several translational challenges remain. First, most antigenic peptides used (eg, survivin, MAGE-A1, and NY-ESO-1) represent shared tumor-associated antigens rather than patient-specific neoantigens, raising questions regarding immunogenic breadth and escape mechanisms in heterogeneous TNBC populations. Second, although CD40 agonism is mechanistically attractive, systemic CD40 activation carries potential risks of cytokine release and off-target immune toxicity, and the safety profile of peptide-based multivalent CD40 clustering requires further investigation. Third, many studies rely on murine models that may not fully replicate human DC subset diversity, particularly conventional type 1 DCs, which are critical for crosspresentation. Furthermore, the durability of T cell memory induced by these peptide systems and their ability to prevent recurrence remains insufficiently explored.

Future peptide-based DC immunotherapies are likely to move toward greater personalization and immune context adaptation. Integration with tumor neoantigen sequencing could enable customizable peptide assemblies tailored to patient-specific mutational landscapes. Advanced delivery systems incorporating spatiotemporal release mechanisms or codelivery of innate immune agonists may further refine DC activation without excessive systemic inflammation. For example, combining DC-targeted peptides with checkpoint blockade, TAM repolarization strategies, or neutrophil-modulating systems may enable coordinated multicompartment immune remodeling in TNBC. Ultimately, clinical translation will require enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and durable immune memory, capable of preventing metastatic relapse.

Immunomodulation of NK cells by peptides

NK cells are large granular lymphocytes of the innate immune system that are identified by the expression of CD56 and lack of CD3. These consist mainly of 2 subsets: CD56dim NK cells, with strong cytotoxic activity, and CD56bright NK cells that mainly regulate immune responses.79 NK cell functions depend on the balance between inhibitory receptors (eg, killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors and NK group 2 member A [NKG2A]) that recognize MHC class I molecules to avoid killing healthy cells, and activating receptors (eg, NKG2D, natural cytotoxicity receptors, and NKp30 and NKp46) that detect stress ligands often overexpressed on TNBCs.80 NK cells suppress tumor progression by releasing cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes, inducing apoptosis via Fas ligand and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand, which binds with the death receptors on TNBC, and mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity through CD16 (Fc γ receptor III).81 However, TNBCs evade NK cell attack by expressing ligands that activate NK cells inhibitory receptors, reducing NK activity and promoting tumor progression. Cytokine therapies such as IL-2 and IL-15 boost NK proliferation and cytotoxicity but have systemic toxicity and short half-life. Immune checkpoint inhibitor targeting receptors such as NKG2A (eg, monalizumab) can restore NK function but show variable efficacies in solid tumors. Adoptive NK cell transfer and chimeric antigen receptor-NK therapies are promising but face challenges in sustained activity and tumor infiltration.82 Peptide-based therapies offer a safe and effective way to enhance NK cell activation and are typically designed to target NK cells receptors involved in crosstalk with TNBCs or target receptors and pathways involved in immunomodulation by promoting key activation markers and immune cell recruitment without the toxicity or complexity.11

For example, immunomodulatory effects of the frog skin derived amphipathic peptide frenatin 2.1S [GLVGTLLGHIGKAILG], enhancing NK cell recruitment, activation, and tumor-killing activities were demonstrated. Mice injected with frenatin 2.1S, showed significant increase in NKG2D expressing NK cells, an activating receptor involved in immune surveillance and showed elevated expression of CD69, an early marker of NK cell activation and of CD107a, a marker linked to degranulation and cytotoxic function. The expression of FasL, apoptosis mediating ligand in target cells, was also higher in the frenatin 2.1S treated group, indicating enhanced killing potential. Finally, the peptide increased the ratio of IFN-γ to IL-10 producing NK cells, suggesting a shift toward a proinflammatory and antitumor immune response. These findings collectively demonstrate that frenatin 2.1S promotes both the activation and functional enhancement of NK cells.38

The peptides that can perturb interactions between NKp44-PCNA axis, a critical mechanism by which tumors evade NK-mediated immune surveillance were designed and evaluated for immunotherapy treatment. NKp44 a dual functional activating and inhibitory receptor recognize and interact with PCNA, a protein commonly overexpressed in TNBC, causing reduced NK cell activity, and facilitating immune evasion by TNBC cells.83 An NKp44-derived amphiphilic peptide 8 [EASALVCIRLVTSSKPRTVA] that binds PCNA via electrostatic interactions, whereas hydrophobic residues promote stable binding within nonpolar pockets of PCNA, blocking NKp44–PCNA interactions was investigated for its immunomodulatory capabilities. When fused with the arginine-rich nuclear localization signal (R11-NLS), peptide 8 effectively entered 4T1 cells reducing cell viability and suppressed tumor growth in 4T1 orthotopic mouse model, showing efficacies comparable to 5-fluorouracil.39

In another study, dual action, cationic amphiphilic peptides that can selectively interact with the tumor cells surface, while restoring NK cell functions were designed. The therapeutic peptide LTX-315 [KKWWKKWDipK-NH2] markedly reduced 4T1 cell survival and proliferative capacity, by increased infiltration of CD45+ immune cells, notably NK cells and DCs, while reducing immunosuppressive myeloid populations. Removal of NK cells by antiasialo GM1 antibodies significantly reduced the tumor-killing efficacy of LTX-315, confirming that NK cells are critical mediators of its therapeutic effect. Complementary RNA sequencing further revealed that LTX-315 significantly altered the tumor transcriptional program, particularly in combination with radiation therapy, leading to the upregulation of immune-related pathways. Specifically, type I interferon–associated genes (Ifit1, Ifit3, and Ifit3b), T cell infiltration markers (Cd27, Cd3e, Cd3g, Cd8a, and Cd8b1), and NK cell activity–associated transcripts (Klrd1, Klri2, Klrel, Klrc2, and Klrc1) were enriched.40

In addition to utilizing peptides as immunomodulators, cytotoxic, tumor targeting peptides were combined with immunomodulatory drugs to enhance anticancer efficacies in TNBC. For example, stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway of NK cells was stimulated to enhance cytotoxic function of membrane disrupting peptides against TNBC.84 The combination of stimulator of interferon genes agonist (cGAMP) with an antivascular peptide (RGD-(KLAKLAK)2) to selectively target cancer cells via integrin β3 binding (through the RGD motif) induced cell death by disrupting membranes, while stimulating innate immunity and the production of type I interferons, due to the activation of NK cells. In vivo analysis in 4T1 model demonstrated that cGAMP and RGD-(KLAKLAK)2 monotherapies significantly reduced tumor volume—∼2-fold compared with the control—whereas the combination therapy led to a 5-fold reduction, demonstrating enhanced antitumor efficacies. Immunologically, the combination treatment resulted in ∼1.5-fold increase in activated NK cells within the TME, however, a consistent decrease in CD8+ T cells, particularly in exhausted PD-1+ CD8+ T cells across all treatment groups was observed, suggesting a shift in the immune response toward NK cell-mediated activity.41

The immunomodulatory nanoparticles with metallic core were also developed to improve peptide stability and to yield stimuli-responsive, drug delivery carriers. The selenopeptide nanoparticle [Sec(Dod)2KGPLGVRGRGD] targeting NKG2A mediated signaling were prepared by integrating a tumor targeting RGD motif, an MMP-2–cleavable linker, and a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive tail, enabling selective delivery of DOX to solid tumors while stimulating immune responses (Fig. 7). After accumulating in tumor tissues, MMP-2 cleavage reduced nanoparticle size to improve penetration, and ROS triggered DOX release. In parallel, oxidative metabolites from selenopeptide downregulated human leukocyte antigen E (HLA-E) expression on tumor cells, disrupting NKG2A signaling and thereby enhancing NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity in orthotopic MDA/MB231 tumor model.42

The peptide-mediated immunomodulation of NK cells in TNBC reflects a strategic emphasis on restoring innate cytotoxic surveillance while reshaping TME. NK-targeted peptides are designed to enhance direct cytotoxic activity rather than primarily modifying suppressive cellular networks. The studies demonstrate consistent efforts to increase the expression of activation-associated receptors and cytotoxic mediators, including NKG2D, CD69, and CD107a, thereby strengthening NK cell effector function. Additional approaches interfere with inhibitory receptor-ligand interactions, such as the NKp44–PCNA and NKG2A–HLA-E axes, to relieve functional suppression and restore NK responsiveness. Furthermore, certain multifunctional cytotoxic peptides promote immunogenic tumor cell death, which secondarily facilitates NK cell recruitment and activation within the TME. Together, these findings indicate that peptide-based NK cell modulation serves both to enhance direct cytotoxic capacity and to support broader immune activation in TNBC.

Among the translational issues, most preclinical on murine models that may not accurately replicate human NK receptor diversity and inhibitory checkpoint complexity, particularly because of the interspecies differences in killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors. The durability of NK activation after peptide administration remains unclear; transient activation may not translate into sustained tumor control without concurrent modulation of suppressive cytokines such as transforming growth factor-β within the TME. Although checkpoint-disrupting peptides show mechanistic promise, their specificity and potential off-target effects on normal proliferative tissues require careful evaluation. Additionally, combination strategies that preferentially shift immune dominance toward NK cells, as observed in certain STING peptide systems, raise important questions about the durability of adaptive immune memory and the equilibrium between innate and adaptive immune responses.

Looking forward, the next generation of NK-targeted peptide therapeutics may include bifunctional peptides capable of simultaneously blocking inhibitory receptors and delivering cytokine mimetics with reduced systemic toxicity. Integration with adoptive NK or chimeric antigen receptor-NK therapies could enhance in vivo persistence and infiltration, whereas peptide-based modulation of stromal barriers may improve NK trafficking into poorly infiltrated TNBC lesions. Ultimately, clinical translation will require demonstrating not only enhanced NK infiltration and activation but also durable tumor control without inducing systemic inflammatory toxicity.

Immunomodulation of adaptive immune cells by peptides

The adaptive immune response against tumors is directly linked with the activation of innate immunomodulation, hence, peptide-based strategies that activate DCs ultimately stimulate adaptive immunity. In the context of adaptive immunity, T lymphocytes, and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) play a dominant role in TNBC treatment. CTLs recognize tumor-derived antigens presented on MHC class I molecules and eliminate malignant cells through mechanisms such as perforin-granzyme-mediated cytolysis and Fas-FasL-induced apoptosis. CTL functions are critically supported by CD4+ helper T cells, which enhance CTL activity by secreting key cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ, promoting proliferation, survival, and functional maturation. Additionally, CD4+ T cells play a pivotal role in licensing DCs and facilitating the development of memory T cell subsets, which are crucial for long-term antitumor immunity.85 Current therapeutic strategies to activate T cells in TNBC including adaptive chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy, monoclonal antibodies that bind with tumor antigens (eg, antimesothelin antibodies), and cancer vaccines based on tumor-specific neoantigens (such as lifileucel) show some promise in preclinical settings, but also face challenges such as complex manufacturing, high costs, limited antigen specificity, and potential systemic side effects.86, 87, 88, 89 Peptide base therapeutics are typically developed using patient-derived tumor-associated antigens, to generate tumor-specific T cells, enhancing antitumor immunity.

For example, mesothelin and nucleolin tumor-associated antigens are significantly overexpressed in TNBCs, with mesothelin found in 34%–67% and nucleolin in 75%–80% of cases and their coexpression is associated with poor prognostic features, including high histological grade, and increased local recurrence.90, 91, 92 Treatment of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with mesothelin and nucleolin derived short peptides termed as pM-01 [SLLFLLFSL], pM-02 [VLPLTVAEV], pN-01 [KMAPPPKEV], and pN-02 [VLSNLSYSA], respectively generated antigen-specific T cells, and demonstrated a functionally immunogenic approach for TNBC targeting.43 The molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that all 4 peptides—pM-01, pM-02, pN-01, and pN-02—exhibit favorable structural compatibility with HLA-A02:01 (Fig. 8) and demonstrated dose-dependent HLA-A∗02:01 binding specifically for pM-02 and pN-01, as reflected by increased mean fluorescence intensity.

Similarly, patient-specific neoantigens-derived peptides identified by whole-exome sequencing, bioinformatics, and proteomics, showed effective binding to HLA class I alleles. The synthetic patient HLA binding peptide of net anionic character [MLREQLDQV] demonstrated enhanced MHC interaction and T cell receptor recognition, stimulated autologous CD8+ T cells and mediated CD8+ T cell anticancer response in 87.5% of donors. Combination of peptide with an oncolytic vaccinia virus for a murine TNBC mammary adenocarcinoma treatment induced robust neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cell response, slowed tumor growth, prolonged survival, and remodeled TME toward antitumor immunity.44

In another study, a multiepitope long peptide (MLP) vaccine targeting the MAGE-A4 antigen expressed on TNBC was evaluated for robust T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Three peptides MLP-1 [AESLFREALSNKVDELAHFLLRKYRAKEL], MLP-2 [AEMLERVIKNYKRCFPVIFGKASESLK], and MLP-3 [RALAETSYVKVLEHVVRVNARVRIAYPSLR] selected using bioinformatics analyses of MAGE-A4, were designed for effective binding with MHC molecules, facilitating robust T cell activation and immune recognition. The peptides stimulated human PBMCs exhibited increased expression of costimulatory molecules (CD137, CD80/CD86), elevated secretion of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α and enhanced cytotoxic activity against tumor cells. The MLP immunization of female Balb/c mice before implantation of 4T1-MAGE-A4+ TNBC cells significantly reduced tumor growth, prolonged survival, and enhanced effector memory T cell responses, which were further amplified by R848 coadministration.45

The immunomodulatory peptides that interact and block signaling pathways involved in TNBC growth while improving CTLs activity are also investigated. GK-1 an 18-amino acid immunomodulatory peptide [GYYYPSDPNTFYAPPYSA] originally identified in Taenia crassiceps, a parasitic cestode46 promoted adaptive immune responses via MHC interactions and suppressed tumor growth and metastasis while enhancing antitumor immunity. The cationic amphipathic GK-1 permeates biological membranes, and modulated TME in 4T1 orthotopic model. GK-1 treatment enhanced the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumor, spleen, and in lymph nodes, indicating increased T cell presence and reduced the expression of PD-1, a marker of T cell exhaustion, suggesting a restoration of T cell function. Together, these findings demonstrate that GK-1 not only promotes T cell accumulation and activation but also mitigates immune exhaustion.46 Histological evaluation further demonstrated decreased intratumoral vascularization, correlated with downregulation of key proangiogenic factors within the TME.

In comparison with peptides that mediate immune cell modulation by inhibiting a single pathway, a multifunctional peptide AXT201 [LRRFSTAPFAFININNVINF] that binds integrin and blocks multiple receptors including vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2, a hepatocyte growth factor receptor, and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor demonstrated potent anticancer efficacies in 2 orthotopic mouse TNBC models. AXT201 treatment of 4T1 and NT4, the latter being a basal-like TNBC cell line derived from Neu-N mice lacking HER2 expression, effectively reduced tumor growth, decreased vascular density, increased pericyte coverage, and improved survival. The treatment also selectively reduced suppressive Tregs while promoting activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, contributing to an improved antitumor immune microenvironment.47

The peptide-mediated immunomodulation of adaptive immune cells in TNBC underscores a strategic emphasis on generating tumor-specific cytotoxic responses and reshaping the immunosuppressive TME. Among the studied approaches development of tumor antigen–derived peptides that induce robust MHC-I–restricted CD8+ T cell responses, multifunctional immunomodulatory peptides that simultaneously enhance T cell activation and inhibit immunosuppressive pathways within the TME, and MLP vaccines capable of broad-spectrum T cell stimulation, promoting both effector and memory responses are highlighted. The adaptive immune–targeted peptides are being positioned as precision tools to restore effective antigen-specific immunity while remodeling the TME toward an antitumor phenotype.

However, issues such as differences in T cell receptor diversity between patients and murine models, the durability of immunomodulatory responses particularly in the context of chronic antigen exposure, and safety concerns related to off-target T cell activation are yet to be explored. Finally, most multifunctional peptide constructs require further evaluation in combination with standard-of-care therapies, to understand synergistic or antagonistic interactions.

Looking forward, the next generation of immunomodulation of adaptive immune cells will likely include rationally designed neoantigen cocktails tailored to individual patient HLA types, bifunctional peptides capable of simultaneous checkpoint inhibition and antigen presentation enhancement, or integration with adjuvants and nanoparticle carriers for improved lymph node targeting. Combination therapies with innate immune activators (eg, NK or DC-targeted peptides) or conventional cytotoxic agents may further potentiate T cell-mediated tumor clearance while maintaining immune homeostasis. Emerging technologies in single-cell immunoprofiling, spatially resolved mapping of T cell subsets, and longitudinal tracking of T cell receptor clonotypes will guide the rational design of peptides capable of inducing potent, durable, and tumor-restricted adaptive immune responses. Ultimately, successful clinical translation will require demonstrating potent T cell activation, sustained effector and memory responses, and effective tumor regression without inducing systemic toxicity or immune-related adverse events.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Immune checkpoints play crucial role in maintaining immune tolerance by recognizing self-antigens; however, with tumor progression, the expression of these checkpoints becomes dysregulated on both TNBC and immune cells, facilitating immune evasion.93 These immune checkpoints consist of ligands including PD-L1, CD80/CD86, MHC II, and galectin-9 on cancer cells that interact with corresponding receptors on CD8+ T cells through receptors including PD-1, CTL-associated antigen 4, lymphocyte-activating gene 3, T cell immunoglobulin mucin-3, and T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin ITIM domains.94 Among immune checkpoint pathways that suppress CD8+ T cell function, PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 are the most extensively studied and clinically targeted in TNBC. Although other receptors such as CTL-associated antigen 4, lymphocyte-activating gene 3, T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin ITIM domains, and T cell immunoglobulin mucin-3 also contribute to immune suppression, PD-1/PD-L1 blockade remains the cornerstone of current immunotherapy because of its proven efficacy and regulatory approval. Therefore, therapeutics that specifically engage with PD-1/PD-L1 axis are of key importance in the context of TNBC.

PD-L1 expressed on TNBC, interacts with PD-1 on T cells, inhibiting immune responses and enabling immune evasion, a key mechanism that allows TNBC to evade immune surveillance, making it an important target for immunotherapy.95 For TNBC treatment, pembrolizumab is the only US Food and Drug Administration-approved immune checkpoint inhibitor that blocks PD-1 receptor on T cells, preventing interaction with PD-L1 expressed on tumor and immune cells. This blockade restores T cell-mediated antitumor immunity and enhances cytotoxic response within the immunosuppressive TME. Although other PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors such as atezolizumab have shown activity in certain malignancies, they are not approved for TNBC, and therefore their clinical relevance in this context is limited96, 97, 98 Despite these advances, antibody-based therapies are frequently associated with significant immune-related adverse events, including pneumonitis, colitis, dermatitis, and endocrine toxicities, which limit their widespread clinical use.99 Furthermore, the large size of antibodies restricts their penetration into tumor tissues, potentially reducing therapeutic efficacies and increasing systemic exposure and off-target effects.100 To address these issues, peptide-based small molecule inhibitors are being explored as an alternative strategy.101,102

For example, YT-16, [YRCMISYGGADYKCIT], a synthetic peptide designed to specifically target PD-1 axis, demonstrated T cell specific cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells in a dose-dependent manner.48 Others combined, anticancer effects of chemotherapeutics with anti-PD-L1 peptides, in the form of drug loaded nanoparticles (PD-NPs). PD-NPs consisting of 3 key components: the anti-PD-L1 peptide [CVRARTR], a cathepsin B-cleavable linker (FRRG), and the chemotherapeutic agent DOX, accumulated in tumors via the enhanced permeability and retention effect and were internalized by cancer cells through PD-L1 receptor-mediated endocytosis (Fig. 9A and B), whereas cathepsin B linker degradation released DOX, causing anticancer efficacies (Fig. 9C). Simultaneously, PD-NPs blocked PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, promoting PD-L1 degradation, enhancing T cell activation and antitumor immunity marked by CD8+ T cell infiltration, reduced Treg population49 (Fig. 9D).

In another study therapeutic potential of PD-1/PD-L1 axis was combined with imaging agents with immunomodulation to simultaneously treat and monitor tumor growth. PD-L1 blocking peptide [CALNNCVRARTR]-functionalized with magnetic resonance imaging agent (NaGdF4 nanodots) used its positive charge, polar surface residues, and potential disulfide-stabilized structure to bind with PD-L1 effectively, mimicking key contact points of PD-1 and thereby inhibiting the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint interaction. The conjugation to NaGdF4 nanodots with the targeted peptide ensured precise tumor targeting and significantly boosted T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging contrast (T1-weighted: an imaging sequence that provided bright signals in regions where the nanodots accumulated, aiding in high-resolution tumor visualization).50

Given the significant interest in PD/PDL1 therapeutic potential, new peptides derived from ectodomain of PD-1 and PD-L1 are also developed using computational modeling approach.51 The peptides L7 and physiologically stable version mL7N [TEGMLLNVTSNLRVNA], generated by substituting threonine with asparagine at position 11 of the original peptide (L7) sequence demonstrated increased nuclear factor of activated T cells-driven luciferase activity, with mL7N showing stronger activation than anti-PD-1 antibodies (Fig. 10). The biological stability and activity of peptides was further improved by palmitic acid conjugation, yielding boosted IL-2 secretion, and demonstrated dose-dependent, inhibition of tumor growth exceeding anti-PD-1 antibody efficacies at higher doses, along with increased CD8+ T cell infiltration.51

Similarly, α-helical peptides derived from apolipoprotein A-I that could mimic PD-1/PD-L1 interactions were designed and screened using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. The peptides with strongest binding affinity (peptides 4F-16 [SHFSASYDKYAEKF] and 4F-32 and [DWFKAFYDKINETYNK]) inhibited the PD-1/PD-L1 interactions in coculture experiments with Jurkat and MDA-MB-231 cells, resulting in enhanced IL-2 secretion and restoration of T cell function. In 4T1 xenograft model, treatment with 4F-16 and 4F-32 significantly inhibited tumor growth and enhanced tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells with elevated granzyme B and IFN-γ levels, indicating effective antitumor immunity.52

In comparison with monotherapy, dual blockage of CD24/Siglec-10 and PD-1/PD-L1-with a D-amino acid peptide CSBP [IDVFLYSE] yielded superior antitumor efficacies by restoring macrophage phagocytosis and stimulating CD8+ T cells. The dual action CSBP generated by phage display method demonstrated enhanced binding affinity for CD24 and PD-L1 with hydrophobic residues of the peptide likely interacting with nonpolar surfaces on Siglec-10 and PD-L1, whereas the polar and acidic residues stabilized the peptide via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with CD24 and PD-1. This amphipathic arrangement enabled CSBP simultaneous blocking of CD24/Siglec-10 and PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, hence, increasing phagocytosis of 4T1 tumor cells by bone marrow derived macrophages and MDSCs. Furthermore, combination of CSBP with radiotherapy markedly reduced tumor burden in 4T1 orthotopic mouse model, increased tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and MDSCs and promoted macrophage polarization toward an M1 phenotype (CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ CD11c+ CD206−), indicative of proinflammatory and antitumor activity.53

The peptide-based modulation of immune checkpoints in TNBC highlights the potential for precise inhibition of suppressive pathways to restore antitumor immunity while reducing the systemic toxicity often associated with conventional antibodies. Across the studies discussed, the main theme is the strategic design of peptides that either directly block PD-1/PD-L1 interactions by mimicking natural receptor-ligand binding, integrating checkpoint inhibition with additional functionalities such as cytotoxic payload delivery or imaging, or simultaneously target multiple immunosuppressive axes, exemplified by constructs such as CSBP that engage both CD24/Siglec-10 and PD-1/PD-L1. This combination of immunomodulation with tumor targeting, and therapeutic monitoring within a single platform may define the roadmap for new theranostics for TNBC treatment.

Among the knowledge gaps identified, peptide inhibitors can mimic antibody functions, their in vivo stability, half-life, and bioavailability require careful optimization to ensure durable checkpoint inhibition. The multifunctional peptides, though promising, introduce uncertainties regarding off-target effects, potential immunogenicity, and should be evaluated for the optimal balance between innate and adaptive immune activation. Finally, the integration of peptide-based checkpoint inhibitors with conventional therapies (chemotherapy and radiotherapy) necessitates careful assessment of synergistic versus antagonistic effects on the immune milieu.

Immunogenic cell death peptide

ICD is a form of regulated cell death that not only eliminates tumor cells but also activates antigen-presenting cells through the release and exposure of DAMPs such as ATP and high mobility group box 1.103 In TNBC, where tumors often resist conventional therapies, triggering ICD can convert dying tumor cells into endogenous source of immune stimulation. This process promotes the maturation of DCs, the recruitment and activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, TAMs, neutrophils, and NK cells, thereby transforming the immunosuppressive TME into a more immunoreactive niche.104 Various treatment modalities—including anthracyclines, oxaliplatin, photodynamic therapy, and oncolytic viruses—are known to induce ICD by triggering cellular stress response and endoplasmic reticulum damage, however, their clinical application remains constrained by systemic toxicity, lack of selectivity, and TME-induced immunosuppression.105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 110 Rationally designed peptides can selectively disrupt tumor cell membranes or engage cellular stress pathways, leading to hallmark features of ICD such as calreticulin exposure and DAMP release. Many such peptides exhibit caspase-independent, ROS- and calcium-dependent killing mechanisms, which bypass common resistance pathways adopted by cancer cells. Furthermore, their dual role in DCs activation and proinflammatory cytokine production mediate tumor-specific cytolysis and immune stimulation. Compared with conventional ICD inducers, peptides offer enhanced biocompatibility, minimal off-target effects, and modular design for further optimization, making ICD-inducing peptides particularly attractive for immunotherapy applications in TNBC.111,112

For examples, a synthetic 24-amino-acid fragment [NGRKACLNPASPIVKKIIEKMLNS] of CXCL1, chemokinostatin-1,54 effectively disrupted cellular and mitochondrial integrity and activated apoptotic pathways in TNBCs. The membrane rupture in 4T1 cells postpeptide treatment, triggered rapid release of DAMPs (ATP within 30 minutes and HMGB-1 after 6 hours) activating DCs and promoting ICD.54 Similarly, TNBC cells membrane disruption by cationic amphipathic peptide, hainanenin-1 [FALGAVTKLLPSLLCMITRKC] released DAMPs and stimulated STING pathway leading to DCs activation. In an orthotopic 4T1 tumor model, hainanenin-1 treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth, promoting the infiltration of DCs and CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, while reducing the populations of Tregs and MDSCs.55

In another example, PKHB1 [KRFYVVMWKK], a synthetic peptide derived from the CD47-binding domain of thrombospondin-1, selectively induced ICD in cancer cells through a nonapoptotic, caspase-independent mechanism dependent on intracellular calcium signaling.113 PKHB1 treatment elevated cytosolic calcium, resulted in loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and ROS accumulation—hallmarks of cellular stress that initiate ICD in TNBCs.56 This led to rapid exposure of calreticulin and membrane-bound HSP70/90, along with extracellular release of ATP and HMGB1 collectively defining a robust DAMPs profile activating DCs, as evidenced by significant upregulation of CD86 and other costimulatory markers in DCs cocultured with PKHB1-killed cancer cells. Notably, the immune-stimulatory potential of PKHB1-treated cells was not merely a byproduct of cytotoxicity but reflects a programmed immunogenic process that converts dying tumor cells into a source of antigens and adjuvants.56

Combination of ICD specific peptide with chemotherapeutics further combined membrane disrupting and immunogenic properties of peptides with anticancer efficacies of drugs for TNBC treatment. In an orthotopic 4T1 model, an ICD specific peptide, LTX-315 [KKWWKKWDipK] and DOX cotreatment markedly inhibited tumor progression compared with monotherapies, and significantly extended median survival to 64.5 days compared with 25 days in the PBS-treated control, reflecting durable therapeutic response. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated increased infiltration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD45+ leukocytes within the tumor tissue, consistent with a robust immune response.57

In a recent study, short self-assembling peptides that selectively induce endoplasmic reticulum autophagy (ER-phagy) triggering pyroptosis, a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death that promotes ICD were developed. The peptides, collectively referred to as D-FFxFF, were designed to balance hydrophobicity for stable assembly and ER localization, whereas hydrophilicity and positive charge mediated membrane interactions and cellular uptake, a combination essential for activating the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3–gasdermin D pathway. Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 forms an inflammasome complex in response to stress, activating caspase-1 to cleave gasdermin D, and generated membrane pores causing cell swelling, lysis, and release of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. Cytotoxicity screening across TNBC (4T1, MDA-MB-231) and non-TNBC (HeLa, A375, MCF-7) cell lines showed cancer cell dependent cytotoxicity of the peptide D-FFcFF and demonstrated autophagy-linked pyroptosis. In vivo, an orthotopic 4T1 tumor model treated with D-FFcFF showed marked tumor growth inhibition along with enhanced DCs maturation (CD80+/CD86+) and increased infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, demonstrating a robust antitumor immune response.58

c often fails to generate sufficient ICD because of the poor tumor accumulation and limited ROS production, leading to weak immune activation.114 Combination of ICD-inducing peptides with PDT in the form of a hybrid nanosystem comprised of serum albumin functionalized boehmite nanoparticles (AlO(OH)) loaded with photosensitizer chlorin e6 and the peptide melittin [GIGAVLKVLTTGLPALISWIKRKRQQ], a membrane disrupting peptide, was developed. The nanoplatform synthesized enhanced cellular uptake of chlorin e6, along with membrane perturbation capabilities, amplifying ROS generation during PDT. In an orthotopic 4T1 tumor model, laser irradiation of nanoparticles significantly inhibited tumor growth and increased ICD markers, including calreticulin and ATP. The treatment enhanced DCs activation and infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ and helper CD4+ T cells, while reducing Tregs, indicating a robust antitumor immune response.59

The peptide-mediated induction of ICD in TNBC represents a strategic approach to eliminate tumor cells while transforming the immunosuppressive TME into an immunoreactive niche. Peptides can directly disrupt membranes to trigger DAMP release, a hallmark of ICD, and their combination with chemotherapeutic, photodynamic modalities, intracellular stress pathways, such as ER-phagy and inflammasome activation may promote both tumor cytotoxicity and immune priming, establishing a feed-forward loop that enhances DC maturation, T cell infiltration, and NK cell recruitment.

However, stability, pharmacokinetics, and tumor penetration of ICD-inducing peptides in human TNBC require optimization to ensure effective DAMP release and sustained immune activation. The interplay between ICD-driven innate and adaptive immune responses in heterogeneous TME requires further understating, particularly regarding the balance between CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and immunosuppressive populations (Tregs and MDSCs). The multifunctional peptide platforms combining ICD induction with chemotherapeutics or PDT introduce complexities in dosing, toxicity management, and potential off-target effects that should be studied in detail.

Looking forward, the next generation of ICD peptides will likely integrate precise tumor targeting, enhanced stability, and multifunctionality with immune context awareness. Strategies may include peptide conjugation with nanocarriers for selective tumor delivery, rational design to engage multiple ICD pathways, and combination with immunostimulatory adjuvants to amplify adaptive immunity. Ultimately, clinical translation will require demonstrating robust ICD induction, durable T cell and NK cell responses, and reshaping of the immunosuppressive TME, all while minimizing systemic toxicity, paving the way for ICD peptides as versatile immunotherapeutic agents in TNBC.

Conclusions and future directions

Peptide-based immunomodulation of the TME offers a promising strategy to enhance therapeutic outcomes in TNBC. Leveraging their physicochemical properties, peptides can influence both innate and adaptive immune responses. These peptides are typically designed using natural or synthetic templates, including antimicrobial peptides, tumor-homing sequences, or immune-modulating motifs, and are optimized through rational design or computational modeling to achieve desired immunological functions. Solid-phase peptide synthesis, the most common method for peptide synthesis, enables precise synthesis of peptide sequences with controlled composition, length, and modifications. Amphipathic and hydrophobic regions of peptide facilitate membrane penetration and intracellular delivery, whereas cationic residues promote selective binding to the cell surface and immune receptors. Collectively, these attributes allow peptides to reprogram TAMs, activate NK cells, DCs, neutrophils, and T cells, inhibit immune checkpoints such as PD-1/PD-L1, and induce ICD by disrupting tumor membranes and releasing DAMPs.

Several peptides have been shown to effectively modulate innate immune cell responses. In TAMs, peptides can selectively deplete M2-like immunosuppressive populations or repolarize them toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype, thereby restoring phagocytic function and enhancing T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Neutrophil-targeted peptides regulate key neutrophil functions within the TME by inhibiting NET formation, reducing chemokine-mediated recruitment, and suppressing protumorigenic signaling pathways such as IL-8–mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Peptides tailored for DCs promote DC maturation and upregulate costimulatory molecules, including CD80 and CD86, while enhancing IL-12 secretion. DCs improve antigen processing and presentation via MHC class I, facilitate CD8+ T cell recruitment and activation, and induce the release of proinflammatory cytokines. NK cell–associated peptides enhance cytotoxicity by promoting degranulation, increasing perforin and granzyme B secretion, and stimulate IFN-γ production, resulting in direct tumor cell apoptosis. NK cells also modulate inhibitory signaling pathways in the TME, and their activity can be further enhanced in combination with immunostimulatory agents, such as STING or TLR agonists, or with membrane-disruptive approaches that facilitate tumor cell lysis.

Although, activation of innate immune system does affect adaptive immunity ultimately, the peptide-based strategies are also studied to specifically stimulate adaptive immune response by promoting antigen-specific CD8+ T cell activation and enhancing CTL response. These peptides can increase T cell infiltration into tumors, elevate secretion of effector cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, and induce potent tumor-specific cytotoxicity. In addition, the peptides can modulate immunosuppressive pathways within the TME by reducing inhibitory checkpoint expression and limiting Tregs activity, thereby further supporting CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor response and inhibiting tumor progression.

Peptides that block or disrupt PD-1/PD-L1 interactions overcome checkpoint-mediated inhibition, leading to enhanced CD8+ T cell activity and reduced Tregs function within the TME. When combined with imaging agents, such as gadolinium-based nanodots, or nanocarriers, such as polymeric or lipid-based nanoparticles, these peptides enable simultaneous tumor visualization and localized checkpoint blockade. Furthermore, dual-targeting approaches that disrupt PD-1/PD-L1 together with other immunosuppressive pathways, such as CD24/Siglec-10, improve antigen-specific T cell response and promote macrophage polarization toward an antitumor phenotype. Peptide-based induction of ICD converts TNBC into immunogenic niches by releasing DAMPs, promoting DCs maturation, and activating CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and M1-like TAMs. Mechanistically, peptides promote ICD through pathways such as membrane disruption, ROS generation, calcium-dependent stress responses, and endoplasmic reticulum–associated cell death, leading to enhanced effector T cell infiltration and favorable CD8+/Treg ratios.

Despite significant potential of peptides as immunotherapeutics, aggregation and degradation of peptides in biological milieu remains a major concern. Structural modifications, including cyclization and stapling, has the potential to improve peptide stability, bioavailability, and resistance to proteolytic degradation. With emerging emphasis on precision medicine, computational design and artificial intelligence driven modeling can be used for rapid identification of high-affinity, TNBC-targeted immunomodulatory peptides. The immunotherapeutics tailored to patient-specific tumor antigens can be developed by the combination of artificial intelligence with peptide synthesis approaches hence, maximizing the potential of therapeutic precision. Furthermore, combinatorial strategies, integrating immunomodulatory peptide with checkpoint inhibitors, chemo- or radiotherapeutics can yield synergistic results with improved anticancer efficacies. Although much work on immunomodulatory approaches is focused on TAMs, multifunctional peptides capable of activating DCs, NK cells, and CTLs, while inducing ICD are being prioritized to enhance immune responses within TME. Additionally, engineering peptides to modulate tumor vasculature and alleviate hypoxia-driven immunosuppression offers a promising strategy to overcome TME barriers, positioning immunomodulatory peptides as versatile tools for next generation TNBC immunotherapy.
