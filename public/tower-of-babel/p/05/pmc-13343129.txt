Sonochemical cavitation–driven interfacial-selective nucleation of silver nanoparticles via silver carboxylate precursors for Ag/Si nanocomposites☆
Authors: Haruto Tsuchiya, Haruki Kawamoto, Hirotsugu Takizawa, Minoru Ueshima, Yamato Hayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Silver nanoparticles exhibit excellent electrical [1], [2] and thermal [3], [4] conductivities, oxidation resistance [5], and low-temperature sintering properties [6], making them widely used as catalysts, conductive materials, and antimicrobial materials [7], [8], [9]. In particular, the silver-sintered bodies used in power-semiconductor bonding applications have a high melting point (961 °C) and high conductivity. However, stress concentration and reliability degradation caused by the mismatch in the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) between silver (19.7 × 10-6/℃) and semiconductor chips (e.g., SiC, 4.4 × 10-6/℃) or ceramic substrates remain significant challenges [10], [11], [12]. To address this issue, Ag/Si nanocomposites incorporating low-CTE Si particles (2.6 × 10-6/℃) are an effective design strategy. In addition to reducing the effective thermal expansion coefficient, such composite structures may provide opportunities for reducing Ag consumption through localized Ag utilization at functional interfaces. The concept of this approach is illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Schematic comparison between silver-only solder and Ag/Si nanocomposites for semiconductor bonding, illustrating reduced thermal stress via mitigation of CTE mismatch.

Schematic comparison between silver-only solder and Ag/Si nanocomposites for semiconductor bonding, illustrating reduced thermal stress via mitigation of CTE mismatch.

Physical grinding and chemical reduction methods [13], [14] are widely used to produce silver nanoparticles, but they present challenges such as broad particle size distribution, the need for high-temperature conditions, and complex counterion and dispersant removal processes [13], [15]. By contrast, the sonochemical method utilizing ultrasonic irradiation provides a reaction environment capable of inducing precursor decomposition and reduction without heating the entire solution [16], [17], [18], [19]. This is achieved through the formation of hot spots (several thousand kelvin and hundreds to thousands of atmospheres) [20], [21], [22] and microjets [23], [24], [25], [26] generated during the collapse of acoustic cavitation bubbles. Numerous reports have documented the reduction of metal salts and the formation of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles under ultrasonic irradiation [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], demonstrating the potential of low-temperature nanomaterial synthesis utilizing radical generation and localized heating. While these reactions typically depend on bulk temperature in conventional thermal processes, the ultrasonic reaction field periodically generates nanosecond-scale localized high-temperature and high-pressure environments. This allows precursor formation and decomposition to proceed under nonequilibrium conditions. Such nonequilibrium reaction fields may play an important role in inducing interfacial-selective nucleation.

However, most previous reports have focused on particle formation via homogeneous nucleation in solution, often requiring surfactants or dispersants for dispersion stabilization [35], [36]. Furthermore, systematic studies on how the precursor structure and solvent properties influence the nucleation modes (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous nucleation) within ultrasonic reaction fields are scarce, and examples of intentionally designed interfacial-selective nucleation remain limited.

A silver carboxylate intermediate with a layered structure, formed using an organic precursor paint-reduction method, has been reported in recent years [37], [38]. The Ag ion layer within this intermediate generates extremely fine Ag nuclei during the initial reduction stage, enabling the formation of high-aspect-ratio nanostructures. Silver carboxylates possess a structure in which the Ag ions and carboxylic acid layers are alternately stacked [39]. Localized reduction within this layered structure limits the diffusion distance between Ag atoms, enabling the formation of fine, high-density nuclei [40]. A schematic of the proposed mechanism is shown in Fig. 2. This finding suggests that silver carboxylate functions as not only a silver source but also a structural precursor that can influence the nucleation sites and growth behavior.Fig. 2Schematic illustration of the layered silver carboxylate structure, illustrating suppression of silver nanoparticle growth and aggregation via spatial confinement within the stacked organic layers.

This study investigates a dispersant-free sonochemical route for the formation of Ag nanoparticle-supported Si composite particles using Ag2O, mixed carboxylic acids, and Si powder under low-temperature conditions. The Ag/Si system was selected as a model platform for examining how precursor chemistry and ultrasonic irradiation influence Ag nanoparticle formation at solid–liquid interfaces. This was achieved by reacting silver oxide with two carboxylic acids (formic and acetic acid) under ultrasonic irradiation to form silver carboxylate intermediates, which were associated with subsequent Ag nanoparticle formation on Si-particle surfaces. The Ag/Si system was employed as an experimental platform to examine how the formation and transformation behavior of silver carboxylate intermediates, solvent properties, and ultrasonic irradiation influence Ag nanoparticle formation at solid–liquid interfaces. Particular attention was paid to how the stability differences between silver formate and silver acetate, along with variations in the solvent dielectric constant, may influence the chemical state of the Ag species and the nucleation behavior within the ultrasonic cavitation field. Specifically, the composition of the carboxylic acid and solvent properties are potential factors governing the Ag supply behavior and selectivity of interfacial nucleation. Furthermore, clarifying how chemical effects (hot-spot reactions and radical generation) and physical effects (surface renewal via microjets and enhanced mass transport) [22] derived from acoustic cavitation contribute to precursor formation and decomposition behavior is crucial for understanding interfacial-selective nanoparticle formation.

In this study, the ultrasonic irradiation temperature, carboxylic acid composition ratio, and solvent type were systematically varied to examine the formation and transformation behavior of silver carboxylate intermediates and the resulting Ag nanoparticle formation in the Ag2O–carboxylic acid–Si system. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to investigate the relationship between silver carboxylate transformation and the phase evolution and morphology of the products. The results demonstrate that a low-temperature, dispersant-free process can form Ag nanoparticles from Ag2O in the presence of mixed carboxylic acids and Si particles. They further indicate that the carboxylic acid composition, solvent type, and ultrasonic irradiation conditions influence precursor transformation and are associated with preferential Ag nanoparticle formation on Si-particle surfaces. Based on these observations, a cavitation-assisted mechanism for silver carboxylate-mediated Ag formation at Si-particle interfaces is discussed.

Experimental

Chemicals

Ag2O (99.0+%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) and silicon powder (average particle size 5 μm, Sakai Chemical Industry) were used as starting materials. Formic acid (HCOOH; 98.0%, FUJIFILM Wako) and acetic acid (CH3COOH; 99.7%, FUJIFILM Wako) were used as carboxylic acids, and ethanol (99.5%, FUJIFILM Wako) and isopropanol (99.7%, FUJIFILM Wako) were used as solvents. All reagents were used as received without further purification.

Equipment

Fig. 3A shows a diagram of the ultrasonic irradiation setup. Ultrasonic irradiation was performed using a sonoreactor system (Honda Electronics). This apparatus consisted of a synthesizer (WF19458, NF), matching box (Honda Electronics), and power amplifier (Electronics and Innovation). The sonoreactor was connected to a transducer (Honda Electronics) placed in a water bath. Ultrasonic waves were transmitted through water to the reaction solution in a 300 mL conical flask. The temperatures of the water bath and solution inside the flask during ultrasonic irradiation were kept constant using a thermostatic circulator (CTP-3000, Tokyo Rikakikai).Fig. 3(A) Schematic diagram of the ultrasonic irradiation system and (B) synthesis procedure.

(A) Schematic diagram of the ultrasonic irradiation system and (B) synthesis procedure.

Preparation

Fig. 3B illustrates the flow of the synthesis. Silver oxide and silicon powder were weighed to yield 5 g of Ag/Si composite particles and 10 vol% supported Ag, and then added to a 300 mL conical flask along with 200 mL of an alcohol solvent. Next, formic and acetic acids were mixed in a predetermined composition ratio, ensuring the total amount of carboxylic acid was equimolar to that of Ag. The resulting mixture was then added to a flask. Subsequently, ultrasonic irradiation (43 kHz, 100 W) was applied.

The temperature and solvent were fixed at 50 °C and ethanol, respectively, and the carboxylic acid composition ratio was varied as shown in Table 1. These samples were designated as (a)–(e). For samples (f) and (g), the composition ratio was fixed at 9:1 (HCOOH:CH3COOH), 2-propanol was used as the solvent, and the temperature was set to 50 and 60 °C, respectively. Ultrasonic irradiation was generally performed for up to 3 h, and samples were collected every hour. However, for sample (g), samples were collected every 30 min. Characterization

XRD (D2 Phaser, Bruker) was used to identify the crystalline phases of the products. Cu-Kα radiation was employed as the radiation source, with a measurement range of 10–80° (2θ). Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-7610F, JEOL) was used to analyze the particle morphology.

Results and discussion

Examination of carboxylic acid composition

The effect of the carboxylic acid composition of the precursor on the formation behavior of the Ag/Si particles was investigated. Fig. 4A and 4B show the XRD patterns of samples (a)–(e) (3 h). Under formic acid–rich conditions, the peaks originating from silver formate disappeared, and the metallic Ag diffraction peaks significantly intensified. By contrast, under acetate-rich conditions, peaks corresponding to silver acetate were observed, and the increase in the intensity of the silver peak was limited.Fig. 4(A) XRD patterns of samples (a)–(e) after 3 h, (B) XRD patterns of samples (a) and (e) after 1 and 3 h, and (C) SEM images of raw Si and samples (a)–(e) after 3 h.

(A) XRD patterns of samples (a)–(e) after 3 h, (B) XRD patterns of samples (a) and (e) after 1 and 3 h, and (C) SEM images of raw Si and samples (a)–(e) after 3 h.

Notably, even for the sample subjected to 3 h of ultrasonic irradiation under acetate-rich conditions, peaks originating from silver acetate were clearly observed. This suggests that the decomposition of silver acetate was limited, even under the ultrasonic irradiation conditions used in this study, specifically within the hot-spot environment generated by cavitation collapse. Thus, silver acetate functioned as a relatively stable silver reservoir phase in this system.

Furthermore, SEM (Fig. 4C) revealed localized aggregates of silver nanoparticles under formic acid–rich conditions, whereas needle-like crystals primarily attributed to silver acetate [39] were observed under acetate-rich conditions. Ag formate decomposes readily at relatively low temperatures and is, therefore, a rapid source of Ag. By contrast, silver acetate was more thermally stable [41], [42]. Therefore, under the ultrasonic conditions used in this study, the decomposition likely proceeded insufficiently, leaving it unreacted.

This difference in precursor stability may have led to more rapid silver release from silver carboxylate under formic acid–rich conditions, potentially forming localized highly supersaturated regions. Consequently, homogeneous nucleation in the solution phase may have partially competed with interfacial nucleation, leading to the aggregation of AgNPs observed in the SEM images. Conversely, under acetate-rich conditions, the reduced Ag supply rate likely suppresses sufficient Ag formation, leaving the silver carboxylate crystals intact. Differences in vapor pressure and volatility between formic acid and acetic acid may alter the vapor composition within cavitation bubbles and thereby affect local collapse conditions, including transient temperature, pressure, and radical-generation behavior. These physical-acoustic effects may contribute to the formation and decomposition behavior of silver carboxylate intermediates. Although the present study did not directly evaluate cavitation intensity or radical generation, the observed phase evolution is consistent with the different thermal stabilities of the silver carboxylate species. Therefore, the present results suggest that precursor stability is an important factor, while volatility-dependent changes in the cavitation field may also contribute to the observed reaction behavior.

These results suggest that the balance between precursor transformation and Ag formation is associated with the observed particle morphology. Under the examined conditions, formic acid-rich compositions promoted Ag formation, whereas acetate-rich compositions resulted in the persistence of silver carboxylate phases.

Key factors governing Ag supply dynamics

As discussed in the previous section, metallic Ag formation is significantly suppressed under acetate-rich conditions. Therefore, we attempted to increase the Ag supply by modifying the solvent to control the cavitation field; however, no major differences were observed in the XRD patterns or SEM images. This suggests that the decomposition of silver acetate may depend strongly on the intrinsic thermodynamic stability of the precursor rather than on differences in the cavitation behavior. This indicates that although the solvent can modulate the reaction field, it is unlikely to substantially reduce the activation energy required for silver acetate decomposition.

Examination of solvent and temperature terms

As mentioned in the previous section, the Ag supply mechanism via cavitation is maintained under formic acid–rich conditions. However, the increased reduction rate suggests that homogeneous nucleation may partially compete. Therefore, this section examines how changing the solvent influences Ag nanoparticle formation while maintaining formic acid-rich conditions.

The XRD patterns of samples (f) and (g), synthesized in 2-propanol, are shown in Fig. 5A. Sample (f) (1 h) gave rise to a peak originating from the starting material, silver oxide, which was not clearly visible in the ethanol system, suggesting a relative delay in the decomposition and reduction of silver. The SEM image (Fig. 5B) revealed that under condition (f) (2-propanol, 50 °C, HCOOH:CH3COOH = 9:1), ultrasonic irradiation for 2 h resulted in highly dispersed, fine silver nanoparticles supported on the Si surface. This morphology is consistent with preferential formation of fine Ag nanoparticles on the Si-particle surfaces under the examined conditions. In addition to the relaxation of the reduction rate, the low dielectric constant of 2-propanol is likely to reduce the solvation stability of Ag+ ions, which may in turn limit the formation rate of reactive Ag species and decrease the effective Ag supply flux. These solvent-dependent effects may reduce the rate of Ag-species transformation and influence the local Ag supply behavior, thereby contributing to conditions under which fine Ag nanoparticles are preferentially observed on Si-particle surfaces.

Furthermore, for sample (g) prepared at 60 °C, fine Ag nanoparticles supported on the Si-particle surfaces were already observed after 30 min of ultrasonic irradiation. The mean particle diameter at this time was 19.6 ± 6.8 nm, which was comparable to that observed for sample (f) at 50 °C after 2 h of irradiation (18.0 ± 6.0 nm). This comparison indicates that increasing the reaction temperature from 50 to 60 °C shortened the time required to obtain a fine Ag nanoparticle-supported structure under the examined 2-propanol conditions.

After 1 h of ultrasonic irradiation at 60 °C, the mean particle diameter increased to 27.3 ± 11.1 nm, accompanied by a broadening of the particle-size distribution. This temporal change is consistent with continued particle growth and/or coalescence after the initial formation of fine Ag nanoparticles. Thus, under both samples (f) and (g), a fine and relatively dispersed Ag nanoparticle-supported structure was obtained only within a limited irradiation-time window, whereas prolonged irradiation promoted particle growth.

The shorter time required to obtain fine Ag nanoparticles at 60 °C is consistent with faster overall precursor transformation and Ag formation at the higher temperature. However, because the formation of nuclei and the conversion behavior of individual Ag-containing species were not directly monitored, the present results do not allow the nucleation pathway or the relative contributions of homogeneous and interfacial processes to be quantitatively distinguished. The similar particle morphology observed for samples (f) and (g) is therefore interpreted as being consistent with preferential formation of fine Ag nanoparticles on Si-particle surfaces under the examined 2-propanol conditions, rather than as direct evidence that a single nucleation pathway was maintained.

The low dielectric constant of 2-propanol may affect the solvation and transformation behavior of Ag-containing species and thereby influence the local Ag supply behavior. In combination with the lower reducing ability of the oxidation product of 2-propanol relative to that of ethanol, these solvent-dependent effects may contribute to the slower overall Ag formation observed in the 2-propanol system and to the formation of fine Ag nanoparticles on Si-particle surfaces. However, quantitative clarification of the relationship among solvent properties, dissolved and solid Ag-containing species, precursor transformation, and nanoparticle formation will require time-resolved analysis of both liquid- and solid-phase species.

Similarly, prolonged ultrasonic irradiation of samples (f) and (g) led to the coarsening and partial aggregation of the silver nanoparticles. This indicates that, after the initial formation of highly dispersed nuclei, particle growth and coalescence became dominant at longer reaction times. To quantitatively evaluate the nanoparticle growth behavior, particle size distributions were determined from SEM images for samples (f) and (g) (Fig. 5C). For sample (f), the average particle diameter increased from 18 nm after 2 h to 35 nm after 3 h of ultrasonic irradiation. Similarly, for sample (g), the average diameter increased from 20 nm after 30 min to 27 nm after 1 h. In both cases, the particle size distribution shifted toward larger diameters with increasing irradiation time, indicating that the Ag nanoparticles continued to grow after the initial nucleation stage. Despite the increase in particle size with prolonged irradiation, the nanoparticles remained within the nanometer size range and no large micrometer-scale Ag aggregates were observed, indicating that a highly dispersed supported structure was maintained on the Si particle surfaces. Therefore, a highly dispersed supported structure was observed within a limited irradiation-time window, before further particle growth and partial coalescence became more pronounced.

To further examine the role of the ultrasound parameters, the frequency dependence was evaluated and found to be relatively weak, causing only minor changes in the particle morphology (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). Although the frequency-dependent experiments shown in the Supporting Information were conducted in methanol during the preliminary stage of this study, these results are provided to qualitatively demonstrate that ultrasonic frequency can influence the reaction rate and Ag deposition behavior. Because the solvent, carboxylic-acid composition, irradiation time, and other reaction conditions differed from those used in the main experiments, a direct quantitative comparison between the methanol-based frequency-dependent experiments and the ethanol- or 2-propanol-based results is not intended. By contrast, the reaction did not proceed effectively in the absence of ultrasonic irradiation, and residual organic crystalline species (presumably silver carboxylates) were detected (Fig. S2, Supporting Information), indicating that acoustic cavitation plays an essential role in both the precursor transformation and nanoparticle formation.

The solvent effect observed in this study is not necessarily attributable solely to differences in reducing ability. It may also reflect solvent-dependent changes in the solvation and transformation behavior of Ag-containing species, as well as changes in the cavitation-associated reaction environment. The observed precipitation behavior is therefore discussed as the combined result of the carboxylic acid composition, which was associated with the stability and transformation behavior of silver carboxylate intermediates, and the solvent type, which was associated with differences in the resulting Ag nanoparticle morphology. Although the nucleation process was not observed directly in this study, this interpretation provides a reasonable and consistent explanation for the observed XRD and SEM trends.

The transport and interfacial immobilization of silver carboxylate play important roles in the formation of AgNPs on the Si surface. The generated silver carboxylate species are considered to be transported toward the Si-particle surfaces under the combined influence of the solvent environment and ultrasonic irradiation. The solvent may affect the solvation and transformation behavior of Ag-containing species, whereas cavitation-induced fluid motion may contribute to transport and contact between precursor species and Si-particle surfaces. In addition to dielectric properties, alcohol solvents may influence the sonochemical reaction environment through interactions with cavitation-generated reactive species. Alcohols can act as hydroxyl-radical scavengers and may also generate solvent-derived reactive species during ultrasonic irradiation. These effects may modify the balance of reactive species involved in precursor formation and transformation. Because radical species were not directly detected in the present study, the solvent dependence observed here is discussed as the combined result of solvation effects, solvent-dependent radical chemistry, and cavitation-associated precursor transformation. Furthermore, a native SiO2 layer is present on the Si particle surface. Its silanol groups are expected to interact via hydrogen bonding with carboxyl groups [43], [44], [45], potentially allowing silver carboxylate to be stably anchored at the interface. Additionally, microjets and cavitation-induced fluid motion may contribute to local transport, collision frequency, and interfacial contact between Ag-containing precursor species and Si-particle surfaces [23], [24], [25], [26]. These effects may facilitate interfacial immobilization and Ag nanoparticle formation; however, their individual contributions were not directly quantified in the present study.

The silver carboxylate localized at the interface decomposes and is reduced (Equation (3)) owing to the hot-spot environment and the reducing action of alcohol. Silver ions are thought to be locally reduced within the layered structure of silver carboxylate, leading to the formation of fine silver nuclei [39], [40]. Initially, the presence of carboxylic acid components around the nucleus temporarily suppresses particle aggregation. However, as the reduction progressed and the organic components were released, contact sintering between adjacent particles advanced, leading to the formation of a highly dispersed and dense AgNP phase. Particle growth in this system is likely to be strongly influenced by the contribution of the instantaneous, localized high-temperature and high-pressure fields associated with cavitation collapse, rather than the bulk solution temperature. In the later stages of the reaction, cavitation-induced physical effects contribute to the selective retention of AgNPs on the Si particle surfaces. Specifically, particles with weak interfacial bonding or heavily aggregated species may be removed by shock waves and microjets [23], [24], [25], [26], leaving only firmly anchored silver nuclei. Furthermore, silver carboxylate intermediates may be formed and consumed during the reaction. Carboxylic-acid-derived species may react with residual Ag2O or other oxidized Ag-containing precursor species to form additional silver carboxylate intermediates. Thus, the precipitated silver carboxylate phase is considered to be associated with the Ag supply process during nanoparticle formation.

Therefore, the formation of Ag nanoparticles in this system is discussed as a multistage precursor-transformation process in which silver carboxylate formation, transformation, transport, and interfacial interaction may occur concurrently under ultrasonic irradiation. The present results indicate that the carboxylic acid composition is associated with the stability and transformation behavior of silver carboxylate intermediates, whereas the solvent type is associated with differences in the resulting Ag nanoparticle morphology. Ultrasonic irradiation is essential for the observed precursor transformation and Ag nanoparticle formation under the examined conditions. Together, these observations are consistent with a cavitation-assisted interfacial process that favors the formation of finely dispersed Ag nanoparticles on Si-particle surfaces. However, the detailed transport pathway, nucleation kinetics, and individual contributions of cavitation-associated chemical and physical effects remain to be clarified by future quantitative studies.

Conclusion

This study investigated the sonochemical synthesis of Ag/Si nanocomposites in which highly dispersed silver nanoparticles were supported on Si particle surfaces. In a reaction system using silver oxide and carboxylic acids as raw materials, ultrasonic irradiation induces the formation of silver carboxylate intermediates. By controlling the formation and decomposition behaviors of these intermediates, a dispersant-free process for supporting AgNPs was achieved.

The results reveal that both the carboxylic acid composition and solvent type significantly influence nanoparticle loading behavior. The carboxylic acid composition affects the stability of the silver carboxylate intermediate and the supply behavior of Ag species, while solvent properties appear to influence the chemical state of the Ag species and the nucleation behavior. The results indicate that the carboxylic acid composition and solvent type influence the formation and transformation behavior of silver carboxylate intermediates and are associated with preferential formation of Ag nanoparticles on Si-particle surfaces under the examined conditions.

The observed phase evolution and morphology are consistent with a cavitation-assisted precursor-transformation process. Cavitation-associated chemical and physical effects may contribute to the formation, transformation, transport, and interfacial immobilization of silver carboxylate species; however, their individual contributions were not directly quantified in the present study.

As this process does not require dispersants, it offers advantages for applications in sintered materials by minimizing residue contamination. The present results show that the formation of finely dispersed Ag nanoparticles on Si-particle surfaces is associated with the combined effects of silver carboxylate precursor stability, solvent-dependent reaction behavior, and ultrasonic irradiation conditions. In the present system, formic acid-rich conditions promoted Ag formation, whereas acetate-rich conditions resulted in the persistence of silver carboxylate phases.

The use of 2-propanol under the examined conditions enabled the formation of fine Ag nanoparticles on Si-particle surfaces, while prolonged ultrasonic irradiation promoted particle growth and broadening of the particle-size distribution. These observations provide experimentally based considerations for controlling Ag nanoparticle formation in dispersant-free ultrasonic solid–liquid reaction systems.
