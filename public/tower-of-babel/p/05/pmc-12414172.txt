Retrospective population-based study of 132 000 Canadians on the relationship between community belonging and diabetes incidence
Authors: Sarah M Mah, Mackenzie Hurst, Mindy Lu, Laura C Rosella
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCommunity belonging is a dimension of subjective well-being that is of growing public health interest for mitigating chronic disease. However, there is limited longitudinal evidence that such a relationship exists. We assessed the effect of community belonging on the subsequent 5-year risk of diabetes.Research design and methodsThis population-based cohort study consisted of 132 295 Ontario respondents of the Canadian Community Health Survey (2000–2014) aged 30–75 years and diabetes-free. Individuals were linked to a validated diabetes registry and followed for 5 years or until 31 March 2020. Survey respondents rated their sense of belonging to community on a 4-point scale (very strong, somewhat strong, somewhat weak or very weak belonging). We estimated the effect of community belonging on 5-year diabetes incidence using Cox proportional hazards models. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to account for sociodemographic and behavioural confounders. Analyses were also stratified, weighted and modelled by key individual-level characteristics.ResultsAt survey response, 9.5% of the sample reported having very weak sense of belonging to their communities. By the end of study follow-up, 5.3% of the sample developed diabetes. IPT weighting achieved balance between community belonging groups across the sample and subgroups. Respondents with a very weak sense of belonging exhibited higher risk of diabetes (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.46, compared with those with somewhat weak, somewhat strong and very strong sense of belonging), even after accounting for individual-level factors via IPTW (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01, 1.34). Those with very weak belonging exhibited elevated risk of diabetes across most subgroups, and this association remained conclusive after IPT weighting for Canadian-born and white participants.ConclusionsThe association between weak sense of belonging to community and diabetes risk demonstrated by this study highlights the critical role that social connections play in chronic disease epidemiology. Our findings signal a need to include social and community factors in population health strategies for chronic disease prevention.

Introduction

There is increasing interest in supporting healthy and resilient communities from a variety of sectors, particularly in the context of the growing burden of chronic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, which threaten the health and well-being of populations.1 Traditional risk factors for diabetes such as weight status and health behaviours have been extensively studied, but aspects of subjective well-being related to social connections that are known to influence health and health-related behaviours have not been thoroughly investigated for their potential significance to chronic disease prevention. Social networks could be an important factor for diabetes risk and management.2

Among various measures of social connection, sense of belonging to community—the degree to which individuals perceive themselves to be connected to their community3—has been linked to a number of self-rated health outcomes,47 health behaviours,8 avoidable hospitalisation9 and premature mortality.10 Community belonging is a unique measure that captures neighbourhood aspects of social capital,11 place attachment and rootedness.12 Previous research has demonstrated that individuals’ sense of belonging to community is associated with their neighbourhood’s broader collective sense of belonging, reinforcing the notion that this construct functions at the level of neighbourhoods and communities.13 Community belonging may also be important for those already diagnosed with diabetes. A Canadian study showed higher odds of diabetes-related hospitalisations among adults with pre-existing diabetes who reported having a weak sense of belonging to community.14

While not the focus of this paper, several pathways through which community belonging might influence diabetes risk offer conceptual grounding for studying this relationship.6 15 Social ecological models have been used to describe the impact of social networks on health15 and type 2 diabetes risk2 and suggest that these effects could be a result of: (1) overarching ‘macro-level’ sociocultural conditions that impact community resources for diabetes prevention; (2) ‘meso-level’ aspects of one’s social network (eg, size, number of contacts, frequency of contact) that impact one’s access to resources; (3) ‘micro-level’ psychosocial pathways that allow for social support, engagement and information sharing and finally, (4) the behavioural, psychological and physiological mechanisms that lead to chronic disease. Consistent with the ‘stress-buffering’ hypothesis,16 individuals who feel less connected to their communities may be less likely to have access to the psychosocial supports that mitigate stress, anxiety and adverse mental health states linked to higher risk for type 2 diabetes.17 Fewer connections to one’s local community may also mean poorer access to material resources and lower engagement with preventive healthcare services and information. On the other hand, stronger links to one’s community might translate to greater emotional and instrumental support for those managing or at risk of developing diabetes. Being part of communities with health-promoting social norms could promote and reinforce more favourable health behaviours and, in turn, lead to lower chronic disease risk—an idea consistent with the ‘social contagion’ hypothesis.18 One piece of evidence to support this hypothesis comes from a Canadian study which observed a dose-response relationship between higher sense of belonging and positive changes in exercise and diet.8

The evidence base for the relationship between social connections and reductions in diabetes-related risks and complications is growing.2 Examining the impact of community belonging, an aspect of social connection that has been shown to be associated with a variety of health outcomes, may lend insight into the role that community ties could play in diabetes risk and prevention. The relationship between sense of community belonging and diabetes incidence could also differ across key population subgroups for whom social connections and community participation may vary in important ways.6 19 20 The objective of this study is to assess the effect of community belonging on subsequent 5-year risk of diabetes in Ontario, Canada.

Methods

Study setting and population

The Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS) is a repeated cross-sectional survey that collects nationally representative information on the health and health behaviours of Canadians aged 12 years and older and has been widely used and described previously.21 Diabetes-free Ontario respondents aged 30–75 years from the CCHS (2000–2014) were linked to a validated diabetes registry and followed for 5 years or until 31 March 2020. These datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analysed at ICES, an independent, non-profit research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyse healthcare and demographic data. We selected adults aged 30–75 years at survey response because this age range encompasses a significant proportion of the population at risk for type 2 diabetes. Youth and younger adults were excluded because the determinants of early-onset diabetes are likely to differ from those of middle-aged and older adults. Adults over 75 years old were excluded because differences in the influence of social connections on chronic disease health, as well as the increased risk of comorbidities in older adults, are likely to impact our assessment of the effect of community belonging on diabetes incidence. We also excluded respondents who did not respond to the survey question about community belonging.

Community belonging

Sense of belonging to community was assessed through a single-item question, ‘How would you describe your sense of belonging to your local community?’ Respondents were asked to rate their level of community belonging on a 4-point Likert scale, which included ‘very strong,’ ‘somewhat strong,’ ‘somewhat weak,’ and ‘very weak.’ A body of Canadian research has shown that this single-item measure relates to other measures of social capital and exhibits associations with several health-related behaviours and health outcomes.6 8 10 11

Primary outcome

The Ontario Diabetes Dataset (ODD) is a validated registry derived from hospitalisation records and physicians’ service claims22 and was used to obtain information on diabetes status from the CCHS interview date to the end of follow-up (31 March 2020). The ODD algorithm allows for the identification of incident cases of physician-diagnosed diabetes with high sensitivity (86%) and specificity (97%), but does not allow us to differentiate between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Considering our cohort’s minimum age cut-off is 30 years, most new cases in this cohort are expected to be type 2 diabetes.

Baseline variables

We identified and included in the analysis the following potential confounders that were available in the survey data: sex, age, racialised group (yes/no), urban/rural status, marital status (married/common-law, divorced/separated, widowed, single), immigrant status (Canadian-born, immigrated over 5 years before survey response, recently immigrated within 5 years of survey response), education (less than secondary graduation, secondary graduation, some postsecondary education), income quintile, body mass index (BMI) category (underweight, normal, overweight, moderately obese, very obese, severely obese) corrected for self-report,23 smoking status (never, former light, former heavy, current light, current heavy), alcohol consumption (never, light, moderate, heavy) and physical activity (active, moderate, inactive). We also accounted for the presence of major chronic conditions at baseline, which included ischaemic heart disease (IHD), hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and/or cancer at baseline (yes/no for each condition). Like the ODD, these conditions were identified using previously validated case definitions for IHD,24 ICES-derived cohorts for hypertension,25 COPD26 and the Ontario Cancer Registry.

Statistical analysis

First, baseline characteristics and diabetes incidence rates were summarised by sense of community belonging using normalised survey weights. Second, we modelled the relationship between community belonging and 5-year diabetes incidence using Cox proportional hazards models for the entire sample. Models were adjusted for a range of sociodemographic and behavioural confounders using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to account for imbalances in the distribution of baseline covariates among community belonging groups and to reduce risk of bias.27 As IPT weights are estimated using logistic regression models for binary treatment variables, we conducted two distinct comparisons in which the most extreme groups (very weak and very strong) were used as the designated treatment categories for each separate set of IPTW weights, that is: (1) very weak was compared with somewhat weak, somewhat strong or very strong sense of community belonging and (2) very strong was compared with somewhat strong, somewhat weak or very weak sense of community belonging. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed based on visual inspection of Kaplan-Meier curves.

Weights were stabilised to mitigate the potential impacts of extreme weights and better control variance of the effect estimates.28 As an additional measure, we truncated weights at the first and 99th percentiles to further improve accuracy and precision of the estimates. To evaluate whether the positivity assumption was met, we monitored the mean, SD and maximum values of the stabilised weights. These diagnostics also served as indicators of the bias-variance trade-off. The following covariates were added into the logistic regression models used to produce the IPT weights: age, sex, educational attainment, income quintile, marital status, immigrant status, race, urban/rural area of residence, BMI category, alcohol consumption, smoking status, hypertension, IHD, COPD, cancer and survey cycle. Age was included as a continuous variable.

Ontario is the largest province in Canada and one of the most diverse. The relationship between community belonging and diabetes risk could vary across different characteristics, such as age,6 sex,19 20 immigrant status,29 race and ethnicity,5 income30 and whether one lives in an urban or rural context.7 To explore whether the relationship between sense of community belonging and 5-year diabetes incidence varies across these key population subgroups of interest, we employed the same modelling approach to pair-wise subgroups by: sex (female, male), age (30–54 years, 55–75 years), income (high: Q4–5, low: Q1–3), immigrant status (yes, no), racialisation (yes, no) and residential area (urban, rural), as summarised in figure 1. We also considered the possibility that the relationship between health and community belonging—an aspect of well-being at the level of communities—might depend on individuals’ personal well-being. The CCHS contains a survey question on life satisfaction, a key individual-level measure of well-being that has been shown to correlate with community belonging.31 We therefore included an additional pair-wise subgroup analysis of those reporting high versus low life satisfaction (high: satisfied or very satisfied, low: neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, dissatisfied or very dissatisfied).

IPT weights were generated separately for each subgroup in the same manner as previously described for the overall sample, using: (1) very weak belonging as the treatment group and (2) very strong belonging as the treatment group. All analyses were performed using SAS software (V.9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Imputation and sensitivity analysis

We employed cell-based random hot-deck imputation, a common method for addressing missing values in large-scale surveys.32 ‘Recipient’ units with missing data were replaced with observed responses from ‘donor’ units with similarities in sex, age, cycle and urban/rural status. Additionally, we incorporated survey weights and bootstraps in the imputation process to account for survey design characteristics. We used mode imputation to address remaining missing values for a small proportion of recipient units for which no donors were found (online supplemental table S1). We stabilised and truncated IPT weights for the final models to reduce variability among the weights, to reduce the influence of individuals with extreme weights and, ultimately, to achieve more reliable estimates with a more balanced pseudo-population. As a sensitivity analysis, we ran models with and without stabilisation and truncation for the overall sample using very weak belonging as the treatment group.

Patient and public involvement statement

There was no patient or public involvement in this study.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The final sample size for this study was 132 295 individuals (figure 1), achieved after combining CCHS cycles 2000–2014, restricting to participants ages 30–75 years at interview and excluding records with inconsistent death and survey response dates as well as those missing on community belonging. Much of the sample reported having a very strong (18.1%) or somewhat strong (48.1%) sense of belonging to community, while fewer reported having somewhat weak (24.3%) or very weak (9.5%) sense of community belonging (table 1). Individuals with a very strong sense of community belonging tended to have the following characteristics: they were older, more likely to be married or in a common-law relationship, identified as white, were Canadian born, lived in rural areas, never smoked, were physically active and reported having chronic conditions at survey response. Conversely, those with a very weak sense of belonging were more likely to have never been married or to be separated or divorced. Those with very weak community belonging were also more likely to identify as a racialised group, be long-term or recent immigrants, fall within the two lowest income quintiles, have lower educational attainment, reside in urban areas, abstain from or heavily consume alcohol, currently smoke and lead physically active lifestyles.

IPTW survival analysis

Across an average weighted follow-up period of 4.84 years (total available follow-up time of 635 766 person-years, unweighted), 6974 individuals (5% of the sample, weighted) developed diabetes (table 2). Individuals who reported having very weak sense of belonging experienced the highest proportion and crude incidence rate for diabetes (6.2%, 13.06 cases per 1000 person-years), followed by those with very strong sense of community belonging (5.5%, 11.42 cases per 1000 person-years). Those with somewhat strong and somewhat weak sense of belonging exhibited similar proportions and crude incidence rates for diabetes incidence that were lower than those reporting very strong or very weak belonging (respectively, 4.7%, 9.70 cases per 1000 person-years; 4.6%, 9.41 cases per 1000 person-years).

Focusing first on models in which very weak sense of community belonging was designated as the treatment group, the HR for incident diabetes associated with very weak belonging was 1.26 (95% CI 1.09 to 1.46) before weighting, and was attenuated but still conclusive at 1.16 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.34) after weighting (table 3). Across most subgroups, very weak community belonging was associated with higher diabetes incidence (table 3). After IPT weighting, conclusive associations between very weak belonging and heightened diabetes incidence were seen among Canadian-born respondents (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.50) and white respondents (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.40). While inconclusive, greater effect sizes were seen among those living in urban areas compared with those living in rural areas and among those reporting low versus high life satisfaction.

Results for a very strong sense of community belonging as the treatment group are presented in online supplemental table S2. A very strong sense of belonging was associated with higher risk of incident diabetes 1.17 (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.30) but was rendered inconclusive after IPTW was applied (HR 1.08, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.22). While some subgroups also exhibited higher risk of diabetes incidence with very strong community belonging, no conclusive associations were seen after IPT weighting. Visual inspection of Kaplan-Meier curves indicated no meaningful departures from the proportional hazards assumption.

Sensitivity analyses

Models without stabilised and/or trimmed weights yielded estimates of similar direction and magnitude to the main model (online supplemental table S3). However, models that included trimmed weights (ie, individuals with extreme weights that were excluded in the main model) rendered slightly stronger estimates before IPT weighting and weaker inconclusive estimates after IPT weighting.

Discussion

This population-based cohort study of 132 295 adults 30–75 years living in Ontario, Canada, used linked survey and administrative health data to investigate the relationship between sense of belonging to community and diabetes incidence. We found differences in baseline sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics across levels of community belonging, and observed the highest crude diabetes incidence rates among the 9.5% of the sample that reported having very weak sense of belonging. After achieving balance between those with very weak belonging and those with somewhat weak, somewhat strong or very strong belonging across the sample, IPT-weighted survival analysis indicated that having a very weak sense of belonging was associated with an increased 5-year risk of developing diabetes. This elevated risk of diabetes for those with very weak belonging was observed across all subgroups and remained conclusive for Canadian-born and white participants after weighting. While we also observed elevated crude diabetes incidence rates and higher 5-year diabetes risk among those with very strong sense of community belonging, estimates were rendered inconclusive after IPTW. This study is among the first cohort studies to link community belonging with subsequent population health outcomes9 10 and the only study to link community belonging with individual-level chronic disease incidence rates to date.

Our results should be taken in the context of the growing number of Canadian studies that demonstrate a relationship between weak community belonging and health-related outcomes including poorer self-rated health,6 higher risk of hospitalisation9 14 and premature mortality for some age groups.10 However, direct comparisons of our key findings with prior research are challenging and require a broader consideration of the literature on closely related concepts such as social cohesion, loneliness and social support. In general, our study aligns with numerous studies pointing to a relationship between low social support, loneliness, social isolation and higher diabetes incidence1920 3340 and prevalence,394144 although estimates were often attenuated and were sometimes rendered inconclusive after adjusting for individual-level factors.19 37 38 40 The conclusive associations seen for our study, compared with less definitive results of some previous studies of broader measures of social support, may be attributed to the direct influence of one’s local neighbourhood. Unlike general social support, which can involve distant connections with delayed responses, our measure focuses specifically on one’s local community—which may impart more immediate and potentially intimate effects on an individual’s daily life. In general, our findings are consistent with previous research that suggests that weak ties to one’s community could lead to higher diabetes risk.2

However, we also observed elevated diabetes risk among those with very strong community belonging. Although effect estimates were rendered inconclusive after IPTW, the direction of the association was unexpected. We find the pattern difficult to dismiss, especially given recent Canadian studies that demonstrate similar counterintuitive associations between very strong community belonging and less favourable health-related outcomes.9 10 These studies suggest that community belonging could vary in meaning and importance across the life course and depend on the community to which one belongs. Another study found an inverse relationship between living close by to family and friends (understood to constitute a higher degree of social support) and higher diabetes prevalence,45 which the authors suggest could be due to the negative impacts of social networks that promote poor health behaviours. Our study may indicate that being deeply embedded in one’s community may sometimes carry detrimental effects on diabetes incidence. This could be due to community characteristics or norms that support harmful or risky health behaviours45 or fail to promote proactive healthcare use, the stress and loss of autonomy stemming from the weight of obligations to one’s community, or perhaps the pervasive oversight of a tight-knit community.46 However, our study overwhelmingly suggests that, overall, some degree of community belonging confers protective effects against diabetes.

Our analyses of subgroups suggest that the relationship between very weak community belonging and higher diabetes incidence could be concentrated among Canadian-born respondents and white respondents. Although inconclusive, the direction and magnitude of effect estimates for residents of urban areas also suggest that this relationship could be more relevant to urban settings, as opposed to rural settings. Our findings revealed no significant differences between sexes, contrary to the findings of a review2 and studies reporting sex-specific associations between social support or living alone, and diabetes incidence19 20 38 and prevalence.44

While the associations between very weak belonging and diabetes across subgroups were in line with our overarching hypothesis, we expected community belonging would be of particular importance to immigrant and racialised populations.47 A Canadian study found associations between community belonging and positive mental health among both immigrants and Canadian-born residents, but found overall lower levels of belonging among newly immigrated residents.29 While we observed higher proportions of those with very weak belonging among immigrants and racialised populations, we did not see a relationship between very weak community belonging and diabetes incidence within these two groups. Our results might indicate that being connected to one’s local community may not play as important a role in the prevention of diabetes for those who have had less time to build local contacts. Immigrants may also rely more heavily on familial ties, connections with their country of origin or cultural and non-local community networks. Canadian-born and white respondents may interpret and experience ‘community’ and ‘belonging’ in ways that differ from immigrant and racialised respondents. The absence of associations among immigrants and racialised populations could also be a result of structural barriers or heterogeneity in neighbourhood-level aspects beyond the scope of this study, such as neighbourhood immigrant concentration, which could play a more important role in fostering local social connections among immigrant residents that are health-promoting in the long term.48

Strengths and limitations

This study carries several strengths. Our use of a large population-based, survey-weighted sample linked with objective administrative health data allowed us to assess associations with precision across different population subgroups. Of all previous studies of social connection and diabetes incidence, our study boasts the largest sample size of men and women, second only to an American study of 1 39 000 women.35 We accounted for a large number of individual-level sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics and employed IPTW to account for these differences among those reporting weak and strong belonging. Moreover, by mimicking the balance normally attained in randomised controlled trials, IPTW allows us to draw causal inferences about the impact of community belonging on incident diabetes.

However, we acknowledge that this study carries limitations. First, observational studies are vulnerable to residual confounding, and there could still be imbalances among covariates, especially among subgroups. Local contextual factors, such as urban design and access to resources, may also influence sense of belonging and social connections. Future work could incorporate additional covariates and contextual data to explore these pathways more fully. Second, our study results could be vulnerable to potential selection bias. The likelihood and ability of survey respondents in poorer health to form connections in community may vary from those in good health, which could in turn distort the true relationship between community belonging and diabetes incidence. However, we excluded those with pre-existing diabetes and included indicator variables for major pre-existing chronic conditions at baseline in our inverse probability weights. Third, sense of belonging to community was measured at a single time point by self-report. We were not able to account for changes in participants’ subjective assessments over the study period; however, by restricting follow-up duration to 5 years, we were able to focus on a period after survey response where community belonging could be presumed to influence diabetes onset most, as well as minimise loss to follow-up. Finally, our approach relies on a single subjective measure of community belonging that, while parsimonious, does not capture multiple dimensions of belonging. Some studies have been able to study multiple measures of social support and their relationship to diabetes incidence.353744 Our data do not contain other measures of belonging. However, we and others have argued that this survey question represents a unique aspect of local social support that, distinct from previous studies, captures collective aspects related to one’s ties to their neighbourhood49 and has been validated across a growing array of health outcomes.468

Conclusion

Our study indicates that weak sense of community belonging is associated with increased diabetes risk in Canadians aged 30–75 years old overall and to varying degrees across demographic groups. Unadjusted associations were also found for very strong belonging, although they were smaller in magnitude than those for very weak belonging and became inconclusive after IPT weighting. Our findings suggest that there is untapped potential in community belonging as a factor for diabetes prevention. A recent review of empirical studies indicates that sense of community is both an outcome of interest and a predictor of the success of disease prevention and health promotion interventions,50 although the review’s authors note that much work needs to be done to define and contextualise measures of community belonging and examine its role in diverse contexts. Such work may be accomplished by leveraging ongoing public health partnerships and collaborations with local communities, as well as enhancing existing diabetes prevention and management programmes. This area of work could benefit from additional longitudinal research to establish causality, track temporal variations and importance across the life course and further delineate the mechanisms through which community belonging might impact chronic disease outcomes. Moreover, the development of novel measures to capture multiple aspects of social connections—such as their extent, duration and quality—will advance our understanding of their influence on long-term health outcomes. Future studies could also explore the needs of specific groups and communities to help tailor and promote community-centred healthcare approaches.
