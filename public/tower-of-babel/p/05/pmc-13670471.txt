Integrating plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria and machine learning for drought tolerance assessment in green bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.)
Authors: Akife Dalda-Sekerci, Hasan Ali İrik, Emel Unlu, Ahmet Say, Musab A. Isak, Hande Seda Özdal, Onur Okumuş, Özhan Şimşek, Tolga İzgü, Halit Yetişir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought stress is one of the most critical constraints limiting crop productivity worldwide, particularly under increasing water scarcity driven by climate change. In this study, the effects of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) on green bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) under varying irrigation regimes were evaluated, and their responses were further analyzed using machine learning (ML) approaches. A greenhouse experiment was conducted using four irrigation levels (I100, I80, I60, and I40) and three bacterial treatments, including Stenotrophomonas rhizophila EU.2B33 (R2), Bacillus marisflavi EU.18 (R1), and their combination (R3). Severe water deficit (I40) reduced shoot and root biomass by approximately 40% compared to full irrigation. However, PGPR applications significantly mitigated these effects, with S. rhizophila EU.2B33 (R2) producing the highest shoot fresh weight (68.03 g), representing an increase of approximately 20% compared to the control. Chlorophyll content declined markedly under drought stress, whereas PGPR treatments increased total chlorophyll levels by more than 80% under moderate stress conditions (I60). The most pronounced PGPR-mediated improvements were observed under moderate water deficit (I60–I80), while effects under severe stress (I40) were present but comparatively limited. In addition, PGPR applications reduced oxidative stress indicators, including hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA), and decreased membrane damage. To better capture nonlinear relationships among treatments and plant responses, multilayer perceptron neural network (MLPNN) and generalized regression neural network (GRNN) models were developed. GRNN demonstrated superior predictive performance for stress-related traits, achieving up to R2 = 0.87 for catalase activity, whereas MLPNN performed better for more stable traits such as carotenoids (R2 = 0.89).The integration of PGPR-based treatments and machine learning provides a robust framework for improving drought tolerance assessment and optimizing sustainable crop management under water-limited conditions.

Introduction

Climate change is increasingly exacerbating the prevalence of abiotic stress and its detrimental effects on plants across vast regions. According to FAO, [19] more than 95% of global agricultural lands are affected by abiotic stress conditions driven by global warming. The significant rise in greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere has led to increased surface temperatures and alterations in precipitation patterns [21]. Studies have shown that a 1 °C increase in surface temperature can elevate plant water requirements by approximately 4–4.5% [58, 73]. These temperature increases, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions, limit crop production due to reduced available irrigation water, thereby intensifying drought stress [55].

Drought stress is a major limiting factor for plant growth and development, significantly affecting a wide range of biochemical and physiological processes in plants [55, 64]. Water deficit disrupts cellular homeostasis, leading to impaired water balance within plant tissues and reduced metabolic activity [16]. One of the most critical consequences of drought stress is the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induces oxidative damage to cellular components [24, 75]. This oxidative stress can result in lipid peroxidation, protein degradation, and membrane instability, ultimately reducing plant growth and productivity [24, 57]. To mitigate the adverse impacts of drought, plants exhibit a range of adaptive responses. Under water stress, plant growth may be restricted, and morphological alterations may occur. One key survival mechanism involves regulating osmotic balance; plants often accumulate osmolytes, such as proline, to cope with drought stress [42]. Another essential defense mechanism is the antioxidant defense system. When exposed to stress, plants regulate both enzymatic antioxidants, such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and non-enzymatic antioxidants including tocopherol, ascorbate, and glutathione [40]. Water deficiency can negatively impact morphological, biochemical, and physiological parameters in plants by disrupting various critical cellular processes [18]. Among these effects, damage to the photosynthetic apparatus [12, 39], as well as oxidative injuries to proteins, membrane lipids, and other cellular components, have been reported [18, 52, 77]. These adverse effects are often closely associated with reductions in soil microbial activity [54] and declines in the availability of macro- and micronutrients for plant uptake [27, 71]. The application of beneficial microorganisms as biostimulants is increasingly recognized as an environmentally friendly approach for sustainable agriculture [13, 51]. According to [37], the use of PGPR can alleviate the adverse effects of limited water availability on plants. This mitigation involves numerous physiological and biochemical processes, including alterations in phytohormone levels, activation of the antioxidant defense system, and the accumulation of various organic compounds such as sugars, amino acids, and polyamines [51]. Rhizobacteria not only enhance plant nutrient and water uptake, and thus promote plant growth, by altering the hormonal status of roots, but also stimulate the secretion of various biochemical compounds in the root zone, thereby facilitating microbial colonization and the establishment of symbiotic relationships [32, 48]. Under water-limited conditions, several studies have demonstrated that PGPRs stimulate the biosynthesis of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and abscisic acid (ABA), leading to significant root elongation [14, 59].

Rhizobacteria are reported to play a crucial role in sustainable agricultural production by mitigating the adverse effects of abiotic stress [46, 62, 76]. In particular, species of the genus Bacillus have been shown to enhance the production of antioxidant compounds under salt stress conditions [76]. Under limited water conditions, rhizobacteria-induced changes in plants can be assessed at the morphological and antioxidant activity levels; however, further elaboration of these effects through advanced statistical analysis methods is of great significance. Machine learning (ML) techniques have become indispensable tools in modern plant science, offering powerful means to model complex biological responses, optimize experimental procedures, and develop adaptation strategies to abiotic stress. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of algorithms such as multilayer perceptron (MLP), random forest (RF), k-nearest neighbors (kNN), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) in various applications within plant biotechnology [26, 31]. ML methods have been successfully applied in a wide range of areas, from improving in vitro propagation systems [10, 30, 50, 61, 67] to modeling genotype responses under drought and heavy metal stress [8, 69]. Moreover, ML models have shown high predictive accuracy in estimating germination dynamics, sterilization efficiency, and abiotic stress tolerance under fluctuating saline and thermal conditions [49, 53, 56]. Comparative studies have indicated that no single ML model consistently outperforms others across all datasets, highlighting the need for multi-model evaluations that take into account factors such as data structure, noise levels, and feature complexity [50]. Furthermore, ML approaches excel at detecting nonlinear and complex interactions that are often overlooked by conventional statistical methods [26].

Despite numerous studies on the role of PGPR in mitigating drought-induced damage, most have primarily focused on descriptive physiological assessments, often overlooking the potential of integrative data-driven approaches. Unlike earlier works emphasizing only morphological or biochemical traits, the present study combines conventional physiological and biochemical evaluations with advanced ML models-namely, Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network (MLPNN) and General Regression Neural Network (GRNN) to quantitatively predict and differentiate the drought response of green bean plants under varying irrigation levels. This dual experimental–computational strategy establishes a novel framework for understanding the nonlinear interactions between PGPR-mediated physiological regulation and drought stress. We hypothesized that inoculation with specific PGPR strains, Bacillus marisflavi EU.18 and Stenotrophomonas rhizophila EU.2B33, would enhance drought tolerance in Phaseolus vulgaris L. by improving growth performance, photosynthetic pigments, and antioxidant defense systems under limited water availability. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning aimed to overcome the constraints of conventional statistical analyses and capture complex, nonlinear stress–response relationships that better reflect real-world plant-soil-microbe interactions. Therefore, the objective of this study was to (i) evaluate the effects of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) on the morphological, physiological, and biochemical responses of green bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) under varying irrigation levels, and (ii) apply machine learning models (MLPNN and GRNN) to model plant responses and identify key physiological and biochemical indicators associated with drought tolerance.

Material and methods

Plant material

The plant material used in this study was the bush-type green bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) cultivar ‘ALBENİ’, a commercially preferred variety widely used in green bean cultivation in Türkiye. The cultivar ‘ALBENİ’ was registered on May 26, 2015 by Safgen Seed Company Agricultural Products Industry and Trade Ltd. Co. Certified seeds were purchased from Safgen Seed Company (Konya, Türkiye) and used for all experiments.

Preparation of rhizobacteria

Two rhizobacterial strains, Bacillus marisflavi EU.18 (R1) and Stenotrophomonas rhizophila EU.2B33 (R2), previously identified at the Faculty of Agriculture, Erciyes University, through 16S rRNA gene sequencing and confirmed to possess the ACC deaminase enzyme via PCR analysis, were used in this study. In addition to the individual applications, a combined rhizobacterial formulation (R3) was prepared by mixing equal volumes of both strains. To activate the rhizobacteria, stock cultures of S. rhizophila EU.2B33 and B. marisflavi EU.18 stored at –80 °C were inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) liquid medium and incubated for 24 h. After observing bacterial growth, the cultures were purified and further incubated overnight in LB broth at 35–37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm to obtain active cultures. Rhizobacterial suspensions were prepared at a final concentration of 3 × 10⁸ CFU/mL.

Experimental design and research topics

The study was conducted between May and September 2024 in an unheated polyethylene greenhouse at Erciyes University. The experiment was conducted using a commercially available, standardized seedling substrate to ensure uniform growth conditions across treatments. Although a detailed physicochemical analysis of the substrate was not performed prior to planting, all experimental units received the same substrate, thereby minimizing variability related to soil properties. Green bean seeds were sown directly into plastic pots with a bottom diameter of 19 cm, top diameter of 29 cm, and height of 24 cm, each filled with 5 kg of seedling substrate. Once the seedlings reached the 2–3 true leaf stage, the experiment was established using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with four irrigation levels: full irrigation (I100) and three deficit irrigation treatments (I80, I60, and I40). In addition, three PGPR treatments were applied, including R1, R2, and R3, at a concentration of 3 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻1. Each treatment was arranged in a completely randomized design with three independent replicates, and each replicate consisted of two pots. The irrigation amounts were determined based on the full irrigation control (I100), which did not receive PGPR application. In this control treatment, irrigation was applied when soil moisture decreased to approximately 45–50% of field capacity and restored to field capacity. Soil moisture was monitored using the gravimetric method. The deficit irrigation treatments (I80, I60, and I40) received 80%, 60%, and 40% of the irrigation volume applied to the control, respectively. PGPR applications were initiated following the establishment of irrigation treatments and the onset of visible drought stress symptoms. Rhizobacterial suspensions were applied as a soil drench at a rate of 5 mL per pot (3 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻1), and treatments were repeated three times at weekly intervals. All cultural practices, such as irrigation management, manual removal of weeds, and routine inspection for pest and disease incidence, were performed promptly and as required throughout the experimental period.

Determination of plant parameters

Observations and measurements were conducted when drought stress symptoms became visually evident in green bean plants. The onset of visible stress was determined based on the appearance of characteristic drought-related symptoms, including leaf wilting, slight leaf rolling, reduced leaf turgor, and decreased shoot vigor compared to the fully irrigated control plants. Morphological measurements included plant height, stem diameter, shoot fresh weight, shoot dry weight, root length, root fresh weight, root dry weight, and fruit weight. Plant height was measured from the soil surface to the apical meristem using a ruler, while stem diameter was measured at the stem base using a digital caliper. Root length was determined after carefully washing the roots free of substrate using a ruler. For biomass measurements, three whole plants from each replicate were carefully uprooted, and the shoots and roots were separated and weighed immediately using a precision digital balance to determine fresh weight. Subsequently, plant samples were dried in a forced-air oven at 72 °C for 48 h until constant weight was achieved, and dry weights were recorded and expressed as g plant⁻1.

Determination of chlorophyll and carotenoid content

A modified version of the method developed by Iskeep and Bloom [28] was used to determine the chlorophyll content in the plant samples. For this purpose, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen, and 5 mL of 80% acetone solution pre-cooled to + 4 °C was added. The entire mixture was transferred into capped tubes and kept in the dark at + 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 10 °C. Absorbance was measured at 663 nm and 647 nm for chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b determination, respectively, and at 470 nm for carotenoid content using a spectrophotometer. Cold acetone served as the blank.

Electrolyte leakage index (ELI)

Electrolyte leakage resulting from stress induced by treatments was determined according to the method described by [5]. For this analysis, leaf discs (1 cm in diameter) were collected from the plants in each replicate and thoroughly rinsed with distilled water. The discs were then placed in 20 mL of distilled water and incubated at room temperature for 24 h. After the incubation period, the electrical conductivity (EC) of the solution was measured and recorded as EC₁ (O. D1). Subsequently, the samples were autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min to completely disrupt the cell membranes, and after cooling to room temperature, the final EC was measured and recorded as EC₂ (O. D2). Determination of proline content

The method described by [34] was modified and used for the determination of proline content. A total of 0.05 g of dried leaf samples were ground in a mortar with 5 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid solution. The extract was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. From the supernatant, 2 mL aliquots were taken into tubes in duplicate. Then, 2 mL of glacial acetic acid and 2 mL of acid-ninhydrin reagent (acetic acid:phosphoric acid:ninhydrin) were added and mixed thoroughly. The reaction mixture was incubated in boiling water for 1 h and then immediately cooled. Afterward, 4 mL of toluene was added and mixed well. The toluene phase was transferred to a cuvette, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm. Proline concentrations were calculated using a standard calibration curve generated from known concentrations of proline solutions.

Determination of membrane lipid peroxidation (MDA) content

Lipid peroxidation was determined based on the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method, as modified from [63]. Leaf samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. Then, 4 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of 0.5% TBA solution. The reaction mixture was incubated in boiling water (95 °C) for 30 min and then rapidly cooled in an ice bath. The mixture was centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻1 cm⁻1. To eliminate non-specific turbidity, the absorbance at 600 nm was subtracted from that at 532 nm.

Determination of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content in the harvested plants was determined following the method of [70]. Leaf tissues (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5 mL of cold 0.1% TCA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. From the resulting supernatant, 0.5 mL was transferred to a new tube and mixed with 0.5 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide (KI). The absorbance was measured at 390 nm. The amount of H₂O₂ was calculated using a standard curve and expressed as μmol H₂O₂ per gram of fresh tissue (μmol g⁻1 FW).

Enzyme extraction

For enzyme activity assays, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. To each sample, 5 mL of cold extraction buffer (0.1 M KH₂PO₄, pH 7.0, containing 1% PVP and 1 mM EDTA) was added. The homogenate was transferred into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected into clean Eppendorf tubes and used in appropriate volumes for antioxidant enzyme activity assays [3].

Determination of catalase (CAT) enzyme activity

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by monitoring the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm, which corresponds to the decomposition of H₂O₂ into H₂O and O₂ [25]. A standard curve for H₂O₂ was first prepared using a five mM H₂O₂ stock solution. Varying volumes (0.15–1.5 mL) of H₂O₂ were added to 3 mL quartz cuvettes, and the final volume was adjusted with distilled water and 1.470 mL of 103 mM KH₂PO₄ buffer along with 30 µL of water. Absorbance was recorded at 240 nm every 10 s for 3 min against a blank. Using these values, a standard calibration curve was generated.

For the assay, 1.475 mL of 103 mM KH₂PO₄ buffer and 1.5 mL of 40 mM H₂O₂ solution were added to 3 mL quartz cuvettes, followed by 25 µL of enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance was monitored at 240 nm at 1-min intervals for 3 min against a blank. The rate of absorbance decline per minute in the linear region was calculated. The amount of H₂O₂ consumed was determined using the standard curve. One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that decomposes one µmol of H₂O₂ per minute at 25 °C, and results were expressed as enzyme units per gram fresh leaf tissue (EU/g leaf).

Determination of peroxidase (POD) enzyme activity

Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined by measuring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm due to the formation of a colored compound resulting from the reaction of guaiacol and H₂O₂ [3]. For the assay, 3 mL of substrate solution containing 0.1 M NaH₂PO₄ buffer (pH 5.5), five mM guaiacol, and five mM H₂O₂ was added to quartz cuvettes, followed by 10 µL of enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm was recorded every minute for 5 min. The rate of absorbance increase during the linear phase was calculated. One unit of POD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that increases absorbance by 0.01 per minute at 25 °C, and results were expressed as EU/g leaf.

Determination of superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme activity

SOD activity was determined based on the inhibition of the photochemical reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), as described by [1] and [74]. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 50 mM KH₂PO₄ buffer (pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, and 0.1 mM EDTA. For the assay, 2.84 mL of this reaction mixture was placed in a cuvette, and 100 µL of enzyme extract was added. The reaction was initiated by adding 60 µL of 2 µM riboflavin solution, and the mixture was exposed to a white light source for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm against a blank. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of NBT reduction under assay conditions. Results were expressed as EU/g fresh leaf weight. NBT was dissolved in methanol, and riboflavin was dissolved in 0.1 N NaOH to prepare stock solutions at concentrations of 8 mg/mL.

Machine learning analyses

Artificial neural network (ANN) models

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) represent advanced computational models inspired by the fundamental structure and function of biological neurons. These networks comprise interconnected processing units called "neurons," organized in multiple layers, including an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Each connection among these layers carries a weight that is modified during the training process [44]. Through the iterative updating of these weights via data-driven learning algorithms, such as backpropagation, ANNs can autonomously learn complex nonlinear relationships from provided examples without explicit programming. This adaptive learning capability, coupled with their layered architecture, equips ANNs to effectively model intricate patterns and generalize findings to novel data instances. As a result, ANNs have been extensively adopted across many fields, including computer vision, natural language processing, recommender systems, and financial decision-making, thereby highlighting their versatile contributions to modern artificial intelligence [66].

Generalized Regression Neural Network (GRNN)

The Generalized Regression Neural Network (GRNN) represents a memory-based neural architecture designed explicitly for regression and function approximation tasks. This model captures the complex, nonlinear relationships inherent in experimental data without requiring iterative weight training. Our study employed the GRNN to forecast plants' physiological and biochemical traits (e.g., chlorophyll content, antioxidant enzyme activities, growth metrics, and fruit weight) utilizing categorical input variables that signify different irrigation regimes and bacterial treatments. The GRNN architecture comprises four distinct layers: input, pattern, summation, and output layers (Fig. 2). The input layer receives the encoded treatment information, which is subsequently conveyed to the pattern layer, where each neuron corresponds to a training instance derived from the dataset. These instances maintain the comprehensive input–output mappings from the training data. The pattern layer computes the Euclidean distance between the newly presented input vector and all previously stored training samples. Subsequently, these distances are transformed into similar values utilizing a Gaussian kernel function. The summation layer consists of two units: the denominator summation unit, which consolidates the similarity values, and the numerator summation unit, which determines the weighted sum of output values based on these similarities. Ultimately, the output layer performs a normalized weighted average, yielding the predicted values for each plant trait [53].Fig. 2Architecture of the Generalized Regression Neural Network (GRNN)

ANN modeling procedure

This study developed two artificial neural network (ANN) models, MLPNN and GRNN, to forecast various physiological and biochemical traits in plants subjected to different irrigation strategies and bacterial inoculation treatments. All modeling tasks were performed in Python (version 3.11), utilizing essential libraries such as Pandas and NumPy for data preprocessing and scikit-learn for model development and assessment. The input variables comprised categorical treatment factors (irrigation and bacterial types), which were encoded using LabelEncoder. The output variables included nineteen traits: chlorophyll-a, chlorophyll-b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids, ion leakage, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), malondialdehyde (MDA), proline, superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), shoot fresh and dry weights, plant height, stem diameter, root length, root fresh and dry weights, and fruit weight. Before modeling, all features were normalized to a uniform scale to improve convergence and learning efficiency. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets, and a tenfold cross-validation (k = 10) approach was employed to ensure the robustness of the model and prevent overfitting. The MLPNN model was implemented utilizing the MLPRegressor class, which features an architecture comprising an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Hyperparameters were tuned empirically based on their performance metrics, including the number of hidden neurons, the activation function (ReLU), the learning rate, and the solver (Adam). In contrast, the General Regression Neural Network (GRNN) model was constructed by combining the Radial Basis Function Sampler (RBFSampler) for radial basis function approximation with Ridge regression as the linear estimator, thereby providing a non-iterative, fast-learning structure well-suited for nonlinear regression tasks. Results

Effects of limited irrigation and rhizobacteria applications on growth parameters in green bean

Under limited irrigation conditions, the application of three different rhizobacteria strains to green bean plants had statistically significant effects on growth parameters (Table 1). Water deficit caused substantial reductions across all plant development parameters. As the degree of irrigation restriction increased, both above-ground and below-ground organs of the green bean plants exhibited marked decreases. Compared to the control group, the most severe irrigation restriction treatment (I40) resulted in approximately a 40% reduction in both fresh and dry shoot weight. Similarly, fresh and dry root weights decreased by 40% and 48%, respectively. Rhizobacteria applications had a statistically significant impact on plant growth parameters. Model performance antioxidant enzyme activities

Table 5 elucidates the modeling outcomes for three essential antioxidant enzymes-superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), which assume fundamental roles in plant defense mechanisms under stress conditions. The results clearly indicate variances in the models' capacity to encapsulate the dynamics of enzymatic activity, likely representing the intrinsic biological variability and data distribution associated with each trait. Among the analyzed enzymes, catalase (CAT) demonstrated the highest predictability in both models, with GRNN significantly surpassing MLPNN. GRNN recorded an R2 value of 0.87, signifying that the model accounted for almost 87% of the variability in CAT activity, along with notably lower MAE (87.0) and RMSE (97.4) values. This indicates that CAT activity reacted to the experimental treatments in a relatively consistent and interpretable manner, effectively captured by GRNN's memory-based architecture. In contrast, MLPNN exhibited more moderate performance (R2 = 0.56), underscoring its challenges in managing complex yet structured response patterns. The findings regarding POD activity were more complex. Both models demonstrated moderate predictive capabilities, but GRNN had a clear edge, with an R2 of 0.64 compared to 0.44 for MLPNN. Notably, GRNN reduced the RMSE and MAE values by half compared to MLPNN, indicating a heightened sensitivity to treatment-induced changes in peroxidase response. POD activity tends to vary in response to oxidative stress in nonlinear, time-dependent ways, which may clarify GRNN's better performance due to its strengths in interpolative pattern recognition. Modeling SOD activity turned out to be the most challenging aspect. The results from both models showed relatively low R2 values (0.38 for GRNN and 0.31 for MLPNN), indicating that a significant amount of variance remained unexplained. Although GRNN did significantly lower prediction errors (MAE = 16.16 compared to 22.34; RMSE = 18.8 versus 27.4), the overall predictability remained constrained. This limitation may stem from the considerable biological variability in SOD activity in response to varying environmental factors, as well as possible experimental noise. Together, these findings show that GRNN provides more accurate and stable predictions for antioxidant enzyme responses, particularly for traits that exhibit nonlinear, stress-induced fluctuations. Discussion

The results obtained from this study demonstrate that inoculation with PGPR effectively mitigates the adverse effects of drought stress in Phaseolus vulgaris L. plants. By integrating physiological, biochemical, and computational analyses, our findings provide strong evidence for the multifaceted role of PGPR in enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stress. Furthermore, the application of ML tools significantly strengthened our ability to interpret complex biological responses and identify key markers associated with drought resilience. A significant increase in chlorophyll content and relative water content (RWC) was observed in PGPR-inoculated plants, indicating enhanced photosynthetic efficiency and improved water retention. These results align with previous reports, which demonstrate that PGPR can enhance root architecture, stimulate exopolysaccharide production, and improve water and nutrient uptake [43, 45, 71].

Biochemically, PGPR application significantly enhanced the accumulation of proline and the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD).(REF)? These responses play vital roles in maintaining redox homeostasis by detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS) under drought-induced oxidative stress [20]. The observed reduction in malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) further supports the role of PGPR in limiting lipid peroxidation and oxidative cell damage. Our findings are in agreement with previous studies, which demonstrate that PGPR, such as Bacillus subtilis and Pseudomonas fluorescens, can activate antioxidant defense mechanisms by upregulating related genes [15, 22]. Similar results were reported by, [11] who demonstrated that inoculation with drought-tolerant Bacillus spp. significantly improved plant performance under both moderate and severe drought stress through enhanced root development and reduced oxidative damage. In their study, PGPR application decreased ROS accumulation, MDA levels, and membrane injury, which closely aligns with our observations showing reduced H₂O₂ content, lower lipid peroxidation, and decreased ion leakage in PGPR-treated Phaseolus vulgaris plants under limited irrigation. [60] highlighted that drought-tolerant Bacillus spp. isolated from the rhizosphere of cactus plants possess strong plant growth-promoting traits and contribute to improved plant performance under water-limited conditions. Their study demonstrated that Bacillus isolates enhanced plant growth under severe water stress by supporting nutrient availability and stress tolerance, which is consistent with the improved growth parameters and root development observed in PGPR-treated Phaseolus vulgaris plants in the present study. The ability of drought-adapted Bacillus strains to function as effective bio-inoculants under low soil moisture further supports our results, indicating that PGPR originating from stress-prone environments play a crucial role in improving crop resilience and productivity under limited irrigation. Similarly, [2] reported that bioinoculation with drought-tolerant rhizobacteria significantly improved plant biomass across different levels of water limitation. Their observation that PGPR maintained both shoot and root growth under progressively reduced water availability supports our results, where PGPR-treated Phaseolus vulgaris plants sustained higher biomass and productivity under limited irrigation. The consistent growth response across multiple water regimes suggests that PGPR enhance plant capacity to cope with water deficit through improved resource use and stress adjustment, reinforcing the applicability of our findings beyond legume systems. Also, [4] demonstrated that the combined application of biofilm-forming PGPR strains resulted in superior drought tolerance compared to single-strain inoculation, highlighting the importance of synergistic microbial interactions. Their findings showed that consortium-based PGPR treatments enhanced plant survival and growth under drought by improving root colonization and regulating key stress-related traits. This observation aligns with our results, where combined PGPR application consistently promoted plant growth and stress tolerance under limited irrigation. The reported advantages of cooperative PGPR action support our conclusion that multi-strain formulations can provide more stable and sustained plant responses to drought stress than individual inoculants. [7] reported pronounced increases in CAT, POD, and SOD activities under drought stress, with PGPR-treated plants maintaining significantly higher antioxidant enzyme levels than non-inoculated controls across moderate and severe water deficits. In their study, drought stress alone markedly elevated CAT, POD, and SOD activities, whereas PGPR application stabilized these responses while sustaining higher growth and physiological performance. This pattern closely mirrors our results, where CAT and POD activities sharply increased under severe irrigation restriction, while PGPR-treated plants exhibited moderated enzyme activities accompanied by reduced oxidative damage indicators. The comparable directional responses of CAT, POD, and SOD across both studies indicate that PGPR-mediated drought tolerance operates through a controlled antioxidant response rather than excessive stress-induced enzyme overaccumulation. Proline accumulation in particular was markedly higher in PGPR-treated plants under water-deficit conditions. This osmolyte is known to function as an osmoprotectant, contributing to osmotic adjustment, membrane stability, and free radical scavenging [65]. Our results support the hypothesis that PGPR enhance plant stress responses not only through hormonal or growth-related pathways, but also by modulating cellular osmotic balance [9, 47]. In addition to enzymatic defenses, the non-enzymatic antioxidant system was also strengthened by PGPR inoculation. Treated plants exhibited increased levels of total phenolics and flavonoids, along with enhanced DPPH radical scavenging activity. These findings suggest that PGPR stimulate the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites involved in ROS detoxification, corroborating earlier reports in maize and tomato under drought conditions [17, 33, 68]. Beyond model performance, the machine learning analyses provided important biological insights into plant responses under combined drought and PGPR treatments. Among the evaluated traits, catalase (CAT) activity and carotenoid content emerged as the most consistently predictable parameters, indicating that these traits represent stable physiological markers of stress response. The high predictive accuracy observed for CAT (R2 = 0.87 in GRNN) suggests that antioxidant enzyme regulation plays a central role in mediating drought tolerance under PGPR application. Similarly, carotenoids, which are directly associated with photoprotection and oxidative stress mitigation, were effectively modeled, highlighting their relevance as indicators of plant resilience. In contrast, traits such as proline and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) exhibited more variable patterns, with GRNN outperforming MLPNN, suggesting the presence of nonlinear and treatment-dependent responses. These findings indicate that osmotic adjustment and oxidative stress signaling are highly dynamic processes influenced by both irrigation level and rhizobacterial inoculation. Importantly, the integration of ML models enabled the identification of key traits that respond consistently to combined stress and biostimulant treatments. Therefore, parameters such as CAT activity and carotenoid content may serve as reliable indicators for evaluating treatment effectiveness and drought tolerance in green bean. Overall, these results demonstrate that machine learning approaches not only provide accurate predictions but also contribute to a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying plant–stress interactions, thereby supporting their use as complementary tools in stress physiology research. Our findings echo recent advances in the use of ML in plant stress research, where complex datasets can be mined to reveal biologically meaningful patterns and improve predictive modeling [6, 41]. In line with the modeling outcomes observed in the present study, [72] demonstrated that ensemble-based and nonlinear machine learning models exhibit superior predictive performance for complex plant growth traits compared to single-architecture approaches. In their in vitro study on Aronia melanocarpa, XGBoost and Random Forest models achieved very high predictive accuracy (R2 > 0.95) for key morphogenic traits such as shoot number and root length, underscoring the capacity of tree-based and ensemble algorithms to capture nonlinear interactions among experimental factors. Similarly, our results showed that advanced neural network-based models were more effective in predicting physiological, biochemical, and growth-related responses under stress conditions than simpler modeling structures. Together, these findings suggest that model performance is strongly trait-dependent and that algorithms capable of handling nonlinear, multivariate relationships are particularly well suited for biological systems characterized by complex plant–environment interactions. Similarly, [38] demonstrated that hybrid artificial intelligence models outperform conventional algorithms when predicting drought- and stress-tolerance indices in maize. Their results showed that genetic algorithm–based models, particularly SVM-GA, provided higher predictive accuracy than standalone MLP or SVM approaches, highlighting the advantage of coupling optimization techniques with learning algorithms. This finding complements our results by reinforcing that model performance is not solely dependent on algorithm type, but also on how effectively model parameters and input structures are optimized. Together, these studies suggest that advanced or hybrid modeling strategies are especially effective for capturing complex, multivariate stress responses, supporting the robustness of AI-driven approaches for drought-related trait prediction across different plant systems. [23] also demonstrated that ensemble-based machine learning approaches provide higher predictive accuracy than single models when classifying drought tolerance, particularly when root and physiological traits are jointly considered. Their stacking ensemble achieved a classification accuracy of 81.8%, outperforming Random Forest and MLP, and consistently identified H₂O₂ and relative water content as key predictors of drought tolerance. This outcome aligns with our results, where oxidative stress indicators and water-related traits emerged as dominant contributors to model performance under drought conditions. Consistent with our modeling outcomes, recent in vitro studies have shown that machine learning model performance is strongly influenced by stress type, genotype, and target trait complexity rather than a single universally superior algorithm. For instance, [69] reported that MLP achieved high predictive accuracy for both shoot and root traits under cadmium stress, while RF and XGBoost also contributed effectively depending on the response variable. Similarly, [61] demonstrated that RF outperformed other models in predicting drought-induced morphological changes in strawberry cultivars, although MLP and GP showed trait- and genotype-specific advantages. Together with our results, these studies indicate that ML model effectiveness is context-dependent, reinforcing the need for multi-model evaluation when predicting plant responses to different abiotic stresses under controlled conditions. Overall, PGPR inoculation conferred drought tolerance through a coordinated enhancement of osmoprotective, antioxidant, and water retention mechanisms. The integration of ML tools further highlighted the robustness of these responses, offering a promising dual strategy-combining microbial inoculants and data-driven analysis-for improving crop resilience in the face of climate-induced abiotic stresses.

Conclusion

This study represents one of the first comprehensive efforts to integrate General Regression Neural Network and Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network modeling approaches into the evaluation of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria-mediated drought tolerance in Phaseolus vulgaris L. The results clearly demonstrate that PGPR inoculation effectively alleviates drought-induced damage by enhancing key physiological and biochemical parameters, including chlorophyll content, relative water content (RWC), and antioxidant enzyme activity. These improvements collectively contributed to better water-use efficiency, enhanced photosynthetic capacity, and overall plant vigor under limited irrigation conditions. The outcomes of this research reaffirm the growing importance of biostimulant-based strategies as sustainable alternatives to synthetic agrochemicals. In particular, the inoculation of Bacillus marisflavi EU.18 and Stenotrophomonas rhizophila EU.2B33 enhanced root development, exopolysaccharide production, and nutrient absorption-mechanisms that jointly underpin improved stress resilience and plant productivity. These multifaceted benefits underscore the potential of PGPR to serve as bio-enhancers for climate-resilient horticultural systems, especially in water-scarce regions. A key contribution of this work lies in its integration of machine learning algorithms with physiological and biochemical datasets to uncover nonlinear and complex plant–microbe–environment interactions that are often overlooked by conventional statistical analyses. The predictive capability of GRNN and MLPNN models not only improved the interpretation of plant responses but also identified discriminative markers associated with drought tolerance. This computational insight provides a new perspective for precision phenotyping, targeted breeding, and data-driven stress management in legume crops. Ultimately, this study reinforces the view that combining biological inoculation strategies with advanced analytical tools represents a robust and sustainable pathway for modern agriculture. Future studies should validate these findings under multi-environment field conditions, optimize PGPR formulations, and integrate molecular profiling to refine model predictions. By bridging experimental evidence with computational intelligence, this research contributes to the development of predictive, eco-friendly, and resilient agricultural systems that can sustain productivity in the face of global climate variability.
