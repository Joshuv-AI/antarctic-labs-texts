Risk-aware tactical path planning in partially observable environments via trajectory-value factorized recurrent PPO
Authors: Seongmin Kim, Seohyeong Kim, Hyeongju Jeong, Hyun Kwon, Jun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOperating unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) in partially observable battlefields requires balancing movement efficiency and proxy risk exposure. Traditional shortest-path algorithms typically struggle here, as their use of purely geometric distances often yields tactically vulnerable routes. We confront this challenge by introducing a risk-aware path-planning framework driven by Trajectory-Value Factorized Recurrent Proximal Policy Optimization (TVF-RPPO). Our integrated navigation pipeline combines a SwiftFormer perception unit to map terrain-specific costs and risks with a GRU-based belief state that continuously tracks observation history. Unlike standard approaches that fuse competing goals into a single scalar reward, TVF-RPPO explicitly splits value estimation into separate time-efficiency and risk-avoidance channels. This structural shift is the key mechanism that enables the network to efficiently regulate speed and safety. During our 2D evaluations involving static, dynamic, and active threat scenarios, the technique reduced proxy risk exposure compared with baseline planners while maintaining competitive mission-completion performance in the tested simulation scenarios. A major practical benefit of this factorization is its adjustability. Introduction

With recent progress within autonomous driving technology, unmanned-vehicle-based path planning has become a core technology across domains such as logistics, agriculture, and disaster response [1]. However, path planning in battlefield environments brings structural and surrounding challenges distinct from those in typical civilian autonomous driving cases. On a battlefield, the objective of a vehicle is not simply to reach a destination via the shortest distance or minimum time; rather, the primary goal is tactical maneuvering to reduce risk exposure and improve mission viability by avoiding enemy surveillance and attack-prone regions under the adopted simulation risk model. This key difference extends the path-planning problem from simple geometric optimization to a risk-aware decision-making problem.

A battlefield environment entails three fundamental challenges: unstructured terrain, dynamic threats, and incomplete information. First, unlike metropolitan environments that focus on paved roads, battlefield terrain is unstructured, comprising forests, unpaved paths, mountainous areas, and debris. Each terrain type imposes distinct traversal costs and risk levels. Second, enemy positions and behaviors cannot be fully predicted in advance, and new threats may emerge abruptly during movement. Third, owing to sensor and communication restrictions, a vehicle cannot observe the global state of the environment and must make decisions based solely on limited information within its field of view. Consequently, it is appropriate to model the battlefield path planning problem as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) [2]. In this study, these problems are examined in an internal two-dimensional battlefield simulation. Therefore, the reported risk-exposure and mission-completion results should be interpreted as simulation-based evidence instead of direct estimates of real-world survivability.

Traditional path planning algorithms, such as Dijkstra’s, A*, and bug algorithms and rapidly exploring random trees (RRT), are effective for generating the shortest or collision-free paths in fully known environments [3–6]. However, they fail to incorporate risk perception and tactical judgment. These methods generally rely on fixed-cost maps or heuristics, making it difficult to explicitly account for battlefield-specific factors such as exposure to enemy lines of sight or the use of concealment and cover. Particularly in partially observable settings, plans derived from prior maps frequently deviate from reality, and their capability to cope with dynamic threats is limited. To provide a stronger empirical comparison, our experiments include both classical planners and RL-based baselines, including PPO, recurrent PPO, scalarized recurrent PPO, and a risk-aware RL variant. For dynamic replanning scenarios, replanning-specialized baselines such as D* Lite or LPA* are additionally considered [7, 8].

Reinforcement learning (RL)-based path planning has gained notable attention as an alternative to overcome these limitations. RL enables the learning of policies through trial-and-error interactions with the environment, effectively addressing complex decision problems that resist explicit modeling [9]. In particular, deep RL excels at processing high-dimensional sensor inputs and has been actively investigated in the fields of autonomous driving and robotic navigation [10]. Nonetheless, most existing RL-based path-planning studies focus on crash prevention and travel effectiveness, inadequately representing the tactical imperatives of minimizing risk exposure and maintaining mission completion under risk-constrained conditions in battlefield contexts. Furthermore, dependence on a single scalar reward or value function complicates the interpretation and adjustment of the tradeoff between path efficiency and risk avoidance. Kim et al. [11] proposed a tactical pathfinding framework that integrates semantic segmentation with a cost-weighted potential field and DQN-based selection of repulsive coefficients. Their work showed the usefulness of terrain-aware perception for tactical path generation. Nevertheless, that framework was mainly developed for potential-field-based pathfinding on semantic maps and was not intended to model incomplete observability through recurrent belief states or to explicitly decompose the efficiency–risk trade-off at the value-function level.

This study proposes an RL-based model for risk-aware tactical path planning and replanning in partially observable battlefield environments. The main contribution lies in integrating factorized time/risk value estimation, recurrent belief-state modeling, and perception-driven cost/risk representation into a unified tactical path-planning framework. To achieve this, we introduce the trajectory-value factorized recurrent proximal policy optimization (TVF-RPPO) algorithm. TVF-RPPO extends the proximal policy optimization (PPO) actor-critic framework by incorporating distinct critic heads to estimate time efficiency and risk avoidance values, which are then leveraged in a value-aware actor for policy decisions [12, 13]. In addition, to manage uncertainty in partially observable environments, we employed a gated recurrent unit (GRU)-based recurrent neural network (RNN) for belief state representation, compressing historical observations into hidden states [14]. This enables the vehicle to maintain an implicit environmental belief without requiring an explicit global map. Furthermore, a SwiftFormer-based lightweight perception module dynamically generates traversal cost and risk maps from the battlefield terrain, integrating them into the RL agent’s observations to enhance the fidelity of tactical decisions [15]. Because these generated cost and risk maps directly influence downstream planning, we also evaluate segmentation performance, class-wise behavior, confusion patterns, and planning performance under ground-truth versus predicted maps.

A key contribution of this study is the provision of a path-generation mechanism that allows post-training adjustment of risk preferences. By modulating a risk-weighting parameter during deployment, the model can flexibly produce conservative (risk-averse) or aggressive (speed-prioritized) tactical paths from a single policy, which is consistent with common scalarization-based approaches in multiobjective RL and goal-conditioned value learning [12, 16]. It also offers practical advantages by facilitating mission dependent preference adjustment within the considered simulation setting. The risk weight parameter is interpreted as a relative risk aversion coefficient rather than as a universal optimum selector. Accordingly, the resulting paths are analyzed as tactical operating points along an efficiency risk trade-off curve.

Through simulation experiments across diverse battlefield scenarios, the proposed TVF-RPPO model demonstrated lower proxy risk-exposure time than classical and RL-based baselines while maintaining competitive mission-completion performance in the tested simulation scenarios. Notably, in threat-laden scenarios, the approach learns tactical maneuvers that prioritize exposure reduction, even at the expense of an increased path length, aligning with the decision-making exigencies of battlefield operations.

Related work

Traditional path planning

Traditional robotic path planning evolved based on graph searches and geometric heuristics [1]. Algorithms such as Dijkstra’s and A* are widely employed to compute shortest distance or minimum cost paths on predefined cost maps, guaranteeing theoretical optimality in fully observable environments [3, 4]. However, these methods typically require predefined cost representations and are often configured around distance, time, or static traversal cost.

Sensor-based approaches, such as bug algorithms, allow obstacle avoidance without prior maps but often generate inefficient paths in complex terrains because of their reliance on local information [5]. Conversely, sampling-based methods, such as RRT, facilitate rapid exploration in high-dimensional spaces and accommodate dynamic environments, yet they do not guarantee path optimality or risk minimization [6]. In battlefield environments where risks are spatially continuous and probabilistically distributed, these traditional techniques are less suited to tactical risk-aware planning under partial observability unless risk models and cost functions are carefully hand-crafted, particularly when complex exposure patterns must be reduced to simple cost metrics.

Incremental and dynamic replanning

Incremental search algorithms, such as D* Lite and LPA* [7, 8], are widely used for dynamic replanning when edge costs or obstacle configurations change after an initial plan has been computed. These methods are effective for updating paths without recomputing an entire plan from scratch. However, they still depend on explicit cost-map definitions and do not learn exposure-aware tactical preferences from repeated interaction. In this study, such methods are used as specialized replanning baselines in the dynamic obstacle scenario to evaluate whether TVF-RPPO provides additional benefits beyond conventional incremental replanning.

Reinforcement learning-based path planning

RL allows agents to learn task-oriented policies through trial and error without explicit environmental modeling, making it effective for complex autonomous driving tasks [9]. Early implementations utilized Q-learning or deep Q-Networks (DQN) for obstacle avoidance and goal navigation [10, 17], whereas subsequent actor–critic architectures enabled stable learning within continuous action spaces [18].

Recently, policy gradient-based methods, such as PPO, have been established as standard approaches in autonomous driving and robotic navigation [13]. PPO achieves high performance while ensuring training stability through clipping-based updates, making it suitable for path planning in dynamic environments [19]. However, many existing RL-based path planning studies rely on single scalar rewards or value functions for policy learning, which hinders the explicit interpretation or adjustment of the tradeoff between path efficiency and risk avoidance.

Moreover, numerous studies have assumed civilian autonomous driving contexts, treating battlefield-specific elements such as concealment, cover utilization, exposure minimization, and exposure-aware mission decision-making as secondary or merely incorporating them as simple penalty terms. This often simplifies risks to binary events such as “collision occurrence” or “prohibited zone entry,” failing to adequately capture the concept of continuous risk exposure.

Path planning under partial observability

In battlefield environments, vehicles cannot observe the full state owing to sensor ranges and communication constraints, naturally modeling path planning as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) [2]. Classical POMDP research employs Bayesian or particle filters to explicitly compute belief states; however, these methods incur high computational costs when applied to large-state-space robotic path planning problems [20].

To mitigate this problem, recent studies have proposed learning-based approximations of belief states using RNNs to summarize historical observations implicitly [21]. In particular, policies based on GRU or long short-term memory (LSTM) networks can maintain long-term dependencies in partially observable settings, proving to be effective in autonomous driving and robotic control tasks [14, 22]. Nevertheless, many of these studies limit belief states to policy inputs without explicitly structuring risk and efficiency as separate value-estimation objectives.

Risk-aware and tactical path planning

Risk-aware path planning extends beyond simple obstacle avoidance by guiding robots to select paths that account for potential threats. Techniques that leverage line-of-sight, cover objects, and occlusions to generate paths minimizing exposure to adversaries have been studied in the context of covert/stealth navigation [23, 24]. Although these are meaningful attempts to incorporate battlefield specifics, they largely depend on fixed heuristics or deterministic cost functions, thereby imposing limitations.

Research combining RL with risk concepts has also emerged; however, many approaches incorporate risk as a monolithic penalty term or assign large negative rewards upon mission failure, resulting in policies dependent on sparse and discrete signals [25]. Such problems can be formalized using constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) [26], and a range of constrained and risk-aware policy optimization methods have been proposed (e.g., CPO and CVaR/chance constraints) [27, 28]. Nonetheless, for tactical navigation in the considered simulation setting, a framework is needed that can explicitly learn and balance the competing objectives of mission efficiency and proxy risk exposure while preserving mission completion.

Perception-driven cost and risk map generation

Recent navigation systems increasingly rely on semantic or terrain-aware perception to construct traversability maps, cost maps, or risk maps for downstream planning. Vision-based terrain segmentation can provide richer environmental structure than purely geometric obstacle maps, particularly in unstructured off-road or battlefield-like environments. However, when a learned perception module is used to generate planning inputs, segmentation errors can propagate into downstream path selection. Therefore, perception modules used in tactical planning should be evaluated not only by segmentation metrics but also by their downstream effect on planning performance.

In this study, the SwiftFormer-based perception module is used to generate terrain-driven cost and risk maps. To account for its influence on the full pipeline, we evaluate segmentation performance, class-wise behavior, confusion patterns, and planning performance under ground-truth versus predicted maps. Kim et al. [11] integrated semantic segmentation with a cost-weighted potential field and DQN-based repulsive coefficient selection for tactical pathfinding. This line of work demonstrates the value of coupling terrain semantics with tactical planning. However, because the decision-making component is formulated as coefficient selection for a potential-field planner, it does not explicitly address recurrent belief-state learning under partial observability or factorized time/risk value estimation. The present work extends the perception-to-planning direction toward recurrent policy learning with value-factorized risk-aware control.

Multi-objective and value-factorized reinforcement learning

Multi-objective reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for problems in which multiple goals, such as efficiency and safety, must be optimized simultaneously. Common approaches include scalarization, Pareto-front learning, preference-conditioned policies, and constrained formulations. In path-planning applications, however, many studies still combine objectives into a single scalar reward or use risk as an additional penalty, which makes it difficult to interpret how the learned policy balances travel efficiency and risk avoidance.

The proposed TVF-RPPO framework is related to this line of research but differs in its application-oriented integration of recurrent belief-state modeling, factorized time/risk value estimation, and value-aware action selection under partial observability. Rather than introducing a new general theory of multi-objective RL, this study focuses on using value factorization as a practical mechanism for controlling the efficiency–risk trade-off in a simulated tactical navigation problem.

Proposed method

The proposed framework consists of four main components: perception-based cost/risk map generation, POMDP-based state–action–reward formulation, GRU-based belief-state encoding, and trajectory-value factorized recurrent PPO. First, a lightweight SwiftFormer-based perception module estimates semantic terrain probabilities from the input image and converts them into traversability cost and risk maps. Second, the tactical path-planning problem is formulated as a partially observable decision-making problem with explicit state, action, and reward definitions. Third, a GRU-based recurrent module encodes observation history into a belief state. Finally, TVF-RPPO factorizes the value estimation into time-efficiency and risk-related components and uses these factorized estimates for value-aware action selection.

The simulation map consisted of a 100 × 100 grid in which each cell possessed terrain types and risk attributes. Discussion

Existing classical path planning techniques, such as Dijkstra’s algorithm, bug algorithms, and RRT, operate based on the global shortest paths, local avoidance, or stochastic sampling. Consequently, they face limitations in structurally incorporating risk as a core variable of the objective function and in maintaining decision consistency because of incomplete information in partially observable environments.

To address these limitations, the proposed TVF-RPPO introduces a TVF structure that evaluates the time/efficiency and risk-exposure aspects of a path separately rather than relying on a single scalar value. This approach reflects the multi-objective nature of tactical path planning by separating efficiency-related and exposure-related value estimates. Rather than guaranteeing a globally optimal solution, the factorized formulation makes the learned efficiency–risk trade-off more interpretable than a single scalar-value formulation. Our experiments demonstrated a clear trade-off: adjusting the risk weighting parameter λ caused the risk exposure time to decrease sharply at the cost of increased total travel distance. In the tested scenarios, λ around 1.0 provided one practical operating point at which exposure reduction was achieved without an excessive increase in path distance. This should not be interpreted as a universal optimum; rather, λ defines a family of tactical operating points along the efficiency–risk trade-off curve. This implies that the proposed model provides a flexible policy space capable of adapting to mission regulations or commander intent rather than offering a fixed solution.

The ablation study provides empirical evidence regarding the contribution of each component to the agent’s performance in the considered partially observable environments. Specifically, we observed that the removal of the GRU correlated with an increase in the total travel distance, the exclusion of TVF affected the risk–efficiency balance, and the omission of the value-aware actor architecture was associated with reduced policy precision under the tested scenarios.

While the proposed framework demonstrates promising results in navigating complex tactical environments, several limitations should be noted. First, our validation was conducted in a custom-built 2D battlefield simulation environment. Although we incorporated realistic terrain and threat models, the gap between simulation and real-world conditions, such as sensor noise, communication latency, and unpredictable physical dynamics, remains a challenge. Future work should focus on bridging this "sim-to-real" gap through domain randomization and field testing with physical platforms.

Second, the current risk model relies on a predefined risk field assumption where threats are static or follow predictable patterns. In more realistic adversarial settings, threats may adapt their behavior dynamically. Extending the framework to dynamic adversarial threats using multi-agent reinforcement learning, robust control, or game-theoretic modeling would be a valuable direction.

Third, the reliance on semantic segmentation for risk map generation introduces a potential source of downstream error. Although the perception module achieved reasonable segmentation performance in the internal simulation, predicted maps still produced measurable changes in downstream path distance and exposure compared with ground-truth maps. Incorporating uncertainty quantification into the perception pipeline may improve robustness against segmentation errors.

Finally, while the tunable parameter λ provides a flexible mechanism for controlling the trade-off between movement efficiency and proxy risk exposure, selecting λ currently requires manual adjustment based on mission preference or operator judgment. Developing an adaptive mechanism that adjusts λ according to situational context or mission constraints would improve the autonomy of the framework.

Overall, the results should be interpreted as evidence of improved performance on proxy risk-exposure and mission-completion metrics in the considered 2D simulation, rather than as direct evidence of real-world survivability. This interpretation is consistent with the evaluation design and avoids overextending the conclusions beyond the tested simulation setting.

Conclusion and future work

This paper proposes TVF-RPPO to address path planning challenges in partially observable battlefield environments. The proposed framework integrates SwiftFormer-based terrain perception, GRU-based belief-state modeling, and trajectory-value factorized recurrent PPO into a unified planning architecture. The perception module generates terrain-driven cost and risk maps, the GRU encodes observation history under partial observability, and the factorized value structure separates time-efficiency and risk-exposure estimates for value-aware action selection.

Experiments in a custom-built 2D battlefield simulation showed that TVF-RPPO reduced proxy risk-exposure time compared with both classical and RL-based baselines while maintaining competitive mission-completion performance. The dynamic replanning experiment further showed that the proposed method remains competitive against replanning-specialized baselines such as D* Lite and LPA*. In addition, the mission-completion and failure-type analyses helped align the conclusions with directly measured outcomes, and the perception evaluation quantified both segmentation performance and the downstream effect of predicted cost/risk maps.

These results suggest that factorized recurrent value learning can provide a practical mechanism for controlling the efficiency–risk trade-off in simulated tactical navigation. Importantly, the reported improvements should be interpreted as improvements on proxy risk-exposure metrics within the considered simulation environment, not as direct estimates of real-world survivability.

Future research should expand the proposed framework in four directions. First, spatiotemporal risk fields should be incorporated to handle moving enemies and time-varying terrain conditions more realistically. Second, the manual configuration of the risk parameter λ should be replaced or complemented by adaptive risk-preference selection, such as hierarchical RL or context-conditioned policy modulation. Third, the framework should be extended to multi-agent path planning for coordinated UGV operations. Finally, future work should address sim-to-real transfer by incorporating sensor noise, communication delay, vehicle dynamics, and field validation with physical platforms.
