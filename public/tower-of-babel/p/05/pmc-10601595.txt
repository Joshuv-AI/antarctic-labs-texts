Promoting gender diversity in the editorial boards of major otorhinolaryngology journals: A call for inclusion and equal representation
Authors: Asude Ünal, Ayşe Çeçen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of our study was to investigate the representation of women in the editorial boards of otorhinolaryngology journals indexed in the Science Citation Index‐Expanded (SCIE) database.MethodsWe examined the gender distribution of editors‐in‐chief, editorial board members, and associate/section editors in otorhinolaryngology journals indexed in the Web of Science SCI‐E core collection. We also analyzed the number of years the journals have been publishing, their categories (general otorhinolaryngology, otology‐audiology, phoniatrics, rhinology‐allergy), the country and continent of publication, the Journal Citation Indicator (JCI) 2021 values, 2‐year and 5‐year impact factors (IF), H‐index, and quartile rank of the journals.ResultsOut of the 54 editors‐in‐chief included in our study from a total of 44 journals, only 6 (11.1%) were women. Women constituted 21.6% of the editorial board members and 35.1% of the associate/section editors. The proportion of women in the editorial boards of journals with a 5‐year impact factor >3.0 was significantly higher compared to others. As the 5‐year impact factor of the journals increased, the number of women in the boards showed a linear increase. When evaluated based on journal categories, phoniatrics journals had higher representation of women in both editorial board membership and associate/section editor roles compared to other categories.ConclusionThe representation of women in the editorial boards of otorhinolaryngology journals is still inadequate. To rectify this situation, it is important for the entire academic community to exhibit a collective attitude rather than individual efforts.Level of Evidence2.Keywords: editorial board, gender diversity, otorhinolaryngology

INTRODUCTION

Equality between women and men is a universally recognized fundamental legal principle. However, gender inequality remains a well‐known reality worldwide that has yet to be resolved. 1 In recent years, there have been improvements in the representation of women in the fields of medicine and surgery. Nevertheless, the proportion of female medical students in surgical and subspecialty fields remains lower. 2 Academic success and progress rely on a range of factors such as research, publications, and participation in journal leadership. 3 Editorial boards play a crucial role in determining the quality and direction of scientific publications. Additionally, membership in editorial boards, which hold a prestigious position in medicine, enhances intellectual knowledge, fosters networking abilities, and serves as a criterion for promotion and appointment committees. The underrepresentation of women in the editorial boards of medical journals is a significant issue because women in editorial positions can contribute greater diversity during the review process and improve the quality of published studies that represent progress in the field. Therefore, failing to invest in female representation not only exacerbates the unequal distribution of career resources by journals, but also diminishes the scientific productivity by disregarding a talented pool of female academics. The question of whether women have sufficient representation in leadership positions and scientific publications in the field of otolaryngology, as in many other disciplines, remains contentious. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Our aim in this study was to systematically examine the representation of women in the editorial boards of SCIE (Science Citation Index‐Expanded) journals in the field of otolaryngology and create awareness on this issue. To achieve this goal, we planned to identify the regional variations, metrics, and categories of otolaryngology journals indexed in the Web of Science (WoS) Science Citation Index‐Expanded (SCI‐E) core collection. We evaluated the gender diversity of editors‐in‐chief and editorial board members in these journals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Approval was obtained from the Samsun University Clinical Research Ethics Committee under the reference number SÜKAEK‐2023 11/2 for our study. We included editors‐in‐chief, editorial board members, and associate/section editors of otolaryngology journals indexed in the WoS SCI‐E core collection. Journals that could not be accessed for information on editorial board members or had missing publication metric data were excluded from the study. The list of journals was determined based on a search conducted on the WoS Master Journal List (https://mjl.clarivate.com/home) using the keyword “otorhinolaryngology” as of June 8, 2023. Information about the editors‐in‐chief, associate/section editors, and editorial board members of the journals was obtained from their official websites. The gender of the participants was determined based on their names, photographs, and/or other available information. In cases of uncertainty, an internet search was conducted using the individual's name to consider their affiliated hospitals, universities, workplaces, and associations. If the gender could not be determined, a web‐based online service (https://genderize.io/) was used to determine the gender. Individuals whose gender could not be categorized were excluded from the study. Former or honorary board members, founding editors, and advisory boards were excluded from the analysis. Journal metrics such as the number of years the journals have been published, the category they belong to (general otorhinolaryngology, otology‐audiology, phoniatrics, and rhinology‐allergy), the country of publication, the continent where the country is located, Journal Citation Indicator (JCI) 2021 values, 2‐year and 5‐year impact factors (IF), H‐index, and quartile were recorded from the journals within the scope of WoS.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

After coding the data obtained from the study, it was transferred to a computer and analyzed using SPSS software (Version 22 for Windows, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The normal distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When evaluating the data, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum and maximum values). Frequency data were expressed as numbers and percentages (%). In the study, for each journal, the “percentage of female editorial board members” was calculated by dividing the number of female editorial board members and associate/section editors by the total number of editorial board members and then multiplying the result by 100. The “percentage of female associate/section editors” was calculated similarly. The journals were grouped according to various characteristics, and the female percentage values were compared. Due to the non‐normal distribution of the variables, the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used for comparisons between groups. In variables where significant differences were found between two or more groups, the “Bonferroni‐corrected Mann–Whitney U test” was performed to determine the group that contributed to the difference. The correlation between the female editorial board member percentage, female associate/section editor percentage, and continuous variables of the journals was examined using the Spearman correlation test. Based on the obtained correlation coefficient (r), the strength of the relationship was classified as follows: r = .00–.24 (weak), r = .25–.49 (moderate), r = .50–.74 (strong), and r = .75–1.00 (very strong). The statistical significance level was set at p < .05 for all tests.

RESULTS

Out of the 44 journals examined, 23 (52.3%) were published in the United States, 19 (43.2%) in Europe, and 2 (4.5%) in Asia, spanning 12 different countries. The most frequently published country was the United States with 22 journals (50%) and followed by the United Kingdom with 6 journals (13.6%). When evaluated by journal categories, the most common categories were General Otolaryngology (59.1%) and Otology‐Audiology (25%). The average publication duration of the journals was determined to be 44.8 ± 33.9 (min: 7–max: 136) years. The mean Journal Citation Indicator (JCI) value of the journals was 0.9 ± 0.4 (min: 0.4–max: 2.41), and the 2‐year H‐index was calculated to be an average of 73.3 ± 34.6 (min: 20–max: 168). In terms of quartile ranking, 21 journals (47.7%) were in Q1, 17 journals (38.6%) in Q2, 4 journals (9.1%) in Q3, and 2 journals (4.5%) in Q4.(Table 1).

In the analysis conducted, it was determined that there were a total of 54 editors‐in‐chief, with 38 (86.4%) in 1 journal and 6 (13.6%) in >1 journal. Among the journals with >1 editor‐in‐chief, there were no female editors‐in‐chief (0.0%), while in 6 (15.7%) of the journals with one editor‐in‐chief, the editors‐in‐chief were women. In total, female editors‐in‐chief accounted for 13.6% of all journals. Out of the total 54 editors‐in‐chief, 11.1% were women. However, due to the insufficient number of female editors‐in‐chief (n = 6) for statistical comparisons, advanced analyses could not be performed in the study.

In our study, seven journals (15.9%) had no information about the editorial board members, only editor‐in‐chief and/or associate/section editors were listed. Among the remaining 37 journals, there were a total of 2010 editorial board members, varying in numbers from 4 to 187. Two of these journals (5.4%) had no female members, while the other 35 journals had a total of 435 female members. Women constituted 21.6% of the editorial board members. There were associate/section editors in 40 journals, with a total of 655 members. Among them, 230 (35.1%) were women. The gender of seven members (7/2665) of the editorial board and associate/section editors could not be determined in the study. In the statistical evaluations, it was found that there was a significantly lower proportion of female editorial board members in European journals (p = .05). Among European countries, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and Germany had the lowest median values for female editorial board membership (between 8.0% and 11.1%), while the Netherlands had the highest (18.9%). However, when considering all countries, there was no significant difference in terms of female representation in editorial board membership (p = .12). When evaluated in terms of journal categories, the proportion of women in both editorial board membership and associate/section editorship was higher in phoniatrics journals compared to other categories (p = .009 and p = .003, respectively). (Table 2).

It was determined that the proportion of women in editorial board membership of journals with a 5‐year impact factor >3.0 was statistically significantly higher compared to others (p = .035). However, there was no significant difference in terms of WoS Journal Citation Indicator (JCI) 2021 values, 2‐year impact factor (IF), H‐index, and quartile rank for both editorial board membership and associate/section editorship in terms of the proportion of women. (Table 2).

In a correlational analysis conducted by considering continuous data, it was found that the proportions of women in both the editorial board and associate/section editor positions of journals exhibited a moderately close linear relationship with the journals’ 5‐year impact factor scores (r = .29, p = .09, r = .31, and p = .05, respectively). In other words, as the 5‐year impact factor scores of journals increased, the number of women in their boards also showed a linear increase. However, no significant linear correlation was found between the proportions of women in editorial board and associate/section editor positions and the quartiles (Q1–Q4), publication period, Journal Citation Indicator (JCI) score, H‐index, and 2021 impact factor values of the journals (p > .05).

DISCUSSION

Gender equality and representation of women should be considered important issues in the field of medicine. As observed in many medical fields, there is an insufficient representation of women in the editorial boards of journals that publish in the field of otolaryngology (Ear, Nose, and Throat—ENT). This situation suggests that efforts toward gender equality are not sufficient and that the talents of women scientists are not adequately appreciated.

Numerous previous studies had reported inadequate representation of women in editorial boards across various medical fields. This issue appears to be particularly pronounced in surgical specialties such as orthopedics, anesthesia, general surgery, spine surgery, neurosurgery, when compared to internal medicine disciplines. 4 , 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Choi and Miller had noted proportional representation of female otolaryngologists in journal editorships and editorial memberships. 7 However, after examining data from 9 otolaryngology journals, Litvack et al. pointed that, despite observing an increase in the representation of women in editorial boards from 7.2% to 17.7% between 1997 and 2017 (p = .0001), and an increase in the proportion of women associate/section editors from 9.3% to 20.9% (p = .09), women were still inadequately represented in editorial boards. Furthermore, they reported that none of these significant otolaryngology journals had a female editor‐in‐chief. 3 In our study, out of a total of 54 chief editors, only 6 (11.1%) were women. Three of them were in the field of otology‐audiology, two in general otolaryngology, and one in rhinology. Among the 44 journals we examined, 21.6% of the 2010 editorial board members were women, while 35.1% of associate/section editors were women. In the study conducted by Litvack et al., only nine journals with high impact factors were included. Limiting the examination to journals with a high impact factor restricts the scope of the study by excluding all other otorhinolaryngology journals. In contrast, our study encompassed an analysis of 44 otorhinolaryngology journals within the SCIE database, aiming to shed light on the current state of gender representation. We conducted a comprehensive comparison, considering various journal metrics as well as different journal categories. Our study exclusively focused on examining gender disparities within a binary framework, distinguishing between male and female representation. The study did not assess racial, ethnic differences, or the representation of nonbinary identities. This limitation arises from our research's lack of exploration into the complex interplay between these various forms of discrimination and their relationship with gender representation. Because our study is a cross‐sectional study, it provides valuable insight into current landscape but it does not capture changes over time. Therefore, while we observed an increase in female representation compared to previous studies, which offers promising prospects, a more extensive timeframe and the incorporation of additional parameters in future research are needed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the current state and future trends. Nonetheless, the observed increase in female representation remains promising.

In our study, we found that there was a significantly lower proportion of female editorial board members in European journals (14.2%). This rate was determined to be 20.8% in the American continent and 19% in Asia. It was considered that this disparity could be attributed to intercontinental social and cultural differences.

Additionally, in our study, it was determined that journals with a 5‐year impact factor >3.0 had a statistically significantly higher proportion of women in their editorial boards compared to others (p = .035). As the 5‐year impact factor scores of the journals increased, the number of women in their boards also showed a linear increase. These findings suggest that adequate representation of women in editorial boards is important for the scientific productivity and quality of journals.

In our study, when journals were evaluated based on their categories, it was found that the proportions of women in both the editorial board and associate/section editor positions were higher in phoniatrics journals compared to other categories (p = .009 and p = .003, respectively). This finding suggests that the higher representation of women working in the field of phoniatrics may be reflected in the editorial boards of the journals.

The underrepresentation of women in editorial boards of journals can have multiple reasons. Gender roles and societal expectations can still have an influence, particularly in the field of medicine, especially surgical specialties. These expectations can create barriers for women in their academic careers and limit their participation in editorial boards. The lack of supportive infrastructure that promotes women's academic success is another contributing factor to the low representation of women in editorial boards. Issues such as childcare and family responsibilities can pose additional challenges for women in their professional lives. Discrimination, whether conscious or unconscious, in the selection process of editorial board members can diminish women's representation. Biases can lead to a preference for male candidates over female candidates. In addressing this issue, it is important to provide education and create awareness to achieve gender balance in editorial boards. Such approaches will encourage editorial boards to make efforts to achieve gender balance and inspire women scientists to participate more. Providing mentorship and support to women scientists can encourage their advancement in academic careers and help them work with strong role models and develop themselves.

CONCLUSION

The representation of women in editorial boards of ENT journals is an important step toward achieving gender equality. Overcoming traditional gender roles and discriminatory selection processes requires education and awareness. Providing support to women scientists can encourage their advancement in academic careers. However, it is not only individual efforts that matter, but also a collective attitude from the entire academic community. Such an attitude and collaboration can contribute to creating a more equitable scientific community by increasing the representation of women in ENT journals.
