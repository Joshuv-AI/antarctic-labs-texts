Clozapine‐induced diarrhea: A case report and mechanistic overview
Authors: Shahab Dastmardi, Soha Namazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClozapine is an atypical antipsychotic that is highly effective in managing treatment‐resistant schizophrenia. However, its use is associated with a wide range of adverse effects, including agranulocytosis, seizures, sedation, and gastrointestinal issues such as constipation, and less commonly, diarrhea. While constipation is a well‐documented side effect of clozapine, diarrhea induced by the drug remains underreported and poorly understood.Case PresentationA 46‐year‐old male patient with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia developed severe diarrhea, fever, and weight loss shortly after initiating clozapine at a dose of 100 mg three times daily (300 mg/day). These symptoms persisted until clozapine was discontinued, after which they resolved. When clozapine was reintroduced, the symptoms of diarrhea recurred, confirming that clozapine was the causative agent of the gastrointestinal distress. The patient's clinical condition improved once clozapine was discontinued again, highlighting the potential for severe gastrointestinal adverse effects associated with this medication.ConclusionClozapine remains a valuable treatment option for individuals with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, but clinicians should be aware of its potential to cause severe gastrointestinal adverse effects, including diarrhea. Early recognition of these side effects, appropriate diagnostic evaluation, and timely management are essential to ensure patient safety and optimize treatment outcomes. In cases where gastrointestinal symptoms are severe, discontinuation of clozapine may be necessary to resolve the issues and prevent further complications.Keywords: clozapine, clozapine‐induced diarrhea, pharmacotherapy, schizophrenia treatment

INTRODUCTION

Clozapine is an atypical antipsychotic widely recognized for its efficacy in managing treatment‐resistant schizophrenia. 1 Approximately 30%–60% of all schizophrenic patients who fail to respond to typical antipsychotics may respond to clozapine. 2

Despite its effectiveness, clozapine is associated with numerous adverse effects. These include agranulocytosis, seizures, sedation, delirium, obsessive–compulsive symptoms, hypotension, tachycardia, weight gain, sialorrhea, elevated liver enzymes, constipation, nausea, enuresis, fever, and neuromuscular effects. 3

While constipation is a well‐recognized adverse effect, affecting 30%–60% of patients on clozapine, diarrhea remains an underreported and less understood reaction. 4

The pathophysiology of clozapine‐induced diarrhea is not well established, but it has been hypothesized to result from drug‐induced hypersensitivity reactions, dysregulation of gut motility, or inflammatory responses. 5

Recent case reports have linked clozapine to conditions such as microscopic colitis and eosinophilic colitis, suggesting a possible immune‐mediated or inflammatory mechanism. Patients experiencing clozapine‐induced diarrhea have presented with severe symptoms, including fever, eosinophilia, and abdominal pain, sometimes requiring treatment discontinuation. In some cases, the presence of Charcot–Leyden crystals in stool has been indicative of eosinophilic involvement. 1 , 4 , 5

We here describe the case of a patient who consistently reacted to clozapine prescription early in her treatment, with a constellation of fever, abdominal pain, weight loss, and diarrhea.

CASE REPORT

In this case report, a 46‐year‐old male patient with a history of treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, who had been on multiple antipsychotic medications including perphenazine (2 mg three times daily [TDS]), risperidone (2 mg TDS), haloperidol (0.5 mg TDS), and trihexyphenidyl (2 mg TDS), was recently prescribed clozapine was initiated as an adjunct to the patient's existing antipsychotic medications. Clozapine was administered to the patient at a dosage of 100 mg TDS.

The patient reported active cigarette smoking (approximately 10 cigarettes/day), and his body mass index (BMI) was approximately 24 kg/m2.

On Day 4 of clozapine treatment, the patient developed diarrhea with 6–8 daily episodes of watery, green stools, accompanied by fever and chills.

There was no history of chronic gastrointestinal, autoimmune, or infectious disease.

After 2 weeks, he was admitted to Imam Khomeini Complex Hospital (affiliated to TUMS*). Empirical antibiotic therapy, including ciprofloxacin (400 mg twice daily) and metronidazole (500 mg every 8 h), was initiated to address a potential infectious cause based on the clinical presentation. However, after ruling out infectious etiologies (both microbial and viral) through viral testing (influenza A/B and COVID‐19) and stool analysis (Table 1), the antibiotics were discontinued.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed a significant elevation in white blood cell count (WBC = 17.3 × 103/µL) with neutrophilia (90.2%). Furthermore, biochemical tests indicated elevated blood urea nitrogen (78 mg/dL) and creatinine levels (2.1 mg/dL), while the eosinophil count was 0.3%. Acute renal dysfunction at presentation was attributed to dehydration due to severe diarrhea.

Plasma clozapine and norclozapine concentrations were not measured during treatment.

Colonoscopy revealed normal findings throughout the entire intestine, including the anus, rectum, sigmoid colon, ascending colon, and terminal ileum, with no gross pathological lesions detected. A biopsy was obtained.

As a result of the timing of diarrhea onset, associated symptoms, and colonoscopy findings, clozapine‐induced diarrhea with microscopic colitis was suspected. It was reported that the diarrhea resolved within a week after discontinuing clozapine and administering supportive therapy, including loperamide. To treat the patient's treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, psychiatry and pharmacotherapy services recommended cautiously restarting clozapine at 25 mg daily, with gradual titration under close supervision. If adverse effects, such as diarrhea, persist, the patient should seek medical attention. Following a telephone follow‐up, we noticed the patient's diarrhea a week after starting treatment again, and the patient was asked to return, but unfortunately, the patient did not return, and we no longer have information about the patient's condition.

Biopsy results received 2 weeks after discharge showed multiple crypt abscesses with mild architectural irregularities in the terminal ileum, ascending colon, and sigmoid colon. The pathology report highlighted infectious, drug‐induced, and ischemic causes. It also considered the potential for early inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which requires further clinicopathologic evaluation.

DISCUSSION

Clozapine, as a second‐generation antipsychotic, has considerable adverse effects, including causing severe blood conditions, cardiomyopathy, anticholinergic effects, gastrointestinal hypomotility, and so forth.

Clozapine commonly causes gastrointestinal hypomotility and constipation, but rarely causes diarrhea (3%–5% of patients). However, serious complications such as clozapine‐induced colitis, including microscopic or necrotizing colitis, have been reported, requiring discontinuation and further evaluation. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear. 6

Immune‐mediated reaction

Clozapine has been linked to eosinophilic or microscopic colitis in some patients, with diarrhea, peripheral eosinophilia, and Charcot–Leyden crystals in stool appearing early in treatment. This suggests a hypersensitivity reaction in the colon. Abnormal Human Leukocyte Antigen – DR isotype expression on colonic cells may indicate genetic susceptibility to immune activation. 1 , 7

Serotonergic receptor modulation

Clozapine acts as an antagonist at multiple serotonin receptors (such as 5‐HT2 and possibly 5‐HT3 receptors) in the gastrointestinal tract. Disruption of normal serotonergic signaling can alter gut secretions and motility. In some cases, these effects might shift the balance from hypomotility (and constipation) to increased secretion and rapid transit, contributing to diarrhea. 8

Cholinergic rebound phenomenon

Clozapine exhibits anticholinergic properties that typically reduce gastrointestinal motility (contributing to constipation). However, abrupt changes in clozapine levels, such as rapid dose adjustments or discontinuation, might lead to a rebound increase in cholinergic activity. This rebound can enhance intestinal secretions and motility, which in some cases may manifest as diarrhea. 9

Clozapine alters gut microbiota composition

Since gut microbiota play a crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, disruptions caused by clozapine‐induced alterations in microbiota may contribute to gastrointestinal side effects, such as diarrhea. 10

Smoking status and BMI are important determinants of clozapine pharmacokinetics and plasma concentrations. Cigarette smoking induces CYP1A2† activity, which may reduce clozapine plasma levels and lower the concentration‐to‐dose (C/D) ratio, whereas higher BMI has been associated with increased clozapine exposure.

In the present case, the patient was an active smoker (approximately 10 cigarettes per day) with a BMI of approximately 24 kg/m2. Despite the potential for smoking‐related induction of clozapine metabolism, the patient developed early‐onset gastrointestinal symptoms shortly after clozapine initiation. This observation suggests that factors beyond smoking status alone, such as individual susceptibility or immune‐mediated mechanisms, may have contributed to the adverse reaction.

This study presents a new case of clozapine‐related diarrhea. The key features of this case include passing watery, greenish, non‐bloody stool 6–8 times a day, weight loss, fever, and elevation in leukocyte and creatinine levels in laboratory tests. Colonoscopy findings were normal with no gross pathological lesions. Symptoms disappeared after clozapine was discontinued, but appeared again after drug rechallenge. Several case reports have been published, but the pathomechanisms are still speculative and unclear.

In 2005, a case report was published about a 45‐year‐old schizophrenic man with concurrent administration of clozapine and other antipsychotics. After 2 weeks of clozapine therapy with 200 mg/day, he developed symptoms such as fever and non‐bloody diarrhea. As all the different potential causes were excluded, laboratory tests confirmed a significant elevation in peripheral eosinophilia. After 10 days, a colonoscopy was performed, and with random biopsies, histopathological evidence was in favor of eosinophilic colitis. 11

A 2018 study conducted in a Finnish hospital found that all four cases developed severe diarrhea 1–2 weeks after starting clozapine at a dose range of 125–200 mg/day. Two patients developed peripheral eosinophilia, one of whom showed Charcot–Leyden crystals. One patient's colonoscopy confirmed microscopic colitis, while another discontinued clozapine due to the severe symptoms related to colitis; however, slightly peripheral eosinophilia occurred after drug discontinuation. 1

In 2020, a woman with schizophrenia and a history of substance use experienced a rapid onset of diarrhea, vomiting, and fever during three separate clozapine trials, with symptoms resolving upon discontinuation. Elevated eosinophil counts, C‐reactive protein (CRP), and body temperature were observed, and a Naranjo score of 9 confirmed a definitive association between clozapine and these reactions. 4

In 2012, a 32‐year‐old man with chronic schizophrenia, who had been on 700 mg of clozapine for 15 years, was hospitalized for 55 days due to diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Lab tests showed elevated CRP and WBC. Sigmoidoscopy revealed necrotizing colitis, probably caused by clozapine's anticholinergic effects, leading to increased intraluminal pressure, mucosal vessel compression, bacterial translocation, and ischemia. 12

Management of clozapine‐induced colitis starts with discontinuing clozapine, which resolves most symptoms. Severe cases may require budesonide (9 mg/day for 6–8 weeks) to reduce inflammation. In refractory cases, immunosuppressants or biologics like infliximab may be used. Close monitoring is essential to prevent recurrence. 13

In our case, 1 week after restarting clozapine at 25 mg, recurrent diarrhea was reported by an accompanying family member, and the patient did not seek further hospital‐based evaluation.

This case emphasizes the need for careful monitoring of clozapine's rare side effects and thorough clinical and pathological evaluation of gastrointestinal issues in schizophrenia patients, especially when reinitiating treatment. Differential causes were excluded through lab tests, including negative results for viral markers, influenza, COVID‐19, and stool analysis for infections.

Recent evidence suggests that early inflammatory adverse events during clozapine initiation may be associated with rapid dose escalation. The clozapine C/D ratio has been proposed as a surrogate marker of metabolic capacity, with higher ratios indicating poor metabolizers who may be at increased risk of early adverse reactions and may require slower titration strategies. 14

In the present case, plasma clozapine and norclozapine concentrations were not available, which limits assessment of the patient's metabolic status.

Clozapine is the most effective treatment for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorders, despite its side effects, which may lead to its discontinuation. A study in Finland revealed that reinitiating clozapine after discontinuation was associated with the lowest risks of readmission, treatment failure, and mortality, although restarting it following severe adverse effects, such as neutropenia, is uncommon. Olanzapine serves as a robust second‐line option, while aripiprazole has demonstrated effectiveness in both monotherapy and combination therapy. Studies in the United Kingdom and Finland suggest that antipsychotic polytherapy, including clozapine with olanzapine and quetiapine, is effective in preventing ward readmission. 15

Studies showed that electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) augmentation improves response rates in treatment‐resistant schizophrenia. A case of clozapine‐induced agranulocytosis treated with monthly ECT and long‐acting injectable paliperidone (150 mg/month) improved symptoms and prevented relapse for 17 months, allowing the patient to return to work. 16

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, while clozapine is highly effective for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, clinicians must remain vigilant about its gastrointestinal side effects, particularly diarrhea. Prompt recognition, accurate diagnosis, and effective management are critical for ensuring patient safety. Additional research is necessary to better understand these mechanisms and to develop improved prevention and treatment strategies.
