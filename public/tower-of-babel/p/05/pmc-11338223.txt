Succinate dehydrogenase–complex II regulates skeletal muscle cellular respiration and contractility but not muscle mass in genetically induced pulmonary emphysema
Authors: Joseph Balnis, Ankita Tufts, Emily L. Jackson, Lisa A. Drake, Diane V. Singer, David Lacomis, Chun Geun Lee, Jack A. Elias, Jason D. Doles, L. James Maher, Annie Jen, Joshua J. Coon, David Jourd’heuil, Harold A. Singer, Catherine E. Vincent, Ariel Jaitovich
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Reduced skeletal muscle mass and oxidative capacity coexist in patients with pulmonary emphysema and are independently associated with higher mortality. If reduced cellular respiration contributes to muscle atrophy in that setting remains unknown. Using a mouse with genetically induced pulmonary emphysema that recapitulates muscle dysfunction, we found that reduced activity of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) is a hallmark of its myopathic changes. We generated an inducible, muscle-specific SDH knockout mouse that demonstrates lower mitochondrial oxygen consumption, myofiber contractility, and exercise endurance. Respirometry analyses show that in vitro complex I respiration is unaffected by loss of SDH subunit C in muscle mitochondria, which is consistent with the pulmonary emphysema animal data. SDH knockout initially causes succinate accumulation associated with a down-regulated transcriptome but modest proteome effects. Muscle mass, myofiber type composition, and overall body mass constituents remain unaltered in the transgenic mice. Thus, while SDH regulates myofiber respiration in experimental pulmonary emphysema, it does not control muscle mass or other body constituents.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, the interaction between locomotor skeletal muscle mass and myofiber respiration in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has been debated (1–9). Both muscle atrophy and myofiber respiratory perturbations are independent predictors of mortality, even after multivariably adjusting for the magnitude of pulmonary disease (10–12). As both reduced muscle mass and lower myofiber oxidative capacity typically coexist in patients with COPD (1, 13), it is currently unknown whether abnormal myofiber respiration causes muscle mass deterioration or not. The lack of adequate animal models has precluded a mechanistic evaluation of these processes. A better understanding of cellular and molecular mechanisms controlling muscle integrity in COPD is critical to develop strategies to improve patient outcomes, including survival (13).

Among patients with COPD, skeletal muscle dysfunction is more prevalent in those with pulmonary emphysema than those with chronic bronchitis (14). We have recently reported that an animal model of genetically induced pulmonary emphysema develops a skeletal muscle profile with features of COPD-associated myopathy, including muscle atrophy and reduced force generation capacity (15), oxidative stress response (16), and dysfunctional regeneration potential (17). As this animal also demonstrates lower muscle oxygen consumption and fatigue tolerance, we performed an unbiased analysis of the skeletal muscle proteome that revealed reduced expression of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) subunit C (SDHC) and lower SDH activity (15, 18).

Notably, diminished muscle SDH expression and activity are markers of poor oxidative capacity in multiple clinical settings including COPD (19–23). SDH is the only enzyme of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle that also participates in the electron transport chain (ETC) as complex II (24). Complex II–dependent respiration is reduced in mitochondria from wasted muscles of patients with COPD, even when fully supplemented with succinate, suggesting SDH dysfunction in this setting (5, 7, 8). Moreover, SDHC is an anchor of complex II in the mitochondrial inner membrane, and SDHC down-regulation destabilizes the entire SDH complex (25). A previous study has also suggested that succinate dietary supplementation increases oxygen consumption and mitochondrial biogenesis in the skeletal muscle via interaction with succinate receptor SUCNR1 (26), suggesting that succinate accumulation due to SDH dysfunction influences muscle integrity as well. The fact that succinate inhibits dozens of 2-ketoglutarate–dependent dioxygenases, including those regulating histone and DNA epigenetic methylation and the stability of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), further points to cellular deregulation by SDH loss (27).

We have previously shown that reconstitution of SDH activity in COPD animal muscles leads to higher oxygen consumption and fatigue tolerance as well as reduced succinate accumulation, all suggesting that SDH deficiency may be causally responsible for impaired muscle myofiber oxidative capacity in COPD (18). Because our previous study evaluated the regulation of SDH in an animal model with COPD, which is associated with multiple confounders such as hypoxemia and lower motility (15, 18), we aimed here to investigate if SDH deficiency alone, without other effects associated with the COPD model, is sufficient to drive defects in muscle function. To do that, we have now generated a conditional, skeletal muscle–specific SDHC knockout (KO) mouse and evaluated the effects of SDH/complex II reduction on multiple domains of muscle integrity. While we predicted that these mice would have a substantial muscle wasting phenotype, to our surprise, they showed a resilient muscle profile characterized by reduction of both fatigue tolerance and maximal muscle contractility with preservation of muscle mass and myofiber type identity. Investigation of mitochondrial respiration showed a reduction in complex II–dependent oxygen consumption rate (OCR), which did not influence complex I activity. SDHC KO causes an initial sharp down-regulation of the muscle transcriptomic landscape with less conspicuous effects on the corresponding proteome. We present a model of SDH-driven skeletal muscle function regulation in vivo, demonstrating the resilience of the skeletal muscle to the reduction of a critical mitochondrial enzyme characterizing COPD myopathy.

RESULTS

Conditional, Club cell–specific interleukin-13 overexpression causes pulmonary emphysema with an associated muscle metabolic and morphologic phenotype reminiscent of COPD myopathy

To revalidate our selected animal model of genetically induced pulmonary emphysema/COPD, we provided doxycycline to induce IL13TG (COPD) mice and compared these with doxycycline-treated IL13WT [wild type (WT)] counterparts. This animal holds a Club cell–specific, doxycycline-inducible interleukin-13 (IL-13) construct (28). Thus, while this animal is unremarkable without the transgene induction, upon stimulation with doxycycline, it develops a pulmonary emphysema phenotype that is accompanied by remarkable muscle wasting features, which follow the trajectory seen in patients with COPD with muscle dysfunction (15). Lung macroscopy demonstrated evidence of hyperinflation as reflected by a larger organ size, which was corroborated by microscopic examination of lung sections showing airspace enlargement in IL13TG compared with IL13WT animals (fig. S1, A and B). COPD mice demonstrate a reduced expression and activity of mitochondrial SDH (18). This SDH activity reduction is associated with elevation of SDH substrate succinate in COPD mice, indicating in vivo relevance (fig. S1C). To define the muscle wasting phenotype of COPD mice, we obtained extensor digitorum longus (EDL) muscles and processed them by sectioning and staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and unbiasedly scoring fiber sizes with CellProfiler. This indicated a COPD-induced redistribution of fiber sizes to the lower range as indicated in the histogram (fig. S1D) and a reduction of the mean value of muscle cross-sectional area (fig. S1E). These data indicate that, as previously reported (15–18, 29), this animal model of genetically induced pulmonary emphysema elicits a muscle phenotype that is reminiscent of COPD myopathy. These results supported the rationale to further investigate SDH regulation of muscle integrity using validated methods.

Conditional, muscle-specific SDHC KO causes an early regulation of transcriptome and proteome

To independently investigate the effect of muscle respiration apart from confounding aspects of COPD myopathy, we crossed an animal with an Sdhc gene exon floxed allele (30, 31) with a mouse whose genome encoded tamoxifen-dependent Cre recombinase expression under the control of the human skeletal α-actin (HSA or ACTA1) promoter and compared these with WT littermates receiving tamoxifen (Fig. 1A). This mutation leads to a skeletal muscle–specific ablation of SDHC, which is located at the ubiquinol binding site. This is the SDH subunit we found to be specifically down-regulated in the COPD animal model (Fig. 1B) (15, 18). To unbiasedly observe the early effects of SDHC KO in the skeletal muscle, we initially selected a time point 21 days after tamoxifen-induced Sdhc rearrangement (Fig. 1C). This has previously been reported to be the earliest time of significant SDH product down-regulation in SDHC KO mice (30). An RNA sequencing analysis revealed remarkable transcriptional down-regulation of all but one of the significantly regulated products, including SDHC mRNA (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Tables). These data are consistent with the possible epigenetic effects of succinate accumulation due to SDH loss of function, such as histone and DNA hypermethylation (32). As conditional SDHC KO mice were phenotypically unremarkable at this time point, to identify subtler changes, we conducted an unbiased proteomics analysis of samples from the same individuals used to generate the RNA sequencing data. This demonstrated an unexpectedly milder effect of SDHC KO on the proteomic landscape yet with evidence of multiple SDH subunits being down-regulated (Fig. 1, E, E1, and E2, and Supplementary Tables). Succinate is a powerful regulator of gene expression by inhibition of the activity of 2-oxoglutarate–dependent dioxygenases, a family of enzymes catalyzing hydroxylation reactions (33), which include epigenetic regulators ten-eleven translocases (TETs) and histone lysine demethylases (KDMs) (34). To provide insight into the possible epigenetic derangement due to SDHC KO, we measured metabolites at this early time point and found an increase in the succinate/α-ketoglutarate ratio relevant to dioxygenase inhibition (Fig. 1F and fig. S2). Together, these experiments indicate that the SDH loss of function regulates the early muscle transcriptomic landscape. This may be due to epigenetic effects of accumulated succinate. Effects on the proteome are more limited, perhaps suggesting that many adult muscle proteins are relatively stable such that their levels are not immediately responsive to mRNA changes.

Conditional, muscle-specific SDHC KO mice are viable and exhibit reduced SDH expression associated with muscle and plasma succinate accumulation

Our previous research using a mouse model of genetically induced pulmonary emphysema was conducted in adult mice, corresponding to the time frame when the COPD animal develops a muscle wasting phenotype (Fig. 1C). As these are C57BL/6 adult animals, this 34-week-old, 20-week post-induction timing also prevents the confounding effect of age-related sarcopenia (35). At that age, conditional, muscle-specific SDHC KO mice were viable and demonstrated no conspicuous distress or phenotype. The 20-week post-induction survival curves of WT and KO animals over the course of the study were similar (fig. S3). To ascertain the muscle specificity of SDHC KO, we conducted Western blot analyses of multiple tissues and found that SDHC protein was specifically depleted in the skeletal muscle (Fig. 2A). These changes were associated with depletion of the SDHC RNA transcript, but not depletion of SDHA, SDHB, or SDHD transcripts (Fig. 2B). Muscles were then processed for liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis of TCA intermediates, which revealed a large accumulation of SDH substrate succinate and reduction of SDH product fumarate and downstream intermediate malate while showing a preservation of adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP), adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP), and adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP) levels, comparable to those observed in the muscles of WT mice (Fig. 2C). Moreover, as succinate is reported to be transported across biological membranes (36), we determined plasma metabolite concentrations and corroborated accumulation of succinate in the extracellular space (fig. S4). To identify potential target genes whose expression might accompany SDH reduction at this later time point, we conducted a transcriptome analysis that revealed, along with the expected reduction in SDHC expression, altered levels of multiple other transcripts (Fig. 2D). Gene ontology analysis noted associations with angiogenesis and biotic stimuli as up-regulated and terms associated with protein posttranslational modification being down-regulated (Fig. 2E). We were surprised by the contrasting differences between the transcriptomic landscapes induced by SDHC KO at the earlier and later time points, suggesting compensatory mechanisms dampening the succinate-driven transcriptional effects. Nevertheless, these data suggest that the muscle-specific SDHC KO mouse is a viable model to investigate effects of SDH loss in the skeletal muscle.

Sdhc ablation reduces complex II activity and does not influence complex I activity in muscles from SDHC KO animals

In the next set of experiments, we determined the activity of SDH/complex II by measuring mitochondrial respiratory function. To this end, we measured OCRs in permeabilized tibialis anterior (TA) muscles by high-resolution respirometry (37). We found that a complex I–dependent OCR in the presence of substrates (glutamate, malate, and ADP) was similar in WT and SDHC KO mice. Thus, SDHC KO mice do not lose complex I function [Fig. 3, A and B, emphasis on L (leak) and CI (complex I)]. In contrast, addition of the SDH substrate (succinate) resulted in a further increase in OCR only in WT muscles but not in SDHC KO muscles. This indicates that, even upon maximal substrate availability, complex II–dependent respiration is not operational in the SDHC KO mice (Fig. 3, A and B, emphasis on CI+CII). CI+CII analysis was conducted by adding a substrate for complex II while already providing a substrate for complex I, which revealed that the ~40 pmol/s per minute of O2 flux generated by SDHC KO mice depends exclusively on an operational complex I, and KO muscles receive no further increase in OCR as observed in WT mice. Maximal respiratory capacity [carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) addition] was also significantly decreased in SDHC KO mice (Fig. 3, A and B, ETS). While these results were consistent with previous data showing that the ETC in skeletal muscles usually operates near maximal respiratory capacity (38), they clearly indicate that the net reduction in mitochondrial capacity in the muscles of SDHC KO mice is due primarily to complex II dysfunction. Last, inhibition of complex I by rotenone led to near-complete blunting of OCR in the SDHC KO muscles (Fig. 3, A and B, CII). In contrast, muscles from WT mice maintained a high OCR, suggesting that, during substrate-rich assays in vitro, complexes I and II contribute electrons to the ETC.

We found that the respiratory profile of muscles from COPD mice was similar to the conditional SDHC KO mice, i.e., loss of a complex II–dependent OCR (Fig. 3B). These results obtained by high-resolution respirometry were also consistent with past studies on COPD mouse muscles obtained from plate respirometry analysis (15, 18). This is the same respiratory pattern shown by isolated mitochondria obtained from wasted vastus lateralis muscles of patients with COPD (5, 7, 8). Last, to rule out the possibility that a mitochondrial ultrastructural defect could contribute to the reduced OCR profile in SDHC mice, we conducted a dedicated mitochondria-focused electron microscopy analysis on muscles from these animals and found them to be unremarkable relative to COPD and WT mice (15, 18). There were no notable inclusion bodies, edema, membrane breaks, smaller or larger sizes, vacuolization, or any other surrogate of structural dysfunction (Fig. 3D). Overall, our results indicate that both COPD and SDHC KO models cause a reduced muscle oxygen consumption due to complex II loss of function without affecting complex I activity.

Muscle-specific SDHC KO does not cause the wasting phenotype observed in genetically induced mouse and human COPD

It is unknown if reduced muscle oxidative capacity leads to skeletal muscle wasting. To test this, we determined the impact of SDH KO on muscle integrity. Total body mass, which often covaries with muscle atrophy in COPD (39), was preserved in muscle-specific SDHC KO mice relative to WT controls across sexes (Fig. 4A). As food intake and spontaneous motion could confound the wasting-inducing signal of transgenic animals, we determined that these characteristics were similar across genotypes (Fig. 4, B and C). We evaluated muscle mass by obtaining TA, EDL, and soleus muscles from WT and SDHC KO animals and determined their wet weights. These did not differ between genotypes (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the scoring of cross-sectional areas in cryosections from EDL and soleus muscles showed no difference between SDHC KO and control mice (Fig. 4E). As succinate has been previously reported to regulate muscle myofiber composition (26), we stained EDL and soleus muscle sections using isoform-specific antibodies for myosin heavy chain (MHC). Both the myofiber-specific cross-sectional areas (Fig. 4, F and G) and the percentage of myofibers expressing a given isoform (Fig. 4, H and I) were found similar across genotypes. Isoform-specific MHC staining is a surrogate for oxidative capacity in the skeletal muscle (40). However, multiple reports indicate that myofibers can regulate respiration capacity without a change in MHC staining (40, 41). Thus, we performed adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) staining, the canonical method to specify muscle fiber oxidative profile (42), on the same samples. These data also showed no difference between WT and KO mice (Fig. 4J and fig. S5). As succinate is known to be transported across biological membranes and regulate adipose tissue mitochondrial function (36, 43) and because global SDHC deletion influences body composition (30), we considered that body mass constituents other than the muscle could be altered in KO animals. To address that hypothesis, we conducted a body composition analysis via dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan and found that the muscle (lean), fat, and bone constituents were similar between WT and KO mice (Fig. 4, K and L). Last, to query more subtle changes in muscle integrity changes, we conducted an electron microscopy analysis of embedded sections. This demonstrated preservation of sarcomere ultrastructure and qualitatively unremarkable A and I bands and H zones as well as lack of aberrant ultrastructure processes such as sarcolemma detachment or inclusion bodies (Fig. 4M). Together, these data indicate that SDH dysfunction does not lead to conspicuous muscle mass deterioration.

Muscle SDH dysfunction causes reduced exercise endurance and contractility specifically in oxidative myofibers

To further evaluate the functional repercussions of SDHC KO, we conducted a treadmill endurance exercise test. This showed that SDHC KO mice displayed a significantly shorter time to fatigue compared to WT littermates (Fig. 5A). However, given that skeletal muscle Sdhc gene deletion includes the diaphragm and because ventilatory capacity is critical to maintain endurance exercise, we sought to discern potential ventilatory effects by conducting isolated muscle contractility experiments. We tested two different muscles: EDL, which is glycolytic and substantially less dependent on mitochondrial ATP generation, and soleus, which is oxidative and heavily dependent on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (40, 44). EDL and soleus muscles represent extreme opposites in the spectrum of respiratory profiles (glycolytic versus oxidative) (45) but make up a small contribution to the mass of locomotor skeletal muscle compared to mixed fiber-type muscles (40, 45), yet they are ideal models to appreciate differences driven by fiber oxidative capacity.

Muscle SDH dysfunction causes reduced exercise endurance and contractility specifically in oxidative myofibers.

(A) Endurance of WT and KO 20-week post-induction mice was determined using a treadmill endurance test with the goal of 2200 m at a constant rate (12 m/min), which demonstrated a significantly different endurance tolerance between genotypes; WT maintained running capacity at the experimental endpoint, while all KO mice had fatigued by 1400 m (n = 6). (B) Contractile strength of EDL (glycolytic) muscles from WT and KO mice was determined using the isolated contractility platform, indicating that both specific and absolute contractility were similar between genotypes (n = 5). (C) Similarly, the contractile strength of soleus (oxidative) muscles from WT and KO mice was analyzed on the isolated contractility platform, indicating that KO had a significantly reduced specific contractility; the reduction in the absolute contractility is fully accounted by the effect of the specific contractility reduction (n = 5); *P < 0.05.

While the highly glycolytic EDL muscle from SDHC KOs and controls displayed no significant change in absolute or specific contractility, the oxidative soleus muscle from SDHC KOs demonstrated a significant reduction in isolated contractility compared to controls, driven by an intrinsic (specific) contractile defect (Fig. 5B). These data indicate that the reduced force generation capacity of the soleus muscle of SDHC KO mice in comparison to WT littermates is attributed to specific myofiber-type dysfunction rather than the muscle size (the latter being unchanged in KO mice as previously described; Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we present evidence that SDH activity is not required to maintain adult skeletal muscle mass, yet it plays a critical role in muscle contractility and ultimately exercise endurance. COPD myopathy is characterized by a complex combination of muscle atrophy and reduced strength and endurance. While inferential models indicate that both muscle mass and reduced fiber respiratory capacity are independently associated with mortality in this setting (10), these contributions to muscle dysfunction have never been separately investigated. Moreover, interventions targeting COPD muscle dysfunction have so far failed to improve survival (1, 2, 46). Recently, we have reported that a mouse with the genetic overexpression of IL-13 in Club cells leading to pulmonary emphysema recapitulates most of the features of COPD-associated muscle dysfunction (15–17, 29). We have also found that these animals demonstrate a reduction of SDHC expression and SDH activity despite ample succinate availability and that reconstitution of SDH activity via in vivo SDH overexpression leads to improved cellular respiration and functional outcomes, with reduced succinate accumulation (18). Skeletal muscle SDH down-regulation is a sensitive marker of poor myofiber respiration in patients with COPD (47), and elevated serum succinate has been reported as a marker of short-term mortality in that setting (48, 49). Moreover, respirometry conducted on muscles of patients with COPD demonstrate reduced complex II–dependent mitochondrial respiration, even when fully supplemented with succinate, suggesting SDH dysfunction in this setting (5, 7, 8).

Because COPD is a complex disease with multiple confounders of muscle dysfunction such as hypoxemia and motion reduction, we hypothesized that muscle-specific, conditional SDHC KO would recapitulate those COPD myopathy aspects that are downstream of SDH. As SDH is an integral part of the TCA cycle that also participates in the ETC as complex II (24), we anticipated that substantial muscle deterioration would occur in KO mice. To our surprise, conditional SDHC KO mice demonstrate similar survival to WT controls and a resilient phenotype characterized by preservation of muscle mass and fiber type identities. The only hints of pathology are reduced exercise capacity and reduced intrinsic muscle contractility. Both endurance exercise and contractility depend on ATP availability (50). Given that the ETC is critical to support the mitochondrial intermembrane proton gradient driving ATP generation, we conducted high-resolution respirometry. This technology allowed us to interrogate the activity of ETC complexes in conditions of selective substrate and inhibitor supply giving insight into the interplay of multiple ETC constituents with OCR as a respiration readout (51). We found that, while complex I can support similar OCRs in WT and SDHC KO animals, the subsequent addition of succinate (complex II substrate) leads to an elevation in OCR exclusively in WT animals, confirming that complex II does not contribute to cellular respiration in SDHC KO mice.

The use of complex I inhibitor rotenone, which forces the system to contribute electrons to the ETC exclusively through complex II, demonstrates that SDHC KO mice are unable to consume oxygen through complex II. In contrast, WT littermates support a detectable residual OCR in that context. A comparable muscle respiratory profile was seen in COPD animals, consistent with a causal effect of SDH reduction in these mice abnormal force-generation capacity.

These in vitro results should be considered with caution as mitochondrial function measured with high-resolution respirometry scores complexes I and II contribution to the ETC independently under a full, externally supplied substrate. By contrast, complex II–driven respiration in vivo depends on the substrate provided by upstream steps of the TCA that are, in turn, contributed by products of SDH activity, which means that the functions of complexes I and II are interdependent. Thus, future studies evaluating complexes I and II interaction in vivo will be needed to investigate if compensatory mechanisms between the two complexes occur in intact muscles. These studies could be accomplished either by conducting experiments using a complex I/II double KO animal or by treating SDHC KO mice with potent complex I inhibitors (52).

Moreover, we did not address the alternative routing of carbon substrate handling upon SDHC loss. Previous studies conducted in replicative cells indicate the loss of SDH activity is compensated by pyruvate carboxylase–driven substrate oxidation, which sustains a Warburg-like bioenergetic process to enable cellular survival (53, 54). Future research supplying labeled carbon substrates will clarify how muscles without SDH accommodate the excess succinate and the derangement of the TCA cycle. The observation that upstream intermediate α-ketoglutarate is not accumulated in SDHC KO mice might suggest cataplerotic reactions involving glutamate and glutamine generation (55), which will require future studies as well. Also, the fact that our SDHC animals do not show lactate accumulation at baseline suggests that they do not engage in a Warburg-like respiration.

Mechanistic research involving SDH loss is generally not conducted with post-mitotic cells such as skeletal muscle. In cancer cellular lines, SDHC KO has been shown to establish a repressive hypermethylated DNA landscape that contributes to the maintenance of the transformed phenotype (32). At early time points during the animal SDHC KO process we conducted RNA sequencing analysis and found a remarkably suppressed transcriptional landscape. An unbiased proteomics analysis demonstrated a robust down-regulation of SDH subunits but a more nuanced landscape in reference to the contemporary transcriptome, suggesting that the adult muscle proteome is relatively buffered from changes in gene transcription. It is reasonable to hypothesize that succinate accumulation inhibits chromatin-modifying KDMs (56) and/or TET family proteins controlling DNA methylation (57). It is also possible that prolyl 4-hydroxylase domain proteins that catalyze HIF-1α hydroxylation (58–60) are also involved in this deranged regulation. The observed early transcriptomic changes might facilitate the expression of enzymes to selectively rewire metabolism via alternative pathways, as shown in multiple models of mitochondrial dysfunction (61–63).

Succinate has been found to regulate muscle oxygen consumption and mitochondrial biogenesis in the skeletal muscle by interaction with succinate receptor SUCNR1 (26). We found a different phenotype associated with SDHC-induced succinate accumulation, specifically lacking changes in fiber type composition and other surrogates of mitochondrial biogenesis. These differences might be attributed to the source of succinate, which is endogenous in our model and exogenous in a prior report (26).

A concept of potential relevance to muscle adaptation after SDH KO involves reactive oxygen species (ROS)–induced cellular signaling. In inflammatory cells, succinate oxidation via SDH has been shown to induce complex I–driven ROS generation via reverse electron transport, causing metabolic reprogramming toward Warburg respiration (64). Previous evidence indicates that dysfunction of SDH subunits located at the ubiquinol binding site (as in subunit C) leads to increased ROS formation, unlike dysfunction in SDHA (65). Complex II–dependent ROS increase has been demonstrated in muscles of patients with COPD (6). In an analysis of our recently established COPD animal model muscle proteome, we have reported the up-regulation of antioxidant ferroproteins, likely indicating ongoing activation of oxidative stress signaling (15, 16). Future studies focused on the potential ROS generation role of SDH dysfunction are indicated.

Limitations: While our study shows that SDH/complex II activity is not necessary to maintain muscle mass, it does not substantiate a mechanism accounting for the bioenergetic resilience of these transgenic animal muscles. Future in vivo studies involving conditional complex I/II double KO animals could define if complex I participates in that homeostatic response or if other more complex systems are required to support the muscle integrity (63).

In summary, we present data indicating that skeletal muscle–specific SDH KO leads to reduced complex II–mediated respiration without compromising complex I activity. Consistent with data from our recently generated animal model of genetically induced pulmonary emphysema/COPD-driven muscle dysfunction, loss of SDH function causes reduced muscle contractility and exercise endurance. In contrast with COPD, however, SDH loss is associated with preservation of muscle mass. Our research suggests that muscle mass and myofiber respiration are regulated by independent biological mechanisms in COPD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

COPD model

Experiments were conducted using CC10-rtTA-IL-13 (COPD) doxycycline-inducible transgenic mice that develop chronic lung remodeling reminiscent of pulmonary emphysema upon induction (28). CC10-rtTA-IL-13 heterozygote animals were bred to C57BL/6 WT mice to obtain hemizygous COPD experimental animals and homozygous IL13WT (WT) littermate controls. Both IL13TG (COPD) and IL13WT (non-emphysema; used as WT control littermates) mice were provided doxycycline (0.5 g/liter) in the drinking water along with sucrose (0.5 mg/ml), starting at 5 weeks of age for a total of 17 weeks (~22 weeks of age).

SDHC KO

Animals with a tamoxifen-inducible skeletal muscle–specific KO of SDHC were generated using B6.Cg-Tg(ACTA1-cre)79Jme/J (HSA-Cre) from the Jackson Laboratory and Sdhcfl/fl;R26M2rtTA/M2rtTA (Lox-SDHC) as previously described (31). These animals were bred to obtain HSA+/−SDHCf/f experimental KO animals and HSA−/−SDHCf/f WT control littermates. At 12 weeks of age, mice were provided tamoxifen diet (500 mg/kg, Inotiv) for 2 weeks. On week three, tamoxifen diet was continued and, in addition, mice were intraperitoneally injected with tamoxifen (2 mg/mouse per day) in sunflower oil for five consecutive days. The double route of tamoxifen administration has been shown to provide higher levels of Cre-induced effect (66). After injections, mice were removed from all tamoxifen and considered fully induced.

Male and female mice were used for the studies. Results are reported as aggregate data from both sexes, except when indicated otherwise. Food and water were accessible ad libitum, and a 12-hour/12-hour light/dark cycle was maintained by our animal resource facility. Samples were collected directly after euthanasia by cervical dislocation. The time elapsed between animals’ euthanasia and sample procurement never exceeded 3 min when methodologically feasible. All the procedures involving animals were approved by the Albany Medical College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUP 22-12001), and animals were handled according to the National Institutes of Health guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, as stated by the journal and public agencies.

Succinate quantification

Fully induced muscle was freshly isolated, and the amount of succinate was determined using a succinate colorimetric assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK184) following the manufacturer’s protocol using the mouse TA muscle lysate. Absorbance measurements were collected using a Cytation 5 plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT).

Lung histology

Animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation; a median sternotomy was performed immediately to collect lungs. Lungs were fixed overnight in 10% formalin without inflation, embedded in paraffin, cut at 5-μm-thick sections, and stained with H&E. Sections were imaged using a Cytation 5 imager (BioTek, Winooski, VT).

Muscle histology

Freshly isolated muscles were placed on saline-moistened gauze in a 60-mm culture dish on ice until freezing. A metal cup containing isopentane was cooled in liquid nitrogen until crystals formed of isopentane at the bottom of the cup. Muscles were transferred to precooled Tissue-Tek embedding cassettes (EMS, Hatfield, PA, 62520), which were dropped into the cooled isopentane, submerging the muscle for 1 min. Muscle samples were then drained and dried on gauze pads at −20°C to remove all isopentane. Frozen muscles were adhered to the sample stage using a small amount of Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature compound (EMS, Hatfield, PA, 62550) and 10-μm sections were taken for analysis using a Leica CM1860 Cryostat (Wetzlar, Germany).

Muscle myofiber cross-sectional area

Muscle myofiber delineations at ×10 magnification on sections stained for laminin were analyzed with the CellProfiler software (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA) to measure the myofiber cross-sectional area in an unbiased manner. Output muscle traces were reviewed to assure the accuracy of measurement of the software pipeline. A fiber size distribution histogram was produced by binning individual myofiber-determined sizes into 100-μm intervals of size and recording the frequency of myofibers in each bin. Myofiber-specific cross-sectional area is determined by analyzing only myofibers that specifically stain for the different MHC markers.

ATPase staining

Muscles were collected, preserved histologically, and sectioned as mentioned above. Slides were delivered to the Albany Medical Center Pathology laboratory for ATPase staining following standard protocols. Sections were analyzed at pH 9.4 and imaged using a Cytation 5 imager (BioTek, Winooski, VT).

Western blotting

Muscles were lysed by bead beating three times for 30 s, with a 30-s rest on ice between each interval. Samples were centrifuged at 22,000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Samples were prepared for Western blotting using a Bio-Rad 4X Laemmli buffer, and protein amounts were determined and normalized by bovine serum albumin assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pierce Protein Biology Products, Waltham, MA). Protein gels were run using either a 15-well 4 to 20% gradient polyacrylamide gel or a 15-well 10% polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) for 45 to 55 min at 150 V. Protein was transferred to a 0.45-μm nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) using either a wet transfer for 40 to 50 min at 100 V or a semidry transfer for 20 to 30 min at 10 to 15 V. Blots were blocked with 5% dry milk in 1X tris-buffered saline with 0.1% tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Primary antibodies were incubated at a 1:500 or a 1:1000 in 1% milk in 1X TBST with an appropriate antibody overnight at 4°C. Blots were washed three times for 10 min in 1X TBST at RT, then the secondary antibody was incubated at a 1:2000 to 1:10,000 ratio in 1X clear milk blocking buffer (CMBB) in 1X TBST (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 1 to 2 hours. Blots were washed three times for 15 min in 1X TBST at RT. Chemiluminescent pictures were taken using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, no. 734BR3626) by activating the horseradish peroxidase (HRP) with enhanced chemiluminesence reagents (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Blots were washed with 1X TBST for 15 min at RT and incubated with anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH)–HRP in 1X CMBB overnight using a ratio of 1:5000, then imaging was repeated. Densitometry was analyzed using the Bio-Rad Image Lab software.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative RT-PCR

RNA from the TA muscle was extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (Machery-Nagel, Düren, Germany). The reason TA was used is due to the higher yield of RNA per sample relative to other muscles. cDNA was synthesized using the Quantitect Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Qiagen). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a CFX96 Real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad). Each sample was run in triplicate, and relative expression levels of transcripts of interest were calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method with GAPDH as a housekeeping gene. Primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IA), and a list of their sequences is presented in the Supplementary Materials.

RNA sequencing

RNA from the TA muscle was isolated using TRIzol with an added step of DNA digestion. RNA quantity was determined using a Qubit Flex Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA). Library preparation was performed using an Ion Chef System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) followed by sequencing using an Ion GeneStudio S5 Plus System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) both following the manufacturer’s suggested protocols for the Ion AmpliSeq Transcriptome Mouse Gene Expression Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA). Differential expressions were determined using the Transcriptome Analysis Console, and pathway enrichments were performed using Metascape.

Food intake

Food intake of WT and KO animals was compared by recording the difference in the mass of food given to single animals every day for 3 days.

Motion detection

Motion detection cages (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy, 47420) were used for quantitative movement monitoring of animals for 24 hours following standard protocols, as previously described (67).

Muscle fiber typing immunofluorescence

Muscle sections were fixed for 15 min in acetone at −20°C and then left at RT to dry for 30 min. Blocking was performed using the Mouse-on-Mouse blocking reagent (Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA) for 1 hour at RT. Sections were then incubated for 45 min at 37°C or overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies indicated in the table at the end of the figures. Three washes were then performed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The following secondary antibodies from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories were added all at 1:250 and incubated for 45 min at 37°C: anti-mouse immunoglobulin G2b–DyLight 405, anti-mouse immunoglobulin G1–Alexa Fluor 488, anti-mouse immunoglobulin M–Alexa Fluor 594, and anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G–Alexa Fluor 647. Three washes were then performed with PBS. Samples were mounted with the ibidi mounting medium (Martinsried, Germany). Images were captured the same day using confocal microscopy (Leica SPE).

Body mass

Mouse body mass was determined using a top loading balance (Mettler, Toledo, Ohio).

Isolated muscle mass determination

Immediately after animal euthanasia, the leg skin was dissected with tweezers and muscles exposed. The distal tendon was cut using dissecting scissors (Roboz RS-5840), the muscle lifted, then the proximal tendon was severed, and the muscle was gently removed. After the proximal tendon was severed, the muscle was placed on gauze under magnification to eliminate the remaining blood, fat, and, if necessary, the remaining tendon using scissors. Wet muscle weight was then determined using an analytical balance (Sartorius, Entris, Germany). Mass determinations were done on TA, EDL, and soleus muscles.

Body composition analysis

Fully induced animals were euthanized with CO2 overdose and frozen. Frozen animals were sent to the Center for Skeletal Analysis at Massachusetts General Hospital. Briefly, a PIXImus dual-energy x-ray absorptiometer was used to measure body composition and bone mineral content.

Electron microscopy

EDL muscle samples were placed in M. J. Karnovsky fixative immediately after procurement. Samples were dehydrated, embedded, cut into 60-nm-thick sections, mounted on corresponding grids, and imaged by the University of Pittsburgh EM core facility.

Treadmill fatigue test

Fully induced animals were allowed a week to train and acclimate on the treadmill (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy) following standard protocols. After training, the mice were allowed 48 hours to rest and recover. On test day, mice were placed on the treadmill and the treadmill was run at a 10° incline and a 12 m/min speed until the mouse failed by spending more than 5 s behind the fatigue line as previously described (68). Failure time was recorded.

Isolated muscle contractility

Absolute force determination

EDL and soleus muscles were surgically isolated from the mouse by carefully tying a suture around the tendons at each end and cutting the tendons to release the muscle. Special care was taken not to stretch or damage the muscle integrity, as previously established. Once removed, the analyzed muscle was equilibrated for 15 min in ice-cold Ringer’s solution supplemented with 5.5 mM glucose, adjusted to a pH between 7.4 and 8.0, and slowly bubbled with carbogen. The muscle was then suspended between the isometric force transducer (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA) and the platinum-stimulating electrode tissue support (Radnoti, Covina, CA, 160152), lowered into the 25-ml tissue bath (Radnoti, Covina, CA, 166026) containing the same solution, also bubbled slowly with carbogen, but this time at RT, and allowed to equilibrate for an additional 15 min. Muscle tension was escalated until baseline tension started to increase. A single 1-Hz, 40-V stimulus was delivered with a Grass S88 electrical stimulator, and the peak contraction was recorded. After a 30-s rest, the voltage was increased by 10 V and delivered again, recording the peak contraction. This process was repeated until no additional increase in the peak contraction force was observed. The optimal length of the muscle was then determined by slowly increasing the muscle tension and delivering a single, maximal stimulus, as previously determined above, while recording the peak force. After a 30-s rest, the tension was slightly increased, and another stimulus was delivered while recording the peak contraction. This process was repeated until maximal peak contraction force was achieved. Subsequent stimuli were delivered at 1, 10, 20, 30, 50, 80, 100, and 120 Hz while recording the peak force at each point and allowed for 1-min rest between each stimulus.

Specific force determination

After the final stimulus was recorded, the muscle mass was determined. With that information, the following equation was used to obtain the specific force at each point: specific force (N/cm2) = peak contraction (kg) × 9.8 (m/s2) × optimal length (cm) × 1.056 (g/cm3)/muscle mass (g). From the single twitch contraction (1 Hz), dT/dtmax (contraction time) and −dT/dtmax (relaxation time) were determined. The slope (in units of g/s) was calculated from raw contraction data at each point from the baseline to the maximum value (that is dT/dtmax) and then the most negative one (that is −dT/dtmax).

High-resolution respirometry analysis

High-resolution respirometry analysis was conducted with an Oroboros instrument following the SUIT-011 pfi D024 analysis protocol. TA muscles from fully induced animals were freshly isolated and a 1-mm punch biopsy was used to collect a small sample (2 to 4 mg in size). Biopsies were permeabilized with saponin following Oroboros SOP MiPNet14.14 permeabilized fiber preparation. Analysis medium used (MiR05), all required substrates, uncouplers, and inhibitors were ordered from the indicated suppliers and prepared according to instructions in Oroboros SOP MiPNet03.02 chemicals and media. Oxidative phosphorylation respiration analysis was conducted using the protocol SUIT-011 pfi D024 following the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed using a protocol-specific Oroboros analysis template.

LC-MS/MS proteomics

LC-MS/MS proteomics were performed by the National Center for Quantitative Biology of Complex Systems at the University of Wisconsin, Madison, WI. All experiments were performed using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano LC instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) coupled to a field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS)-enabled Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA). Reversed-phase columns were made in-house by packing a fused silica capillary [75-m inside diameter and 360-m outer diameter, with an integrated electrospray emitter (New Objective, Woburn, MA)] with a 1.7-m diameter, 130-Å pore size bridged ethylene hybrid C18 particles (Waters, Milford, MA) to a final length of 30 cm. The column was heated to 50°C for all experiments. Samples were loaded onto the column in 99:1 buffer A(water and 0.2% formic acid):buffer B (acetonitrile and 0.2% formic acid) at a flow rate of 0.30 liters/min. Peptides were eluted using the following gradient: an increase to 8% B over 10 min, followed by a 100-min linear gradient from 8 to 55% B, and followed by a 5-min linear gradient to 100% B, which was held for 4 min. The column was equilibrated with 1% buffer B for an additional 10 min. For each experiment, 2 g of peptides was loaded onto the column. Precursor peptide cations were generated from the eluent through the utilization of a nanoelectrospray ionization source. MS instrument methods for sample analysis consisted of MS1 survey scans (1 × 106 target value, 120,000 resolution, and 300 to 1500 Th) that were used to guide subsequent data-dependent MS/MS scans of the most intense precursors for 1 s. The MS/MS analyses were performed in the ion trap (0.7-Th isolation window, high collision dissociation fragmentation, normalized collision energy of 30, 3 × 104 target value, and ion trap turbo scan). Dynamic exclusion duration was set to 20 s with an exclusion width of 10 parts per million of the selected average mass. Charge states that were unknown, 1 or 5, were excluded from the analysis. Maximum injection times were set to 50 ms for all MS scans and 14 ms for MS/MS scans. FAIMS compensation voltages were alternated between 50 and 70 V.

LC-MS/MS metabolomics

All samples were assayed by LC-MS/MS on a Sciex 5500 with an ExionLC AC front end. LC-MS/MS methodologies were adapted from previous methods for both the analysis of TCA cycle intermediates (69) and the analysis of adenosine phosphates (70). Additional details can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistics

Data are expressed as the means ± SEM. When comparisons were performed between two groups, significance was evaluated by Student’s t test, and when more than two groups were compared, analysis of variance was used followed by the Tukey test using the GraphPad Prism software. Results were considered significant when P < 0.05.
