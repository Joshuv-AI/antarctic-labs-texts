UNICORN TAVR in Native Complex Congenital Aortic Stenosis at High Risk of Coronary Obstruction
Authors: Harish Sharma, William J. Chadwick, Russell Franks, James D. Newton, Elizabeth Orchard, Sam Dawkins, Thomas J. Cahill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery obstruction is a rare but serious complication of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Undermining iatrogenic coronary obstruction with a radiofrequency needle (UNICORN) is a novel technique of intraleaflet TAVR valve deployment for mitigating this risk. There is limited experience in native aortic valve anatomy, particularly in congenital aortic stenosis.Case SummaryA 26-year-old man in cardiogenic shock secondary to severe congenital aortic stenosis was successfully treated with emergency TAVR, with leaflet modification using the UNICORN technique due to the high risk of coronary obstruction.DiscussionUNICORN is a novel technique for preventing coronary obstruction in TAVR. Here we describe the first case of UNICORN in congenital aortic stenosis in cardiogenic shock at prohibitive surgical risk.Take-Home MessagesUNICORN can be used successfully in carefully selected native valves at risk of coronary obstruction during TAVR. Use of leaflet modification techniques such as UNICORN enables intervention in a broader cohort of patients unfit for surgery.

History of Presentation

The patient was a 26-year-old man with a history of congenital aortic and subaortic stenosis. He underwent surgical resection of a subaortic membrane and partial septal myectomy at age 10 years but had recurrence of aortic stenosis in adulthood, which was under surveillance. His other relevant background included global developmental delay, episodes of ventricular tachycardia (VT), and a previous ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest, after which he underwent implantable cardioverter-defibrillator insertion. On admission he presented with a VT storm complicated by cardiogenic shock. He had multiple appropriate therapies from the implantable cardioverter-defibrillator and was treated with intubation, intravenous inotropes, and lidocaine infusion.Take-Home Messages•UNICORN can be used successfully in abnormal native valves at risk of coronary obstruction during transcatheter aortic valve replacement.•Use of leaflet-modification techniques such as UNICORN enables intervention in a broader cohort of patients who are not surgical candidates.

Investigations

Blood tests on admission showed an N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide level of 25,399 ng/L. Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated severe left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 20% and severe aortic stenosis of a trileaflet valve with a Vmax of 4.2 m/s and a mean and peak aortic gradient of 45 and 70 mm Hg, respectively (Figure 1). There was also severe aortic regurgitation. A computed tomography (CT) aortogram demonstrated challenging features for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), including an extremely small annulus (area 296 mm2; perimeter 64.3 mm) with small and effaced sinuses measuring 19 to 22 mm. The risk of coronary occlusion was high due to long coronary leaflets (14.6 mm on the left) relative to a low left main coronary artery ostium (10.3 mm; 11.8 mm on the right). The virtual valve-to-coronary distance was 3.5 mm on the left, in keeping with high risk for occlusion. Management

The case was discussed in an urgent Heart Team meeting. The patient was in cardiogenic shock, and his surgical risk was prohibitive. Balloon aortic valvuloplasty was not suitable because of severe aortic regurgitation. TAVR with a coronary protection strategy was considered to be the only potential treatment strategy. Given his young age, chimney stenting was considered to be an unsatisfactory approach. We also considered bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA), but the patient's hemodynamic instability and shock favored a rapid procedural strategy. We therefore elected to undertake the undermining iatrogenic coronary obstruction with a radiofrequency needle (UNICORN) technique for TAVR.

The procedure was undertaken under general anesthetic with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. The patient's condition was stabilized on intravenous inotropes at the time of the procedure. In the event of hemodynamic compromise (with a percutaneous salvage option, as there was no option for cardiac surgical bailout), Impella (Abiomed) support (after transcatheter valve implantation) and/or peripheral extracorporeal membrane oxygenation were potential options, depending on the nature and anticipated duration of the instability. After the introduction of a 14-F E-Sheath (Edwards Lifesciences) via the right femoral artery, a 6-F Amplatzer Left 1 guide catheter was advanced into the left coronary sinus. Within the guide, we positioned a 6-F guide extension and a Corsair microcatheter (Asahi Intecc). The Corsair was selected in this case because of its greater stiffness and higher torque transmission in the event of difficulty crossing the fibrotic native leaflets.

Inside the microcatheter, we advanced an Astato XS 20 wire (Asahi Intecc) to make contact with the base of the left coronary leaflet (Figure 2). The position and trajectory were confirmed in side-on and en face fluoroscopic views. The coating from the back end of the Astato wire was denuded, and the wire was attached to a Bovie electrosurgery knife with a grounding pad attached to the patient to complete the circuit. Electrocautery traversal of the left coronary leaflet was performed on the first attempt at 70 W, allowing the Astato wire to advance into the LV outflow tract. Wire position was confirmed on TEE, and the microcatheter was then advanced over the Astato wire. The Astato wire was then exchanged for a 300-cm Sion blue extra support wire (Asahi Intecc). Over this wire, 3-mm and 6-mm coronary balloons were used to dilate the passageway created within the left coronary leaflet (Figure 3). Progressive dilatation of the leaflet was confirmed on TEE with severe aortic regurgitation noted through the leaflet. The patient was hemodynamically stable throughout. The additional 6-mm balloon dilatation allowed the Amplatzer Left 1 guide catheter to be directly advanced through the leaflet over the Sion blue wire. A double-length J wire was then advanced through the guide catheter into the LV apex. Over this wire, a 12-mm Oceanus balloon (iVascular) was inflated to 6 atm. Given the root anatomy and very high risk of coronary obstruction, we elected to protect the left main stem in case the UNICORN procedure was not sufficient. A JL 3.5 guide catheter was placed in the left main coronary artery, with a wire in the distal left anterior descending artery and a guide extension and stent in place to facilitate bailout stenting if required. A 20-mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra Resilia valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was selected and positioned using fluoroscopy and TEE guidance. The valve was deployed under rapid pacing. Coronary angiography and aortography demonstrated satisfactory valve positioning with no evidence of coronary obstruction (Figure 4), and so the coronary stent was removed undeployed. Discussion

Coronary artery obstruction is a rare but serious potential complication of TAVR, which is more likely in valve-in-valve procedures.1 In native valves, adverse anatomical features such as low coronary ostia, long leaflets, and shallow sinuses of Valsalva predispose to coronary obstruction.1,2 CT planning plays a crucial role in patient selection by identifying such anatomical factors in advance.1, 2, 3 For those patients at high risk of coronary obstruction who are deemed unfit for surgery, dedicated techniques have been developed to enable transcatheter valve intervention while minimizing the risk of coronary obstruction,4,5 including leaflet modification techniques, of which BASILICA is the best-studied.4

BASILICA has been shown to enable successful TAVR without coronary obstruction in high-risk patients who would not otherwise have been deemed eligible for TAVR,6 but is procedurally complex.4 UNICORN is a novel alternative leaflet laceration technique, first described for valve-in-valve procedures.7 In UNICORN, the leaflet is traversed in a similar way to BASILICA via monopolar electrosurgery, but the valve is deployed inside the (predilated) cusp fenestration, lacerating the cusp as it expands. Without the need for the creation of a lasso within the LVOT for electrosurgical leaflet laceration before valve deployment, UNICORN is a simpler procedure.

This patient's highly abnormal anatomy (Figure 1) made him especially high-risk for left coronary artery obstruction. In particular, the combination of low coronary heights and effaced sinuses of Valsalva is strongly associated with coronary obstruction,1 while long leaflets also increase the risk of obstruction due to sinus sequestration, which is rare in native valve procedures.2,4 In this case, surgical management (in the form of a Ross-Konno procedure or heart transplant) was considered but rejected due to prohibitive surgical risk.

Congenital aortic valve disease poses distinct challenges for leaflet modification. Congenital aortic stenosis most commonly occurs in bicuspid valves and may be associated with aortopathy. Bicuspid aortic valve disease is characterized by asymmetric and heavy calcification, calcified raphe, abnormal coronary take-off, large and noncircular annuli, and horizontal aortic angles. These issues can create significant challenges with electrosurgical leaflet modification, including positioning on the leaflet, traversal, and the efficacy of the subsequent leaflet laceration. In contrast, this patient had trileaflet aortic valve anatomy, and although there were thickened and unusually long and dysplastic leaflets, the annulus was small and the leaflets were noncalcified.

A key limitation of the UNICORN procedure is that the direction of leaflet splitting cannot be controlled. When performed in patients with a prior surgical or transcatheter valve replacement, the existing valve frame acts as a “backstop,” protecting the native annulus against injury as the new transcatheter heart valve (THV) expands. In native aortic stenosis, no such “backstop” exists, and annular injury caused by the expanding THV is an important concern. In this patient, where leaflet calcification was minimal, we judged that splitting would occur toward the leaflet tip, where the tissue is thinnest, rather than toward the annulus. In contrast, in heavily calcified leaflets, tip calcification may prevent distal splitting and redirect force toward the annulus, and UNICORN would therefore be inadvisable in this setting. We also hypothesize that gentle, progressive balloon dilatation using sequentially larger balloons within the lacerated leaflet may favor splitting toward the leaflet tip rather than the annulus. By applying radial force incrementally, smaller balloons preferentially stress the distal, thinner portion of the leaflet, potentially initiating and propagating a split toward the tip. In contrast, rapid inflation of a large balloon applies force at a higher strain rate—a property of viscoelastic tissues such as the aortic valve leaflet—potentially directing the split along planes determined by instantaneous stress distribution rather than underlying tissue architecture.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report of the UNICORN technique being used in a congenital aortic stenosis procedure. It exemplifies the value of dedicated leaflet modification techniques in enabling safe TAVR in patients with unacceptably high surgical risk who are also predisposed to coronary obstruction.

Conclusions

CT planning can identify individual anatomical factors that predispose to coronary obstruction, a rare but serious complication of TAVR. Modified TAVR techniques such as UNICORN can enable successful transcatheter valve intervention even in very high-risk patients who are unfit for surgical management, including in carefully selected native valves. Potential pitfalls of UNICORN for native valve aortic stenosis include unpredictable leaflet splitting, annular injury (due to a lack of surgical or TAVR frame as a backstop), failure to cross the leaflet when implanting the THV, failure of coronary protection, and iatrogenic aortic regurgitation leading to hemodynamic instability before THV deployment. Meticulous CT evaluation, avoidance of leaflet calcium, and serial incrementing balloon dilatation were important aspects in this case contributing to a successful outcome.
