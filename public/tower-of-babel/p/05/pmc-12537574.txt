Injectable reactive oxygen and nitrogen species-scavenging hydrogels for repairing traumatic brain injury
Authors: Luzhong Zhang, Wenhui Li, Jie Cao, Yumin Yang, Huasong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical therapies for traumatic brain injury included pharmacological interventions and surgical procedures; however, the efficacy of these approaches remains unsatisfactory. Traumatic brain injury was commonly accompanied by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitrogen species (RNS). The overexpressed ROS/RNS generally led to secondary brain injury. To address these issues, we synthesized the injectable poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA)/cystamine/agmatine (PCA) hydrogels through Michael addition for repairing traumatic brain injury. Injectable PCA hydrogels could not only fill the brain trauma cavities, but also alleviate the secondary damage to the brain by reducing ROS and RNS. The disulfide bonds of cystamine and agmatine in the hydrogels could separately scavenge ROS and inhibit the activity of nitric oxide synthase. Consequently, injectable hydrogels were locally administrated to the injured brain. Therapeutically, PCA hydrogels significantly enhanced the motor functional recovery, improved the learning ability and memory retention, preserved the survival of neurons, and reduced the activation of glial cells and pro-inflammatory macrophages. The injectable hydrogels offered a potential therapeutic strategy for traumatic brain injury and other neurological diseases.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, Reactive oxygen species, Injectable hydrogels, Reactive nitrogen species

Introduction

Annually, approximately two million new cases of traumatic brain injury are documented worldwide [1]. After traumatic brain injury, the physiological and pathological changes not only involve the damage induced by initial external forces, but also encompass secondary injuries, including neurological, metabolic, cellular, and other systemic reactions [2]. Traumatic brain injury could also induce chronic neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, accompanied by exacerbated neurological dysfunction [3]. Currently, hypertonic solutions and surgical resection of the diseased lesion are employed in traumatic brain injury [4]. Nevertheless, these therapeutic strategies have been demonstrated to be insufficient in treatments of traumatic brain injury [5]. Therefore, there is an imperative necessity to address these challenges during the acute phase of post-traumatic brain injury.

Following a traumatic brain injury, neuroinflammatory responses facilitate the recruitment of macrophages and activate glial cells in the injured sites, leading to an increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) [6,7]. The excessive accumulation of RNS and ROS within the injured brain creates a toxic microenvironment [8]. ROS referred to the collective term for oxygen-containing free radicals and peroxides. These ROS are generated during mitochondrial metabolism through respiration, as well as on the membranes of neutrophils and phagosomes via nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate in organisms [9]. Under physiological conditions, ROS production and elimination reached an equilibrium. When the equilibrium was perturbed, the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) could escalate significantly beyond the body's capacity for clearance, resulting in oxidative stress, cellular damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction [10]. Secondary brain injury is predominantly attributed to oxidative stress, which is intricately linked to the elevated levels of ROS and RNS within the cerebral environment [11].

Aside from ROS, RNS including nitrogen oxide and nitrogen dioxide play significant roles in inflammatory and physiological processes [12]. Furthermore, excessive RNS demonstrate greater cytotoxic effects than ROS through the peroxidation and nitration of biomolecules [13]. RNS are synthesized from L-arginine mediated by nitric oxide synthase [14]. In the initial phases of traumatic brain injury, activated nitric oxide synthase secreted the excessive amount of reactive nitrogen species (RNS) at the injured site [15]. The prolonged elevation of RNS promote calcium ion influx via the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor-mediated pathway, thereby inducing to neurotoxicity [16]. Agmatine has been reported to inhibit the activity of nitric oxide synthase and exhibit irreversible inhibition against neuronal nitric oxide synthase [17,18]. Recently, many studies have reported the use of nanozymes and inorganic nanomaterials encapsulated in hydrogels for ROS/RNS scavenging agents [19,20]. However, the enzymes are fragile and susceptible to proteolytic degradation, posing significant challenges for clinical application [21]. Furthermore, Direct scavenging biomaterials include polyphenols, curcumin, polydopamine, tannic acid, vitamin E, vitamin C and sulfur-based oxidative biomaterials [22]. Therefore, there is a need to develop an effective strategy to scavenge excessive ROS/RNS.

Injectable hydrogels represent a promising approach for the treatment of traumatic brain injury [23]. Topically administered hydrogels could effectively mimic the extracellular matrix and provide a three-dimensional physical microenvironment to facilitate cellular infiltration and tissue regeneration [24]. Moreover, injectable hydrogels served as efficient carriers that enhance the bioavailability of active agents through localized administration [25]. Consequently, active agents could be conjugated or encapsulated within these injectable hydrogels to facilitate functional recovery following traumatic brain injury.

In this study, poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA)/cystamine/agmatine (PCA) hydrogels were synthesized through Michael addition between acrylate bond and amino groups for repairing traumatic brain injury (Fig. 1A and B). Cystamine served as the cross-linkers in PCA hydrogels, PEGDA and arginine formed the main chain. The hydrophilic and low-immunogenic properties of PEGDA make it highly appealing in biomedical applications. The soft and hydrated characteristics enable PEGDA to mimic the behavior of living tissues effectively [26]. PCA hydrogels could be easily manipulated by heat, and other cross-linkers to achieve the desired functionalities. Furthermore, disulfide bonds of cystamine in the hydrogels are capable of scavenging excessive ROS at the brain injured sites [27]. Moreover, the degraded agmatine from the hydrogels could restrain the activity of nitric oxide synthase to decrease RNS generation at the brain injured sites. In a traumatic brain injury model, the injectable hydrogels were administrated topically to the injured sites by modulating the ROS and RNS. The motor functional recovery, learning ability and memory retention of injured rats treated with PCA were assessed through rod rotation tests, water maze experiments and modified neurological severity score (mNSS) analysis. Pathological analysis of PCA hydrogels on rats were systematically evaluated by immunofluorescence and hematoxylin & eosin staining.

Materials and methods

Materials

PEGDA (Mw 600) and (2-carboxyethyl) phosphorus hydrochloride (TCEP) were purchased from Aladdin. Cystamine, and agmatine were supplied by Shanghai Adamas. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), hematoxylin & eosin, Griess detection kit, dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and DAPI were supported by Beyotime Biotechnology. Anti-GFAP antibody (ab4674), anti-Iba1 antibody (ab289370), anti-CD86 antibody (ab238468), Goat anti-Chicken IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor@ 488, ab150173), Goat anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488, ab150113), Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Cy3 ®, ab6939) were purchase from Abcam. Anti-MAP2 antibody (ab5622) was supplied by Sigma. Fetal bovine Serum (FBS) and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) were supported by Gibco. MTT Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (C0009M) Total Nitric Oxide Assay Kit (S0024) were purchased from Nantong Beyotime.

Synthesis of PCA hydrogels

PEGDA/cystamine/agmatine (PCA) hydrogels were synthesized via Michael addition [28]. Briefly, PEGDA (390 mg, 0.65 mmol), cystamine (0.13 mmol), and agmatine (0.13 mmol) were completely dissolved in 1 mL of deionized water. The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 24 h to obtain PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogel. Subsequently, the PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogels were dialyzed against the distilled water for 24 h to remove the unreacted small molecules. Similarly, the PCA-medium and PCA-high hydrogels were synthesized following the abovementioned steps, and coded as mention in Table S1.

Characterization

1H NMR spectra were conducted on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400M spectrometer, and deuterium oxide was used as the solvent. FT-IR spectra were recorded on a Bruker TENSOR 27 spectrometer in the spectral range of 400–4500 cm−1 with transmission mode. Microscopic images of PCA hydrogels were observed by a Hitachi S-3400N II SEM. PCA hydrogels were freeze-dried, and sputtered with platinum. The rheological performances of PCA hydrogels were investigated on a HAAKE MARS rheometer with 35 mm diameter parallel plates. The gelation time of PCA hydrogels was determined by a HAAKE MARS rotating rheometer at 80 °C. The intersection points of the energy storage modulus (G') and the loss modulus (G") were regarded as the gelation point. 1 mL of hydrogel precursor was added to the rotating plate (P35/Ti, diameter 35 mm), and analyzed at the frequency of 1 Hz and the gap of 1.0 mm. Each experiment was carried out for at least three parallel samples.

Mechanical properties analysis

The compressive properties of PCA hydrogels were assessed using an electronic universal testing machine at room temperature. Cuboid hydrogel specimens with a length of 8 mm, a width of 8 mm and a height of 4 mm were fabricated using a mold. The compressive rate was maintained at 5 mm/min, the resulting compressive stress-strain curves were recorded during compression.

RNS detection

Mouse macrophage RAW.264.7 cells were planted into a 24-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 per well. The planted cells were co-incubated with 100 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and 10 mg of PCA hydrogels for 24 h (n = 4). After incubation, the medium was transferred into a 96-well plate, and the RNS concentration were measured by a Griess detection kit. The absorbance of samples was determined by a spectrophotometric microplate reader (Bio-Rad) at 540 nm.

ROS detection

Mouse macrophage RAW.264.7 cells were also seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 per well. The seeded cells were co-incubated with 100 μg/mL LPS and 10 mg of PCA hydrogels for 24 h (n = 4). After incubation, the culture medium was replenished with fresh medium containing 10 μmol/L of 2',7'-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), and further incubated for 20 min. After being washed with distilled water, macrophage cells were observed by a laser confocal microscope with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm.

Establish of traumatic brain injury model

All animals' procedures were performed according to the standard guidelines approved by the Nantong University Ethics Committee (Ethical NO. S20221222-003). The traumatic brain injury model was established by a free-falling weight impactor instrument. Adult male SD rats (weight 200–250 g) were randomly divided into PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.27/0.27 hydrogels and the control groups without treatment groups (n = 5). After being anesthetized with 2.5 % of 2, 2, 2-trifluoroethanol, the rat's brain skin was cut to expose the skull. A bone window with a diameter of 5 mm was drilled in the left parietal area 3 mm away from the bragma and 2 mm from the sagital sutures utilizing a hand-held drill. Moderate traumatic brain was established in SD rats using a 60 g free-falling impactor from a height of 20 cm. Post-injury, the PCA hydrogels (200 μL) were slowly injected into the cortical defects of SD rats. In the control group, 100 μL of PBS was administered into the trauma cavity.

Assessment of animal behaviors

On the 1st, 3rd, 7th, 14th, 21st, and 28th days post-surgery, the rats were assessed through the modified neurological severity score (mNSS) using a double-blind analysis. The mNSS scores were assessed on a scale ranging from 0 (normal state) to 18 (most severe state) in accordance with motor, sensory, balance, and reflex functions.

The Morris water maze analysis was carried out to assess the learning and memory abilities. The water maze was divided into four quadrants namely I, II, III, and IV. The standing platform was placed at quadrant II, and the water temperature was set at 24 °C. Before the water maze tests, each rat underwent a four-day training period to search for the standing platform and stay there for 15 s. If the animals failed to find the platform within 2 min, they were guided to the platform of the water maze, enabling them to learn and remember spatially. On the 28th day, the small platform in the water maze was removed. The injured rats were placed in the water maze and subjected to 2 min swimming tests. The retention time in the platform quadrant and the frequency of platform crossing were recorded.

The sensorimotor coordination of rats was evaluated by rotating rod tests. Before the rotating rod tests, each rat underwent a three-day training period. Each training lasted for 10 min, during which the speed of the rotating rod was accelerated from 5 rmp/min to 40 rmp/min. If the rat fell off the rotator during the training, it would be placed back on the rotator until the training time ended. The rotating rod test was carried out on the 1st, 3rd, 7th, 14th, 21st, and 28th days post-injury. When the rat fell from the rotating rod device, the falling time was recorded.

Hematoxylin & eosin staining

The rats were sacrificed, and the major organs and brains of rats were collected after four-weeks post-injury. The collected tissues were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated with gradient sucrose, and processed into 10 μm thick coronal sections by using a freeze slicer. The sectioned slices were routinely stained by hematoxylin & eosin medium. For the immunofluorescence staining, the sectioned slices were blocked with BSA for 2 h, and then were successively stained with the primary antibody, the fluorescent labeled second antibody, and DAPI. The stained images of the slices were captured by a Leica fluorescent microscopy. The fluorescent intensities of images were processed with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. All independent experiments were replicated at least three times. Student's t-test was used to evaluate significant differences between two groups, and the analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to analyze three or four groups. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was defined as follows: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

In this study, PEGDA/cystamine/agmatine (PCA) hydrogels were synthesized through Michael addition under heated conditions [29]. Cystamine served as the cross-linkers in PCA hydrogels, PEGDA and arginine formed the main chain. Disulfide bonds of cystamine in hydrogels, which prevalently exist in protein molecules, stabilize the spatial structure of the peptide chain and exert crucial impacts on biological activity and functions. Furthermore, disulfide bonds can regulate the microenvironment of nerve regeneration by reducing reactive oxygen species [30]. Additionally, agmatine can inhibit nitric oxide synthase, and suppress the generation of nitric oxide within the body [31]. PEGDA, as a biocompatible polymer in the hydrogels, can react with the amino groups of cystamine and agmatine to form cross-linked hydrogels [32].

The synthesis of PCA hydrogels was verified by 1H NMR spectra. As shown in Fig. 2A, the characteristic peaks at δ = 6.04–6.50 ppm and δ = 3.70–4.36 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum of PEGDA were attributed to the chemical shifts of unsaturated double bonds and methylene groups, respectively. The featured peaks located at both δ = 2.82 ppm and δ = 2.70 ppm in the spectrum of cystamine were assigned to methylene groups. In the hydrogel group, the peaks found at δ = 3.70 ppm were assigned the methylene groups in the cross-linked main framework. In the PCA hydrogel group, the most obvious peak identified at δ = 3.70 ppm was attributed to the methylene groups within the cross-linked side-chain. It should be noted that the ratio of unsaturated double bonds in PEGDA and methylene groups in cystamine decreased significantly. Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR) were further conducted to confirm the chemical structure of PCA hydrogels. As depicted in Fig. S1, the symmetric stretching vibrations of -CH and the amine characteristic peaks were detected at 2866 cm−1 in cystamine/agmatine and 3366 cm−1 in PEGDA, respectively. The absorption peaks at 1637 cm−1 and 1716 cm−1 were assigned to stretching vibrations of C=C and C=O in PEGDA. After the formation of PCA hydrogel, both the amine characteristic peaks and the unsaturated double bonds vanished, indicating the successful synthesis of PCA hydrogels. PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 hydrogels were synthesized via the modulation of feeding ratio of compositions (Table S1). As depicted in Fig. 2B, the representative PEGDA/cystamine/agmatine solution formed transparent hydrogels that maintained their integrity within a glass vial when inclined. We quantitatively investigated the ability of the aqueous mixture of PEGDA/cystamine/agmatine to form hydrogels. However, the formation efficiencies of PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 hydrogels were determined to be 64.09 ± 2.41 %, 58.62 ± 3.74 % and 50.55 ± 4.84 %, respectively (Fig. 2C).

The morphology of PCA hydrogels was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). All PCA hydrogels of different compositions showed a rough structure, which were conducive to cellular growth (Fig. 2D). The rough surface became increasingly coarser with the escalating concentration of hydrogels. The mean pore sizes of PCA-1, PCA-2 and PCA-3 hydrogels were measured to be 102.61 ± 34.87 μm, 116.30 ± 51.34 μm and 164.48 ± 57.23 μm (Fig. S2). Hydrogel mechanical properties serve as crucial indicators influencing cellular growth and tissue regeneration [33]. The mechanical properties of synthesized PCA hydrogels were measured using a universal electronic mechanical tensioning machine. The stress-strain curves, Young's modulus and maximum compressive strength of PCA hydrogels were shown in Fig. 2E–G. PCA 0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogel exhibited deformation under lower stress, and the escalation of stress led to the rupture of the hydrogel. PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 demonstrated excellent performance in withstanding higher mechanical stresses and deformations, which could effectively prevent hydrogel rupture and collapse within the body. Young's modulus of PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 hydrogels were determined to be 10.24 ± 4.02 kPa, 16.73 ± 4.1 kPa and 26.02 ± 2.74 kPa, respectively. This tendency was similarly reflected in the maximum compressive strength of the PCA hydrogels. These results suggested that the enhanced mechanical properties of PCA hydrogels could be attributed to the increased cross-linking degree. The kinetics of PCA hydrogel formation were analyzed by rheological analysis (Fig. 3A–C). Initially, loss modulus (G") was lower than energy storage modulus (G') at 80 °C. As time elapsed, G' surpassed G", indicating the formation of the formation of crosslinked hydrogels. The gelation time of PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 were determined as 2966 ± 25 s, 4397 ± 42 s, and 8421 ± 60 s, respectively. As depicted in Fig. S3, the viscosity of all the tested PCA hydrogels declined with the escalation of shear rate, indicative of their shear thinning properties. The shear thinning behaviors suggested that the viscosity of PCA hydrogels continuously changes in response to the varying shear rate. The shear thinning behavior becomes increasingly obvious with increased concentration of hydrogels. PCA hydrogels exhibited injectable behaviors (Fig. S3B). The thixotropic analysis of PCA hydrogels were conducted to investigate the behavior of hydrogels recovery after extruding the hydrogel with high shear stress. A low strain (1 %) and a high strain (98 %) were sequentially and repeatedly applied on the PCA hydrogels. PCA hydrogels showed solid-like behaviors (G’>G’’) at the low strain, and transformed to liquid-like characteristics at the high strain. After returned to the original low strain, a quick transformation was subsequently observed, suggesting their excellent in-situ injection properties (Fig. S4).

The swelling rates of PCA hydrogels served as a key factor for evaluating potential occupancy and compression effects, and reflected their ability to absorb the interstitial fluid [34]. The dried hydrogels were immersed for 4 h in a phosphate buffer solution to simulate body fluids. During the initial 30 min, the swelling rates of PCA hydrogels rapidly increased (Fig. 3D). All the hydrogels were able to reach the equilibrium state within 1 h. The equilibrium swelling rates of PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 were 295.55 ± 43.15 %, 254.92 ± 36.05 % and 236.21 ± 12.65 %, respectively. Decreased swelling rates of PCA hydrogels were ascribed to the rising of initial feeding monomer concentrations. As the initial feeding monomer concentrations rose, the cross-linked degree of PCA hydrogels become higher. The degradation properties of implanted hydrogels played a significant impact on tissue regeneration [35]. The implanted hydrogels offer mechanical support and facilitate the growth of regenerated tissue. The degradation rates of PCA hydrogels were investigated in PBS and 20 mmol/L of tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine medium. PCA hydrogels showed a tendency of slow degradation rates ranging from 34.21 ± 2.19 % to 41.49 ± 4.55 % in the PBS medium at the tested four weeks (Fig. 3E). PCA hydrogels underwent rapid degradation during the first three days and then experienced slow degradation throughout the tested time. The elevated degradation rates of PCA hydrogels (ranging from 45.28 ± 1.56 % to 74.09 ± 2.07 %) in tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine medium can be attributed to the rapid reduction of disulfides within the hydrogels (Fig. 3F), which is consistent with the findings reported by Bingol et al. [28] The main chain of the PCA hydrogel is primarily composed of polyethylene glycol diacrylate and tertiary amines. Cystamine in the hydrogels was served as the cross-linking agent. The ester bonds of the main chain of PCA hydrogels could degraded into soluble small molecules by surface erosion. Furthermore, the released agmatine from PCA hydrogels were measured by an agmatine ELISA kit. Agmatine release profile of PCA-1 hydrogel were shown in Fig. S4, and ∼20.12 ± 3.56 % of agmatine was released from hydrogels at the tested fifteen days.

Similar to chemical signaling molecules and mechanical properties, the surface hydrophilicity of biomaterials also influenced cellular proliferation, migration, and differentiation [36]. The surface hydrophilicity of PCA hydrogels were investigated by a contact angle measuring instrument (Fig. 3G–I). The contact angles of wet PCA hydrogels were measured to range from 20.33 ± 1.46° and 10.25 ± 0.74°, while those of dry PCA hydrogels were determined to be between 42.45 ± 1.29° and 25.63 ± 1.30°. The biocompatibility of PCA hydrogels were assessed by cellular proliferation assay (Fig. S5). In vitro cellular viability of PCA hydrogels exceeded 87 % after incubation with mouse fibroblasts L-929 and rat neuroblastoma cell line ND7/23, indicating the excellent biocompatibility of PCA hydrogels. Moreover, blood compatibility is of paramount significance for implanted hydrogels [37]. Rabbit blood was collected and incubated with PCA hydrogels for 1 h. Erythrocytes in PCA hydrogels showed no significant disparity with the negative control group. No complete red blood cells, but only ruptured debris were observed in positive control group. The hemolysis rates of PCA0.65/0.13/0.13, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 were measured to be 0.198 ± 0.12 %, 0.115 ± 0.12 %, and 0.365 ± 0.12 % respectively (Fig. S6), suggesting their good blood compatibility.

Traumatic brain injuries also produced superfluous reactive oxygen species by dysfunctional mitochondria, which accumulated in the injured sites and ultimately induced neuronal death [38]. PCA hydrogels contained a substantial quantity of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) scavengers. Consequently, we bypassed the preliminary ROS/RNS clearance experiment and proceeded directly to cellular experiments. Under LPS stimulation, macrophages were induced to undergo pro-inflammatory differentiation, and produced a large amount of ROS. To explore whether the PCA hydrogels could effectively deplete ROS, 2',7'-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH) fluorescent probe was utilized to analyze the cellular levels of reactive oxygen species (Fig. 4A) [39]. After incubated with PCA hydrogels for 48 h, macrophages were observed by laser confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 4B, LPS group exhibited the strongest DCFH fluorescent intensity, followed by PCA hydrogels. DCFH fluorescent intensities also displayed a decreased tendency along with the reduction of the feeding cystamine ratio, which was correlated to the elimination of nitric oxide. The disulfide bonds from PCA hydrogels could be oxidized to sulphone groups by reactive oxygen species.

Nitric oxide acted an important modulator of cellular functions in biological systems [40]. Traumatic brain injuries typically generated a substantial amount of cytotoxic nitric oxide at the injured sites [41]. After traumatic brain injury, a large amount of glutamic acid was released from injured nerves, which stimulated the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor to induce Ca2+ influx [42]. When the intracellular Ca2+ overloaded, nitric oxide synthase (NOS) was activated, and induced the synthesis of excessive nitric oxide in the injured locations. Agmatine was capable of suppress the activity of NOS and reduce the level of nitric oxide in the body. Macrophage could secrete excessive nitric oxide under LPS stimulation. After 48 h of LPS stimulation, nitric oxide in the culture medium was determined to be 4.17 ± 0.20 μmol/L (Fig. 4C), which was significantly higher than the control group (0.83 ± 0.08 μmol/L). PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogels group showed the lowest nitric oxide level (1.63 ± 0.30 μmol/L), followed by PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 group (2.56 ± 0.36 μmol/L). PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 hydrogels group exhibited the highest nitric oxide level after LPS stimulation. These results could be attributed to the fact that the degraded agmatine from PCA hydrogels reduced cellular nitric oxide levels [43].

To assess the therapeutic effects of PCA hydrogels, traumatic brain injury models were established. PCA hydrogels were locally injected into the injured sites. The spatial learning and memory capabilities of rats were evaluated through the Morris water maze tests [44]. The water maze assessments comprised a positioning cruise evaluation and a spatial exploration assessment. The water maze of the circular pool was divided into four quadrants I, II, III, and IV. The standing platform of water maze was located at quadrant II of water maze. From 22nd day post-surgery, the injured rats underwent a four-day positioning cruise training for finding the standing platform. Fig. 5A showed the representative swimming maps of injured rats in water maze on the 26nd day post-surgery. The control injured group demonstrated persistent movement along the outer perimeter of the water maze, suggesting a deficit in learning capacity. PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogels showed the most focused swimming paths in quadrants I and II to seek standing platform, indicative of their excellent cured learning abilities. The trajectory of rats in pursuit of the platform demonstrated a positive correlation with the increased feeding ratio of PCA hydrogels. Subsequently, the standing platform in the circular pool was removed. Injured rats were subjected to a 2-min swimming task in water, during which their trajectories in quadrant II were monitored to assess their memory capabilities (Fig. 5A). The motion trajectories of the control injured group were dispersed throughout the water maze. Thereafter, the original platform at quadrant II was removed. The injured rats were replaced into the water maze and underwent 120 s swimming tests. The duration time in the original platform quadrant II and the frequency of platform crossing were recorded. The prolonged duration of injured rats in the original platform quadrant, as well as the increased frequency of platform crossings, exhibited a positive correlation with memory recovery. The PCA hydrogels groups exhibited an extended duration in quadrant II, where the original standing platform was situated (Fig. 5B). The duration of PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group (59.99 ± 3.81 s) showed the longest in quadrant II, followed by PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 (53.96 ± 2.10 s) and PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 (49.86 ± 3.28 s), which were significantly higher than that of the control injured rats The number of platform-crossing for rats with traumatic brain injury were also measured at 2-min swimming task (Fig. 5C). PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group showed the highest crossing frequency (6.67 ± 0.94), followed by PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 (5.02 ± 0.82) and PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 (3.67 ± 0.47). These values were significantly greater than that of the control injured rats (1.67 ± 0.47). These findings indicated that PCA hydrogels could facilitate the recovery of learning and memory function in injured rats.

After traumatic brain injury, the rats were typically associated with motor dysfunction. To assess the motor function, rotating rod fatigue tests were carried out on traumatic brain injured rats [45]. Prior to traumatic brain injuries, the rats underwent three days of rotating rod fatigue training (Fig. 5D and E). The retention time of the injured rats was recorded on the rotating rod fatigue meter at 1, 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28 days post-surgery. The mean retention time of rats treated with PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group was longest, followed by PCA1.35/0.26/0.26. Among all the PCA hydrogels, PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogels had the shortest retention time, which was significantly higher than that of the control groups. The modified neurological severity scores (mNSS), which encompassed sensory assessments, motor evaluations, reflex testing, and balance beam trials, were employed to assess the functional impairments in injured rats [46]. The mNSS scores of rats in each group at 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-traumatic brain injury were presented in Fig. 5F. At 28 days after surgery, the mNSS scores of PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 (1.00 ± 0.02) and PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 (1.00 ± 0.51) hydrogels groups were better than PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogels (1.38 ± 0.41) and control (2.03 ± 1.02) groups. These results showed that PCA hydrogels improved neurological functions of injured rats.

Following the initial mechanical trauma, microglia and astrocytes initiated a cascade of immune responses through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [47]. These immune responses facilitated the infiltration and recruitment of peripheral monocyte-derived macrophages and neutrophils, while concurrently activating resident glial cells [48]. The infiltrated cells and activated glial cells secreted ROS, NOS and pro-apoptotic proteins, which further exacerbated neuronal damage. Consequently, immunofluorescence staining of activated microglia and macrophages was performed using ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA-1) and recombinant cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86) antibodies, respectively (Fig. 6A). At 28 days post-injury, the proportion of CD86+ macrophages of PCA1.35/0.26/0.26 hydrogels was the lowest, followed by that of PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group (Fig. 6B). However, there was no significant difference between these two groups. In contrast, the control injured rats showed the highest ratio of CD86+ macrophages, which was comparable to PCA0.65/0.13/0.13 hydrogels group. Although similar trends were observed in the IBA-1 staining, PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group exhibited the lowest ratio of IBA-1+ stained activated microglia (Fig. 6C).

(A) Representative immunostaining images of CD86 (microglia) and IBA1 (macrophages) stained slices in rat brain, scale bar = 500 μm. (B–C) Quantitative analysis of CD86 and IBA1 fluorescent intensity (n = 3). Data were represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD); ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ANOVA.

To further validate the activated glial scars and survived neurons in injured sites, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP-2) staining was performed (Fig. 7A) [49]. The GFAP+ stained glial scars in the PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group were fewer compared to other PCA hydrogels, and no significant differences were observed among these groups (Fig. 7B). It should be noted that the GFAP+ glial scars in the PCA hydrogels were significantly reduced when compared to control injured group. The proportion of MAP-2+ neurons in PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogel group was highest (Fig. 7C). However, there was no significant difference in all tested groups. Collectively, PCA hydrogels have been demonstrated to improve motor function, learning capacity, and memory retention in injured rats. PCA1.0/0.2/0.2 hydrogels group exhibited the most significant inhibition of glial scar formation and inflammatory responses. To verify the systemic safety of PCA hydrogels, the major tissues including heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney were collected. The collected tissues were sectioned and stained by hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). As shown in Fig. S7, no significant structural damage, atrophy, fibrosis or other pathological changes were observed in PCA hydrogels, indicating the excellent biocompatibility of PCA hydrogels.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed injectable PCA hydrogels as potential therapeutic strategies for the treatment of traumatic brain injury. PCA hydrogels could be easily synthesized by Michael addition. Cystamine and agmatine in hydrogels could scavenge excessive ROS and inhibit the generation of RNS, providing a suitable micro-environment for preserving the survival of neurons, reducing the activation of glial cells and pro-inflammatory macrophages. These ROS/RNS scavenging hydrogels were locally injected into the injured brain to mitigate secondary brain injury. The topically injected hydrogels mimicked the extracellular matrix and facilitated motor and sensory functional recovery in traumatic brain injury models. Collectively, biocompatible PCA hydrogels offered a novel topical therapeutic approach and potential clinical application for the treatment of traumatic brain injury.
