Korean red ginseng extract suppresses food allergy by remodeling the gut microbiota and restoring immune homeostasis in IL4raF709 mice
Authors: Suyoung Choi, Thi Thuy Duong Pham, Tae-Keun Jeong, Ju-Gyeong Kang, Sang-Woo Lee, Bu Yeon Heo, Jung-Hyun Park, Jeong Suk Koh, Myung-Won Lee, Dae-Sik Lim, Ik-Chan Song, Jaeyul Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFood allergy (FA) is a growing health concern with limited therapeutic options. While Korean Red Ginseng Extract (KRGE) exhibits immunomodulatory and microbiota-modulating properties, its specific effects and mechanisms in FA are not fully understood.MethodsWe evaluated the anti-allergic efficacy of KRGE in IL4raF709 mice, a model genetically predisposed to IgE-mediated anaphylaxis. Mice were sensitized with ovalbumin(OVA) and staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB) and challenged with OVA. KRGE was administered orally prior to and during sensitization. Clinical symptoms, serum IgE levels, IL-33 levels, and intestinal histology were assessed. Mesenteric lymph nodes and Peyer's patches immune cell populations were analyzed by flow cytometry. Fecal microbiota composition was profiled using 16S rRNA sequencing and quantitative PCR; correlations between phenotype and microbiota were investigated.ResultsResults showed KRGE significantly suppressed anaphylactic symptoms, including hypothermia and mortality, and reduced OVA-specific IgE and IL-33 levels. KRGE restored intestinal epithelial integrity and normalized Peyer's patch hypertrophy. Immunologically, it decreased IL-13-producing T follicular helper cells and rebalanced dendritic cell subsets, increasing tolerogenic CD103+ cDC1 and reducing pro-allergic CD11b+ cDC2. Microbiome analysis revealed that OVA/SEB increased pro-inflammatory Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae while depleting beneficial Lactobacillaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae. KRGE reversed these changes, notably enriching Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus gasseri. Correlation analysis revealed that Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Christensenellaceae were negatively correlated with allergic markers and positively correlated with epithelial integrity. In contrast, Oscillospiraceae (Eubacterium_g8, Acetobacter, and Pseudoflavonifractor) was associated with allergy exacerbation.ConclusionKRGE mitigates FA in IL4raF709 mice by restoring gut microbiota balance and immune homeostasis, suggesting its potential as a microbiota-targeted intervention for FA.

Introduction

Food allergy (FA), a reproducible immune-mediated hypersensitivity encompassing various clinical entities including Immunoglobulin E(IgE)-mediated anaphylaxis, has dramatically increased in incidence in recent decades, constituting a significant public health concern with limited therapeutic options [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Current limited management strategies focus on strict avoidance of trigger foods and symptomatic control [6].

The pathogenesis of IgE-mediated FA involves complex immune cell interactions, critical for both sensitization and effector phases [7]. FA occurrence involves many immune cells, with the early responses of dendritic cells (DCs), especially CD103+ conventional DC type 1 (cDC1s), and T cells being particularly significant. In the gut, CD103+ cDC1s capture food antigens, migrate to lymph nodes, and are key in initiating tolerogenic T cell responses, driving the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) essential for oral tolerance [8,9]. During sensitization, allergen-specific IgE binds to mast cell receptors. Re-exposure triggers IgE cross-linking and mast cell degranulation, potentially leading to life-threatening anaphylaxis [10,11]. While Th2 cells were previously thought to be primary, follicular helper T cells (particularly Tfh13) are now recognized as crucial for high-affinity IgE generation [12,13]. A key trigger is the release of IL-33 from epithelial cells, which activates target cells, driving Th2 polarization in germinal centers (GC) and amplifying inflammation [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Activated DCs then prime naive T cells towards Th2 and Tfh differentiation in lymph nodes. Crucially, intestinal barrier integrity prevents dysregulated immune activation; its disruption allows allergen penetration and systemic immune dysregulation [19].

IL4raF709 mouse model, characterized by a functional mutation in the IL-4 receptor α chain (IL-4Rα), serves as a genetically predisposed system to study IgE-mediated FApathogenesis. This mutation makes IL4raF709 mice highly susceptible to oral sensitization with dietary antigens like ovalbumin (OVA) [20,21]. The model also exhibits impaired oral tolerance by Th2 polarization, closely mimicking human food allergy [[22], [23], [24]].

Korean ginseng (Panax ginseng Meyer) has long been used in traditional medicine for various ailments [25]. Korean red ginseng extract (KRGE) contains functional components with demonstrated immunomodulatory and anti-allergic efficacy [26]. Recent evidence links red ginseng's effects to the host's gut microbiome [27], which is central to nutrient digestion, immune regulation, and inflammatory responses [28]. Notably, several studies indicate that Korean red ginseng acts as a prebiotic, promoting beneficial gut bacteria and maintaining gut microbiome homeostasis, thereby supporting overall host health [29,30].

This study investigates how KRGE mitigates FA in IL4raF709 mice, focusing on its potential to restore gut microbiota balance and immune homeostasis as a microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Korean red ginseng extract (KRGE)

KRGE was kindly provided with a concentrated form by the Korea Ginseng Corporation (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The major saponin components in KRGE were quantified as follows, as shown in the table (Supplemental Table 1).

Mice

WT and IL4raF709 mice, aged 4–6 weeks, were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and maintained at the Chungnam National University Preclinical Experiment Center. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light-dark cycle, and they had free access to water and food. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chungnam National University (CNUH-2022-IA0061-00).

OVA/SEB sensitization and challenge in the FA model

All experiments used littermate WT and IL4raF709 mice to standardize the gut microbial environment in the FA model. Mice were co-housed and exchanged bedding for 4 weeks before the oral administration procedure. For sensitization of the IL4raF709 mice, sterile PBS or OVA (150 μg) and Staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB, 144–153, Fremont, CA, USA) (10 μg) in sterile PBS were administered weekly for 8 weeks. The mice were then challenged by intragastric gavage of either sterile PBS or high-concentration OVA (150 mg/kg) on the ninth week. KRGE-treated groups received intragastric administration of 100 mg/kg KRGE daily for 1 week before OVA/SEB sensitization and every 2 days during the weeks of sensitization. WT or IL4raF709 control mice groups received the same volume of PBS. These concentrations were determined through a prior literature review and pilot experiments [[31], [32], [33]].

Anaphylactic body temperature reduction was assessed by measuring rectal core body temperature with a manual rectal thermometer Testo925 (Testo SE & Co. KGaA, Titisee-Neustadt, Germany) at 15-min intervals before the challenge. Severity of redness, hair loss in the face area, and anaphylactic reactions was evaluated on a scale of 0–5 grades.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Plasma was collected from blood samples and stored at −80 °C until ELISA analysis. The levels of IL-33, IgE, and OVA-specific IgE were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's protocols (R&D Systems, BioLegend, and Invitrogen).

Single cell isolation of gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT)

Single cell suspensions from mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN), and Peyer's patches (PP) were prepared using a standard protocol for flow cytometry analysis [34]. In brief, MLN cells were harvested by mechanical disruption and filtration, with additionally hemolyzed. Peyer's patches were isolated from intestinal tissue, digested with collagenase, and then lysed and filtered. All cells were preserved in cold HBSS solution until staining.

Flow cytometry from MLN and small intestine(SI) PP cells

Flow cytometry analysis was performed as previously described [29]. To experiment, a single cell suspension was prepared from each tissue as described above. These samples were stained with an L/D staining kit (L34965, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) to remove the dead cells. Cells were fluorescently stained in individual panels for Treg, DC, and Th. Information on the monoclonal antibodies used in the experiments is shown in Supplemental Table 2.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Stool samples were collected directly from mice between 7 and 9 am on the day of collection and stored at −80 °C as soon as possible. Genomic DNA was extracted from each 0.5g of stool samples using the Power Fecal Pro DNA kit (QIAamp, MD, USA). The standard curve for A. muciniphila was constructed using a serial dilution of DNA isolated from pure-culture A. muciniphila (Stock# KCTC 15667, Jeongeup, Republic of Korea). A. muciniphila specific primer (Forward: 3′-GCGTAGGCTGTTTCGTAAGTCGTGTGTGAAAG-5′ and Reverse: 3′-GAGTGTTCCCGATATCTACGCATTTCA 5′) was pretested by polymerase chain reaction and agarose gel electrophoresis [32]. In brief, absolute quantification of A. muciniphila was performed using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of purified genomic DNA of cultured A. muciniphila. DNA copy numbers for the curve were calculated based on the genome size (2.78 Mb) and the average molecular weight of a DNA base pair (660 g/mol), using Avogadro's constant.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Small intestine and colon tissues were fixed in 10 % neutral-buffered formalin overnight at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 0.4 μm thickness with a Leica RM2245 microtome, stained with H&E (Sigma, MO, United States), and imaged with Pannoramic MIDI II slide scanner (3DHistech, Hungary). Imaging and analysis were performed using CaseViewer (version 2.4.0) and SideViewer (version 2.8).

The 16s rRNA based microbiome taxonomic profiling (MTP) assay

To investigate changes in the gut microbiome composition and diversity, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on mouse fecal samples. Downstream bioinformatics analyses, including quality control, taxonomic profiling, diversity assessments, and potential biomarker candidate identification, were conducted using EZbiocloud (https://www.ezbiocloud.net) and R with microeco (1.12.0). Detailed methodologies for sequencing can be found in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Unless otherwise noted, One-way ANOVA was used, and other statistical tests are described in the figure legends. In brief, the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was used to compare survival rates between multiple groups, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to calculate alpha and beta diversity indices for selected taxonomic comparisons.

Results

KRGE suppresses IgE-Mediated anaphylaxis in OVA/SEB-sensitized Il4raF709 mice by attenuating hypothermia and mortality

To evaluate whether oral KRGE can regulate food allergy, we first established a model by sensitizing IL4raF709 mice, an animal model of IgE-dependent FA, with oral intragastric gavage of OVA and SEB. Then we challenged them with a high dose of OVA (Fig. 1A). The mice were randomly divided into four experimental groups as outlined in the table. IL4raF709 mice sensitized to OVA/SEB exhibited hypothermia, characterized by a rapid decrease in body temperature of 2–4 °C within 1 h of oral OVA administration, and displayed decreased movement and wheezing behavioral changes, which are symptoms of anaphylaxis (Fig. 1B). In contrast, the KRGE-treated group maintained their body temperature and actively moved around the cage. Throughout the sensitization period, no significant difference in body temperature was observed between unsensitized IL4raF709 mice and wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 1B). No significant weight loss was shown with red ginseng and OVA administration over the trial period (Fig. 1C). Approximately 20 % (data not shown) of OVA/SEB-sensitized IL4raF709+OVA mice died due to acute anaphylaxis by OVA challenge. In addition, mild signs of anaphylaxis, such as hives and skin redness, were reduced by KRGE treatment in IL4raF709 mice as measured by hair loss and diarrhea symptom scores (Fig. 1D and E). H&E staining revealed larger PP in OVA-treated IL4raF709 mice compared to WT controls, which was successfully reversed by KRGE treatment (Fig. 2C and D). It also shows the same trend in SI PPs results (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, we investigated the impact on intestinal epithelial barrier morphology and arrangement, which are crucial for barrier integrity. OVA challenge in IL4raF709 mice severely damaged the intestinal epithelial barrier structure, evidenced by shortened crypts and disrupted intestinal villi. Remarkably, KRGE treatment restored the morphology and arrangement of the epithelial layer in sensitized IL4raF709 mice to a state resembling non-sensitized WT mice (Fig. 2C and D). The above results suggest that KRGE treatment inhibits or restores the intestinal cross-sectional changes and intestinal epithelial barrier damage induced by OVA/SEB sensitization.

KRGE suppresses IgE-Mediated sensitization and epithelial IL-33 release in food allergy-prone IL4raF709 mice

Food sensitization, defined by the presence of antigen-specific IgE, is a significant risk factor for IgE-mediated food allergy. As expected, following OVA/SEB sensitization and OVA challenge, IL4raF709 mice showed significantly increased serum OVA-specific IgE levels (approximately 200 μg/ml). Conversely, KRGE treatment significantly suppressed these levels to 50 μg/ml (Fig. 2E). OVA/SEB-sensitized IL4raF709 mice produced high levels of IL-33 before the OVA challenge. These levels remained consistent until after the challenge, which was inhibited by KRGE treatment in both sensitized and challenged mice (Fig. 2F). The data above show that KRGE treatment inhibits the production of allergen-specific IgE, a key marker of food sensitization, and also inhibits the production of IL-33, an alarmin whose release strongly indicates an initial trigger in allergic responses.

The frequency of Tfh (CD4+TCR + PD1+CXCR5+) cells was significantly higher in the MLN of PBS-treated IL4raF709 mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 3B). Moreover, OVA/SEB sensitization and OVA challenge increased Tfh cell frequency in IL4raF709 mice, a trend reversed by KRGE treatment.

The frequency of IL-13-producing Tfh cells was also significantly increased in unsensitized IL4raF709 mice compared to WT, further increasing after OVA/SEB sensitization and OVA challenge; however, KRGE treatment reduced their frequency in sensitized IL4raF709 mice (Fig. 3B). Similarly, KRGE treatment reduced Tfh cell frequency in PP compared to the OVA group, though without a difference in cytokine production.

In MLN, KRGE treatment significantly decreased the frequency of Foxp3+ Treg cells compared to unsensitized Il4raF709 mice. However, there was no difference from the OVA/SEB treatment group (Fig. 3C). Notably, the frequency of IL17A-producing CD4+ T cells in MLN was significantly increased by OVA/SEB sensitization, and this increase was significantly suppressed by KRGE treatment.

These data suggest that KRGE treatment in IL4raF709 mice inhibits the production of allergen-specific high-affinity and anaphylactic IgE by decreasing the frequency of IL-13-producing Tfh cells and modulating MLN immune cell populations, including Tregs and IL-17A producing CD4+ T cells.

KRGE reverses sensitization-induced migratory DC subset imbalance in mesenteric lymph nodes to suppress Tfh-driven IgE production”

The production of high-affinity allergen-specific IgE antibodies by B cells relies on T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, supported by specific DC subsets in MLNs that promote GC formation [12,40]. Specifically, the cDC2 subset is crucial for driving T cells towards the Tfh lineage by transporting antigens to the T-B boundary [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Conversely, CD103+ DCs in MLNs, encompassing CD103+CD11b+ and CD103+CD11b− DCs, are vital for initiating tolerogenic responses [[41], [42], [43], [44]].

We observed that OVA/SEB sensitization and OVA challenge in IL4raF709 mice led to altered DC frequencies in MLNs. While the overall frequency of Lin−CD11c+ DCs showed a trend towards an increase, reversed by KRGE treatment (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 3A and B), the frequency of migratory (Lin−CD11c + MHCIIʰi) DCs slightly increased after OVA treatment across groups (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Among the migratory (Lin−CD11c + MHCIIʰi) DCs, the frequency of tolerogenic cDC1s (CD103+CD11b−) was decreased by OVA/SEB sensitization but restored by KRGE treatment. Conversely, pro-allergic migratory CD103−cDC2s (CD103−CD11b+) were significantly increased by sensitization and challenge, a trend also reversed by KRGE. The frequency changes in the CD103−CD11b− DC subgroup mirrored those of migratory CD103−cDC2s, while migratory CD103+cDC2s (CD103+CD11b+) showed no significant changes (Fig. 4B). An increased frequency of migratory dendritic cells was observed in Peyer's patches and mesenteric lymph nodes, consistent with previous reports that dendritic cells can traffic through the lamina propria en route to the mesenteric lymph nodes following antigen sampling (Supplementary Fig. 4) [[45], [46], [47]]. For resident (Lin−CD11c+MHCIIˡᵒʷ) DCs, sensitization resulted in a general decrease Zin CD8+CD11b− and CD8−CD11b+ DCs, with a corresponding increase in CD8−CD11b− DCs; however, KRGE treatment had no significant effect on these specific trends (Fig. 4C).

Further analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) within the Lin−CD11c+ DC group revealed important changes in surface marker expression. CD103 expression, a key marker of intestinal tolerance in cDC1s, significantly decreased in Lin−CD11c + MHCIIʰi DCs after OVA treatment but significantly increased following KRGE treatment (Fig. 4D and E). CD11b expression decreased in both MHCIIʰi and MHCIIˡᵒʷ DC groups after KRGE treatment, with a statistically significant decrease in the MHCIIˡᵒʷ group (Supplementary Fig. 5). These MFI results suggest that KRGE influences DC subpopulation composition by reducing CD11b expression and enhancing CD103 expression within the Lin−CD11c+ DC compartment.

Our data collectively show that OVA/SEB sensitization and challenge cause specific changes in MLN DC subsets of IL4raF709 mice, marked by an increase in migratory CD103−cDC2s and a decrease in migratory cDC1s. KRGE treatment reverses these DC subtype changes in OVA/SEB-sensitized IL4raF709 mice, which aligns with the suppression of allergic symptoms in our model, including a lower frequency of IL-13-producing Tfh cells and reduced production of OVA-specific IgE.

The values of Chao1 and Shannon's index, alpha diversity indices of the microbiota, did not show significant differences among the three mice. (Fig. 5B). KRGE administration showed a tendency to normalize the elevated microbial richness and diversity induced by OVA sensitization, as reflected by the decreased Chao1 and Shannon indices, although these changes did not reach statistical significance. Principal component analysis (PCA) reveals that the three groups of mice are located in different regions along the PC2 axis, each with a unique microbiome composition (Fig. 5C). Along the PC1 axis, the PCA plot shows that the OVA-sensitized mice were significantly distinct from the non-sensitized mice. Also, this result confirms that Lactobacillus and Akkermansia, which are commonly considered beneficial bacteria, are associated with PBS and KRGE treatment groups, respectively. These data demonstrated that KRGE treatment caused the microbiome composition to shift back toward that of the healthy control group, suggesting that KRGE restores the gut microbiota to a healthy state.

LEfSe analysis identifies KRGE-induced restoration of Commensal taxa and enrichment of akkermansia in FA suppression

Next, we examined the microbiome changes that distinguished the three mouse groups using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect sizes (LEfSe) to identify indicator taxa that effectively separated the groups compared.

First, we applied LEfSe to microbiome data from unsensitized and OVA-sensitized IL4raF709 mice, which resulted in 31 differentially enriched clusters (α = 0.01) with LDA scores higher than 2.0 (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Fig. 6A). At the family level, 14 families were identified as indicator families in the non-sensitized mice, including Lactobacillaceae, Christensenellaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Corynebacteriaceae. In comparison, seven families were identified as indicator families in the sensitized mice, including Lachnospiraceae, Bacteroidaceae, and Lachnospira, Bacteroides, and Luminococcaceae. At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Muribacillum, and Roseburia are among the 16 indicator genera in the non-sensitized mice, while Bacteroides, Pseudoflavomitrifactor, and Eubacterium_g23 are among the 34 indicator genera in the sensitized mice. According to the cladogram, many genera in the Clostridiales and Ruminococcaceae are identified as indicator taxa in sensitized mice. However, Christensenella, a member of the Clostridiales, is included as an indicator taxon in non-sensitized mice.

Next, we compared the microbiome of sensitive mice with severe food allergy to another group of sensitive mice whose food allergy was suppressed by KRGE treatment using LEfSe analysis to identify characteristic changes in the microbiome after KRGE treatment. Ten families, including Muribaculaceae, Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Coriobacteriaceae, were enriched in the microbiome of KRGE-treated sensitized IL4raF709 mice (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 6B). At the genus level, 14 genera were enriched by KRGE treatment in the microbiome of sensitized mice, including, in order of highest RDA score, PAC000186_g, Akkermansia, PAC001066_g, Lactobacillus, Eubacterium_g17, KE159628, PAC001141_g, KE-159628, Bifidobacterium, and Roseburia. According to the cladogram, taxa belonging to the families Muribaculaceae, Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Coriobacteriaceae were enriched by KRGE treatment. Several genera belonging to the family Muribaculaceae were enriched in KRGE-treated sensitized mice, including PAC000186_g and PAC001066_g.

Comparison of genus-level indicator taxa obtained by LEfSe analysis in unsensitized and KRGE-treated sensitized mice revealed that the indicator taxa Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Roseburia, PAC000186_g, PAC001141_g, PAC001266_g, KE159605_g, and PAC001109_g from unsensitized mice were also identified as indicator taxa in KRGE-treated OVA-sensitized mice (Supplementary Fig. 6A and B). This indicates that these microbial genera characteristic of unsensitized mice were reduced by the OVA sensitization process and then restored by KRGE treatment. The newly identified indicator taxa in the microbiomes of KRGE-treated sensitized mice that were not indicator taxa in the non-sensitized mice were Akkermansia from the family Akkermansiaceae, Muribaculaceae, PAC001066_g, Eubacterium_g17, KE159628_g, Lachnospiraceae, PAC000692_g, Dehalobacterium, and PAC002390_g.

KRGE modulates species-specific gut microbiota restoring Lactobacillus and Akkermansia muciniphila for FA suppression

At the species level, we identified microbes that exhibited characteristic abundance changes in the three groups of mice (Fig. 5F). Lactobacillus gasseri and Lactobacillus reuteri were present at high abundance in unsensitized IL4raF709 mice. However, their abundance was dramatically reduced by OVA sensitization, with some recovery observed following KRGE treatment. Akkermansia muciniphila from Akkermansiaceae, PAC001126_s and KB822463_s from the Lachnospiraceae family, Acutalibacter muris from the Ruminococcaceae family, and PAC001141_s from the Christensenellaceae family showed characteristic changes that were strongly increased by KRGE treatment. However, their numbers were lower in the other two groups of mice. These microbial groups, which are characteristically increased upon KRGE treatment, are likely to be associated with the KRGE-dependent suppression of FA. On the other hand, species that may be linked to the development of food allergies were also found. Species that were low in relative abundance in the other two groups of mice but characteristically increased in the sensitized mice included the PAC002512_s group, PAC001516_s, PAC001366_s from the family Lachnospiraceae, and PAC001095_s from the family Ruminococcaceae. Furthermore, PAC002512_s, PAC001366_s, and PAC001516_s from the family Lachnospiridae and PAC001095_s, PAC001244_s, KI535319, and PAC001188_s from the family Ruminococcaceae is among the species with these features. An R2 value of 0.999 was obtained from the standard curve generated using a purified culture of A. muciniphila via quantitative PCR (qPCR). Our analysis revealed that A. muciniphila abundance, which was highest in wild-type mice, was reduced by both genetic predisposition and OVA sensitization. Notably, KRGE treatment partially restored this decrease. This finding suggests that KRGE can counteract the negative effects of both genetic and environmental factors on the abundance of this beneficial microbe (Fig. 5G). In WT mice, Akkermansia muciniphila levels averaged 2.6 × 106 copies per μg of DNA. This abundance was significantly reduced in IL4raF709 mice, with values dropping to 1.0 × 106 copies per μg and decreasing further to below 1.0 × 106 copies per μg in OVA-sensitized mice. Notably, KRGE treatment led to a partial restoration, increasing Akkermansia muciniphila levels to 1.8 × 106 copies per μg. These findings suggest that both genetic predisposition and allergic sensitization reduce the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila in the gut. At the same time, KRGE administration can partially counteract this effect, supporting its role in modulating the gut microbiome and potentially enhancing gut barrier function in the context of food allergy.

Microbiota-phenotype correlation heatmap reveals protective taxa enhancing gut barrier and harmful taxa driving allergic responses

Next, to elucidate functional links, we correlated indicator taxa with FA-related phenotypes. At the genus level, several taxa, including Oscillospiraceae(Eubacterium_g8, Acutalibacter, and Pseudoflavonifractor), family Muribaculaceae, Lactobacillus of the family Lactobacillaceae, Staphylococcus of the family Staphylococcaceae, KE159605_g of the family Lachnospiraceae, and Akkermansia of the family Akkermansiaceae were positively correlated with shortening intestinal epithelial crypt length and body temperature drop, and negatively correlated with IL-33 levels, OVA-specific IgE levels, and PP area, which are indicators of FA (Fig. 5H).

At the species level (Fig. 5I), specific species exhibited similar correlations. Beneficial species, including Akkermansia muciniphila from the family Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillus gasseri and Lactobacillus reuteri from the family Lactobacillaceae, PAC002411_s from the family Corynebacteriaceae, EU842956_s and PAC001786_s from the family Ruminococcaceae, and PAC001141_s and PAC001371_s from the family Christensenellaceae, were negatively correlated with FA markers and positively correlated with protective phenotypes. The family with the most species identified in this relationship is Lachnospiraceae, with six species identified (PAC001126_s, KB822463_s, PAC001796_s, PAC001382_s, PAC002005_s, and PAC001554_s). Species that correlated with experimental measures in the opposite direction to the above, i.e. in the direction of FA development, were most frequently identified in the family Ruminococcaceae, with a total of 10 species identified as follows: PAC001548_s, EU622658_s, PAC001370_s, KI535319_s, PAC001379_s, PAC001784_s, PAC001590_s, PAC001584_s group, PAC001188_s, and PAC001095_s and the family Lachnospiraceae had a total of six species identified: PAC001710_s, PAC001516_s, PAC001387_s, PAC001366_s, and PAC002512_s. Additionally, Romboutsia timonensis (Peptostreptococcaceae), EF096150_s (Erysipelotrichaceae), PAC001360_g_uc (Christensenellaceae), and PAC002010_s (Mogibacterium_f) were identified, emphasizing complex microbial shift linked to FA.

These correlative findings emphasize that key beneficial microbes, particularly Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Lactobacillus reuteri, serve as important potential biomarker candidates and therapeutic targets in food allergy. Additionally, these other associated microbial changes suggest the restoration of the gut microbial environment in FA.

Discussion

This study clearly demonstrates that KRGE suppresses food allergy by remodeling the gut microbiota and restoring immune homeostasis. The shifts in microbial communities induced by KRGE were closely associated with the suppression of IL-13-producing Tfh13 cells and the restoration of tolerogenic dendritic cell subsets.

KRGE enriches key probiotic gut microbes, most notably Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, which are well known to promote the tolerogenic differentiation of dendritic cells. In addition, KRGE treatment enhances immune tolerance and inhibits the generation of high-affinity IgE, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for the central immunoregulatory effect of KRGE in mitigating allergic responses. These microbial shifts correlated with the suppression of IL-13-producing Tfh13 cells and the restoration of tolerogenic dendritic cell subsets, both of which critically contribute to immune central formation, particularly GC generation, suggesting a microbiome-immune axis underpinning KRGE's therapeutic efficacy. This study positions KRGE as a promising candidate for microbiome-targeted interventions in food allergy, bridging microbial ecology with immune regulation to restore gut barrier and systemic immune homeostasis.

KRGE treatment modulated gut microbiota across taxonomic hierarchies, notably increasing Akkermansia muciniphila and reversing OVA-induced dysbiosis characterized by Clostridiales proliferation and depletion of Lactobacillus/Bifidobacteriales. Notably, Muribaculaceae, a key butyrate-producing family, and Akkermansia muciniphila, a mucin-degrading species critical for epithelial barrier integrity, showed KRGE-dependent enrichment [29,48]. These microbial shifts align with prior findings that ginseng saponins selectively promote Lactobacillus and Akkermansia [49,50], while gut microbiota metabolites enhance KRGE's bioactivity [51]. The resurgence of these taxa correlates with FA suppression, suggesting KRGE acts through a tripartite mechanism: (1) rebalancing dysbiotic communities, (2) reinforcing intestinal tight junctions via short-chain fatty acids (SCFA)-producing microbes, and (3) modulating dendritic cell-Tfh cross-talk to reduce IgE affinity maturation.

Intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction is a central mechanism driving FA pathogenesis, and our data reveal that KRGE mitigates this by enriching microbiota critical for barrier repair. LEfSe analysis identified Lactobacillaceae, Coriobacteriaceae, and Bifidobacteriaceae as family-level potential biomarkers in both the KRGE-treated and non-sensitized groups, while Akkermansiaceae and Muribaculaceae were unique to KRGE-treated mice. At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Roseburia shared potential biomarkers, whereas Akkermansia was exclusive to KRGE. Notably, Akkermansia muciniphila (Akkermansiaceae) enhances goblet cell density, mucus production, and intestinal stem cell-mediated epithelial regeneration, directly repairing mucosal damage [[52], [53], [54]]. This species also generates acetate and propionate, providing substrates for butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Coriobacteriaceae and Roseburia, which are essential for SCFAs in colonocyte energy metabolism and the reinforcement of tight junctions [55,56]. Concurrently, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium bolster barrier integrity by stimulating tight junction proteins and mucus secretion [57,58]. Although we did not perform direct functional assays of epithelial permeability, these microbial shifts, together with the observed histological changes in mucosal structure, suggest a potential contribution of KRGE to epithelial barrier stabilization. Collectively, these findings suggest that KRGE's FA-suppressive effects are likely mediated, at least in part, through microbiota-driven support of epithelial homeostasis, with further functional confirmation required in future studies.

KRGE suppresses FA by modulating the immune response through DC subset-specific mechanisms, addressing both tolerance induction and sensitization pathways. Oral tolerance relies on migratory CD103+ cDC1s, which traffic via lymphatic vessels to mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) to promote Tregs that suppress allergic responses [[59], [60], [61]], while IgE-mediated sensitization involves CD11b+ cDC2s driving IL-13+ T follicular helper (Tfh) cells for high-affinity IgE production [41,[62], [63], [64]]. In OVA/SEB-sensitized mice, KRGE restored CD103+cDC1 frequency, counteracting SEB-induced CD103 downregulation and Treg impairment, while suppressing pathogenic CD11b+cDC2 expansion, which correlated with reduced IL-13+Tfh cells and antigen-specific IgE titers [13,37,65]. This dual DC subset rebalancing highlights KRGE's novel mechanism, which enhances cDC1-mediated tolerance pathways and inhibits cDC2-driven sensitization. Unlike prior studies focusing on broad DC activation or cytokine modulation, KRGE demonstrates subset-specific precision, rectifying DC-driven immune dysregulation underlying FA. By synergizing microbiome restoration (e.g., Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus enrichment) with DC subset reprogramming, KRGE emerges as a multi-modal therapeutic targeting both microbial and immunological axes of FA pathogenesis. Recent studies show that Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus spp. directly influence DC subset fate and function via their unique molecular structures [[66], [67], [68]]. For example, Akkermansia outer membrane proteins (OMPs, such as Amuc_1100) engage Toll-like receptor 2/4 (TLR2/4) on DCs, driving a tolerogenic phenotype with increased IL-10 and retinoic acid, promoting Foxp3+ Treg expansion and suppressing Th2/Tfh13 cells [[69], [70], [71], [72]]. Similarly, exopolysaccharides and lipoteichoic acids from Lactobacillus activate DC TLR2 signaling, boosting anti-inflammatory cytokines and mucosal immune tolerance. KRGE's allergy-suppressive effects appear to leverage these microbiota-DC signaling axes, as evidenced by our own findings and recent literature, forming a mechanistic basis for multi-modal immune recalibration [[73], [74], [75]].

The dramatic rise in FA prevalence, particularly in Westernized nations, underscores the critical role of environmental factors, including dietary shifts and reduced microbial exposure, in disrupting gut microbiome equilibrium and immune tolerance pathways. Early-life dysbiosis, characterized by diminished microbial diversity and impaired production of SCFAs, is a recognized predictor of fatty acid persistence and severity [[76], [77], [78]]. Our findings position KRGE as a novel therapeutic intervention that directly addresses these environmental determinants by restoring keystone taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus gasseri, which drive SCFA synthesis (e.g., butyrate, acetate) to reinforce gut barrier integrity and suppress Th2 polarization [29,48,[55], [56], [57], [58]]. Unlike conventional approaches that focus on symptom management, KRGE uniquely recalibrates the microbiome-immune axis, reversing OVA-induced depletion of immunoregulatory Clostridiales while enriching mucin-degrading Verrucomicrobia, a dual action not observed in prior ginseng studies [[49], [50], [51]]. By concurrently enhancing epithelial repair through Akkermansia muciniphila-mediated goblet cell activation and modulating DC subsets to favor tolerogenic CD103+cDC1 over pro-allergic CD11b+cDC2 [13,37,41,[59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65],79], KRGE offers a groundbreaking strategy that bridges microbial ecology with precision immunomodulation, addressing the root causes of FA in industrialized populations.
