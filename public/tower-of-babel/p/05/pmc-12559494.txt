Identifying Potential Gaps in Care for Patients with Higher-Risk Myelodysplastic Syndromes in a Large US Health System
Authors: Kristin J Moore, Nicole M Engel-Nitz, Peter McMahon, Jason Beal, Teraneh Z Jhaveri, Mellissa Williamson, Kate Andrade, Christina Steiger, Cosmina Hogea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigher-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (HR-MDS) may be difficult to diagnose because patients present with nonspecific signs and symptoms. This can prolong diagnosis, even though disease progression can occur quickly in HR-MDS.ObjectivesThis study identified places along the care journey where there are gaps in care in the identification, testing, diagnosis, and treatment of insured US patients with HR-MDS.MethodsThis retrospective study utilized nationally representative US administrative claims and linked socioeconomic and social determinants of health (SDoH) data to characterize newly diagnosed patients with HR-MDS between 1 January 2017 and 30 April 2022.ResultsThe study included 1710 patients, of which a large proportion experienced some level of unmet need for modifiable SDoH characteristics. The median time between first sign or symptom and HR-MDS diagnosis was nearly the full look-back period of 12 months. Fewer than half of the patients received guideline-recommended treatment. Few patients had clinical trial participation.ConclusionsOpportunities to improve care include designing programs that support SDoH needs of patients (such as transportation access or social isolation), shortening the time between initial signs or symptoms and diagnosis and improving access to clinical trial participation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-025-00600-3.

Key Points for Decision Makers

The median time between first sign or symptom and higher-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (HR-MDS) diagnosis was 10.3 months, suggesting that there is often a long time lag between presentation of signs/symptoms and subsequent HR-MDS diagnosis.Nearly 37% of patients in our study were on active treatment regimens, but approximately one-third of the study population did not initiate any treatment during the follow-up period; few patients were found to have clinical trial participation.The findings of this study could be used to further develop targeted interventions to improve patient care among patients in this population.

Introduction

The Leukemia and Lymphoma Society (LLS) estimates that 20,000 new cases of myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are reported every year in the USA [1]. The incidence of MDS may be underestimated owing to underreporting and underdiagnosis [2]. Patients with MDS present with nonspecific signs and symptoms such as fatigue, infections, bruising, and bleeding; testing may identify anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and thrombocytopathy [3]. Diagnosis of MDS is confirmed by performing a bone marrow aspiration and biopsy [4]. Risk stratification, using scoring systems, is an important indicator of disease prognosis and planning appropriate treatment strategies [5, 6]. The Molecular International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-M) combines hematologic parameters, cytogenetic alterations, and somatic mutations of 31 genes [7]. Patients are stratified into six categories ranging from very low- to very high-risk MDS (HR-MDS).

Patients with HR-MDS comprise approximately 43% of the MDS population and have worse overall survival and higher risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [8]. Identifying patients with HR-MDS is important because practice guidelines [9, 10] recommend that these patients should be considered for therapies that alter the course of disease, such as hypomethylating agents (azacitadine and decitabine) or an allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Patients are also recommended to participate in clinical trials. When treated using hypomethylating therapies, a minority of patients with HR-MDS achieve complete response, with studies showing an overall survival on these therapies of only 1–2 years [11]. This highlights the importance of speed to diagnosis and risk classification as well as the urgency for patients to receive appropriate treatment and disease management in a timely manner. However, selecting the optimal therapies and obtaining an accurate diagnosis were rated as the most challenging issue in managing patients with MDS among a survey of hematology/oncology healthcare providers [12]. Providers noted a difficulty in identifying patients who would most benefit from the expanding therapeutic landscape.

Demographic factors such as age, sex, race, and ethnicity have been shown to be important factors in cancer etiology and survival outcomes in many cancers. In addition, differences in practice patterns for treatment of MDS have suggested that patients who are treated at academic centers and patients who are younger are treated more aggressively with patients treated at community-based centers and patients with advanced age [3]. Owing to the nonspecific nature of signs and symptoms of the disease and the need for additional genomic testing for risk stratification, it is possible that treatment guidelines and current clinical practice for HR-MDS may unintentionally lead to disparities in the identification of MDS and the selection of more effective treatments across patient subgroups [13].

This study sought to identify the situations along the real-world patient care journey where there may be gaps in care in the identification, testing, diagnosis, and treatment among insured US patients with HR-MDS. Understanding where gaps in care occur will help to identify areas of need for developing specific interventions to improve overall patient care and outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used the Optum Research Database (ORD) of administrative medical and pharmacy insurance claims for commercial and Medicare Advantage enrollees in the USA. The ORD, which is fully deidentified and used in a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA)-compliant manner, comprises medical and pharmacy claims data (including linked enrollment) from 1993 to present on more than 90 million lives. This secondary data source covered approximately 8% of the US commercially insured population and 18% of the Medicare Advantage population (with medical and pharmacy claims). Claims include associated health-plan and patient-paid amounts. Additional data, which include socioeconomic (SES) and social determinants of health (SDoH) characteristics that are not available in typical claims data, were linked from additional Optum data sources using unique patient identification numbers. Patients with missing SES or SDoH data remained in the study population. As no identifiable protected health information was accessed in the conduct of this study, institutional review board approval or waiver of approval was not required.

Patient Selection

Patients eligible for the study had evidence of incident HR-MDS between 1 January 2017 and 30 April 2022. Evidence of HR-MDS was defined as having at least one inpatient medical claim with an International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnostic code for HR-MDS (D46.20–D46.22) or at least two outpatient claims with an ICD-10-CM diagnostic code for MDS (D46.0–46.9, D46.A–D46.Z) or HR-MDS on separate dates but within 1 year and with at least one of these claims coded as HR-MDS [14]. The earliest date of HR-MDS diagnosis was the index date. Patients were required to be at least 18 years old and have continuous health plan enrollment for at least 12 months prior to (baseline period) and at least 3 months on or after the index date (or less if due to death) (follow-up period). They were followed until earliest AML diagnosis, death, disenrollment, or study end date, whichever occurred first. Patients were excluded if they had any claims with a diagnosis of HR-MDS in the 12-month baseline period to approximate a “newly diagnosed” HR-MDS study population. In addition, patients were excluded if they had evidence of a stem cell transplant, other primary cancers (except nonmelanoma skin cancer), metastatic disease, acute myeloid leukemia (AML), myelofibrosis, or pregnancy in the baseline period. Patients who had evidence of MDS but no diagnoses of HR-MDS during baseline were retained in the study.

Study Measures

Patient demographic, SES, and clinical characteristics were measured during the baseline period. Patients were also evaluated for a prior MDS diagnosis defined as either having a claim for MDS prior to the set of claims used to establish the index date or receiving active treatment for MDS during the baseline period.

The study measured social determinants of health (SDoH) using indices for five SDoH domains: financial stress, food insecurity, housing insecurity, social isolation, and transportation difficulty. Each SDoH index is based on predictive models that use a combination of individual-level consumer data and publicly available data by geography to predict the likelihood of an individual having unmet needs in each of the SDoH domains. Optum uses proprietary steps to review and validate a model for each SDoH domain. Briefly, Lasso regression is used to reduce dimensionality, an XGBoost model is used to choose the top important features, a correlation analysis is used to identify multicollinearity, and top features are selected for the final model [15]. Each model is validated on unseen data, and the area under the curve is reviewed for sensitivity and specificity levels. The SDoH indices are scaled from 0 to 9, with 0 indicating the highest level of unmet need—or patients having more insecurity—and 9 indicating the lowest risk of insecurity for that domain. In this study, patients were further categorized into tertiles of a high level of unmet need (0–3), moderate level of unmet need (4–6), and no unmet need (≥ 7). The transportation access measure was developed and is presented for patients aged ≥ 65 years only.

MDS-related signs, symptoms, and diagnostic procedures were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and identified using codes. MDS-related signs/symptoms identified using diagnosis codes included anemia, neutropenia, bleeding, thrombocytopenia, bruising, dyspnea, infections, Sweet syndrome, and fatigue. The time between the earliest claim for each sign/symptom and the index date was calculated for each patient. Patients were then classified by the earliest sign/symptom present during the baseline period, and the time from earliest sign/symptom to the index date was presented stratified across demographic and clinical characteristics. Similarly, the earliest of claims with procedure codes for a bone marrow biopsy and aspiration, blood cell counts, cytogenic analysis, flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, gene sequencing, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing were identified for each patient. Time between each procedure and the index date was calculated, and patients were classified by the earliest diagnostic procedure present during the 12-month baseline period. Time from HR-MDS diagnosis to progression of AML was assessed.

Patients were classified on the basis of treatments received during the variable follow-up period. If patients received multiple treatments, they were classified into categories on the basis of the following order of more definitive treatment: (1) HSCT, (2) active treatment (azacitidine, decitabine, decitabine and cedazuridine, lenalidomide, cyclosporine, thalidomide, anti-thymocyte globulin, clofarabine, daunorubicin, idarubicin, cytarabine, fludarabine, venetoclax, and topotecan), (3) MDS supportive care treatments (hydroxyurea and hematopoietic growth factors), and (4) none of the treatments in categories 1–3.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics and treatment patterns were analyzed descriptively. Length of time between the initial presence of a sign or symptom during the 12-month baseline period and the HR-MDS diagnosis was classified into a longer (> 6 months) versus shorter time to diagnosis. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to identify baseline demographic, SES, SDoH, and clinical characteristics that were associated with a longer versus shorter time to diagnosis. A multivariable proportional hazards regression model was used to describe the association between baseline demographic, SES, SDoH, and clinical characteristics and progression to AML. A backward stepwise selection process was used to identify characteristics with the strongest relationships to remain in each final model. Covariables remained in the model if they had a p-value < 0.1. We examined variance inflation factors (VIFs) in the final models to confirm that there were no issues with multicollinearity in the final model.

A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient Selection and Demographics

The final sample included 1710 patients meeting the study inclusion criteria (Supplementary Fig. 1). The mean age was 76.2 years (standard deviation [SD] 9.4), and the majority of patients were male (58.5%; Table 1). Most patients were covered by Medicare Advantage plans (85.7%), and only 14.3% of patients were covered by commercial insurance plans. The majority of patients resided in the South (42.8%) and Midwest (31.2%). Most patients were non-Hispanic white (62.5%) and had some college or an associate degree (46.8%; Table 1). Depending on measure, 14–22% of patients were missing SES data while 34–43% were missing SDoH data. The most common household income category was lower than US$50,000 (28.8%), but the most common net worth category was more than US$500,000 (26.0%; Table 1). Between 13.0% and 19.0% of the study population had a high unmet need for each of the SDoH characteristics (Fig. 1). The median baseline National Cancer Institute (NCI) Charlson comorbidity index was 2.0 (interquartile range [IQR] 1–4) (Table 1).

Timing of Sign or Symptom Identification to HR-MDS Diagnosis

During the baseline period, 97.5% of patients had evidence of any of the selected signs/symptoms of interest. The most commonly occurring signs or symptoms were anemia (90.0%), infection (70.7%), and dyspnea (43.2%) (Supplementary Table 1). Anemia and infection were the earliest signs for 48.2% and 33.4% of the study population, respectively. The least frequently occurring first signs/symptoms identified in this study population were Sweet syndrome (no patients), bruising (< 0.3%), neutropenia (3.0%), bleeding (7.8%), and fatigue (8.2%).

Overall, the median time between the occurrence of any sign/symptom and the HR-MDS diagnosis was 10.3 months (IQR 6.9–11.8; Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). When categorized by specific sign or symptom, anemia (8.1 months [IQR 2.6–11.3 months]) and infection (8.4 months [IQR 4.8–11.0 months]) first appeared at least 6 months prior to the HR-MDS diagnosis. Signs/symptoms that first appeared right around 6 months prior to being diagnosed included fatigue (median 5.4 months [IQR 1.7–9.5 months]), bleeding (median 5.5 months [IQR 1.9–9.7 months]), and dyspnea (median 5.7 months [IQR 2.1–9.5 months]). Neutropenia (median 2.3 months [IQR 0.7–6.7 months]), Sweet syndrome (median 2.6 months [IQR 2.3–7.4 months]), bruising (median 3.8 months [IQR 0.9–6.9 months]), and thrombocytopenia (median 3.9 months [IQR 1.2–9.1 months]) first appeared within 4 months prior to HR-MDS diagnosis.

In a multivariable logistic regression, patients covered by commercial insurance were less likely to have documented signs or symptoms earlier in the baseline period (odds ratio [OR] 0.59, 95% confidence interval [CI] [0.41–0.84]; Fig. 3A). Patients with an autoimmune disorder (OR 1.46, [95% CI 1.06–1.99]), renal disease (OR 1.35, [95% CI 1.01–1.81]), and other conditions were more likely to have evidence of signs/symptoms earlier during baseline. Not unexpectedly, patients who had more comorbidities were also more likely to have signs/symptoms earlier during baseline than those with fewer comorbidities. Patients who were diagnosed in later calendar years during the study were less likely to have signs/symptoms earlier in the baseline period, suggesting a shorter time between the earliest sign/symptom and HR-MDS diagnosis.

Timing of Diagnostic Testing to HR-MDS Diagnosis

Blood cell counts were the most common first diagnostic measure identified in the study population. More than 96% of patients had any laboratory procedures for blood cell counts identified during the baseline period. Other common diagnostic measures included flow cytometry (53.4%), immunohistochemistry (45.6%), and cytogenic analyses (43.8%). Approximately three-quarters of patients had evidence of a bone marrow biopsy or aspiration during the 12-month baseline period prior to HR-MDS diagnosis or anytime during follow-up. The median time between the procedure and HR-MDS diagnosis in this study population was between 1 and 2 months prior to the HR-MDS diagnosis (median 1.1; IQR 0.5–4.2 months; Fig. 2). Similarly, the first evidence of gene sequencing occurred within 1–2 months before the HR-MDS diagnosis (median 1.6; IQR 0.5–4.3).

Follow-Up Treatment Patterns

During follow-up, patients were classified into groups depending on whether they received an HSCT, active treatment, MDS supportive treatment only, or none of the listed treatments. Continuous follow-up time was similar between these treatment groups (median 9.1 months for active treatment [IQR 4.8–17.6 months] and 7.6 months for no treatment [IQR 1.8–22.2 months]; not shown). The most common treatment groups were active treatment (33.0%) and none of the listed treatments (37.1%; not shown). Just over 26.3% of patients had MDS supportive treatment only, and few patients had evidence of HSCT (3.6%; not shown). Patients who had HSCT were younger (median age 65 years; mean [95% confidence interval (CI)] 64 years [62–65 years]) compared with the active treatment group (median 77 years; mean [95% CI] 76 years [75–77 years]), no treatment group (median 77 years; mean [95% CI] 76 years [75–77 years]), and MDS supportive treatment group (median 80 years; mean [95% CI] 79 years [78–80 years]) (data not shown). Correspondingly, the HSCT cohort had the lowest NCI Charlson score (median 1.0; IQR 0.0–1.0) followed by those who received active treatment or no treatment (median 2.0; IQR 1.0–4.0 for both) and MDS supportive treatment (median 3.0; IQR 1.0–5.0; data not shown). Male patients made up 64.4% [95% CI 60.3–68.4%] of the active treatment group but only 54.1% [95% CI 49.4–58.8%] of the MDS supportive treatment group (Fig. 4A). Patients who received active treatment and those who received no treatment appeared to have a similar prevalence of baseline comorbidities (autoimmune conditions, diabetes, and hepatic and renal diseases) and similarities in underlying SDoH and SES characteristics (Fig. 4B). Patients who received an HSCT had the least likelihood of having any high unmet needs across all the SDoH measures (Fig. 4B). Patients who received MDS supportive treatment only had the lowest proportion of patients with no unmet needs, indicating that they were more likely to show signs of insecurity for SDoH measures (Fig. 4B).

Progression to AML

Male patients were found to have an elevated risk of progression to AML compared with female patients (hazard ratio [HR] 1.26 [95% CI 1.01–1.56]; Fig. 3B). Other demographic or clinical characteristics were found to be associated with an elevated risk of progression to AML, for example, having a later index year, having a household income of less than US$50,000 (HR 1.56 [95% CI 1.08–2.25]), and having evidence of certain comorbidities such as cardiac disease (HR 1.32 [95% CI 1.03–1.69]), disorders of lipid metabolism (HR 1.30 [95% CI 1.02–1.64]), coagulation or hemorrhagic disorders (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.18–1.80), and diseases of white blood cells (HR 1.51 [95% CI 1.23–1.87]). Patients with moderate unmet needs related to transportation access were also found to have an elevated risk of progression to AML compared with those with no unmet needs (HR 1.44 [95% CI 1.04–2.01]). Interestingly, certain comorbidities were associated with a decreased risk of progression to AML including cerebrovascular diseases (HR 0.75 [95% CI 0.58–0.98]), eye disorders (HR 0.81 [95% CI 0.65–0.99]), and an age of 75 years or older at index (HR 0.65 [95% CI 0.52–0.82]).

Discussion

This study sought to identify instances along the patient care journey where there may be gaps in the identification, testing, diagnosis, and treatment of patients with HR-MDS. We found that many signs and symptoms of disease presented early in the baseline period prior to diagnosis, suggesting that there is often a time lag between presentation of signs/symptoms and subsequent HR-MDS diagnosis. However, we did find that this may improve over time as patients diagnosed more recently were less likely to have signs/symptoms present earlier in baseline period after adjustment for demographic, SES, SDoH, and clinical characteristics. This study found that a significant proportion of patients experience moderate to high levels of unmet need related to modifiable SDoH risk factors such as social isolation and transportation access. Finally, few patients in this study were undergoing HSCT, a large portion of patients had no evidence of active treatment, and there was low participation in clinical trials. The World Health Organization has noted that addressing SDoH elements is fundamental in improving inequities in heath, and that these factors can outweigh genetic influences [16]. The results of this study point to a need to better address SDoH factors to improve care for patients with MDS. Further research is needed to understand the complex interactions between SDoH elements and treatment-related decisions such as the decision to refuse treatment.

This study found a median time between first sign or symptom to diagnosis of 10.3 months (IQR 6.9–11.8 months) and a median time from bone marrow biopsy or aspiration to diagnosis of 1.1 months (IQR 0.5–4.2 months). The recent retrospective study by Rosetti et al. [17] from a single institution found that the median time from first sign or symptom to bone marrow biopsy was 19.3 months (IQR 8.9–44.3 months), and that the time from first sign or symptom to treatment initiation was 22 months (IQR 11.4–45.8 months). The present study only looked at the 12 months prior to the HR-MDS diagnosis, suggesting that the length of time to diagnosis was potentially longer than identifiable in our study design. However, similar to our study, the most common sign among patients was anemia (77.2%). Rosetti et al. [17] also reported high proportions of patients presenting with thrombocytopenia (66.4%), fatigue (64.3%), and neutropenia (43.3%). Our study also found patients who presented with these signs and symptoms, albeit at different proportions (e.g., 48% of patients first presented with anemia versus 77% reported by Rossetti et al. [17]). This likely reflects different study designs; the study by Rosetti et al. [17] was a review of medical records, whereas we relied on the presence of diagnosis and procedure codes in medical claims. This study and the study by Rosetti et al. [17] also differed in terms of identification periods (1 January 2014 to 30 June 2022 for Rosetti et al.) and follow-up period lengths (minimum of 3 months in Rosetti et al.). As documented in a survey study by DeZern et al., providers often experience difficulty with accurately diagnosing and developing individualized treatment plans for patients with MDS [12].

We found that few patients in this study had evidence of clinical trial participation, although the number of clinical trials available for MDS has increased in recent years [11, 18]. We are unable to assess whether the patients in our study had access to clinical trials or whether they did not meet the often stringent inclusion criteria that are required for participation in clinical trials. Those patients who had evidence of clinical trial participation in our study were younger, had fewer comorbidities, and had fewer unmet SDoH needs than those who did not participate. Patients who did not participate in clinical trials during the study period were more likely to have unmet transportation need, a potential area to target for improved participation. As noted by Duetz et al., clinical trials involving patients with MDS could benefit from the inclusion of quality-of-life measures, which may aid in more thoroughly understanding and characterizing the benefits of these trials in this older population [18]. Future studies may also benefit from better understanding the conversations between patients with HR-MDS and providers around the decision to seek out and participate in clinical trials.

In patients with HR-MDS, eligibility to undergo HSCT should first be evaluated before starting any treatment [19]. We found that nearly 37% of patients in our study were on active treatment regimens, but approximately one-third of the study population did not initiate any treatment during our variable follow-up period. Follow-up time was similar between these treatment groups (median 9.1 months for active treatment [IQR 4.8–17.6 months] and 7.6 months for no treatment [IQR 1.8–22.2 months]). While there were clinical characteristics not measurable in this study (e.g., laboratory results and genetic mutations), patients in these two groups appeared similar in age, prevalence of baseline comorbidities, and underlying SDoH and SES characteristics. If patients were not experiencing severe signs or symptoms, providers may have used active surveillance (“watchful waiting”) in certain patients knowing that responses to HMAs are limited and poor outcomes exist for patients who fail therapy. However, given the low survival rates for these patients, early treatment initiation is often recommended [12].

This study has several strengths, including the large sample size, geographic distribution, and completeness of the claims data; however, several limitations must also be considered. First, claims data are collected for reimbursement rather than for research. The algorithm used in this study incorporated both MDS and HR-MDS diagnosis codes, allowing for more flexibility in cases in which patients progressed from MDS to HR-MDS. However, this algorithm could underestimate patients who progress to HR-MDS if providers do not change the diagnosis codes included in submitted claims. While a large proportion of patients underwent diagnostic testing prior to diagnosis, we did not have access to the results (e.g., mutational analyses) to confirm that these patients met the classification of HR-MDS or their appropriate IPSS-M classification. The study included patients who progressed to HR-MDS from MDS. There were some limitations to this. It may be more difficult to assess the delay in diagnosis for high-risk status among patients with existing MDS since some of the same elements used to identify delay could represent MDS alone. The study presumed that MDS was successfully treated or stable, with progression to high risk represented by additional symptoms such as cytopenia. A total of 666 out of 1710 patients had a prior MDS diagnosis. Median time from first symptom to diagnosis was similar for patients without a previous MDS diagnosis (10 months) and those with prior MDS (11 months). We required at least 1 year of complete health plan claims coverage prior to the patients’ diagnosis; however, the median time from the first sign/symptom to diagnosis was close to 1 year (median 10 months), and the study was unable to confirm whether these were present prior to the baseline period. It is likely that there are patients who have more than 1 year between their first signs/symptoms and diagnosis [17]. Thus, the observation of the earliest symptom was censored at the beginning of the 12-month period: therefore, patients may have had symptoms prior to that time period that were not captured in the study.

There are also limitations inherent in a retrospective cohort study design. Although we performed a robust statistical analysis accounting for several baseline demographic, clinical, SES, and SDoH measures to adjust for confounding variables, findings may still be affected by unobservable confounding. This study provides initial data on the relationship between SDoH measures and MDS; future work should further explore this relationship using multivariable modeling. There was between 14% and 43% missing data for some SES and SDoH characteristics. It is difficult to predict the influence that this missingness could have on the results; however, we did compare baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between those with missing SDoH characteristics and those without. Those with missing SDoH characteristics had a slightly higher NCI Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) (mean 2.9 missing SDoH versus 2.5 not missing SDoH; p < 0.01) and were more likely to be female (45.4% missing SDoH versus 39% not-missing SDoH; p < 0.01). This study only included patients from the USA and may not be generalizable to other countries. Furthermore, the SDoH indices were created for patients in the USA and are likely not generalizable to those outside of the USA. Finally, this database only includes commercial or Medicare Advantage plans and may not be generalizable to all patients with MDS including those who are uninsured.

Similar to the previously described study [17], we found a long time between the occurrence of the first sign/symptom and HR-MDS diagnosis. Efforts should be made to shorten this time, and tools could be developed to improve the ability to promptly follow up with patients who might be at risk. Fewer than half of the patients in this study received guideline-recommended treatment including HSCT or hypomethylating agent. Another gap in care that should be addressed is improving access to clinical trial participation. We found that HSCTs were generally attempted in only the healthiest patients with the lowest level of unmet need. Future clinical research should investigate factors associated with a lower risk of progression to AML and whether this is due to underlying biological changes, medication given for treatment of these comorbidities, or confounding by patient age. A large portion of patients experienced some level of unmet need for modifiable SDoH characteristics such as transportation access and social isolation. This information could be used to develop targeted interventions to improve patient care.
