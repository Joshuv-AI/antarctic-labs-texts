TGF-β inhibition enhances anti-tumor immunity and sensitizes PD-1 blockade therapy of bone marrow-derived myofibroblasts-induced “immunotherapy resistance” tumor model
Authors: Xin Cheng, Lina He, Cong Zhou, Qingli Li, Baiwen Zhang, Jianzheng Wang, Xiaojiao Cheng, Shuiping Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy in certain solid tumors remains a major research focus; yet, the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. Our previous studies demonstrated that subcutaneous tumors formed by a mixture of bone marrow-derived myofibroblasts (BMFs) and tumor cells not only grew faster than those formed by tumor cells alone but were also resistant to ICB therapy. However, the specific mechanism by which BMFs mediate this immune resistance remained unclear. In this study, we established a “BMF + MC38” mixed-cell subcutaneous tumor model to further validate its ICB therapy-resistant phenotype, investigate the mechanism underlying BMF-mediated immune resistance, and explore potential therapeutic strategies for this “immune-excluded” type of tumor. Our findings revealed that “BMF + MC38” mixed-cell subcutaneous tumors lost sensitivity to anti-PD-1 antibody treatment. Furthermore, these mixed-cell subcutaneous tumors exhibited significantly impaired CD8+ T cell infiltration and function, along with an increased proportion of regulatory T cells (Tregs). Higher expression of TGFβ1 was detected in the mixed-cell tumors compared to the MC38 cell tumors. Importantly, either reducing BMF-derived TGFβ1 expression or concurrently targeting PD-1 and TGFβ effectively restored the sensitivity to PD-1 blockade, rescued CD8+ T cell infiltration and function. Additionally, this study revealed that simultaneously targeting PD-1, TGFβ, and VEGFR further suppressed the growth of these mixed-cell subcutaneous tumors. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related death globally in both males and females [1]. Its pathogenesis is complex, potentially involving mutations that disrupt key signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and TGF-β [2]. While colonic polyps and early-stage CRC can be cured by endoscopic or surgical resection, the prognosis for patients with metastatic CRC (mCRC) remains poor despite aggressive systemic treatment [3]. Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, representative agents of ICB therapy, have shown significant efficacy in various advanced solid tumors [4, 5]. However, approximately 85% of CRC patients, who harbor pMMR/MSS tumors, are insensitive to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibody treatment. Even the remaining 15% with dMMR/MSI-H tumors only exhibit partial sensitivity [6]. Consequently, very few mCRC patients benefit from anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, and overcoming this resistance is an urgent priority.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are widely reported to be involved in CRC cell proliferation, cancer stem cell maintenance, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metastasis [7, 8]. CAFs are thought to originate from various sources, including resident tissue fibroblasts, bone marrow, cancer cells (via EMT), and the vascular system [9]. With increasing research in tumor immunology, CAFs have been frequently implicated in modulating the activity and function of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment [10]. Myofibroblastic CAFs (mCAFs), an important CAF subtype characterized by high expression of α-SMA, are extensively studied for their role in establishing and maintaining the tumor microenvironment [11]. Among them, bone marrow-derived myofibroblasts have been reported by multiple studies to be closely associated with tumor progression [12–15]; yet, the specific mechanisms by which BMFs influence tumor and immune cells remain poorly understood.

TGF-β is a crucial cytokine for mCAF formation and is also secreted in large quantities by mCAFs to exert their functions. TGF-β plays a significant role in regulating tumor immunity [16–18]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TGF-β suppresses CD8+ T cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, promotes immune evasion, and that inhibiting TGF-β can enhance anti-tumor immunity [19–22]. However, research on how TGF-β specifically affects CD8+ T cell function in the tumor microenvironment and on combination therapies simultaneously targeting TGF-β and PD-1/PD-L1 is relatively limited.

Our previous studies have confirmed that BMFs interact with tumor cells via the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling loop and the TGF-β signaling pathway, influencing tumor growth and metastasis [23–26]. Concurrently, activation of the Wnt pathway in BMFs promotes PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, thereby inhibiting the infiltration and function of tumor-killing T cells [27]. Furthermore, prior research has established a “BMF + tumor cells” subcutaneous tumor model, which is innately resistant to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [24, 27], providing a robust model for further investigating strategies to overcome ICB therapy resistance. Building upon this foundation, the current study further validates the pathological characteristics and immune microenvironment features of the “BMF + colon cancer cell” subcutaneous model and identifies the critical role of TGF-β in regulating BMF-mediated immunosuppression. This study also evaluates TGF-β inhibition and multi-target combination therapies in this model, aiming to provide new insights for addressing the clinical challenge of immunotherapy resistance in CRC.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

Mouse colorectal cancer cell line MC38 was bought from ATCC. Bone marrow–derived myofibroblasts (BMFs and EGFP+) were isolated from gastric dysplastic tissues of EGFP+ bone marrow-transplanted mice according to our previous method [11]. BMFs within 12 generations were used. BMFTGFβ1−KD was established by TGFβ1-shRNA lentivirus (Shanghai Genomeditech). It was confirmed that mycoplasma was negative in all cell lines. MC38 and BMFs were cultured in DMEM media (C11995500, Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Cat. No. 16140071), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 mg/ml streptomycin (KGY0023, KeyGEN BioTECH). Anti-PD-1 antibody and JS201 were gifts from Shanghai Junshi Biosciences (Shanghai, China). Fruquintinib (HMPL-013) was a gift from Hutchison MediPharma Limited (Shanghai, China).

Tumor syngeneic models

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China) under specific pathogen-free conditions. And the animal experiments were conducted in the sterilized animal room at Shanghai Model Organisms Center. All animal operations and euthanasia were carried out in accordance with the permitted ethical standards by the Institution of Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Model Organisms Center (SMOC-IACUC NO.2022-0023). To develop the Tumor syngeneic models, 1*106 MC38 cells alone or mixed with 1*106 BMFs or BMFsTGFβ1−KD were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flanks of mice, respectively. Tumor volume and body weight of mice were measured every 3 days. After the tumors grew to 50-80mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly grouped (n = 5 or 6) and dosed with isotype antibody (2.5 mg/kg, dissolved in PBS) as a control, anti-PD1 antibody (2.5 mg/kg, dissolved in PBS), and/or JS201 (10 mg/kg, dissolved in PBS) via intraperitoneal administration each 3 days. Fruquintinib was completely dissolved in 0.5% CMC-Na and gavaged as 2.5 mg/kg once a day. Before euthanasia, pentobarbital sodium was injected (P3761, Sigma, 50 mg/kg, i.p.) for anesthesia, and blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture. Pentobarbital sodium was injected (150 mg/kg, i.p.) again through the cardiac route for euthanasia, with confirmation of death secondarily. After the mice were sacrificed, tumor tissues were weighed and harvested for the following experiments. Peripheral blood serum samples and major organs, including the lung, heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys, were also collected to evaluate the biosafety of various treatments. Tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: V = L × W × W/2 (L, the long diameter; W, the shorter diameter), and the “tumor inhibitory rate” of the treatments was calculated as (MCtrl − MTreated)/MCtrl (M, the mass of tumors).

Masson’s trichrome staining and H&E staining

Following formaldehyde fixation and dehydration, the tissues were embedded in paraffin. Tissue paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and washed with deionized water. Masson’s trichrome staining: Sections were sequentially stained using the reagents from the Masson staining kit (S191006, Pinuofei Biotechnology). They were first incubated in Staining Solution 1 overnight. Following a rinse with deionized water, sections were stained with Staining Solution 2 at 65 °C for 5 min and rinsed. This was followed by staining with Staining Solution 3 for 1 min and another rinse. Subsequently, sections were stained with Staining Solution 4 at 65 °C for 10 s and rinsed. Finally, the sections were immersed in glacial acetic acid for 10 s. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) Staining: Sections were sequentially stained using the reagents from the H&E staining kit (P0040, Pinuofei Biotechnology). They were first stained with Staining Solution 1 for 5 min and rinsed with deionized water. This was followed by staining with Staining Solution 2 for 5 s and a rinse, and then with Staining Solution 3 for 5 s, with a final rinse. After Masson’s trichrome staining or H&E staining, the sections were dehydrated, mounted, and scanned using VS200 ASW (Olympus Corporation) for observation.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and then subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% H₂O₂. Sections were blocked with blocking buffer (5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) for one hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies against PD-L1(1:500, 17,952-1-AP, Proteintech), Ki67 (1:200, ab16667, Abcam), perforin (1:100, A0093, Abclonal), or Hif-1α (1:400, 48,085, Cell Signal Technology) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS to remove unbound primary antibodies, the sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, AS014, Abclonal) for 1 h at room temperature. Following another PBS wash, color development was performed using a DAB substrate (SK-4100, Vector Laboratories). Once specific brown staining appeared, the DAB solution was rinsed off with deionized water. Cell nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (G1080, Solarbio). Finally, the sections were dehydrated, mounted, and scanned using VS200 ASW (Olympus Corporation) for observation.

Immunofluorescence staining

Deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking were performed using the same protocol as described for immunohistochemistry. The first primary antibody was applied, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, AS014, Abclonal) and tyramide signal amplification (TSA, XBL2502/2503, Xibailai Biotechnology). Subsequently, the slides were subjected to microwave treatment. Following this, antigen retrieval and blocking procedures were repeated. The process was then also repeated using another primary antibody, an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, and a TSA reagent conjugated to a distinct fluorescent dye. The third primary antibody was applied in the same way as the second primary antibody. Finally, slides were mounted with an antifade mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and scanned using VS200 ASW (Olympus Corporation) for observation. ImageJ was used to analyze the stained cell counts in each region. The primary antibodies used in these experiments: α-SMA (1: 2000, ab124964, Abcam), EGFP (1: 500, ab183735, Abcam), CD8 (1:500, ab217344, Abcam), GZMB (1:200, 44,153, Cell Signal Technology), CD4 (1:1000, ab183685, Abcam), Foxp3 (1:1000, 12,653, Cell Signal Technology), TCF-1 (1:200, 2203, Cell Signal Technology), and TOX (1:200, A08441-2, Boster).

Flow cytometry

The tumor tissues for flow cytometry were collected at day 9. When the differences in tumor volume were not yet significant. Tumor tissues were minced into small pieces and incubated in Hank’s balanced salt solution containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (17,104,019, Thermofisher) and 0.2 mg/mL DNase I (18,047,019, Thermofisher) at 37 °C with shaking for 40 min. The resulting suspension was subsequently filtered through a 70 μm strainer to obtain a single‑cell suspension. Red blood cells were lysed using a lysis buffer (R1010, Solarbio) at room temperature for 10 min, and the reaction was terminated by adding PBS. Cells were blocked with Fc block (20ul/test, 14-9161-71, Invitrogen) at 4 °C for 15 min. After washing with flow cytometry buffer (PBS with 1% FBS) to remove the Fc block, the cell suspension was stained with anti‑CD45‑PerCP‑Cy5.5 (103,131, Biolegend), anti‑CD3‑FITC (100,204, Biolegend), and anti‑CD8‑BV510 (100,751, Biolegend) at 4 °C for 30 min (antibody dosage: 0.5 μg per million cells). Following staining, cells were washed with flow cytometry buffer to remove unbound dyes and finally resuspended in flow cytometry buffer containing DAPI (0.01 μg per million cells,564,907, BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry was performed on BD LSR Fortessa device (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Cell mix-culturing experiment

Mix-culturing and sorting experiment: Equivalent BMFs and MC38 cells were cultured together for 36 h and sorted by EGFP fluorescence of BMFs on BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences). The post-mix-cultured MC38 cells (MC38-PMC) and BMFs (BMF-PMC) were used for RNA extraction. Real-time observation experiment: Equivalent BMFs and MC38 cells were cultured alone or together for 72 h on the Livecyte device (Phase Focus Limited), and pictures of each region were taken on bright field or green fluorescence field each 40 min. For each group, three repeated wells of a 12-well plate were set up, and five regions were selected from each well. The cell number of each region was counted by the Livecyte analysis software.

RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA of MC38 cells, MC38-PMC, BMFs, and BMF-PMC was extracted from the samples using Biozol Reagent (BW-R7311, BIOMIGA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA quality was assessed using Qubit 4.0 (Invitrogen, USA), and integrity was verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel with 500 ng of RNA. The procedure, including mRNA isolation and fragmentation, double-stranded cDNA synthesis, PCR enrichment, and library quantification, was conducted by Xuran Biotech. Final sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq Xplus platform (Illumina, USA).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted from cells or tissue using RNA extraction reagent kit (SB-MR009, Share-bio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the RT SuperMix (R333, Vazyme Biotech) was used for cDNA generation. Quantitative real-time PCR using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme Biotech) was performed on LightCycler480 II Real-Time PCR System (Roche Life Science).

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted from tumor tissues using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing 1 × phosphatase inhibitors (P1046, Beyotime), 1 × protease inhibitors (P1046, Beyotime), and 5 mM EDTA (C0196, Beyotime). The protein concentration of each tissue lysate was quantified using the Bradford assay (P0006, Beyotime), and the samples were equalized to the same concentration using RIPA lysis buffer. Loading buffer was added to the samples, followed by boiling for 5 min. The proteins were separated by electrophoresis on a 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membrane was blocked at room temperature for 1 h with 5% skimmed milk. It was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TGF-β1 (1:1000, ab215715, Abcam), VEGFA (1:1000, A0280, Abclonal), and HSP90β (1:5000, ab203085, Abcam). After washing with TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary (1:10,000, AS014, Abclonal) antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using FemtoDet enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) HRP substrate kit (SB-WB004, Share-bio), and images were captured and analyzed using Amersham Imager 600 (Dynamax Biotech).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of TGF-β1 in the cell culture medium supernatant without FBS of BMF and BMFTGFβ1−KD were determined by using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (JL12223, Jonlnbio). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm by a Multiscan MC reader, and the samples were analyzed by DELTA SOFT II software (BioMetallics).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad software. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

Result

BMFs mediate the resistance of subcutaneous tumors of MC38 cells to PD-1 blocking therapy

To investigate the role of BMFs in MC38 tumor formation in vivo, we established subcutaneous tumors by injecting MC38 cells alone or mixed with BMFs into mice, and harvested tumor tissues on days 7, 14, and 21 (Fig. 1A). Given that BMFs carry EGFP and exhibit characteristics of myofibroblasts, including high expression of α-SMA and TGFβ1 (Fig. S1A), we performed immunofluorescence co-staining for EGFP and α-SMA on paraffin sections from tumors harvested at different time points. Results showed that EGFP expression remained high in MC38 + BMF tumors harvested on day 7, but was minimal in tumors harvested on days 14 and 21 (Fig. 1B). This indicates that a significant proportion of the initially injected BMFs remained viable at day 7 but had largely died off by days 14 and 21. However, analysis of α-SMA+ cell number revealed that the counts of α-SMA+ cells in “MC38 + BMF” mixed-cell tumors on days 14 and 21 were still higher than those in MC38 cell tumors (Fig. 1C, S1B). This suggests that although the injected BMFs gradually died during tumor growth, their impact on α-SMA + myofibroblasts within the tumor microenvironment was sustained.

In order to further explore the long-term effects of injected BMFs on MC38 tumors, we performed Masson’s trichrome staining on day 21 tumor sections. Results showed higher collagen fiber expression in “MC38 + BMF” mixed-cell tumors, further supporting the sustained influence of injected BMFs on mCAF activity within MC38 tumors (Fig. 1D-E). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining of tumors on day 21 revealed higher expression of both PD-L1 and Ki67 in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors (Fig. 1D, F-G), suggesting that BMFs may promote tumor cell proliferation and influence the response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy.

To investigate the impact of BMFs on immune cell function in MC38 tumors, we treated mice bearing either MC38 cell or MC38 + BMF mixed-cell subcutaneous tumors with anti-PD-1 antibody (Fig. 1H). Results showed that MC38 cell tumors were sensitive to anti-PD-1 therapy, whereas MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors were highly resistant and grew faster than MC38 cell tumors (Fig. 1I–L). These results indicate that incorporating exogenous BMFs during MC38 tumor establishment not only promotes tumor growth but also alters the anti-tumor immune response, rendering the tumors insensitive to anti-PD-1 antibody therapy.

Weaker anti-tumor immunity of CD8+ T cells is exhibited in the subcutaneous tumors of MC38 cells mixed with BMFs

To further characterize changes in the immune microenvironment of MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors, we built the mouse syngeneic tumor model again and analyzed CD8+ T cell infiltration by flow cytometry at day 9 from tumor implantation (Fig. 2A). Results showed that the number of CD8+ T cells was lower in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors compared to MC38 cell tumors, and anti-PD-1 treatment did not increase CD8+ T cell numbers in the mixed-cell tumors (Fig. 2B-C). To assess CD8+ T cell activity, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining for CD8 and GZMB (Granzyme B), and immunohistochemistry for perforin on tumor sections. The number of CD8+ GZMB + cells and the expression level of perforin were lower in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors compared to MC38 cell tumors. While anti-PD-1 treatment significantly increased the number of CD8+ GZMB + cells and perforin expression in MC38 cell tumors, it had no significant effect on these parameters in the mixed-cell tumors (Fig. 2D-E, S2A-B). These results indicate that incorporating exogenous BMFs significantly reduces both the number and activity of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment and diminishes their responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy.

Considering the important role of Tregs in anti-tumor immunity [28, 29], we performed CD4 and Foxp3 co-staining on tumor tissues. The number of Tregs was higher in MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors than in MC38 cell tumors. Although anti-PD-1 treatment significantly increased Treg numbers in both tumor types, Treg numbers remained higher in the mixed-cell tumors (Fig. 2F-G). These results suggest that the incorporation of BMFs increases the infiltration of immunosuppressive Tregs. T cell exhaustion is another critical factor affecting CD8+ T cell function and anti-tumor immunity [30–32]. We therefore performed CD8, TCF-1, and TOX co-staining on tumor tissues. The proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells, particularly terminally exhausted subsets, was higher in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors. Anti-PD-1 treatment significantly reduced the proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells in both tumor models, most notably the terminally exhausted population, but the proportion remained higher in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors (Fig. 2H-I). These results indicate that exogenous BMFs promote CD8+ T cell exhaustion, further substantiating their suppressive effect on CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity.

Collectively, these results, examining CD8+ T cell number, activity, exhaustion, and Treg infiltration, demonstrate that incorporating BMFs during MC38 tumor establishment significantly alters the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings provide a preliminary explanation for the weakened anti-tumor immunity and insensitivity to anti-PD-1 antibody observed in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors.

Enhanced TGFβ signaling is detected in MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors

To identify the specific factors derived from BMFs that influence MC38 cells, we established a co-culture system. We dynamically monitored the effect of BMFs on MC38 proliferation and, after a period of co-culture, used flow cytometry to sort the co-cultured MC38 cells and BMFs for analysis of gene expression changes compared to their pre-co-culture states (Fig. 3A). Dynamic monitoring revealed that MC38 cells co-cultured with BMFs proliferated faster than those cultured alone (Fig. 3B-C, S3A), with MC38 cells often growing in a pattern surrounding the BMFs (S3A). To further investigate the impact of BMFs on MC38 cells during co-culture, we performed RNA-seq analysis on MC38 cells after co-culture (MC38-PMC) versus MC38 cells cultured alone. KEGG pathway analysis of genes upregulated in MC38-PMC showed significant enrichment for terms related to extracellular matrix (ECM), pathways in cancer, and the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 3D, S3B). Further, diseases KEGG analysis of the upregulated genes in MC38-PMC also revealed significant enrichment for terms such as tumor progression and angiogenesis, alongside numerous other cancer-related pathways (Fig. S3C). We then generated heatmaps displaying genes with significant expression changes in MC38-PMC (both upregulated and downregulated) associated with ECM, pathways in cancer, and the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 3E). Given the broad scope of ECM and pathways in cancer, and the reported crucial role of the TGF-β pathway in tumor immune regulation, we focused our subsequent investigation on the role of the TGF-β signaling pathway in the BMF-MC38 interaction.

Analysis of the human COAD dataset in GEPIA2 revealed a significant positive correlation between TGFβ1 and ACTA2 expression [33] (Fig. 3F). Considering our previous validation of high α-SMA (encoded by ACTA2) expression in MC38 + BMF mixed cell subcutaneous tumors, we detected TGFβ1 protein levels in tumor tissues. The results showed that TGFβ1 levels were significantly higher in MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors compared to MC38 cell tumors (Fig. 3G). Further measurement of TGFβ1 mRNA in MC38 cells and BMFs after co-culture showed no significant change in TGFβ1 expression in MC38 cells, but a significant increase in BMFs compared to those cultured alone (Fig. 3H). This indicates that BMFs are likely the primary source of elevated TGFβ1 in MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors.

Subsequent analysis in the GSCA COAD dataset [34] showed that high TGFβ1 expression correlated with reduced infiltration of effector/memory CD8+ T cells and increased expression of iTregs and T cell exhaustion markers. These bioinformatic findings are consistent with our previous experimental results (Fig. 2C–I). Collectively, these results suggest that BMF-derived TGFβ1 is a key factor contributing to impaired anti-tumor immunity in MC38 subcutaneous tumors.

TGFβ1 acts as a significant factor in the anti-tumor immunity suppression effect induced by BMFs

To further investigate the impact of BMF-derived TGFβ1 on anti-tumor immunity in MC38 cell subcutaneous tumors, we established a stable BMF cell line with knockdown of TGFβ1 (BMFTGFβ1−KD) (Fig. 4A). We then treated mice bearing subcutaneous tumors formed by MC38 cells mixed with either BMF or BMFTGFβ1−KD with anti-PD-1 antibody (Fig. 4B). Results showed that tumors formed by MC38 + BMFTGFβ1−KD cells regained sensitivity to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, although the sizes of untreated MC38 + BMF and MC38 + BMFTGFβ1−KD mixed cell tumors showed no significant difference (Fig. 4C–F).

Flow cytometric analysis of the tumors revealed a higher number of infiltrating CD8+ T cells in MC38 + BMFTGFβ1−KD mixed cell tumors compared to MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors. Furthermore, anti-PD-1 treatment significantly increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in MC38 + BMFTGFβ1−KD tumors, but not in MC38 + BMF tumors (Fig. 4G-H). Immunofluorescence co-staining (CD8/GZMB, CD4/FOXP3, CD8/TCF-1/TOX) on tumor sections further demonstrated that knocking down TGFβ1 in BMFs increased the number of CD8+ GZMB + cells, decreased the number of Tregs, and reduced the proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells in MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors. After anti-PD-1 treatment, MC38 + BMFTGFβ1−KD tumors exhibited a stronger anti-tumor immune response, characterized by higher numbers of CD8+ GZMB + cells, fewer Tregs, and a lower proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4I-N).

These results demonstrate that BMF-derived TGFβ1 suppresses CD8+ T cell infiltration and activity, promotes CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and enhances the infiltration of immunosuppressive Tregs in MC38 subcutaneous tumors, ultimately leading to weakened anti-tumor immunity and insensitivity to anti-PD-1 therapy.

TGFβ/PD-1 blocking relieves the anti-tumor immunity suppression effect induced by BMFs

JS201 is a recombinant bifunctional protein developed by Junshi Biosciences that simultaneously targets PD-1 (via the Toripalimab antibody) and TGF-β (via the TGFβRII extracellular domain) [35]. To further explore the impact of TGF-β in the tumor microenvironment on anti-tumor immunity in MC38 tumors, we treated mice bearing either MC38 cell or MC38 + BMF mixed-cell subcutaneous tumors with anti-PD-1 antibody or JS201 (Fig. 5A). For MC38 cell tumors, the efficacy of anti-PD-1 and JS201 was not significantly different. However, for MC38 + BMF mixed tumors, JS201 showed excellent efficacy, whereas the anti-PD-1 antibody was ineffective (Fig. 5B–E). These results indicate that for our PD-1 antibody-resistant MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumor model, concurrent blockade of TGF-β restores sensitivity to PD-1 pathway inhibition.

Flow cytometric analysis of MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors revealed greater CD8+ T cell infiltration in JS201-treated tumors compared to those treated with anti-PD-1 antibody alone (Fig. 5F-G). Immunofluorescence of tumor tissue sections showed that JS201 treatment resulted in more CD8+ GZMB + cells, fewer Tregs, and a lower proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells compared to anti-PD-1 monotherapy (Fig. 5H–K). This indicates that blocking TGF-β in the MC38 + BMF tumor microenvironment promotes CD8+ T cell infiltration and activity, reduces CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and decreases the infiltration of immunosuppressive Tregs.

These results demonstrate that dual blockade of TGF-β and PD-1 effectively enhances anti-tumor immunity and inhibits tumor growth in the MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumor model.

Combination therapy of TGFβ, PD-1, and VEGF blocking significantly inhibits tumor growth

It was reported that treatment with a recombinant protein combining a non-immunosuppressive anti-CD4 antibody and the TGFβRII extracellular domain (4 T-Trap) can induce tissue hypoxia and increase VEGFA production in tumors [36]. To investigate whether JS201 treatment similarly induces tumor hypoxia or upregulates VEGFA expression, we assessed Hif1α and VEGFA levels in JS201-treated tumors. In MC38 cell tumors, JS201 treatment resulted in significantly larger Hif1α-positive areas (hypoxic regions) and higher VEGFA expression compared to the control antibody or anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy groups (Fig. 6A–C). In contrast, for MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors, no significant differences in Hif1α-positive areas or VEGFA expression were observed among the control antibody, anti-PD-1 antibody, or JS201 treatment groups (Fig. 6A–C). We then measured VEGFA mRNA in MC38 cells and BMFs after co-culture and found that VEGFA expression was significantly increased in both MC38 cells and BMFs following co-culture compared to those cultured alone (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that JS201 treatment induces intratumoral hypoxia and subsequent VEGFA upregulation in MC38 cell tumors, potentially explaining the comparable efficacy observed between anti-PD-1 monotherapy and JS201 in this model (Fig. 5B–E). For MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors, the interaction between MC38 cells and BMFs maintains a constitutively high level of VEGFA expression. Consequently, JS201 treatment does not further increase hypoxic regions or VEGFA levels in this model.

Given the high baseline VEGFA expression in MC38 + BMF mixed cell tumors, we treated them with the VEGFR small molecule inhibitor fruquintinib in combination with JS201 (Fig. 6E). The combination of JS201 and fruquintinib demonstrated significantly superior efficacy compared to either agent alone (Fig. 6F-I). Further immunohistochemical staining for Ki67 in these tumors revealed that fruquintinib treatment significantly reduced Ki67 expression, indicating effective inhibition of tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 6J-K). These results demonstrate that for MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors, adding VEGF pathway blockade to combined PD-1/TGF-β targeting further suppresses tumor cell growth and yields improved therapeutic outcomes.

We subsequently evaluated the safety profile of JS201 and fruquintinib treatment in mice. Throughout the treatment period, continuous monitoring of mouse body weight revealed that neither JS201 nor fruquintinib, either alone or in combination, caused significant weight loss (Fig. S4A). Furthermore, analysis of key peripheral blood cell counts and biochemical indices showed no significant differences among the treatment groups for parameters including WBC, HGB, PLA, ALT, AST, TBIL, DBIL, Creatinine, CK, CK-MB, and LDH (Fig. S4B). H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from these mice revealed healthy morphology without obvious abnormal pathological findings across all treatment groups (Fig. S4C). These results indicate that, in C57BL/6 mice, treatment with JS201 and/or fruquintinib at the administered concentrations did not cause significant adverse health effects and exhibited a favorable safety profile.

Discussion

In this study, we confirmed that the subcutaneous tumor model formed by mouse BMFs mixed with MC38 cells is resistant to anti-PD-1 antibody therapy. We found that BMF + MC38 mixed cell tumors exhibited higher PD-L1 expression and less CD8+ T cell infiltration and activity compared to MC38 cell tumors. Knocking down TGFβ1 in BMFs sensitized BMF + MC38 mixed cell tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy, concomitant with increased CD8+ T cell numbers and cytotoxic activity. Treatment of the bifunctional TGF-β/PD-1 targeting antibody effectively suppressed the growth of BMF + MC38 mixed-cell tumors, and this effect was further enhanced by combination with the VEGFR inhibitor fruquintinib.

Our previous research had established that subcutaneous tumor models formed by BMFs mixed with various cancer cell lines (colon, gastric, and lung) grow faster and are insensitive to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibody therapy compared to tumor cell-alone models [23–27]. This study further validates these findings specifically for the BMF + MC38 mixed cell tumor model. Building upon this, we comprehensively assessed CD8+ T cell number, activity, exhaustion status, and the number of immunosuppressive Tregs within the BMF + MC38 mixed cell tumor microenvironment. We found that BMF + MC38 mixed cell tumors exhibited significantly reduced CD8+ T cell numbers and cytotoxic activity, alongside a marked increase in Treg numbers and the proportion of terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells. These results provide deeper insight into the cellular biological mechanisms underlying BMF-mediated tumor immune resistance.

The interaction between BMFs and tumor cells has been a focus of our prior work. Our previous studies demonstrated that IL-6 derived from BMFs promotes TGFβ1 production by tumor cells, which in turn further stimulates IL-6 secretion from BMFs, forming a positive feedback loop that may be a key mechanism for BMF-promoted tumor growth and metastasis [23–25]. In the current study, we found that co-culture of MC38 cells with BMFs significantly increased TGFβ1 expression specifically in BMFs, not in MC38 cells. Subsequent knockdown of TGFβ1 in BMFs, followed by the mixed-cell tumor experiment, revealed that reducing the production of BMF-derived TGFβ1 enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and activity in the mixed-cell tumors and alleviated the BMF-mediated resistance to anti-PD-1 antibody. This reaffirms the critical role of BMF-derived TGFβ1 in regulating anti-tumor immunity.

Furthermore, in this study, we used JS201, the recombinant protein simultaneously targeting PD-1 and TGF-β, to treat MC38 + BMF mixed-cell tumors. JS201 demonstrated excellent efficacy, significantly increasing the number and activity of CD8+ T cells while reducing the proportion of terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment. Previous studies have also highlighted the therapeutic potential of co-targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and TGF-β in tumors insensitive to PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy [16, 20, 21]. Also, there are some other recombinant bifunctional proteins that simultaneously targets PD-1/PD-L1 and TGF-β, which exhibit remarkable efficacy both in clinical and preclinical studies, such as M7824, SHR-1701, YM101, and so on [37–41]. Additionally, our study found that MC38 + BMF mixed tumors express high levels of VEGFA, and combining JS201 with the VEGFR inhibitor fruquintinib led to further suppression of tumor growth. Recent studies have also indicated that simultaneously targeting PD-1/PD-L1, TGF-β, and VEGF can more effectively inhibit the growth of immunotherapy-insensitive tumors [36, 42]. These findings, together with our results, provide a theoretical basis for triple-targeting therapy in PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy-resistant tumors, offering new treatment strategies for patients with immunotherapy-resistant cancers.

Nevertheless, both our mechanistic investigations and therapeutic explorations were performed in our mouse models. The extent to which these findings recapitulate human pathophysiology, as well as whether these combination regimens exert therapeutic efficacy in patients resistant to PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy, remains to be validated by further preclinical and clinical studies. In addition, in the present study, we did not conduct an in-depth investigation into the downstream signaling pathways of TGFβ. Whether the canonical TGFβ-Smad signaling pathway or other non-canonical TGFβ signaling pathways play a predominant role in the immunosuppressive process mediated by BMFs will be the main direction of our future research. Besides, we preliminarily observed that enhanced VEGF signaling may also contribute substantially to BMF-driven tumor progression in this study. However, we did not conduct an in-depth mechanistic dissection of this pathway. Instead, we only treated our immune-resistant mouse model using a small molecule VEGFR inhibitor. Although favorable therapeutic efficacy was achieved, there is insufficient mechanistic evidence to fully support the observed effects. The role of the VEGF signaling pathway in BMF‑mediated tumor promotion, as well as the crosstalk and synergistic effects between VEGF and TGFβ signaling pathways in the tumor microenvironment, will constitute another important direction for our future investigation.

In summary, this study validates the “MC38 + BMF” mixed cell subcutaneous tumor as a model resistant to anti-PD-1 therapy and identifies a crucial role for BMF-derived TGFβ1 in regulating tumor immunity. We also demonstrate that multi-targeted therapy against PD-1, TGF-β, and VEGF significantly inhibits tumor growth in this model. Our findings not only provide a naturally immunotherapy-resistant subcutaneous tumor model for evaluating novel immunotherapeutic agents but also establish the theoretical feasibility of simultaneously targeting PD-1/PD-L1, TGF-β, and VEGF for tumors resistant to PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy. This offers new directions for future clinical research and potential new treatment options for a broad range of cancer patients.
