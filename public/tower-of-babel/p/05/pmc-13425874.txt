Communication skills and organizational silence as predictors of perioperative competence in operating room nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hee Eun Kim, Sangjin Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to examine the collective associations of teamwork, communication skills, and organizational silence on the perioperative competence of operating room (OR) nurses and identify the key determinants of their competence.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted with 160 nurses from five hospitals in South Korea. Data were collected using self-report questionnaires on teamwork, communication skills, organizational silence, and perioperative competence. Multiple regression analysis was performed to identify the predictors of competence.ResultsThe multiple regression model was statistically significant (F = 10.82, p <.001), explaining 45% of the variance in perioperative competence. While teamwork was significant in univariate analysis, it was no longer a significant predictor in the regression model, in which communication skills (β = 0.47, p <.001), along with clinical career in the OR (β = 0.22 to 0.58), served as a significant positive predictor. Organizational silence (β = -0.19, p =.008) was a significant negative predictor.ConclusionThe findings highlight that clinical career in the OR and communication skills are essential determinants for enhancing perioperative competence, while organizational silence poses a significant barrier. These results underscore the critical need for developing management strategies that foster professional tenure and open communication while actively mitigating organizational silence to elevate performance in the OR.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Operating room nursing, Clinical competence, Communication

Introduction

The rapid evolution of surgical technology and sub-specialization has fundamentally reshaped the operating room (OR), and thereby generated demands for nurses to become highly specialized professionals who are equipped with complex technical skills [1–3]. Given the nature of perioperative nursing, nurses must acquire knowledge and skills related to various surgical procedures, instruments, equipment, and the specific preferences of surgeons [3, 4]. In addition, they are required to prepare for emergencies in highly stressful situations [5]. Consequently, perioperative competence encompasses not only the technical ability to facilitate complex surgeries but also comprehensive nontechnical skills such as communication, leadership, situation awareness, teamwork, and decision-making, to ensure swift and accurate performance within the team [2, 4].

Ideally, the OR staff should operate as a cohesive team, and this necessitates close multidisciplinary cooperation. Such teamwork is enhanced through open communication and a mutual understanding of other team members’ verbal and nonverbal cues [6]. From a conceptual standpoint, although teamwork represents a broad, perception-based organizational climate, it is fundamentally operationalized and channeled through individual behavioral communication skills [7, 8]. Nevertheless, the OR environment is characterized by time pressures and varying work patterns, which creates numerous barriers to effective communication [9]. In particular, the distinct perspectives of the three core professional groups—surgeons, anesthesiologists, and nurses—regarding patient information and surgical processes can hinder interprofessional communication and teamwork [10]. Although previous studies have indicated that positive communication behaviors and information sharing enhance team-based performance and efficiency [7, 8], organizational silence presents a considerable barrier [11]. Organizational silence refers to the collective practice of intentionally withholding thoughts or opinions on organizational improvements [12], and it acts as a structural and psychological inhibitor that directly suppresses interpersonal interactions and compromises psychological safety [13]. Given these dynamics, organizational silence may act as a barrier to open communication, and thereby presents a negative relationship with OR nurses’ perioperative competence.

Despite the importance of these factors, reports in the literature have largely focused on isolated variables, such as teamwork-enhancement programs [14] and comparisons of communication skills [15]. Few studies have integrated teamwork, communication skills, and organizational silence to examine their collective associations with perioperative competence in the specific context of the OR. By establishing a comprehensive conceptual framework (Fig. 1), this study explicitly considered the potential conceptual overlap between individual communication competencies and the broader perception of teamwork. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to identify the relationships between teamwork, communication skills, and organizational silence in OR nurses and to determine the statistical predictors of perioperative competence. These findings provide foundational data for improving the work environment and enhancing the professional perioperative competence of OR nurses.

Methods

Study design

This descriptive cross-sectional survey was designed to examine the factors associated with perioperative competence among OR nurses, specifically focusing on the roles of teamwork, communication skills, and organizational silence.

Participants

Participants were OR nurses employed at five hospitals located in U Metropolitan City, South Korea. Inclusion criteria were nurses currently working in the OR who directly participated in surgeries and provided written informed consent. Nurses with less than one year of clinical experience in the OR were excluded because they were in a period of adaptation, which could limit the accurate assessment of teamwork, communication skills, organizational silence, and perioperative competence.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software for multiple regression analysis. To maintain a conservative approach during the initial research design, the sample size was powered based on a medium effect size of 0.15, a significance level (alpha) of 0.05, a power (1-beta) of 0.80, and 18 potential predictors (four independent variables and 14 general characteristics), which yielded a minimum required sample size of 150. In total, 180 questionnaires were distributed, accounting for a potential dropout rate of 20%. After excluding 20 incomplete responses, 160 questionnaires were included in the final analysis. Although a more parsimonious model utilizing fewer final variables was ultimately executed based on univariate pre-screening (as detailed in the Results section), the final sample of 160 provides more than adequate statistical power for the analyzed model.

Instruments

General characteristics

The general characteristics included 14 items: age, sex, marital status, religion, education level, clinical career in the OR, work pattern, job satisfaction, position, monthly income, working hours, preceptor experience, number of hospital beds, and primary surgical department.

Teamwork

Perceptions of teamwork were measured using the TeamSTEPPS® Teamwork Perceptions Questionnaire (T-TPQ) [16]. The tool consists of 35 items across five dimensions (seven items per dimension): team structure, team leadership, situation monitoring, mutual support, and communication. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 35 to 175, with higher scores indicating a more positive perception of teamwork. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88-0.95 [16], and 0.96 in this study.

Communication skills

Communication skills were measured using the operating room professional communication questionnaire [17]. The scale comprises 41 items, and items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The total score ranges from 41 to 205, with higher total scores indicating better communication skills. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88 in the original study [17] and 0.85 in this study.

Organizational silence

Organizational silence was measured using the Hospital Nurses’ Silence Behavior Scale (HNSBS) [18]. The instrument consists of 31 items across two subdomains: silence situations (16 items) and silence motivations (15 items). The silence situations were measured by assessing how often nurses remain silent in specific contexts, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Conversely, silence motivations (reasons for withholding opinions) were measured on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 31 to 155, with higher scores indicating a higher level of organizational silence. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92 in the original study [18], and 0.94 in this study.

Perioperative competence

Perioperative competence was measured using the Perceived Perioperative Competence Scale-Revised (PPCS-R) [19]. The scale consists of 40 items across six domains: foundational knowledge and skills (nine items), leadership (eight items), collaboration (six items), proficiency (six items), empathy (five items), and professional development (six items). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The total score ranges from 40 to 200, with higher scores indicating higher competence. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.96 in the original study [19] and 0.96 in this study.

Data collection

Data were collected from August 18 to September 4, 2023, after explaining the study purpose and methods to the nursing departments and OR managers of the participating hospitals (one tertiary hospital and four general hospitals in U Metropolitan City) and obtaining their permission. The researcher visited the sites, explained the questionnaires to the nurses, and distributed 180 questionnaires to those who voluntarily agreed to participate. A total of 160 questionnaires were analyzed after excluding 20 incomplete responses. The participants were informed that there would be no disadvantages or risks associated with participation and that they could withdraw at any time. To ensure anonymity and confidentiality, participants were instructed to place their completed questionnaires in sealed envelopes, which were collected directly by the researcher. The survey took approximately 20 min to complete, and participants received a small gift.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ulsan University Hospital (Approval No. 2023-06-003-001). Data collection commenced after obtaining cooperation from the nursing departments and OR managers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the purpose, methods, potential benefits, and risks of the study. The consent form included information regarding the protection of personal information, the right to refuse participation, the use of data solely for research purposes, and the data retention policy (storage for three years post-study, followed by permanent destruction via shredding).

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA); general characteristics and study variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation). The levels of teamwork, communication skills, organizational silence, and perioperative competence are presented as means, standard deviations, minima, and maxima. Differences in variables according to general characteristics were analyzed using independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA, followed by Scheffé’s test for post hoc analysis. Correlations between teamwork, communication skills, organizational silence, and perioperative competence were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Statistical predictors of perioperative competence were identified using multiple regression analysis.

Results

Differences in perioperative competence according to general characteristics

The largest age group was 30 to < 40 years (n = 71, 44.4%), and the majority were female (n = 137, 85.6%). Regarding marital status, 60.6% were unmarried. Most participants reported not having a religious affiliation (67.5%), and the majority held a bachelor’s degree (70.6%). Regarding clinical careers in the OR, 22.5% had 3 to < 6 years of experience. Shift workers accounted for 53.1%. Perioperative job satisfaction was mostly reported as neither (45.0%) or satisfied (45.0%). The most common job position was staff nurse (58.8%). Regarding hospital size, the largest proportion worked in hospitals with ≥ 800 (61.2%), whereas the smallest group worked in hospitals with 400 to < 800 beds (16.3%).

Perioperative competence differed markedly according to age, marital status, clinical career in the OR, working pattern, and number of hospital beds. Specifically, Scheffé’s post hoc analysis revealed that nurses aged ≥ 40 years demonstrated significantly higher competence (153.50 ± 28.49) than those < 30 years (139.82 ± 19.73; F = 4.97, p =.008). Married nurses scored significantly higher (151.71 ± 23.04) than unmarried nurses (142.81 ± 20.59; t = -2.55, p =.012). Nurses with 10 to < 15 years of clinical career in the OR demonstrated highest competence (156.16 ± 20.77), which was significantly higher than those with 1 to < 3 years (137.36 ± 19.58) and 3 to < 6 years (139.03 ± 19.07; F = 4.99, p <.001). Non-shift workers scored significantly higher than shift workers (t = -2.57, p =.011). A significant difference was also observed based on the number of hospital beds (F = 5.18, p =.007). Post hoc analysis indicated that nurses working in smaller hospitals with fewer than 400 beds had significantly higher competence (156.42 ± 20.12) than those working in large-scale hospitals with ≥ 800 beds (143.28 ± 22.25) (Table 1).

Levels of research variables

The mean score for teamwork was 134.09 ± 19.22 points (out of 175); among the subdomains, team structure had the highest scores (27.94 ± 4.45), while team leadership scored the lowest (24.69 ± 5.28). The mean score for communication skills was 152.13 ± 13.27 points (out of 205). The mean score for organizational silence was 98.85 ± 19.91 points (out of 155), with motivation subdomain scoring higher (51.23 ± 10.93) than the situation subdomain (47.62 ± 11.14). Finally, the mean perioperative competence score was 146.32 ± 21.95 points (out of 200). Among its subdomains, because the number of items differs across subdomains, item-mean scores were compared: collaboration had the highest item mean (23.92 ± 3.39; item mean = 3.99), whereas leadership had the lowest (24.63 ± 7.07; item mean = 3.08) (Table 2).

Correlations among research variables

A Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between the study variables. Perioperative competence showed a significant positive correlation with teamwork (r = .40, p <.001), communication skills (r = .53, p <.001), while displaying a significant negative correlation with organizational silence (r = -.27, p <.001). Organizational silence was significantly and negatively correlated with teamwork (r = -.44, p <.001), but showed no statistically significant correlation with communication skill (r = -.08, p =.300). In contrast, teamwork showed a strong positive correlation with communication skills (r = .54, p < .001) (Table 3).

Statistical predictors of perioperative competence

To identify the statistical predictors of perioperative competence, multiple regression analysis was conducted. Independent variables included general characteristics that showed significant differences in the univariate analysis (age, marital status, clinical career in the OR, work pattern, and number of hospital beds), as well as the main variables significantly correlated with perioperative competence (teamwork, communication skills, and organizational silence). Prior to analysis, the assumptions of the regression model were verified. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 1.74, close to 2, indicating no autocorrelation among the error terms. No serious multicollinearity was detected, as the tolerance values ranged from 0.17 to 0.67 (all > 0.1) and the variance inflation factors (VIF) ranged from 1.48 to 5.75 (all < 10). The regression model was statistically significant (F = 10.82, p <.001) and explained approximately 45% of the total variance in perioperative competence (Adjusted R2 = 0.45).

The analysis revealed that clinical career in the OR, communication skills, and organizational silence were significant predictors of perioperative competence, whereas teamwork (p =.326), age, marital status, work pattern, and hospital size lost their statistical significance in the adjusted model. The clinical career in the OR dummy variables were all statistically significant positive predictors of perioperative competence compared to the reference group (1 to < 3 years), with the magnitude of the adjusted effect generally increasing across tenure categories: 3 to < 6 years (B = 11.44, β = 0.22, p =.008), 6 to < 10 years (B = 16.81, β = 0.31, p =.001), 10 to 15 years (B = 23.39, β = 0.39, p <.001), and ≥ 15 years (B = 31.46, β = 0.58, p <.001). Furthermore, higher communication skills (B = 0.77, β = 0.47, p <.001) were associated with higher competence, while greater organizational silence (B = -0.21, β = -0.19, p =.008) was a significant negative predictor (Table 4).

Discussion

The most notable finding of this study was that clinical career in the OR, communication skills, and organizational silence were predictors of perioperative competence. Regarding descriptive levels, the participants demonstrated a critical gap between their foundational clinical skills and their capacity to exercise situational leadership or pursue advanced professional development. This deficit may be related to the high-pressure clinical realities of South Korean OR hierarchies, wherein a Confucian-influenced professional culture that emphasizes internal harmony over open confrontation possibly suppresses nursing autonomy. Because cultural factors were not measured in this study, this interpretation remains speculative. This underscores the urgent need for targeted training programs beyond technical skills to empower nurses in safety management and team coordination. Crucially, this relationship between experience and communicative capability is consistent with Benner’s ‘From Novice to Expert’ framework [20]. According to Benner, transitioning to an expert position with an intuitive situational grasp requires more than the passive accumulation of clinical tenure; it demands active interpersonal dynamics to overcome organizational constraints. Our findings demonstrate that clinical career duration alone does not guarantee perioperative competence; rather, it relies heavily on tacit knowledge as well as immediate clinical judgment that is acquired through repeated exposure to diverse surgical cases [21]. However, relying solely on natural tenure accumulation poses inherent risks to patient safety, which makes the traditional ‘see one, do one, teach one’ paradigm obsolete [22]. To safely accelerate this transition, healthcare institutions must deploy stage-tailored educational strategies, such as high-fidelity simulation training, which compresses the learning curve in a risk-free environment [22, 23].

In this study, communication skills were identified as key predictors of competence. In an OR, poor communication can lead to serious accidents; therefore, clear verbal and nonverbal communication is essential [24]. This finding aligns with the previous literature, which suggests that high-quality interactions among professionals directly affect patient care and safety [25]. In practice, effective communication in an OR is hindered frequently by time pressure [9], noise and masks [26], and a hierarchical culture [27]. Our findings suggest that nurses with strong communication skills can overcome these environmental barriers and demonstrate greater competence. Interestingly, although teamwork was correlated with competence, it was not a significant predictor in the regression model. This statistical discrepancy may reflect a conceptual overlap between the two constructs, as the shared variance between teamwork and communication skills was largely accounted for by communication skills after they were included in the model. However, as no formal mediation analysis has been conducted, this interpretation remains tentative. Clinically, this may be because OR staff frequently work as individuals who perform separate tasks in the same room rather than functioning as a team [24]. This environment reflects the concept of silos, where although professionals from different fields (e.g., surgeons, anesthesiologists, and nurses) work in the same space, they have little functional interdependence [28]. This unique contextual factor implies that even in a good team environment, specific and clear communication skills are directly related to individual performance. Therefore, training should focus on active strategies, such as simulation-based programs and structured briefing/debriefing protocols, to improve these essential skills.

Organizational silence was a significant negative predictor of perioperative competence. In contrast to our conceptual framework, however, organizational silence was not significantly correlated with communication skills. This suggests that it may undermine perioperative competence through a direct pathway—or via teamwork, with which it was strongly associated—rather than by suppressing communication per se. This unexpected finding warrants further investigation. Notably, the motivation for silence was largely prosocial, with the desire to preserve harmonious interpersonal relationships as the primary factor. However, this well-intended reticence in the face of ambiguity or errors not only threatens patient safety [29] but also hinders nurses’ ability to perform proactively. Within the broader patient safety literature, this dynamic is directly linked to the concept of psychological safety—the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking [30]. In hierarchical healthcare environments, a lack of psychological safety suppresses essential speaking behaviors, and thereby transforms potential learning opportunities or error-correction moments into silent non-events [11, 29]. Therefore, it is crucial to improve a rigid and vertical organizational culture. Hospital leadership must foster a culture of open communication that ensures psychological safety [30] and allows nurses to voice concerns freely without fear of disrupting relationships, while rewarding transparency through non-punitive speaking-up policies.

Nonetheless, this study had some limitations. First, the sample was drawn from five hospitals in a single metropolitan area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographic regions or healthcare settings. Metropolitan hospitals typically possess greater clinical resources and more standardized training systems than rural hospitals. Additionally, as the participating institutions were primarily large-scale hospitals, the results may not be fully applicable to smaller community hospitals. Therefore, future studies should use more geographically diverse samples that encompass various hospital sizes. Second, the use of self-reported questionnaires may introduce response bias, such as social desirability bias, whereby participants may overestimate their competence or underreport silence. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of definitive causal relationships. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and objective multisource performance metrics to validate these associations across wider geographical and institutional contexts.

Conclusions

This study highlights that perioperative competence is not solely a product of individual clinical experience but is also significantly shaped by interpersonal dynamics and organizational culture. The findings demonstrate that while clinical career duration is a foundational predictor of competence, communication skills and organizational silence act as critical enhancers or barriers. While experience takes time to accumulate, communication and silence are factors that can be improved through active intervention. Therefore, to improve OR nursing competence, hospitals need to update their educational and management strategies. Instead of relying solely on traditional apprenticeship, education should focus on practical training or simulation to teach specific communication skills. On the management side, it is essential to address prosocial silence, where nurses hesitate to speak up to maintain harmony. Managers should create a safe environment fostering psychological safety using non-punitive speaking-up policies so that nurses view voicing concerns as a professional responsibility, not an interpersonal conflict.
