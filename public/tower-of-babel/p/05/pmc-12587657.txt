Shaping the physiotherapy process through health professions education: a qualitative study from Türkiye
Authors: Guler Atalay, Ugur Ugrak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe physiotherapy and rehabilitation process in Türkiye is carried out by “specialist physicians” and “physiotherapists”. This phenomenological qualitative research aimed to explore how health professions education, particularly that of physiotherapists and referring physicians, shapes the physiotherapy process in Türkiye, through the experiences and perspectives of key stakeholders.MethodsA purposive sampling method was employed to select the study group. Data were collected from a heterogeneous group of 12 individuals with extensive experience related to the research phenomenon. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were used for data collection. Verified transcripts were analyzed using an inductive approach that involved content, thematic, and descriptive analyses. The NVivo 10 software program was used for qualitative analysis.ResultsThe analyses identified three main themes with subthemes. A total of 140 meaningful expressions were coded. The main themes were identified as “independent practice,” “education,” and “treatment process.” Despite physiotherapists’ competence in managing treatment for diagnosed patients, the current system does not allow such practices. Furthermore, significant criticisms have been identified regarding the inadequacy of physiotherapy education policies.ConclusionThe study reveals that the educational foundations of both physiotherapists and physicians significantly shape the physiotherapy process in Türkiye. Although physiotherapists receive extensive undergraduate training, inconsistencies in the quality of education and regulatory restrictions limit their clinical autonomy, impacting the efficiency of care delivery. Additionally, insufficient interdisciplinary training among physicians may hinder the implementation of collaborative rehabilitation practices. Enhancing the consistency, specialization, and integration of health professions education is essential for improving physiotherapy service quality and patient outcomes in Türkiye.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08104-8.

Background

Physiotherapy and rehabilitation services play crucial roles in the recovery and quality of life of patients with various physical disabilities [1]. The physiotherapy and rehabilitation process in Türkiye has been shaped by the collaboration among physiatrists, physiotherapists, and other healthcare professionals, each contributing their expertise in the development of personalized treatment plans for patients [2].

The education of health professionals is one of the most critical determinants of healthcare quality and patient safety. In the context of physiotherapy, undergraduate and postgraduate education play a foundational role in shaping clinical competencies, as well as defining the scope of professional autonomy and decision-making capacity. Globally, physiotherapy programs have evolved to reflect international standards that emphasize evidence-based practice, direct patient access, and interdisciplinary collaboration [3]. In Türkiye, the roles, authorities, and responsibilities of physiotherapists were most recently defined in the “Regulation on Job and Duty Descriptions of Health Professionals and Other Professions Working in Healthcare Services,” published in 2014. According to this regulation, for healthy individuals, physiotherapists “plan and implement individualized physical activity and exercise programs to regulate physical activities and enhance mobility.” For cases of illness, the regulation states that physiotherapists “carry out necessary practices aimed at eliminating or improving patients’ mobility and physical function disorders based on the diagnosis and treatment direction of a physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist physician or other specialized physicians who have undergone relevant training in physical medicine and rehabilitation during or after their specialty education.” [2]. Despite this definition, according to the Health Services Application Regulation No. 27,012 dated September 29, 2008, physiotherapists in Türkiye are only allowed to treat patients under the supervision of a physical medicine physician. If these conditions are not met, the Social Security Institution (SGK) does not make payments to service providers [4]. The process of referring patients to physiotherapists by physical therapy and rehabilitation physicians is carried out in the same way in both the private sector and the public health system [5].

Despite a four-year university education and increasing demand for physiotherapy services, Türkiye’s healthcare policies continue to restrict physiotherapists from exercising full clinical autonomy. Legal regulations require physiotherapists to operate under the supervision of a physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist, even when managing routine and stable cases [2].

In contrast, the World Confederation for Physical Therapy (WCPT) emphasizes the principle of autonomy, stating that physiotherapists “can act as first-contact practitioners, allowing patients/clients to seek services directly without a referral from another healthcare professional” [6]. Direct access to physiotherapists and patient self-referral are manifestations of professional autonomy [7]. Unlike in Türkiye, in the United Kingdom, patients have direct access to physiotherapists [8]. Similarly, in the United States, patients can access physiotherapists directly, a practice known as “direct access” [9].

According to the World Physiotherapy Education Framework, the professional autonomy of physiotherapists is grounded in a comprehensive set of competencies acquired during entry-level education. These include the ability to conduct independent physiotherapy assessment and intervention, adherence to ethical and professional practice, effective communication, and the integration of evidence-based practice. In addition, physiotherapists are expected to collaborate within interprofessional teams, engage in reflective practice and lifelong learning, contribute to quality improvement, and demonstrate leadership and management skills. Collectively, these eight domains of competence ensure that physiotherapists are prepared to act as autonomous practitioners, capable of making clinical decisions and providing safe, effective, and patient-centered care [10].

According to regulations and international examples [1–3], while physiotherapists are authorized to work with all kinds of specialist of physicians who have undergone physical therapy rotations during their medical training, in Türkiye, the Social Security Institution (SGK) requires patients to be examined by a physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist and subsequently treated under their supervision for the costs of treatment to be reimbursed. Due to this regulation, patients requiring physiotherapy cannot directly access a physiotherapist, even if a physician from another specialty has made a diagnosis. This situation can delay or even prevent patients needing physiotherapy from accessing healthcare on time [11].

In Türkiye, the expansion of physiotherapy education programs over the past decade has aimed to meet the increasing demand for rehabilitation services. However, this rapid growth has raised concerns regarding the consistency and quality of training across institutions. Recent studies indicate significant variation in curricula, inadequate faculty staffing, and limited opportunities for specialization, factors that may compromise the readiness of graduates to practice independently [12, 13].

This structural issue within Türkiye’s physiotherapy process constitutes the starting point of this study. This interpretative phenomenological qualitative study aims to explore how the educational background and training of healthcare professionals, particularly physiotherapists and physicians, influence the physiotherapy process in Türkiye. By focusing on stakeholder perspectives, the study seeks to understand how health professions education shapes clinical autonomy, interdisciplinary collaboration, and treatment delivery in rehabilitation settings. The findings of this study are expected to shed light on the managerial shortcomings of the physiotherapy process and the expectations of patients, providing valuable insights for both healthcare professionals and administrators.

Methods

This section provides the methodological details of the study, which was conducted via an Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) [14, 15]. The methodological process of this study was implemented based on the checklist developed by O’Brien et al. [16]

Research methodology

Phenomenological research is a qualitative approach that seeks to uncover the meaning of individuals’ lived experiences of a phenomenon [17, 18]. Two main types exist: empirical/transcendental phenomenology [19], which focuses on bracketing and extracting the essence from participants’ statements, and interpretative (hermeneutic) phenomenology [20], which emphasizes interpreting the meanings attributed to experiences. This study adopted an interpretative phenomenological approach, as the aim was not only to describe participants’ experiences of the physiotherapy process but also to interpret the meanings they ascribed within their social and professional contexts.

The approach proposed by Smith and Osborn [15] was adopted within the framework of interpretative phenomenological methodology [15]. In this framework, the researcher is not a passive observer but an active participant in the process of meaning-making. The focus is on how participants construct meaning within their social and professional contexts, while the researcher incorporates reflexivity by acknowledging and integrating their own perspective into the analysis [21].

An inductive approach was employed in analyzing the collected data [17]. In this context, the “physiotherapy process” was chosen as the research phenomenon to enable an in-depth exploration. This study defines the physiotherapy process as “a comprehensive process encompassing all stages from identifying the need for physiotherapy during the diagnosis and treatment phases of patients to the implementation and completion of physiotherapy.” [22].

Participants of the study

In qualitative studies, there are no definitive numbers or strict limitations regarding the number of participants [23]. However, the literature on phenomenological studies suggests a range of participants, typically between 3 and 4, and up to 19, depending on the relevance and experience with the studied phenomenon [17, 24, 25].

Participants were selected from the private and public sectors based on ease of access. Participants meeting these criteria were included in the study, and the data collection process was initiated. Data collection continued until no new themes or expressions emerged, indicating data saturation had been achieved. In-depth interviews were conducted with 12 participants, who were deemed to have significant insights and experience related to the subject: four physiotherapists, one physical medicine physician, two orthopedists, one cardiologist, and four patients. Efforts were made to reach a heterogeneous group to enrich the research phenomenon. All interviews were conducted in Turkish, and coding as well as thematic analysis were performed on the original transcripts.

Data collection tools

This study used a semi-structured interview form as the data collection tool. The primary purpose of a semi-structured interview technique was to identify participants’ views and experiences regarding predefined dimensions of the “physiotherapy and rehabilitation process in Türkiye.” This method allowed participants to freely express their thoughts and feelings about the phenomenon under investigation while ensuring that discussions remained focused on the topic.

The interview questions were initially developed in alignment with the study’s objectives and a thorough literature review. The form consisted of three primary questions for each profession and patient, with additional questions introduced as needed based on the flow of the interviews. The interview questions and guide are presented in Supplementary 1 (Interview Guide).

Data collection

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Çankırı Karatekin University during the 11th meeting on February 5, 2024. The data collection process was conducted between December 21, 2023, and January 1, 2024, in the central district of Ankara.

The participants in the study were contacted via telephone or e-mail, provided with the interview questions in advance, and informed that the planned interview would last approximately 30 min. The appointments were scheduled accordingly. The choice of interview location was left to the participants to ensure their comfort and convenience. Before the interviews, the researcher obtained written or verbal consent from the participants. For verbal consent, assurances were given regarding the confidentiality of personal information, and participants were informed that pseudonyms would be used in the study.

At the beginning of each interview, the researchers introduced themselves, thanked the participants for their interest and time, and engaged in a brief conversation to create a more relaxed and friendly atmosphere. Once permission was granted, the interviews were conducted. The semi-structured interview method was used for the interviews. With the participants’ consent, all interviews were recorded using audio recording devices, based on their written or verbal approval.

Although efforts were made to include a diverse group of healthcare professionals, it was not possible to interview a larger number of physical medicine and rehabilitation physicians. Several physicians from this specialty declined to participate or were unwilling to be interviewed despite multiple contact attempts. This limitation was noted during the data collection process.

Data analysis

The audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed by the researcher using the MS 365 Dictation program. The raw transcripts were reviewed by listening to the recordings to verify their accuracy, and necessary corrections were made. The finalized transcripts were sent to the research participants for verification of content.

The transcripts validated by the participants were then analyzed by another researcher using an inductive approach in line with the principles of IPA [14, 15]. The analytic process was conducted in several stages:

To ensure accuracy and reliability, the coding and theme generation were reviewed by another researcher not involved in the study [17]. In addition, researcher bracketing and reflexivity were maintained throughout the analysis to minimize potential bias. For this purpose, researchers kept reflexive notes during the process and shared tasks to reduce individual influence. The detailed procedures are documented in Supplementary File 2.

Data collection and analysis were carried out simultaneously. During repeated readings of the transcripts, meaningful statements were identified, compared across participants, and coded iteratively. The emergence of new codes and themes was systematically tracked throughout the analysis. The point at which meaningful statements in subsequent transcripts confirmed previously identified themes without generating new ones was considered data saturation. This point was reached through consensus among the researchers. Once data saturation was achieved, data collection was terminated, and the saturation of data was used as a criterion for the adequacy of the findings.

The meaningful expressions included in the findings were translated by a bilingual researcher proficient in both Turkish and English. The translated content was then reviewed for semantic accuracy by another bilingual researcher. The final presentation of the findings was organized through themes, supported by participants’ quotations, to enhance credibility and transparency.

Role of the researchers

This study employed a design focused on bracketing the researchers’ prior experiences. The first researcher conducted the data collection process, while the second researcher carried out the data analysis. For external observation, support was sought from an academic with expertise in the subject matter.

The first researcher is a physiotherapist with a Ph.D., possessing 15 years of clinical experience in orthopedics, neurology, and pediatrics, as well as three years of academic experience.

The second researcher holds a Ph.D. in health management and focuses on research areas such as health management, patient safety, and employee safety. Additionally, he has 13 years of experience working as a technician and mid-level manager in healthcare institutions (Given in Supplementary 2 – Reflexivity Table).

Validity and reliability of the study

The audio recordings obtained during the interviews were transcribed independently by multiple researchers and cross-compared for consistency to ensure the validity of the research data. One researcher conducted the interviews and their initial transcription, while another researcher completed the qualitative analysis of the resulting texts via NVivo 10 software. An additional researcher with expertise in qualitative research methods reviewed the final themes and codes. The roles of the researchers in the study were clearly defined and transparently presented. The transcribed texts were shared with the participants for content verification and approval to establish reliability. Furthermore, the researchers conceptually reviewed the coding and examined the relationships between the identified themes to ensure consistency and coherence in the analysis.

At the beginning of the study, potential researcher biases were identified and documented as part of a bracketing strategy to minimize their influence on data interpretation. These potential sources of bias, arising from the researchers’ professional backgrounds and prior experiences, were explicitly stated in the role of the Researchers section. During the interview and analysis phases, the researchers engaged in ongoing reflexivity, maintaining reflective journals to critically examine their assumptions, interactions, and interpretations throughout the research process. To ensure participant confidentiality, pseudonyms were used in place of real names, and identifiable information was anonymized. Specifically, age was reported in ranges rather than exact numbers to prevent potential identification.

Thematic findings

This section presents the findings related to the themes derived from the content analysis, along with participant quotations that support these findings.

Theme 1: education

The Education theme comprises two subthemes: undergraduate education (Sources: 7, References: 9) and education quality (Sources: 5, References: 8). This theme reflects findings based on participants’ statements regarding the education received by physiotherapists.

A significant portion of the participants indicated that the content and level of undergraduate education provided to physiotherapists were sufficient for planning and managing treatment for patients. Specifically, the undergraduate education that physiotherapists receive was adequate for planning and conducting treatments for routine patients diagnosed by a physician (7/12). The participant statements supporting this finding are as follows:

“This is the essence of the profession. Physiotherapists are trained for this purpose. They do not need to work under the supervision or control of a physical medicine specialist or another physician, such as a two-year graduate technician. Once they complete their four-year education, they should be able to autonomously handle patients who are diagnosed, medically stable, and referred for physiotherapy by a physician. However, which physician, you ask?” Physiotherapist 1 (♂, Exp.: 18 years, Public Sector - Academic).

“This is the essence of the profession. Physiotherapists are trained for this purpose. They do not need to work under the supervision or control of a physical medicine specialist or another physician, such as a two-year graduate technician. Once they complete their four-year education, they should be able to autonomously handle patients who are diagnosed, medically stable, and referred for physiotherapy by a physician. However, which physician, you ask?” Physiotherapist 1 (♂, Exp.: 18 years, Public Sector - Academic).

This confidence in physiotherapists’ qualifications was shared by patients, who highlighted the competence and trustworthiness of physiotherapists based on their formal training:

“In Türkiye, this is how things are, no matter what you say. Nothing can be done about it. I believe that a fully trained physiotherapist with a four-year degree is as competent as a doctor in providing care.” Patient_1 (♀, foot, elbow, shoulder, and lower back issues).

This view was also supported by young professionals, who emphasized the academic rigor of their training and its comparability to medical education:

“As you know, we take similar courses to medical students during the first two years of our education, including the same anatomy courses. For this reason, I believe it is necessary for us to know the same things they foresee in terms of professional competence.” Physiotherapist_3 (♀, Exp.: 3 years, Public Sector).

This sentiment extended beyond physiotherapists and patients. A cardiologist underscored the inconsistency between the duration of education and the limited authority granted to physiotherapists:

“I think it’s wrong not to grant authority to a profession with four years of education. This also applies to other professions, such as dietetics and nursing. Regardless of the field, I think it’s wrong. In my opinion, " four years of education is too much, or the authority given is too little.” Cardiologist (♂, Exp.: 34 years, Public-Academic).

Another prominent finding within the education theme was that while many participants believed physiotherapists are equipped to function independently within multidisciplinary teams, concerns about the overall quality and consistency of undergraduate education were also raised (5/12). These concerns point to systemic challenges in physiotherapy training programs across institutions.

A senior academic physiotherapist reflected on the long-term implications of rapid program expansion and insufficient educational standards:

“Since 2009–2010, the increasing number of individuals practicing physiotherapy and the decline in the quality of schools have resulted in graduates who are barely more qualified than technicians, and this is a problem for many professions.” Physiotherapist 1 (♂, Exp.: 18 years, Public Sector - Academic).

“I believe the undergraduate education level in Türkiye is insufficient. Specialization should be encouraged; for example, creating specialized fields such as cardiovascular or neurological rehabilitation is important.” Physical Medicine Physician (♀, Experience: 11 years, Public Sector).

Younger professionals also recognized disparities in educational outcomes across institutions and individual efforts:

“Just as not every physician is equally competent, I don’t think every physiotherapist is either. There may be those who do not improve themselves, and I also believe there is a significant difference between universities.” Physiotherapist 3 (♀, Experience: 3 years, Public Sector).

Another physiotherapist suggested a potential solution to ensure standardized competence among graduates:

“With the uncontrolled and nonstandardized opening of schools, physiotherapists’ qualifications have become reliant on a board exam. I believe that a board exam should be implemented.” Physiotherapist 2 (♂, Exp.: 13 years, Public Sector).

Based on these findings, it can be concluded that the rapid increase in the number of universities in Türkiye is a factor contributing to the decline in educational standards. The decline in the quality of education may be a critical factor affecting physiotherapists’ ability to work independently.

Theme 2: independent practice

The theme of independent practice comprises four subthemes: physician support (Sources: 9, References: 28), professional education and competence (Sources: 9, References: 31), multidisciplinary collaboration (Sources: 8, References: 22), and treatment planning and management (Sources: 8, References: 21).

This finding suggests that physiotherapists require physicians’ diagnoses and referrals to conduct the treatment process effectively. In this context, the term “physicians” was not limited to physical medicine specialists but also included other specialties such as orthopedists and cardiologists. The findings suggested that physiotherapists could independently operate while collaborating and communicating with these professionals (9/12). Participant quotations related to this finding are provided below. A physical therapy physician emphasized that collaboration with physicians remains a legal necessity in Türkiye,

“I believe that physiotherapists should collaborate closely with physicians to determine patients’ need for physiotherapy. In Türkiye, patients are required to obtain approval from physicians before receiving physiotherapy services.” Physical Therapy Physician (♀, Exp.: 11 years, Public Sector).

“I believe that physiotherapists should collaborate closely with physicians to determine patients’ need for physiotherapy. In Türkiye, patients are required to obtain approval from physicians before receiving physiotherapy services.” Physical Therapy Physician (♀, Exp.: 11 years, Public Sector).

A patient with long-term rehabilitation experience further illustrates how this restriction is internalized by patients themselves, reinforcing the physician-centered access model:“Right now, without a physician’s referral, we cannot consult a physiotherapist directly. We have to go through a physician first. For example, I look up physiotherapists online, watch their videos, and so on, but I do not fully trust them.” Patient_3 (♀, Polio, Lower Back, and Spine Issues).

“Right now, without a physician’s referral, we cannot consult a physiotherapist directly. We have to go through a physician first. For example, I look up physiotherapists online, watch their videos, and so on, but I do not fully trust them.” Patient_3 (♀, Polio, Lower Back, and Spine Issues).

In contrast, a physiotherapist working in the private sector pointed out a crucial distinction between diagnostic dependency and therapeutic autonomy, arguing that physicians are essential for diagnosis but not necessarily for treatment implementation:“Well, in terms of diagnosis, I definitely need physicians. I generally avoid taking on patients without a proper diagnosis. So yes, I need them for diagnostic purposes, but when it comes to treatment, I don’t think I need them that much.” Physiotherapist_4 (♂, Exp.: 17 years, Private Sector).

Discussion

Physiotherapy education, as defined in international frameworks, equips graduates with a set of competencies that directly shape their ability to act with clinical autonomy, to collaborate effectively within interdisciplinary teams, and to contribute to high-quality treatment delivery in rehabilitation settings [27]. The results of our study highlight that in Türkiye, although physiotherapists are trained according to international standards that emphasize independent assessment, evidence-based practice, and professional responsibility, their clinical autonomy is constrained by current legal regulations. This mismatch between the competencies acquired during education and the limited professional independence allowed in practice not only affects physiotherapists’ professional identity but also has implications for interdisciplinary collaboration and the overall effectiveness of rehabilitation services.

This phenomenological qualitative study identifies opportunities for improvement within Türkiye’s physiotherapy healthcare system and contributes to developing a comparative perspective with international systems.

Although an attempt was made to include all professional groups equally that play a leading role in the physiotherapy and rehabilitation process in Türkiye, the ratio of physiotherapists to physical medicine physicians was 4:1 due to the reluctance of physical medicine physicians to participate. For this reason, the themes obtained from the study were shaped more by the perspectives of physiotherapists.

Education

This study highlights critical gaps in physiotherapy education in Türkiye, particularly regarding curriculum consistency, specialization opportunities, and national competency evaluation. While physiotherapists receive a four-year university education, the lack of structured postgraduate pathways and board examinations undermines their professional recognition and autonomy in clinical settings. These findings echo challenges reported in other rapidly expanding health education systems, particularly in middle-income countries, where quantitative expansion has not always been accompanied by quality assurance. Similarly, Frenk et al. (2010) emphasize in their published study that the rapid expansion of health education does not always ensure quality and requires reform [28]. Kiguli-Malwadde et al. [29] show that rapid student growth creates quality and adequacy problems [29].

Therefore, the issues identified in this study are not solely localized but may reflect broader global trends in physiotherapy education and regulation. Enhancing competency-based education models and harmonizing curricula with international frameworks such as WCPT recommendations may support global standardization and mobility of physiotherapy professionals.

The WCPT recommends that physiotherapy education programs be offered at the university level and last a minimum of four years. This professional education equips physiotherapists with the principles of autonomous practice and the ability to collaborate with other healthcare professionals [30]. Studies in the United Kingdom also support the autonomous practice of physiotherapists and patients’ direct access to physiotherapy services [6, 31, 32]. In addition to Europe, the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) classifies physical therapy practitioners as “physical therapists” or “physical therapist assistants.” A physical therapist is defined as a professional practitioner of physical therapy services, whereas a physical therapist assistant is the only individual authorized to assist the therapist during practice [33].

To practice as a physiotherapist in the United States, one must obtain a Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) degree from a program accredited by the Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education (CAPTE) and pass a state licensing examination. Professional education programs typically span three years. Additionally, physiotherapists in the U.S. are authorized to practice autonomously [27].

A study conducted by Aslan et al. [12] in Türkiye examined perceived threats to the profession among physiotherapy students. The most commonly identified threats included the admission of lower-achieving students due to increased quotas, leading to the graduation of less qualified physiotherapists, and the establishment of numerous schools with inadequate academic staff and insufficient educational facilities, resulting in an increase in unqualified graduates [12].

In recent years, concerns among physiotherapists regarding the qualifications of their colleagues have increased noticeably [13]. From this perspective, it was also observed during this study that physiotherapists frequently expressed similar concerns. This divergence in findings could be attributed to generational differences or biases stemming from the rapid establishment of physiotherapy programs in recent years without adequate infrastructure. In other countries, for example, in Austria, the demand for physiotherapy education far exceeds the available quotas, with a ratio of approximately 13:1. This significant disparity ensures the selection of students who are most suited to the profession and contributes to the overall quality of education [34]. Similarly, projections indicate that the surplus in the number of physiotherapists, as seen in Türkiye, is also expected to increase in the United States [35].

Independent practice

One of the prominent themes to emerge from this study was physiotherapists’ expressed desire for greater independent practice. This emphasis should be interpreted in the context of the sample composition: the majority of participants were physiotherapists, supplemented by other specialists and patients; thus, the findings principally reflect the perspectives of these groups. The topicality of professional autonomy in Türkiye, an issue increasingly debated in professional and policy forums, may have further amplified its salience in interviews.

A consistent message across physiotherapists and the non-PM&R physicians interviewed (notably orthopaedists and cardiologists) was that collaborative working with physiotherapists is both practicable and commonplace across specialties; several physicians who had undertaken physical therapy rotations explicitly reported no objection to direct clinical collaboration with physiotherapists. Participants described arrangements in which physiotherapists manage rehabilitative care for referred, medically stable patients while consulting physicians when complexities arise, suggesting that interdisciplinary cooperation, rather than exclusive oversight by physical medicine specialists, often underpins routine rehabilitation in practice.

Nevertheless, the regulatory environment in Türkiye constrains this professional latitude. The Health Practice Communiqué [5] and the regulation on the Job and Duty Descriptions of Healthcare Professionals [2] establish legal boundaries that position physical medicine and rehabilitation specialists centrally in the delivery and medico-legal supervision of physiotherapy services. While physiotherapists retain authority to design exercise programmes for healthy individuals, the statutory framework effectively limits autonomous patient management, requiring supervision or referral pathways for clinical treatment. As a result, clinically capable physiotherapists frequently report functioning mainly as implementers of prescribed regimens rather than as independent clinical decision-makers.

This regulatory posture contrasts with international trends toward greater physiotherapy autonomy. Since the late 20th century several jurisdictions have adopted models of direct access or first-contact physiotherapy; notably, the WCPT (now World Physiotherapy) formalised support for direct access at the 2011 General Meeting, and reviews indicate that many member countries permit patient self-referral, with professional training judged sufficient for such roles in a majority of those states [6]. However, the literature also cautions that autonomy carries responsibilities: maintaining safety and public trust requires demonstrable professionalism and adherence to standards [7]. In Türkiye, participants argued that the combination of limited statutory autonomy and entrenched supervisory practices constrains professional development and can reduce job satisfaction among physiotherapists [36].

Importantly, study participants, both physiotherapists and several physician respondents, consistently maintained that the current undergraduate training (a four-year programme) provides the foundational competencies necessary to assume responsibility for routine patient care, including assessment, treatment planning, and ongoing management. Despite this, the mismatch between educational preparation and the scope of practice permitted in law and reimbursement policy means that formal training does not uniformly translate into independent clinical authority.

Finally, participants highlighted a relational dimension that supports the argument for greater clinical autonomy: physiotherapists tend to spend more time with patients during rehabilitation episodes and therefore develop closer therapeutic relationships. Effective communication, attention to patients’ concerns, and continuity of care, elements emphasised by participants, are core contributors to patient-centred outcomes and satisfaction in physiotherapy practice [37, 38]. These communicative competencies, combined with formal clinical training, underpin the professional argument for expanding physiotherapists’ operational responsibilities within safe, well-governed care pathways.

Treatment process

Physiotherapists, due to the extended duration of their sessions, often around one hour, are uniquely positioned to observe subtle changes in patients’ conditions and to develop close therapeutic relationships. This proximity not only enhances clinical monitoring but also enables physiotherapists to identify patients’ primary concerns and expectations, a process commonly referred to in the literature as determining the “patient’s agenda” [39]. The integration of patients’ agendas into clinical reasoning is foundational for patient-centered and individualized rehabilitation, a core principle in modern physiotherapy practice. These findings align with international frameworks that promote therapeutic alliance and collaborative goal-setting as central elements of effective care delivery.

However, our study reveals that in Türkiye, systemic barriers limit the full realization of these benefits. Patients interviewed described delays in accessing physiotherapy services, often consulting with physicians only at the beginning and end of the treatment process. This fragmented care pathway contrasts with the ideal of continuity in patient-centered rehabilitation and is largely shaped by the requirement for physician referrals. Physiotherapists in our study suggested that allowing direct patient access to their services, with referral to physicians when clinically necessary, could not only streamline the treatment process but also alleviate the growing workload on physicians.

These perspectives align with global evidence that emphasizes the value of early physiotherapy access in enhancing clinical outcomes and the barriers imposed by referral-based systems [40]. Despite physiotherapy’s rising popularity as a non-invasive and cost-effective intervention, its accessibility in Türkiye remains constrained by regulatory dependence on physician oversight. Thus, the Turkish context provides a case where professional competencies exceed the structural opportunities for their application, highlighting a significant misalignment between training and practice.

Challenges in resource distribution further complicate this tension. The Turkish Physiotherapists Association (TPA) survey [41] indicated substantial treatment delays, with an average of 150 patients on waiting lists across 22 rehabilitation centers and some patients waiting over two months for intervention. While a lack of physiotherapist employment in public hospitals partly explains these delays, another contributing factor is the paradoxical oversupply of new graduates. According to Çalışkan [42], Türkiye is projected to face a surplus of 192% in the physiotherapist workforce supply by 2023 [42]. This overproduction is echoed in earlier warnings in the literature regarding the unplanned expansion of educational quotas and the establishment of new programs without sufficient infrastructure [13, 43, 44].

These dual challenges, workforce underutilization in clinical settings and overproduction in academia, raise critical concerns about both access to care and professional competency. As suggested by our findings and reinforced by prior studies [42], the current trajectory may not only threaten employment prospects but also jeopardize the quality of physiotherapy services by diluting training standards.

Taken together, the findings underscore a structural disconnect in Türkiye’s physiotherapy landscape: while professional training and patient needs support a more autonomous and accessible physiotherapy model, legal, administrative, and workforce policies constrain its realization. Addressing these systemic barriers is essential not only for improving patient outcomes but also for aligning the physiotherapy profession with international standards of autonomy, efficiency, and quality care delivery.

Limitation

The findings of this qualitative study are confined to the personal perspectives of the participating healthcare professionals and physical therapy patients, as well as the researchers’ interpretations of these perspectives. Throughout the research process, rigorous methodologies were employed to ensure an objective representation of participants’ views. However, the researchers’ backgrounds as healthcare professionals may have introduced a critical lens to the analysis. Furthermore, the study’s scope being limited to the Turkish healthcare system constitutes another notable limitation.

Although a purposive and snowball sampling strategy was employed to ensure a heterogeneous group of participants, we encountered significant difficulties in recruiting physical medicine and rehabilitation (PM&R) physicians. Despite multiple attempts, most PM&R physicians either declined to participate or did not respond to interview requests. As a result, this professional group was underrepresented in the sample. The study mainly reflects the perspectives of physiotherapists and related specialists, so the emphasis on autonomy and collaboration should be interpreted with caution.

While this limitation may restrict the diversity of perspectives captured from medical specialists directly overseeing physiotherapy processes, the study nonetheless offers valuable insights from a multidisciplinary set of participants, including orthopedists, cardiologists, physiotherapists, and patients. The findings should thus be interpreted with consideration of this limitation, while acknowledging the broader contribution of diverse professional experiences to understanding the physiotherapy process in Türkiye.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study aimed to provide a qualitative analysis of the current physiotherapy treatment process in Türkiye, contributing to the existing body of knowledge on physical therapy and rehabilitation. The results of this qualitative research reveal that the expectations of patients, physiotherapists, and physicians within Türkiye’s healthcare system have not been fully met in the context of physiotherapy processes. The primary underlying causes for unmet expectations appear to be related to physiotherapist education policies and workforce supply. While physiotherapists receive comprehensive undergraduate training, current healthcare regulations restrict their ability to apply this knowledge independently, ultimately undermining both the intent and the impact of their education.

In this context, it is crucial to contribute the challenges stakeholders face and their proposed solutions, as identified in this study, to the existing literature. However, the findings of this study should be interpreted in light of the participant profile. Most of the insights were derived from physiotherapists and other health professionals, while the perspectives of physical medicine and rehabilitation (PM&R) physicians were less represented.

Future studies should include more PM&R physicians to reflect their perspectives better. Larger and more diverse samples, involving different patient groups and medical specialists, would strengthen the findings. Comparative studies across healthcare systems could highlight similarities and differences in physiotherapy practice. Research on interprofessional education and policy reforms, such as greater physiotherapist autonomy and alternative referral models, would also provide important evidence for improving service quality and collaboration.

The findings of this research are expected to support learning from the experiences of other countries and pave the way for developing more effective, patient-centered rehabilitation practices.
