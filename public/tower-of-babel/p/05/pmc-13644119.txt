Metabolic and functional analysis including repetitive antigen stimulation provides a more accurate assessment for CAR-T cell product quality and long-term functionality

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBCMA-directed CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in multiple myeloma, but robust methods to evaluate product quality and predict long-term functionality remain underdeveloped. While immunophenotypic features of leukapheresis products correlate with clinical outcomes, the characterization of infused CAR-T cell products remains challenging and inconsistent across studies.MethodsWe compared two versions of our academic BCMA-CAR product, CARTemis-1, differing only in their costimulatory domains (4-1BB vs. CD28), and performed a detailed functional and molecular characterization before and after antigen stimulation to identify which analytical approaches best capture functionally relevant differences between CAR-T cell products. Analyses included conventional flow cytometry, short-term cytotoxicity assays, repetitive antigen stimulation, metabolic profiling, and transcriptomic evaluation. Finally, metabolic profile of patient derived CAR-T cells products (n = 15) was also analyzed.ResultsAlthough previous studies have reported functional differences between CAR-T cell products incorporating distinct costimulatory domains, conventional assays in our system revealed no significant differences in viability, expansion dynamics, immunophenotype, or activation/exhaustion marker expression. In contrast, more functionally demanding assays revealed clear differences between constructs. Repetitive antigen stimulation revealed superior long-term cytotoxicity in CARTemis-1-BB, while metabolic profiling demonstrated enhanced spare respiratory capacity and maximal respiration after antigen exposure. Transcriptomic analysis further showed distinct pathway regulation, with enrichment of T cell activation signatures in CARTemis-1-BB and Wnt/TGFβ-related responses in CARTemis-1–28. Importantly, metabolic profiling of infused CAR-T cell products also identified distinct signatures associated with clinical outcome, distinguishing products administered to long-term responders from those infused into short-term or non-responding patients.ConclusionsPersistence and metabolic analyses represent key tools for optimizing CAR-T cell construct selection during preclinical development, providing a more accurate assessment of CAR-T cell product quality than standard immunophenotyping and enabling the identification of constructs with superior therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08571-7.

Background

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy represents a rapidly advancing research area, with two FDA-approved therapies with impressive outcomes in multiple myeloma (MM): Idecabtagene vicleucel [1, 2] and Ciltacabtagene autoleucel [3–5]. As a result, significant efforts are underway to enhance patient outcomes and to discover biomarkers that can reliably predict these outcomes at an early stage [6]. These efforts begin at the preclinical stage, where different CAR constructs are evaluated to identify the most optimal product candidates. At this stage, flow cytometry and short-term cytotoxicity assays are considered standard approaches for CAR-T cell characterization. Flow cytometry enables the assessment of key quality attributes, including transduction efficiency, CD4:CD8 ratios, viability, and immunophenotypic features associated with clinical responses [7]. In this context, in BCMA-CAR T cell clinical trials, the presence of less differentiated subpopulations within the leukapheresis products has been associated with improved efficacy [8, 9]. Specifically, a retrospective analysis of Ide-cel treated patients found that those with long-term durable responses exhibited a higher percentage of naïve and early memory CD4+ T cells, and a lower percentage of senescent CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) starting material [10]. Similarly, the presence of memory T cells and reduced proportion of exhausted T cells in the apheresis product has been linked to better outcomes in patients treated with CD19-CAR T cells [11, 12]. However, the relationship between the characteristics of the infused CAR-T cell product and its efficacy remains contentious. Several studies have failed to identify consistent differences in immunophenotypic composition or exhaustion marker expression of CAR-T cell products infused to responders versus non-responders using conventional flow cytometry approaches [12, 13]. Additionally, studies that have associated the presence of less differentiated subpopulations in CAR-T infusion products with improved patient responses have often employed more sensitive techniques, such as single-cell RNA-sequencing [14] or single-cell multiplex cytokine analysis [13]. These findings suggest a gap in current preclinical evaluation strategies, suggesting that standard assays may not be sufficient to detect functional differences in CAR-T cell products that might correlate with therapeutic outcomes. This limitation may lead to the selection of suboptimal CAR designs for clinical translation, which potentially compromises therapeutic efficacy.

In this study, we sought to address this limitation by performing a comprehensive characterization of two BCMA-CAR T cell products differing only in the costimulatory domain, with the objective of identifying which techniques are most informative for detecting functionally relevant differences between CAR-T cell products. To this end, using our academic BCMA-targeting CAR T-cell product CARTemis-1—currently under evaluation in an ongoing phase I/II clinical trial (EuCT number 2022-503063-15–00) [15]—as the starting point, we developed a second-generation construct in which the original 4-1BB costimulatory domain was replaced with CD28 since published evidence suggests that the presence of the 4-1BB or CD28 costimulatory domains significantly influences CAR-T cell functionality, including cytokine production, metabolism and exhaustion marker expression [16–20]. Our findings show that conventional techniques such as flow cytometry and short-term cytotoxicity assays failed to discriminate between these two CAR-T cell products. In contrast, more sensitive functional readouts, including persistence under repeated antigen stimulation and metabolic profiling, revealed clear differences indicating that the selection of appropriate analytical tools is critical for accurately assessing CAR-T cell product quality and identifying constructs with superior therapeutic potential.

Methods

Human T cells from healthy donors and patient-derived CAR-T cell products

PBMCs were isolated from fresh leukopacks obtained from healthy donors, provided by the Regional Centre for Blood Transfusions at Virgen del Rocío University Hospital, Seville (Spain). PBMCs were separated using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Subsequently, T lymphocytes were separated using the human Pan T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) on an autoMACS Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

CD19 CAR-T cell samples from lymphoma patients were obtained from patients treated within the clinical trial (EudraCT 2020-003133-38) at the Hematology Department, Hospital Sant Pau (Barcelona, Spain), following appropriate ethical approval and informed consent. Dual CD19/CD22 CAR-T cell samples [21] were obtained from patients with B-ALL treated under a compassionate use program at Hospital Universitario La Paz (Madrid, Spain) or the clinical trial NCT06709469. NKG2D CAR-T cell samples were obtained from patients with Ewing sarcoma treated at Hospital Universitario La Paz (Madrid, Spain) under either a compassionate use program or the clinical trial NCT06087341. BCMA-CAR T cell infusion products from multiple myeloma patients were obtained from Hospital Clínic (Barcelona, Spain). All CAR-T cell products were generated under GMP-compliant conditions following established clinical manufacturing protocols.

Cell lines

HEK-293T cells (LentiX, Takara Bio) were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (all from Gibco, Darmstadt, Germany).

The human MM cell lines MM.1S and OPM-2 were obtained from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany). The K562 (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany) was further modified to express the BCMA antigen by transduction with lentiviral vectors, followed by sorting for BCMA-positive cells to create a homogeneous K562-BCMA cell line. For in vitro assays, all cell lines were modified to express firefly luciferase (ffluc) and green fluorescence protein (GFP) via lentiviral vectors. Tumour cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% of heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 1% of GlutaMAX (all from all from Gibco, Darmstadt, Germany), and maintained at 37 °C in a fully humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Construction and generation of CAR encoding lentiviral vectors

BCMA-CARs were assembled as previously described [15]. The codon-optimized single-chain variable fragment (scFv) was fused to a IgG4 hinge and the transmembrane region of the CD28 molecule. Two versions of the construct were synthetized: CARTemis-1-BB, which incorporated the 4-1BB costimulatory domain, and CARTemis-1–28, which replaced the 4-1BB domain with the CD28 costimulatory domain. Both constructs included the a CD3ζ signalling domain and a truncated human epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFRt) fused with a T2A element.

Replication-defective third-generation lentiviral vectors were produced by cotransfection of HEK-293T with CARTemis-1-BB or CARTemis-1–28 expression lentiviral vectors, along with the packaging vectors PCHGP-2, PCMV-Rev2 and PCMV-G, using PEIpro (Polyplus, Illkirch, France). After 72 hours, lentiviral supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 3600 rpm for 15 minutes to remove cellular debris, and concentrated for 2 hours using high-speed ultracentrifugation at 24,900 rpm. The resulting lentiviral pellets were resuspended in DPBS solution (Gibco, Darmstadt, Germany) and stored at −80 °C.

Manufacturing of CARTemis-1 CAR-T cells

T cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin and streptomycin and 1% GlutaMAX (all from Gibco, Darmstadt, Germany). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a fully humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

For generating CARTemis-1 CAR-T cells, T cells were seeded in 48-wells plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL and cultured with 10 ng/mL each of IL-7 and IL-15 (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch-Gladbach, Germany). T cells were activated with anti-CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Gibco, Darmstadt, Germany) at a bead-to-T-cell ratio of 1:1. 24 hours after activation, T cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding CARTemis-1 CARs via spinoculation at 2100 rpm for 45 minutes at 32 °C in the presence of polybrene (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA), and cell expansion was performed with 10 ng/mL each of IL-7 and IL-15 during 8 days.

CAR expression was monitored by flow cytometry using an APC-labelled anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody (Biolegend, Clone: AY13) on day 8 of the culture.

Flow cytometry

For the immunophenotypic characterization of CARTemis-1 CAR-T cells, cells were washed with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and stained for 20 minutes at 4º with fluorescent antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) as previously described [22]. Following staining, cells were washed with PBS, and data were acquired using the FACS Canto II Flow Cytometer System (BD). Additionally, activation and inhibition markers, as well as subpopulation compartments, were analysed by flow cytometry either before and after three days of antigen stimulation. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo™ v10.7 Software (BD Life Sciences) or Infinicyt 2.0 (Cytognos) software.

In vitro analysis of antitumor BCMA-CAR T cells persistence

The persistence of antitumor activity of BCMA-CAR T cells was assessed through repeated exposure to tumour antigen. CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 were independently cocultured with BCMA-expressing tumour cells every 72 hours for a total of five stimulations. CAR expression was assessed prior to each assay, and an equal number of CAR+ cells were used at the start of each experiment. Metabolic analysis

Metabolic analysis of mitochondrial activity of BCMA-CAR T cells and patient derived CAR-T cells was assessed with Seahorse XF T Cell Metabolic Profiling Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions. Basically, 1 × 105 BCMA-CAR T cells were seeded in Seahorse XF Cell Culture Miniplates previously coated with poly-D-lysine on Seahorse XF RPMI Medium, pH 7.4 supplemented with 10 mM XF Glucose, 1 mM XF Pyruvate and 2 mM XF Glutamine. XF sensor cartridges were hydrated with 200 μL of calibrant solution and compound solutions were loaded onto the cartridges as follows: 25 μL of Oligomycin A on port A (final concentration 1.5 μM), 25 μL of BAM15 on port B (final concentration 2.5 μM) and 25 μL of Rotenone/Antimycin A on port C (final concentration 0.5 μM each). Metabolic profiling was performed using the XF Seahorse Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and data were subsequently analysed with Seahorse Analytics software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For the antigen stimulation analysis, CAR T cells were co-cultured with K562-BCMA+ target cells at an E:T ratio of 1:4. Target cells were irradiated at 80 Gy prior to co-culture to prevent proliferation and minimize their metabolic activity. The co-culture was maintained for 5 days, after which cells from the co-culture were used for metabolic assessment. Seahorse metabolic analyses were performed using CAR T cells generated from three independent donors (n = 3). For each donor, measurements were performed in technical replicates, and the average value per donor was used for statistical analysis.

RNA extraction and RNA-seq analysis

For RNA extraction and subsequent RNA-seq analysis, CAR+ cells were isolated from three independent donors (n = 3) by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) following five days of antigen stimulation with irradiated K562-BCMA cell line at E:T 1:4 ratio. Cells were stained with anti-EGFR and anti-CD3 antibodies, and 7-AAD was used as viability marker. After staining, EGFR+ cells were selected using FACS Aria Fusion (BD) sorter. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA was frozen at −80 °C until use. We measured RNA purity and concentration using Nanodrop, and samples with a concentration higher than 200 ng, A260/280 ratio between 1.8–2.2, and A260/230 > 1.8 were selected for RNA sequencing. cDNA library preparation and Ilumina sequencing were performed by NovoGene (Cambridge, UK). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using read count normalization followed by model-based p-value estimation. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 were defined as those with |log2(fold change)| >0 and nominal p-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the identified DEGs. Enrichment p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR), and GO terms with padj < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Statistical analysis

No normalization was implemented in the flow cytometry data analysis. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism v8.4.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA) and R (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We have performed outliers’ identification to eliminate values detected to be outliers, and we have determined the normality of our data using Shapiro-Wilk test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and D’Agostino & Pearson test. Data with a normal distribution were analysed for statistical significance using paired or unpaired t-tests, and one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analysed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test and the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple comparisons. For Seahorse data analysis, a linear mixed-effects model was applied to account for repeated measures. Model assumptions were evaluated and verified prior to analysis. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant and p values are represented by asterisks as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001 and **** p < 0.0001.

CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 show similar viability, expansion, and CAR+ cells proportion prior to antigen stimulation

We designed two versions of our academic anti-BCMA CAR product CARTemis-1, with the only difference of the costimulatory domain (Fig. 1A). After generating the constructs, we evaluated the effect of the costimulatory domain on CAR-T cell viability and expansion on day 8 of the manufacturing process, prior to antigen stimulation. First, we assessed cell viability using Annexin V and 7-AAD staining, followed by flow cytometry, and found no significant differences in viability between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 (Fig. 1B). Next, we measured cell expansion using trypan blue staining and observed no significant differences in fold expansion between the two constructs (Fig. 1C). To further investigate the expansion of CAR-positive (CAR+) T cells in both products, we analyzed the percentage of CAR+ cells on day 8 of the manufacturing process. Although the percentage of CAR+ cells was slightly higher in CARTemis-1-BB (25.9 ± 8.5) compared to CARTemis-1–28 (20.7 ± 16.0, Fig. 1D), this difference was not statistically significant. When examining the total expansion of CAR+ cells, a higher value was observed for CARTemis-1-BB (2.85 × 106) compared to CARTemis-1–28 (1.04 × 106, p = 0.051, Fig. 1E). Next, we investigated the changes in the immunophenotype of CAR-T cells following antigen stimulation by analysing T cell subpopulation compartments and the expression of activation and exhaustion markers. Among the different subsets analysed, significant differences were observed only in CD4+ central memory and CD8+ effector CAR-T cells, with higher frequencies detected in CARTemis-1-BB compared to CARTemis-1–28 (CD4+ central memory: 36.35% vs. 22.41%, p = 0.05; CD8+ effector: 46.38% vs. 37.97%, p = 0.001, Fig. 3B), whereas all other subsets were comparable between products. The expression of activation and exhaustion markers was also similar, with no differences in CD25, PD-1, or LAG-3 levels (Fig. 3C).

Considering these modest shifts in immunophenotype, we next assessed whether they were accompanied by measurable functional differences. Accordingly, we assessed the cytotoxic activity of both products using a single-exposure coculture assay with MM target cell lines, with specific lysis quantified 24 hours post-stimulation. Anti-tumour efficacy was comparable between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 at all effector-to-target (E:T) ratio and this trend was consistent across both MM.1S and OPM-2 cell lines (Fig. 3D).

Additionally, to characterise the phenotypic changes induced by antigen exposure in each product, we examined CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 at baseline and after antigen stimulation. Viability in CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 remained stable following antigen stimulation (Supplementary Figures 1A and 2A). Moreover, the proportion of CAR+ T cells increased after antigen stimulation in both constructs (Supplementary Figures 1B and 2B). Interestingly, the percentage of CD25+ CAR-T cells decreased after three days of antigen stimulation in both CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28, while exhaustion markers such as PD-1+/LAG-3+ remained unchanged (Supplementary Figures 1C and 2C). As expected, antigen stimulation resulted in a shift from stem cell memory toward effector CAR-T cell subsets in both products. (Supplementary Figures 1D and 2D).

Taken together, CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 showed comparable viability, expansion, immunophenotype, and short-term cytotoxic activity following antigen stimulation. Although CARTemis-1-BB displayed modestly higher proportions of CD4+ central memory and CD8+ effector subsets, these immunophenotypic differences did not translate into detectable changes in a single-exposure coculture assay. Besides, activation and exhaustion markers remained similar between constructs, suggesting equivalent functional responses to antigen exposure.

CARTemis-1-BB demonstrated enhanced functionality and persistence following sequential in vitro re-exposure to tumour cells compared to CARTemis-1–28

To address this, we assessed the antitumor activity of both CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 under conditions of repeated antigen stimulation, simulating stress conditions. CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 were exposed in vitro to five consecutive tumour challenges using two MM cell lines. To control for potential differences in transduction efficiency between products, CAR expression was quantified by flow cytometry prior to each assay, and experiments were initiated with equal numbers of CAR+ cells, thereby minimizing the impact of transduction variability on cytotoxicity outcomes. Initially, both CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 exhibited comparable antitumor efficacy after the first antigen encounter with the MM.1S and OPM-2 cell lines, as previously reported in short-term cytotoxicity assays. However, CARTemis-1-BB maintained robust cytotoxicity following the fifth challenge, while CARTemis-1–28 showed a progressive loss of cytotoxic activity after sequential exposure to MM.1S and OPM-2 tumour cells, which was particularly pronounced at lower E:T ratios. For instance, after five challenges, the tumour cell death of MM.1S at an E:T ratio of 2.5:1 was 88.68% for CARTemis-1-BB compared to 40.06% for CARTemis-1–28 (p = 0.0009) (Fig. 4B). CARTemis-1-BB showed higher maximal respiration and spare capacity after antigen stimulation

Given the enhanced persistence observed in CARTemis-1-BB during repeated antigen stimulation and considering that spare respiratory capacity (SRC) has been associated with T cell persistence and functional fitness, we next sought to determine whether high-resolution metabolic profiling could reveal additional differences between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 before and after antigen stimulation. To minimize the potential impact of differences in CAR expression on the observed metabolic profiles, we ensured similar transduction efficiencies between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 across donors both before and after antigen stimulation (Figs. 5A and 5C). Initially, we analysed the OCR of CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 prior to antigen stimulation and observed no significant differences in maximal respiration and SRC between the two constructs (Fig. 5B). Specifically, CARTemis-1-BB exhibited a greater SRC (CARTemis-1-BB: 33.86 ± 6.25 vs. CARTemis-1–28: 11.20 ± 0.44, Fig. 5Dii) and higher maximal respiration (44.24 ± 8.61, vs. CARTemis-1–28: 16.03 ± 1.35, Fig. 5Diii). We have also analysed the basal ECAR and ATP production. Under basal conditions, no significant differences in ECAR or glycolytic ATP production were observed between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28, indicating comparable glycolytic activity prior to antigen stimulation (Fig. 5E). Following antigen stimulation, CARTemis-1-BB exhibited increased ECAR and higher ATP production compared to CARTemis-1–28, supporting enhanced overall metabolic activity (Fig. 5F). This trend was consistently observed in two additional donors (Supplementary Figure 4). Notably, no viable tumour cells were detected in the culture after 5 days of co-culture, as determined by flow cytometry, supporting that tumour cells did not contribute to the metabolic measurements (Supplementary Figure 5). These findings suggest that, while no metabolic differences were observed before antigen stimulation, CARTemis-1-BB underwent a shift toward a more oxidative metabolism following antigen engagement, in contrast to CARTemis-1–28. To explore the potential translational relevance of metabolic profiling approaches identified in our preclinical studies, we performed a whole functional and metabolic characterization of CAR-T cell products infused into patients with sufficient follow-up time. To increase the number of cases available for analysis, we included different CAR-T cell products comprising 3 CD19, 3 Dual CD19/CD22, 2 NKG2D and 7 BCMA CAR-T cell products. Samples from patients were stratified into long-term progression-free survivors (LT-PFS) (progression-free survival > 18 months) and non-responders/relapsing patients (NR/RP) (no response or PFS < 18 months), and both immunophenotypic and metabolic features of the infusion products were analysed. Immunophenotypic analysis revealed that LT-PFS exhibited a higher proportion of CD4 and CD8 CD25+ CAR-T cells in the infusion products (CD4: 74,14% vs 36,67%, p = 0.013; CD8: 79,68% vs 46,36%, p = 0.01, Fig. 7A). In addition, LT-PFS products exhibited a higher percentage of TCM and a lower percentage of TEM CAR-T cells (Fig. 7B), whereas no significant differences were observed in exhaustion marker expression. Discussion

The preclinical evaluation of CAR-T cell product quality and functionality is a critical step in the development pipeline, guiding the selection of constructs for clinical translation. In this context, our data highlight the importance of incorporating robust, high-resolution functional methodologies capable of identifying clinically relevant differences between CAR-T cell products that are not captured by conventional assays. The adoption of such approaches may contribute to refining quality metrics and to improving the discrimination of high- and low-performing products, with potential implications for the definition of more informative potency and release criteria. Among these methodologies, flow cytometry remains the most widely used tool for evaluating CAR-T cell product characteristics and quality. It has been employed to characterize both leukapheresis and final infusion products, particularly in autologous CAR-T cell therapies, where product composition significantly varies depending on patient-specific factors [24]. Flow cytometry has enabled the identification of parameters in the apheresis product that correlate with therapeutic efficacy. Notably, enrichment of memory T cell subpopulations within the apheresis material has been associated with improved clinical outcomes [12, 20, 25]. Conversely, the presence of contaminating non-T cell subpopulations, such as monocytes, has been shown to hinder CAR-T cell transduction and expansion [26]. Nevertheless, thorough characterization of final CAR-T cell infusion products by flow cytometry remains challenging and is not feasible for commercial CAR-T products. Differences in subpopulation distribution and exhaustion markers observed at this stage do not consistently correlate with clinical efficacy [12, 13]. Furthermore, the limited availability of immunophenotypic data from infused CAR-T cell products in clinical trials complicates the establishment of standardized quality control criteria required to ensure therapeutic potency. This limitation underscores the need to develop robust in vitro assays capable of identifying optimized CAR-T cell products at both preclinical and clinical stages. The integration of such approaches is essential not only to enhance product functionality and clinical efficacy, but also to support the definition of more informative potency and quality attributes. Importantly, the selection of CAR-T cell constructs at these stages has direct implications for clinical translation, as suboptimal candidates may progress into clinical development if conventional assays fail to detect functionally relevant differences.

In this context, we employed two versions of our academic BCMA-CAR construct, CARTemis-1: CARTemis-1-BB, incorporating the 4-1BB costimulatory domain, and CARTemis-1–28, incorporating CD28. Our objective was to evaluate which analytical techniques could effectively identify functional differences between these two constructs, as previous studies have shown that the nature of the costimulatory domain (4-1BB vs CD28) has significant implications for CAR-T cell phenotype and clinical performance [16–20, 27]. Both CAR-T cell products were manufactured using an identical expansion protocol, maintaining consistent hinge and transmembrane domains, which are also known to influence CAR-T cell differentiation and proliferative capacity [28, 29]. We characterized the properties of CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 after eight days of manufacturing. This manufacturing process is critical to the phenotype of the final product, which directly affects CAR-T cell potency and persistence [30]. At this pre-antigen stimulation stage, prior to CAR engagement, it has been reported that CARs containing 4-1BB or CD28 costimulatory domains can exhibit distinct functional and phenotypic properties in an antigen-independent manner [18, 19]. Based on this, we aimed to identify differences between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 that could predict their respective functionalities. On day 8 post-manufacturing, we analysed viability, expansion and immunophenotype in both products. Despite the differing costimulatory domains, flow cytometry did not reveal significant differences in phenotype between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 before antigen stimulation. These results align with findings from other groups, who have also reported that flow cytometry fails to detect functional or phenotypic differences between CAR-T products containing 4-1BB versus CD28 domains prior to antigen stimulation [31, 32]. Moreover, these findings underscore the limitations of conventional assays used in preclinical development to identify functional differences between CAR-T cell products that may differ in their clinical performance.

Subsequently, we performed a detailed immunophenotypic analysis of both CAR constructs following antigen-specific activation, with the expectation that functional disparities between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 would become evident. The influence of the costimulatory domain on the immunophenotype of CAR-T cell product is particularly relevant after antigen engagement, as the intracellular signalling cascades associated with each receptor are believed to be preserved upon their incorporation into the CAR construct. These signalling pathways can modulate the kinetics and amplitude of downstream protein phosphorylation events, ultimately affecting CAR-T cell functionality [17, 33]. To explore these effects, we designed an activation assay using irradiated K562 cells engineered to express the BCMA antigen, enabling specific CAR-mediated stimulation. We then assessed CAR+ T cell differentiation across subpopulation compartments, alongside expression of activation and exhaustion markers. The only notable differences observed were higher frequencies of CD4+ central memory and CD8+ effector RA+ CAR-T cells in CARTemis-1-BB compared to CARTemis-1–28, while activation and exhaustion markers remained similar between the two constructs. Despite these modest immunophenotypic differences, short-term cytotoxicity assays conducted after a single antigen stimulation revealed no significant variation in antitumor efficacy between the two CAR-T products.

Considering the subtle differences observed following a single stimulation, we further extended our analysis using a stress model involving repeated antigenic stimulations with tumour target cells to assess CAR-T cell persistence. In this setting, CARTemis-1-BB demonstrated superior antitumor activity against two multiple myeloma cell lines, with enhanced efficacy becoming especially evident after the third round of antigen exposure. These findings align with previous studies reporting increased persistence of CAR-T cells incorporating the 4-1BB costimulatory domain [34]. CAR-T cell persistence in peripheral blood is a key factor associated with prolonged progression-free survival in patients, especially in the context of MM, where one of the predominant mechanisms underlying relapse is the loss of CAR expression [2, 35, 36]. Accordingly, in vitro re-exposure models evaluating CAR-T cell persistence following repeated antigen challenge constitute an essential approach for assessing the quality and long-term functionality of CAR-T cell products.

To further refine strategies for selecting optimized CAR-T products, we conducted a comparative analysis of the metabolic profiles of CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 constructs. SRC and maximal respiration are established indicators of T cell metabolic fitness, reflecting the capacity of T cells to adapt under stress conditions and meet elevated energetic demands [37]. Furthermore, metabolic reprogramming has been shown to be influenced by the choice of costimulatory domain in CAR-T cell constructs [38, 39]. Upon antigen stimulation, T cells undergo metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis to meet the biosynthetic demands of activation and proliferation. Effector T cells primarily rely on glycolytic metabolism, whereas, following antigen clearance, memory T cell subsets persist by utilizing OXPHOS and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to sustain their metabolic needs [40]. In our study, both CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 exhibited comparable metabolic profiles prior to antigen exposure. However, following antigen exposure, CARTemis-1-BB displayed greater reliance on OXPHOS to fulfil its metabolic requirements. Additionally, CARTemis-1-BB demonstrated significantly higher SRC and maximal respiration compared to CARTemis-1–28, which may partially account for its enhanced persistence in vitro. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that CAR-T cells incorporating the 4-1BB costimulatory domain preferentially engage OXPHOS over glycolysis [20]. It is important to consider that differences in transduction efficiency between CAR-T cell products may represent a limitation when interpreting bulk metabolic analyses. Variations in the proportion of CAR+ and CAR- cells within the culture could influence overall metabolic readouts, particularly given that CAR expression can induce tonic signalling and metabolic activation [19]. In our study, for these experiments, we selected matched donors with comparable CAR expression levels. Notably, metabolic differences between CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 became evident only following antigen stimulation, under conditions in which CAR expression levels were comparable between matched samples. These observations suggest that the metabolic differences identified are driven by intrinsic properties of the CAR constructs rather than by differences in CAR expression or tonic signalling.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the metabolic differences observed in our preclinical model, we performed transcriptomic analyses comparing CARTemis-1-BB and CARTemis-1–28 following antigen stimulation. To this end, CAR+ T cells were sorted after five days of stimulation, followed by bulk RNA extraction and RNA sequencing. PCA was initially performed to identify factors contributing to sample clustering. Our analysis revealed that samples primarily clustered according to donor variability rather than the type of costimulatory domain, consistent with previous studies reporting that CAR-T cell profiles post-antigen stimulation are more strongly influenced by variables such as the activation stimulus or CD4/CD8 ratios than by the specific CAR construct [18]. This observation highlights one important limitation of our study, as the relatively small sample size included in our analysis may have limited the statistical power to resolve transcriptional differences attributable to the costimulatory domain over the strong inter-donor variability observed. Despite this limitation, differential expression and GO enrichment analyses revealed biologically coherent trends. GO analysis revealed upregulation of pathways associated with T cell activation in CARTemis-1-BB, including genes involved in cytokine response, leukocyte chemotaxis, and cellular migration. This transcriptional profile is consistent with an enhanced capacity to respond to chemotactic signals and to infiltrate tissues, potentially contributing to its superior antitumor efficacy and persistence. In contrast, CARTemis-1–28 exhibited enrichment of genes associated with the Wnt signalling pathway and the cellular response to TGF-β. The Wnt pathway regulates T cell proliferation and differentiation, and its upregulation has been linked to the inhibition of CD8+ T cell differentiation into effector cells [41], which aligns with our phenotypic findings of a lower proportion of CD8+ effector memory cells in CARTemis-1–28. Moreover, TGF-β signalling, which is prominent within the tumour microenvironment and promotes T cell dysfunction and impaired mitochondrial respiration [42, 43],may be consistent with the reduced spare respiratory capacity and maximal respiration observed in this construct. While GO enrichment analysis in CARTemis-1-BB retained statistical significance after FDR correction, enrichment results in CARTemis-1–28 did not remain statistically significant following multiple testing adjustment, likely reflecting limited statistical power associated with the small sample size. Accordingly, enrichment trends observed in CARTemis-1–28 should be interpreted cautiously, and validation in larger independent cohorts will be required to confirm their robustness.

To explore the translational relevance of these findings, we extended our analysis to CAR-T cell products infused into patients, performing both immunophenotypic and metabolic characterization. Given the limited number of cases with sufficient follow-up, we included CAR-T cell products targeting different antigens and derived from patients with diverse haematological malignancies and paediatric sarcomas, acknowledging the increased heterogeneity of the cohort. Although limited immunophenotypic differences were observed between LT-PFS and NR/RP groups, metabolic analysis captured distinct oxygen consumption profiles across patient-derived products, with LT-PFS products showing higher metabolic activity despite the heterogeneity introduced by including different CAR-T cell products. Collectively, these findings support the use of metabolic profiling emerges as a crucial analytical approach in the optimization of CAR-T cell constructs, offering a potential quality control metric predictive of CAR-T cell functionality.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this study we demonstrate that traditional flow cytometry and short-term cytotoxic analysis may fail to reveal critical functional differences between CAR-T cell products. Our findings highlight the importance of evaluating persistence and metabolic fitness following antigen exposure as essential parameters for distinguishing CAR-T cell products with superior therapeutic potential. Importantly, our results support the need for the validation and implementation of robust, high-resolution functional methodologies capable of reliably discriminating between high- and low-performing CAR-T cell products. These insights underscore the need to incorporate functional post-stimulation assays as well as metabolic profiling into CAR-T cell product characterization pipelines, as such approaches will not only refine quality standards but may also serve as predictive markers for clinical efficacy.
