Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by symmetrical polyarthritis. Affecting 0.5–1% of the population, RA is the most prevalent autoimmune disease [1]. Beyond joint involvement, RA can manifest with a broad range of extra-articular complications, among which, lung involvement is one of the most common. Various compartments of the respiratory system may be affected, including the airways (bronchiectasis, lymphoid/follicular and constrictive bronchiolitis), lung parenchyma (interstitial lung disease (ILD), rheumatoid nodules and emphysema) and pleura (pleural effusion).The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms likely involve citrullination, primarily occurring in the lung, as well as the implication of bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue. Both lung disease and citrullination are associated with smoking and genetic predisposition, which may also explain the higher prevalence of emphysema among RA patients. Notably, some studies suggest that immune mechanisms could contribute to emphysema in nonsmoking RA patients [2]. Although RA predominantly affects women, existing studies indicate a higher prevalence of ILD in men [3]. Reported prevalence rates of RA-ILD range from 3–50% [4–7], depending largely on the selected population and study methodology. Interestingly, the prevalence of RA-associated pleural disease and bronchiectasis seems to be decreasing, likely due to improved disease management. Finally, the exact prevalence of small airways disease in RA remains unknown [8].

The currently identified risk factors for RA-ILD include disease activity, male sex and genetic predisposition. In 2018, Juge and colleagues [9] demonstrated that the gain-of-function MUC5B promoter rs35705950 variant confers an increased risk for RA-ILD, similar to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [10]. In 2022, a risk score was proposed [11] based on a retrospective analysis of two French cohorts of early RA patients (163 and 89 patients, respectively). However, it remains unclear whether all RA patients should be systematically screened for ILD and, if so, at what stage of the disease. Furthermore, prospective data on lung manifestations beyond ILD remain limited.

To address these gaps, we have initiated a multicentric prospective study aiming at detecting RA-ILD and lung involvement in newly diagnosed RA patients.

Material and methods

Study population and design

The factors of ILD in newly diagnosed rheumatoid arthritis (FIND-RA) is a prospective and multicentric study.

From September 2019 to April 2023 adult patients who fulfilled the following criteria were recruited: 1) diagnosis of RA according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) 2010 criteria; 2) RA diagnosis present from at least 1 year and, at most, 10 years; and 3) willingness to participate in the study and ability to provide informed consent. Patients were recruited from the rheumatology departments of the Cliniques Universitaires Saint-Luc (coordinating centre), CHU-UCL Namur and Centre Hospitalier de Mouscron in Belgium.

Following signed informed consent, patients underwent a comprehensive clinical assessment, which included Disease Activity Score-28 (DAS-28)-C-reactive protein (CRP), HAQ evaluation and an environmental questionnaire (supplementary figure S1). Additionally, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), chest high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and blood sampling for serum and DNA analysis were performed (the latter requiring a separate informed consent form). Dyspnoea was assessed based on medical records using modified Medical Research Council Scale (mMRC), ranging from no dyspnoea (mMRC 0) to dyspnoea at rest (mMRC 4).

Our primary objective was to detect ILD and investigate its related factors. Secondary objectives included identifying other lung abnormalities, such as emphysema, air trapping, nodules, bronchiectasis and pleural effusion. We also analysed the correlation between HRCT findings and clinical, functional, biological and genetic data.

Pulmonary function tests

Patients underwent pulmonary function tests (PFTs), including measurements of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory flow at 25–75% of FVC (FEF25–75%) and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO). All PFT data were centrally reviewed (MP and AF). Results were expressed in percentage of the expected value, based on Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) references values [12, 13].

Lung imaging

All patients underwent standardised HRCT acquisitions, with state-of-the-art HRCT on supine position in full inspiration and full end-expiration acquisitions using a 256-detector row CT (Ingenuity CT, Philips Healthcare, Cleveland, OH, USA). Additional acquisition in a prone position was left to the discretion of the radiologist in charge of the patient to differentiate pathological lung infiltration from dependent transitory lung opacities. Images were centrally reviewed by two thoracic radiologists (LV and BG, with 5 years and more than 30 years of experience, respectively).

Radiologists investigated the presence of an ILD, considering ground-glass opacities, reticulations, traction bronchiectasis/bronchiolectasis and honeycombing. They also looked at the presence of emphysema, air trapping, nodules, bronchiectasis and pleural effusion.

Lung involvement definitions

We defined iILD using the currently accepted definition, namely the presence of diffuse parenchymal abnormalities involving at least 5% of a lung segment. We defined preclinical ILD as an ILD without clinical involvement (mMRC 0 dyspnoea and normal PFTs), according to the definition by Hatabu and colleagues [14].

Our multidisciplinary team, involving pulmonologists, radiologists, pathologists and rheumatologists, confirmed all ILD diagnoses.

Provided that a small proportion of air trapping may be considered as physiological [15], a quantification method previously described by Bankier and colleagues [16] and Franquet and colleagues [17] was used to study air trapping. Each lung was divided into three lung zones: an upper zone (above the carina), a middle zone (from the carina to the right inferior pulmonary vein) and a lower zone (below the right inferior pulmonary vein). Each lung zone (six lung zones in total) was quantified according to a scale of 0–4 depending on the percentage of expiratory air trapping: score 0 (no trapping), score 1 (1–25%), score 2 (26–50%), score 3 (51–75%) and score 4 (>75%). The maximum possible score for each lung was 12 (three zones multiplied by four points for each zone) and 24 for both lungs. Finally, the total scores out of 24 points were transformed into a percentage. A proportion of more than 20% of air trapping was deemed significant.

The nature of the nodules was solely based on radiologist opinions. Multiple, regular, subpleural nodules in the appropriate setting were suspected of rheumatoid nodules. The need for a biopsy was based on the multidisciplinary team's opinion.

Gene sequencing for variants of interest

For each patient, a blood sample was collected in an EDTA tube. The next-generation sequencing reads were mapped against the reference sequences of the four genes of interest (MUC5B, TOLLIP, FAM13A and TERT) using minimap2 v.2.26. The resulting SAM files were converted to the more compact BAM format using SAMtools v.1.17. Finally, FreeBayes v.1.3.7 was employed for variant calling to identify the genotypes of each patient. The extraction method is detailed in the appendix.

Statistical analysis

All statistics were performed by the Statistical Methodology and Computing Service (SMCS), UCLouvain, Belgium. Statistical analyses were performed using the JMP® Pro v.17.1.0 software. A p-value <0.05 was deemed significant.

Based on an a priori 20% prevalence of lung abnormalities, a sample size of at least 246 patients was requested to achieve a confidence level of 95% with a 5% margin of error.

A chi-squared test of independence was used to compare categorical variables. The Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test was used to compare groups for continuous variables. A multiple logistic regression was used to model the probability of developing lung disease. The explanatory variables included age, sex, higher SAS-28 CRP, rheumatoid factor and MUC5B, which were selected using stepwise based on Akaike Information Criterion. The model's performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves and the area under the curve (AUC). The association between explanatory variables and the presence of ILD was quantified using odds ratios (ORs), accompanied by their 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Ethical considerations

This study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04002765) and was approved by all participating centres: CHU-UCL Namur registration 146/2021, Centre Hospitalier de Mouscron registration OM174 and Cliniques Iniversitaires Saint-Luc (central review board) registration 2019/21JUI/269.

All participants provided signed informed consent form (ICF) prior to enrolment. A separate ICF was requested for DNA analysis.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

We screened 290 patients from September 2019 to August 2023. Of these, 32 were excluded (30 for not undergoing the required examinations and two for having a RA diagnosis exceeding 10 years), resulting in a final cohort of 258 patients. Additionally, 45 patients declined to provide blood samples for DNA analysis and 13 blood samples were lost due to technical issues, leaving 200 DNA samples for analysis. Figure 1 presents the study flowchart and table 1 details the cohort's characteristics. The median age of the included patients was 56 years. The majority (71%) were female and 49% were either ex- or current smokers, with a median smoking history of 20 pack-years. Rheumatoid factor (RF) was positive in 71% of patients and 67% tested positive for anti-citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies. Treatment mainly consisted of methotrexate (76%) and/or a biologic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug (DMARD) (50%). Occupational and/or domestic exposure was identified in 26% of patients, primarily among house cleaners and industrial workers exposed to chemicals.

Primary objective: prevalence of interstitial lung disease

We detected ILD in 38 patients (14.8%). As detailed in table 2, the presence of ILD was associated with male sex (47% versus 26% in non-ILD patients; p=0.008) and older age (median 65 versus 54 in non-ILD patients; p<0.001). RA-ILD patients also had more active disease, as reflected by a significantly higher DAS-28-CRP score (2.4 versus 1.9; p<0.03). ILD was also associated with lower DLCO (71 versus 84%; p<0.001). Regarding our genetic analysis, we found a higher (but not statistically significant) proportion of carriers of MUC5B variant in the ILD group (31% versus 18%; p=0.09). The majority of ILD patients were asymptomatic (pre-clinical ILD): only 11 patients (29%) reported shortness of breath (mMRC dyspnoea scale≥1), 16 (42%) had a low DLCO and 3 (8%) had a low FVC according to GLI guidelines on PFT interpretation. Of note, the interval between RA diagnosis and inclusion did not influence the risk of ILD in univariate models.

The first model included age, RF, DAS-28-CRP, sex, DLCO, time from diagnosis to inclusion and smoking status (figure 2 and table 3). This model yielded an AUC of 0.84. In this model, every supplementary point of DAS-28-CRP was associated with an OR of 1.6 (95% CI 1.17–2.33) of having an ILD (p=0.004), male sex conferred a 2.4 OR of having an ILD (0.98–5.71; p=0.05) and every supplemental year of age was associated with a 1.08 (1.04–1.12; p <0.001) OR of having an ILD. We then built a second model including our data on the MUC5B variant (on 200 patients). This model yielded a slightly better performance, with an AUC of 0.88. In that model also, positivity for RF was associated with the strongest risk increase (OR 30.2, 95% CI 2.8–319.2; p=0.004). Of note, age and sex are interdependent in our study, men being older in our population (58 versus 54 years old; p=0.03). Therefore, we built two new models, the first including age and the second including sex (supplementary figure S2). Neither of those models performed better than the models combining age and sex.

Secondary objectives: other lung abnormalities

HRCT showed emphysema in 51 patients (19.8%). Emphysema was associated with male sex (53% versus 23%; p<0.001), older age (median 59 versus 54 years old; p<0.001), active or former smoking (p<0.001), presence of anti-CCP antibodies (p=0.004), presence of RF (p=0.001), lower median %FEV1 (89% versus 96%; p=0.004) and lower median %DLCO (71% versus 86%; p<0.001) (supplementary table S1). Of note, the presence of emphysema on HRCT was also associated with ILD features, namely of reticulations (p=0.02) and traction bronchiectasis (p=0.001). We also found a lower prevalence of significant air trapping in RA patients with emphysema (26% versus 44%; p=0.03). From a genetic point of view, we found a smaller proportion of carriers or the TERT variant (62.5% versus 79%; p=0.03) in patients with emphysema.

We also tried to build predictive models to predict emphysema based on our results. The best predictive model was a univariate logistic regression considering the number of pack-years smoked. In our model, we obtained the best sensitivity:specificity ratio with a threshold of 13 pack-years, providing 94% sensitivity and 51% specificity (figure 3).

All patients underwent HRCT with expiratory acquisition, as detailed in the Methods section. We defined significant trapping as >20% threshold following previous studies [15, 17]. Significant air trapping (>20%) was observed in 89 patients (34%), of whom only 14 (16%) reported dyspnoea and/or had an impaired lung function. Air trapping was not associated with any demographical characteristics or PFT results. Notably, the prevalence of emphysema was lower in patients with air trapping compared with the rest of the cohort (p=0.02).

We detected rheumatoid nodules in nine patients (3.5%) and had no consequence on PFTs. Of note, one patient had a solitary nodule that was biopsied and confirmed benign.

Our results did not allow us to build predictive models for air trapping and rheumatoid nodules.

Finally, no patient from our cohort suffered from bronchiectasis and/or pleural disease.

Discussion

In this study, we sought to estimate the prevalence of ILD and lung abnormalities among a nonselected population of RA patients diagnosed within the past 10 years. The prevalence of ILD and emphysema was 15% and 20%, respectively, with most patients being asymptomatic or presenting only mild symptoms (mMRC 0 dyspnoea). ILD was associated with older age, male sex, lower DLCO and higher disease activity. After adjusting for potential cofounders, the strongest predictors of ILD were age, male sex, RF positivity and higher disease activity. Emphysema correlated with male sex, smoking status, lower FEV1 and RF/anti-CCP seropositivity. The best predictor for emphysema was the number of pack-years, with a threshold of 13 pack-years. Finally, air trapping, likely indicative of small airways involvement (constrictive bronchiolitis), was detected in 34% of patients. This involvement was not reflected in standard PFTs and did not present with clinical symptoms. We found no association with sex, age, smoking status or disease activity. Our predictive models identified a higher (worse) DAS-28 score, positivity of RF, age, male sex and % DLCO as significant predictors of ILD in our RA population. The addition of the MUC5B variant slightly improved our model, as previously suggested in a French retrospective cohort [11].

The prevalence of ILD in our cohort was similar, though slightly lower, than in studies by Matson et al. [18] and Juge et al. [11]. This discrepancy may stem from our study's focus on patients with a relatively recent RA diagnosis (less than 10 years), thereby excluding cases of ILD related to long-standing uncontrolled disease. Consequently, our findings are relevant to determining whether RA patients should undergo early ILD screening (an issue not full addressed in recent ACR/American College of Chest Physicians guidelines) [19]. In our study, seven HRCT scans were required to detect a single ILD case. However, most patients exhibited mild interstitial abnormalities without symptoms (preclinical ILD). Based on these findings, we suggest restricting systematic screening (in asymptomatic patients) to seropositive patients older than 60 years old and those with uncontrolled disease despite treatment. Regarding emphysema, screening could be considered for all patients with a smoking history of at least 13 pack-years, regardless of sex and age.

We found a higher prevalence of the rs35705950 MUC5B variant in RA-ILD patients compared with non-RA-ILD patients (30.7% versus 17.8%), aligning with previous data [9]. However, this proportion applied to the subset of patients who consented to provide DNA (200). Adding MUC5B status to predictive models marginally improved their performance (AUC 0.87 versus 0.84). Whether this addition has clinical utility remains uncertain, as previous studies have yielded conflicting results: Juge et al. [11] found that integrating the MUC5B parameter within a matrix score improved ILD detection sensitivity, whereas another study failed to demonstrate a predictive value [20]. Some studies used combined single-nucleotide polymorphisms in a genetic score, allowing the restriction of screening [21], especially in low-risk populations. This latter approach still requires validation both at a general level and among specific subpopulations. In this regard, the upcoming international study ANCHOR-RA [22] may provide further insights.

All patients underwent HRCT with expiratory acquisition, allowing us to quantify air trapping. Based on previous studies [15–17], we defined significant air trapping using a 20% threshold, irrespective of clinical status. Despite air trapping being common (34% of our patients), its clinical relevance remains undetermined as only a small subset reported symptoms or had impaired lung function. Our results further underscore the difficulty of assessing small airways involvement using standard PFTs. Whether the “bronchiolitis” reflects that lungs may be one of the primary sites for RA onset warrants further investigation and prospective follow-up. Additionally, prone CT acquisition was not systematically performed, as it was left to the discretion of the radiologist to minimise radiation exposure. Whether routine prone positioning would improve small airway disease detection remains uncertain [23].

Interestingly, smoking history was not associated with ILD but, as expected, was strongly linked to emphysema. A systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the OR for smoking and ILD at 1.91 [24].

Finally, we observed a relatively low incidence of rheumatoid nodules (3.5%) and no cases of pleural effusion or hyper-productive bronchiectasis. This is significantly lower than in older cohort studies [4] and might relate to the fact that we included patients with relatively recent and well-controlled disease. Notably, the incidence or RA-related pleural effusion has been declining, suggesting that this extra-articular complication may relate to poor disease control.

To our knowledge, this is the largest multicentric and prospective cohort investigating the prevalence and characteristics of RA-related lung disease among a nonselected population of early-stage RA patients. It is also the only study to comprehensively analyse ILD, emphysema and air trapping using centrally reviewed HRCT. However, our study has some limitations. First, despite being multicentric, the majority of RA patients were recruited from two tertiary care centres. Our findings are based on patients in remission or with low disease activity due to a high proportion receiving biologic DMARD therapies, which may limit generalisability. Additionally, we cannot confirm whether our results apply to non-European populations. Second, all HRCT scans were centrally reviewed by an expert radiologist, ensuring accurate differentiation of lung abnormalities. Whether similar screening approaches would be feasible in general hospitals remains unclear. Third, the relatively small number of ILD cases in this “recent RA” population limited our ability to perform robust comparisons for certain parameters. Last, our study was underpowered for detecting statistically significant genetic associations (power of 27%).

In conclusion, our study in RA patients demonstrated an early prevalence of ILD and emphysema of 15 and 20%, respectively. Key risk factors for ILD were male sex, older age, disease activity, RF seropositivity and the MUC5B variant, whereas emphysema was primarily associated with smoking. If screening strategies for RA-related lung disease are implemented, our findings suggest prioritising respiratory evaluations for male patients, older patients and those with seropositive and active disease despite treatment, as these factors were the strongest predictors in our models.
