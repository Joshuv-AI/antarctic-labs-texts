Understanding the impact of streptozotocin on diabetic male infertility: perspectives from rodent models and pharmacological networking
Authors: Muhammad Arif Asghar, Lu Li, Jing Wu, Guojiang Zhang, Rui Xiao, Shixin Tang, Bing Wan, Xiao Zhang, Qinjian Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStreptozotocin (STZ) is a common agent for inducing diabetes in animal models, including those used to study male infertility. However, the lack of standardized validation for these models poses challenges for research in this area. This study aimed to comprehensively assess the impact of STZ on male fertility parameters in rodent models of diabetic male infertility. Additionally, it sought to investigate into the underlying molecular mechanisms using pharmacological networking tools.Materials and methodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and PsycINFO yielded 92 eligible studies involving 1535 male rodents. Hedges’ g was utilized to compare fertility parameters between STZ-treated and control groups. Meta-regression analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between STZ dosing and fertility damage. Pharmacological networking techniques were employed to identify critical targets and pathways associated with STZ-induced infertility.ResultsThe analysis revealed significant reductions in sperm count (Hedges’ g = -8.56), motility (Hedges’ g = -8.21), and testis weight (Hedges’ g = -7.95) following STZ administration, accompanied by an increase in abnormal sperm morphology (Hedges’ g = 7.80). However, no clear dose-response relationship was observed between STZ dosing and fertility damage, indicating potential threshold effects. The power analysis revealed adequate statistical power (estimated at 0.82) for detecting the estimated effect size in our meta-analysis of male fertility outcomes. Pharmacological networking identified key targets (GAPDH, PTGS2, MAPK1, CASP3) and pathways potentially implicated in STZ-induced infertility i.e. IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, Cellular senescence emerged as the primary pathways linked to oxidative stress and inflammation.DiscussionThese findings highlight the substantial impact of STZ on male fertility parameters in rodent models of diabetic male infertility. The absence of a clear dose-response relationship suggests complexities in STZ-induced infertility mechanisms, warranting further investigation. Identified molecular targets and pathways provide promising opportunities for future therapeutic interventions.ConclusionStandardizing STZ-induced diabetic male infertility models is imperative for advancing research in this field. The study’s insights into potential therapeutic targets based on identified molecular mechanisms offer the way for further investigations and the development of targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-00998-w.Keywords: Streptozotocin, Male infertility, Diabetes, Animal modelling, Meta-analysis, Pharmacological networking

Background

Male infertility is a global reproductive health issue, with one in every six individuals of reproductive age experiencing infertility during their lifetime, according to the WHO [1]. In addition, at least 180 million couples worldwide are affected [2]. The male partner is solely responsible for approximately 20% of cases and contributes to an additional 30% to 40% of all infertility cases [3]. Quality of life (QoL) is strongly linked to male sexual health, making it a significant area of focus in healthcare. Various factors contribute to male infertility, including oligospermia (reduced sperm count), asthenozoospermia (reduced sperm motility), abnormal sperm morphology, hormonal imbalances, genetic predispositions, lifestyle factors, and environmental influences. However, the precise pathogenesis of male infertility, especially oligoasthenospermia, remains poorly understood, and effective therapeutic options are limited [4, 5].

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder that leads to high levels of blood glucose. It is a global epidemic that affects millions of people worldwide due to lifestyle changes and genetic factors. This condition is known to cause a range of systemic complications that have significant implications for male reproductive health [6, 7]. The association between diabetes and male infertility is a growing area of research, highlighting the interaction between metabolic health and reproductive function. Understanding this link is vital for addressing the complex effects of diabetes on male fertility and developing novel interventions to mitigate its impact.

Animal models are essential to better understanding the molecular mechanisms of male infertility and developing preclinical therapeutic strategies. Chemical methods are preferred for modelling animal spermatogenesis dysfunction due to their simplicity and practicality. However, these methods have limitations, i.e., extended modelling cycles and associated high mortality rates [8, 9].

Streptozotocin (STZ), a diabetogenic agent, is widely used to induce diabetes in animal models due to its ability to selectively target and destroy pancreatic beta cells, resulting in insulin deficiency and hyperglycemia [10]. STZ-induced diabetic animal models serve as invaluable tools for studying diabetes pathophysiology and complications, including male reproductive dysfunction [11]. However, despite its widespread use, the overall effect of STZ on male fertility still needs to be more adequately quantified and critically validated in preclinical research, demanding further evidence to establish STZ modelling for male infertility.

In recent years, computational analysis of pharmacological networking has emerged as a practical approach for elucidating complex molecular mechanisms underlying disease pathogenesis and therapeutic interventions. This integrated approach offers a synergistic framework for accelerating drug discovery and development in the field of diabetic male infertility. It helps to transform multidisciplinary data to construct a “drug-gene-target disease” network based on their biological properties and then predict the action mechanisms of small molecules on diseases [12, 13].

In most studies, the evaluation of modelling methods needs to be more comprehensive to systematically analyze multiple factors, including animal species, dosing, time points for outcome observations, and indicators for evaluation. Consequently, quantification and comparable outcomes are rarely available to researchers. Therefore, the aim of this study was to systematically review and meta-analyze preclinical studies investigating the effects of STZ on diabetic male fertility in experimental models. Our objectives include synthesizing existing evidence regarding the impact of STZ-induced diabetes on sperm quality and testicular morphology by reviewing the experimental literature of the past twenty years. Additionally, we aim to evaluate how variability in STZ dosing, route of administration, and duration of diabetes induction may influence the severity and progression of diabetes-related male infertility. We also predict the associated molecular mechanism and pathways for STZ to induce diabetic male infertility via pharmacological networking tools.

Methods

Study design and search strategy

The present study was designed according to the PRISMA guidelines [14]. Using the Collaborative Approach to Meta-analysis and Review of Animal Data in Experimental Studies (CAMARADES) bias risk checklist, a systematic review and meta-analysis were performed on all relevant studies [15]. Rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria were used to ensure the comprehensive collection of studies using STZ for diabetic-induced male infertility in rodents. Study abstracts from conferences were excluded because limited information were available in them. Furthermore, the ethical committee approval was deemed unnecessary for this review study.

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted across multiple scientific databases, including Medline, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Library, and the Chinese Biomedical Database from January 1, 2004, to July 20, 2025, utilizing both quick and advanced search tools. The search was performed on July 22, 2024, to extract animal experiments modelling related to diabetic-induced male infertility using STZ in the past 20 years. The following search terms were included in titles and abstracts (also in combination with MESH terms): (mice OR rat OR animal OR rodents modelling) AND (male infertility OR reproduction OR reproductive function OR reproductive disorders OR sperm analysis OR spermatogenesis disorder OR oligospermia OR oligoasthenospermia) AND (streptozotocin OR STZ) AND (diabetes OR hyperglycemia). Additionally, the reference lists from each searched article were manually reviewed to identify further relevant studies, and the search was restricted to publications in the English language.

Study selection

Two authors performed the study search, screening, and evaluation following a standardized reported method for systematic review and meta-analysis [16, 17]. Only full-text research articles were considered for inclusion, while non-original studies, those lacking sufficient data, or review articles were excluded from further analysis. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until a consensus was achieved. All experimental studies were considered eligible if they fulfilled the following criteria: (1) involve mice or rats as experimental subjects; (2) STZ was administered experimentally to make modelling related to diabetic-induced male infertility; (3) measure male fertility parameters (specifically related to oligoasthenospermia). Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) absence of control or matched group; (2) transgenic studies; (3) STZ was not administered as a single drug for animal modelling; (4) incomplete data related to male fertility; (5) full-text articles were not available in English. The complete screening and selection of studies are given in Fig S1.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction from selected studies was performed according to the CAMARADES and PRISMA guidelines, including information from published articles, characteristics of study design, and data outcomes related to male fertility. Equivalency measures were applied for male fertility-related data for each meta-analysis, with preference given to final assessment data when evaluations were conducted at multiple time points. The potential risk of study bias was assessed using the CAMARADES bias risk checklist [15, 18]. Articles scoring over six stars on these scales were deemed to be of high-quality publications. Quality assessments and bias evaluations were independently conducted by two reviewers, with any disparities resolved through discussion.

Quantitative data synthesis

The statistical software program SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) version 29 was used to analyze the extracted data. A meta-analysis was conducted using the statistical program R. The included studies were summarized qualitatively, describing the nature of direct comparisons. The relative effect of each outcome was calculated with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Classical pairwise meta-analyses were conducted to synthesize studies that compared the two groups (control and model) using the robust random-effects method [19]. Meta-regression

We conducted univariate meta-regression analyses to investigate the impact of varying STZ doses on male fertility parameters and blood glucose levels. Notably, the timing of blood glucose analysis post-STZ administration was standardized to one week. Moreover, a multivariate meta-regression approach was applied to evaluate the combined influence of several factors. These factors included the duration of evaluation for male fertility parameters, STZ dosage, and the types of animals (rats and mice) involved.

Power analysis

We conducted a power analysis for our meta-analysis to ensure adequate detection of a true effect. Based on preliminary literature review, we set an expected effect size (hedge’s g) of 0.5, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.80 [22, 23]. Including 40 preclinical studies, we used the metafor package in R to calculate power, considering moderate between-study variability (τ² = 0.04). Sensitivity analyses were performed by varying key parameters to ensure robustness.

Publication bias

A visual inspection of Begg’s funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test were used to examine the potential publication bias among outcomes related to all male fertility parameters and blood glucose levels.

Other statistical analysis

In one-factor analyses, the KruskalWallis or U-Mann Whitney tests were used to compare the several variables that summarized the studies included in the meta-analysis. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pharmacological networking

Collection of diabetic-induced male infertility-related gene symbols & construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks

Initially, STZ targets were predicted using its chemical structure imported into the SwissTargetPrediction database (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). Then, the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) was used to standardize target names. The primary gene symbols underlying diabetic-induced male infertility were sourced from four databases: DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://omim.org/) and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). The selected intersecting genes were then uploaded into STRING 11.0 (https://string-db.org/cgi/input.pl/) to generate the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for STZ-associated male infertility [13, 24].

Construction of networks and analysis of target pathways

A “drug-target network” was constructed using Cytoscape 3.6.1 software to further investigate the molecular mechanism of STZ-associated diabetic male infertility. Then, Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were conducted using the DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8 database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) to explore potential pathways affected by STZ in diabetic male infertility [25].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The database search returned 6,015 research studies from 2004 to 2025, and after eliminating 1,088 duplicates, 4,927 studies related to STZ modelling for diabetic male infertility were found. Upon further inspection of the titles and abstracts of these retrieved studies, 3,161 studies were excluded, and 1,766 publications were left for further evaluation based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, ninety-two studies met the inclusion criteria for meta-analysis (Fig. S1). These studies, published from 2005 to 2025, involved a cumulative cohort of 1,535 male rodent subjects (760 controls and 775 models). Rats were utilized as experimental subjects in seventy-three of these studies, while mice were employed in nineteen studies. STZ was administered in all of the studies for diabetic-induced male infertility modelling. All the selected studies were animal pharmacodynamics experiments, and only the data of control and model groups were extracted. The baseline characteristics of all selected publications related to rats are presented in Table 1. The modelling methods in rats included a single dose of intraperitoneal injection or intravascular injection prior to one night of fasting, and the doses of STZ were found in the range of 35 mg/kg to 100 mg/kg. After modelling, observation time varied between 14 days and 90 days. Most of the studies were performed on Wistar rats (54 studies), while few studies, i.e. 19 studies, used the Sprague Dawley strain as experimental models. Seventy studies on rats evaluated male fertility by quantitatively analyzing sperm count and sperm motility. Additionally, forty-six of these studies assessed the percentage of abnormal sperm morphology, while thirty-five studies examined the weight of the testes.

Meta-analysis

The analysis, conducted as a sub-group analysis on rats and mice using the random-effects model, has considered moderate heterogeneity across the studies. The meta-analysis findings have revealed that the primary method used to induce diabetic-induced oligoasthenozoospermia models with STZ involved intraperitoneal injection. In rats, a single dose was commonly administered, while in mice, the typical regimen consisted of single or five consecutive days of dosing.

Figure 1 shows that sperm count decreased significantly in rats and mice after administration of STZ compared with the control groups. The pooled sperm counts decreased in rats by 40.81 × 106/mL (Hedges’ g = -9.03, P = 0.000) while in mice modelling studies, sperm count dropped by 23.05 × 106/mL (Hedges’ g = -6.60, P = 0.000) in the model groups compared to the control groups. However, these values and heterogeneity between studies did not considerably alter in subgroup analysis. Similar decreasing trends were observed in the percentage of pooled sperm motility after STZ dosing in both rats and mice i.e. 32.82% (Hedges’ g = -8.79, P = 0.000) and 28.79% (Hedges’ g = -6.65, P = 0.000) respectively as shown in Fig. 2.

Figure 3 indicates a significant increase in the percentage of abnormal sperm morphology by 23.09% (Hedges’ g = 8.53, P = 0.000) in rats, while abnormal sperm morphology rises in mice as well, i.e. 22.56% (Hedges’ g = 5.92, P = 0.000). There was a significant reduction observed in the weight of testes in rats by 0.534 g (Hedges’ g = -7.84, P = 0.000), while a non-significant decrease was found in mice studies 0.201 g (Hedges’ g = -8.13, P = 0.07) as reflected in Fig. 4. In addition, blood glucose levels were also increased significantly after average one week from STZ administration in both rats and mice i.e. 294.63 mg/dL (Hedges’ g = 18.46, P = 0.000) and 291.99 mg/dL (Hedges’ g = 12.95, P = 0.000) respectively as reflected in Fig. S2. However, using sub-group analysis, the significant overall effects of STZ were validated on all male fertility parameters at different dosing of STZ. This effect remained robust in sensitivity analysis, and the statistical significance was unaffected by any single study included in the meta-analysis as reflected in Table S1.

Effects of STZ dose to induced diabetic male infertility via meta-regression analysis

In the majority of studies, diabetes was successfully induced within approximately one week following the administration of STZ dosing. However, a rigorous meta-regression analysis was performed to comprehensively evaluate the correlation between STZ doses and both blood glucose levels and male fertility parameters. The results of the univariate meta-regression reveal that there is no significant difference found in the effect of worsening in male fertility parameters concerning change in STZ dosing: sperm count (P = 0.137), sperm motility (P = 0.056), percentage of abnormal sperm morphology (P = 0.367), and weight of testis (P = 0.998) as shown in Fig. 5A-D. Furthermore, our analysis found no significant relationship between the doses of STZ administered to induce diabetic or hyperglycemic conditions in both rat and mouse models. This was supported by the absence of changes in blood glucose levels (P = 0.295) at different doses of STZ (Fig. S3). Table S2 provides a comprehensive summary of the collective influence of various factors as evaluated through multivariate meta-regression analysis, such as: duration of evaluation for male fertility parameters (P > 0.05), the dosage of STZ administered (P > 0.05), and the animal species involved (P > 0.05). The analysis focuses on their impact on the effect size, which was defined as the deterioration of diabetic male fertility.

Power analysis

The power analysis indicated that, with the specified parameters, this meta-analysis has an estimated power of approximately 0.81, which is above the desired threshold of 0.80. This suggests that this study is sufficiently powered to detect a true effect size of hedge’s g = 0.5. The sensitivity analysis showed that the power remained robust under different scenarios, such as varying the effect size, number of studies, and sample sizes.

Publication bias

The study of funnel plot standard error by random effect (Hedges’ g) for each variable did not show significant publication bias (Figs. S4-S8). However, it is notable that several SMD values lie beyond the bounds of the funnel plots for each male fertility parameter, especially in the weight of the testis. Table 4 shows the overall results of Egger’s linear regression as followed: sperm count (Coefficient = 1.017; P = < 0.001), sperm motility (Coefficient = 1.105; P = < 0.001), percentage of abnormal sperm morphology (Coefficient = -1.042; P = 0.008), weight of testes (Coefficient = 1.911; P < 0.001), and blood glucose levels (Coefficient = 1.911; P = 0.092).

Pharmacological networking

Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of STZ for diabetic male infertility

We retrieved 108 targets associated with STZ from the SwissTargetPrediction data library and gathered 1264 potential targets for diabetic male infertility from databases including GeneCards, DisGeNET, TTD, and OMIM. This resulted in identifying 84 targets shared between STZ and diabetic male infertility. We then used the STRING database to map the targets of STZ onto diabetic male infertility, as shown in Fig. 6. We focused on three vital topological features (closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and degree) to identify crucial targets within the network. Among these, we identified 60 nodes as key targets of STZ for diabetic male infertility, including GAPDH, PTGS2, CASP3, MAPK1, EFGR and many others.

KEGG and GO enrichment analysis

To investigate the biological properties of relevant STZ targets for diabetic male infertility, detailed KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were conducted. KEGG pathway enrichment revealed a total of 73 relevant pathways associated with STZ, encompassing oxidative stress-related and inflammation-related pathways. Notably, the IL-17 signalling pathway, TNF signalling pathway, and Cellular senescence emerged as the primary pathways linked to oxidative stress and inflammation. From these, the top twenty pathways are presented and selected to construct the lollipop chart (Fig. 7). In addition, the enrichment analysis indicated significant findings across biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). In BP, 56 enrichment terms (P < 0.05) were identified, with the top 20 illustrated in Fig. S9, i.e. response to oxygen-containing compound, protein phosphorylation, and response to organic cyclic compound. CC analysis highlighted various key enrichment terms, including cyclin A2-CDK2 complex, cyclin-dependent protein kinase holoenzyme complex, lysosomal lumen, extracellular membrane-bounded organelle, protein kinase complex, cell-substrate junction, shown in Fig. S10. Meanwhile, Fig. S11 shows 20 enriched MF terms such as G protein-coupled adenosine receptor activity, peroxidase activity, oxidoreductase activity acting on peroxide as acceptor, peroxidase activity, beta-N-acetylhexosaminidase activity (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Despite extensive researches were conducted on animal models of male infertility, validity and clarity of these models was remains uncertain. This uncertainty includes several factors such as delivery method, dosing, modelling cycle time, model recovery, mortality rates, and understanding the underlying mechanisms [8, 116]. Our study aimed to address some of these uncertainties by conducting a meta-analysis to quantitatively measure the effects of STZ on male infertility factors. This study provides a comprehensive understanding of STZ effects on sperm count, motility, abnormal sperm morphology, and testis weight by synthesizing data from various preclinical studies. A recent study by Badejogbin et al., 2024 highlighted the need for a systematic exploration of STZ-induced diabetic male infertility, yet it did not quantitatively address the molecular and physiological mechanisms [117]. However, current study offers a first systematic review, meta-analysis and network pharmacology that validates the STZ model as a useful tool for studying diabetic male infertility.

This study includes data from 92 studies for meta-analysis, reveals a significant impact of STZ on male fertility factors. These results align with earlier findings by Al-khawaldeh et al., 2024, who demonstrated a reduction in sperm motility and sperm count in diabetic rodents [112]. However, current study further expands the effects of STZ on percentage of abnormal sperm morphology and testis weight in order to provide the complete impact of STZ on the reproductive dysfunction. The overall pooled Hedges’ g of -8.56, -8.21, 7.80, and − 7.95 for sperm count, motility, abnormal sperm percentage, and testis weight, respectively, highlight the significant impairment of spermatogenesis and testicular function in STZ-treated rodents. Moreover, alongside the observed declines in male fertility factors, STZ induction was associated with elevated blood glucose levels (overall pooled Hedges’ g = 16.92), indicating the successful induction of diabetic conditions. This dual impact of STZ indicates the complex interaction between metabolic dysfunction and reproductive disorders [118]. Notably, the associated increase in abnormal sperm morphology further reflects the severity of reproductive dysfunction induced by STZ.

Interestingly, there was no significant differences were observed in the effects of STZ between rat and mouse models, despite variations in study designs. This finding contrasts with a previous study by Wang et al., 2024, which suggested species-specific variations in rodent model studies [119]. However, our study suggests that the STZ impact in terms of male infertility outcomes is robust and consistent across rodent species, further supporting the applicability of this model. In addition, a dose-response relationship between STZ dosage and male infertility were found inconsistent. Previous study reported a dose-dependent effect of STZ, but our findings indicate a threshold effect beyond which increasing the STZ dose does not further worsen male fertility damage [120]. This dose-independent effect may be related to several factors, including inter-study variability such as changes in dosing regimens and evaluation timelines. Additionally, it may be possible that once a threshold level systemic oxidative stress and β-cell destruction level is reached, further increases in the dose of STZ do not proportionally exacerbate reproductive toxicity [121]. These observations significantly highlight the non-linear and complex relationship between diabetes and reproductive dysfunction, which requires further investigation.

The findings from the multivariate meta-regression analysis also indicate the robustness of the STZ-induced diabetic male infertility modeling method. Particularly, no significant changes were observed in male fertility damage with variations in several factors, including the duration of male infertility induction, animal species, and STZ dosage. In addition, the high statistical power of this study enhances the reliability of our findings and increases our confidence in the validity of the results obtained from meta-analysis.

In terms of mechanism related to STZ-induced diabetic male infertility, this study proposes a more detailed exploration of the pathways relating hyperglycemia to male reproductive disorders via linking earlier researches and network pharmacology. STZ, a potent diabetogenic compound, causes beta-cell destruction in the pancreas, leading to insulin deficiency and hyperglycemia [101]. Various mechanisms have been proposed in previous studies to elucidate the impact of hyperglycemia on male reproductive health. One mechanism involves the high level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation due to increased glucose metabolism and impaired antioxidant defense mechanisms. Elevated ROS levels contribute to oxidative stress, damaging sperm DNA, membrane lipids, and proteins, damaging sperm quality and other male fertility factors [44, 122]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia activates inflammatory pathways, such as the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, which releases pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. These inflammatory mediators increase oxidative stress and contribute to testicular dysfunction [123, 124]. Additionally, hyperglycemia-induced changes in advanced glycation end products (AGEs) have been associated with reproductive disorders. AGEs accumulate in the testes and contribute to fibrosis, collagen cross-linking, and impaired vascular function, further harmful impact on testicular microcirculation and spermatogenesis [125, 126].

Our study further elucidates these mechanisms by identifying key molecular targets and pathways involved in STZ-induced diabetic male infertility. Using different bioinformatics tools and databases, we identified 84 shared targets between STZ and diabetic male infertility, including key genes such as GAPDH, PTGS2, MAPK1, CASP3, CASP8, MMP9, EGFR, CCND1, and GSK3B. These targets play critical roles in various cellular processes, including apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell proliferation, which are associated in diabetic male infertility [127, 128]. In addition, a comprehensive STZ-target-pathway network was constructed, revealing complex interactions involving the drug, disease, targets, and pathways involved in diabetic male infertility. Key targets such as GAPDH, PTGS2, and MAPK1 occupied central positions within the network, highlighting their essential roles in mediating the effects of STZ on male reproductive function. This network analysis provides a visual representation of the coordination of key molecular targets involved in STZ-induced diabetic male infertility, offering valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of reproductive dysfunction in diabetic conditions. Moreover, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified 73 relevant pathways associated with STZ exposure, focusing on oxidative stress-related and inflammation-related pathways. Notably, the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and Cellular senescence found as key pathways linking STZ-induced oxidative stress and inflammation to diabetic male infertility [129, 130]. These pathways represent potential therapeutic targets for mitigating the adverse effects of STZ on male reproductive health.

Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis highlighted significant biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions associated with STZ-induced diabetic male infertility. Processes such as response to oxygen-containing compounds, protein phosphorylation, and response to organic cyclic compounds were among the top enriched terms, highlighting the complex nature of the molecular alterations responsible for diabetic male infertility induced by STZ exposure. In summary, this study integrates computational and bioinformatics approaches to comprehensively understand the molecular mechanisms underlying STZ-associated diabetic male infertility. By identifying key targets, pathways, and network interactions, provide valuable understandings into the pathophysiology of diabetic male infertility and offer potential strategies for developing targeted therapeutic interventions.

These all findings indicated that the STZ-induced animal models present several advantages for studying diabetic male infertility. These models closely mimic human conditions, offering controlled induction, high reproducibility, flexibility in species, and the exploration of molecular mechanisms using network pharmacology tools. Comparatively, when compared with commonly used models like genetic, diet-induced, hormonal manipulation, and surgical models, STZ-induced models provide a systemic approach to studying the complex nature of diabetic male infertility and its interactions with hormonal pathways. This comparison emphasizes the significance of STZ-induced models in elucidating the mechanism of diabetic male infertility and in the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. This study provides important insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying STZ-induced diabetic male infertility; it also reveals several gaps in current knowledge.

Limitations

The study also faced a few limitations, which should be addressed to provide a more balanced perspective. Firstly, the significant heterogeneity was observed in male fertility outcomes across studies. This variability is linked to differences in rodent’s species, multiple methods for assessing male fertility factors across the analyzed studies. However, we applied random-effects models and performed sub-group analysis, which can help to reduce this heterogeneity [131]. However, challenges were remained persisted in analyzing and comparing specific data findings, such as hormonal changes and histopathological analysis, due to variability in reported outcomes. The absence of a consistent dose-dependent relationship between STZ dosing and its detrimental effects on male infertility parameters was also found a key limitation of our study. This inconsistency in dosing-effects may reduce the ability to establish optimal dosing protocols. In addition, although rodent models provide significant mechanistic insights, the findings of these studies may not fully translate to clinical studies, and further clinical validation is always warranted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression analysis validate the induction of STZ as a robust and reliable model for investigating diabetic male infertility in rodents. Across 92 studies involving 1535 male rodents, consistent impairments in sperm count, motility, and testis weight, along with increased abnormal sperm morphology, were observed after few days of STZ administration. Network pharmacology highlights key targets and pathways associated with oxidative stress and inflammation, reflecting key targets for therapeutic interventions. However, standardization of protocols and consideration of confounding factors related to male infertility are needed to further strengthen the model’s validity. Future translational studies bridging findings from animal models to clinical settings, along with exploration of alternative models, hold promise for advancing our understanding and treatment strategies for diabetic male infertility. By addressing these aspects, the STZ-induced diabetic male infertility model can continue to serve as a valuable tool for studying male reproductive health associated with metabolic disorders. In addition, STZ is a clinically approved drug, so it is vital to take more caution regarding its use in reproductive-age male patients, considering its potential impact on fertility and reproductive health.
