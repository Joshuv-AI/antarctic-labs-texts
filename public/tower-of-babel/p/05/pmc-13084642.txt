Pleural and pericardial effusion in a patient with CLL: CT findings
Authors: Francesco Messina, Grazia Calabrese, Carmela Tebala, Lorena Turano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the case of a 40-year-old female patient with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) who had been chest pain and worsening dyspnea for about ten days. She went to the Emergency Department due to persistent symptoms, and she had clinical, laboratory, and imaging tests. An unenhanced Computed Tomography (CT) was urgently made to treat the patient and for monitoring her clinical conditions. CT had documented the presence of a large amount of pericardial effusion (cardiac tamponade) that was evident circumferentially around the heart, and also a bilateral pleural effusion.Keywords: Pericardial effusion, Pleural effusion, MPR, Computed tomography

Background

Pleural and pericardial effusion may be caused by a variety of diseases, including malignant tumors, pneumonia, tuberculosis, and heart failure. However, pleural and pericardial effusion in an oncological patient, with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) are the evidence of a complication of the disease and therefore they represent a cause of worsening of the patient's quality of life. The use of the computing tomography (CT) imaging in the diagnosis, control and follow-up had rapidly grown, and radiological patterns along the disease course are increasingly understood [1].

Case presentation

A 40-year-old female presented to the Emergency Department of our Hospital with a 2 months complaint of progressive exertional and orthopneic dyspnea when it suddenly got worse. She had symptoms as: tachycardia, tachypnea and hypotension; she also had persistent chest pain (retrosternal, intensified when coughing and laying down) and fever (38°C) since about a week.

She had urgently made an unenhanced Computed Tomography (since she also had a renal failure, from nefropaty), that demonstrated (Figs. 1A and B) the presence of a large amount of pericardial effusion (cardiac tamponade) that was circumferentially around the heart, with the following thicknesses: 7 cms (antero-lateral); 4 cms (left postero-lateral); 3 cms (antero-apical).

Computed tomography patterns and MPR reconstructions: (A) axial and (B) sagittal. Pericardial effusion (cardiac tamponade) that was CT identified circumferentially around the heart (red arrows). A pleural effusion was identified bilaterally (blue arrows).

CT had also showed a bilateral bi-basal pleural effusion, with some areas of pulmonary consolidations on the left lung. Some lymphadenopathies were also found in the mediastinal area, which should be correlated with the haematological disease.

The patient was immediately hospitalized, and other clinical and laboratory tests were subsequently carried out. An emergency pericardiocentesis was also performed. After about 3 weeks of hospitalization, the patient had returned at home. Now the patient will subsequently perform a clinical-radiological and laboratory follow-up.

Discussion

The development of a malignant pericardial effusion in patients with disseminated malignancy is not uncommon. The most common tumors to involve the pericardium are lung, breast, lymphomas, leukemias, and melanoma. They account for approximately 70% of malignant pericardial effusions that are clinically diagnosed. Occasionally pericardial involvement may be the first clinical manifestation of an undiagnosed malignant tumor. Recognizing its presence is particularly important because of the life-threatening potential of cardiac tamponade. Management is guided by the haemodynamic impact, size, presence of inflammation (ie, pericarditis), associated medical conditions, and the aetiology whenever possible [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

CLL is a blood cancer that affects B lymphocytes of the immune system. As the leukemia disease progresses, which has a highly variable clinical course, symptoms such as lymphadenopathies, fatigue, fever, night sweats and weight loss may appear. While there is no direct relationship between CLL and pericardial and pleural effusion, in some cases, CLL may be associated with both of them, often as a complication of other conditions or treatments. CLL may be associated with infections, which in turn can cause pericardial and pleural effusion. Furthermore, CLL can lead to a weakened immune system, making the patient more susceptible to infections that can cause pericardial and pleural effusion [6,7].

Computed Tomography is a very important useful diagnostic tool for assessing the extent of pleural and pericardial effusion in leukemia or in other oncologic conditions. CT can help locate the pericardial and pleural effusion, assess their quantity, and identify any other diagnostic causes, such as infectious and respiratory hypotheses [8,9]. Conclusions

This case illustrates that pericardial effusion and pleural effusion are 2 important conditions that must be promptly identified, treated and monitored over time. We think that an integrative approach, with Computed Tomography, can help very well to display the detailed anatomic features of the entire pericardium and pleura more clearly than other methodics, and so CT had been found to be an excellent tool for detecting pericardial and pleural effusion and their clinical cHse, also for a better management of the patients.
