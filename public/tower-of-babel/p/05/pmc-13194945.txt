Modulating nuclear stiffness and envelope barrier facilitates AAV nuclear entry and reduces immunogenicity
Authors: Yangsen Ou, Fuhua Wu, Chunting He, Shuang Luo, Zhaofei Guo, Yongshun Zhang, Rui Hu, Yanhua Xu, Xue Tang, Yuanhao Zhao, Lin Ye, Xiaoyan Chen, Shuting Bai, Guangsheng Du, Xun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAVs) have shown promise for orthopedic diseases. However, conventional AAV vectors face major challenges, where immune responses reduce transgene efficacy and inadequate vector potency requires higher doses, leading to increased costs and safety concerns. Here, we show that Pluronic F-127 (PF127) micelles encapsulating rapamycin (PF127-RAPA), co-administered with AAV vectors, significantly enhanced AAV transduction efficiency and reduce its immunogenicity. Mechanistically, PF127-RAPA modulates the morphology and mechanical properties of the nuclear membrane, which is associated with enhanced passive transport mediated by nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) and thereby increased AAV nuclear entry. Concurrently, PF127-RAPA reduced AAV particle accumulation in the cytoplasm, minimizing MHC class I presentation of capsid proteins and attenuating the cellular immune response. Notably, in postmenopausal osteoporosis and rheumatoid arthritis mouse models, PF127-RAPA enabled AAV6-SEMA3A to achieve a 75% reduction in dosage while maintaining potent efficacy. This work presents a clinically translatable, cost-effective nanotechnology approach to enhance AAV-based therapies for osteoimmunological disorders.Subject terms: Gene delivery, Nanobiotechnology, Gene therapy

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is a 26 nm non-enveloped parvovirus1. Its high transduction efficiency, persistent transgene expression, and lack of pathogenicity make AAV one of the most attractive gene therapy vectors currently available. As of August 2025, there were 214 interventional clinical trials involving rAAV registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, primarily focusing on diseases affecting the eyes, muscles, nervous system, or metabolic disorders. However, gene therapies for bone and joint diseases based on AAV are limited. Colella et al.2 identified two key challenges in achieving and maintaining therapeutic transgene expression: AAV immunogenicity and vector potency. First, seroprevalence studies show that 40–80% of humans harbor pre-existing neutralizing antibodies against natural AAV variants, limiting its clinical translation1. The continuous presence of the host adaptive immune response can impact the effectiveness of AAV in therapeutic applications. Second, enhancing AAV potency is crucial to achieving more efficient transgene expression at lower vector doses, which can further reduce immune toxicity and manufacturing costs. Preclinical studies have shown that high vector doses can lead to acute liver failure and shock in non-human primates3. Furthermore, the high production costs of AAV remain a significant barrier to gene therapy. Even a 50% reduction in vector dose could substantially impact both safety and cost. To address these challenges, we sought breakthroughs by focusing on the nuclear trafficking, one of the most important events in AAV infection4,5. The nuclear entry capability of AAV not only determines its transduction efficacy but also directly affects the immune response against the AAV capsid6. Moreover, clinical trials have demonstrated that capsid-specific CD8+ T cell responses can mediate the elimination of AAV-transduced cells, thereby undermining the therapeutic efficacy of gene delivery7. Thus, enhancing nuclear entry can improve AAV transduction and diminish the immune response.

Recently, it has been reported that the increased nuclear membrane tension leads to the stretching of nuclear transport complex (NPCs) and dilation of their diameter8, further improving the nucleocytoplasmic transport activity. Amphipathic molecules, such as Pluronic F-68 and F-127 (currently the only two Pluronics known to be FDA-approved, biocompatible, and in clinical use), insert their hydrophobic tails into the lipid bilayer of biological membranes, while their hydrophilic heads remain exposed to the membrane’s exterior or interior. This insertion alters the physical properties of biological membranes, such as increasing permeability, compromising membrane integrity, or inducing membrane reorganization9,10. Therefore, we aim to modulate the concentration of amphiphilic molecules (above the critical micelle concentration) to facilitate micelle formation in the extracellular space, with the hydrophobic segments shielded internally. Once internalized, the micelles would degrade into monomeric forms in the cytoplasm, exposing the hydrophobic segments. This process would then reprogram the nuclear membrane, promoting AAV entry into the cell nucleus11–13. Additionally, to further enhance the inhibition of the immune response to AAV capsids, we aim to leverage the drug delivery capabilities of Pluronic micelles. By co-loading small molecule immunosuppressants, we seek to achieve a synergistic effect between the Pluronic micelles and the immunosuppressants. Currently, some clinical trials use rapamycin as an immunosuppressive regimen to reduce immune responses caused by AAV14. In addition, there have been reports of using ImmTOR nanoparticles encapsulating a high dose of rapamycin (200 µg) in combination with AAV vectors to inhibit AAV capsid-specific immune responses15,16. However, the use of high-dose rapamycin often increases the risk of infections in patients and can have certain detrimental effects on kidney function and hematopoiesis16,17. Our previous research suggested that low-dose rapamycin (5–10 μg per mouse) could have a better induction of immune tolerance compared to high-dose rapamycin (over 20 μg per mouse), while also avoiding the systemic immunosuppression associated with high doses18. Harnessing Pluronic’s dual capacity to remodel biomembrane and to form rapamycin-loaded nanomicelles, we propose a strategy that simultaneously promotes AAV nuclear entry and quells immunity, thereby amplifying vector potency while curbing immunogenicity.

The field of osteoimmunology mainly explores the intricate relationship between the skeletal and immune systems, aiming to unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying bone destruction in inflammatory conditions19–21. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and postmenopausal osteoporosis are two prevalent orthopedic disorders. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), characterized by immune system dysregulation, manifest in enhanced bone resorption driven by osteoclasts, leading to bone damage22–24. Similarly, postmenopausal osteoporosis, primarily triggered by estrogen deficiency, involves immune-mediated inflammation, exacerbating osteoclast differentiation and bone loss25–27. Hence, the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis and postmenopausal osteoporosis is associated with disruptions in the bone-immune system. Current treatment approaches, however, typically target individual diseases with symptomatic therapies. The former is primarily managed with corticosteroids and monoclonal antibodies, while the latter is treated with anti-resorptive agents (bisphosphonates) and bone-forming drugs (Teriparatide acetate)28. Unfortunately, these therapies are often expensive, require frequent administration, and are associated with significant long-term side effects29,30. Additionally, the pathological processes of RA and postmenopausal osteoporosis demonstrate substantial overlap, yet current treatments are largely disease-specific and do not provide a unified approach to simultaneously address both conditions. Thus, the quest for novel therapies simultaneously addressing both conditions is paramount, aiming to rebalance immune activation and restore bone homeostasis.

Semaphorin3a (SEMA3A), a member of the semaphorin family, holds a unique position in bone protection and immune regulation31,32. It acts as a vital regulator of bone homeostasis under estrogen influence, inhibiting osteoclastic bone resorption while promoting osteoblastic bone formation33. Furthermore, SEMA3A, serving as an effective immunosuppressive factor, is expressed and secreted by activated T cells, binding to Nrp-1 and PlexinA1 on DCs to suppress immune synapse-mediated T cell activation34,35. These properties highlight SEMA3A’s potential in modulating the balance between the bone and immune systems, offering promise in treating RA and postmenopausal osteoporosis.

In this study, to enhance AAV therapeutic efficacy and reduce capsid-related immune responses, we developed PF127 micelles loaded with rapamycin (PF127-RAPA). A simple mixture of AAV and PF127-RAPA micelles can significantly enhance AAV transduction efficiency both in vitro and in vivo, while also mitigating capsid-induced immune responses. Additionally, our research revealed that PF127 micelles enhance AAV nuclear entry by altering the tension of the nuclear membrane, thereby increasing AAV transduction efficiency. To further assess the therapeutic effects and adverse reactions of PF127-RAPA micelles in disease models, we engineered a recombinant adeno-associated virus expressing Semaphorin3a (AAV6-SEMA3A). In osteoporosis and rheumatoid arthritis models, we demonstrated that local delivery of rAAV6 carrying the SEMA3A gene through various administration routes effectively transduced the femur, muscles, or popliteal lymph nodes, thereby suppressing the inflammatory phenotype and bone damage in collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mice, while restoring bone mass in ovariectomy (OVX) mice. Additionally, one forth dose of AAV with PF127-RAPA micelles achieved therapeutic efficacy equivalent to AAV only group while effectively reducing capsid antibody production. This offers a straightforward and effective formulation approach with clear mechanistic insights, promising in improving cost-effectiveness and reducing adverse reactions of AAV clinical utilization.

Results

PF127-RAPA micelles enhance AAV transduction and reduce capsid antibodies

In clinical applications, the dosage for local administration of AAV is in the range of 1012–1013 vg/site, while the dosage for systemic administration can be as high as 1013–1014 vg/kg36–38. In addition, a significant number of patients are excluded from clinical trials due to pre-existing neutralizing antibodies. Vector potency and immunotoxicity are key factors limiting AAV dosage. Herein, we demonstrated that PF127 micelles loaded with rapamycin significantly enhanced AAV transduction efficiency while reducing the capsid neutralizing antibodies.

PEO-PPO-PEO copolymers are nonionic triblock copolymers based on hydrophilic polyethylene oxide (PEO) and hydrophobic polypropylene oxide (PPO). Currently, two Pluronic copolymers, PF127 and PF68, have been approved for clinical use39. These copolymers are capable of self-assembling into micelles above the critical micelle concentration. We purposed the highly hydrophilic PEO segments interact with the amino acid residues abundant on the AAV capsid surface via hydrogen bonding, enabling their co-delivery (Fig. 1a). We first evaluated the effect of two Pluronic (PF127&PF68) on AAV transduction capability at the cellular level. We set three concentrations of Poloxamers: 0.01% (below CMC), 0.4% (greater than CMC), and 1% (far greater than CMC). We observed that 0.4% PF127 enhanced AAV6 expression in HEK293T cells by approximately 1.4-fold. However, at 0.01% (below the CMC), PF127 did not improve AAV transduction efficiency (Fig. 1b, c). In contrast, PF68 only exhibited a comparable effect at a 1% concentration. Notably, cytotoxicity assay revealed that both Pluronic compounds showed significant cytotoxicity at this concentration (1%) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, the observed increase in transgene expression may be attributed to a higher MOI resulting from the cytotoxicity of 1% Pluronics, rather than a direct enhancement of transduction efficiency.

Due to the success of ImmTOR nanoparticles in reducing the in vivo neutralizing antibody levels of monoclonal antibody15, we considered encapsulating the water-insoluble drug rapamycin (RAPA) into PF127 micelles, with the goal of reducing the impact of neutralizing antibodies against the AAV capsid (Fig. 1d). We assessed the particle size distribution and zeta potential of the PF127 micelles encapsulating rapamycin (PF127-RAPA). The results revealed that the hydrodynamic diameter of the PF127-RAPA micelles is approximately 96.08 ± 2.386 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of less than 0.2. Electrophoretic light scattering analysis revealed a zeta potential of −0.87 ± 0.01 mV for PF127-RAPA (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Additionally, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements demonstrated that the particle size and PDI of PF127-RAPA remained stable at room temperature over a period of 7 days (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Furthermore, to investigate its stability in biological media, we mixed PF127-RAPA with an equal volume of fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated the mixture at 37 °C. The transmittance remained above 90% within 24 h, indicating that the prepared PF127-RAPA exhibits good serum stability (Supplementary Fig. 2d). As illustrated in Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3, AAV could be effectively displayed on the surface of PF127-RAPA micelles through a simple mixing process, driven by the abundant hydrogen bond interactions. We first validated this approach in HEK293T cells, finding that PF127-RAPA micelles significantly improved AAV6 transduction efficiency when mixed with the virus. This effect was comparable to that observed with PF127 + AAV (Fig. 1f, g). Additionally, we also investigated the ability of PF127-RAPA micelles and PF127 micelles to enhance transduction efficiency of another AAV serotype, AAV8, in ARPE19 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Furthermore, we specifically investigated whether PF127 could enhance the transduction efficiency of AAV in primary and non-dividing cells. We isolated and induced differentiation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDC) and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) from C57BL/6J mice. The BMDC and BMDM differentiated in vitro can be considered terminally differentiated cells that lack proliferative capacity. Consequently, we utilized AAV6 to infect BMDC and AAV1 to infect BMDM (Supplementary Fig. 5). The results demonstrated that both PF127 and PF127-RAPA effectively enhanced AAV transduction in these two types of primary cells (non-dividing cells). The results showed that both PF127-RAPA micelles and PF127 micelles elevated AAV transduction efficiency to a similar extent across different cell lines and serotypes, underscoring the broad applicability of the approach.

To further investigate whether the enhancement effect of PF127-RAPA micelles on AAV can achieve dose-sparing benefits. We designed an in vitro experiment to explore the enhanced transduction capacity of AAV by PF127-RAPA. In this approach, we used varying doses of AAV6-GFP (with or without PF127-RAPA) to infect HEK293T cells. We first established a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 × 104 as the full dose (1). Subsequently, we set up intermediate doses at MOIs of 7.5 × 103 (3/4), 5 × 103 (1/2), 2.5 × 103 (1/4), 1.25 × 103 (1/8), 6.25 × 102 (1/16), 3.125 × 102 (1/32), and 1.56 × 102 (1/64). We quantified the transgene expression of AAV based on the positive rates observed in flow cytometry for different doses. The results indicated that PF127-RAPA enabled the 1/8 dose of AAV (14.02%) to achieve a level of HEK293T cell infection comparable to that of the full dose AAV (15.04%) (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, when the dose was reduced to 1/16, the combination of PF127-RAPA and AAV yielded only a 5.93% positive cell rate, significantly lower than that of the full dose. Therefore, we conclude that the dosage threshold for PF127-RAPA-enhanced AAV transduction is 1/8.

Furthermore, we investigated the effect of PF127-RAPA micelles and PF127 micelles on AAV transduction efficiency in vivo. We injected 5 × 109 vg of AAV6-Luc alone or mixed with PF127 micelles, PF127-RAPA micelles, or free rapamycin into the ankle joints of mice and monitored the expression kinetics. The results demonstrated that both PF127-RAPA micelles and PF127 micelles significantly enhanced AAV-mediated transgene expression at the injection site in mice (Fig. 1h). Specifically, PF127-RAPA micelles increased transgene expression by 4.7-fold compared to free AAV on day 30, by 145-fold on day 60, and still achieved a 98-fold increase compared to free AAV on day 80 (Fig. 1i) (PF127 group was comparable to PF127-RAPA group). In addition, to investigate whether PF127-RAPA micelles can reduce AAV capsid antibodies, we measured the AAV6 capsid-specific antibody levels on day 30 using ELISA. The results showed that the AAV capsid antibody titers in the PF127-RAPA micelle group were significantly lower than those in the free AAV group (4.5-fold) (Fig. 1j). However, PF127 group and free RAPA group only showed a 2-fold reduction in titer levels. Additionally, using intramuscular injection, we found that PF127-RAPA micelles could also enhance AAV in vivo transduction while reducing capsid antibody titers (Supplementary Fig. 7). Overall, these in vivo results confirmed that both PF127 and PF127-RAPA micelles effectively enhance AAV transduction efficiency. These results suggested that the enhanced transduction efficiency is primarily attributable to PF127, with rapamycin playing a less prominent role under these conditions.

PF127-RAPA micelles enhance AAV transduction by increasing the efficiency of AAV nuclear entry

Nuclear translocation of AAV is regarded as a limiting step for successful transduction. It has been reported only 17% of AAV particles are able to successfully enter the nucleus40. Furthermore, AAV particles failing to access the nuclear are subjected to proteasomal degradation and ubiquitination, which triggers a cellular immune response against the AAV capsid and results in the diminished AAV transduction efficiency (Fig. 2a). Herein, we explored the mechanisms underlying the enhanced AAV transduction efficiency of PF127-RAPA micelles by examining the four key steps involved in AAV transduction, including (1) AAV binding, (2) AAV released, (3) Nuclear entry and (4) Second strand synthesis.

First, we used the Cy5-labeled AAV6 to infect HEK293T cells, observing that the higher MOI correlated with stronger membrane localization of AAV. However, confocal images showed that PF127 micelles did not enhance the colocalization of Cy5-AAV at the cell membrane (Fig. 2b, c). A virus-cell binding assay further confirmed that neither PF127 micelles, PF127-RAPA micelles, nor free rapamycin affected the binding of AAV to cells (Fig. 2d). We then investigated whether PF127 micelles might provide a novel intracellular entry pathway independent of receptor-mediated mechanisms. To test this possibility, we used neuraminidase to remove sialic acid from the cell surface and conducted the infection study with AAV1, an AAV serotype that primarily uses sialic acid as a cellular receptor. As expected, sialic acid removal significantly reduced AAV transduction in all groups. Notably, PF127 micelles significantly enhanced transgene expression efficiency (1.90-fold) even after the removal of sialic acid, a level comparable to the increase observed when sialic acid levels are normal (2.05-fold). However, following sialidase treatment, the overall expression level was lower than that observed without sialidase treatment. Furthermore, to probe the intracellular basis of PF127-mediated enhancement, we set up a sequential administration of PF127 and AAV with a 4 h interval—matching the reported timeframe for AAV nuclear entry, which created distinct spatiotemporal distributions of AAV and PF127 micelles within the cells (Fig. 2g). Firstly, sequential administration of PF127 micelles and AAV (non-admix), can also enhance transgene expression. Notably, pre-treating cells with PF127 micelles prior to AAV infection yields a greater increase in transduction efficiency compared to administering PF127 after AAV infection (Fig. 2h). This suggests that PF127 micelles might act intracellularly to facilitate subsequent AAV transduction, potentially altering the intracellular fate of AAV. Thus, these findings suggested that PF127 micelles influenced the intracellular trafficking of AAV, ultimately leading to enhanced transduction.

Furthermore, we conducted a nuclear uptake assay to quantitatively measure viral genome content in the cytoplasm and nucleus of cells 4 h post-AAV infection (Fig. 2i). The nuclear fraction ratios for PF127 micelles (43.6%) and PF127-RAPA micelles (43.0%) were significantly higher than that of free AAV (24.4%) (Fig. 2j).

PF127 alters nuclear membrane mechanics, affecting AAV transcytosis across nuclear envelope

To further investigate the mechanism by which PF127 influences AAV nuclear entry, we conducted a more detailed investigation into the effects of PF127 on both active and passive transport mechanisms into the nucleus. Firstly, we treated ARPE19 cells with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), an inhibitor of active nuclear import, and then added PF127 micelles and PF127-RAPA micelles, each mixed with AAV8-GFP (Fig. 3a). WGA significantly inhibited AAV-mediated transgene expression by suppressing AAV nuclear entry. However, even in the presence of WGA, both PF127 and PF127-RAPA micelles still enhanced AAV transgene expression by 2.13-fold and 2.26-fold, respectively (Fig. 3a). AAV capsid proteins typically contain nuclear localization signals (NLS) and rely on the importin-α/β receptor complex to traverse the nuclear pore complex (NPC) into the nucleus. To investigate whether PF127 enhances AAV nuclear entry via the NPC, we utilized Importazole, a specific NPC blocker, to inhibit NPC-mediated AAV nuclear entry (Fig. 3b). Our results demonstrated that Importazole effectively suppressed AAV transduction. However, both PF127 and PF127-RAPA mitigated this inhibition, increasing AAV transgene expression levels by approximately 3.99-fold and 3.81-fold, respectively. Additionally, we explored the impact of PF127 on passive transport into the nucleus using fluorescent dextrans of varying sizes (10 kDa, 40 kDa, and 70 kDa). The results revealed a clear size effect on passive transport, as larger molecular weights corresponded to decreased fluorescent signals within the nucleus. Notably, PF127 significantly enhanced the passive transport efficiency of 40 kDa and 70 kDa fluorescent dextrans into the nucleus (Fig. 3c). Therefore, these results suggest that the enhancement of AAV nuclear entry by PF127 is related to its role in promoting passive transport into the nucleus. These results further support the hypothesis that PF127 enhances AAV transduction by promoting nuclear entry. Next, we conducted an in vivo study to validate this conclusion (Supplementary Fig. 8a). On day 18, after the intramuscular injection of AAV6-GFP with or without PF127-RAPA micelles, we quantitatively measured the viral genome content in the tissue of interest. In muscle, the PF127-RAPA + AAV6-GFP group showed a 1.94-fold increase in AAV copy number per unit mass compared to the free AAV6-GFP group. In femur, this increase was 2.8-fold (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Because the measurement was performed 18 days post-infection, by which time most AAV genomes are expected to have entered the nucleus and initiated expression41, the detected AAV genomes likely reflect primarily nuclear localization.

The diameter of the nuclear pore complex (NPC) directly influences nuclear transport activities, and changes in nuclear tension are reflected by alterations in NPC diameter8. We first showed that PF127 micelles co-localize with the cell nucleus, providing a spatial basis for modulating nuclear morphology (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, we assessed the nuclear stiffness following incubation with PF127 micelles or AAV using nanoindentation (Fig. 3e). The results indicated a trend of increased nuclear stiffness following AAV infection (from 1.36 kPa to 1.99 kPa), likely due to AAV binding to the nuclear membrane, which overall enhanced nuclear rigidity. The addition of PF127 micelles markedly augmented the nuclear stiffness after AAV infection (3.04 kPa), and PF127 micelles alone also elevated nuclear stiffness (2.25 kPa). To further investigate the effect of PF127 on the mechanical properties of the nuclear membrane, we examined the impact of PF127 on the nuclear membrane fluidity in HEK293T and B16F10 cells (Fig. 3f, g). Through Laurdan staining of the nuclear membrane, we observed that PF127 significantly increased the generalized polarization (GP) values in these two cell types, indicating that PF127 effectively enhances the stiffness of the nuclear membrane, which is consistent with the results of the Young’s modulus measurements (Fig. 3e).

Since changes in nuclear membrane tension can directly affect the spacing between the inner and outer nuclear membranes42, we employed electron microscopy to examine the morphology of the nuclear membrane (Fig. 3h, i). Our results demonstrated that treatment with PF127 resulted in a reduction of approximately 38% in nuclear membrane spacing (NE distance), indicating that the nuclear membrane is in a stretched state and suggesting an increase in nuclear membrane tension.

These effects may be due to PF127 incorporation into the nuclear membrane’s lipid bilayer, thereby increasing membrane tension. These findings suggest that PF127 enhances AAV nuclear entry by modulating nuclear tension, which leads to an expansion of NPC diameter and facilitates passive transport of AAV into the nucleus (Fig. 3j).

PF127-RAPA micelles attenuate the cellular immune response triggered by AAV capsids

From the results above, we found that PF127 micelles can increase the nuclear entry of AAV, reducing the amount of AAV in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2h). The reduction of cytoplasmic AAV may decrease proteasomal degradation of AAV capsids, potentially leading to reduced MHC class I presentation and suppression of capsid-specific cellular immune responses. Additionally, rapamycin, as an immunosuppressant, has also been reported to suppress antibody responses against AAV capsids15. Therefore, we further investigated the effect of PF127-RAPA micelles on AAV capsid-specific immune responses (Fig. 4a). We measured AAV6 capsid-specific antibodies by ELISA at Days 14–38 after AAV injections. The results showed that intramuscular AAV injection induced a strong anti-AAV6 capsid immunoglobulin G (IgG) and IgG2a antibody responses, while IgG1 antibodies were almost absent (Fig. 4b). This also suggested that the AAV capsid may primarily induce a Th1-skewed immune response, consistent with previous reports43,44. In addition, when AAV was mixed with PF127-RAPA micelles and rapamycin, the levels of IgG and IgG2a antibodies were significantly reduced at each time point, respectively. Admixing AAV with PF127 micelles also resulted in a noticeable decrease in IgG and IgG2a antibody levels, beginning at day 28 (Fig. 4b).

Immune cell profiling of spleen samples revealed that all intervention groups (PF127-RAPA, PF127, and RAPA) inhibited Th1 cell differentiation. This suppression was more pronounced in the PF127-RAPA and RAPA groups (Fig. 4c). This also inhibited cytotoxic CD8+ T cell differentiation (Fig. 4d, e). Meanwhile, both the PF127-RAPA and RAPA groups inhibited the differentiation of functional CD4+ memory T cells (CD4+CD44+MHC-II-TNF-α+) and CD8+ memory T cells (CD8+CD44+MHC-II-TNF-α+ and CD8+CD44+MHC-II-IFN-γ+) (Fig. 4f–h). Furthermore, ELISPOT analysis showed that the PF127-RAPA and RAPA groups significantly reduced IFN-γ production, with levels 2.72-fold and 1.78-fold lower than the AAV6-GFP group, respectively (Fig. 4i). These results suggest that these treatments may also influence immune memory against the AAV capsid. Consistent with the above findings, the PF127-RAPA group exhibited the lowest levels of TNF-α and IL-4 secreted by stimulated splenic lymphocytes (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Notably, the PF127-RAPA group significantly increased IL-10 secretion (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 9c). Furthermore, we observed similar effects in T cells within the popliteal lymph nodes (Supplementary Fig. 10). The results showed that PF127-RAPA micelles inhibited the formation of germinal centers in the pLNs (Supplementary Fig. 10a), which may be directly correlated with a reduction in antibody titers. Additionally, PF127-RAPA micelles suppressed T cell activation within the lymph nodes and facilitated the differentiation of Treg cells (Supplementary Fig. 10b–d).

To determine whether PF127-RAPA micelles could reduce the capsid immune response to AAV administration in the presence of pre-existing AAV antibodies, we performed the following experiment. Mice were intramuscularly injected with free AAV6-GFP or PF127-RAPA + AAV6-GFP on day 0 to establish a pre-existing immune response. On day 43, we intravenously injected the mice with AAV6-Luc or PF127-RAPA + AAV6-Luc (Fig. 3i). As expected, repeated administration of the same AAV serotype, in the presence of pre-existing antibodies, resulted in a stronger immune response (increased 36-fold compared to the antibody titer on Day 38). However, when the second dose was administered with PF127-RAPA + AAV, the increase in the capsid immune response was significantly reduced (only increased 3-fold compared to the antibody titer on Day 38). When both doses were administered with PF127-RAPA + AAV, the initial immune response was much lower, and the increase after the second dose was only 2.8-fold compared to the antibody titer on Day 38 (Fig. 3j). These results further indicated that co-administration of PF127-RAPA micelles with AAV can mitigate the adaptive immune response elicited by repeated vector administrations.

We propose that PF127-RAPA not only reduces capsid-specific cellular immune responses (TNF-α+CD8+ T and IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells), thereby decreasing the attack on cells carrying AAV-transgenes, but also enhances AAV nuclear entry. This enhancement may reduce the degradation of capsids by proteasomes in the cytoplasm and decrease the presentation of capsid peptides via MHC class I, thereby minimizing the number of target cells recognizable by capsid-specific CD8+ T cells. To validate these two hypotheses, we designed an in vitro cytotoxicity assay (Fig. 4m). We immunized mice with AAV2-GFP and collected splenic lymphocytes for culture on day 21 post-immunization. Concurrently, we infected Hepa1-6 cells (a mouse cell line with strong MHC class I presentation capability) with AAV2-Luc and PF127 + AAV2-Luc. After 24 h, we co-cultured splenic lymphocytes containing capsid-specific CD8+ T cells with the infected Hepa1-6 cells for 5 h, followed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in the supernatant to assess cytotoxicity. The results indicated that at an MOI of 5 × 104, the addition of PF127 reduced AAV capsid-mediated CTL killing efficiency from 31.3% to 23.0%, and at an MOI of 2 × 104, it decreased the killing efficiency from 26.0% to 16.4% (Fig. 4n). This demonstrates that PF127 effectively reduces the clearance of transgene-expressing cells by capsid-specific CD8+ T cells. Furthermore, to confirm that this reduction in clearance rate is due to decreased capsid MHC class I presentation, we assessed the ubiquitination levels of capsids in AAV-infected cells. Referring to previous experimental protocols (Fig. 4o), we employed an anti-AAV2 capsid antibody (A20R) for immunoprecipitation to enrich AAV2 capsid proteins, followed by quantification of capsid protein ubiquitination levels using Ubiquitination-specific ELISA. The results revealed that PF127 significantly reduced the levels of ubiquitinated AAV2 capsid proteins in HeLa cells (Fig. 4p).

SEMA3A regulated the crosstalk between the bone and immune cells to maintain bone homeostasis in vitro

To further evaluate the role of PF127-RAPA micelles in enhancing AAV-mediated gene therapy and mitigating capsid-induced immune responses, we selected rheumatoid arthritis and postmenopausal osteoporosis mouse models for validation. As both conditions are inflammatory bone diseases resulting from the disruption of bone immune homeostasis, our goal is to restore this homeostasis with a single therapeutic gene, thereby addressing both diseases. Previous studies have shown that SEMA3A alleviates inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis and prevents bone loss in osteoporosis45,46. Thus, we aimed to assess whether SEMA3A can concurrently target both diseases through the bone-immune network.

Activation of T cells is mainly driven by antigen presentation and costimulatory molecule expression by DC cells. Activated T cells differentiate into Th1 or Th17 cells and secrete proinflammatory cytokines, which promote macrophage M1 polarization and induce osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 5a). Due to the disrupted osteoimmune system homeostasis, osteoclasts apoptosis is inhibited, leading to a prolongation of lifespan. In order to investigate whether SEMA3A could promote the apoptosis of osteoclasts, the apoptosis profile was determined by the annexin V-FITC/PI assay (Fig. 5b). As shown in Fig. 4c, 200 ng/ml SEMA3A treatment significantly increased the apoptosis ratio of osteoclasts. Furthermore, we examined whether SEMA3A can reprogram macrophages with M1 phenotype to M2 phenotype (Fig. 5b). The polarization of bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) was determined by flow cytometry assay. Compared with the control group, SEMA3A treatment significantly decreased the percentages of M1 macrophages (F4/80+CD80+CD206−) and increased the percentages of M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD80−CD206+) (Fig.5d, e). In addition, IL-6 and TNF-α levels detected in the culture supernatant of cells treated with 200 ng/mL SEMA3A were reduced by 2.19-fold and 2.02-fold, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 5f, g). Furthermore, IL-10 levels detected in the culture supernatant of cells treated with 200 ng/mL SEMA3A were increased by 3.51-fold, compared to the control (Fig. 5h).

To investigate whether SEMA3A could inhibit the interaction between DC and T cells, a mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) was performed. Specifically, SIINFEKL-activated BMDCs were co-cultured with splenocytes in the absence or presence of SEMA3A (Fig. 5i). As we found, SEMA3A treatment decreased the proportion of activated T cells (CD3+CD69+) (Fig. 5j). Moreover, SEMA3A could promote the differentiation of Tregs, contributing to the maintenance of immune homeostasis (Fig. 5k). Furthermore, to examine the effect of SEMA3A on immune synapse formation, B3Z T cell hybridomas (which produce β-galactosidase upon recognizing MHC-I-SIINFEKL complexes) were co-cultured with BMDCs in the presence of SEMA3A. The β-galactosidase activity assay showed that 200 ng/mL SEMA3A suppressed MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation, demonstrating that SEMA3A inhibits T cell activation by interfering with immune synapse formation (Fig. 5l). These results demonstrated that SEMA3A effectively maintained osteoimmune homeostasis by inhibiting T cell activation, repolarizing macrophages, and promoting OC apoptosis and Tregs differentiation.

Transduction of AAV serotypes in ankle joints

In previous studies, AAV5, AAV6, and AAV8 were reported as transgene vectors in joints47–49. To identify the optimal AAV serotype for ankle infection, we packaged the luciferase (Luc) reporter gene into three AAV capsids (AAV5, AAV6, and AAV8), and these rAAVs were administered intra-articularly (i.a.) into the ankle joints of 8-week-old DBA/1 mice. Ankle and liver luminescence were evaluated to compare the transgene expression and potential hepatotoxicities. In general, AAV6 showed the highest ankle transduction efficiency and a relatively low liver tropism (Supplementary Fig. 11a–c). The total radiance of ankle for AAV6 is more than 14 times that of AAV8 and 128 times that of AAV5 on day 21. Meanwhile, AAV8 transduced liver much more efficiently than AAV5 and AAV6 on day 21, indicating a higher hepatotoxicity of AAV8 with intra-articular injection. Considering the chronic condition of many orthopedics, the long-term therapeutic transgene expression is important. To investigate the persistence of AAV6-based transgenic in the ankle, we injected AAV6-Luc into both ankle joints. Notably, luciferase expression was detected at a high level for at least 24 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e).

The influence of administration routes on AAV distribution and transgene therapeutic efficacy

Based on previous results (Supplementary Fig. 11a–e) and preliminary data from our research consortium, AAV6 was identified as the optimal serotype for concurrent intra-articular and intramuscular injections50. While various AAV administration routes, including intramuscular, intra-articular, and intravenous injections, have been used in orthopedic therapeutic studies51, different administration routes can significantly affect AAV distribution. Therefore, we evaluated the effects of three administration methods on AAV6 distribution to determine the optimal approach for treating osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. Eighteen days post-administration, viral genomes were extracted from various mouse tissues and normalized by weight (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Our findings indicated that following intra-articular injection of ankle, AAV6 showed significantly higher enrichment in the ankle joint and popliteal lymph nodes compared to other administration routes (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Moreover, intramuscular injection led to greater enrichment of AAV6 in muscle tissues and the femur/tibia compared to intra-articular and intravenous injections (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Additionally, compared to intravenous injection, both intramuscular and intra-articular injections resulted in notably weaker transduction of visceral organ, effectively reducing the off-target side effects of AAV.

To examine the tropism of AAV6 to popliteal lymph node and bone, mCherry expression was monitored by fluorescence microscopy of Popliteal lymph nodes (pLN), bone, and muscle cryosections (Supplementary Fig. 13a-d). Surprisingly, mCherry was mainly expressed by T cells in the popliteal lymph nodes, with minimal expression in B cells (Supplementary Fig. 13a). This indicated that AAV6 had a higher infection efficiency in T cells. As for bone-related sites, we found that in the femur and ankle joints, mCherry was primarily expressed in the bone marrow, while growth plates, trabecular bone, and articular cartilage showed minimal mCherry expression (Supplementary Fig. 13b, c). Additionally, AAV6 vector also showed high mCherry expression in adjacent muscles and tendons (Supplementary Fig. 13c, d). This may suggest that the long-term expression of AAV6 in joint and bone-related tissues was primarily due to transduction of tissues with longer lifespans, such as muscles and tendons.

Due to the immunomodulatory capacity of SEMA3A, we further analyzed the AAV6-mediated transduction in the pLNs and bone marrow with flowcytometry (Supplementary Fig. 14a). It was found that the GFP+ population was co-positive for a variety of other cell markers including Ly6G, CD19, CD3, CD11c and F4/80, indicating that AAV6-transduced neutrophils, B cells, T cells, dendritic cells and macrophages in pLNs (Supplementary Fig. 14b). By raw numbers, the largest contributors to the GFP-positive cell population in pLNs were Ly6G+ (neutrophils) and CD3+ (T cells) (Supplementary Fig. 14c). In addition, neutrophils were more highly transduced as a population by AAV6 than any other immune cells in bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 14d). The results indicated that AAV6 could transduced immune cells in both bone marrow and pLNs. Notably, the variance in distribution of immune cells resulted in distinct primary transduced cell types across these two sites.

AAV6-SEMA3A with intramuscular injection preventing bone loss on osteoporosis

We first constructed SEMA3A overexpression cassette and validated the secretory expression of SEMA3A (Supplementary Fig. 15a). Constructs were then packaged into AAV6 capsids, and the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and the SDS-PAGE silver stain were used to evaluate the quality of AAV6-CAG-SEMA3A (Supplementary Fig. 15b, c) (hereafter AAV6-SEMA3A). The results showed that we obtained AAV6-SEMA3A with excellent purity and a low empty shell rate, which was beneficial for the follow-up experiments.

The therapeutic effects of AAV6-SEMA3A with different administration routes were evaluated in the OVX model. The estrogen deficiency-induced osteoporosis model was established using ovariectomized (OVX) mice. After 50 days of recovery, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (Fig. 6a). Both AAV treatment groups were injected only once, and β-Estradiol, as the positive control, was injected every three days for a total of six injections. Micro-CT was used to examine the trabecular bone architecture parameters in the femoral region. The results showed that OVX group exhibited significant bone mass loss due to estrogen deficiency (Fig. 6b). Although parameters, including trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), and SMI (Fig. 6c), were recovered in the E2 and AAV6-SEMA3A i.a. groups, mice in the AAV6-SEMA3A i.m. group showed the best therapeutic effect, with the parameters completely recovered compared to those of the naïve and sham groups. Serum TRAP levels, the number of acid phosphatase-positive osteoclasts, and the apoptosis level of osteoclasts in bone marrow were also examined (Fig. 6d–f). The results showed that AAV6-SEMA3A with i.m. injection induced the apoptosis of osteoclasts and decreased the level of TRAP, indicating that AAV6-SEMA3A with intramuscular injection could effectively inhibit the bone resorption.

To further examine the interaction between bone and immune system, the bone and inflammation related mRNA expression was measured by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6g). We found that the lack of estrogen in mice increased the expression of bone resorption and inflammation-related genes, while the expression of bone formation genes was significantly inhibited (Supplementary Fig. 16a–c). The supplementation of estrogen slightly recovered the imbalance of bone resorption and formation, and AAV6-SEMA3A treatment groups reversed the balance between bone resorption and bone formation, while inhibiting inflammation in bone marrow. The results indicated that SEMA3A could effectively restore bone immune homeostasis, thereby increasing bone mass.

Postmenopausal osteoporosis predominantly targets the femur and tibia, with the femoral bone marrow playing a pivotal role as an immune organ. It has been reported that estrogen deficiency may lead to immune imbalance in the femoral bone marrow, thereby triggering osteoporosis52. Therefore, AAV6-SEMA3A administered via intramuscular injection efficiently transduced muscle and femoral (Supplementary Figs. 12c and 13b, d). On one hand, it formed a therapeutic protein expression reservoir in muscles, ensuring long-term expression and secretion of SEMA3A. On the other hand, it transduced immune cells in the bone marrow, regulating immune balance within the marrow (Supplementary Fig. 14d). This may explain why intramuscular injection of AAV6-SEMA3A exhibited superior therapeutic effects compared to intra-articular injection in the treatment of osteoporosis.

AAV6-SEMA3A with intra-articular injection alleviated joint inflammation and bone erosion on RA

The therapeutic effects of AAV6-SEMA3A treatment with different administration routes were also evaluated in CIA model. The CIA model was established with twice immunization of CII antigen. The experimental scheme is outlined in Fig. 6h. CIA mice were injected with AAV6-SEMA3A i.a. or i.m. only once. Anti-TNF (Adalimumab, Humira, USA) was also employed to treat CIA mice with dose of 8 mg/kg in this study. And AAV6-mcherry group was used as vector control. Adalimumab group showed relatively low therapeutic efficiency in reducing swelling in ankle and paw. Compared to the control and AAV6-mcherry treated mice, AAV6-SEMA3A treated with i.a. or i.m. injection showed higher efficiency in decreasing paw thickness and clinical score. Wherein, AAV6-SEMA3A with intraarticularly injection showed better therapeutic effect than that with intramuscularly injection, yielding a joint status and paw thickness closer to that of the Naïve group at the endpoint of treatment (Fig. 6i, j). In addition, bright field images of the paws showed that no clear paw swelling and redness were observed in AAV6-SEMA3A i.a. group (Fig. 6k). H&E staining showed there was severe synovial hyperplasia accompanied by extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in the control and AAV6-mcherry treated CIA mice (Fig. 6k). In contrast, the severity of these symptoms was much lower in both of AAV6-SEMA3A treated groups. Furthermore, to illustrate that AAV6-SEMA3A could reduce cartilage loss, the ankles were sectioned for safranin O-fast green staining (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. 17). Both of AAV6-SEMA3A groups had smoother and more continuous cartilage than the control, Adalimumab, and capsid control groups, which were closer to the Naïve group. These results indicated that AAV6-SEMA3A with locally injected at the lesion site, was more effective in promoting synovium and cartilage protection compared to that with distal intramuscular injection.

Additionally, in the case of inflammatory cytokines, all treatment groups decreased the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the paws by more than two times. AAV6-SEMA3A with i.a. injection further decreased the concentrations of IL-1β and TNF-α by over 7 times and 13 times, respectively (Fig. 6l, m).

Due to the frequent presence of bone damage in RA patients, we further investigated the bone-protective effects of AAV6-SEMA3A by using micro-CT (Fig. 6n). The images depicted that CIA mice of control and AAV6-mcherry groups had rough bone surfaces and severe bone erosion, especially on finger and ankle joints. AAV6-SEMA3A i.m. and Adalimumab showed moderate efficiency in reducing bone erosion, but there still existed erosion on finger joints. Remarkably, the AAV6-SEMA3A i.a group showed smooth bone surface, suggesting that AAV6-SEMA3A with intraarticular injection could efficiently reverse bone erosion. Furthermore, we found that only the AAV6-SEMA3A treatment groups could significantly increase the expression of osteogenic factor OPG at the inflammatory joint site, inhibit the expression of the osteoclast marker TRAP, and reduce osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 6o, p and Supplementary Fig. 18). However, the adalimumab treatment group had almost no effect on regulating the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Furthermore, we explored the impact of AAV-mediated overexpression of SEMA3A on the mRNA expression levels of bone and inflammation related mRNA levels in CIA mice (Supplementary Fig. 19). The results demonstrate that AAV6-SEMA3A significantly reduces the expression of bone resorption-related factors (DC-STAMP, TRAF6, CatK, MMP13, TRAP) and pro-inflammatory factors (NF-κB, IL-6, iNOS), while enhancing the expression of bone remodeling-related factors (TGF-β, Runx2, OPG, OSX).

Rheumatoid arthritis, being an autoimmune disease primarily affecting joints, implicates the popliteal lymph nodes as crucial draining lymph nodes for the ankle joints. Immunological dysregulation within pLNs may contribute to disease progression. Intra-articular injection of AAV6-SEMA3A may facilitate efficient expression and secretion of SEMA3A at the lesion site, thereby exerting a localized therapeutic effect (Supplementary Figs. 12b and 13c). Additionally, intra-articular injection of AAV6 efficiently transduced immune cells within draining lymph nodes, leading to immunomodulatory effects (Supplementary Fig. 14c). This may explain why intra-articular injection of AAV6-SEMA3A exhibited superior therapeutic effects compared to intramuscular injection in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis.

AAV-mediated SEMA3A overexpression reshaping bone-immune system homeostasis

We proved that SEMA3A could inhibit DC-mediated T cell activation and M1 polarization of macrophages and induce the apoptosis of OCs in vitro (Fig. 5). Thus, we speculated that SEMA3A may also exert a bone-protective effect by modulating the crosstalk between bone and immune system. Moreover, to explore the treatment mechanism in vivo, we further investigated the regulatory role of SEMA3A on the immune system in orthopedic disease models.

Th17 cells drive bone destruction in mice and humans by inducing the expression of RANKL to stimulate OC differentiation53. Furthermore, the imbalance of Th17/Treg cells leads to dysregulation of the bone-immune system, which triggers osteoporosis21. To investigate whether the expression of SEMA3A by AAV6-SEMA3A at the bone marrow can recover the inflammatory microenvironment, we conducted a study on the immune cell profiling in the bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 20a). It was found that AAV6-SEMA3A significantly increased the abundance of Tregs in bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 20b–d). In addition, AAV6-SEMA3A group exhibited the most significant efficacy in suppressing Th17 cells (Supplementary Fig. 20e, f). Furthermore, AAV6-SEMA3A group exhibited the most remarkable effectiveness in suppressing T cell activation and M1 polarization of macrophages in bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 20g, h). Meanwhile, it has been reported that a deficiency in estrogen may lead to a decrease in FASL on DCs, thereby prolonging their lifespan and promoting the differentiation of tissue-resident memory T cells in bone marrow52. Surprisingly, AAV6-SEMA3A could also promote the expression of FASL on DCs in bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 20i), thereby reducing the abundance of mature DCs (CD80+CD11c+) and tissue-resident memory T cells (CD103+CD44+ among CD4+ cells) (Supplementary Fig. 20j, k). Moreover, AAV6-SEMA3A significantly reduced the secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 11n). In addition, AAV6-SEMA3A had markedly regulated the Th17/Treg in pLNs (Supplementary Fig. 20l), Notably, AAV6-SEMA3A inhibited the differentiation of Th17 cells and the activation of T cells in the spleen, while inducing the highest level of IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 20m).

Rheumatoid arthritis patients exhibit an inflammatory microenvironment at the lesion site due to excessive positive immune responses in the body. Therefore, we analyzed the immune cell phenotypes from ankle joints (Supplementary Fig. 21a). It was found that AAV6-SEMA3A markedly suppressed the expression of M1 macrophage markers CD80 and iNOS, as well as the expression of synovial macrophage marker CD68 in ankle joints. In contrast, it promoted the expression of the M2 macrophage marker CD206 (Supplementary Fig. 21b–d). Additionally, AAV6-SEMA3A significantly inhibited the activation of T cells in ankle joints, while inducing the differentiation of Tregs (Supplementary Fig. 21e, f). Considering the high infectivity of AAV6 in lymph nodes through intra-articular injection (Supplementary Fig. 12b), we found that AAV6-SEMA3A significantly reduced the activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the draining lymph nodes, thereby further alleviating the infiltration of immune cells in the inflamed joints (Supplementary Fig. 21g, h).

PF127-RAPA micelles admixed with AAV6-SEMA3A lead to enhanced therapeutic efficacy and permit dose sparing

Building on the promising efficacy of AAV6-SEMA3A monotherapy in the OVX and CIA models, we next evaluated whether PF127-RAPA micelles could reduce the required AAV6-SEMA3A dose in disease models, enhance its therapeutic effect, and mitigate the immune response induced by AAV capsid.

As shown in Fig. 7a, the mice were randomly divided into seven groups. Thirty five days after injection, trabecular bone architecture parameters of femur and vertebra were examined by Micro-CT. It was found that femurs and vertebras from PF127-RAPA and AAV-low groups exhibited significant losses of bone mass, whereas bone-mass loss in the PF127-RAPA + AAV-low, AAV-high and E2 groups was greatly reduced (Fig. 7b). Meanwhile, no improvement in parameters of PF127-RAPA group and AAV6-SEMA3A-low group, mice in the PF127-RAPA + AAV6-SEMA3A-low group showed equally favorable therapeutic effects as AAV6-SEMA3A-high group, with the parameters completely recovered compared to that of the sham and E2 groups (Fig. 7c, d). The results indicated that PF127-RAPA micelles could enhance the therapeutic efficacy of low-dose AAV to a level comparable to high-dose AAV. In addition, PF127-RAPA micelles also significantly reduced the anti-AAV capsids' IgG titers (Fig. 7e).

Additionally, we also investigated the long-term therapeutic effects of low-dose AAV6-SEMA3A in combination with PF127-RAPA micelles on the OVX model (Fig. 7f). After 108 days of receiving either six doses of estrogen or a single treatment of AAV6-SEMA3A, Micro-CT scans were conducted on the femurs and vertebra of the OVX mice. Notably, compared to E2 group, PF127-RAPA + AAV-low and AAV-high groups exhibited significantly elevated bone mass in the femurs and vertebrae (Fig. 7g, i, j). In addition, osteogenic differentiation levels were evaluated by ALP staining of bone marrow cells from each group after osteogenic induction (Fig. 7h). The results showed that PF127-RAPA + AAV-low and AAV-high groups induced a greater number of osteoblasts than E2 group, indicating that the long-term expression of SEMA3A mediated by AAV6 restored the level of osteoblasts in the bone marrow, thus rebalancing bone formation and resorption

We next examined the capacity of PF127-RAPA micelles to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of AAV6-SEMA3A on CIA mice with a low dose. As shown in Fig. 8a, the CIA mice in the PF127-RAPA + AAV-low and AAV-low groups were administered a single intra-articular injection of 5 × 109 vg of AAV6-SEMA3A. The 4-fold dose of AAV6-SEMA3A (2 × 1010 vg) served as AAV-high group. Adalimumab was also employed to treat CIA mice with dose of 8 mg/kg in this study. The paw thickness and clinical score were monitored every other day during the treatment. The results showed that AAV6-SEMA3A only provided slight relief of inflammation with a dose of 5 × 109 vg. However, PF127-RAPA micelles enabled low-dose AAV6-SEMA3A to achieve equally outstanding therapeutic effects as high-dose AAV. Despite using a low dose of rapamycin (5 μg/ankle), we still investigated whether PF127-RAPA micelles could directly exert a therapeutic effect. The results indicated that PF127-RAPA micelles did not display any therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 8b–d and Supplementary Fig. 22). Furthermore, the abundance of CD68+ cells in the joint also indicated that PF127-RAPA + AAV-low could successfully inhibit the proliferation of synovial macrophages (Fig. 8e). In addition, at the endpoint of the experiment, AAV6 capsid-specific antibodies were measured. It was found that PF127-RAPA + AAV-low group showed a 13.5-fold decrease in capsid IgG titers, compared to that of AAV-low group (Fig. 8f).

In addition, we observed that the severity of arthritis in the PF127-RAPA group (administered via intra-articular injection) was greater compared to the control group. We propose that the PF127-RAPA micelle group exhibited results comparable to those of the other treatment groups, likely due to the use of intra-articular injection as the method of administration. In contrast, the Control group did not receive intra-articular injections. Given that intra-articular injections are invasive, the injection procedure may have caused secondary damage to the joints of the mice, potentially exacerbating inflammation during the acute phase of arthritis. To rigorously assess the immunogenicity of PF127 micelles, we conducted an additional in vivo experiment (Supplementary Fig. 23). The results showed that the immunogenicity of PF127-RAPA micelles alone seems inadequate to elevate the immune response against CII collagen in CIA mice. Furthermore, at the treatment endpoint, routine blood test was performed according to the user manual. The results showed that the detected values for all groups were within the normal reference range (Supplementary Fig. 24a–e). Furthermore, no significant pathological changes were observed in major organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys in all groups. This indicated that both AAV and PF127-RAPA micelles have good in vivo safety (Supplementary Fig. 24f).

Based on the currently observed pharmacodynamic parameters, the PF127-RAPA combined low-dose AAV shows no significant difference compared to the high-dose group, suggesting a potentially comparable therapeutic effect.

Discussion

AAV vectors possess several unique advantages, including broad tissue tropism, low immunogenicity, ease of production, stability, long-term gene expression, non-pathogenicity, and minimal integration into the host genome, making them one of the most promising candidates for in vivo gene therapy1,54. However, despite these advantages, their clinical application is constrained by dose-limiting immunotoxicity and limited vector potency6.

Researchers are exploring strategies to enhance AAV transduction while reducing its immunogenicity. One approach utilized bortezomib, a proteasome inhibitor, to reduce AAV degradation and enhance transduction55. However, bortezomib, as an anti-cancer drug, has a rather high incidence of side effects, which limits its clinical feasibility for enhancing AAV transduction. Another common strategy involves engineering AAV capsid mutants with altered properties to directly improve transduction efficiency54. Nevertheless, this approach suffers from high production costs for the mutant capsids and often leads to decreased virus packaging efficiency, further increasing production costs. In addition, after AAV enters the cell nucleus, it must convert its single-stranded DNA into double-stranded DNA for transcription. The phosphorylated FKBP52 inhibits AAV’s second-strand DNA synthesis, limiting transduction efficiency56. Recent studies have reported using T-Cell Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase (TTP) to dephosphorylate FKBP52, alleviating this inhibition. However, this method relies on intracellular signaling, and FKBP52 expression and phosphorylation can vary across tissues. Additionally, TTP delivery depends on auxiliary viral vectors for co-infection with AAV, introducing uncertainty in ensuring both viruses enter the same cell, while also potentially increasing the immune response against AAV57. Thus, this strategy may be better suited for in vitro applications. Various strategies are being explored to mitigate immune responses to the AAV capsid, including the use of multiple immunosuppressants. However, preclinical studies evaluating combinations of potent immunosuppressive agents have shown limited success. Currently, researchers are investigating whether chronic B-cell depletion and daily rapamycin treatment can reduce AAV capsid immunogenicity58,59. Given that patients will undergo immunosuppressive treatment for up to 8 months, such extended regimens may be unacceptable for many. Strategies to enhance AAV transduction typically focus on modulating its cellular uptake efficiency or cytoplasmic fate, while suppression of AAV capsid-induced immune responses depends solely on immunosuppressants. Currently, no single approach can simultaneously achieve both enhanced transduction and reduced immune responses. However, existing studies have overlooked AAV nuclear translocation, a critical step in transduction. The efficiency of nuclear translocation can influence transgene expression and MHC class I presentation of cytoplasmic AAV particles6. Enhancing AAV nuclear entry may therefore offer a more efficient approach, simultaneously improving transduction and reducing capsid-mediated immune responses.

In eukaryotic cells, nuclear-cytoplasmic transport occurs via two mechanisms: passive diffusion and facilitated transport. The former is restricted to molecules smaller than 40 kDa (approximately 10 nm in diameter), while the latter relies on transport receptors60–62. Given its larger capsid diameter (26 nm), AAV is widely believed to rely on active transport through the NPC for nuclear entry63. The NPC scaffold is mechanically sensitive, with its diameter regulated by membrane tension8. External influences can lead to global or local changes in nuclear envelope tension, which may alter NPC diameter and affect its function. Studies suggested that disrupting hydrophobic interactions can induce reversible changes in NPC permeability13,64, supporting our hypothesis that PF127 micelles, through their hydrophobic segments, perturb the nuclear membrane, modulate its tension, and thereby influence NPC transport dynamics (Fig. 3j). While there have been studies utilizing Pluronic to enhance AAV transduction in vivo, these investigations primarily focus on the temperature-sensitive hydrogel properties of PF127, which allows for the encapsulation of AAV within the hydrogel11,65. This local retention effect reduces AAV diffusion and consequently increases local transduction efficiency. However, researchers often overlook the potential regulatory effects of PF127 once it enters the cell, being misled by the spatial retention aspect. Our study reveals for the first time that PF127 alters cellular states from a biomechanical perspective, rendering cells more susceptible to AAV transduction. This finding encourages future researchers to explore enhancement strategies from a biomechanical standpoint, such as applying physical stimuli (like ultrasound or pressure) to modulate the biomechanical state of cells, or intervening at the molecular level to adjust the expression of nuclear lamina proteins, thereby altering nuclear stiffness and enhancing AAV transduction.

Herein, we proposed a novel pharmaceutical strategy utilizing PF127-RAPA micelles to concurrently improve AAV transduction efficiency and diminish capsid-induced immune response, potentially addressing these hurdles. This study is the first, to our knowledge, to demonstrate that PF127 micelles can enhance AAV nuclear entry by increasing nuclear tension, thereby improving AAV transduction efficiency (Fig. 3j). Additionally, we further demonstrated that PF127-RAPA micelles could suppress the humoral and cellular immune responses induced by AAV capsid (Fig. 4). This effect may be due to the combined actions of PF127 and rapamycin. PF127 promoted AAV nuclear entry, reducing the MHC class I-mediated immune response induced by capsid ubiquitination in the cytoplasm (Figs. 3j and 4o, p), while rapamycin directly inhibits the activation of DCs and T cells. Furthermore, in the models of rheumatoid arthritis and osteoporosis, we validated that PF127-RAPA micelles enable a low-dose of AAV6-SEMA3A to achieve therapeutic efficacy comparable to the high-dose group, while reducing capsid antibodies (Figs. 7 and 8). Moreover, we investigated the general applicability of PF127-RAPA micelles across various AAV serotypes, demonstrating their potential as a universal approach to enhance AAV gene therapy. Our newly developed biomechanical strategy allows for more precise modulation of the viral gene delivery vector transduction process by adjusting the biomechanical state of cells. This approach effectively enhances transduction efficiency both in vitro and in vivo, while simultaneously reducing the immunogenicity induced by the capsid. In the future, this strategy could improve the application prospects of viral gene delivery vectors in fields such as CAR-T therapy.

Beyond rheumatoid arthritis and postmenopausal osteoporosis, a range of diseases arises from imbalances in the bone-immune system, including arthritis associated with psoriasis and systemic lupus erythematosus, as well as age-related bone loss66,67. Given the immune system dysregulation observed in these patients, the immunogenicity of AAV vectors poses a potential risk for adverse reactions68. Here, we proposed that PF127-RAPA micelles could facilitate AAV6-mediated delivery of SEMA3A, offering therapeutic potential for various immune and orthopedic disorders. This approach opens avenues for exploring SEMA3A’s therapeutic effects in diverse immune and musculoskeletal diseases. SEMA3A emerges as a promising therapeutic target for rheumatoid arthritis and osteoporosis. Our findings demonstrated that: (1) SEMA3A overexpression in inflamed joints and draining lymph nodes effectively suppressed inflammation and bone damage in CIA mouse models. (2) Similarly, SEMA3A overexpression in the gastrocnemius muscle and femur completely prevented bone loss in osteoporosis models. (3) These therapeutic effects stemmed from SEMA3A’s ability to regulate bone-immune system homeostasis (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). Current treatments for these diseases primarily rely on monoclonal antibodies targeting downstream effectors like TNF-α or RANKL22. By targeting an upstream regulator, SEMA3A offered the potential to address the root cause of these diseases compared to therapies directly modulating downstream effectors in vivo. Furthermore, our study revealed that SEMA3A regulated bone-immune homeostasis in both CIA and OVX models, suggesting its potential as a universal target for other diseases arising from bone-immune system imbalances, such as psoriasis and systemic lupus erythematosus. While previous studies explored SEMA3A protein or DNA delivery for rheumatoid arthritis and osteoporosis45,46, our work demonstrated that AAV-mediated SEMA3A overexpression achieved treatment for both diseases with a single dose and elucidated the mechanism underlying bone-immune system homeostasis regulation. Given the chronic and largely irreversible nature of postmenopausal osteoporosis, we evaluated the long-term therapeutic effects of AAV6-SEMA3A in OVX models (Fig. 7f–j). Our findings suggest that AAV6-mediated SEMA3A delivery has the potential to provide patients with lifelong benefits from a single administration.

Additionally, we observed that RAPA did not exhibit independent therapeutic activity at low-dose administration in this study (Supplementary Fig. 23b, c). However, when rapamycin was encapsulated within PF127 micelles (PF127-RAPA), PF127 acted as an AAV transduction enhancer, thereby augmenting AAV-mediated transgene expression (Fig. 1f–i). Meanwhile, rapamycin served as an immunomodulator, suppressing the specific immune response induced by the AAV capsid (Fig. 4a–j). This synergistic effect significantly enhanced both the intensity and durability of AAV-mediated transgene expression, resulting in a marked improvement in the therapeutic efficacy of AAV-SEMA3A in the OVX and CIA mouse models.

Importantly, PF127-RAPA micelles have been shown to enhance AAV nuclear entry, potentially mitigating MHC class I immune responses triggered by AAV proteasomal degradation. To facilitate clinical translation, a comprehensive evaluation of PF127’s capacity to lessen AAV-induced cell-mediated immune responses is warranted. Furthermore, given the prevalence of intravenous administration for AAV gene therapy vectors, future studies should investigate the impact of PF127-RAPA micelles on AAV following intravenous delivery to definitively establish safety and efficacy.

Admittedly, there are certain limitations in the current study. While we have established a series of mechanistic experiments to investigate the detailed mechanisms by which PF127 enhances AAV transduction, it remains uncertain whether PF127 exerts effects beyond simply modulating the mechanical properties of the nuclear membrane in facilitating AAV nuclear entry. Given the current limitations in research on AAV nuclear entry, many specific mechanisms are still not well understood. Thus, it is plausible that PF127 may regulate alternative, non-NPC pathways for AAV nuclear entry, warranting concurrent explorations of these mechanisms. Moreover, caution must be exercised when interpreting the enhanced therapeutic effects of low-dose AAV in comparison to high-dose AAV in the context of PF127-RAPA micelles. This study was not designed as a pre-defined equivalence or non-inferiority trial. We did not establish a consensus-based equivalence margin prior to the commencement of the study, and therefore, formal statistical equivalence testing was not performed. Consequently, whether PF127-RAPA micelles can, in strict terms, render the therapeutic effect of low-dose AAV equivalent to that of high-dose AAV remains to be verified in future prospective studies, which should be grounded in the establishment of reasonable pre-defined equivalence margins relevant to clinical settings.

Methods

rAAV vector design and production

The codon-optimized SEMA3A gene based on mouse SEMA3A gene (NM_001243072.1) was cloned into a pAAV-CAG-GFP to get pAAV-CAG-SEMA3A. The pAAV-CAG-SEMA3A was packaged into AAV6 capsids. In addition, the pAAV-CAG-Luc was packaged into AAV5 (3.8 × 1012 vg/ml), AAV6 (1.4 × 1012 vg/ml), and AAV8 (8 × 1012 vg/ml) capsids. Recombinant AAVs production was carried out as previously described50,69,70. In brief, rAAVs were produced by triple transfection of AAV293 cells using Polyethylenimine (linear, MW 40,000; Polysciences, USA, #24765-1). After 72 h of transfection, the cells were collected and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min to pellet the cells. The cell pellet was then resuspended in TNE buffer (100 mmol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 20 mmol/L EDTA) and subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles. The crude lysate containing AAV was treated with Benzonase and 10% sodium deoxycholate at 37 °C for 1 h to degrade unencapsulated rAAV DNA. Following this, the lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min to separate encapsulated rAAV particles from cell debris. Subsequently, PEG/NaCl solution (10% polyethylene glycol 8000 (w/v), 0.5 mol/L NaCl) was added, and the mixture was allowed to precipitate at 4 °C for 16 h.

To further purify the rAAV, a gradient centrifugation using pre-prepared iodixanol solutions (60%, 40%, 25%, and 10%) was performed, and the 40% iodixanol layer was collected. The rAAV was further purified using affinity chromatography with POROS™ CaptureSelect™ AAVX Affinity Resin (Thermo, USA, #A36745) and subsequent anion exchange chromatography. The purified rAAV was dialyzed in storage buffer (20 mmol/L Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 180 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L MgCl₂, 0.003% Poloxamer 188) using Slide-A-Lyzer™ Dialysis Cassettes (Thermo, 20 K MWCO, 12 mL, #66012). The vector titer was determined using SybrGreen qPCR with the following primer pairs listed in Supplementary Table 1.

AAV empty capsid rate was determined through observation using transmission electron microscopy. For each sample, three fields of view were examined to ensure that the number of viral particles in each field exceeded 100. Empty Capsid Rate (%) = (Number of empty capsids)/(Total Number of capsids) × 100%. We calculated the empty capsid rates for AAV6-SEMA3A, AAV5-luc, AAV6-Luc, and AAV8-Luc, with the results presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Preparation of PF127 and PF127-RAPA micelles

PF127 (Sigma, USA, CAS-NO: 9003-11-6) and PF127-RAPA micelles were prepared through thin-film hydration method. In brief, PF127 and rapamycin were dissolved in ethanol and evaporated. Then the thin-film was hydrated by the AAV stock buffer and under ultrasound bath for 5 min (PF127 0.4%, w/w and rapamycin 0.5 mg/ml).

Cell lines and animal

HEK293T cells, AAV293 cells, and RAW264.7 were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. ARPE19 cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM-F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. DC2.4 and B3Z were purchased from ATCC and cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. In addition, BMDCs were isolated from the 8-week-old mice (C57BL/6J) and were maintained in RPMI1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin as described. Bone marrow cells were flushed from the femurs and tibias of 8-week-old mice (C57BL/6J), and cultured with 30 ng/ml of M-CSF for 2 days and used as BMDMs. BMDMs were then differentiated into OCs in the presence of 50 ng/ml of RANKL and 30 ng/ml of M-CSF for 4 days. To generate M1 phenotype macrophages, BMMs were treated with 200 ng/ml of LPS for 24 h. All cells were cultured under 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Healthy female Balb/C mice (20 ± 2 g, 8 weeks old), female C57BL/6 J mice (20 ± 2 g, 8 weeks old), and male DBA/1 mice (20 ± 2 g, 8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience (Beijing, China). All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with the ambient temperature and relative humidity controlled at 21 ± 1 °C and 60 ± 5%, respectively. The mice were subjected to a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided with unrestricted access to food and water. All animal studies were performed with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Sichuan University (K2023031).

The repolarization of M1 phenotype macrophages

BMDMs were seeded in 24-well plates and were pretreated with 750 ng/ml LPS for 3 h. followed by three washes with cold PBS. Then cells were treated with the concentration gradient of SEMA3A (Sino Biological) in the complete medium for 24 h. Then, the supernatants were collected to analyze the concentration of cytokines of IL-6 and TNF-α. Cells were collected, centrifuged, and then incubated with PECy5-anti-F4/80, FITC-anti-CD80, and Perpeflour710-anti-CD206 at 4 °C for 40 min, and then detected with flow cytometry. The corresponding flow cytometry gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 25.

Apoptosis study of osteoclasts

OCs were treated with the concentration gradient of SEMA3A (0 ng/ml, 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, 200 ng/ml) for 48 h. Cell apoptosis was determined with an Annexin-FITC/PI Apoptosis kit.

Mixed lymphocyte reaction

BMDCs were seeded in 96-well plates and were treated with SIINFEKL (Genescript, 1 μg/ml). After an 18 h incubation, BMDCs were collected and co-cultured with B3Z cells (1:2), and treated with SEMA3A (50, 100, 200 ng/ml) for 24 h. Then, collected cells were resuspended in chlorophenol red β-D-galactopyranoside buffer for 72 h. The absorbance of cells at 570 nm was determined by a microplate reader.

BMDCs were seeded in 24-well plates and were treated with SIIFEKL (5 μg/ml) for 24 h. Lymphocytes of spleens were obtained from OVA-immunized C57BL/6J mice using lymphocyte separation media and co-cultured with the BMDCs after activation, and treated with SEMA3A (200 ng/ml) and IL-2 (Pepro Tech, 10 ng/ml) for 48 h. Cells were collected and incubated with PE-anti-CD4 at 4 °C for 40 min. After two washes, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set, followed by staining with BV421-anti-FoxP3, and then detected with flow cytometry.

BMDCs were seeded in 24-well plates and were treated with SIIFEKL (5 μg/ml) for 24 h. Lymphocytes of spleens were obtained from 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice using lymphocyte separation media and co-cultured with the BMDCs after activation, and treated with SEMA3A (200 ng/ml) for 48 h. Cells were collected and incubated with APC-anti-CD3 and PEcy7-anti-CD69 at 4 °C for 40 min, and then detected with flow cytometry.

In vivo transduction of rAAV serotypes

For screening rAAV vectors in vivo, 8-week-old DBA/1 mice were intraarticularly. injected into ankle joints with 10 μl of AAV5-Luc, AAV6-Luc and AAV8-Luc. The luminous signal was gathered by the IVIS Lumina III system (PerkinElmer, USA). In all groups, the dose of AAV vectors was 8 × 109 vg/ankle.

rAAV6-mediated gene delivery in ankle with intra-articular injection

For evaluating the lasting time of rAAV6 mediated gene expression, 10 μl of rAAV6-Luc vectors (2 × 1010 vg) was i.a. injected into ankle joints of 8-week-old BALB/C mice. The luminous signal was gathered by the IVIS Lumina III system (PerkinElmer, USA).

Therapeutic efficacy of rAAV6-mediated delivery of SEMA3A in RA

For CIA model, 8-week-old male DBA/1 mice were immunized according to a previous study47,71. CIA mice were randomly divided into six groups, and on the day after the second inoculation, mice were injected with 2 × 1010 vg of AAV6-SEMA3A via i.a. or i.m only once. And the AAV capsid control mice were injected intra-articularly with AAV6-mcherry. Adalimumab (8 mg/kg, Humira, USA) was used as the positive control and injected subcutaneously every other two weeks. Inflammatory symptoms were assessed by hind paw clinical scoring and thickness during the treatment.

Therapeutic efficacy of rAAV6-mediated delivery of SEMA3A in osteoporosis

The osteoporosis model was established by ovariectomy of 8-week-old female C57BL/6J mice according to a previous study72. After 8 weeks recovery, the mice were randomly divided into six groups: Naïve, Sham, OVX, E2 (3 μg/leg every other three days, i.m.), AAV6-SEMA3A (2 × 1010 vg/ankle, i.a.), AAV6-SEMA3A (2 × 1010 vg/leg, i.m.).

Micro-CT analysis

Ankle joints from CIA mice, femurs, and vertebrae from OVX mice were scanned using ex vivo micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT, Quantum GX II, USA) at 90 kV with a resolution of 10 μm. The dataset was then reconstructed to obtain 3D images. And the bone parameters, such as BMD, bone surface vs. bone volume (BS/BV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), connectivity density (Conn.D.), and structure model index (SMI) were also quantitatively analyzed.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA from femurs was purified with TRIzol RNA isolation reagents, and cDNA was synthesized using high-capacity cDNA reverse Transcription Kit. Quantitative Real-time PCR was used to measure levels of mRNAs and normalized to levels of 18S mRNA. All primers are shown in Supplementary Table. 1.

PF127 and PF127-RAPA micelles optimized the transduction efficiency of different serotype AAV in vitro

HEK293T cells and ARPE19 were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates. 1 × 109 vg AAV6-GFP or 7.5 × 109 vg AAV8-GFP were added to the HEK293T or ARPE19 cells, respectively. Cells were gathered after 72 h of incubation, followed by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry examination

PF127 and PF127-RAPA micelles improved the transduction efficiency and reduce capsid antibody of AAV in vivo

Eight-week-old female Balb/c mice were intraarticularly injected into ankle joints with AAV6-Luc, PF127 + AAV6-Luc, and PF127-RAPA + AAV6-Luc. The luminous signal on days 3, 7, 14, 21, 30, 60, and 80 was gathered by the IVIS Lumina III system (PerkinElmer, USA). In all groups, the dose of rAAV vectors was 5 × 109 vg/ankle.

For capsid antibody assay, blood samples were obtained from angular vein of mice on day 30 to examine AAV6 capsid-specific IgG antibody detected by ELISA. The endpoint titer was defined as the highest reciprocal serum dilution that yielded an absorbance ≥2-fold over the average values of negative control samples.

AAV binding assay

For AAV binding studies, HEK293T cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates and incubated for 2 h. Cells were placed in a prechilled at 4 °C for 30 min and then the medium was changed to 1000 μl cold serum-free medium containing Free Cy5-labeled AAV6 (MOI-low = 5 × 104; MOI-high = 1 × 105) or PF127 + Cy5-AAV6 (MOI-low = 5 × 104) at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by three washes with cold PBS. After gathering cells that had bound AAV particles, cells were labeled with TRITC Phalloidin, and then the colocalization of AAV and membrane was detected with confocal microscopy and flow cytometry examination.

AAV receptor-mediated endocytosis assay

HEK293T cells treated with or without neuraminidase (Diamond) were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates and incubated with Free AAV1-mCherry and PF127 + AAV1-mCherry (MOI = 1 × 106) for 72 h, followed by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry examination.

AAV trafficking assay in vitro

For interrogation of nuclear uptake, HEK293T cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates and incubated with Free AAV6-GFP, PF127 + AAV6-GFP, PF127-RAPA + AAV6-GFP, and RAPA + AAV6-GFP (MOI = 1 × 104) for 4 h, followed by cell collection and viral genome extraction from cytoplasm and nucleus, respectively. Total viral genomic DNA for quantification was isolated and subjected to qPCR measurement.

AAV trafficking assay in vivo

Eight-week-old female Balb/c mice were intramuscularly injected with AAV6-GFP or PF127-RAPA + AAV6-GFP (5 × 109 vg/leg). After 18 days of injection, the femurs and muscles were collected, and the viral genome DNA was isolated and subjected to qPCR measurement.

Detection of ubiquitination levels in AAV capsids

Approximately 2 × 106 HeLa cells were treated with MG-132 (10 μM) for 4 h. Then, the cells were subjected to infection with AAV2-GFP or PF127 + AAV2-GFP (MOI = 5 × 103) for 4 h. For immunoprecipitations, cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (containing 1% PMSF), and the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation to yield the whole cell lysates (WCLs). WCLs were cleared of nonspecific binding by incubation with 0.25 mg of normal mouse IgG together with 20 μL of protein G-agarose beads. After preclearing, 2 μg of capsid antibody against intact AAV2 particles (anti-AAV2 mouse recombinant, A20R, PROGEN #690298S) were added and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by precipitation with protein G-agarose beads. Subsequently, the samples enriched by immunoprecipitation were analyzed for ubiquitination levels using the ELISA method.

Nanoindentation

To determine nuclear properties, B16F10 cells were seeded at 2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated with Free AAV6-GFP, PF127 + AAV6-GFP (MOI = 5 × 103), and PF127 micelles for 4 h. For nuclear mechanical characterization, after the aforementioned treatments, the cells were subjected to nanoindentation measurements using a displacement-controlled nanoindenter (Piuma; Optics11, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). This system is equipped with a ferrule-top cantilever probe that enables simultaneous force application and indentation depth recording via a fiber-optic readout. A spherical indentation probe (radius: 10.5 μm; spring constant: 0.47 N/m) was employed throughout the experiments. Prior to each measurement session, the cantilever’s bending stiffness was calibrated by indenting a hard, reference surface, which allowed for the conversion of cantilever deflection into precise probe displacement. Subsequently, the probe was precisely positioned over the target area on the cell nucleus for indentation.

ALP staining and alizarin red staining

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were harvested from drug-treated mice for osteogenic differentiation assessment. The isolation procedure involved flushing femurs and tibiae to collect mononuclear progenitor cells, which were then cultured in α-MEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After 48-h incubation, non-adherent hematopoietic cells were removed while adherent stromal cells were maintained in fresh culture medium. Subsequent medium changes were performed every 48 h until 80% confluence was achieved, followed by trypsin-mediated subculturing. Second-passage cells were utilized for experimental analyses.

For osteogenic differentiation assays, P2 BMSCs were plated in 24-well culture plates at 2 × 10⁶ cells/well using osteogenic induction medium (α-MEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 50 mg/L ascorbic acid from Solarbio, 10 nM dexamethasone from Sigma-Aldrich, and 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate from Solarbio). Medium renewal was consistently performed every two days throughout the differentiation period.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was evaluated at day 14 using a commercial detection kit (Beyotime), while matrix mineralization was assessed at day 21 through Alizarin Red S staining (Beyotime). Both cellular staining patterns were documented using inverted fluorescence microscopy imaging systems.

Intracellular factor staining

For the experiment on the regulation of the cell immune response induced by the AAV capsid with PF127-RAPA micelles, cells were isolated from popliteal lymph nodes and spleen of mice following drug treatment. The spleen cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 4 × 10⁶ cells per well and stimulated for 1 h with a mixture of MHC class I epitopes derived from the AAV6 capsid (SYAHSQSL; VLPGYKYL; RTGNNFTF; QGPIWAKI, 3 μg/mL) and AAV6-GFP (8 × 10⁹ vg/mL). Afterward, a protein transport inhibitor cocktail (Invitrogen, USA) was added, and the cells were incubated for 8 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation and stained with 100 μL of FACS staining buffer containing APC-Cy7-anti-mouse-CD4, FITC-anti-mouse-CD8a, APC-anti-mouse-CD44, and eFluor506-anti-mouse-MHC-II at 4 °C in the dark for 40 min. After two washes, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set, followed by staining with PE-Cy5-anti-mouse-Foxp3, eFluor450-anti-mouse-TNF-α, and PE-Cy7-anti-mouse-IFN-γ for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the samples were washed twice with PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry. The corresponding flow cytometry gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 26.

The pLNs cells were collected by centrifugation and stained with BV510 dye at 4 °C for 40 minutes. After two washes, the cells were collected by centrifugation and stained with 100 μL of FACS staining buffer containing PE-anti-mouse-CD19, AlexaFluor488-anti-mouse-GL7, and APC-anti-mouse-CD45R at 4 °C in the dark for 40 min. Finally, the samples were washed twice with PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry. The corresponding flow cytometry gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 27.

Biosafety evaluation

At the end of the treatment, mouse serum samples were collected to assess RBC, WBC, PLT, Mon, and lymph levels. Additionally, main organs, including the heart, liver, lung, and kidney, were separated for H&E staining, then observed by an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Statistical methods

The analyses were performed with Graphpad Prism v10.0. Two-tailed unpaired t-test, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA were used for data comparison. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. As shown in the box plots in Figs. 7e and 8f, the boxes represent the interquartile range (IQR, from the 25th to the 75th percentile) with the median indicated by the central line. The whiskers denote the minimum and maximum values, and all individual data points are overlaid.
